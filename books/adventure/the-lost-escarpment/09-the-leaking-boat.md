## 9. The Leaking Boat

The rain had stopped being weather somewhere above the gorge and become a condition of the world instead—a fact of existence, like gravity or the color of blood. It came down in gray sheets that erased the far bank of the river, erased the sky, erased the distinction between the water falling and the water rising. Orsen sat in the stern of the freighter canoe with his hands wrapped around a bail bucket that had once held engine coolant, and he bailed, and bailed, and watched the river climb.

They had been on the water four days. The first two had been almost kind—fast current, easy lines through the lower rapids, Wren in the bow reading the water with an instinct that surprised even her, Callum amidships with his easy grin and his stories about camps he'd supposedly seen. Then the monsoon had arrived in earnest, and the river had changed its mind about them.

The canoe was taking on water faster than they could fight it. Orsen could hear it coming in—not through any single hole but everywhere at once, a whispering seep along every seam, the old canvas-and-resin hull giving up its long argument against the world. The bilge sloshed around their ankles, around the duffels they'd lashed high, around the medicine kit Wren kept dry inside a rubber bag she guarded like a child.

"Left side's worse," Wren called back over the rain. Her voice had gone hoarse from shouting over water for four days. "There's a seam opening up just behind your knee."

"I see it." He didn't, not really. He saw gray water and gray rain and the gray blur of the banks, and somewhere in all that grayness a place to stop before the river finished what it had started.

Callum leaned forward, rain streaming off his jaw. "There's a settlement ahead—or there was. Old mission station, maybe. I remember a church spire from my last run down this stretch."

"You said you ran this stretch in dry season," Orsen said. "In August. When the river was three meters lower and you weren't lying about half of what you told us."

Callum smiled the smile that Orsen had learned not to trust and couldn't quite dislike anyway. "The spire doesn't move, pilot. Look for the spire."

Orsen looked. And there it was—a dark vertical line against the curtain of rain, maybe a kilometer downstream on the left bank, leaning slightly, like a man standing too long in bad weather. A church. Or what was left of one.

He swung the canoe toward it, and the river fought him the whole way.

* * *

Getting ashore nearly killed them. The current near the bank was a lie—it looked slow until it took hold of the hull and dragged them sideways into a stand of drowned saplings, and then Orsen was in the water to his chest, hauling on a gunwale while Wren threw him a line and Callum cursed and slipped and went under once, twice, coming up each time with his grin intact and his eyes wide with something that wasn't amusement. They got the canoe beached in the flooded grass at the foot of the churchyard wall, tied it to a fence post that might have been iron once and was mostly rust now, and stood gasping in the rain, looking up at the building.

It had been a proper church once, Orsen thought. Timber-framed, tin-roofed, built by missionaries who'd believed this river valley needed saving and hadn't asked whether anyone wanted it saved. Now the roof was half gone, peeled back like the lid of a sardine tin, and the floor was under forty centimeters of brown floodwater, and the pews floated gently in rows like they were waiting for a congregation of drowned men.

But the walls stood. The eastern end still had a roof over it, sagging but whole, and there was a woodstove in the corner—black, ancient, probably cracked—and stacked against the wall beneath a tarp someone had left decades ago, a cache of firewood gone soft at the bottom but sound at the top.

"Home sweet home," Callum said.

Orsen didn't answer. He was already counting. Firewood enough for two days if they were careful. A stove. Walls. Height—the floor was raised on stone piers, which meant if the river rose another meter they'd have warning before it reached them. It would do. It would have to do.

They carried everything up out of the canoe in three trips, Wren working with a silent ferocity that made Orsen glad she was on his side of whatever was coming. By the time the last duffel was inside, the light was failing, and the rain had found a new gear, hammering the remaining roof like fists on a door.

* * *

The stove lit on the third try, with a handful of resin-soaked shavings Orsen kept in a tin for exactly this kind of night. The smoke was bitter and the draft was wrong and the heat barely reached past arm's length, but it was heat, and after four days of monsoon it felt like a miracle. They hung their wet clothes on nails driven into the wall by hands long dead, and draped themselves in the blankets from the duffels, and ate cold rice and smoked fish while the floodwater lapped quietly at the pew ends below them, patient as anything.

Wren sat closest to the stove, her father's map open across her knees, protected now by nothing but a sheet of oilcloth. She wasn't reading it. Orsen had noticed that over the past days—she didn't need to read it anymore. She'd memorized it. Every contour line, every annotation in Elias Marsh's cramped hand, every coordinate. She looked at it the way people look at photographs of the dead.

"He camped here," she said suddenly, tapping a spot on the map where the river bent. "Third summer. He wrote 'mosquitoes beyond bearing, lost half my tobacco to mold.' That's all. Just—" She stopped. Her thumb moved over the paper. "Thirty years of notes like that. Weather and mosquitoes and how his boots wore out. And underneath all of it, the thing he was actually doing, and he never wrote a word of that down. Not directly. You'd need the coordinates and the dates together to see it."

"The surveyor's stamp," Orsen said.

"The stamp." She folded the map along its old creases, careful, careful. "Camp K-7. A camp that isn't in any record. My father spent thirty years proving a place existed that everyone official swears never did."

Callum, stretched out on a pew with his boots off, said nothing. But Orsen saw his eyes track to the map and stay there a moment too long before sliding away.

Orsen got up and waded to the canoe. The hull sat low in the black water, and when he tilted it he heard the seep again—that whisper along the seams, worse now, a seam aft opened visibly, a dark split in the canvas the length of his forearm. He hauled the canoe up onto the churchyard wall itself, wedged it against a leaning grave marker, and turned it over to look at the damage by the light of the storm-lit sky.

It was bad. Not fatal—he'd patched worse in his years of flying, patched fuel tanks and cowlings and once, memorably, a float that had met a rock bar at speed—but bad, and getting worse, and every hour it sat here was an hour the resin wouldn't cure properly in this wet, and every day they waited was a day Harker gained.

He thought about Harker the way you think about a tooth that's started to ache: constantly, unwillingly, probing the pain to see if it's still there. The quiet man from the trading post checkpoint. The note Callum had passed him—Orsen had seen it, had said nothing, had spent three days deciding what saying nothing meant. He still hadn't decided. That was the trouble with trust; it wasn't a thing you decided once. It was a thing you decided every morning, over and over, and some mornings the evidence argued louder than others.

He carried the canoe's repair kit up to the church: a roll of canvas, a brick of resin, a pot for melting it, a scraper, a strip of tarpaulin. His hands knew the work. His father had been a boatbuilder before the war took everything else, and Orsen had spent boyhood summers learning seams and resins before he'd ever touched a control yoke. Some knowledge stays in the hands forever, sleeping, waiting.

"What are you doing?" Wren asked.

"Patching. Tonight, while we've got fire. Resin won't set right in the rain, but under a roof, with a stove—" He shrugged. "We'll see. If I can get the main seam sealed we can run the next stretch tomorrow between squalls. After that the gorge starts, and I don't want to be in that water with a leaking hull."

Callum sat up. "The gorge is two days' hard paddling. Maybe three in this."

"Then we'd better start tonight."

* * *

The work took hours. Orsen worked by stove-light and the blue flicker of lightning through the broken roof, kneeling on the overturned hull with the resin pot steaming beside him, feeding heat into the canvas until it was dry enough to take the patch. Wren helped without being asked—holding the tarp strip in place while he laid the hot resin, wiping the excess, learning the rhythm of it fast. She was good with her hands. Dock work did that, he supposed. Everything about her was competent in a way that never announced itself.

Callum bailed the church. That was his job, self-assigned: a bucket, the flooded nave, the endless transfer of brown water out the western door into the rain. It was futile—the river just came back in—but he did it anyway, steadily, humming something tuneless, and Orsen decided that counted for something. A man who'd sold them out wouldn't bail a flooded church for three hours. Probably. The *probably* was the part he lived with now.

Around midnight the storm eased—not ended, never ended, but dropped from fury to a steady drumming—and the fire caught properly in the old stove, and the church filled for the first time in years with something like warmth.

They dried their clothes. They drank tea made from spruce tips Wren had gathered on the bank, bitter and resinous and somehow exactly right. And because there was nothing else to do, and because the fire made silence feel less like peace and more like a held breath, they talked.

Not about the map. Not about Harker. About small things. Wren talked about the dock at Kestrel Landing—the smell of it, the way the salmon came in silver and left as money, the foreman who cheated the younger workers and the older woman who kept a ledger of every theft and swore someday she'd present it. Callum talked about places he'd been, and half the stories were clearly embellished and half were clearly true and it was impossible to sort them, and somehow that was fine. Orsen talked least. But at some point he heard himself describing the first plane he'd ever flown, a wrecked bush trainer he'd rebuilt at sixteen from three other wrecks, and realized he was talking freely, easily, in a way he hadn't in years.

That was the strange thing about the church, he thought later. He had built his whole life on not being known—on landing at strips where nobody remembered his face, paying cash, moving on before the second night. Invisibility as a discipline, invisibility as armor. And here, in a drowned missionary church in the middle of a monsoon, with two people he'd known for less than two weeks, he was telling stories. Being seen. And the sky hadn't fallen.

Wren must have caught something in his face, because she looked over at him across the fire and said, quietly, "You don't talk much about yourself."

"There's not much to talk about."

"That's a lie," she said, without heat. "Everyone says that, and it's always a lie. There's always something."

He fed a stick of driftwood into the stove. Driftwood burned green and hissing, full of salt and old water, and the flames turned strange colors for a moment—blue and green ghosts dancing off the black iron.

"My name isn't Orsen Blackwood," he said.

The rain filled the pause. Callum had gone very still on his pew.

"It's the name I use. One of several. I've had five that I can remember, maybe six. I flew supply runs for a mining outfit once—younger, stupider—and I flew things I shouldn't have, cargo I didn't ask about, and when I finally understood what I'd been part of, I didn't go to the authorities. I just left. Changed my name, burned my license history, became a ghost." He watched the fire. "I told myself it was penance. Living invisible. Really it was just another way of not looking at it."

Wren said nothing. She pulled the blanket tighter around her shoulders and watched him with those level eyes, and she didn't offer absolution, and he was grateful for that. Absolution would have been cheap.

"So when your father gave me that map," Orsen went on, "and asked me to carry it to you—I almost said no. Every instinct I have said no. Ghosts don't carry evidence. Ghosts don't get involved." He looked up at her. "But he was dying, and he trusted me, and I couldn't figure out how to be the kind of man who says no to that. So here I am. Leaking boat, flooded church, company killers upstream. All because I couldn't say no."

"Good," Wren said.

He blinked. "Good?"

"You couldn't say no." She nodded slowly, as if confirming something to herself. "My father spent thirty years watching good people say no. Pilots who wouldn't fly him. Traders who wouldn't hold his letters. Everyone had reasons. Everyone had instincts." Her voice stayed flat, controlled, but something moved underneath it like a fish under ice. "You're the first one in thirty years who couldn't."

The fire popped. Out in the dark, the river kept rising, patient, certain.

Orsen found he had no answer, so he gave none. He got up instead, checked the patch—cooling now, curing, holding—and checked the river through the western door, where the water had climbed another hand's width up the churchyard wall. Close. Too close. They'd launch at first light regardless, because staying was its own kind of drowning.

* * *

They slept in shifts, though sleep was mostly a rumor that night. Orsen took first watch, sitting by the stove with the shotgun across his knees, listening to the rain and the river and the small sounds of the church settling. Around two he woke Wren, and she took the watch without complaint, and he lay down on the hard pew and stared at the broken ceiling where the stars should have been and found only cloud.

He dreamed of flying. Low over the river, in fog, the way he'd flown out of the burning strip town weeks ago—except in the dream the fog cleared and below him was the escarpment, and the plateau was covered in people, hundreds of them, standing in rows among the graves, all of them looking up at him, all of them waiting for him to land.

He woke before dawn with his heart going hard, and the rain had softened to a gray murmur, and Wren was shaking his shoulder.

"Listen," she whispered.

He listened. Under the rain, under the river, faint and far upstream but unmistakable: an engine. Not an outboard—an aircraft, throttled low, circling, searching.

Company planes didn't fly this valley. No legal reason to. No charter route, no claim staked this far upriver.

"They found the trading post," Wren breathed. "Harker knows which way we went."

Out in the gray dawn, the engine sound faded northward, hunting. Orsen stood in the doorway of the drowned church, the patched canoe waiting below him on the wall, the river running high and fast and angry toward the gorge, and made the only calculation left to make.

"We launch now," he said. "Before they come back. We run for the gorge while it's still raining."
