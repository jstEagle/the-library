## 10. Callum's Story

The rain had found a rhythm by the second night in the flooded church, and Callum had learned to hate it the way you hate a voice that won't stop talking. It came off the roof in a thousand small percussions, drumming the tin, dripping through the holes into the buckets they'd set out, filling the silence between the three of them with something that was almost company. The driftwood fire burned low and orange on a platform of flat stones they'd carried up from the riverbank, and its light moved across the ruined nave like something alive—sliding over the warped pews stacked against one wall, the water-stained plaster, the small wooden cross above the altar that had gone gray as driftwood itself.

Orsen was on bailer duty. He worked the canoe turned upside down beside the fire, running his palms along the hull seam by seam, checking his patch job with the patience of a man who had nothing else in the world to do and intended to do it perfectly. Wren sat with her back against a pew, her father's map open across her knees even though she no longer needed it. Callum had watched her fold and unfold it a dozen times since the trading post. She wasn't reading it. She was holding it, the way a person holds a hand.

He'd been waiting two days for this. Not for the moment exactly—he couldn't have said what the moment would look like—but for the pressure behind his ribs to become unbearable enough that the story would have to come out. Stories were like that. You could carry them sealed for years, and then one night the rain got loud enough and the fire got small enough and the people across from you got quiet enough, and the seal just went.

"So," he said, and his voice cracked on the single syllable like a boy's. He cleared his throat. "You want to know why I know the river."

Orsen's hands stopped moving on the hull. He didn't turn around. "I didn't ask."

"You didn't have to. You've been asking since the dock." Callum fed a piece of driftwood to the fire and watched the bark catch. "You just had the manners not to say it out loud. That's rarer than you'd think, in my experience."

Wren looked up. In the firelight her face was all planes and shadow, her father's stubborn jaw and none of her father's softness—though maybe that was unfair, maybe Elias Marsh had been hard too, at the end, hard enough to spend thirty years chasing a massacre up a river nobody would name. "Then say it out loud," she said. "Whatever it is. I'm tired of watching you decide things we don't know about."

Callum laughed, and it came out wrong, too high, and he hated that too. He pulled his knees up and wrapped his arms around them, making himself small the way he'd learned to make himself small a long time ago, in a place where small was survival.

"Camp Meridian," he said. "That's what they called it. You won't find it on any map. You won't find it in any filing cabinet in the company offices, because as far as the company is concerned, it never existed." He nodded at the map on Wren's knees. "But your father knew. He spent thirty years knowing, didn't he. I could see it in his margins."

Wren's hand flattened on the paper. "Go on."

"I was born there. Or near enough—my mother was brought up the river when she was pregnant, one of the contract laborers, the ones they recruited out of the coastal villages with promises of wages and a return passage." He heard himself talking in the flat recitation voice and couldn't stop it; it was the only way the story would come. "The passage never came. The wages were company scrip, redeemable only at the company store, and the store's prices were set so you always ended the season owing more than you'd earned. That's not a job. That's a cage with arithmetic for bars."

The fire popped. Orsen had turned around now. He sat on his heels with his forearms on his knees, and he was watching Callum with those gray eyes that gave away nothing, and Callum found that he needed that—needed the man's silence like a wall to push against.

"I was six when I started working," he said. "Sizing ore. You sit at a table with a screen and you pick the rock that goes through from the rock that doesn't, ten hours a day, and your fingers bleed, and after a while they don't bleed anymore because the skin gets hard, and after a while you don't feel anything at all, which is the whole point of the place. There were eleven of us children. We ate in a separate mess. We slept in a bunkroom at the end of the camp, near the powder magazine, because—" his mouth twisted, "—because the adults said it was safer there, and the adults who said it were lying, and the adults who knew it was a lie had stopped being able to say anything at all."

"What happened?" Wren asked. Her voice was very quiet.

"You know what happened. Your father spent half his life finding out what happened." Callum looked at the fire. "It was the winter the ore ran poor. The company had been running the camp on paper—showing production to investors, paying off the surveyors, cooking the assays. When the vein thinned, the paper couldn't hold. There were forty-one people at Meridian who could testify that they'd been worked without pay, that the company doctor had let men die of infections a shot of penicillin would have fixed, that the foremen kept order with rifle butts and worse. Forty-one witnesses to fraud, to assault, to a death or two that was never investigated. And the camp was illegal to begin with—no lease, no filings, no record. The easiest way to make forty-one problems disappear is to make sure they were never anywhere to begin with."

He stopped. The rain filled the pause, patient and enormous.

"I was eleven," he said. "The night it happened, I couldn't sleep. The powder magazine—there was a storm, and the tarp over the magazine roof had come loose, and I was scared of the thunder getting into the powder, I don't know, children make up reasons. I went out. I got as far as the tree line before the shooting started." His hands had become fists inside his arms, knuckles white. "I stood in the trees and I watched the lanterns and I heard it, all of it, and I did not move. Eleven years old, and I understood, in the way children understand things completely and cannot say them, that if I made a sound I would be the twelfth grave. So I watched. And then it stopped, and there was fire, and in the morning there were men from the company survey team painting over the camp with new coordinates, and I walked downriver. It took me nine days. I ate bark. I drank from leaf cups. I was a small child and the river was low and God, if there is one, wanted a witness."

Wren had put her hand over her mouth. Orsen hadn't moved at all, but something in the set of his shoulders had changed—some judgment suspended, some weight redistributed.

"The company found me eventually," Callum went on. "Not the men who did it—they were never told my name. But the ones who keep the lists. Because a survivor is a ledger entry that didn't balance, and there are people whose entire job is balancing ledgers. I've been running for twenty-two years. I've had four names. I've worked docks from here to the coast, and every couple of years a stranger buys me a drink and asks friendly questions, and I move on before the drink is finished." He looked up, and let them see his face, all of it, the performance stripped off or the performance put on—he genuinely no longer knew which. "That's why I know the rapids below the escarpment. Because I came down them, at eleven, in the dark, on a raft of deadfall. It's the only map I've ever needed, and I've been trying to get back up that river my whole life, and every time I got close, I lost my nerve. Because going back means being seen. And being seen, for people like us, means being dead."

The silence that followed was long. The rain drummed. The fire settled into coals.

It was Orsen who spoke first, and his voice was rough, like a man waking. "Eleven children," he said. "You said eleven of you in the bunkroom."

"Ten graves plus me," Callum said. "Yes."

"And the adults. Forty-one."

"Forty-one souls, all told. Your father's number was thirty-eight. He was close." Callum smiled, and there was nothing of humor in it. "He was three short because he never found the children's bunkroom. It was upstream of the camp proper, hidden by the tailings pile. He marked the tailings on his map." He nodded at Wren's knees. "Look at the northwest corner. There's a hachure mark that doesn't match the others. That's not a cliff. That's where they put the children."

Wren made a sound that was not quite a word, and folded the map closed with hands that shook, and pressed it flat against her chest.

Orsen rose, crossed the space between them, and sat down on the upturned canoe across from Callum. For a while he said nothing. Then: "I've spent nine years making sure nobody knew my name. I know what it costs, being unseen. I didn't know what it costs, being seen." He rubbed his jaw. "If any of what you said is true—"

"If," Callum said.

"—then Harker isn't just chasing a map. He's chasing the last witness. Which means we're not running from him anymore. We're carrying him toward the one place he can't afford to let us reach."

"That's about the shape of it."

Orsen looked at him for a long moment, and then he did something Callum had not seen him do: he extended his hand. Callum took it. The grip was dry and hard and brief, and it was the first handshake Callum had accepted in six years without calculating the exit.

Wren unrolled her bedroll without a word. But as she lay down, facing the fire, she said, "My father wrote in the margin once—just once, where the ink had run. He wrote: *the youngest one got out.*" Her eyes found Callum's across the flames. "I used to think it was a wish. Something he hoped for."

"And now?"

"Now I think it was a question. And I think I'd like it to be an answer." She turned away. "But answers can be bought, in my experience. So I'll believe you tomorrow. And the day after. And every day you don't sell us."

"That's fair," Callum said softly.

He took the middle watch, as he'd insisted he would, and sat by the fire with the rifle across his knees while the other two slept, and the rain came down, and the river spoke in the dark below the church like a voice practicing names.

And here was the thing he could not have said to them, the thing he held in the sealed place where the story had been: sitting there in the firelight, telling it, he had felt the story *fit*—every detail sliding into every other detail with the smoothness of long rehearsal—and he could no longer tell whether he was a survivor remembering, or a liar who had told the lie so long it had grown a childhood around itself. There had been a camp. There had been a bunkroom. There had been blood. Those things were true the way bedrock is true. Whether he had watched from the trees or heard it secondhand in a dockside bar from a dying man's mouth—whether his mother had died at Meridian or in a coastal town of ordinary fever—was a door in him that had closed, and behind it, only the story remained, and the story was load-bearing now. He lived inside it. It held up the sky.

He only knew that when Wren had pressed the map to her chest, something in him had ached so fiercely and so specifically that it felt like memory.

Near dawn the rain thinned. Callum stood, stiff-kneed, and went to the broken window at the front of the church to look at the river—and stopped.

Downstream, around the bend, where the flooded channel widened, there was a light. Not a campfire. Not lightning. A lantern, moving steadily along the far bank, low to the ground, the way a man carries a light when he is reading tracks and does not want the tracks to see him reading.

It vanished behind the trees.

Callum stood a long time in the gray half-dark, watching the bend, and then he turned back toward the fire, toward the two sleepers, and began—quietly, without a sound—to wake them.
