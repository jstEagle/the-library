## 11. Harker Closes

The rain came down the way it always did in the interior—not falling so much as settling, a gray permanence that soaked through canvas and wool and eventually through the part of a man that believed himself dry. Harker stood under the eave of the trading post at Two Rivers Junction and watched the river climb its banks, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees south like matchsticks.

Three days behind them. Maybe four.

He knew this because he was good at his work, and because his work had never once let him sleep the way other men slept. He counted things. Canoe marks on the mud where a freighter had been dragged to water. The particular way a pilot's hands left grease on a fuel drum. The half-smoked cigarette a drifter named Callum had ground into the boards of the dock at Kestrel Landing, a brand Harker recognized from a bar in Fort Alder three years ago, when Callum had been calling himself something else.

He counted, and the counting told him: two days' hard poling upriver, no more. The girl was stronger than the pilot gave her credit for. The pilot was more careful than the drifter was. And the drifter was the loose thread in the whole weave, which was why Harker had let him live at the checkpoint when a quieter man would have put him in the river.

He turned the cigarette case over in his palm. Inside, where a man might keep a photograph, Harker kept a folded slip of paper gone soft as cloth from handling. He did not take it out. He knew it by heart. He had known it by heart for nineteen years, and he had read it perhaps four hundred times, and every reading changed nothing.

*Burn it all. No survey, no camp, no names. You were never there.*

The signature was a company stamp. The order had come down through three intermediaries, which was how such orders traveled, each hand washing the last. Harker had been thirty-one years old and hungry and certain that the men above him knew things he did not, that obedience was a kind of competence. He had taken six men upriver in the dry season and burned a camp that did not officially exist, and when the burning was done there had been a ledger in the surveyor's safe that he was ordered to leave, because fire was for the buildings and the graves were for the dead and paper was a problem for men with better salaries.

He had left the paper. Nineteen years he had told himself that leaving the paper was the one clean thing he'd done, the thread of mercy in the whole ugly weave. It had taken him until last spring, when a dying prospector started asking questions in the wrong bars, to understand that the paper was not his mercy. It was his sentence. As long as it existed, he existed inside its margins—*you were never there*—and the company had spent nineteen years making sure that wherever Harker went, the sentence went with him. They paid him well. They promoted him slowly. They moved him to postings where there was nothing to do but watch, and they never let him forget that the watching was mutual.

Now the map was loose, and the map led to the vault, and the vault held the paper with the stamp on it, and somewhere in that paper—Harker had never seen the final version, only felt its weight in the room when the order was read aloud—was a line that named him. He was sure of it the way a man is sure of a scar. The company did not issue orders like that one without a signature to hang them on. They had hung theirs on him. If the vault ever opened in daylight, the record would not read *the company erased the camp.* It would read *Harker erased the camp.* One name, at the bottom, in his handwriting, on the receipt he'd been made to sign for the kerosene.

He had come upriver to burn a map. He told himself that was all.

* * *

The trading post at Two Rivers Junction was the last roofed building before the gorge, and Harker had reached it an hour ahead of the storm's worst, which meant he'd reached it an hour ahead of nothing, because the storm did not care about roofs. He sat in the post's back room with the factor, a Creole man named Deslauriers who had sold goods on this river for forty years and feared nothing except the tax man, and who looked at Harker now with the flat professional courtesy of a man calculating exactly how much trouble a customer was worth.

"You said three travelers," Deslauriers said. "Freighter canoe. Leaking, you said."

"Green hull, patched at the bow. The pilot's maybe forty, gray coming in at the temples, keeps his gear tied like a soldier. The girl's younger. Dark hair, works a dock—she'd have hands like a rigger's. And a third man, charming, talks too much, buys rounds he can't pay for." Harker laid three bills on the table, more than the information was worth, less than Deslauriers would pretend it was. "When did they pass?"

"Two nights back. Bought resin, a bail bucket, and all my coffee." The factor's mouth twitched. "The girl counted my change for me. The drifter tried to charm my wife, and my wife charmed him back out of twenty dollars, so we'll call it even. They were on the water before dawn."

"Alone?"

Deslauriers looked at him a long moment. On this river, *alone* was a question with a price attached, and both men knew it. "A launch went by upstream yesterday," the factor said finally. "Company hull, gray, running quiet. Four men. They didn't stop." He gathered the bills. "They didn't stop," he said again, and the repetition was the whole of his advice: whatever you are, and whatever they are, keep it off my riverbank.

Harker nodded and went out into the rain.

Company hull, four men, running quiet. So Voss—the name the pilot was using this year, though Harker had a file three inches thick that said the man had used nine—was not only ahead of him. He was ahead of the team too. Harker had asked for this job alone, which the company had granted with the particular generosity of men who were pleased to have a deniable instrument doing deniable work. If the map burned quietly, Harker got his quiet. If it went badly, the four men in the gray launch were the story, and Harker was a consultant who had never been issued a weapon.

He had spent nineteen years being never there. He was beginning to understand how the dead in the unmarked graves had felt about it.

* * *

He walked the bank below the post that evening, in the last gray light, and found what the rain had not yet erased: a campsite under a leaning cedar, three bedrolls, the cold ring of a driftwood fire. He crouched over it the way other men knelt in church.

The pilot had banked the fire properly—sod over the coals, no smoke, no waste of fuel. The girl had gutted fish and buried the offal deep, downwind. The drifter had left a boot print angled toward the water, toward the launch route, and Harker stood over that print for a long time.

He had been watching Callum Reyes—though that was only one of the names—for eleven days now, since the wire from Kestrel Landing. The file was thin but poisonous. Juvenile detention in the coast districts at thirteen, the age fitting, almost exactly, the age a child at the hidden camp would have been in the year of the burning. Harker had pulled the camp's labor rolls from a box he was not authorized to open, and there was a boy listed, no surname, entered only as *C—, coast-born, age 12,* assigned to the survey crew's kitchen because he was too small for the cut lines.

Too small for the cut lines. Harker had read that line and then walked out of the archive and been sick in a stairwell, quietly, like a man with a hangover, and gone back to his desk.

If the drifter was that boy—and Harker was not certain, because the drifter lied the way other men breathed, and a survivor of that camp had every reason to lie—if he was that boy, then he was not a thread in the weave. He was a witness. And witnesses were the one thing the company's whole architecture was built to prevent: no survey, no camp, no names. A living witness with a map and a grudge was a catastrophe wearing a charming smile.

And yet Harker had let him walk out of the checkpoint. He turned the decision over now, on the wet bank, the way he turned the paper over in his palm. He told himself it was tactics: let the drifter lead him to the map, to the vault, to the pilot's route. Let the loose threads gather into a rope and then cut the rope once. It was even true. But underneath the tactics, in the place where a man keeps the things he cannot say in reports, there was another reason, and it was this: when Callum had looked at Harker across the checkpoint table, there had been a moment—not fear, not performance, something older and flatter—when the drifter had looked at him the way you look at a man you have seen before, somewhere you were never officially present, doing something that never happened.

Harker did not remember the boy. There had been a hundred faces at the camp, and he had been thirty-one and following orders and careful not to memorize faces, because faces were the kind of paper that burned slowest. But the boy remembered him. That was the arithmetic he could not escape. Somewhere in the drifter's head was a kitchen doorway, a gray uniform, a face, and Harker had walked out of that doorway and into nineteen years of never being there, and the boy had walked out of it and into a lifetime of running, and both of them had been carrying the same camp on their backs the whole time.

He wanted the map destroyed before it named him. That was the mission, and it was true, and it was also the smallest true thing in him. The larger true thing was this: he wanted the vault opened. He wanted the ledgers read aloud in a courtroom with the windows open. He wanted the graves at the escarpment to stop being unmarked, wanted the boy in the kitchen rolls to get a surname in the record, wanted the sentence—*you were never there*—torn up by the same hand that had written it, even if the tearing took him down with it.

He was not a good man. He had stopped applying for that title around the time the smoke from the burning camp reached the river and he had stood in the bow of the launch and told himself the wind would take it out over the water. But he was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep, and tired men, given a choice between carrying a thing nineteen more years and setting it down even if it crushes them, will sometimes set it down.

He just needed to be the one holding it when it came time to choose.

* * *

The gray launch came back downstream at dusk, running quiet, and Harker watched it from the tree line with field glasses and a feeling in his gut like a stone dropping through cold water. Four men. He counted them going out; he counted them coming back. Four and four. But the launch was riding two inches lower on the return, and there was a tarp lashed amidships that had not been lashed before, and one of the men at the stern was doing something with a long-handled tool that Harker's eye understood before his mind agreed to: a gaff. A boat hook.

They had stopped somewhere upriver after all. Deslauriers had said they didn't stop, and they hadn't stopped at his post, and Harker had been fool enough to take the factor's repetition at face value.

He glassed the launch until it passed out of sight below the bend, then stood a long time in the rain, doing the arithmetic he was best at. The travelers were two nights ahead, poling a leaking canoe into the gorge. The team was behind them with a boat hook and a tarp and orders that Harker could guess to the word: *no survey, no camp, no names.* The map, the pilot, the girl, the witness—all of it was supposed to go into the river and stay there, and the river was high with monsoon, and the gorge below the escarpment was a meat grinder in high water, and four men with a gaff would not even need to be cruel. The river would do the work and sign nothing.

Harker had imagined he would catch them at the trading post, or at the last camp above the falls, and burn the map in a stove and be done, and go back to his posting and his never-being-there for whatever years the company had left him. That was the clean version. The clean version was dead now, lying in the bottom of a gray launch under a tarp with whatever the gaff had pulled from the water.

He went back to the post, bought Deslauriers' second-best canoe at a price that made the factor's eyebrows rise, and a paddle, and a tarp of his own, and a coil of rope, and a rifle he paid for in cash and would deny to his grave. He loaded it all in the dark. The rain had settled in for the night, patient as everything else in this country.

Two nights behind the canoe. One night behind the launch. The gorge between all of them, and the calving season opening at the glacier in a handful of days—a window the pilot would know about, because pilots always knew about ice, and a window the company's men would ignore, because company men did not believe the escarpment was real enough to die on.

Harker pushed off from the bank into the brown, climbing water and took the first stroke, and the river took the canoe the way it took everything, without malice and without mercy, and he let it.

He was not going upriver to burn a map anymore. He had stopped lying to himself somewhere around the boot print under the cedar, or maybe the stairwell, or maybe nineteen years ago in the bow of a launch with smoke on the water. He was going upriver because the record was going to open one way or another—by fire or by daylight—and he had decided, with the calm of a man who has already fallen and is only choosing where to land, that if his name was going to be in it either way, he would rather it be read aloud than burned.

The rain thickened. The gorge was two days ahead, and the dark came down on the water like a lid, and Harker counted his strokes into it, one by one by one, the way he counted everything, all the way to the falls.
