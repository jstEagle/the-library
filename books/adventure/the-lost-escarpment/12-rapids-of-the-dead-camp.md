## 12. Rapids of the Dead Camp

The river told them before the sky did. All morning it had been talking in a new voice—deeper, rougher, a growl under its usual rush—and by the time they rounded the bend below the burned trading post, Orsen could feel the change through the hull, a tremor in the aluminum like an animal deciding whether to run.

He'd been running water since he was old enough to be trusted with a tiller, and he knew that tremor. It was the sound of a river narrowing. Somewhere ahead, the valley walls were closing their hands.

"Listen," he said, and cut the engine.

Wren lifted her head from the bail bucket. Callum, who had spent two days being charmingly useless at the stern oar, sat up straighter. For once none of them spoke. The gorge ahead made its own argument: black walls of granite leaning together until the sky was a ragged gray seam, and through that seam came the river's roar, doubled and redoubled off stone, a sound less like water than like machinery—the whole interior grinding itself down to silt.

"That's the Gates," Callum said. His voice had gone flat, careful. "I told you about them. The survey crews called it the Gates because nobody who went in came back out the same."

"You said you ran it once," Wren said.

"I said I *saw* it once." He looked at Orsen, and there was something in his face Orsen hadn't seen before—not fear exactly, but the memory of fear, worn smooth like a stone carried too long in a pocket. "From the rim. With binoculars. We watched a company barge try it in high water. It went in at the top and came out at the bottom as kindling and crates. Eleven men." He swallowed. "They didn't even list the names. Just 'eleven lost with cargo.'"

Orsen looked upriver, then back the way they'd come. Behind them lay four days of monsoon-swollen current, two checkpoints, and Harker somewhere on the water with a radio and all the patience of a man being paid by the hour. Ahead lay the gorge. There was no portage trail marked on any map he owned; the cliffs on both sides rose sheer for what looked like half a mile, streaked green with runoff, dripping.

"There'll be a line," he said. "There's always a line. Water that big can't be all white. Find me the quiet part."

They drifted while he studied it, engine up, the canoe yawing lazily in the current, and the river did what rivers do—it showed them everything and nothing. Through the gray seam of the gorge he could see foam, long banners of it unrolling off submerged ledges, and between the banners, briefly, a tongue of darker water hugging the left wall where the current piled against rock and turned back on itself. A seam. Maybe forty feet wide. Maybe.

"Left side," he said. "Hard left, right from the drop-in. If we get pushed center, we're cargo."

Callum laughed, a short ugly sound. "You make it sound so reasonable."

"It's not reasonable. It's arithmetic." Orsen checked the lashings on the supply case—the vault evidence, wrapped in oilcloth, lashed amidships beneath the thwart where no wave could reach it without taking the boat apart first. Wren's pack next, then his own, then the fuel drum, nearly empty now, riding light. He'd already made himself say goodbye to the drum if it came to it. Weight was life and weight was death, and the trick was knowing which pound was which.

"Wren." She looked at him. Rain beaded her hair; she hadn't complained about the rain in three days, and he had come to understand that this was simply who she was—her father's daughter, someone who treated hardship as weather, which is to say as fact. "When I say bail, you bail like the boat's already sinking. Don't watch the rocks. The rocks will still be there after."

"And you?"

"I'll watch the rocks." He put his hand briefly on the case. "This goes over my dead body, not before it."

"Don't joke," she said quietly.

"I'm not joking. That's the problem."

* * *

The drop-in took them before he was fully ready, the way these things always do. One moment the canoe was sliding on brown glass; the next the valley walls had swallowed the sky and the river stood up underneath them like something waking.

"Hold!" he shouted, though hold was all any of them could do, and drove for the left wall.

The noise was total. It wasn't sound anymore; it was pressure, a fist of spray and thunder that made thought impossible and instinct everything. The cliff came at them out of the murk, black and streaming, and Orsen leaned into the oars with his whole back and felt the current take the boat sideways, spinning them, showing him in one sickening flash the whole gallery of the gorge—white water stacked in steps below them, foam boiling off ledges like smoke, and everywhere the seam, that dark thread along the wall, threading away into the roar.

"LEFT! LEFT! BAIL!"

Wren was bailing—he could hear the bucket even through the din, a frantic percussion—and Callum was on the stern oar, actually pulling, actually helping, and for one bright instant Orsen thought: *we might do this.*

Then the river showed them the ledge.

It wasn't on the surface. That was the obscenity of it—a shelf of rock lying just under the brown water, invisible until the current poured over it and the boat, committed to the seam, hit the tail of it with a crack he felt in his teeth. The bow kicked skyward. The world went white. And beneath his feet, under the thrash and the cold, Orsen heard the sound every boatman knows and dreads: not the crack of impact but the longer, tearing groan of aluminum giving up, a seam opening along the hull like a wound.

"We're holed!" Callum screamed. "We're—"

Water came in the way water does, indifferent and total. The canoe settled even as it ran, sitting lower, shipping more, the current now pushing a drowned thing rather than carrying a live one. Orsen's mind went cold and fast, the way it had learned to go in other lives, other years: *the case first, the case is the mission, the packs second, the boat is already dead.*

"Case stays lashed! Wren—packs! Get them on!"

She was already moving, hauling her pack onto her shoulders, cinching it wet, then reaching across him for his. The river chose that moment to throw them at the wall again. Rock scraped the hull with a shriek, spun them, and the seam of quiet water was gone—they were in the middle now, in the stacked white, going over the first of the drops backward.

The drop itself was short, maybe six feet, but six feet of that river was like falling off a building into a firehose. They went under whole. Orsen's world became brown violence and the rope-burn ache of his grip on the oar, and when they surfaced he was facing astern, Wren was coughing, Callum was gone—

No. Not gone. Callum was in the water.

He'd been torn off the stern oar, or he'd let go—Orsen would never know which, and suspected later that Callum himself didn't know—and now he was downstream of them, an arm flailing out of the foam, going fast toward the second ledge.

"Line!" Orsen roared, and Wren understood instantly, hauling the coiled throwline from under the thwart, and for three seconds—one, two, three—that neither of them ever forgot, the whole enterprise hung on a coil of polypropylene and a drowning man's willingness to catch it.

Callum caught it. Whether from courage or animal terror, he caught it, and Wren braced against the thwart and Orsen put the last of the boat's dying strength into holding them both, and the river, having made its point, let them go—all of them—spitting the canoe and its cargo out of the gorge's throat into a long, wide, gravel-bottomed run where the water suddenly remembered how to be merely fast instead of murderous.

They grounded on a shingle bar a quarter mile down, half swamped, shaking, alive.

* * *

The inventory, taken kneeling in the shallows with rain still needling the water around them, was brutal in its simplicity.

The canoe was finished. The tear ran two feet along the starboard strake, aluminum peeled back like the lid of a tin, and no tarp-and-resin patch in Orsen's kit was going to argue with a wound like that in moving water. Half their food was in the river, along with the fuel drum—which, mercifully empty, had simply vanished—and one of the two tents, and Callum's bedroll, and, most painfully, the spare bail bucket, which floated past them mockingly upright and out of reach.

What remained: the evidence case, soaked outside, dry inside, lashed exactly where he'd sworn it would stay. Wren's pack. His own pack, lighter by the tent and half the rations. The rifle, wrapped and sealed, miraculously dry. The throwline. A tarp. A pot. Two lighters, one of them working. The map—Wren's copy of the coordinates inked onto oilcloth in her own hand, because she had memorized them years ago and written them out the night Callum stole the original, and Orsen had understood then, watching her write, that the theft had cost Callum almost nothing and taught Orsen almost everything about the woman crouched beside him now, wringing out her sleeves with steady, furious hands.

"So," Callum said. He was sitting slightly apart, wrapped in the tarp, and there was river water and something else—shame, maybe—running off him. "That's the Gates."

"That's the Gates," Orsen agreed.

"I want to say—" Callum stopped. Started again. "In the water. You could have cut the line. Saved the supplies."

"The supplies float," Orsen said. "People don't."

It wasn't generosity. It was arithmetic again, the same cold ledger he'd opened in the gorge, and he watched Callum understand that, and watched something flicker behind the drifter's eyes—gratitude, or its counterfeit, hard to tell the difference with a man who'd been performing so long he'd lost the original script.

Wren said nothing at all. She had walked the length of the shingle bar and stood now at its upstream end, looking back at the gorge. Orsen joined her, and followed her gaze up.

The cliffs here fell back from the river, opening into a narrow bench of flat ground above the flood line—old flood line, higher than anything the monsoon had thrown at them—and it was the ground itself that made Wren go still. It was too level. Level in the way ground only gets when hands have made it so, terraced and drained, and along the base of the cliff above it ran a streak of color that had no business existing in a place like this: faded paint, orange and white, laid on the rock in long vertical bars and, above them, stenciled letters gone ghost-pale with thirty years of rain.

Orsen climbed to it. The letters resolved slowly, the way things do when you already know what they must say.

**SURVEY CAMP 9 — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL**

Below that, smaller, half obliterated: a stamp, circular, official-looking, the same stamp Elias Marsh had shown him on the map in a trapping cabin that smelled of blood and woodsmoke. The camp that never existed. The camp whose payroll had named men who were never reported missing.

"They worked them right up against the gorge," Wren said behind him. Her voice was very quiet. "Barges in, barges out. That's why they ran the rapids. That's why eleven men died and nobody wrote their names." She touched the stencil, the paint flaking under her finger like old skin. "Dad's notes said the camp was here. Right here. Below the escarpment, on the only flat ground for fifty miles." She turned and looked at the terrace, and Orsen watched her see it the way her father must have seen it in his mind for thirty years: the barracks footings, the rusted cable still strung between two rotting posts, the shape of a road that went nowhere because it had only ever needed to go down to the water.

And then she saw the other thing, and went whiter than the river-foam.

At the far end of the terrace, where the ground rose toward the treeline, there were mounds. Rows of them. Dozens. Not natural—nothing that regular is natural—each marked with a post, and some of the posts still bearing slats, and on one slat, legible through the gray rain, a number.

Orsen had flown over enough country to know graves when he saw them, even from a distance, even from the air. He had never stood among them.

"We can't stay here," he heard himself say, and hated the words even as he said them, because they were true. "Harker's behind us. This is the first landing above the gorge—if he ran it, and he will, this is where he stops. But we can't go forward either." He looked up. Above the camp the valley walls stepped upward through cloud, and somewhere beyond that cloud, unseen, waited the escarpment rim and the glacier route, and the calving window that would open in days and close behind them like a door. "No boat. No trail on this side. Which leaves—"

"The goat track," Wren said. "Dad drew it. Off the map proper, in the margin, in pencil. He said the surveyors kept a path up to a lookout on the rim." She was already digging the oilcloth from inside her jacket, unfolding it with hands that shook only a little. "Two days' climb, he wrote. Three in bad weather."

Orsen looked at the sky, which promised nothing but more of itself. He looked at their remaining food, which he could carry in one hand. He looked at the rows of mounds at the treeline, and at the case containing the names of everyone buried in them, and he found that the dread that had lived in his chest for twenty years—the low constant hum of a man who stayed invisible, who never slept twice in one place—had gone strangely quiet.

There was nowhere left to hide to. There was only up.

"We rest tonight," he said. "Off the terrace, up in the trees, cold camp, no fire. First light, we climb." He glanced at Callum, hunched under the tarp, watching the river as if expecting Harker's boats to come through it at any moment. "All of us. Nobody gets left on this bank."

Callum nodded slowly. Then, not looking at either of them, he said: "There's something I should tell you about the track. Something I remembered, seeing the camp." He paused, and the pause had a weight to it that made Wren stop folding the map. "The reason the surveyors kept a path to the rim. It wasn't for the view." He finally raised his eyes. "It was the route they used to bring the men up. The ones who tried to leave."

Rain ticked on the tarp. Downriver, faint under the water's voice, Orsen thought he could hear an engine—or thought he could, which amounted to the same thing.

"First light," he said again, and began counting what was left.
