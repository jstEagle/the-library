## 13. Betrayal

Wren woke to the sound of the river and the absence of a man.

She had learned, in the weeks since Kestrel Landing, to measure the world by small absences—the way her father's silence had once filled a cabin, the way the dock bell's missing clang had meant ice on the harbor. Now it was Callum's breathing she missed, that theatrical snore he performed from his bedroll near the bow of the overturned canoe they'd dragged beneath the church's collapsed gallery. He snored like a man who wanted witnesses. She'd teased him about it at the trading post, two nights ago, before Harker's shadow fell across their trail and trust became a currency they counted in whispers.

The rain had stopped sometime before dawn. That was wrong too. The monsoon didn't stop; it paused, gathering itself like a fist. In the hush, the flooded nave dripped its slow inventory—water finding its way through the ruined roof, plinking into the shallows that still covered half the floorboards, silvered now with the first gray light coming through the broken windows where saints had once been painted and had long since flaked away.

Orsen slept on his side near the fire pit, one arm curled under his head, his face in sleep younger than waking ever allowed him. She watched him for a moment the way she'd been watching him without admitting it—the competence of him even unconscious, the coiled readiness, the way his hand lay open near the knife he never let farther than an arm's reach. A man built like a locked door. And yet he'd opened it for her father's map, for her, against every instinct he claimed to have.

She sat up quietly, meaning to check the hull patch, and saw instead that Callum's bedroll was empty.

Not disturbed-empty. *Gone*-empty. Rolled tight, the wool blanket squared with the neatness of a man who'd served somewhere or fled somewhere and never lost the habit of leaving no sign. His pack was gone too. And the tin box—

Her stomach dropped before her mind caught up.

The tin box where they kept the map.

* * *

She didn't call out. That was the first thing—the instinct older than thought, inherited from a father who'd taught her, without ever meaning to teach it, that the world rewarded quiet. She crossed the flooded floor on the tops of the pews, boards slick under her bare feet, and reached the place where they'd cached their supplies behind the altar rail: the food sack, the resin can, Orsen's tool roll, the ammunition, and the radio.

The radio was there. The battery was not.

They had carried three batteries out of Kestrel Landing. One had drowned in the gorge when the canoe tore open, its casing full of brown water, dead as a stone. The second had died slowly over six nights of static and half-heard weather bulletins, its voice thinning until Orsen shut it off to save what remained. The third—the last—was supposed to be their voice to the outside world once they reached the escarpment, once they had something worth saying. Orsen had rationed it like insulin, like the medicine he ferried to strips no doctor visited. *You don't spend your last word until you know what you want to say.*

Callum had spent it for them. Or taken it to spend elsewhere.

And the map. Her father's map. Thirty years of Elias Marsh's life folded into oilcloth and ink, the coordinates pressed so hard in places they'd nearly torn the paper, the margin notes in his cramped hand—*water here in dry season, don't trust the east ridge, they watched this valley*—all of it gone.

Wren knelt there in the rising light and felt the grief arrive sideways, the way it always did now: not as a wave but as a specific, physical knowledge. Her father had died with this map under his hands. He'd pressed it into a stranger's grip because he trusted the stranger's promise more than he trusted his own luck. And now a charming drifter with a scarred knuckle and a story about being the massacre's youngest survivor had carried it off into the dark like a man taking out the trash.

She should have known. Part of her *had* known. She'd seen him slip the note to Harker at the checkpoint and said nothing—or said too little—and Orsen had made his choice to keep Callum close anyway, because the river rapids didn't forgive strangers and Callum knew them like a hymn. Necessity had sealed the alliance. Necessity had also, apparently, unsealed it.

There was a note. Of course there was a note. Callum was nothing if not a man who liked the last word to be eloquent. It was weighted under the resin can with a flat stone, written on the back of a page torn from her own notebook, which somehow made it worse—a theft inside a theft.

*I'm sorry. Truly. But sorry doesn't get a man out of this country alive, and neither does loyalty to people who are already dead men walking. I have the map and the battery. Harker will trade passage for both—I've seen what he pays. By the time you read this I'll be downriver. Don't follow. The gorge in flood will kill you, and I'd rather remember you living.*

*C.*

Wren read it twice. Then a third time, slower, because the third read was where the anger burned off and something colder and more useful settled underneath.

*Don't follow.* As if following were the point. As if the map were the destination.

She looked at the note again—at the careful handwriting, the reasonable tone, the performance of regret—and understood that Callum had made the same mistake everyone made about her father's map. They thought it was paper. They thought it was information, transferable, ownable, sellable. Harker wanted to burn it because he believed burning paper would burn the truth. Callum wanted to trade it because he believed whoever held the paper held the escarpment.

But Wren Marsh had grown up drawing those coordinates.

* * *

It started before memory, almost. Her father home between prospecting runs, half wild with the interior still on him, sitting at the kitchen table in Kestrel Landing while she did her schoolwork, and instead of helping her with arithmetic he'd draw. Mountains, rivers, a plateau shaped like a broken tooth. He'd sketch it from memory on butcher paper, on the backs of invoices, in the condensation on the window when the mood took him, and little Wren would copy him because copying him was the only way to keep him in the room. The escarpment's rim. The glacier tongue hanging off its eastern shoulder like a tongue of glass. The goat track switchbacking up the southern face. The river gorge below, with its two great bends and the deadfall island in the second bend where the current split.

He'd quiz her sometimes, playful, not knowing he was building something. *Where does the track meet the rim, bird? What's due south of the calving field?* And she'd answer, and he'd laugh his rare laugh, and neither of them knew he was engraving the Lost Escarpment into a child's mind deeper than any paper could hold.

Thirty years he'd spent trying to prove what happened up there. Thirty years of maps drawn and redrawn, of surveys stamped by a camp that officially never existed, of a company's lawyers and enforcers erasing everything he found. The paper was just the latest copy. The original had been in her head since she was seven years old.

So she sat down on the altar rail in the dripping gray light of the abandoned church, took the notebook page Callum had left behind—he'd torn only one, careless—and began to draw.

She drew the plateau first, its outline coming off her pencil the way her own signature came, without deliberation. Then the glacier, the track, the gorge, the bends, the survey paint streaks on the cliffs below the rim that Orsen had described after they washed ashore. She marked the vault's position as her father's margin notes placed it: north wall of the barracks foundation, forty paces from the graves. She wrote the coordinates themselves along the border, the ones she'd memorized at eleven when she'd found the master sheet in her father's chest and copied it out of love and never told him, because some inheritances you keep secret even from the people who leave them to you.

When Orsen woke, twenty minutes later, she had finished.

* * *

He came awake the way he always did—all at once, no surfacing, one breath asleep and the next fully armed. His eyes found her, then the empty bedroll, then the note in her hand, and she watched the whole betrayal land on him in sequence: the map, the battery, the lie of the man's snoring, the note to Harker at the checkpoint suddenly recontextualized into a plan rather than a lapse.

"He's gone," Orsen said. Not a question. His voice was gravel and morning.

"Downriver, he says." She handed him the note. "Trading the map to Harker for safe passage."

Orsen read it twice, jaw working. "I should have—" He stopped. Whatever self-indictment he'd been building, he abandoned it halfway, which was itself new; the Orsen Blackwood she'd met on the dock in Kestrel Landing would have carried the whole weight alone and said nothing. "We're blind now. No radio, no map, and Harker knows exactly where we are."

"No," Wren said. "Harker knows where we *were*. And Callum knows where we are, which is worse, except—" She held out the notebook page. "Except Callum took a copy of a copy."

Orsen took the page. She watched him study it, watched the pilot in him take over—the part that read terrain the way other men read faces. His eyes moved across her lines, checking them against everything they'd seen since the gorge: the bend where the canoe died, the cliffs with the old survey paint, the shape of the country visible through the church's broken windows. Checking. Finding it true.

"You drew this," he said. "Just now."

"I've been drawing it my whole life. My father made sure of it, whether he meant to or not." She heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt. "The map was never the thing, Orsen. It was thirty years of my father trying to write down what happened to him. But what happened is still up there. The vault is still up there. The graves are still up there. Callum stole paper."

"And the battery?"

"That we needed." She let herself feel that loss fully for one breath—the radio that could have called the outside world from the rim, named the dead, brought the law in before the company could bury it twice. Then she set it down. "But a battery buys him nothing either, unless he finds a set. And Harker isn't going to reward him. Harker is going to use him until he's used up. You saw the checkpoint. Men like Harker don't buy traitors. They rent them."

Orsen was quiet a moment, looking at her drawing, and then at her, and something shifted in his face—not softness exactly, but recognition. The look of a man revising an estimate upward.

"Your father," he said finally, "spent thirty years trying to prove a massacre, and the whole time the proof was raising you."

"He wasn't proving it for the world." Wren folded the new map carefully and tucked it inside her shirt, against her skin, where the oilcloth used to sit. "He was proving it so the names would exist somewhere outside his own head. So the dead would be *people* again instead of a rumor the company could deny." She stood. "Which means we don't need Callum, and we don't need the radio, and we don't need anyone's permission. We need legs and four days of clear weather, because the calving window opens soon and closes faster."

"The monsoon's breaking," Orsen said, glancing at the light. "That's why the rain stopped. High pressure building behind the front. Two days of it, maybe three, then the next system." He was already moving, kicking dirt over the fire pit's remains, rolling his bedroll with the same economy Callum had faked. "Goat track's a day's climb if the scree hasn't washed out. We travel light. Everything we can't carry, we bury."

"The boat?"

"Leave it. It's a confession to anyone who finds it, and a coffin to anyone who tries." He paused at the altar rail, looking back at the flooded nave, the drowned saints, the water ticking its slow clock into the shallows. "He left us a note saying don't follow."

"He left *you* a note," Wren said. "He's never once guessed what I am."

* * *

They buried what they couldn't carry beneath the floorboards of the vestry, wrapped in tarp: the spare line, the extra boots, the food they couldn't haul, Orsen's second knife. Wren marked the spot with a chip of the altar rail, thinking absurdly of her father, of all the things buried in this country that no one had bothered to mark. The church got its own small grave goods. Somewhere a congregation would come back, someday, and find resin cans and rifle cartridges under the floor, and make of it whatever story they needed.

By midmorning the clouds were tearing apart overhead, showing hard blue between them, and the air had that rinsed, ringing quality that comes after long rain. The river beside them ran high and brown and furious, carrying branches, carrying the whole drowned season seaward. Somewhere down that river, Wren thought, Callum was running the same water toward Harker, holding a stolen map like a ticket, not knowing the ticket had expired the moment he lifted it.

She felt no triumph about that. Only a tired, clear pity, and underneath it a harder thing: the understanding that Callum had done them a strange favor. He'd stripped the journey down to what it actually was. No allies to manage, no charm to distrust, no third bedroll to listen to. Just her, and Orsen, and the mountain, and the truth waiting at the top of it like a debt thirty years past due.

They climbed out of the river valley through alder scrub still dripping, following the game trail Orsen had scouted the evening before, and by early afternoon the trees opened and she saw it for the first time with her own eyes instead of her father's pencil: the escarpment, rising out of the interior haze, its southern face a wall of gray rock seamed with white, the goat track visible even at this distance as a faint thread stitched up the scree.

Her breath went out of her. All those drawings, all those quizzes at the kitchen table, and none of it—none of it—had prepared her for the sheer *fact* of the place, the way it stood over the landscape like a judge.

"There," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and she didn't care.

Orsen stood beside her, squinting up at the thread of the track, doing pilot's arithmetic—elevation, daylight, weather window. "Two days' climb if the scree holds. Three if it doesn't. Calving field opens when the melt reaches the ice tongue—we'll hear it before we see it." He looked at her. "Your father's notes say the vault is intact?"

"My father's notes say the company never found it. He hid it himself, the last time he came down. Before his lungs went." She touched the folded page against her sternum. "He said the lock was rusted shut and he couldn't force it without tools, and he was afraid to try, because a forced lock tells a story too."

"So we bring tools."

"We brought tools. You brought tools." She almost smiled. "You patch hulls with tarp and resin. I figured a man like that travels with a pry bar."

Orsen looked at her for a long moment, and then he laughed—a short, real sound, the first she'd heard from him, startled out of him like a bird from cover. "A man like that," he said, "travels with a pry bar. Fine. Then we climb."

They turned toward the mountain. Behind them, far downriver, thunder rolled—but it was ordinary thunder, honest weather, not engines, not yet. Wren put her hand flat against the map over her heart and started up the track, one switchback at a time, toward the place where the dead had waited thirty years for someone to come and say their names.

She did not see, far below and back along the river, the single figure in a gray rain shell standing at the tree line, watching them climb—patient, methodical, making notes in a small book. Harker had never needed Callum's map at all. He'd only needed to know which direction they'd run.

And now he knew.
