## 14. The Ascent

The river gave them up without ceremony.

One moment the gorge was roaring around them, brown water stacked in standing waves, and the next they were crawling out of a back-eddy onto a shelf of wet gravel, dragging what remained of their gear behind them like survivors of something larger than a shipwreck. The canoe was gone. It had hung on a submerged ledge for one long, screaming minute—Orsen chest-deep in the current with his fingers hooked under the gunwale, Wren braced against him, both of them shouting words that dissolved before they left their mouths—and then the hull had simply folded, peeled open along the tear like a fruit splitting, and the river had taken it. Taken the spare fuel, the tarp, half the food, the second bail bucket. Taken the last easy version of this journey.

What they had left sat in two heaps on the gravel: the evidence case wrapped in oilcloth and lashed to Orsen's pack frame, Wren's father's map sealed inside a tin against her chest, three days of food if they were careful and two if they were honest, a coil of rope, a hatchet, a battered camp stove with a punctured fuel line, and Harker's rifle, which Orsen had taken off the man at the eddy and now carried slung across his own back, because he did not trust himself to make that decision twice.

Harker stood apart from them, hands empty, watching the water where the boat had been. He looked smaller without it, Orsen thought. Not diminished exactly—compressed. Like a man who had spent years being large for other people and had finally set the size down.

"The track starts above the slide," Harker said. His voice was flat, professional, stripped of everything except information. "Goat trail. Switchbacks through the scree, then a shelf traverse under the overhangs. It puts you on the rim maybe six hours up, eight if the rock is wet."

"Is the rock wet?"

Harker looked at him. For the first time since the trading post, since the checkpoint, since the long pursuit upriver, something moved behind the enforcer's eyes that wasn't calculation. It might have been exhaustion. It might have been the beginning of grief.

"It's monsoon," he said. "Everything is wet."

* * *

They climbed because there was nothing else to do. That was the whole of it, Orsen thought as they gained the first switchback—the entire philosophy of the next day compressed into a single sentence. You climbed because the alternative was the river, and the river had already voted.

The track was worse than Harker had described, which Orsen supposed was the honest way of saying Harker hadn't described it at all. It was less a trail than an argument the mountain had once had with goats and lost narrowly. It traversed the face of the gorge in long diagonal sweeps, sometimes wide enough for two men abreast, sometimes narrowing to a boot-width of crumbling shale with the river four hundred feet below, visible only as a white smear of noise between the walls. Rain came and went in bands—they could watch it move up the valley, a gray curtain sweeping the cliffs like a hand wiping a window—and when it passed, the sun came through hot and sudden, steaming the rock, filling the air with the smell of wet stone and crushed sage and, somewhere below, the green rot of the flooded forest.

Orsen counted his breathing. In for four steps, hold for two, out for six. It was an old discipline from another life, from a time when he'd carried loads up ridgelines for men whose names he'd never learned, and it steadied him the way prayer steadies some people—not because it meant anything, but because it was repeatable. Because it filled the mind so completely there was no room left for the other counting, the one that ran underneath everything now: *how many days until the calving window opens, how many days until it closes, how many hours of light, how much food, how far.*

Wren climbed ahead of him. She had taken the lead within the first hour and never given it back, and Orsen understood why without asking. Her father had drawn this route—or routes like it—in the margins of thirty years of maps, and though Elias Marsh had never walked this particular track, his daughter had grown up tracing lines like it across kitchen tables, learning the grammar of contour intervals the way other children learned nursery rhymes. She read the mountain the way Orsen read weather: not with her eyes but with some older instrument. When the trail split around a fallen slab she chose the high side without hesitating. When the scree field opened ahead of them, gray and treacherous as a frozen lake, she picked the line of darker stone that held together underfoot.

She also, Orsen noticed, said almost nothing. And he recognized that silence too. It was the silence of someone carrying a weight that talking would only shift into a position that hurt more.

At midday they stopped beneath an overhang where the rock folded back like a wave about to break, and ate cold rice and the last of the smoked fish, and Orsen took stock of Wren the way he'd take stock of an engine—listening for what was wrong by what sounded different.

Her hands were shaking. Not badly. Just enough that she kept them busy, wrapping the fish remains in cloth, coiling the rope, doing small necessary things so the tremor would have somewhere to go.

"You should sleep," he said. "An hour. I'll take first watch."

"I'm fine."

"I know you're fine. Sleep anyway."

She looked at him then, and for a moment he saw how far down the tiredness went—past the muscles, past the fear, into some place where her father's voice still lived and had recently started saying final things. Then she nodded, once, and lay down on the bare rock with her arm curled around the map tin like a child with a stuffed animal, and was asleep in under a minute. Orsen watched her breathe and thought about promises. About how cheaply they were made and what they cost to keep, and how Elias Marsh, dying in a cabin that smelled of kerosene and blood, had known exactly which stranger to hand his daughter to—not because Orsen was good, but because Orsen was invisible, and invisible men deliver things.

Except he wasn't invisible anymore. That was the arithmetic of the last two weeks, and it kept refusing to balance. Every mile upriver had made him more legible. Harker knew his face. Callum knew his name—and Callum was out there somewhere with the map's location sold and a head start toward whatever deal he thought would save him. By the time they reached the escarpment, Orsen Blackwood would be a fact in other people's files, and facts could be acted upon.

He found, examining the feeling honestly, that it didn't frighten him the way it should have. That was the strangest part. Somewhere between the flooded church and the wrecked canoe, the dread had changed its shape. It had stopped being *they will find me* and become *I will be found*, and those were different animals entirely.

Below them, far down the valley, he heard it: a sound like distant artillery, rolling and cracking, echoing off the canyon walls. He turned. Upvalley, half-hidden in cloud, the escarpment's shoulder showed white—a glacier tongue spilling down from the plateau, and even at this distance he could see the seracs leaning, the blue-green ice stained gray with rockfall, the whole mass of it groaning its way toward the valley floor.

Calving season. The mountain was shedding its winter. In a few days the meltwater channels would cut through the terminal moraine and open a corridor across the ice—the brief, insane window when a crossing was possible. And then, just as briefly, it would close again, and anything on the wrong side of it would stay there until next year.

Harker crouched at the edge of the overhang, watching the glacier too. "Three days," he said quietly. "Maybe four. After that it's a year."

"I know."

"Do you." Harker didn't look at him. "Because I've watched men misjudge that ice my whole career. It doesn't care what you're carrying or why. It will open under you the same as anyone."

"That's almost comforting," Orsen said. "Impartiality is rare out here."

Something that was almost a laugh moved through Harker's shoulders and died. He went back to watching the ice.

* * *

The rain returned in the afternoon and stayed.

It came down the valley in earnest now, the true monsoon, not the passing bands of the morning but a sustained, deliberate drowning, the kind of rain that seemed less like weather than like a decision. The goat track became a waterfall running downhill in thin sheets, and every handhold was either slick or loose, usually both. They roped up—Orsen insisted, and Harker tied in without argument, which told Orsen something about how the man assessed risk versus pride—and moved at half speed, testing each hold, calling warnings up and down the line in short clipped words: *rock, loose, step left, freeze.*

Twice the mountain tried to kill them.

The first time was small: a slab the size of a door let go under Wren's foot and went cartwheeling down the face, and they flattened against the wall while it boomed away into the gorge, and after it settled they breathed and climbed on. The second time was not small. They were traversing beneath a band of overhangs, pressed close to the rock, when the whole slope above them began to move—a slow, grinding shudder, then a rush of mud and stone pouring down the channel they'd have been walking through ten minutes earlier. It roared past fifty feet above their heads, close enough to spit grit into their faces, and kept going, taking a section of the track with it. When the sound finally faded, the trail ahead was simply gone, erased into a raw scar of churned earth.

They found a way around it by climbing, hands and boots, forty feet up the face and across, Wren leading with the rope in her teeth at one point because she needed both hands and refused to wait for Orsen to anchor it. When they all stood on solid ground again, hearts hammering, Harker looked at her with an expression Orsen had never seen on him—something adjacent to respect, arrived at reluctantly, like a debt paid under protest.

"She climbs like her father," Harker said. It was the first time he'd mentioned Elias Marsh aloud, and the name landed strangely among the three of them, in the rain, on the scarred slope. "Surveyor's instinct. Read the ground before it reads you."

"You knew him," Wren said. Her voice was very level. "You knew what he was looking for. All these years."

Harker was quiet for a long moment. Water ran off the brim of his hat in a steady thread.

"I knew what he found," he said finally. "That was the problem."

He didn't explain, and she didn't push, and they climbed on—but Orsen marked it. Marked the way Harker's jaw worked after he said it, the way his eyes went up-slope and stayed there, fixed on the white shoulder of the glacier as if the answer to something were written in the ice. A man carrying his own ledger. Orsen had spent enough years keeping one to recognize the posture.

* * *

Night caught them on a ledge barely wider than a bedroll, wedged beneath a lean of rock that kept off the worst of the rain. They couldn't risk a fire—the smoke would show, and Callum knew this country, and whatever deal he'd struck, it would involve telling someone where they were headed. So they ate cold food and shared water from the single bottle that hadn't been lost to the river, and Orsen sat with his back to the stone and listened to the dark.

The rain was easing. He could feel it in the way the sound changed—from the roar of water on rock to the softer patter of runoff finding its channels, the mountain drinking instead of drowning. Above the cloud deck, if the clouds broke, there would be stars. There were no clouds breaking. But the pressure had shifted; he felt it in his ears, in the old ache in his knee that predicted weather better than any forecast. The monsoon was spending itself. Two days, maybe three, and then the high country would clear, and the calving would begin in earnest, and the window would stand open for a handful of hours like a door swinging in a wind.

"We'll make the rim tomorrow," Wren said. She wasn't asleep after all. She sat beside him, knees drawn up, the map tin between her boots. "Midday, if the traverse holds."

"If the traverse holds," Orsen agreed.

"And then we're on top." She said it flatly, testing the words. "Thirty years my father drew that place. Coordinates in every margin of every notebook I ever found. I used to think he was mapping gold." She was quiet a moment. "I used to hope he was mapping gold. Gold would have explained why he never came home."

Orsen said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a lie or a cruelty.

"He mapped people," she went on, barely above the rain-noise. "He mapped graves. And I spent my whole life angry at him for it." Her hand found the tin and rested there. "Tomorrow I find out if anger was the wrong inheritance."

"It wasn't wrong," Orsen said. "It kept you alive and pointed here. That's more than most inheritances do."

She looked at him sideways, and something passed between them that neither of them named—the recognition of two people who had each built a life out of staying unreadable, sitting together on a ledge in the rain, thoroughly read.

Across the ledge, Harker slept the way soldiers sleep: instantly, completely, with the tension of a man who knows rest is rationed. Even asleep, his face wasn't peaceful. Orsen wondered what the man dreamed about—orders signed, names unspoken, a camp that officially never existed and had therefore never stopped existing anywhere that mattered. Tomorrow, Orsen thought, the man would walk up onto that plateau and see the barracks and the vault and the rows of unmarked mounds, and he would have to stand in front of the physical evidence of his own obedience. Whatever deal Orsen intended to offer him on the ice—*be named, or help carry the truth out*—would be easier to make after that. Grief made men negotiable. Orsen knew that too well to say it out loud.

He took the middle watch, then woke Wren for hers, then lay down and did not sleep. Instead he lay listening to the mountain settle, and to the far-off thunder of the glacier calving in the dark—great booming exhalations, the ice shedding tons of itself into the mist, clearing its throat for what was coming.

* * *

Dawn came gray and rinsed-clean, the first morning in nine days without rain.

It was as if someone had wiped the world. The air was scrubbed and cold and so clear that distances collapsed—the escarpment rim, which yesterday had been a rumor above the cloud, now stood out in hard relief a thousand feet overhead, its basalt crown edged with snow, the glacier tongue descending from it in a frozen cascade of blue and shattered white. Waterfalls hung everywhere off the cliff faces, silver threads let down overnight, and the valley below steamed gently in the early sun like something newly born.

They climbed the last stretch in silence, because the beauty of it demanded silence, and because none of them trusted the day enough yet to speak into it.

The traverse under the overhangs held. The scree fields, drained by the night's cold, held. And at a little past eleven, Wren Marsh pulled herself over the last lip of broken rock, reached back, and hauled Orsen up onto the rim of the Lost Escarpment.

The plateau opened before them like a held breath finally released—a vast tableland of sedge and stone, glinting with a hundred shallow meltwater pools, ringed by peaks still white with new snow. The air up here was thin and astonishingly sweet after the drowned valley, smelling of sun-warmed rock and lichen and distance itself.

And there, perhaps two miles out across the plain, sagging into the sedge under thirty years of weather: the roofs of the camp.

Collapsed barracks in a row, black timber gone silver. A rusted structure that might be the survey vault. And beyond them, at the edge of sight, a stretch of ground where the grass grew wrong—too evenly, in too straight a line.

Wren stood absolutely still, the wind pulling at her hair, staring at it. Orsen watched her face do several things at once and settle on none of them.

Behind them, down the goat track they'd climbed, a sound drifted up on the clean morning air—faint but unmistakable, carried impossibly far in the post-storm stillness.

A rifle shot. Then another.

Someone was coming up the mountain behind them.
