## 15. The Escarpment

The last hundred feet of the goat track were not a path so much as a scar, a seam of broken shale stitched up the escarpment's face, and Wren climbed it with her father's map folded against her heart like a second rib. Below her the monsoon was dying in long gray rags of cloud, and above her the rim stood black against a sky rinsed clean by three days of rain. Her hands had gone past pain into something numb and mechanical. She did not let herself think about the boat, or Callum, or the case of supplies scattered downriver somewhere in the gorge's white throat. She thought only of the next hold, and the next, because that was how you survived a climb—by refusing to look at anything but what your hands could touch.

Orsen climbed below her, close enough that she could hear his breathing, ragged but steady, a man who had made an economy of his own exhaustion. Neither of them had spoken in hours. There was nothing left to say that the mountain hadn't already said.

Then her hand found grass instead of stone, and she pulled herself over the rim, and the plateau opened before her like a held breath finally released.

She had expected—she didn't know what she had expected. Gold, maybe, glittering in some quartz seam, the treasure her father had chased for thirty years while it ate him alive from the inside out. Or nothing at all: wind-scoured rock and lichen, the anticlimax of all wild goose chases, proof that Elias Marsh had been exactly what the company men said he was, a crazy old prospector who'd spent his life chasing ghosts.

What she saw instead stopped her so completely that Orsen nearly climbed into her before she realized she'd quit moving.

Barracks.

Three rows of them, or what remained of three rows, timber frames collapsed into themselves like ribs around emptied lungs, roofs caved in, doorways gaping at the sky. Moss had taken the wood and turned it green and soft, decades of it, decades of surrender to the weather. Beyond the barracks stood a longer building with its roof half intact, and beyond that, at the plateau's far edge where the ground fell away toward the glacier, a fence.

A fence with no gate.

And inside the fence, rows.

Wren walked toward them without deciding to walk. The grass here grew tall and rank, unmown for thirty years, and it whispered against her legs as she passed through the gap where a gate had once hung—or perhaps there had never been a gate at all, perhaps no one had ever intended to come back this way. The rows resolved as she got closer, and her breath went thin and high in her chest.

Graves. Dozens of them. Simple mounds long since settled into the earth, each marked with a wooden cross gone silver-gray with weather, most of them leaning, some fallen entirely, swallowed by the grass. No names. No dates. Not one mark on any of them except the crosses themselves, identical, anonymous, a congregation of the unnamed planted in the world's most remote churchyard.

She counted without wanting to. Forty-one. Forty-one crosses, forty-one mounds, forty-one people who had lived and died on this plateau and been buried under wood with no name on it, as if they had never existed at all, as if the company had scraped them off the face of the earth along with the camp itself.

"Wren." Orsen's voice behind her, quiet. He had come over the rim and stopped, and she heard in his voice that he understood what she was looking at, understood it in the way of a man who had spent years making himself unmarked and unnamed. "Wren, I'm sorry."

"They're children," she said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. But now that she had, she saw it everywhere—the smaller mounds clustered near the fence's far corner, the little crosses, some of them barely taller than her boot. "Some of them are children, Orsen."

He came and stood beside her. He didn't touch her. She was grateful for that; she didn't think she could have borne being touched just then, not with her skin feeling the way it did, too tight and too thin, everything underneath exposed to the wind.

"My father knew," she said. "Thirty years he knew. And everyone called him a liar."

* * *

They searched the camp in the last light of the broken day, moving through the ruins with the careful tread of people entering a house where someone has only recently died. Wren found the cookhouse first—a great iron stove still squatting in its corner beneath the fallen roof, a ladle fused to the stovetop by rust, tin plates stacked in a cupboard as if the meal had been interrupted mid-serving and never resumed. A child's plate, smaller than the others, with a chip in its rim shaped like a crescent moon.

She set it down gently, the way you set down something sleeping.

Orsen found the office. It was the half-roofed building at the row's end, and when he pushed open a door swollen shut with thirty years of rain, the smell that came out was paper and rust and cold stone. Inside, shelving lined the walls, mostly empty, looted or rotted—but at the back, built into the rock itself, a steel door, painted over so many times it looked like a slab of gray wax, with a handle of the kind you'd find on a ship's safe.

"Survey vault," Orsen said. His voice had changed. She turned and saw him standing very still, looking at the stamp embossed in the old paint on the door's face, and even half-erased by weather she recognized it, because she had drawn it a hundred times in the margins of her school notebooks without ever knowing why: the crossed pick and compass rose, the letters beneath it worn almost smooth.

*Meridian Survey Company. Camp 7.*

A camp that had never existed. A company that had dissolved on paper two years before this vault was sealed. Her father's map had carried that stamp in its corner for thirty years, and she had thought it decoration, or a joke, or one more piece of the madness. It was none of those things. It was a signature.

"There'll be a lock," Orsen said, "and I don't have anything to cut it with."

But there wasn't a lock. There was only the handle, and when Orsen threw his weight against it, the mechanism inside gave with a shriek of corroded metal—not locked at all, merely seized, sealed by time rather than intent. As if whoever left this place had believed no one would ever come. As if the wilderness itself were lock enough.

The door swung open onto darkness and the smell of dry paper, dry as bone meal, preserved by thirty years of cold.

* * *

They waited for morning. There was no choice; the light was going, and neither of them wanted to carry whatever was in that vault out into the dark. They made a fireless camp in the lee of the collapsed barracks, sharing a single foil blanket, shivering, not speaking. Wren lay awake most of the night listening to the wind move through the ruined buildings, and it sounded—she could not stop herself from hearing it—as though the camp were murmuring in its sleep. Once, near dawn, she heard a sound like laughter, high and thin, and told herself it was a bird, some ptarmigan, something alive, and pressed her face against her knees until the sound went away.

Gray light came slowly, the way truth comes, reluctantly and all at once.

The vault was small, a room maybe eight feet square, and in the gray light that fell through the doorway their breath hung in it like smoke. Steel shelving. Boxes. Boxes and boxes, stacked with bureaucratic precision, each one stenciled in fading white: *MERIDIAN SURVEY — CAMP 7 — PERSONNEL — PAYROLL*, *CAMP 7 — SUPPLY REQUISITIONS*, *CAMP 7 — MEDICAL*, *CAMP 7 — CORRESPONDENCE — DO NOT REMOVE*.

"Do not remove," Wren read aloud, and heard her voice crack on the absurdity of it. Thirty years, and the order still hung in the air, addressed to no one, obeyed by no one, and yet somehow, until this morning, obeyed by everyone.

Orsen lifted down the first box and set it on the floor between them, and they knelt over it together like mourners at an open grave—which, Wren thought, was exactly what they were.

Pay ledgers. Bound volumes with canvas spines, columns of names in faded ink, weekly wages entered and initialed by a paymaster whose neat, cramped hand never varied across four years of entries. And beside the wages, other columns. *Deductions.* Fines for broken tools, for short weight, for sickness. Deductions that ate whole weeks' pay. Deductions levied against men who were owed wages at rates that would have been criminal in any town with a newspaper in it—and these men had no town, no newspaper, no way out except a company boat that came twice a year.

And the names. Wren ran her finger down the columns and the names ran back up through her like current. *Olek Marshenko. Tomas Aguilar. Joon-ho Park. Samuel Adeyemi.* Men from everywhere, men whose families had been told—she understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like a physical blow—that their husbands and fathers had gone north and vanished, debts unpaid, letters unanswered. Men who had signed contracts they couldn't read for wages they never received at a camp that officially never existed.

"Look at the dates," Orsen said quietly. He was holding a ledger open across his knees. "Cross-reference the payroll against—" He stopped. He turned the page, and then back again, and Wren watched the color leave his face. "Against the medical log."

The medical log was in the third box. It recorded injuries, illnesses, deaths. Deaths entered in the same neat hand, cause noted with clinical brevity: *pneumonia. cave-in, upper adit. dysentery. gunshot wound, discipline.* Gunshot wound, discipline. Entered like a supply requisition. Fourteen entries in the winter of the last year alone, and fourteen new graves would have been dug in frozen ground, and fourteen names struck from the payroll with a single line through the ink, and the line through the ink was the only funeral any of them got.

And then the correspondence box, and the orders.

They were typed on onionskin carbon copies, and the signatures at the bottom might as well have been carved in granite for all the weight they carried. *Requisition approved. Additional labor required. Retention measures authorized.* Retention measures. Wren read the phrase three times before she could make herself understand it, and when she understood it she had to put the papers down and walk to the vault door and stand in the gray air and breathe, because retention measures meant armed guards at the trailheads, meant confiscated papers, meant men who tried to leave being brought back, meant—

Meant the massacre her father had spent his life trying to prove had been authorized. Signed for. Initialed in triplicate by men whose names meant nothing to her and everything to the world, executives who had sat in offices in cities with streetlights and ordered, in writing, the machinery that ended on this plateau in forty-one anonymous graves.

At the bottom of the last box, wrapped in oilcloth, was a packet of photographs.

Photographs were the thing Wren had not been prepared for. Ledgers were numbers; orders were language; she could hold both at arm's length. But photographs had faces.

Group portraits, mostly, taken by someone with an amateur's eye and a borrowed camera, rows of men squinting into northern light in front of the barracks that now lay collapsed behind her. Some of them smiling. Some of them holding tools raised like trophies. In one, a line of men stood shoulder to shoulder outside the cookhouse, and in the front row, kneeling, a boy of maybe twelve grinned gap-toothed at the camera with his arm slung around a man's shoulders—an older man with a face like weathered wood, and on the back, in pencil, in the same cramped hand as the ledgers: *J. Park & son, arrival day.*

Arrival day. As if it were a holiday. As if they had known.

And on the backs of the photographs—this was the thing, this was the thing that undid her—on the backs, someone had written names. Not all of them. Someone, at some point, near the end, had begun the work of naming, writing identities in pencil on the reverse of each print, and had gotten through perhaps half the stack before stopping. Before being stopped, maybe. Or before there was no one left to name.

Wren sat on the vault's threshold with the photographs spread across her knees and wept. Not gracefully, not quietly—she wept the way the monsoon had rained, in sheets, in gusts, without dignity or restraint, and Orsen sat beside her and did not tell her it would be all right, because he was a man who did not lie, and after a while he put his hand flat between her shoulder blades and left it there, and that was enough.

Because underneath the grief, something else was happening. She could feel it assembling itself in her chest with every photograph, every ledger line, every initialed order: a structure. A case. Her father had spent thirty years carrying a story no one would believe, and here it was, the story itself, in ink and silver salts and canvas-bound volumes, heavy as ballast, real as ballast, able to sink the men who had done this if only it could be carried to water.

Grief transfigured. That was the phrase that came to her, kneeling in the doorway of a vault that should not exist, holding the face of a boy who had arrived smiling and been buried nameless. Grief, transfigured into evidence. Her father's whole mad life suddenly legible, suddenly purposeful, suddenly vindicated in the only currency the world respected: paper, signed, dated, undeniable.

"We take all of it," she said. Her voice came out scraped raw and steady. "Every page. Every photograph. We wrap it in the tarp and we carry it out if we have to carry it on our backs."

Orsen was already looking at the sky. She followed his gaze and saw what he saw: the clouds breaking apart over the eastern ranges, high wind streaming white off the summits, the particular hard clarity of air that follows a storm. Calving season. The glacier route would be opening—if it wasn't open already—and it would stay open for days, not weeks.

"It's four days' walk to the ice," he said. "Five, loaded like we'll be. The window won't wait for us."

"Then we don't wait for the window."

He almost smiled. It was a tired, battered thing, but it reached his eyes, and she realized she had never seen him look less afraid. "No," he said. "I don't suppose we do."

They worked through the morning, wrapping the ledgers in oilcloth and tarp, distributing the weight between two packs that had already given up pretending to be light. Wren took the photographs and the correspondence herself, wrapped them separately, close against her body, where a river or a fall would have to go through her to get at them. When they were ready, they stood a moment at the fence, at the gate that wasn't a gate, and Wren looked back down the rows of silver crosses leaning in the tall grass.

"I'm coming back for you," she said to them. "With your names. All of them."

The wind moved through the grass and the crosses seemed, for a moment, to lean the other way, like a crowd turning to watch her go.

They had made perhaps a mile along the rim trail, packs cutting grooves into their shoulders, when Orsen stopped so abruptly she walked into him. He caught her arm and pointed, and she followed his finger down to the tree line far below, where the river they had lost emerged silver from the gorge—

—and where, on the trail that switchbacked up the valley wall, small and deliberate and unhurried, three figures were climbing.

Not behind them. Ahead of them. Between them and the glacier.

One of the figures paused, straightened, and looked up at the rim where they stood. Even at that distance, even across a mile of thin bright air, Wren felt the look land on her skin like a hand.

Harker had found another way up.
