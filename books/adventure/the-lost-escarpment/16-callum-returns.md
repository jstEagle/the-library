## 16. Callum Returns

The vault had given up its dead the way a river gives up drowned men—slowly, reluctantly, one name at a time.

Orsen crouched in the doorway of the rusted survey building with a ledger open across his knees, and he read the names aloud because Wren had asked him to, because she said the dead deserved to be said out loud at least once before they were written down again in some court record nobody would read. The rain had stopped two hours ago. The monsoon had not broken so much as paused, gathering itself somewhere beyond the escarpment's southern rim, and the silence it left behind was heavier than the noise had been. Water dripped from the collapsed barracks in a slow percussion. The glacier at the plateau's northern edge ticked and groaned like a ship at anchor.

"Alberich Deng," Orsen read. "Pay ledger, month of March. Fourteen days' work. Signed."

"Signed by whom?" Wren didn't look up. She was three ledgers deep, her hair stuck to her temples, her hands black with rust and older dirt.

"R. Voss. Company secretary."

"Voss." She almost laughed. It came out as something else. "You never told me your first name, Orsen."

"It's not short for anything. It's just Orsen." He turned the page. "Different man. Different spelling. The world's full of Vosses who sign things."

He didn't tell her the rest—that he had spent eleven years making sure his name appeared on no ledger anywhere, that he had flown into this country under a license bought from a dead man in a coastal city, that the instinct to be unwritten had once been so strong he'd changed the way he walked when he crossed a town square. She knew some of it. She knew enough. Out here, with the graves in their rows below the cliffs and the photographs spread on an oilcloth between them—forty-one faces, forty-one names recovered from a water-stained personnel file—his old caution felt like something that had happened to another man.

They had been on the plateau for a day and a half. They had slept in the survey building with the vault's contents stacked around them like ballast: ledgers, the orders with executive signatures, the photographs, a company stamp in a wooden box that matched the one on the map. Wren had wept once, briefly, standing over the graves, and then had not wept again, and Orsen had understood that she had made a decision about what to do with her grief, the way you make a decision about cargo weight before a takeoff.

Now the light was going. Not sunset—the clouds were too thick for that—but a general dimming, the day simply giving out, and Orsen felt the change in the air the way he felt weather through the skin of a plane. Pressure shifting. Something moving.

He stood up and walked to the doorway and looked south.

The goat track they'd climbed came up the escarpment's southern face, a seam of switchbacks stitched against the cliff. From here, in the failing light, it looked like a scratch. Nothing moved on it. Nothing should move on it. Callum had the map, but Callum didn't have the last of their food, and the climb was two days hard for a man in condition, and Callum had never in Orsen's experience done anything hard if there was a way around it.

And yet.

"Wren."

"I see it." She was beside him. When had she gotten beside him? She had a way of arriving that reminded him of her father—Elias Marsh had moved the same way, quiet, deliberate, a man who'd spent thirty years listening for company men.

On the lowest visible switchback, something flickered. A lantern, or a flashlight, badly shielded. Then it was gone.

"That's not Harker," Orsen said. "Harker doesn't use light. Harker's been tracking us upriver for nine days and we've never once seen his light."

"Callum uses light." She said the name without heat, which was worse than heat. "Callum thinks the dark is something that happens to other people."

Orsen did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding. If Callum had gone down the track two nights ago with the map and the radio battery, and if he'd made it to the river, and if the river had brought him to Harker—and it would have, because Harker was the river now, Harker was in every eddy—then Callum had traded. That was the whole shape of the man. Callum traded. He'd traded his story for their trust at the dock, traded his knowledge of the rapids for a place in the boat, traded his confession on the flooded church floor for their sympathy, and when sympathy ran out he'd traded the map for whatever Harker had offered. Safe passage. Money. A name in a book that wasn't a ledger of the dead.

And Harker would have taken the trade and then taken Callum anyway, because Harker was methodical, and methodical men do not leave loose threads, and Callum was the loosest thread Orsen had ever met.

"He'll bring him right to us," Wren said.

"He already has."

* * *

They spent an hour preparing, and the preparation was mostly deciding what to abandon, which was a kind of grief Orsen knew well. They couldn't carry everything. The vault's contents were the mission; everything else was weight. They wrapped the ledgers and photographs and the signed orders in the tarp he'd patched the canoe with, sealed it with the last of the resin, and lashed it into a pack that Wren could carry if she had to run. Orsen took the rifle—their one rifle, bought from a trading post man who hadn't asked questions—and checked it with hands that remembered how, though he wished they didn't.

The photographs he almost left. Forty-one faces on oilcloth, and no room.

"Take them," Wren said.

"They're copies of what's in the ledgers."

"They're faces. Ledgers don't have faces." She was already folding the oilcloth with the care of a woman folding a flag. "My father spent thirty years trying to make these people real to somebody. I'm not leaving them on a shelf."

They moved up-plateau, away from the track, to a shelf of granite below the glacier's terminal moraine where an old survey hut clung to the rock like a barnacle—corrugated walls, a roof half gone, a floor of packed gravel. From there they could see the track's upper reaches and the vault building below, and the glacier beyond, white and vast and ticking in the dark.

The calving window. That was the other clock, the one ticking louder than footsteps on a switchback. Elias Marsh's margin notes had been clear, and the old pilot's almanac Orsen kept in his head agreed: the summer melt opened a route across the glacier tongue for a handful of days, maybe a week, when the ice was stable enough between calving events to cross and the meltwater channels ran shallow enough to ford. Then the season turned and the tongue began shedding bergs into the lake below and the route closed until the next brief window, months away. They had watched the glacier all day. It was restless. It was the restlessness of a thing about to change its mind.

They waited in the hut without a fire. Cold crept in through the broken roof. Wren sat with her back against the wall and the photograph bundle on her knees, and Orsen sat where he could see the track, and the dark came down around them like a held breath.

It was nearly full dark when the light came again—closer now, a bobbing glow on the upper switchbacks, and behind it, lower, a second glow, steadier, disciplined. Two parties. One moving fast and carelessly. One moving the way water moves, without hurry, without any possibility of being stopped.

"He's running," Wren whispered.

"From them or to us?"

"Does it matter?"

It did, actually, and Orsen couldn't have said why, except that he had watched Callum for nine days and nine days was enough to learn that the man was a weathervane—spinning, yes, but always spinning toward the wind, and the wind always blew from the direction of survival. If Callum was running toward them, it meant that in whatever room he'd traded the map in, something had gone wrong. Something had shown him the true price of the goods.

The first light came off the track and onto the plateau, and a voice came with it, ragged, carrying across the flat ground in the still air.

"Orsen! Wren! For God's sake—Orsen, I can see your hut, I can see you're up there—"

Callum.

Orsen rose to the doorway with the rifle low and the barrel pointed at nothing and everything. "Stop where you are."

Callum stopped. The lantern lit him from below, and he looked terrible—hollowed, soaked to the chest, a cut over one eyebrow that had been cleaned and closed with a neatness that wasn't his. Someone had patched him up. Someone with field dressings and steady hands.

"They're behind me," Callum said. "Harker. Three others. They let me climb first because they wanted me to lead them to you, and I did, I led them, I'm sorry, I led them, but then—" He stopped, gulped air. "Then on the river, before the gorge, Harker got a radio call. From the company. From somebody above Harker. And whatever they said, he stopped talking to me like a man he'd hired and started talking to me like a man he was going to bury. Because I know things, Orsen. I know the camp from the inside. I'm evidence. They don't need me anymore now they have the map's location, and a man like Harker doesn't carry evidence he can burn."

"Callum." Wren's voice, flat. "The map."

He flinched. In the lantern light Orsen watched the whole architecture of the man's face try three different expressions and fail at all of them. "I sold it. I sold the location, I mean. I never had the map itself, I memorized—I told them where the vault was, what was in it. I thought—" His voice cracked and reformed. "I thought they'd take the information and let me walk. That was the deal. That was always the deal, with them, with everyone, you make the deal and you walk. It's how I've stayed alive since I was nine years old."

"You were nine," Wren said, and there was something in her voice now that wasn't flat at all.

"I was nine." Callum's lantern hand was shaking. "I was nine and I carried survey chain up this same cliff and I heard the shots from the barracks and I ran and I've been running for twenty-two years and I am so tired of running that when they offered me a way out I took it, and now the way out is a hole in the riverbank and a shovel, and you two are the only people on this earth who might—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "You might still need me. The glacier route. I've crossed it. I told you. When I was twelve, with the last survey crew. I know where the tongue is stable and where it isn't, and Harker doesn't know that, and you don't know that, and the window is open now, isn't it? I heard it calving this afternoon. I heard it all the way down the track."

Behind him, down on the plateau, the second light appeared at the top of the track. Steady. Unhurried. It did not swing around searching; it came straight toward the vault building and stopped, and a figure stood beside it, and even at two hundred meters Orsen could see the stillness of the man, the economy of him. Harker had arrived. Harker knew exactly where they were, because Callum had told him, because Callum had told him everything, and now Callum was up here on the granite shelf begging to be useful one more time.

"He'll come up at first light," Orsen said quietly.

"He'll come up now," Callum said. "He's not waiting. He got new orders. He's not a man who waits on new orders."

* * *

Harker came up alone.

That was the thing Orsen hadn't predicted, and it took him a long moment in the dark to understand it. The three others stayed below with the lights, spread out at the trackhead in a loose line—not an assault formation, a containment formation. And Harker climbed the goat track by himself, without a light, in the dark, at a pace that never varied, and Orsen watched him come and felt the hair rise on his arms, because he had known men like this and every one of them had been more dangerous alone than accompanied. Company men traveled in packs. Harker traveled the way a judgment traveled.

He stopped thirty meters from the hut, at the edge of the granite shelf, and stood with his hands loose at his sides and his rifle slung—not raised, slung—and he said, in a voice pitched to carry and no further:

"Marsh. Wren Marsh. And the pilot."

Not Orsen Blackwood. Not any of the names on any of the licenses. The pilot. Harker knew what he was, and what he wasn't, and he was telling Orsen he knew.

"You have the vault contents," Harker said. "I want them. You have the map's daughter and the camp's ghost and a rifle you probably know how to use better than I do, and I want all of it, and I am going to tell you something now that I have never said to anyone, and you can decide what it's worth."

The glacier ticked behind them. The dark pressed down.

"I was there," Harker said. "Not at the massacre. After. I was the man they sent to make it not have happened. I burned the pay records that survived. I filled in the survey cuts. I wrote the report that said the camp never existed, and I signed it, and I have signed my name to that lie for twenty-two years, and every year the company pays me and every year the payment buys less, because what it's buying is a version of me that I killed in that camp and buried under the report." He paused. The pause was the most human thing Orsen had ever heard from him. "I don't want to destroy the evidence anymore. I did, when we started. I want it now for a different reason. I want to be in it. I want my name in the record as the man who took the orders, because a record is the only place I exist, and if the record is a lie then so am I, and I am tired of being a lie."

"Orsen," Callum breathed, at his shoulder. "Don't listen. It's a play. It's all a play with him, everything is a play—"

"Shut up, Callum." Wren. And then, to Harker, her voice carrying across the granite: "You want your name in the record. My father wanted the names in the record. Forty-one names. Do you know them?"

"I know some."

"Then you can help carry them."

For a long moment nobody moved. Orsen kept the rifle low and his thumb off the safety and his eyes on Harker's hands, and Harker's hands stayed loose and empty at his sides, and Orsen began—carefully, the way you trust ice—to believe.

Then Harker's hand moved.

It moved fast, faster than a man his age should move, and it wasn't reaching for the rifle sling, it was reaching inside his coat, and Orsen was bringing his own rifle up when Callum stepped past him—stepped out of the hut, onto open granite, into the space between the doorway and the dark, with his lantern raised high in one shaking hand and nothing in the other.

"Harker!" Callum's voice cracked across the shelf. "The radio call—the one that changed everything—there's more to it. There's a second set of records. In the coast office. I know the combination. I'm the only one alive who knows the combination, and if you shoot anybody on this mountain tonight, that combination dies with me, and you spend the rest of your life being the lie."

Harker had stopped. His hand was inside his coat, and whatever was in it stayed there, and in the lantern light Orsen saw the man's face for the first time clearly: not cruel, not cold—cornered. A man who had come up a mountain to surrender and found, at the last moment, that he didn't know how.

"Put it down," Orsen said. "Whatever it is. Put it down, and we'll talk about the coast office."

The glacier chose that moment to speak. A deep, grinding crack rolled down from the icefall above the tongue, long and resonant, like the mountain clearing its throat, and every one of them—Orsen, Wren, Callum with his lantern, Harker with his hidden hand—turned toward the sound. High on the glacier, a serac the size of a church leaned out over the route and stood there, trembling, deciding.

"The window," Wren whispered. "It's opening. Or closing. We have to know which."

Harker's hand came out of his coat empty. In it had been a radio handset, nothing more—a man checking in with his masters, or deciding not to. He set it on the granite between them like a man laying down a card he couldn't afford to play.

"East side of the tongue is stable until the wind shifts," he said. "I've crossed it twice. Callum's west-side route calved out six years ago. He doesn't know that. He was nine."

Callum's lantern dipped. "That's not—"

"It is." Harker looked at him, and there was something in the look that wasn't hatred, wasn't contempt—something almost like recognition, one survivor of the same fire looking at another. "You've been running on a map that expired, boy. We all have."

Above them, the serac groaned, leaned further, and began, with terrible slowness, to fall.
