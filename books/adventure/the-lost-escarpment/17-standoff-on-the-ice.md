## 17. Standoff on the Ice

The glacier tongue came out of the escarpment's shadow like something spilled—a gray-white river of ice, cracked and heaved and groaning under its own weight, running down the valley toward the fog-choked lowlands where the monsoon still worked itself out in long gray sheets. From the rim they had watched it all morning while Wren taped her blistered hands and Orsen counted the hours against the calendar Elias Marsh had kept in his head for thirty years.

"Calving window opens tomorrow," Wren had said. "Maybe tonight if the rain keeps up. The meltwater lifts the tongue off its bed and the whole face lets go. After that you've got hours—maybe six, maybe ten—before it re-seals."

Orsen had crossed ice before, in another life, in another name. He knew what a calving glacier sounded like: artillery without a war, the deep concussion of a serac going over, the crack that traveled through the soles of your boots like the mountain clearing its throat. He had hoped never to hear it again.

They were halfway down the goat track to the tongue when Harker stepped out from behind a wall of blue ice.

* * *

He had not hurried them. That was the thing Orsen would remember afterward—the patience of it. Harker stood with his rifle slung loose across his chest, snow melting in his gray hair, looking less like an enforcer than like a surveyor who had wandered too far from his instruments. Callum stood three paces to his left, hands jammed in his pockets, unable to meet anyone's eyes.

"Put the case down," Harker said.

The vault contents were lashed into an aluminum document case that had once belonged to the mining company itself—Orsen had appreciated the irony when he'd found it in the barracks rubble. Pay ledgers, signed orders, photographs, the roster of names. Thirty years of the dead, packed into forty pounds of metal.

Wren didn't put it down. She shifted it higher on her shoulder instead, planting herself between Harker and the ice. "You're too late," she said. "Copies exist."

"There are no copies." Harker's voice was quiet, almost gentle. "There is what's in that case, and there is what I was sent to make sure never leaves this valley. Those are the only two versions of events."

"You read it," Wren said. "In the vault. You read your own signature."

Harker said nothing. The wind came down the glacier, cold and mineral-smelling, carrying the faint rotten-egg breath of the ice.

"I was twenty-six," he said at last. "Field man. They told me the camp was being decommissioned. They told me the men who died had been killed in a rockfall, and that the families had been compensated, and that my job was paperwork." His eyes went to the case. "The paperwork was shovels."

Orsen felt Wren go rigid beside him.

"And then?" she asked. Her voice shook, but she held it. "Then what did you do for thirty years?"

"What I was paid to do." Harker's jaw tightened. "I made sure no one came back. Prospector, journalist, one woman with a camera in 'ninety-one. I made sure." He unslung the rifle—not raising it, just holding it now, the way a man holds a tool he has used so long it has become part of his hand. "I am tired. Do you understand? I have been tired for thirty years. And every year I thought: someone will come who I cannot stop, and then it will be over, and I will be done. And every year it was someone I could stop."

"So stop us," Wren said. "Shoot us on the ice and carry the case back yourself. That's the job, isn't it?"

"That was the job," Harker said. "The job changed when I found my name in the ledger."

* * *

Orsen had been watching Callum.

The drifter stood apart, working his jaw, glancing at the glacier face as though measuring it—and Orsen realized with a cold drop in his stomach that Callum was measuring it. The route across. The distance to the far moraine. The places where the ice bridge might hold and the places where it would certainly not. Whatever deal Callum had tried to cut with Harker upriver, whatever he'd sold or failed to sell, some part of him was still planning to cross.

And that meant Callum knew the crossing. Which meant, if it came to it, they might need him alive and willing.

The glacier chose that moment to speak.

It began as a sound beneath their feet—a long, tearing groan, like a ship's hull opening—and then somewhere up-valley, hidden behind a ridge of dirty ice, something enormous let go. The report rolled through the valley and came back off the escarpment walls twice before it died. A white plume rose above the ice ridge, hanging there, slowly collapsing.

"The meltwater's up," Callum said quietly. All the charm had gone out of his voice; what remained was flat and professional. "That was the east flank. The tongue's moving. We've got until midday tomorrow, maybe less. After that the route closes for another year."

"Shut up," Harker said.

"You don't want to shoot us," Callum went on, taking a step forward, palms out. "Think about it. You shoot three people on a glacier, the company buries you with them. You're not a solution anymore, Harker—you're a loose end. The moment those names get out, everyone who ever touched this gets burned clean. You've seen how they work."

"I said shut up." But the rifle barrel dipped, just slightly, and Orsen saw the doubt move through the man like weather.

Orsen stepped forward.

It was not courage. He wanted to be honest with himself about that, standing there on the ice with his heart slamming against his ribs. It was not courage; it was arithmetic. He had spent fifteen years being no one—flying under borrowed names, paying cash, keeping his back to walls, making himself so small the world forgot to look at him. He had done it because being seen had once cost him everything he loved. And here was a man who had spent thirty years doing the same thing in the opposite direction: seen by everyone, known by no one, carrying a name he could not say aloud.

Two men, Orsen thought. Two ways of disappearing.

"Harker," he said.

The enforcer's eyes came to him, wary and level.

"You read the ledger," Orsen said. "So you know the names. Forty-one men. You know which ones signed the order to seal the adit with the survivors inside. You know which executives countersigned the compensation file that never reached anyone." He paused. "And you know that your own signature appears eleven times. Requisitions. Fuel. Explosives. Labor transfers."

Harker's knuckles whitened on the stock.

"Here's what happens next," Orsen said. "Option one. You pull that trigger, and for a few more years you're nobody's problem. Then the company folds—which it will, because companies like this always fold, they eat themselves—and the archive gets sold or shredded, and the only record left of Elias Marsh's thirty years, and forty-one dead men, and the camp that never existed, is whatever survives in your head. And you'll die with it. Quietly. Like you've been dying for thirty years already."

"Or?"

"Or you walk with us." Orsen nodded at the glacier, at the white plume still dissolving downwind. "You cross the ice, you sit in front of a judge, and you say your name out loud. Not the name on the payroll. Your name. And you tell them what you were ordered to do, and what you did, and what it cost. You become the man who buried them and the man who dug them back up. Both things. In the same record. Forever."

The wind gusted. Somewhere deep in the glacier, water moved—a rushing, hollow sound, like a river running under the world.

"They'll take my license," Harker said. "My pension. Maybe my freedom."

"Yes."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you spend the rest of your life as an order-taker," Orsen said. "Which is what you've been since you were twenty-six. The only difference is that this time you'll have chosen it with your eyes open."

Harker looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind his face—not softening exactly, more like a structure settling, a wall deciding whether it was load-bearing anymore.

"You think justice comes out of a courtroom," Harker said. "I've watched courtrooms. Courtrooms are where the company keeps its spare parts."

"It doesn't come out of a courtroom," Orsen said. "It comes out of a name. Everything else is procedure."

* * *

Wren had not spoken. She stood with the case against her hip, watching Harker the way she'd watched the river rapids—reading the water, finding the line. When she finally spoke, her voice was steady in a way that frightened Orsen more than any shouting could have.

"My father traded me this map on his deathbed," she said. "Thirty years he carried it. Thirty years he wrote letters that came back unopened. He named every one of those men in the margins—in pencil, in the dark, in cabins colder than this. He never got to say their names out loud either." She took one step toward Harker, boots crunching on the ice. "So here is what I know about you. You were the last person to see that camp. You were the shovel. And you are the only living soul besides me who can stand in a room and say: I was there, and here is what happened, and here are their names. My father can't do it. He's dead. Callum won't do it—he'd sell his own memory for a boat ride. So it's you, or it's no one, forever."

Harker's breath came out slow and ragged in the cold.

"If I cross with you," he said, "and we reach the other side, and I give testimony—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a man in the ledger. Tomas Reyes. Laborer, age nineteen. He ran on the last night. I was sent after him. I found him four miles downriver, half-drowned, and I—" His voice failed and recovered. "I reported him as recovered. That's the word they used. Recovered."

Nobody spoke. The glacier groaned again, longer this time, a sound like grief given mechanics.

"He has a daughter," Harker finished. "In Kestrel Landing. She thinks he drowned in a rockfall. She thinks that because I wrote it." He looked down at the rifle in his hands as though he'd forgotten what it was for. "I have written a great many things."

"Then write one more thing true," Wren said.

* * *

Harker lowered the rifle.

Not dropped it—lowered it, deliberately, and worked the bolt, and thumbed the rounds out into his palm one by one, and stood holding five brass cartridges in an open hand like a man showing his cards. Then he bent and pushed them into a crevasse mouth at his feet, and the glacier took them without comment.

Callum exhaled explosively. "Okay. Okay. Good. Great. Now can we please talk about the crossing, because the tongue is moving and I have been trying to tell you people for ten minutes—"

"Quiet," Harker said, and there was something new in his voice—not authority, exactly. Relief, maybe. The relief of a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying. He turned to Orsen. "You've crossed calving ice before."

"Once. Under different circumstances."

"Then you know the rule." Harker pointed up-valley with his chin. "We don't cross the tongue. The tongue is garbage—meltwater channels, bridges that look solid and aren't. We go up onto the lateral moraine, traverse high along the east wall, and come down onto the trunk glacier past the icefall, where the old survey route runs. There's a cairn line. The company put it in when the camp was active. It's still there."

"You've used it," Orsen said.

"Twice." Harker's mouth twisted. "Carrying things out that were never supposed to be seen."

The wind shifted. Down-valley, the fog bank that had sat over the lowlands for two days began to lift and shred, and for the first time they could see the full length of the glacier—a river of ice eight miles long, running white and fractured between black walls of stone, and at its head, catching the first thin light behind the clouds, the calving face: a wall of ice two hundred feet tall, streaked blue and gray, seeping meltwater in a hundred silver threads.

Even Harker stopped to look at it.

"It's early," he said quietly. "The face shouldn't be active until tomorrow. The rain accelerated it." He glanced at the sky, reading it the way Orsen read weather. "We have until the rain stops. Once the meltwater drops, the tongue re-seals and the window closes. Could be twelve hours. Could be six."

"Then we move now," Wren said. She shrugged the case higher on her shoulder and started up the moraine without waiting for an answer.

Orsen fell in beside her. Behind them came Harker, rifle slung muzzle-down, walking like a man who had finally decided which direction his debts lay. And behind Harker, at a careful distance, came Callum—still unreadable, still watching the ice, still calculating something Orsen couldn't see.

They climbed the moraine in single file, boots sliding on frost-shattered stone, the glacier breathing below them. Halfway up the traverse, Orsen looked back once. The calving face stood silent in the failing light, patient as a held breath.

Then, from somewhere deep inside it, came a sound like the world cracking its knuckles.

"Move," Harker said. "Now. Faster."

They moved.
