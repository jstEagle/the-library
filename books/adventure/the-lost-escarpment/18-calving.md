## 18. Calving

The sound came first—not a crack but a groan, deep and tectonic, as if the mountain itself were clearing its throat after thirty years of silence. It rolled down the glacier tongue and out across the valley, and every one of them felt it in the bones of their feet before they heard it with their ears.

Wren stood at the lip of the escarpment's eastern shoulder, the evidence case strapped to her back like a child she refused to set down, and watched the ice begin to die.

It was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it—the way catastrophe dressed itself in beauty. The glacier face, a wall of blue-white ice two hundred feet high, shuddered and shed its skin. A slab the size of a church broke free and fell without hurry, rotating as it dropped, catching the thin morning sun so that for one impossible moment it blazed like stained glass before it struck the meltwater lake below and sent up a column of spray that hung in the air, glittering, refusing to fall.

Then the thunder arrived, late and enormous, and the lake rose in a single wave that raced toward the shore where they stood.

"Move!" Harker's voice, stripped of everything quiet and methodical, raw now. "Off the moraine—high ground—*move!*"

They ran. Orsen grabbed Wren's arm and hauled her up the scree slope as the wave hit the boulder field below them, water exploding through gaps between stones, cold spray drenching them to the waist. Behind them the lake heaved, calved again, heaved again—a living thing in labor, giving birth to icebergs that drifted and rolled and ground against each other with sounds like grinding teeth.

And there, across the chaos, the route.

Harker had told them about it the night before, when the gun was still between them and trust was still a currency none of them could afford. The calving season opened the glacier crossing for a few hours each day—no more. When the face shed its weight, the pressure on the tongue eased, crevasses gaped wide enough to see into and therefore to avoid, and for a brief window the ice was honest about what it was. Then the face rebuilt its tension, the crevasses bridged over with fresh snow, and the glacier went back to lying.

"You cross when it calves," Harker had said, watching the fire instead of them. "Not before. Not after. I've done it twice." A pause. "I buried men on it once."

Now they stood on the shoulder and watched the window open beneath them.

* * *

Orsen counted seracs the way he'd once counted fuel gauges—without emotion, because emotion killed pilots and it would kill climbers too. The ice towers along the route leaned at angles that offended geometry, some of them the size of grain silos, balanced on foundations already failing. Every few minutes another one went. The falls came from everywhere: left, right, ahead, behind. There was no rhythm to it, no pattern a man could hide inside. There was only speed, and luck, and the space between one collapse and the next.

He looked at Harker.

The enforcer stood apart from them, rifle slung across his back—he'd slung it himself, an hour ago, without ceremony, and no one had commented because commenting would have made it real. He was studying the glacier with the narrowed eyes of a man reading a document he'd signed once and never wanted to see again. His hands, Orsen noticed, were shaking. Not from cold.

"You've crossed this," Orsen said. Not a question.

"Twice." Harker didn't look at him. "First time with a survey crew, before. Second time—" His jaw worked. "Second time we carried things out. Things that weighed less than people should."

The wave of it hit Orsen then, the full weight of what this man was: not a hunter who had stumbled onto their trail but a hand that had helped do the burying, a man who had spent years paying interest on a debt whose principal was measured in graves. He'd seen Harker's kind before, in another life, under another name. Men who followed orders and then discovered the orders had followed *them*, all the way home, all the way into every mirror.

"The case goes first," Orsen said. "The names go across if nothing else does."

Harker finally turned. Something moved behind his eyes—recognition, maybe, or relief. The look of a man who has been waiting years for someone to give him an order worth following.

"The names go across," Harker said. "I'll get you there. That's the only penance I've got left, so don't waste it."

* * *

They descended onto the ice in the gap between two collapses, and the glacier took them the way the sea takes swimmers—with indifference and occasional violence.

Wren felt the route in her body before her boots confirmed it. Years of drawing her father's coordinates had taught her terrain secondhand; the last three weeks had taught it firsthand, in her legs and lungs and the permanent ache of her shoulders. But this was different. This was walking on something alive and dying at once. Under her feet the ice groaned in long, sliding notes, a choir tuning itself for collapse. Meltwater ran in channels beside the path, turquoise and fast, pouring into holes that breathed cold air upward like open throats.

Harker led. He moved without hesitation, cutting left where the path looked right, right where the path looked left, and twice he stopped them flat with a raised fist while his head tilted, listening, and then a serac they couldn't see came down somewhere ahead with a sound like the sky tearing, and the wind of it reached them a full three seconds later, carrying ice dust that sparkled and stung.

"That one," Harker said quietly, when it settled, "would have taken us. Keep moving."

Callum walked third, between Wren and Orsen, and Wren hated how much she watched him.

She'd memorized the coordinates long ago—that was her private victory, the thing she'd held against Callum's theft like a coal cupped in both hands—but the map itself was gone, traded away by a man who claimed he'd been trying to buy his own life. She believed him and she didn't. Both things lived in her at once, and she'd stopped trying to reconcile them. People were allowed to be contradictions. Her father had been one: a man who loved her enough to spend thirty years chasing ghosts and too proud, or too broken, to send a letter.

But watching Callum on the ice, she saw something she hadn't expected. He wasn't afraid the way the rest of them were afraid. He was afraid the way a man is afraid of a house he grew up in. His eyes kept going to the cliffs above the glacier—to the streaks of old survey paint, faded orange, still visible even at this distance—and each time, his step faltered half a beat before he caught it.

"You've been here," she said. Not accusing. Just asking, finally, out loud.

Callum didn't break stride. "I told you I had."

"You told me you were a child laborer at the camp." She picked her way over a pressure ridge, the case shifting heavy against her spine. "On the boat. In the church. You told us a story."

"I told you a true story." His voice was thin. "Whether it happened to me or someone else—I stopped being able to tell the difference a long time ago. That's what they do, Wren. That's what the company does. They take a thing that happened and they make it unspeakable, and then they make it unbelievable, and then the people who remember start wondering if they invented it." He laughed, short and awful. "I've told my story so many times, to so many people who needed it to be one thing or another, that I don't know anymore which parts are mine. Maybe all of it. Maybe none. Maybe that doesn't matter."

"It matters," Wren said. "It's going to matter very soon."

Ahead of them, Harker raised his fist again.

* * *

The middle of the tongue was a labyrinth.

Orsen had flown over glaciers his whole flying life—had traced their blue veins from altitude, admiring them the way you admire a river from a bridge, safely abstract. Nothing in twenty years of bush flying had prepared him for being *inside* one. The crevasses opened around them like the mouths of wells, some narrow enough to step over, some wide enough that he couldn't see the bottom, just a glow of deeper blue fading into black. Snow bridges spanned several of them, white and innocent-looking, and Harker tested each one with an ice axe before letting anyone cross, tapping twice, listening, tapping again.

"Step where I step," Harker said. "Nothing else. Don't wander. Don't be clever."

Orsen was not, by nature, a follower. Every rule he'd built his life around—the two-night rule, the no-names rule, the never-look-back rule—had been constructed to make sure no one ever led him anywhere. But he walked in Harker's footsteps now, exactly, precisely, and found that surrendering the lead to a man who knew the ground was its own strange mercy. For the first time since Elias Marsh had pressed a map into his hands in a freezing cabin, Orsen Blackwood was not responsible for the person behind him. Someone else was navigating. All he had to do was keep Wren in sight and put his boots where the axe had approved.

It lasted forty minutes. It felt like a year.

Then the glacier spoke again, louder than before, and the whole world tilted toward violence.

It started as a tremor underfoot—faint, then not faint, then the ice beneath them was humming like a struck cable. Ahead and to the left, high on the flank of the route, a serac the size of a cathedral began to lean. Slowly. Terribly slowly. The way a tree leans before it falls, except trees fall in seconds and this tower of ice seemed determined to take its time, to let them watch, to let them understand exactly what was coming and roughly when.

"Run," Harker said. "Don't look at it. Run for the far moraine—go!"

They ran.

The ice was rotten underfoot now, the vibration fracturing the surface into plates that shifted and clacked against each other. Orsen grabbed Wren's pack strap and pulled; she pulled back, keeping them together, the case bouncing between them. Callum was ahead, fast, lighter than any of them, dancing across the breaking surface. Harker brought up the rear, shouting intervals—"Left! LEFT!"—as new cracks shot past their boots like lightning frozen mid-strike.

Behind them, the serac fell.

The sound was beyond sound. It was pressure. It hit Orsen's back like a physical hand and drove the breath from his lungs, and the shockwave came through the ice and knocked Wren to her knees, and the front of the falling mass—shards, debris, a blizzard of shattered blue—raked across the route fifty meters behind them, close enough that fragments stung Orsen's neck and drew blood.

When the dust cleared, the route they had just climbed no longer existed. Where the snow bridges and pressure ridges had been there was a field of rubble, churned and smoking with cold, and beyond it a new crevasse had opened, wide as a road, breathing its cold breath into the sudden silence.

They had come through. They were on the far side of the labyrinth, the moraine rising gray and solid ahead of them, the escarpment's shadow stretching out to meet it.

They had come through—all four of them. Orsen counted heads twice, the old pilot's habit, the tally that kept men alive.

Four. All four.

For one moment, standing on the shaking ice with blood on his neck and his heart slamming, Orsen Blackwood let himself feel it: they were going to make it. The case was intact. The names were intact. The window was still open, the far shore was close, and whatever waited beyond the glacier—warrants, testimony, a world that would finally know his face—it could not be worse than this.

That was the moment Callum turned around.

* * *

Wren saw it happen and would spend the rest of her life seeing it happen.

The evidence case had been riding on Orsen's pack through the labyrinth—no, wait. No. In the scramble after the serac fell, in the running and the falling and the hauling, the straps had shifted. The case had come loose. It lay on the ice, thirty meters back, sitting in the rubble field at the edge of the new crevasse, absurdly upright, like a suitcase set down by a traveler waiting for a train that was never coming.

Thirty meters. Across broken ice. Beside a crevasse that hadn't existed ten minutes ago, in a field still settling, still shedding, still dying.

"No," Wren said. She said it loudly. She said it the way you say *no* to a door closing.

Callum was already moving.

He was fast—he'd always been fast, a wiry ferret of a man, a survivor in the purest and most desperate sense of the word. He covered half the distance in seconds, boots skidding on the fractured surface, and snatched the case by its handle, and for one heartbeat Wren thought: *he's taking it, he's running, he's selling them out one more time,* and she was already reaching for the shout that would stop him—

But Callum turned back toward them. Toward safety. Toward them.

And the ice gave way.

There was no drama to it. That was the horror. No warning groan, no cinematic cracking. One moment the ground was holding him; the next it simply wasn't, a plate of ice the size of a kitchen table tilting and sliding into the dark, and Callum went with it, and the case went with him, and the crevasse swallowed both with a sound like a book closing.

"*CALLUM—*"

Orsen reached the edge first. He dropped flat, arms over the void, and looked down into blue light fading to black, and for one second—one second—he saw him. Callum, wedged against a wall of ice fifteen meters down, the case clutched to his chest, looking up. Not screaming. Looking up. And he smiled—or Orsen thought he smiled; the light was bad, the moment was short—and he shouted something up at them that the wind took most of, but not all.

"—tell them it was *real*—"

Then the ice shifted, the wall calved inward, and the blue closed over him like water, and the crevasse was just a crevasse, deep and silent and indifferent, holding nothing anyone could reach.

Orsen lay on the edge until Harker's hand closed on his collar and pulled him back.

"He's gone." Harker's voice was steady in the way of a man holding himself together with rivets. "You can't help him. Moving on that ice kills more people than staying on it. Get up. *Get up.*"

Wren knelt at the rim, just outside the broken zone, and pressed her palm flat against the ice as if she could reach through it. The glacier hummed beneath her hand, alive, digesting.

"He had it," she said. Her voice came out strange, small, distant. "He went back for it. He could have run. He went *back*."

"He did," Orsen said. He got his arm around her and lifted, gently, the way you lift something that might shatter. "Whatever else he was—whatever was true and whatever wasn't—that part was true. That part was all the way true."

Above them, high on the glacier's flank, another serac leaned, and leaned, and fell, and the thunder rolled down the valley like artillery, like a salute, like the mountain counting its dead.

* * *

They crossed the last stretch in silence.

The far moraine rose to meet them, gray stone and gravel, solid in a way that felt almost obscene after hours of living ice. Harker led them up it without stopping, without speaking, checking the sky and the glacier face behind them with quick, professional glances. The calving was slowing. The window was closing. But they were across—they were *across*—and the escarpment's evidence rode on Wren's back, and the route behind them was rubble, and the man who had hunted them walked three paces ahead carrying no gun in his hands and leading them anyway, because leading them was the only direction he had left.

At the top of the moraine, where the glacier's death gave way to tundra and stone, they stopped. Wren set the case down. Orsen stood beside her, bleeding from a dozen small cuts, shaking in a way he hadn't shaken since the war he never talked about. Below them the glacier went on calving, tower after tower, the lake boiling with fresh icebergs, the whole spectacle lit by a sun that had no opinion about any of it.

"He said tell them it was real," Wren said.

Orsen looked at the case. Thirty years of names. A massacre the company had buried, a camp that officially never existed, a little girl's father who had drawn coordinates on maps until the day his lungs filled with blood. And now a man who might have been the youngest survivor, or might have been a liar, or might—as he'd said himself—have been both, lying at the bottom of a crevasse with the only proof of his own story pressed against his chest.

"We will," Orsen said. "Every word. His and everyone else's."

Harker crouched at the edge of the group, staring back the way they'd come, at the shrinking blue line of the glacier tongue. When he spoke, his voice was low, meant mostly for himself.

"There were eleven of us, the second time I crossed," he said. "Carrying things out. I told you that." He was quiet a moment. "I was the only one who ever came back to look at it. Until now."

He stood, and turned to face them, and Orsen saw that whatever Harker had been carrying for all those years had changed shape on the ice today. Lighter, or heavier—he couldn't tell which. Maybe both.

"The valley road is a day's walk," Harker said. "There's a radio shack at the mine gate. Old, but it works—the company never bothered jamming their own equipment." He met Orsen's eyes. "If you're going to call the outside world, call it from there. And when you do—you'd better know exactly what name you're going to say. Because after that, there's no taking it back."

Orsen looked north, toward the valley, toward the voice waiting at the end of a wire. Somewhere beyond the mountains, beyond the monsoon, the world was turning without him, the way it had for fifteen years, content to leave him unnamed and unseen.

He found he was ready to stop being invisible. He found he'd been ready since a dying man made him promise.

"One day's walk," Orsen said. "Let's not waste the daylight."
