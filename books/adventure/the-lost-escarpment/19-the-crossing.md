## 19. The Crossing

The far shore came up at them like a promise kept.

Orsen saw it first through the curtain of spray and drifting ice—the dark shoulder of moraine where the glacier's tongue thinned into gravel, then the stunted spruce beyond it, bent and wind-shaped, clinging to the slope like men who had decided not to die. Behind them the ice groaned and split in long reports that rolled down the valley like distant artillery. A serac the size of a church went over somewhere to the north, and the whole surface of the glacier shivered under their boots.

Harker walked three paces ahead of them, as he had walked for the last hour—not leading, exactly, and not driving them. Choosing his line with the flat competence of a man who had crossed this ice before, years ago, when crossing it had been a different kind of crime. He carried no rifle now. It lay somewhere back on the tongue, dropped muzzle-down in a blue crevasse by his own hand, an act Orsen still hadn't found words for.

"Ridge," Harker said, without turning. "Take the ridge, not the low ground. Meltwater channel cuts through the moraine—looks solid, isn't."

Wren stumbled beside Orsen, the evidence case strapped across her chest like a child she refused to put down. Her face was gray with cold and exhaustion, her lips cracked, her hands too numb to feel the strap she gripped anyway. She had not let go of it since Callum fell. Since the ice had opened under him and he had thrust the case up out of the water with both arms and gone under with the serac's thunder covering everything else.

They had not even had time to grieve. The route was closing behind them even as they crossed it—cracks propagating outward from every calving event, meltwater running silver in a hundred channels, the whole glacier shrugging off its winter skin. They had perhaps two hours of safe passage, and Harker had spent them well, reading the ice the way Orsen read weather: instinctively, without sentiment.

"Here." Harker stopped at the edge of the moraine and looked back once.

The escarpment stood above the ice field, black and immense, its cliffs streaked with the old survey paint—faded orange lines running down the rock like wounds that never healed. Somewhere on that plateau were the graves they'd left undisturbed, the vault they'd emptied, the names they now carried in oilcloth and steel. Somewhere behind all of it, Callum was under the ice.

"He was a liar," Wren said quietly. It wasn't accusation. It was something more tired than that. "Right up until the end. And then he wasn't."

"They usually are," Harker said. "The liars. Right up until they aren't." He said it without looking at either of them, and there was something in his voice Orsen couldn't name—a weight carried so long it had become part of the man's skeleton. "Keep moving. The melt rises after noon. We want the tree line before it does."

* * *

They came down off the ice at midday, and the world changed around them the way it always did at the tree line—the silence of the glacier giving way to the small sounds of living things: water dripping from branches, a bird somewhere making its argument with the sky, wind moving through needles instead of over stone. Orsen felt the change in his own body, a loosening he hadn't known he was holding.

They made camp early, in the lee of a granite outcrop where a stream ran clear over stones. Harker built the fire without being asked—small, efficient, smokeless—and sat apart from them, on the other side of the flames, the way a man sits when he hasn't yet been told whether he's prisoner or guest.

He was neither. That was the strange truth of it. He had laid his rifle in a crevasse and walked beside them across a dying glacier, calling out crevasses and rotten bridges, guiding them over collapsing ice while seracs fell like artillery around them. Whatever he had been sent upriver to do, he had chosen otherwise on the ice, in front of witnesses, and there was no walking that choice back.

Wren slept first, curled against the pack with the case still strapped to her, one hand resting on its latches even in sleep. Orsen watched her breathe and thought about Elias Marsh, dying alone in a trapping cabin with blood in his lungs, trading thirty years of obsession for one promise from a stranger. *Carry it to my daughter.* The old man had gambled everything on the reliability of a bush pilot nobody had ever heard of. And here she was, alive, with the proof in her arms.

"You knew," Orsen said to Harker, keeping his voice low. "When you caught up to us at the trading post. You already knew what was up there."

Harker fed a stick to the fire. His face, lit from below, looked older than it had on the river—hollowed, the methodical quiet cracked open now, showing whatever lived underneath.

"I burned it," he said. "Twenty-two years ago. My orders were to destroy the camp records and make the site look like nothing. A survey camp that failed. I was young and I was good at following orders, and I told myself the ledgers were just paper." He turned the stick over. "The graves weren't paper. I counted them. Forty-one. I've counted them every night since, whether I wanted to or not."

Orsen said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be either cruelty or absolution, and neither of those was his to give.

"The company sent me to erase you," Harker went on. "A pilot, a dock girl, and a map. Simple job. I've done simpler ones worse." He looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were steady and terrible. "But I read the margin notes on that map before I ever got close enough to take it. Marsh's handwriting. I recognized the coordinates. And I understood that this time the erasing wasn't going to hold, because this time somebody was going to carry it out on foot if they had to." He paused. "So I made a different calculation."

"What happens to you now?" Orsen asked. "When we get out. When this goes public."

Harker almost smiled. It didn't reach anything but the corners of his mouth. "I imagine I'll find out. I'm a witness now. Witnesses get called. That's the point of them." He nodded toward the sleeping Wren. "She'll need someone who can name the men who signed the orders. I can do that. It's the only thing I have left to trade, and unlike your friend Callum, I'm not going to sell it."

* * *

Orsen woke before dawn to a sky washed clean.

The monsoon had broken sometime in the night—the last rain moved off eastward, dragging its skirts across the valley, and above the escarpment the air was scrubbed and glittering, the kind of flying weather he hadn't seen in weeks. He stood at the edge of the camp and breathed it in and felt the old ache rise in him, the ache that had shaped his whole adult life: the wish to be up there, unseen, unnamed, a speck against the ceiling of the world.

For eleven years he had been no one. He had built it carefully, plank by plank—aliases in three provinces, a license under a name that wasn't quite his, a habit of never staying two nights anywhere, of never letting a face attach itself to a story. Invisibility as a discipline. Invisibility as armor. He had told himself it was safety. Sitting on a granite boulder above a valley full of melting snow, watching Wren sleep with her father's evidence strapped to her body, he finally admitted what it had actually been: fear, wearing competence as a disguise.

Because invisibility had a cost, and he had paid it in installments for a decade. No one to call. No one who knew his real name. No one whose grief would be his grief, whose fire would warm him without transaction. He had delivered insulin to strip towns full of people who trusted him completely and knew nothing about him at all. He had been reliable and anonymous, useful and alone, and he had called that freedom.

Elias Marsh had seen through it in one afternoon. Dying men don't waste time on charm; they look for the thing underneath. And the thing underneath Orsen Blackwood—or whatever his true name was, the one he hadn't spoken aloud since the day he'd buried it—was a man who could not walk away from a promise. The old man had bet a map and a daughter's inheritance on that, and won.

Wren stirred. Sat up. Looked at the clear sky, then at him, and something passed between them that needed no words—the same understanding that had carried them up a goat track and across a calving glacier: *this is the day.*

"We're past the ridge," she said, voice rough with sleep. "There's a repeater station on the far peak. My father mentioned it once—company-built, but the frequency's public. Emergency band." She unzipped the case, careful as a surgeon, and lifted out the radio they'd salvaged from the wreck of their supplies, wrapped in oilcloth, dry, charged on the last of their solar skin. "You know how to work one of these better than any of us."

It wasn't a question. It was an offering. She was handing him the door and asking him to walk through it.

Orsen took the radio and held it and did not move for a long moment.

Eleven years of discipline said *no.* Eleven years of aliases said *a satellite phone bought with cash in a border town, a message relayed through strangers.* Every survival instinct he owned said *stay a ghost, stay safe, stay nothing.*

And underneath all of it, quiet as groundwater, was the knowledge that ghosts can't testify. Ghosts can't stand in a courtroom and say *I saw the graves, I carried the ledgers, I am a witness and my name is—*

His name.

He climbed to the high shelf of rock above the camp with the radio in his hands, and Wren followed, and Harker followed her, and none of them spoke. Below them the valley ran gold and green in the new sun. Above them the escarpment stood in full light for the first time since they'd arrived, its cliffs bare and honest, hiding nothing anymore.

Orsen keyed the transmit switch. The little speaker hissed static, then the open emergency band, empty, waiting.

He thought about the first rule he'd ever made himself, back when the running started: *never use the name. The name is the trail. The trail is the end.*

Then he thought about forty-one graves on a plateau, and a woman asleep against a case full of names, and a drifter under the ice who had spent his whole life being no one's child, and a man named Harker who had chosen witness over weapon because someone finally offered him the choice.

"This is Orsen Blackwood," he said, and his voice did not shake, though something in his chest broke open and would not close again. "Bush pilot, registered out of Kestrel Landing. I am transmitting from the Lost Escarpment survey area, grid reference to follow. I am carrying documentary evidence of multiple deaths at a company-operated labor camp, concealed approximately twenty-two years ago. I have a surviving witness prepared to identify the officers who signed the concealment orders." He read the coordinates off the map's margin, slow and clear, twice. "Request immediate contact with provincial authorities and press. Repeat: this is Orsen Blackwood. I am done being invisible."

Static. Long enough that doubt began to crawl in.

Then a voice, tinny, astonished, half-lost in interference: *"Say again your name? Blackwood? Stand by—we have your position. Stand by, Blackwood. Help is coming."*

Orsen set the radio down on the rock, gently, the way you set down something that has just changed your life. The morning wind moved over the escarpment, over the graves, over the three of them standing in the open with nothing hidden anymore.

Below, Wren touched his arm. "You used your name."

"Yeah." He found he was smiling, and that it cost him nothing. "Yeah, I did."

Far to the south, a sound reached them across the valley—thin at first, then unmistakable: engines. Not company boats. Aircraft, more than one, coming up the corridor of the valley toward the coordinates he had given, toward the man who had finally, after eleven years, told the world exactly who and where he was.

Harker shaded his eyes and watched them come. "They'll want statements," he said. "All of us. Starting today."

"Good," Orsen said.

The lead aircraft banked toward them, sunlight flashing off its wings, and Orsen Blackwood stood on the high rock in full view of the whole bright sky, seen at last, and did not look away.
