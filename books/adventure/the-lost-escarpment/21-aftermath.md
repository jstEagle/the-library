## 21. Aftermath

The hearing room in the provincial capital smelled of floor wax and old paper, and Orsen Blackwood sat under the fluorescent lights like a man being slowly unwrapped.

He had worn his good shirt—the one without hydraulic fluid on the cuffs—and it had made no difference. The lawyer for Meridian Consolidated Mining had spent four hours taking him apart stitch by stitch, and she was very good at it. Her name was Aldous-Reyes, and she never raised her voice. She simply asked, and asked, and asked, until the seams gave.

"Mr. Blackwood. Is that your real name?"

"No."

"And what is your real name?"

A pause that lasted three seconds and felt like a season. "Orsen Vale."

"Orsen Vale. Born where?"

"Thunder Bay. Nineteen years ago." He caught himself, corrected it with a dry mouth. "Thirty-one years ago."

The stenographer's fingers did not slow. Somewhere behind him, Wren sat perfectly still in the gallery, and he could feel her stillness the way you feel ballast in a boat—something keeping him level when every instinct said to run.

"So we have Mr. Blackwood of Kestrel Landing, Mr. Vale of Thunder Bay, and at least two other names on file with Transport Canada, all attached to the same fingerprints, the same pilot's license number, the same aircraft serial history. Would you say you've been hiding from something, Mr.—"

"Vale," he said. "I've been hiding from everyone."

* * *

It went on for eleven days.

They took his testimony in pieces, because there was so much of it: the map Elias Marsh had pressed into his hands with blood-flecked fingers; the burned fuel cache at the strip town; Harker's checkpoint at the trading post; the flooded church; the gorge; the survey vault with its pay ledgers and its photographs, each one a face with a name typed beneath it in a dead man's careful hand. And the graves. Two hundred and six rows of them, unmarked, on a plateau the company's maps showed as uninhabited bedrock.

He told it all plainly, because he had learned on the river that plainness was the only thing that held weight. Embellishment sank. Truth floated, even waterlogged.

The company's lawyers fought every inch. They produced employment records showing Callum Reyes—no relation to the counsel, though the coincidence made the room uncomfortable—had been on their payroll as a seasonal laborer at an age no laborer should be. They produced nothing at all about Camp Escarpment, because officially there had never been a Camp Escarpment, and their silence on the subject became its own kind of confession, day after day after day.

On the ninth day they brought out the thing Orsen had known was coming since the first morning: his aliases. All of them. The false references, the forged medical certificates, the charter companies registered to shell names in three jurisdictions. A pilot who could not be traced, they argued, was not a credible witness. A pilot who had spent fifteen years erasing himself was either a criminal or a fantasist, and neither deserved to be believed.

The presiding judge, a narrow woman named Okafor with reading glasses on a chain, let them make the argument completely before she dismantled it.

"Counsel," she said, "the witness has confessed to every alias voluntarily, in open session, before this commission requested them. He surrendered his license without being asked. I would remind the panel that the men whose names appear in these ledgers were killed by an organization that also preferred that no one be traceable. We do not discredit a man for surviving by the same methods his accusers taught him." She looked over her glasses at Orsen. "Continue, Mr. Vale. In your own words. Whichever name those are."

Something in his chest unlocked that afternoon and never fully locked again.

* * *

Harker testified on the tenth day.

Orsen watched him walk in—a big man gone slightly stooped now, gray at the temples, wearing a suit that fit him like an apology. The quiet, methodical enforcer who had tracked them upriver through the monsoon stood at the witness table and put his hands flat on the wood, and told the truth in a voice like gravel settling.

He confirmed the order. Dated nineteen years back, signed above his rank by men who now sat on boards and received honors. He described what he'd been sent to the escarpment to erase: records, equipment, evidence of occupancy—and, when he found the graves, evidence of something worse than occupancy. He described reporting it upward and receiving new orders, and following them, and the arithmetic of his life ever since.

"I was paid to make people into nothing," he said. "I did it. Then I spent nineteen years trying to find out if the nothing could be undone." His eyes crossed the room and found Orsen, and held. "These three undid it. I helped at the end because they offered me the only exit I'd ever been shown. I'm not asking for mercy. I'm asking to be entered into the record as what I am—an order-taker. Let the record take me."

They took him at his word. Indictments issued against four executives within the week. Harker's own charges were read out too—obstruction, destruction of records, accessory after the fact—and he signed each acknowledgment without counsel, standing, like a man signing a manifest for a flight he intended to see through.

Orsen saw him once more afterward, in the corridor outside the hearing rooms, waiting for the escort that would take him into remand. Up close he looked smaller, lighter somehow, as though the testimony had been cargo he'd carried two decades and finally set down.

"You could have let me fall on that ice," Harker said. It wasn't gratitude exactly. It was accounting.

"You fell anyway," Orsen said. "Just slower."

Harker almost smiled. "Your license. I heard they're revoking it. The aliases made the whole certificate invalid—every rating under every name." He shrugged, a big slow movement. "For what it's worth, I checked your flying record under all five names. Not one violation. Not one forced landing you didn't walk away from. Best safety record I ever pulled, and it belongs to nobody."

"It'll belong to somebody again," Orsen said. "Someday. Under one name."

Harker studied him a moment longer, then nodded once, the way pilots nod to each other on strips—acknowledgment, departure, no promises. The escort came. Orsen watched him go and felt, unexpectedly, nothing like victory. Something more like weather changing.

* * *

The revocation notice came on blue paper, official letterhead, delivered to the hotel where he and Wren had been living out of duffel bags for six weeks. It was terse and total: all certificates, ratings, and privileges associated with any identity linked to the respondent were null and void, effective immediately. Reapplication would require—among other things—a legal name change, documented identity, and a full re-examination, written and practical, after a waiting period of no less than eighteen months.

Eighteen months. A year and a half of grounded days.

Wren found him on the hotel roof that evening, reading the letter for the fourth time. The capital sprawled below them, all sodium light and river haze, and somewhere beyond it the interior was already going gold with the end of summer, strips drying out, fish camps lighting their lamps. Every year of his adult life, this was the season he lived for. This year he would watch it from a rooftop.

"They took everything," he said, when she sat down beside him on the tar paper. He handed her the letter rather than summarizing it. She read fast, the way she read everything, dockside manifests and margin notes alike.

"Not everything," she said. "They took the paper. You still have the hands."

"The hands need a certificate to touch a yoke legally."

"Then you'll wait. You've waited longer for worse things." She folded the letter along its crease and set it down between them like something neutral, a shared tool. "You waited fifteen years to be anyone at all."

He looked at her. Below, the city went on making its noise, indifferent, enormous, full of people who knew their own names and used them carelessly, every day, in every doorway. He tried to imagine being one of them and couldn't—not yet—but the distance had closed some. That was the strange arithmetic of the last months: he had lost his invisibility and gained, in exchange, a self he recognized in mirrors.

"Do you regret it?" Wren asked. Quietly. Not testing him—she didn't test, she just asked things straight, the way her father's map had drawn straight lines across country everyone else called impassable. "Any of it. You could have flown out that first night. Burned the map. Nobody would ever have known there was a choice."

"I'd have known," Orsen said. "That's the problem with hiding. You can't hide from yourself. You just get very good at pretending the man in the mirror is a stranger." He turned the letter over, blank side up, and drew one finger across it. "Fifteen years ago I walked away from something I should have stayed for. I told myself survival was a skill. It is. But it's a skill like bailing—it keeps you afloat and takes you nowhere." He breathed out. "No. I don't regret it. I regret the years before it. Every one."

Wren nodded slowly, and then she asked the question he had been circling for days, the one he could feel her building toward the way you feel weather building past a ridge.

"What will you do now?" she said. "Everyone knows your face. Your photograph ran in the Herald next to the escarpment story—you, half-soaked, holding that ledger case like it was a baby. There are warrants with your old names on them in three districts, and a new name in the papers that isn't any of them. You can't go back to being a ghost, Orsen. Ghosts don't get re-licensed." She paused. "So what does Orsen Vale do?"

He was silent a long moment. A tug sounded its horn down on the river, low and patient, and the sound seemed to fill the space where his answer should have been.

"I don't know yet," he admitted. "That's the honest answer. Fifteen years of decisions took ten seconds each—run, hide, refuel, climb, cross. Now the decisions take longer than the weather." He looked at her, and the vulnerability in it didn't frighten him the way it once would have, and that fact itself was the strangest discovery of the whole year. "But I know what I want it to be. The same flying. Medicine, mail, the strips nobody else will serve. Except legally. Under one name. With a certificate that says Vale on it, even if it takes eighteen months and a stack of exams taller than the escarpment." He almost smiled. "And maybe not alone."

Wren went very still beside him, in the way she did when something landed true.

"I've been thinking," she said, and stopped, and started again, which was unlike her. "The commission released my father's map back to me yesterday. Officially. With a little ribbon on the envelope, like it was a diploma." Her mouth did something between a laugh and grief. "I've been looking at it all night. The margins, especially. His notes. Thirty years of them. And I realized—I know every strip in that interior. Every fuel cache, every tide window, every goat track. He taught me the whole country without ever taking me to it. I've been navigating it my whole life on paper." She turned to face him fully. "I'm done with paper."

The tug sounded again, closer now, heading for the harbor lights.

"There's a licensing school in Fort Jameson," Wren said. "Commercial navigation, float ratings, the whole course. Eighteen months." She let the number sit there between them, identical to his sentence, and watched him understand it. "We'd graduate the same week. If we started the same day."

Orsen looked at her—at the wind-toughened woman who had memorized coordinates as a child, bailed a leaking canoe through a monsoon, climbed a glacier route, and carried two hundred and six names out of the dark—and felt the future shift under him like ice deciding to move.

"You're asking to fly with me," he said.

"I'm asking to navigate," she corrected. "You can have the yoke. I want the map."

Below them the city lights burned on, and none of them knew his face except the ones who mattered, and somewhere north the strips were drying out, waiting, patient as promises kept.

"Then we start Monday," Orsen Vale said—and for the first time in fifteen years, saying his own name aloud felt like wheels lifting off a runway.
