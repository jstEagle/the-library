## 22. The Open Strip

The morning fog lay over Meridian Flats like a held breath, and Orsen Blackwood sat in the cockpit of a Cessna 180 that was not his—was, in fact, registered to a medical outreach cooperative with a name long enough to wrap around the fuselage twice—and waited for the sun to burn it off.

He had learned to wait differently now. Before, waiting had been a kind of vigilance: eyes on the tree line, ears tuned for tires on gravel that weren't supposed to be there, one hand always near the throttle. Waiting had been the posture of a man who expected to be found. Now he sat with his hands loose in his lap and watched the fog thin from milk to gauze to nothing at all, and what he felt underneath the old reflexes was something he still didn't have a good word for. Peace sounded too finished. Rest sounded too small.

Maybe it was just this: nobody was looking for him anymore, and so he had finally stopped looking over his shoulder, and the world without the shoulder-checking turned out to be enormous.

"Fuel's topped," Wren said, swinging up onto the wing strut with the easy economy she'd learned on the fish docks and never lost. She dropped into the right seat, unfolded her charts—actual paper charts, because she didn't trust the tablet alone, because her father hadn't trusted paper alone, and some inheritances ran deeper than sense—and began marking their route with a grease pencil. "Kestrel first, then the Flats, then upriver to Two Sisters if the strip's dried out. Doc Halloran says the insulin shipment has to be at Two Sisters by Thursday or he starts rationing."

"Then we'll be there Wednesday," Orsen said.

"We'll be there Wednesday," she agreed, and smiled, and the smile did what it always did now, which was rearrange something in his chest that he'd spent fifteen years nailing shut.

He ran the preflight out of habit rather than necessity—he'd done it twice already—but Wren let him, because she understood that ritual was how he prayed, if he prayed at all. Mixture rich. Fuel selector both. Controls free and correct. He read each item aloud under his breath like a man counting his blessings, which, when you got down to it, was exactly what he was doing.

"You know what I noticed?" Wren said as he taxied out. The strip at Meridian was gravel and drainage ditches and a windsock that had been patched with duct tape until the tape outweighed the fabric, and Orsen loved it more than any paved runway he'd ever seen. "You used to land facing away from town. Always. Took me three trips to figure it out."

Orsen eased the yoke forward and let the tail come up. "Habit."

"It wasn't habit. You wanted the plane pointed at the trees in case you needed to run." She said it without accusation, the way she said most things now, as though they were simply weather to be described. "You don't do it anymore. I checked."

He lifted off into the clearing gold of morning and did not answer, because there wasn't much to say. She was right. Somewhere in the months since the escarpment—since the testimony, since the hearings where he'd stood in a witness box under his own name and watched men in expensive suits learn who he was in real time—somewhere in all that, the pointing-away had stopped. You couldn't run from a courtroom full of journalists. You couldn't run from your own signature on an affidavit. After a while the body understood what the mind took longer to admit: there was no direction left that led away from himself, so he might as well point the airplane wherever the medicine needed to go.

* * *

They flew the river low, because Wren liked to see it and because Orsen liked showing it to her. The monsoon had come and gone and left the country washed and green, the water running high and brown and muscular between banks crowded with new growth. Below them, here and there, were the scars of that season: a raft of storm-flung logs jammed against a bend, a slide of raw earth where the gorge walls had shrugged off their skin, the roof of the flooded church standing out of a receded lake like a drowned man's last gesture.

Wren touched the glass over that one. Neither of them spoke. Some places you flew past quietly, the way you walked past graves.

At Kestrel Landing they put down on the dock-side strip and delivered crates marked with red crosses to a woman named Adele who ran the clinic out of a converted cannery. Adele had known Elias Marsh thirty years ago, before the estrangement, before the map, and she took Wren's hands across the counter and held them a moment longer than commerce required.

"He'd be proud," Adele said. "And terrified. Mostly proud."

"Mostly proud," Wren agreed, and her voice only caught a little.

They ate lunch on the dock with their boots hanging over the water, sharing a thermos of coffee that tasted faintly of kerosene because everything up here eventually did. A fishing boat went by, and the man at the tiller raised two fingers off the wheel—not a wave, exactly; an acknowledgment. *I see you. I know who you are.* Fifteen years ago that gesture would have sent ice down Orsen's spine. Now he raised two fingers back.

"You're staring," Wren said.

"I'm not."

"You are. You get this look every time somebody recognizes you. Like you're waiting for the trick." She tore a piece of bread and threw the crust to the gulls, who fell on it with the moral seriousness of lawyers. "Nobody's going to trick you, Orsen. They know you're the pilot who testified. Half of them think you're a hero. The other half think you're a fool who got lucky. Both halves will sell you fuel."

"That's the most comforting thing anyone's said to me all year."

"I'm very comforting." She leaned her shoulder into his, briefly, the way she'd begun to do sometime around the third month, without announcement or apology, as though it had always been part of the arrangement. "Two Sisters next?"

"Two Sisters next."

* * *

Two Sisters strip had dried out after all, barely—a ribbon of gravel above the river with a bog on either side, the kind of landing that asked politely whether you meant it. Orsen meant it. He set the tailwheel down soft as a kiss and rolled out past a cluster of cabins where the whole settlement seemed to be waiting, because in places like Two Sisters the arrival of the medicine plane was the closest thing to a holiday the calendar offered.

Doc Halloran met them with a hand truck and a grin missing two teeth. "Insulin. Cold chain intact? You kept it cold?"

"Cold as my ex-wife's heart," Orsen said, and Halloran laughed the way people laughed up here, fully and immediately, because humor was a supply item like any other and nobody wasted it.

While Wren supervised the offload—she had a gift for logistics that surprised no one who knew her father's margin notes, those meticulous little annotations in a hand like spider silk—Orsen wandered toward the tree line, where three kids were watching the airplane with the naked hunger he remembered feeling once, a lifetime ago, in another country, before the name Blackwood became one of several names he wore like coats.

One of the kids, a girl of maybe ten with a scrape healing on her chin, worked up the courage to approach. "Are you the one from the trial?"

"I'm the one from the trial."

"My gran says you found the dead people and gave them back their names."

Orsen crouched to her level. Up close her eyes were the flat gray of the river in October. "Your gran is mostly right. Your gran sounds smart."

"She says the company killed them and buried them and wrote them off as deserters, and that for thirty years everybody pretended, and that pretending is how evil gets comfortable." The recitation had the cadence of something heard many times. Then the cadence broke, and the child looked at him with plain curiosity. "Is that true?"

"It's true," Orsen said.

She nodded, satisfied, having apparently required only confirmation and not comfort, and ran back to her friends to report. Orsen stayed crouched a moment longer than he needed to, watching the river, feeling the weight and lightness of the thing she'd handed him. *For thirty years everybody pretended.* He had been one of the pretend-ers. Not of the massacre itself—he hadn't known, not then—but of everything around it: the way a man can make himself small enough, quiet enough, invisible enough, that the pretending never has to reach him. He had built a whole life inside that invisibility. It had kept him alive. It had also, he understood now, been its own kind of grave, and Elias Marsh's map had been the shovel that dug him out.

"Ready when you are," Wren called.

He stood, brushed the gravel dust from his knee, and went to fly.

* * *

They lost the light early that evening, the way northern places do, the sun dropping behind the coastal range like a coin into deep water. There was no question of pushing on to the next strip. There was a lodge at Two Sisters—a generous term for a bunkhouse with a stove—and Halloran insisted they stay, and Orsen heard himself say yes before the old machinery could even spin up its objections.

That was the thing. The objections still existed. He could feel them sometimes at dusk, the ghost-reflex whispering *move along, don't be seen, don't be known*. But they had grown faint, those voices, the way a radio station fades as you fly out of its range, and these days other signals came through clearer. Wren laughing at her own joke before she finished telling it. The specific silence of a cabin where nobody was listening at the door. Sleep that went all the way down.

After supper—moose stew, terrible coffee, Halloran's endless inventory of local ailments—Wren spread her father's map on the bunkhouse table under the lamp. She carried it everywhere now, though it was fragile as a moth wing, though she had long since memorized every line of it. She carried it the way other people carried photographs.

"They're reprinting it," she said. "The university. Archival facsimile, for the exhibit. They asked if I'd write the introduction."

"What are you going to say?"

She was quiet a moment, tracing the surveyor's stamp with one fingertip—the stamp from Camp Meridian, the camp that officially never existed, the stamp that had started everything. "I keep trying to write about him," she said, "and I keep writing about you instead. About the promise. And then I feel guilty, like the story isn't mine to tell that way. But it is, isn't it? The map only mattered because someone carried it. Thirty years he tried to get it somewhere, and it took a stranger with a rule about never staying two nights."

Orsen turned his cup slowly on the table. "I broke the rule."

"You broke every rule you had," she said. "That's the whole story. That's the introduction."

Outside, the wind moved through the spruces with the sound of a long exhalation, and somewhere far off a wolf said one sentence to the dark and got no reply. Orsen looked at the map—at the penciled flight lines Wren had added over the months, the strips they served together, the little grease-pencil crosses marking deliveries made—and saw that without quite deciding to, they had drawn a new chart on top of the old one. The escarpment was still there, would always be there, but around it now was a web of ordinary mercies: insulin and antibiotics and suture kits and vaccine cold-chains, the unglamorous cargo of a life that had chosen a direction.

"Wren," he said.

"Hm."

"The winter schedule. The co-op wants to add the upper river runs. Longer stays. Overnights at some of the posts." He paused. "I told them we'd take it."

She looked up. In the lamplight her face did something complicated and then settled into the expression he had come to trust more than any instrument reading. "We," she repeated.

"If you want. It's your call as much as mine. More."

"I've been living out of a duffel since the dock," she said. "You're asking me if I want a duffel with a better view."

"Something like that."

"Then yes." She folded the map along its old creases, careful, reverent, and slid it into the oilcloth sleeve. "But I'm navigating. You fly like a man who argues with rivers."

"Rivers argue back."

"Exactly my point."

* * *

They left Two Sisters at first light, climbing out of the river valley into air so clean it felt newly manufactured, the peaks to the west wearing their glaciers like old scars that had finally stopped bleeding. At six thousand feet the world opened beneath them—forest and water and the thin silver threads of strips cut into the green, each one a place where somebody was waiting for something, and for the first time in his life Orsen Blackwood was not afraid of being the one who arrived.

He thought about the girl's question. *Is that true?* Children asked it so easily, as though truth were a door that simply needed pushing. Adults knew better—or thought they did. He had spent half his life believing that safety lived behind locked doors, false names, wheels pointed at the trees. Then a dying man had pressed a map into his hands and asked him for one impossible thing, and the doors had all blown open at once, and what came through wasn't the destruction he'd feared. It was a woman with her father's eyes and a talent for navigation. It was a courtroom where the dead got their names back. It was this: a legal airplane, a legitimate cargo, a route drawn in grease pencil, a seat beside him filled.

"Meridian Flats, then home?" Wren said, head bent over the chart.

Home. She'd said it casually, weeks ago, referring to the hangar apartment at the co-op base, and he had gone very quiet, and she had noticed, because she noticed everything, and had said nothing, because she understood everything. Now she said it the same way again, testing nothing, proving nothing. Just a word. Just a direction.

"Flats, then home," he said.

The Cessna droned north into the widening day. Below, the river ran the way it had always run, indifferent and faithful, carrying everything toward the sea. Orsen settled back against the seat and let his hands rest light on the yoke, and did not count the nights ahead, because there was no longer any need. Home was not a place you hid in. He knew that now. It was a heading you held—one degree at a time, through weather, through doubt, through all the long gray miles—until the ground rose up to meet you somewhere you had finally, deliberately, chosen to land.
