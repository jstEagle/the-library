## 1. Salt and Debt

The salt flats don't forgive, and neither do I. That's the job description, more or less — the one they printed on the tin badge I've worn for eleven years: *Mara Marchetti, Desert Ranger. Will pursue. Will not forgive.*

Idris Vale had a nine-hour head start and the good sense to travel at night, which meant he was either smart or scared, and in my experience the two wear the same boots.

I found his first camp at dawn, or what passed for dawn out here — the sun coming up over the flats like a coin flipped by a careless god, turning everything white and blinding and flat as a lie. His fire ring was cold but tidy. He'd banked it properly, the way you do if you learned fire from someone who cared whether you burned to death. A thief with manners. The ledger was gone from my saddlebag before I'd even missed it, along with three days of rations and, insultingly, nothing else. He'd left my sidearm. He'd left my water.

He'd taken exactly one thing, and that told me more than any trail ever could.

* * *

You have to understand what the ledger is. People think a ranger station ledger is receipts and patrol logs, and mostly they're right. But the back pages — the ones we're not supposed to copy, the ones the consortium's auditors collect every quarter like tithes — those pages are debts. Every ranger carries one. The cost of your training, your gear, your keep, all of it signed against your name at an interest rate that would make a loan shark blush. You work it off or you die working, and either way the arithmetic comes out fine for them.

Mine stood at four thousand, two hundred and sixty credits. Eleven years of patrols, rescues, and burying people the desert killed, and I'd paid down exactly none of it, because the interest moved faster than I did. It's a clever system. You can't quit, because quitting converts the debt to a warrant. You can't die cheaply, because your body gets sold to cover the balance. And you can't run, because running means becoming the thing you hunt.

Which is why Idris Vale stealing that ledger wasn't theft. It was surgery. He'd cut out the only organ keeping me on their table.

* * *

My father's name was Tomas Marchetti, and when I was seven years old I saw it written on a piece of paper that smelled of brine and old smoke.

This is the part I don't tell anyone, because telling it makes it small, and it has never once been small. My mother kept a scrap — torn from something official, a manifest or a crew list, the ink faded to the color of weak tea. *T. Marchetti, bosun's mate.* She kept it folded inside her prayer book, which she stopped opening after the Meridian went down, because God had clearly stopped reading His mail.

The Meridian sailed out of Saltmere Harbor the autumn I turned six, carrying a fortune in salvage rights and thirty-one men who believed in the company that owned her. She never made port again. The company said storm. The company said piracy. The company said a great many things, in that smooth voice companies use when they're reading from a script someone else wrote, and then the company paid out settlements so thin my mother joked — once, badly, before she stopped joking altogether — that they'd valued Tomas Marchetti at less than the price of his boots.

Thirty-one names. I know this because my mother said it every night like a rosary, and after she died I said it too, though I'd lost most of them by then, the way you lose anything you carry without a case. But I never lost his. *Tomas Marchetti.* Seven years old, holding a scrap of paper, learning that grief has a paperwork problem.

So no, I didn't become a ranger for justice. Justice is a word the consortium uses in its advertising. I became a ranger because it was the only lawful way to get close to the people who kept the records, and the records were where my father lived now — filed, stamped, archived, erased. I spent eleven years learning where the consortium keeps its dead.

And then a thief with tidy fires walked into my station and stole the one document that could tell me why.

* * *

By midday the heat came up off the flats in visible waves, and I tracked Vale across ground that shouldn't hold tracks at all. But salt remembers. There's a crust over the brine beneath, and a man's weight cracks it in a pattern different from wind or beast, and if you've walked these flats as long as I have, you read the breaks like handwriting. His handwriting said: steady pace, no panic, knows where he's going.

That worried me. Thieves run *from*. This one ran *toward*.

Toward the cliffs, as it happened. The old sea cliffs at the western edge of the flats, the ones the charts marked with a warning symbol and no explanation — *unstable, avoid at high tide*, which is chart-speak for *we don't know and won't admit it*. Nobody went there. The consortium had posted a survey restriction on the whole coastline twenty years back, and restrictions like that are how you know where the bodies are buried.

I watered my horse at a seep I won't describe, because the last ranger who wrote about water sources in his log got reassigned to a place with no water at all, and I ate cold beans and thought about my father's name on a scrap of paper, and I hated Idris Vale with the clean, efficient hatred of a woman who has budgeted for it.

Here's the ugly truth, since we're being honest with ourselves out here alone on the salt: I didn't want justice for the Meridian. Not really. Justice is slow and communal and belongs to everyone, and I have never been good at sharing. What I wanted was the arrest. I wanted Vale in irons, wanted the ledger recovered, wanted the debt closed — *his* debt, whatever he owed, converted into my credit, my balance zeroed, my name struck from the back pages. I wanted to walk into the station and put the ledger on the desk and watch the clerk's face do arithmetic it didn't enjoy.

I needed this arrest the way a drowning woman needs a boat, and I'd have told you it was about justice, because that's what drowning women say.

* * *

Near dusk the wind changed, and the flats did something I'd never seen in eleven years.

The tide went out. Not the ordinary breathing of the sea — I mean it *left*, hauling itself backward off the coast like something startled, draining channels I didn't know existed, exposing mudflats and wreck timber and the ribs of boats that had died generations ago. The charts called the western coast tidal, but this was a strangled tide, a freak low, the kind that happens maybe once in a decade when the moon and the wind conspire, and the whole world smelled of iodine and rot and old, old salt.

And the cliffs opened.

There — where the survey restriction sat fat and unexplained on every map — a cave mouth stood exposed below the tideline, black and tall as a chapel door, dripping. Water still sheeted down its lip. Around it, half-buried in the mud, lay timbers. Not driftwood. *Ship's* timbers, scarred and tarred, and a block of rigging pulley fused into a lump of coral, and something long and pale that I refused, at first, to look at directly.

I should have radioed it in. Procedure said radio it in. Procedure said a great many things, and I had spent eleven years following procedure straight toward a debt I could never pay, and standing there on the drowned land with my father's ghost rising up out of the mud like breath, I found that procedure had finally run out of road.

Vale's tracks went into the cave.

Of course they did. Steady pace, no panic, knows where he's going. He hadn't been fleeing me at all. He'd been leading me — or leading himself — to the one place on this coast the consortium forbade anyone to look.

I drew my sidearm, because habit is the last thing to drown, and I started down across the wet sand toward the open door.

Behind me, far out on the white flats, I saw lights. Three of them, maybe four, moving fast and in formation, the way vehicles move when they belong to a fleet and a fleet belongs to money.

They were coming for the same door I was walking through.

I went in.
