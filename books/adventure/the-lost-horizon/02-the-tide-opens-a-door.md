## 2. The Tide Opens a Door

The sea did something impossible at dawn, and I nearly missed it because I was busy hating a man.

I'd been on Idris Vale's trail for eleven hours. Eleven hours of salt crust in my boot seams, of wind that tasted like old pennies, of following the neat, infuriating footprints of a man who walked like he had nowhere to be and still outpaced me at every turn. He'd robbed the ranger station two nights ago — not the strongbox, mind you, which held actual money, but the ledger. The debt records. My debt record, with my father's name inked beside mine in a column that never shrank no matter how many years I served it off.

So yes. Hating him was easy. It kept me warm.

I'd tracked him to the cliffs at the eastern lip of the flats, where the salt gives way to black rock and the ocean chews at the land like it holds a grudge. His prints ended at the cliff base, and there they should have stopped, because there was nowhere to go. Sheer stone above, open water ahead. A cornered man's ending, and I allowed myself one small, ungenerous smile about it.

Then the sea pulled back.

Not the ordinary breathing of tide — I know that rhythm like my own pulse. This was something else, a long, strange exhale, as if the whole ocean had leaned away from the coast to look at something. The water dropped and kept dropping, farther than any tide chart I'd ever memorized, until it exposed a shelf of seabed glistening under the early light, and then — where I'd have sworn on my badge there was solid cliff — a mouth.

A cave entrance, tall as a chapel door, framed in weed and barnacle and dripping dark. It hadn't been hidden. It had simply never been *there* before, or never for anyone standing where I stood, at this hour, in this year, in this particular temper of the sea. The water lay quivering far below it like a held breath.

And from inside that darkness, faint but unmistakable: footsteps. Idris Vale, going in.

Every sensible instinct I owned said wait. Tides that behave strangely once behave strangely twice, and caves that open can close. But sensible instincts had gotten me nothing for three years except a ledger that wouldn't balance and a name I couldn't cross out. So I did what I always do when sense and want disagree.

I followed the want.

* * *

The cave swallowed sound first. My boots went quiet on wet stone, and my own breathing turned loud and embarrassing, and somewhere ahead Vale's steps echoed back to me doubled, tripled, like the cave was mocking me with copies of the man I couldn't catch.

Then the floor began to rise, dry stone replacing slick, and the air changed — went stale and mineral and old, the smell of a place the sea visits but doesn't own. I unclipped my lantern and thumbed it on, and the light found the walls, and I forgot, briefly and completely, that I was hunting anyone.

Murals. Hundreds of them. Painted directly onto the rock in pigments that had gone soft with age — ochre and crushed shell-white and a blue so deep it looked wet. Ships, mostly. A great three-masted vessel rendered again and again, sometimes proud on open water, sometimes listing, once broken-backed on a reef with tiny figures spilling into painted waves. Around the ships swirled hands — dozens of hands, reaching, clasped, raised, some pressed flat as if taking an oath. And names. Names written in careful script beneath each hand, hundreds of them, columns upon columns climbing toward the cave ceiling where my lantern couldn't reach.

I stood there like an idiot with my mouth open. Ranger Marchetti, who prided herself on never being surprised by anything uglier than a sandstorm, stood in a painted cathedral of the drowned and forgot her own name for a full ten seconds.

"Mind the third step," said a voice. "It lies."

I drew my sidearm before my brain caught up, which is standard procedure and also useless against old women. She sat in an alcove to my left, wrapped in a coat that might once have been navy wool and was now mostly patchwork, holding a lamp of her own and a pencil worn down to a stub. Seventy if she was a day, sharp-eyed, entirely unbothered by the gun.

"The third step," she repeated, "lies. It looks solid. It is not. Ask me how I know."

"How do you—" I holstered the weapon, mostly out of embarrassment. "Who are you?"

"Odile." She said it the way other people say their address. "And you're the ranger chasing the thief. He came through an hour ago. Went left at the ribs."

"At the—"

She didn't answer. She lifted her lamp and gestured deeper into the cave, and my light followed hers, and that's when I saw them.

Bones. Not human ones — ship bones. Ribs of blackened timber arcing up out of the cave floor in a curve forty feet across, the skeleton of a hull so large it made the murals around it make sudden, terrible sense. The Meridian. I knew the story the way every kid on this coast knew it: the treasure ship that vanished with a fortune and thirty-one souls, sometime before my father was born, lost between one port and the next, mourned in songs nobody sang anymore. Salvage men had hunted it for a century. Consortium charters had dragged sonar across these waters for decades and found nothing but sand.

Because it wasn't in the water. It was in here, behind a door the sea only opened when it felt like it, dry as a sermon, waiting.

"You've known about this," I said. My voice came out strange. "How long?"

"Forty-one years," Odile said. "Since I was a girl your daughter's age, if you had a daughter, which you don't, because you're married to a ledger. Don't argue. I can tell. It's in how you stand."

"I don't have time for—"

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You have exactly one kind of time, and it's the kind spent chasing a man through a miracle without looking at it. Go on, then. Left at the ribs. He'll be at the manifest chamber by now, trying to pry open a case he hasn't the patience for. And ranger—" I was already moving, which she clearly expected. Her voice followed me through the dark, unhurried as tide. "When you catch him, ask yourself what a man steals when he steals a list of names. Thieves steal gold. Only the guilty steal lists."

* * *

I caught him at the manifest chamber, all right. Caught him red-handed, crouched before a flat stone slab set into the wall like a tomb door, his lockpicks scattered on the ground where he'd apparently thrown them in frustration, both palms pressed against the stone as if he could push his way through sheer wanting.

He didn't startle when my light hit him. He just looked over his shoulder, and for the first time since the robbery, Idris Vale looked tired instead of amused.

"Took you long enough," he said.

"Don't move."

"I haven't moved in an hour. There's a mechanism. A counterweight, maybe, or a tide-lock — Odile would know, she knows everything, it's frankly unsettling." He tapped the stone. Behind it, faintly, I heard something shift — water, deep below, moving through channels cut by human hands. "This is the manifest chamber, Ranger. Everything the murals promise is behind this door. Every name. Every signature. Including the signatures of the men who put the Meridian on that reef and put thirty-one people in the water."

My heart did something I didn't authorize. "That's a legend."

"It's a ledger," he said, and smiled without any humor in it at all. "You of all people should respect those. The difference is this one balances."

I leveled the lantern at him properly, fell back into the shape of the job — wrist locked, weight forward, the whole liturgy of arrest. Three years of discipline funneled into one clean sentence: "Idris Vale, you're under station arrest for theft of official records. Turn around, hands behind your back."

He turned around. He even offered his wrists. That was the problem — he offered them too easily, like a man stepping aside to let you see what was behind him. Which was the door. Which was the names.

"You don't want to do this," he said quietly.

"I've wanted to do this for three years."

"No. You've wanted to *finish*, and someone told you finishing looks like this." His eyes flicked past me, back toward the ribs, the murals, the old woman with her lamp. "You read the walls coming in. I watched your light stop on them. You stood there for ten seconds and forgot why you came. I know that feeling, Marchetti. I've had it since I was nine years old."

Somewhere far below us, the sea shifted — a long groan through the stone, the sound of a held breath beginning to release. Odile's voice drifted up the passage, calm as Sunday: "Tide's turning, children. Whatever you're deciding, decide faster."

Vale held my gaze. "The fleet will find this place within a day. Brannoch's salvage charter — you've seen their lights on the horizon same as I have. When they come, they won't read the names. They'll burn them, along with the woman who kept them alive for forty years, and they'll call it salvage law." He flexed his bound wrists, gentle, not testing. "Arrest me. Drag me out. Write it up nice and clean. Or put the gun away and help me open a door."

The tide groaned again, closer now, hungry.

And I — Mara Marchetti, who had crossed eleven hours of salt flat for the pleasure of putting this man in irons — heard myself say:

"What's behind the door that matters more than the arrest?"

He almost laughed. "Your father's name," he said. "It's on the crew list. And it's on the other list too — the one the consortium paid to erase from every archive on the coast. The one that says who killed him."

The third step lied under my boot as I took it, and I went down hard on one knee in the dark, and neither of us moved to help the other up, because we were both suddenly kneeling at the same altar, and the sea was coming home.
