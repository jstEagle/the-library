## 3. Six Tides

There are three kinds of cornered, and I've been all of them. There's the kind where you still have a door — that's not cornered, that's Tuesday. There's the kind where you don't, but your pursuer is tired or stupid or both, and you can talk your way through the gap before they remember they're holding a gun. And then there's the third kind: the kind where the sea has closed every exit but one, and the one remaining exit leads directly into the business end of a ranger's rifle.

I was in the third kind.

The tide had come back while I was inside the cave, filling the passage behind me with green-grey water that moved like something alive and hungry, and Mara Marchetti stood at the mouth of it with her boots planted on wet stone and her rifle held low and easy, the way you hold a thing you've carried so long it's stopped being a weapon and become a limb.

"Idris Vale," she said. "You're under arrest."

"You say that like it's a formality," I said. "Like there's a version of this where I come quietly."

"There's a version of this where you drown," she said. "It's my second-favorite."

"And your first?"

She didn't answer, which was its own answer. She wanted the ledger back. She wanted me in irons. What she wanted underneath those things — what she'd crossed forty miles of salt flat to get — was something she hadn't said out loud yet, maybe hadn't even admitted to herself. But I'd spent thirty years reading people who had reasons for the things they did, and I could see it sitting behind her eyes like a coin at the bottom of a well.

So instead of running — because there was nowhere to run, because the water was rising and the old woman in the deeper chamber was watching us both with the patience of someone who had seen a great many young fools make their choices — I sat down on a dry ledge, deliberately, insolently, like a man settling in for a long conversation.

"You might as well shoot me," I said, "or you might as well listen. Those are your options now. The water took the third one."

Her rifle didn't waver. "Talk fast, then."

"I will. But first, a question." I nodded past her, toward the murals on the outer wall — the ones Odile had painted and repainted across four decades, ships and names and dates rendered in pigments ground from the cave itself. "Do you know whose ship that is?"

"I know it's a wreck."

"It's the *Meridian*." I watched the name land. It landed somewhere; I couldn't tell where. "Forty-one years ago she went down off this reef with a cargo of gold bullion and a crew of nineteen, and the world was told it was a storm. It wasn't a storm. It was nine men with dynamite and a salvage contract signed before the ship ever left port. They blew her hull open in calm water, picked the survivors off the reef like crabs off a rock, and split the fortune. And every record of it — every manifest, every name, every court filing — was bought and burned, except for the copies that ended up in here."

Mara's face did something small and controlled. Rangers are trained out of their faces early; hers had been trained harder than most. But I saw the crack go through it anyway, quick as a hairline fracture in glass.

"Why do you care?" she said. "You're a thief. You steal ledgers."

"I steal *records*," I said. "There's a difference, though I'll grant it's a fine one. Ledgers are how the powerful keep score of everyone else. Manifests are how the dead keep score of the powerful. I've spent my life moving between those two documents, and lately—" I stopped. Lately what? Lately I'd been having dreams about a burning office and a boy's hands shaking around a can of lamp oil? No. Not yet. Not first. "Lately I've been looking for one document in particular. And I found it three days ago, in a station ledger, in a drawer it had no business being in."

"That ledger belongs to the service."

"That ledger contains a page that doesn't. A page filed by a man named Corvus Hale, twenty-nine years ago, recording a payment made to a boy of fourteen to set a fire in an archive annex — and recording, in the same hand, the boy's name." I spread my hands. "Mine."

The rifle came up an inch. Not aimed at me — aimed at itself, almost, at the idea of her. "You burned the archive?"

"The consortium burned the archive. I lit the lamp oil, because a man with a soft voice and a hard grip told me that if I didn't, he'd find my mother, who owed him money, and collect in ways I won't describe to a woman holding a rifle." I heard my own voice go flat and careful, the way it does when the truth gets too heavy to carry any other way. "I was fourteen. I've been paying for it ever since — not in prison, which would have been cleaner, but in the currency the powerful prefer: silence. Twenty-nine years of doing their errands, fetching their papers, stealing back the evidence they were too careless to destroy the first time. Until two years ago, when I found out what the errands were *for*. And I started keeping copies."

"In the station ledger."

"In the station ledger, because it was the last place anyone would look — a page sewn into a debt record nobody reads, in a ranger station nobody visits, guarded by rangers too honest to imagine corruption and too bored to inventory their own drawers. Except one ranger read her own debt record. One ranger with a dead father and a chip on her shoulder the size of the salt flats." I tilted my head at her. "You want to know why your father's name isn't in any archive? Because I'm the man who helped erase it. And I've spent two years trying to un-erase it, and the only complete copy left in the world is sitting in the next chamber over, catalogued in an old woman's handwriting, waiting for someone to ask."

Silence. The water hissed against stone. Somewhere deeper in the cave, Odile turned a page — I could hear it, that dry whisper, the sound of forty years of devotion moving one sheet at a time.

Mara lowered the rifle. Not all the way. Just enough that it stopped being a threat and started being a weight she was tired of holding.

"My father," she said, "was Elias Marchetti. He was a deckhand."

"He was a deckhand," I agreed. "Nineteen years old. He's line eleven on the crew manifest. He's also line six on the witness list — the list of men ashore who saw the dynamite being loaded and were paid to forget it. Your father took the money. Then he couldn't live with taking it. He wrote to the harbormaster twice. Both letters are in here. Both letters are why he died — because the second one was intercepted, and nine days later Elias Marchetti fell down a staircase that had never hurt anyone before."

That was the crack. That was where the glass gave. She didn't cry — I don't think this woman cries where anyone can see it, possibly including herself — but she sat down on the ledge opposite me, slowly, like her knees had been given notice, and she looked at her hands for a long time.

"They told me it was an accident," she said finally. "They told me that when I was seven."

"They told everyone everything was an accident. That's the whole architecture of it. Storms, accidents, fires, misfilings. Nine men built a cathedral out of the word 'accident,' and they've been worshipping in it for forty years."

"And you?" Her eyes came up, and they were the coldest thing in that cave, colder than the water. "What are you in it for? Absolution? You just confessed to helping burn the proof."

"I'm in it for the same thing you are," I said. "I just got here by the wrong road. You want justice for your father. I want the ledger page that says a fourteen-year-old boy was coerced — because that page is the only thing standing between me and the rest of my life being theirs. We're not so different, Ranger. We're both people the record failed. The difference is you kept your badge and I kept my hands clean of nothing."

"Don't flatter yourself."

"I never do. It's bad for business."

She almost smiled. Almost. It flickered and died like a moth against glass, but I counted it, because I count everything, and because in my experience an almost-smile from a woman like Mara Marchetti is worth more than a real one from anyone else.

Then the light at the cave mouth changed.

Not the slow amber shift of evening — something else, something mechanical. I crawled to the edge of the passage and looked out, and there they were: lights on the horizon, strung across the darkening water like a necklace of white fire. Three ships. Four. Running lights, deck floods, the orange smudge of a work barge under way. A salvage fleet, coming in with the dawn, and I knew whose flag they flew because I'd stolen enough of their correspondence to recognize the silhouette.

"They're here early," I said.

"Who's here?"

"The consortium. Or their dogs. Brannoch's fleet — I'd bet the manifest itself on it." I turned back to her. "They know the tide exposed the cave. They've known for weeks; that's why I came when I came. They let me lead them here, because I'm more useful as a compass than as a corpse. And at dawn, they're going to come into this cave and take every page in it, and burn them properly this time, and the Meridian will be a storm again, forever."

Mara stood. She looked out at the lights for a long moment, and I watched her do the arithmetic every ranger does — duty on one side, law on the other, and the widening gap between them where a person used to stand.

"How long until dawn?" she asked.

"Six hours."

"How long until high tide seals the deep passages?"

"Four."

She shouldered her rifle. "Then we have four hours to move the manifests somewhere they can't be burned, and two hours to figure out how to stop a fleet with one rifle and a thief." She glanced back at me, and there it was again — not trust, nothing so generous, but the cold clear recognition of two people chained to the same wall. "Don't make me regret this, Vale."

"I never make anyone regret anything," I said. "I only make them wish they'd listened sooner."

Behind us, in the dark, Odile struck a lamp alight, and the flame caught the murals — the ship, the names, the nineteen souls rendered in ochre and salt — and made them burn without burning, which is more than the consortium will ever manage, and more than I deserve, and exactly as much as the truth requires.

The tide was already turning. We had four hours, and the fleet had dawn, and somewhere between the two, one of us was going to learn what the other was really worth.

I meant to find out first.
