## 5. First Blood on the Reef

The tide came back at four in the morning, and with it came the fleet.

I heard them before I saw them — the deep-throated cough of diesel engines carrying across the flats the way sound carries over salt, flat and honest and impossible to hide from. I'd been awake anyway. I was always awake now. Sleep had become a negotiation I kept losing to the memory of Odile's candle-lit face as she read my father's name off a page that should have burned forty years ago.

*Elias Marchetti, bosun's mate. Meridian.*

Three words. They had rearranged my entire life like a hand of cards thrown in the air.

From the ridge above the cave mouth — which the sea had politely closed again behind us, swallowing our secret whole — I counted lights. Four vessels anchored beyond the outer reef, riding their lamps like lantern-fish. Not salvage boats, not really. Salvage boats look hungry and improvised, all welded scrap and bad paint jobs. These were white-hulled, purpose-built, and flying no colors at all, which is what rich people do when they want to steal something without technically being seen doing it.

The consortium hadn't wasted any time. Of course they hadn't. Forty years of silence bought and paid for, and then one old woman and one dead ledger go and start talking.

"They'll send a diver first," Idris said beside me. He'd appeared out of the dark the way he always did, quiet as a debt collector. "Scout dive. Chart the reef, find the entrance, report back. Then the cutting charges come out."

"You know a lot about how fleets work for a man who robs ranger stations."

"I know a lot about how everything works," he said. "It's my one marketable skill."

Below us, down in the cave where the tide permitted, Odile was moving through her archive by lamplight, wrapping the manifest pages in oilcloth with the tenderness other people reserve for infants. She'd refused to leave. We hadn't asked her to twice. There are arguments you lose just by watching someone's hands.

I trained my father's old field glasses on the nearest vessel. A skiff had launched from its shadow — small, fast, running dark except for a single stern light they'd forgotten or didn't care about. One man aboard, maybe two. It slid toward the outer reef and stopped where the water went from black to that particular bruised teal that means coral underneath.

"Scout," Idris confirmed.

"Then we have maybe an hour before this stops being a chase and starts being a war."

"And whose side does a ranger take in a war?" he asked, and there it was, the question I'd been chewing since the tide opened the cliff like a wound. Because here was the thing nobody tells you about the law: it's a building, and buildings have owners, and when you find out who holds the deed, every corridor you ever walked proudly suddenly looks different.

I'd spent eleven years believing my badge pointed at truth the way a compass points north. Eleven years writing up debt records, chasing men like Idris Vale across salt flats, telling myself the ledger was justice because justice had a nice font and an official stamp. And now the same machinery that stamped my badge had erased my father from history so thoroughly that even *I* hadn't known his name belonged anywhere but a grave marker.

The skiff's engine died. A shape went over the side — one diver, alone, lamp off, trusting the moon.

"Well," I said. "There's my answer."

* * *

Getting down to the waterline took twenty minutes of scrambling I felt in my knees for the next three days. The flats at night are treacherous in a way the daytime never admits — the salt crust forms bridges over brine pools that will swallow a boot and the leg inside it, and the whole surface glitters under the moon like it's daring you. Idris moved ahead of me like he'd been born down here, which, knowing him, he possibly had.

"There," he whispered, pointing.

The scout's skiff bobbed at the edge of the reef, tied off to a coral head with a line that would've embarrassed a child. The diver's bubbles rose in silver strings thirty yards out, marking his slow circuit of the reef wall.

I'd sabotaged boats before. It's not glamorous work. You don't cut fuel lines in a dramatic shower of sparks; you loosen a hose clamp a quarter turn and let physics do the paperwork. You crack a seacock fitting just enough that it weeps instead of gushes, so the boat dies slowly, somewhere inconvenient, ideally after its owner has committed to a plan. Sabotage is mostly patience and a multitool.

Idris held the skiff steady while I worked. Fuel line: loosened. Bilge pump float: jammed down, so it would read dry while taking on water. The little chart case lashed under the console — I hesitated over it, then took it. Maps of the reef, pencil-marked with soundings. Evidence, if evidence still meant anything. Souvenir, if it didn't.

"That's the third thing you've stolen this week," Idris murmured. "Starting to worry about you."

"Occupational hazard."

We were easing away from the skiff when the diver surfaced.

He came up ten feet from us, silent, lamp hooded, and for one long moment the three of us hung in the dark water looking at each other like strangers surprised in a doorway. His mask reflected the moon. My knife was in my hand before I'd decided to draw it, and I saw him see it, and I watched him make the calculation every experienced man makes: *how much is this job worth?*

He raised both palms slowly off his gear. Smart. Then he did the un-smart thing, the human thing — he looked past me, at Idris, and his eyes went wide behind the glass.

"Vale?" he said, muffled through his regulator, and the name carried underwater the way names do, straight into the bones.

Because of course. Of course the fleet knew Idris Vale. He'd told us he'd been coerced into the original crime as a boy, that the ledger proved it, that the men who ran the consortium had owned him since before he could grow a beard. But hearing it confirmed by a stranger's recognition — seeing the way the diver's body language shifted from *threat* to *reporting in* — made it real in a way confession hadn't.

I put the knife flat against his chest strap, not his throat. "You saw nothing tonight. You found nothing. Your boat has engine trouble and you're going to swim back and say so."

He nodded. People nod beautifully when they're cold and outgunned.

Then the searchlight hit us.

It came off the lead vessel, a white column that turned the whole bay into noon, and pinned us to the reef like specimens. And a voice rolled across the water from a loudhailer, amplified and enormous and — this was the part that stopped my heart mid-beat — *familiar*. Familiar the way a childhood kitchen is familiar. Familiar the way a scar is.

"Marchetti."

* * *

Captain Brannoch. Commander Brannoch, rather — the promotion had come with the money, I supposed. Fifteen years ago he'd pinned my probationary badge on himself, told me I had the best instincts of any recruit he'd trained, and taught me that a ranger's word was the only currency that never devalued. He'd stood at my father's grave — or what I'd thought was my father's grave — and said, "Your old man served the coast well, girl. So will you."

Now he stood on the foredeck of a ship with no flag, wearing civilian clothes that cost more than my annual salary, and the loudhailer made his voice sound like a stranger's.

"Step away from the diver, Marchetti. You're interfering with a licensed survey operation."

"Since when do you work for the consortium?" I shouted back, and my voice cracked on it, which I hated.

"Since the consortium employs two hundred people in this district," he called down, reasonable as a weather report. "Since they pay port fees and harbor wages and keep this coast alive. Somebody has to be practical, Mara. You always were the romantic one."

The diver had slipped away during the exchange, swimming hard for the fleet. Good. One less hostage in whatever this was becoming.

"Brannoch." I waded closer to the rocks so he could see my face in the searchlight. I wanted him to see it. "Do you know what sank out here forty years ago? Do you know what your employers did?"

"I know what the record says. Storm loss. Tragic."

"The record is a lie somebody paid you to keep reading."

Something crossed his face then — something quick, quickly buried. I'd seen that expression before, on suspects, on witnesses, on myself in mirrors. The expression of a man who knows more than he's saying and has arranged his life around not saying it.

"Mara," he said, quieter now, loudhailer lowered, just his voice carrying over the water the way voices do between people who used to matter to each other. "Come aboard. Bring the thief. We'll talk like professionals. You're standing in open water next to a wanted man, defending a wreck claim with no legal standing whatsoever, and I am asking you — as the officer who swore you in — to stop making this harder than it needs to be."

And God help me, some animal part of me *wanted* to. That's the seduction nobody warns you about. Not gold, not rank — just the chance to un-become the person this week has made you. To climb aboard, shake his hand, hear the old authority in his voice, and let fifteen years of trust paper over the fact that the ship beneath his feet was built on my father's grave.

Idris's hand found my shoulder in the dark. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. He'd spent his whole life being the man legitimate people were asked to walk away from, and he knew exactly what Brannoch was offering, and he was letting me choose it if I needed to.

That, more than anything, decided me.

"No," I called up. "But thank you for the offer, Captain. Here's mine: pull your fleet out of this bay by morning tide, or I swear on my badge — the one you gave me — that every name in that manifest gets read aloud, in public, until this whole coast knows what your practical men did to earn their harbor fees."

Silence. The searchlight held us. Somewhere out on the reef, the sabotaged skiff gave a soft metallic groan as it began, right on schedule, to settle.

"You don't have the manifest," Brannoch said at last. "You have stories. Old woman's stories."

"Then you have nothing to fear."

His jaw worked. And then he did the thing that told me everything — he turned away from me and spoke to someone behind him on the deck, low, and the someone answered, and the answer made Brannoch flinch before he caught it.

When he turned back, the warmth was gone entirely, and what remained was worse than anger. It was procedure.

"By dawn we'll have the reef mapped and the entrance located," he said. "Whatever you think you're protecting, Marchetti, it won't survive dynamite. Go home. Whatever home means to you now."

The searchlight cut out. The dark rushed back in, and with it the sound of the fleet settling on its anchors like wolves lying down around a kill — patient, unhurried, certain.

Idris and I swam for shore in silence. Halfway there, treading water, he finally spoke.

"He knew your father's name was on the manifest," he said quietly. "Didn't he. Before you did."

I didn't answer. The answer was already true, and we both knew it, and somewhere behind us the tide was turning, and in six hours the sun would come up on a reef full of men with drills and charges and orders, and an old woman in a cave with nothing but lamplight and oilcloth and forty years of names between them and the sea.

"We need to move the archive," I said. "Tonight. Before they blast."

"Through the flooded passages?"

"Through the flooded passages."

Idris laughed once, short and joyless. "You know," he said, striking out for shore, "most rangers would've taken the ride on the nice ship."

"Most rangers," I said, "never learned their fathers' names from a thief."
