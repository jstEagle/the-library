## 7. Third Tide, Rising

I have kept this cave for forty years, and in all that time the sea has been my only adversary worth respecting. It comes twice a day, patient as a debt collector, and twice a day I climb to the high shelf with my lantern and my ledgers and I wait it out like an old woman waiting out a rude guest. I know its moods. I know the way it sighs before it swells, the way it goes strangely quiet before it does something unforgivable.

But I had never heard it scream before.

That was the dynamite. The fleet had come at slack water with a barge and a drilling crew, and when the first charge went off against the outer reef, the whole cave shuddered like a struck bell. Dust fell from the ceiling in grey curtains. My catalogue drawers — forty years of my hands, my eyes, my knees on cold stone — rattled in their frames, and one of them slid open and vomited its papers across the floor like a frightened animal emptying its bowels.

"Odile!" Mara's voice, from the tunnel mouth. "They're through the outer shelf! Twenty minutes, maybe less!"

Twenty minutes. I had spent twenty years on the third drawer alone.

I did not run to the tunnel. I will admit that plainly, because I am too old to lie well and too stubborn to lie badly. I went instead to the archive chamber, to the great cedar chest where the original manifest lay wrapped in oilcloth, and I put my body between it and the world. An absurd gesture. A woman of seventy-one, five feet nothing, standing guard with a lantern against a consortium with dynamite. But my mother died in this cave's mouth, or very near it, and I have sometimes thought that the only inheritance she left me was the habit of standing in doorways.

The second blast was closer. The sea itself seemed to flinch — a great groan through the rock, and then water, sudden and brown with silt, sheeting across the lower passage where for forty years there had been only a trickle at spring tide. The tide was turning early, or the blasts had broken some ancient seal and let the ocean in through a wound I had never mapped.

I was still gathering the loose papers from the third drawer when the roof of the passage arch came down.

Not all of it. A section, perhaps the length of a coffin, which felt like an appropriate measurement at the time. I heard it more than saw it — a crack like the world's largest knuckle, and then I was on my knees with a hot weight across my shoulders and my lantern rolling away, its flame guttering in the wet wind that now breathed through the cave like something alive.

I did not cry out. I want that recorded. I have read enough of my own handwriting to know that a person is remembered for what they did not do as much as what they did.

I heard footsteps then, splashing, and Mara's lantern light found me. She dug me out with her hands and her belt knife, shouting for Idris, and the two of them lifted the stone off me like it was a coffin lid and I was rising from the dead, which in a sense I was. My left arm was wrong. I knew it the way you know weather — not by looking but by the pressure of it, the deep wrongness in the bone.

"It's broken," I said, because someone had to say something true. "The manifest. Take the manifest. The copies in the tin box, the originals in the chest — both. And the third drawer, Mara. The third drawer has the paymaster's signatures. Without those, the names are only names."

Mara's face was the face of a woman being torn in half, and I confess I enjoyed it a little. Grief is a luxury. Purpose is a discipline, and I was dispensing it.

"We're not leaving you," she said.

"Don't be sentimental, girl, it's inefficient. I can walk. I've been walking these passages since before your father learned to shave." I regretted it the moment I said it — her father, the manifest, all of it hanging between us like a lamp between two chairs — but there was no time to unsay it, and perhaps there never is.

* * *

Idris carried the cedar chest. He is a thief, and I have known many, and the good ones all have the same quality: they understand weight. He took the chest the way a midwife takes a child, knowing exactly what it was worth and exactly how fragile. Mara had the tin box strapped across her back and me on her arm, and the three of us went into the flooded passage because there was no other way, the main tunnel being full of fleet men and dynamite and the particular enthusiasm of men who have been told there is gold at the end of their shift.

The water was waist-high and rising. Cold enough to stop thought. My broken arm I held above the surface like a woman carrying a candle through a storm, and Mara held me, and I have not been held since my mother's arms stopped working, and I am ashamed of how much I did not mind.

"Odile." Idris, from behind, his voice strange in the dark. "The side channel past the kneeling stone. Does it still go up?"

"Past the kneeling stone it forks. Left goes to the blowhole. Right goes down and does not come back. Choose like your life depends on it, because it does."

"And the manifests?"

"Are going up," I said. "All of them. The blowhole opens on the cliff face, forty feet above the water. There's a ledge. Birds nest there. It smells like the end of the world and it is the safest place on this coast."

The water climbed. I felt it at my ribs, then my chest, and the passage narrowed so that the sea moved faster through it, impatient, and I understood with a clarity that only drowning people and archivists achieve: the ocean does not hate what it takes. It simply takes. It took the Meridian. It took my mother. It was now, in its indifferent way, offering to take me, and the only argument against it was the chest of paper rising slowly above the black water in a thief's arms.

Mara stopped. The water was at her chin.

"Odile. How far?"

"Two hundred paces. I counted them once, the year my eyes were better."

"Two hundred paces," she repeated, and there was something in her voice I recognized from forty years of listening to the sea: the sound of a thing deciding. She shifted me higher on her arm. "Then we take two hundred paces."

We took them. I will not pretend it was dignified. We gasped and swore and swallowed the sea, and once the chest went under and Idris went after it and was gone so long that Mara made a sound I have never heard a ranger make — small, and young, and terrified — before he came up with it, coughing, holding on.

At the fork, the water pulled toward the right-hand passage with a hunger I did not like. I felt it at my legs, that downward drag, the cave inhaling.

"Left," I said. "Left, left, left."

* * *

We came up through the blowhole into evening air and bird stench and a sky the color of a healing bruise, and I lay on the ledge with my broken arm and my soaked ledgers and I laughed. I could not help it. It was not a pleasant laugh; it was the laugh of a woman who has just understood that she is still alive and does not entirely approve.

Below us, the fleet's lights moved on the water like a congregation of fireflies with poor morals. The barge sat against the reef. Somewhere under all that water was my cave, my drawers, my forty years of kneeling on cold stone — and somewhere under it, too, the treasure, which the fleet wanted, and which I had never wanted at all.

"They'll dive the main chamber by morning," Mara said. She was wringing out her hair, staring down at the lights. "They'll find the archive. The drawers that are left."

"They'll find paper," I said. "They won't find the names. The names are here." I touched the tin box on her back. "Forty years I thought I was keeping the dead. It turns out I was keeping ammunition."

Idris set the chest down and sat beside me, and for a moment none of us said anything. The tide roared in the rock below like a crowd that had been promised a hanging.

Then Mara went very still.

"Odile," she said quietly. "The boat. There's a boat coming around the point. No lights."

I raised my head. A dark shape, running dark, sliding along the base of the cliffs toward our ledge — toward the blowhole, the only door we had.

"That's no fleet launch," Idris said. "Fleet runs lights. Fleet runs loud."

"Then who," Mara said, "knows about the blowhole?"

And I, who have spent forty years cataloguing everything that washes up on this coast, felt the old cold walk up my spine, because I knew the shape of that boat. I had catalogued it, once, in another life — and the man who sailed it had been dead on the manifest for thirty years.

Or so the paper said.
