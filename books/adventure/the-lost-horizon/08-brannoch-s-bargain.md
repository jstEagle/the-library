## 8. Brannoch's Bargain

They tied my wrists with the same kind of cord I'd used on a hundred poachers, and I had to admire the symmetry even as it chafed. There's a particular humiliation in being arrested by your own procedures — the knot was textbook, the search was thorough, and the man doing it wouldn't meet my eyes, which told me he'd been a ranger long enough to be ashamed of something.

The *Corvid's Wake* — Brannoch's flagship, once the pride of the coastal service, now flying consortium colors like a whore wearing her client's coat — sat at anchor beyond the blasted reef. They ferried me out in a launch that smelled of diesel and money, and I spent the crossing memorizing faces. Two crewmen flinched when they looked at me. One mouthed something to another that might have been *that's Marchetti* or might have been a prayer. Word travels fast in a fleet, faster than shame.

Brannoch received me in his cabin, because of course he did. Not a brig, not an interrogation room. A cabin with real wood paneling salvaged off some drowned gentleman's yacht, a decanter of brandy, and a table set for two. He'd aged since I'd last stood at attention in front of him — seven years, maybe eight — but he'd aged the way rich men age, softening instead of weathering. The sea had stopped carving him. Money had started sanding him down instead.

"Mara." He said my name like we were resuming a conversation from yesterday. "Sit. You look half-drowned."

"I was in a cave when you blew it up."

"A cave full of stolen property." He poured two glasses anyway, pushed one toward me. The brandy caught the lamplight and turned it amber, expensive, patient. "Sit down, Ranger. Or should I say Sergeant? Old habits."

I stayed standing. It was petty and my legs were shaking and I stood anyway.

"You know what I keep thinking about," Brannoch said, settling into his chair with the ease of a man who had never once been cold or wet or afraid on my behalf, "is the day you made ranger. You were twenty-three. You'd failed the physical twice because of that shoulder, and you came back a third time anyway. I signed the waiver myself." He smiled, and for one treacherous second it was the smile that had gotten a whole generation of us through bad storms. "Grittiest recruit I ever certified. And now look at you. Hiding in holes with thieves and madwomen, defending paper."

"Not paper," I said. "Names."

"Names." He turned the word over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. "Mara, do you know what a name is worth? Nothing. A name is worth nothing until someone pays for it. That's all a ledger is — names waiting for a price. And I am authorized—" he let the word *authorized* land gently, almost kindly, "—to pay very well indeed."

He slid a folder across the table. I didn't touch it. He opened it for me anyway, the way men open doors they've already decided you'll walk through.

"Reinstatement. Full rank. Back pay calculated to the year you went on leave." He tapped a line item. "And this — this is the part I'd want if I were you. The debt. Your father's debt, transferred to you when you were nine years old, accruing interest through every year of your life. Sixty-one thousand, four hundred and twelve marks. Every cent of it discharged. Erased. You walk out of here owing nothing to anyone, for the first time since you were a child."

I looked at the number. Of course I looked at the number. I'd been looking at that number my whole life — scratched into the station ledger Idris stole, recited by creditors' letters, hanging over every choice I'd ever made like a second moon. My mother had died still paying it. I had joined the rangers partly because the uniform came with a wage and partly because a badge makes debt collectors polite.

Sixty-one thousand, four hundred and twelve marks. For that, all I had to do was hand over a list of murderers.

"That's not reinstatement," I said. "That's a purchase."

"It's a restoration," Brannoch said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that everyone will believe it. You come back clean. Promoted, even — I can make that happen. Inspector Marchetti. Your own district. A desk that doesn't leak salt water." He leaned back. "Or you stay a fugitive, and in about six hours the tide comes in and the cave goes under, along with whatever scraps Odile has hoarded for forty years, and none of it will have mattered. The Meridian stays a story. Your father stays a statistic no one wrote down."

*Your father.* He said it deliberately. He knew exactly what he was holding.

Here is the thing I don't like admitting, even to myself, especially now: for about ten seconds — maybe fifteen — I wanted it. Not the rank. The rank was bait for a younger woman, a hungrier one. What I wanted was the arithmetic. The clean slate. To stand up out of that chair and be, for the first time in my life, a person whose past didn't own a lien on her future. I have watched debt bend people my whole career. I've arrested men who stole bread against debts their grandfathers ran up. I know exactly what a number can do to a soul, because one has been doing it to mine since before I could read.

Fifteen seconds. Then I thought of Odile's hands, blue-knuckled and steady, turning pages she'd copied out by lamplight for forty years so that dead men would remain somebody. I thought of Idris, who had carried his own ledger around like a stone in his chest and finally set it down. And I thought about what a debt erased by the people who created it actually is. It isn't forgiveness. It's a receipt. It's them buying the last copy of the bill so no one can ever prove what they charged.

"You want to know the funny thing about that number?" I said. "I finally read the original entry. Idris took the ledger, yes — thief, criminal, all of it — but I got it back, and I read it. Page forty-seven. My father's account. And underneath the debt figures there's a note in the old stationmaster's hand. Dated three weeks after the Meridian went down." I let that sit. Brannoch's face did something small and controlled. "It says: *Debt suspended pending verification of debtor's status. Debtor listed as crew, vessel Meridian. Verification impossible — records sealed by order of the Consortium.*"

The lamp hummed. Somewhere above us, boots crossed the deck.

"They didn't just sink the ship," I said. "They sank the paperwork. My father spends eleven years paying off a boat he bought to work honest routes, then he signs on with the Meridian for one final run — one run to clear the rest — and when the consortium murders him, they seal the record so his death can't be claimed, so his debt can't be questioned, so his daughter inherits the whole rotten balance without appeal. You didn't offer to erase my debt, Brannoch. You offered to finish the job. You're the last page of the same erasure. Forty years later, still rubbing out Tomas Marchetti."

"He was a good man," Brannoch said quietly. Which was worse than anything else he could have said, because it meant he remembered him. It meant he'd known.

"He was a name on a manifest," I said. "That's all any of them were. And that manifest is the only place they exist anymore. So no. Keep your rank. Keep your receipt. I've seen what your legitimacy costs, and I've decided I can't afford it."

He sighed, and the warmth went out of him like a tide going out, and what remained was the thing the warmth had been built to cover: a company man with a fleet and a deadline.

"I hoped you'd be smarter," he said. "You always were stubborn, but I credited you with sense." He closed the folder. "Then hear the alternative. At high tide, my divers go into that cave whether you help or not. Odile is already injured — yes, we know; our scout saw the blood on the landing. If the manifests are damaged in retrieval, that's on you. If the old woman dies in there, that's on you. And the thief — Vale — he's already facing piracy charges. Consortium courts are efficient. You could spare him a rope by testifying that he coerced you."

"There it is," I said. "The real offer. Not my rank. My signature."

"Every empire runs on signatures, Mara. Yours is worth more than most." He stood, buttoned his jacket, became the officer again, the costume fitting him badly now, like a uniform borrowed from a thinner decade. "Take the night. Think about your father's number. Sixty-one thousand, four hundred and twelve. I'm told you know it by heart."

"I do," I said. "And here's what I know that you don't, Commander. That number isn't what I owe. It's what *they* owe. It's just been sitting in the wrong column for thirty years."

They put me in a cabin instead of a brig — Brannoch's courtesy, or his confidence, or his miscalculation, and I intended to find out which. The porthole showed the reef, black against the last grey light, and beyond it the cliffs where a blown-open wound marked the cave mouth. The tide was turning. I could feel it in the ship's slow swing at anchor, the whole fleet pivoting like weathervanes toward the water that was coming to take everything back.

Six hours, he'd said. But Brannoch had made one mistake, the same mistake every man with a fleet makes eventually: he'd assumed the sea was on his side because he'd paid for the boats.

I pressed my palm flat against the hull and felt it — faint, rhythmic, patient. The water below, moving the way it had moved for longer than any ledger, any fleet, any name.

It was coming in. And I had a radio room to reach, a diver's gate to pick, and exactly one tide to spend.

* * *

The sentry outside my door changed shift at the turn of the watch, and the new one was young, and wet-haired, and wouldn't look at me — and when I said, softly, "Tomas Marchetti," his head came up like a man hearing his own name called from underwater.

"My grandfather served on a coastal packet out of Saltmere," he said. "Before the Meridian run."

"I know," I said. "It's in the manifest. All of them are. And in about five hours, every man on this fleet is going to hear them read aloud."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he checked the corridor, and unlocked the door, and handed me the key to the radio room.

"The tide turns in four," he said. "Move."
