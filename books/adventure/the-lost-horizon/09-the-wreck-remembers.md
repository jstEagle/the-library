## 9. The Wreck Remembers

**MARA**

Brannoch's cell was a converted cargo hold with a cot, a bucket, and a view of nothing. He'd put me in it personally, which I took as a compliment — you don't waste a private room on someone you expect to break easily.

He came at dawn with the offer already polished. I could hear the polish in his voice before he said a word.

"Marchetti." He sat on an upturned crate like it was still a command chair. "Twenty years I've watched you work. Best tracker the fleet ever produced. You throw that away for a thief and a madwoman in a cave?"

"I threw it away when you did," I said. "I'm just following it down."

He slid a slate across the floor between us. My debt record, transcribed clean. Every coin my father's death had cost me, every year of rations and salt-wages I'd signed away to buy a badge that would let me chase the men who made me an orphan. The number at the bottom was the shape of my whole life.

"Erased," Brannoch said. "Signed off this morning. Your rank restored. A cabin on the *Corvane*. All you have to do is tell me where Odile keeps the master manifest."

I looked at the slate for a long time. Not because I was tempted. Because I wanted to see it clearly one last time — the arithmetic they'd built me out of. Debt as a leash. Grief as a harness. They'd taken a nine-year-old girl's worst day and turned it into thirty years of obedient service, and called it opportunity.

Then he said the thing that finished it.

"Your father's name isn't on any crew list, Marchetti. You know that. Whatever document you think exists—"

"His name is on the manifest," I said quietly. "Joren Marchetti, boatswain. I've read it. I've touched the ink. And here's what your consortium never understood, Captain: you didn't erase him from the records because he didn't matter. You erased him because he mattered too much. Because if anyone ever read that list, they'd find their own grandfathers on it."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not guilt. Fear.

That was when the lights went out.

**IDRIS**

The treasure hold of the *Meridian* had been sealed behind a bulkhead door for sixty years, and Idris Vale had spent exactly four of those minutes trying to open it before he decided not to.

Mara found him standing in the flooded access tunnel, torch held high, water to his chest, looking at the door like it was a snake he'd caught by the tail.

"You're not opening it," she said.

"I'm flooding it," he said. "There's a difference."

The plan had been Odile's, really — everything good in this cave was. The hold sat below the waterline now; the old sea-cocks were corroded but present, and the tide was coming in fast through the breach the fleet had blasted in the reef. All they had to do was open three valves and let the ocean finish what it started six decades ago. Gold sank. That was the whole beauty of gold. It stayed where you put it.

"They'll dive for it," Mara said, wrenching the first valve beside him. Rust flaked off in sheets. "They'll spend years."

"Good. Let them dig for a fortune that isn't there while the real fortune—" he tapped his satchel, heavy with copied pages, "—is walking out of here on two legs."

The second valve shrieked open. Water began to move beneath them, a cold current threading their legs, pouring into the dark below the deck plates. Somewhere down there lay chests of consortium gold, payment for sixty dead men, stamped with the same seal that was even now bolted to the side of Brannoch's flagship.

Idris paused at the third valve. His hands were shaking, and not from the cold.

"When I was eleven," he said, "a man gave me a boat and a list and told me which names to cross off the harbor registry. I thought I was helping ships sail. I didn't know I was erasing witnesses." He looked at her. "You should hate me. It would be simpler."

"It would," Mara agreed. She put her hand over his on the valve wheel. "Open it anyway."

Together they broke the seal. The sea came in like it had been waiting sixty years for permission, and the Meridian's stolen heart sank home into the dark.

* * *

**ODILE**

Odile had spent forty years learning the cave's voice — which passages sang at high tide, which chambers breathed, where the stone wept salt. So when the fleet's radio room aboard the *Corvane* went unguarded during the mutiny scare Brannoch's own divers were starting, she did not fight her way in. She walked. Old women are invisible; it is the one superpower age grants, and she had earned hers honestly.

She'd taken the radio room's codes from a sleeping technician an hour earlier, along with his boots, which fit poorly but fit.

She keyed the general band. Every diver in the water, every deckhand, every galley boy on every ship of the salvage fleet heard an old woman's voice, dry and clear as paper.

"This is Odile Fenn. Forty years ago I was the cabin girl on the *Meridian*. I am the only one left, and I have been keeping a list."

She read the manifest. Not the cargo columns — the names. Sixty names, and beside each, the name of the man who had signed the order, and beside those, the family lines that grew from them like roots from a poisoned tree. She read for twenty minutes without stopping. She had rehearsed against this hour for forty years, alone, to an audience of bones.

She read the name Voss, and a diver on the reef line stopped swimming.

She read the name Calloway-Brannoch, senior signatory, and heard the flagship's own bridge go silent around her broadcast.

She read Joren Marchetti, boatswain, who refused to cut the lifelines, and somewhere in a flooded corridor a woman who shared his surname pressed her forehead to cold stone and kept moving.

By the time she reached the last name, the divers were surfacing on their own lines, pulling off their masks, refusing orders in voices that cracked. Grandfathers. The word moved through the fleet like water through a hull. Men who had come for gold discovered they had been diving on their own inheritance of blood, and no wage on earth was worth that weight.

When Brannoch's officers finally reached the radio room, Odile was gone, having exited through a maintenance hatch she'd catalogued forty years before the fleet ever drew its charts. She left the transmitter keyed open. Let them hear the empty channel. Silence, properly deployed, is also testimony.

* * *

**MARA**

I got out of the hold because Brannoch's guards deserted their posts to listen to a ghost on the radio, which is the kind of irony I'd have enjoyed more if I weren't swimming through a collapsing tunnel at the time.

The blasted reef entrance was coming apart. The fleet's charges had cracked the old coral arch, and the incoming tide was doing the demolition work nature had always intended. I swam the passage Odile had mapped on my skin in charcoal — third arch, count the breathing cracks, turn where the stone weeps — and surfaced in the cathedral chamber gasping, to find Idris hauling Odile up onto dry rock, her arm bleeding, her catalogue satchel clutched to her chest like a child.

"The copies?" I managed.

"Safe." Odile's grin was ferocious. "All sixty names. They're on their way to every port clerk on the coast by week's end."

Above us, muffled by stone and water, the fleet was tearing itself apart. We heard it in fragments — shouts, a hull klaxon, then something neither of us expected: cheering. Actual cheering, ragged and disbelieving, rolling across the water like weather.

We climbed to the cave mouth as the sun cleared the cliffs and watched Brannoch's authority die in real time. Divers sat on the reefs with their masks off, refusing the boats. Deckhands lowered the consortium flag on the *Corvane* — not torn down, mind you, but *lowered*, ceremonially, the way you retire something dead. And Brannoch stood alone on his own quarterdeck, shouting orders to men who had suddenly remembered whose names were on the manifest, discovering that gold buys silence only until someone reads aloud.

"He'll run," Idris said.

"No," I said. "Look at him. He's waiting."

And he was. Because the truth is a strange kind of weapon — it doesn't kill the man it's aimed at. It just takes away everyone willing to stand beside him. Brannoch stood at the rail of a ship full of mutineers, holding a fortune that was sinking under sixty fathoms of protected water, and I watched him understand, all at once and forever, that the Meridian had won. Not today. Sixty years ago, when its crew died rather than give up each other. Everything since had just been the wreck remembering out loud.

The tide turned. The cave breathed. And Odile, ancient and bleeding and utterly triumphant, said the words I'd carry out of that place:

"Now we go home and make tea. Justice is thirsty work."

But even as I laughed — actually laughed, for the first time since I was nine years old — I saw the longboat cutting across the flats toward the cliff path. Three figures. One of them flying no colors at all, which on this coast meant only one thing: the coastal magistrates had finally gotten off their ledgers and come to see what all the noise was about.

Coming to decide, no doubt, whether the manifest belonged to the courts, the consortium's creditors, or the dead.

They were about to learn it belonged to none of those. It belonged to whoever remembered. And we intended to be very, very memorable.
