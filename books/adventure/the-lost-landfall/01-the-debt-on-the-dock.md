## 1. The Debt on the Dock

The tide was going out of Kessel Bend, and so, it seemed, was everything else Ivo Thornbury had ever owned.

She stood at the rail of the *Marrow*, her riverboat, and watched the water pull back from the pilings like a man retreating from a debt he couldn't pay. Mud flats gleamed in the early light, stinking of sulfur and rot. Somewhere beneath that mud were the moorings she'd sold in the spring, and before those, the brass fittings off the wheelhouse door, and before *those*—well. There was a long list, and Ivo had learned to stop reading it at night.

Three creditors this week. The first had been polite, a clerk with a ledger and a smile like wet paper. The second had spat on her dock boards. The third hadn't spoken at all—just stood at the end of the pier in the rain until dark, letting her know he could wait longer than she could.

"Captain." Tam Wrenn, her deckhand, came up the gangway with his cap in his hands, which he only did when there was bad news or good news, and he never could tell the difference between them. "There's a gentleman asking after you. Down at the fuel office."

"Is he carrying papers or a crowbar?"

"Papers. Fancy ones. Got a seal on them and everything."

A seal. In eleven years on the river, seals had brought her exactly two things: contracts and trouble, usually bound together with the same ribbon. She wiped her hands on her trousers, took a last look at the receding water as if it might offer advice—it didn't; the river kept its own counsel—and went down to meet her fate.

* * *

The gentleman from the Halvorsen Expedition was named Pell, and he sat in the fuel office like a heron standing in shallow water: patient, elegant, and entirely prepared to strike. He had a leather case, a fountain pen, and a contract already drafted in a hand so fine it looked typeset.

"Captain Thornbury." He said her name carefully, the way a man handles a coin he suspects is clipped. "Your reputation precedes you."

"My reputation," Ivo said, "is a rumor with a boat attached. What do you want moved?"

"Supplies. Upstream." He slid a paper across the scarred table. "To the Halvorsen archaeological survey, above the Narrows."

Ivo didn't touch the paper. Above the Narrows. Above Kessel Bend's jurisdiction, past the lock at Graywater, into the high country where the maps went thin and the law went thinner—and through all of it, through every mile of it, the territory of Magistrate Corven Hale.

"You know," she said, "most people who want cargo taken upriver don't start by naming the one place I can't afford to be seen."

"I'm told you're not wanted there," Pell agreed pleasantly. "I'm also told you're the best pilot on this reach, and that your boat draws shallow enough for the upper channels when the others can't pass. Professor Halvorsen pays for competence, Captain. He doesn't ask about the rest."

Everyone asked about the rest. They just didn't say so out loud. Ivo finally picked up the contract and read it the way she read water—looking for the rocks underneath.

The number at the bottom stopped her breath for a moment. Three months' wages for the whole crew. Coal, food, repairs, the works. Enough to buy the *Marrow* free and clear of every lien on her hull. Enough to start over somewhere the name Thornbury meant nothing at all.

And enough, she thought, to make a drowning woman grab at an anchor.

"What's in the crates?" she asked.

"Scientific equipment. Provisions. Standard expedition freight."

"I'll want to inspect—"

"You won't." Pell's pen tapped once against the case. "Sealed manifests, sealed crates. The professor's insurance requires it. You'll receive a manifest listing weights and dimensions only. Nothing more."

"That's not how freight works."

"That," said Pell, "is how *this* freight works. Take it or leave it, Captain. There are other boats."

There weren't, and they both knew it. Every other captain on the reach would have taken the contract without blinking, but they drew too deep for the upper channels, and the season was turning, and Halvorsen's people upstream were waiting on supplies that would keep them alive through the winter. Ivo looked at the number again. Then at the clause beneath it—a small one, printed fine, the kind of clause that lawyers put in when they want a thing understood without being said:

*The carrier accepts no liability for seizure, detention, or confiscation by civil authority.*

*Civil authority.* Two words doing the work of a whole name.

She set the contract down. Outside the office window, the tide had turned; the river was coming back in, slow and indifferent, covering the mud flats the way it covered everything eventually.

"The route passes through Hale's jurisdiction," she said. "You know that."

"I know that the magistrate's interest in the upper river is a matter of public record," Pell said, "and that the expedition's interest is a matter of private science, and that the two have no cause to meet. The professor's permits are in order."

Permits. Ivo had seen what Hale's office did to permits that crossed him. She'd watched him shred a ferryman's license over a disputed toll, watched him ruin a lockkeeper whose cousin had testified at the wrong trial. She knew precisely how much a permit weighed in Corven Hale's hands: about as much as a leaf on a scale built for iron.

But the number on the contract was real, and the liens on her boat were realer, and Tam Wrenn had two daughters who ate like growing things, and the *Marrow* needed a new stern bearing before freeze-up or she'd shake herself apart by spring.

Pragmatism, Ivo told herself. That was all this was. A pragmatic decision made by a pragmatic woman who had spent eleven years learning that the world didn't care about clean hands, only steady ones.

"I'll take it," she said.

Pell smiled and uncapped his pen.

* * *

They loaded at dawn two days later, under a sky the color of unpolished pewter.

Twelve crates came down from the warehouse on a dray, each one strapped and sealed with wax stamps Ivo didn't recognize—some university crest, maybe, or something wearing one's clothes. Heavy ones first, then lighter, then two that made the dray horse lean and left grooves in her dock planking deep enough to hold rainwater.

Tam counted them twice, frowning. "Manifest says twelve hundred pounds total, Captain. That crate there—" he nodded at the last one, still settling onto the deck with a groan of timber—"that one alone weighs better than four hundred. Somebody's arithmetic is drunk."

"Somebody's arithmetic is lying," Ivo said. "Leave it."

She walked the line of crates while the crew lashed them down. She wasn't allowed to open them—that was the shape of the deal—but nobody had said she couldn't *listen*. She pressed her palm flat against the third crate and knocked. Dense thud. No rattle, no shift. Packed solid, whatever it was, packed like something someone very much didn't want bouncing around.

The seventh crate smelled of machine oil and cold metal when she leaned close. The ninth had air holes drilled along its bottom edge, nearly invisible, and from inside it came nothing at all—which was somehow worse than a sound would have been. Things that breathe make noise. Things that are quiet in a box have either been trained or killed.

"Captain?" Tam had come up beside her, quiet as dock rope. "We taking these all the way up? Through the Bend?"

"Through the Bend."

He was silent a while. Tam had been with her five years, ever since she'd fished him out of the Graywater lock pool half-drowned and too grateful to ask questions. He knew her history the way the crew knew the boat's leaks: intimately, and without discussing them in company.

"Hale's men check everything comes through," he said finally. "Everything. And they've been twitchy lately. Double shifts at the toll pier since the equinox."

"I know."

"And we're carrying twelve crates we can't look inside, up a river where the magistrate would pay a year's tolls to know what's in them."

"I *know*, Tam."

He nodded slowly, putting his cap back on, which meant he'd run out of things to say that wouldn't get him a rope's end. But at the gangway he paused, and looked back, and said the thing he'd actually come to say:

"You could've said no, Captain. Just so you know. Nobody on this boat would've thought less of you."

Ivo watched him go. The river said nothing. It never did. It just carried what you gave it, and kept what it liked, and gave back the rest changed beyond recognition.

That was the thing about debts, she thought, turning to the wheelhouse. They never really got paid. They just got passed downstream, to whoever was fool enough or desperate enough to catch them.

* * *

She cast off at mid-morning on the ebb, the *Marrow*'s engine turning over with its familiar uneven rhythm—the starboard cylinder still missing now and then, a stutter she'd learned to hear the way other people heard heartbeats. Kessel Bend fell away behind them: the fuel office, the chandlery, the courthouse with its green dome where Corven Hale held court three days a week and held grudges the other four.

Eleven years ago she'd stood in a courtroom under that dome, twenty-six years old and certain that truth was a kind of armor. She'd learned otherwise in the space of an afternoon. Perjury dressed as testimony. A verdict bought before the trial began. Four years of hard labor commuted to two when a doctor lied about her health, and then the river, because the river was the only place that would take a convicted woman and ask no questions.

The current took the bow. The *Marrow* swung upstream, into the teeth of the flow, and Ivo felt the old dread settle over her shoulders like a wet coat—not sharp anymore, after all these years, just heavy. Familiar. The weight of a name she couldn't outrun, only outdistance, mile by mile, day by day.

Above the Narrows. Past Graywater lock. Through the whole long throat of Hale's country, carrying twelve crates of secrets she'd signed away the right to know.

Tam called the soundings. The banks slid by, willow and alder giving way to grey stone. Somewhere ahead, the river narrowed toward the locks, and beyond the locks lay the high water country where the maps went honest and the men went crooked.

Ivo put her hands on the wheel and steered into it, because the alternative was the mud flats at low tide, and she had never yet learned how to live ashore.

Behind the ninth crate, in the dark, something shifted once—and went quiet again.
