## 2. Passengers Who Don't Talk

They came aboard at Greywater Landing an hour before dawn, when the mist still lay on the river like a sheet pulled over a corpse.

Ivo Thornbury was on the dock checking the mooring lines when she heard them—two sets of boots on the planking, walking in step, which no honest travelers ever did. Honest travelers shuffled. They staggered off night boats with their collars up and their bags dragging. These two came down the ramp like men reporting for duty.

"Passage to Kessel Bend and beyond," the taller one said. He set a leather wallet on her bollard without being asked, and inside it, when she thumbed it open, banknotes crisp enough to shave with. "Upstream. All the way, if you're going all the way."

"I'm going as far as the fuel lasts," Ivo said. "Where's beyond?"

The tall one smiled. It was a good smile, practiced, the kind a man keeps folded in his pocket next to his papers. "Survey work. We're mapping the high-water marks for the Commission."

"The Commission," Ivo repeated. She closed the wallet and slid it back across the bollard. "There hasn't been a Commission on this reach since before the war."

"There's always a Commission somewhere," said the shorter one. He hadn't spoken until then, and his voice had a gravel bottom to it, like a channel that had never been dredged. "You taking the money or counting it?"

She took the money. That was the whole truth of her life these days: she took the money, because the *Maren Lee* drank fuel the way a dying man drinks water, and because Dockmaster Pell's boys had come by twice that week asking after her mooring fees with the particular politeness of men who enjoyed asking. The contract from Halvorsen's people would cover the resupply run, but it wouldn't cover the debts sleeping under her berth like ballast she couldn't jettison. Passage money was passage money.

Still. She watched them stow their gear in the forward cabin—the tall one arranging his bag with corners squared, the short one standing at the rail a moment too long, looking upstream at nothing—and something in her gut turned over slow, like a paddle wheel starting to bite.

"Names?" she called.

"Brenner," said the tall one. "This is Coss."

"And your kit?"

Brenner patted the canvas bags they'd carried aboard. "Instruments. Levels, chains, the usual."

"Uh-huh," Ivo said, and went to wake her engineer.

* * *

By full light she had them logged in the back of her ledger, where she kept things she didn't want found: *Brenner, tall, grey coat, hands clean. Coss, short, quiet, scar through left eyebrow, old. Paid triple rate without haggling. Haggling is what surveyors do.*

It wasn't only the money. It was the boots.

She'd noticed them while the men were settling in—she noticed everything about passengers, it was how she'd stayed alive this long—and the boots were wrong in a way that would have been funny if it weren't so telling. Surveyors waded. They stood in shallows driving stakes, they scrambled down cut banks, they spent their days ankle-deep in silt and their nights drying out by whatever fire they could find. Their boots were ruined things, cracked and patched, or else they wore rubber hip-waders that smelled of pond forever.

These two wore city boots. Oiled leather, low-heeled, soles stitched not nailed. Boots for standing on marble floors and boarding trains. Boots that had never once been in the mud, and were not going to be.

She mentioned it to Odile Marchetti over breakfast, her engineer, a broad-shouldered woman from the lock towns who could take an engine apart blindfolded and put it back together with opinions.

"City boots," Odile said, chewing. "So they're liars. Half your passengers are liars, Ivo. The other half are running from something."

"These aren't running. Running looks different." Ivo turned her cup on its saucer. "Running is scared. These two are comfortable. Comfortable men on my boat means somebody downstream is paying them to be comfortable."

"You think they're Hale's?"

The name sat between them like a dropped tool. Magistrate Corven Hale's jurisdiction began at Kessel Bend and ran all the way up into the gorge country, a stretch of river he governed the way a spider governs a web—with patience, and from the center, and always hungry. Ivo had spent seven years routing around him the way water routes around a stone, and she did not say his name aloud on principle. But Odile had earned it.

"Maybe," Ivo said. "Maybe worse."

"Worse than Hale."

"Hale's just a hand. I want to know whose arm it's attached to."

* * *

Through the morning she ran the *Maren Lee* upriver through the willow flats, and she watched her passengers the way she watched the water—for the thing underneath.

Brenner spent the first hours on deck with an actual surveyor's level, which surprised her until she saw how he held it. Wrong. He gripped it like luggage. A man who'd carried an instrument for years carries it the way a mother carries a child, without thinking, balanced against the body's old habits. Brenner set it up twice on the foredeck, sighted at the banks, made a show of writing figures in a notebook, and every reading he took was of the boat itself—of her lines, her draft marks, the depth sounder in the pilothouse, the way she leaned in the current.

Coss didn't pretend at anything. Coss stood at the stern rail and watched the water going by behind them, and every hour, near enough, he checked a pocket watch. Not to tell the time. To time something. Ivo couldn't have said what, but nobody checks a watch against nothing.

At noon she made her first move.

The river above Greywater was gossip country; news traveled up and down it faster than any boat, carried by lockkeepers and fish buyers and the wives of ferry pilots, and half of it was invented somewhere in the middle. So Ivo invented some. She let herself be seen talking to the lockkeeper at Willow Sluice, leaning close, nodding gravely, and when she came back aboard she made sure to pass close to where Brenner stood fiddling with his level.

"Bad news from upriver," she said, loud enough, casual enough. "Rockslide's closed the Narrows. Whole expedition's trapped above it, they're saying. Might be weeks before anyone gets through."

She didn't look at him. She went into the pilothouse and poured coffee she didn't drink and watched the reflection in the brass fittings, which was better than any mirror.

Brenner set down the level. Walked aft. Spoke low to Coss, maybe forty seconds, head bent. Coss checked his watch.

Then Brenner went below—to the wireless room, which she rented to passengers at a fee, and which had a transmitter powerful enough to reach Kessel Bend and points south. She heard the crackle of the key through the bulkhead, a long transmission, methodical, the fist of a man who'd learned radio in some service or other where you didn't tap out messages pretty, you tapped them out exact.

Ivo drank her terrible coffee and felt the suspicion in her chest sharpen from a dull weight into something with an edge.

Because there was no rockslide. There was no closure of the Narrows. She'd made it up over eggs that morning, and now it was traveling downriver at the speed of a telegraph key toward whoever paid for boots like those.

* * *

The second test she ran at dusk, and it was crueler.

They tied up for the night at a wooding station—a shelf of cut timber and a lean-to where the shore steepened into dark pine—and Ivo cooked beans and salt pork for four and set it out on the deck plates like she didn't care who ate. Food loosens people. Food and fatigue. A man can hold his face together all day, but give him a bowl and a fire and the dark coming down on the water, and things start to slip.

"So," she said, ladling beans. "Commission work. You'll be surveying the drowned reach, then. Above the gorge."

Brenner looked up. "Drowned reach?"

"You haven't heard?" She kept her voice bored, spoon moving, eyes on her bowl. "Before the dam went in—thirty years back, more—they flooded the valley. Towns and all. Aldermere, and the smaller places. Water's been sitting on them ever since. This year's drought has dropped the river so low the old bell tower's showing. Sticking up out of the middle of the channel like a finger."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere in the pines a bird made one complaint and stopped.

"That's a story," Coss said at last, flat.

"It's a fact. Ask any pilot older than me." She shrugged. "Point is, if your Commission wants high-water marks, that reach is where they'd send you. Unless—" and here she yawned, stretched, played the tired captain to the bone— "unless you're not here for the Commission at all."

Brenner laughed. It was a warm laugh, well-made, and it convinced her of exactly nothing. "Captain, everyone thinks everyone's a spy on this river. You've got an imagination."

"I've got a boat and a memory," Ivo said. "Both of them float."

She turned in early, which she almost never did, and instead climbed out through the deck hatch into the shadow of the cargo crates and lay there in the cold with her hat over her face, listening.

Past midnight, Brenner and Coss came out on deck. Their voices carried the way voices do over water, low but complete.

"—confirmed, then? The tower's showing?"

"If she's not lying."

"She's not lying. She wanted us to hear it. Question is why." A pause. The watch-chain clicked. "We transmit at the relay buoy tomorrow, noon. Full report. They'll want to move."

"They'll want to move regardless. You heard the schedule. The expedition's already past the point where—"

"Quiet." The word came down sharp as a hatch closing. Then, softer, in the tone of a man remembering himself: "Noon. The buoy. And Captain Thornbury hears nothing, sees nothing, knows nothing. She's freight, Bren. Freight doesn't get told."

Freight. Ivo lay under her hat in the dark and let the word settle into her like a stone into a riverbed, where it would sit and shift nothing and change everything.

They knew about the expedition. Not as passengers know—vaguely, from newspapers—but specifically, with schedules. *The expedition's already past the point where—* past the point where what? Where contact was expected? Where safety ended?

And someone downstream would be told about the bell tower of Aldermere by noon tomorrow, and someone downstream would *want to move*.

She thought about the crates in her hold. The ones from Halvorsen's agents, sealed, manifested as instruments, heavy in a way instruments shouldn't be, stacked with their markings painted over. She'd signed for them without looking, because looking was a luxury and the creditors didn't take curiosity as payment.

Now she wished she'd looked. Now she understood that she was not carrying supplies upriver. She was carrying something ahead of these two men, or behind them, or through them—she was part of a delivery, and no one had told the freight.

* * *

Dawn again. Mist again. The river running high and brown with snowmelt, patient as everything that intends to win.

Ivo stood at the wheel and brought the *Maren Lee* out from the wooding station, and behind her she heard Brenner and Coss at the rail, and she gave them the back of her head and a face like weathered oak, and inside she was doing arithmetic.

Two men, city boots, service fists on a telegraph key, reporting to someone downstream at noon via the relay buoy. An expedition upstream that had gone quiet—that was the other thing, the thing she'd been carrying since Greywater, the scheduled check-in from the Halvorsen camp that hadn't come, three days overdue now, static where voices should be. She'd told herself rivers eat radios. She'd told herself a lot of things.

A magistrate named Hale who smiled like a hook already set. Sealed crates. Silent men. Dead air.

Odile came up beside her and spat over the rail, which was her way of asking a question.

"Noon," Ivo said quietly. "The relay buoy. They're transmitting at noon."

"Cut the aerial?"

"No." Ivo eased the wheel over, and the bow swung upriver, into the current, into the gorge country where the banks narrowed and the water quickened and Magistrate Corven Hale's jurisdiction began at a bend you could see from here, a thin white line of foam across the channel like a drawn wire. "Let them send it. Let whoever's downstream read every word."

Odile frowned at her. "Then what are we doing?"

"We're going to find out who they're working for," Ivo said, "by arriving before the answer does."

Ahead, the mist was burning off the water, and the first true hills of the upper river stood up out of it, blue and far, and somewhere beyond them a drowned town waited under thirty years of silence with its bell tower raised like a hand.

The *Maren Lee* beat upstream against the current, and Ivo Thornbury, who had spent seven years learning to keep her head down, felt it come up instead—slow, unwilling, inevitable, like something surfacing from deep water.
