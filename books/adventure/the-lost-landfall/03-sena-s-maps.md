## 3. Sena's Maps

The fuel stop at Marrow's Landing was a mud shelf and a rusted pump, the kind of place the river tolerated rather than embraced. Sena Voss stood on the dock with a canvas map case slung across her back like a rifle and a single leather satchel at her feet, watching the *Kestrel* come in against the current with the particular grace of a woman who had learned to make an old boat obey.

She had been waiting three hours. She would have waited three days. The expedition's last radio message had named this boat, this captain, this stretch of the resupply run, and Sena had learned long ago that when a river gives you one thread to hold, you do not ask it for a rope.

The *Kestrel* bumped the pilings. Lines went out. A deckhand — broad, sunburned, missing two fingers on his left hand — caught her satchel when she tossed it before she'd said a word to anyone, which told her something about the crew's reflexes, and then the captain came down the gangway herself.

Ivo Thornbury was taller than Sena had imagined, and harder. Trial transcripts flattened people into paper; in person there was a weathered quality to her face, lines carved deep around the mouth, eyes the grey-brown of water over silt that gave away nothing they didn't choose to. Her handshake was brief and dry and assessed the strength of Sena's grip without pretending otherwise.

"Voss. The cartographer." Not a question. "Halvorsen didn't say you were joining us."

"Halvorsen doesn't know yet," Sena said. "I wired ahead for passage. You're the last boat going up past Kessel Bend before the fuel season closes."

Something flickered behind those flat grey eyes — arithmetic, probably. Cost per mile, weight aboard, risk. Sena had watched engineers do the same calculation about bridges.

"I don't take passengers I can't feed," Ivo said.

"I don't eat much. And I pay cash."

"Cash is good." But the captain's gaze had already dropped to the canvas case on her shoulder, and stayed there a half-second too long. "What's in the tube?"

"Maps."

"What kind?"

"The kind I'm paid to carry."

A pause. Somewhere downstream a loon called, that strange wavering note that sounded like grief practiced until it became music. Ivo Thornbury looked at her the way Sena imagined she looked at rapids — reading the surface for what lay underneath.

"Aboard, then," she said finally. "Deckhand'll show you where you sleep. We cast off in twenty minutes, and I don't come back for stragglers."

---

Sena found her berth — a narrow bunk behind the engine room, warm as a held breath and smelling of diesel and old varnish — and then did what she always did in a new vessel: she went looking for the chart table.

She found it in the wheelhouse, under a slanting window fogged with river damp. And she found the charts, and stopped breathing for a moment.

They were beautiful. That was the first thing. Whoever drew them loved the work — soundings in a fine copperplate hand, channel markers inked in red, marginal notes in pencil that read like a conversation with the river itself: *snag moved 40 yds downstream after spring ice, verify*. This was not a hired captain's paperwork. This was devotion, bound in oilcloth.

It was also wrong. Or rather — it ended. The reach above Kessel Bend faded into blank paper marked only with a dotted line and three words in that same careful hand: *uncharted — local knowledge only.*

Sena unrolled her own case across the table before she could talk herself out of it.

She heard Ivo come up the stairs — the captain moved quietly for someone who owned a boat that groaned like a shipwreck — and felt her stop in the doorway.

"That's not a published chart," Ivo said.

"No."

"Where did you get it?"

"Drew it." Sena kept her voice level, her hands flat on the paper. "From survey data. Some of it mine."

The silence stretched. When Sena glanced up, Ivo was looking at the map the way other people looked at fires — the reach above Kessel Bend rendered in Sena's ink: the drowned valley, the old town plots ghosted beneath contour lines, the river braiding through country that every official atlas showed as a single confident blue line and nothing more.

"You've drawn a reach that doesn't exist," Ivo said slowly. "On government paper, that whole stretch is—" she crossed to the shelf, pulled down the official folio, and laid it open beside Sena's work. The contrast was almost comic: the printed chart's serene emptiness, and Sena's dense, living document with its soundings and ruins and warnings.

"Officially," Sena said, "the Aldermere Reach was closed to navigation thirty years ago. After the dam."

"After the dam," Ivo repeated. Her finger hovered over the ghosted town plots without touching them. "You've marked streets here. Underwater streets."

"They surface sometimes. In drought years." Sena watched the captain's face carefully. "You didn't know what you were carrying supplies to."

"I knew I was carrying crates I wasn't allowed to inspect to a camp that doesn't appear on any manifest, up a river my charts pretend ends at Kessel Bend." Ivo straightened. "I knew enough to be paid well and worried constantly. Those aren't incompatible."

There it was — the pragmatism, dry as August. Sena had met men who would have blustered at being caught ignorant; Thornbury simply stated her position like a sounding, fixed and honest.

"Then let me worry with you," Sena said. "I know that water. Every channel, every drowned fence line, every place the current does something the official charts would get you killed over. Halvorsen's expedition has been up there eight months with no trained navigator. If their boat—"

"If their boat had trouble, we'd have heard." Ivo's jaw tightened. "We haven't heard anything. Which is its own kind of chart, isn't it. Blank space where a signal should be."

For a moment they were simply two women looking at the same absence, and Sena felt the strange vertigo of being understood by a stranger.

Then Ivo rolled the official folio shut with a snap. "I don't allow unofficial charts at my helm. Rules exist because the river kills people who improvise."

"And your rules cover a reach nobody's mapped since before either of us was born?"

"My rules cover everything between this rail and drowning." But her eyes went back to Sena's inkwork — once, twice, the reluctant gravity of a person trying not to admire something. "Dinner's at seven. Cook makes too much anyway."

---

Dinner was salt pork, beans, and the two silent surveyors staring at Sena from the far end of the table with the fixed attention of men memorizing her face. She catalogued them back — the wrong boots, the way neither touched the coffee pot without the other nodding — and said nothing, because she'd learned that information spent early buys nothing.

Afterward, she climbed to the wheelhouse.

Ivo was at the helm, running the night reach by memory and lamplight, one hand on the wheel, the boat answering her like a thought. Sena didn't announce herself; she just settled onto the chart bench and began correcting her own map in pencil — depths she'd misjudged, a bend she'd drawn too generous.

Ten minutes passed. Twenty. The engine thudded beneath them like a large patient heart.

"You draw the way the river moves," Ivo said at last, not looking at her. "Nothing wasted."

"Coming from you, that's either praise or an accusation."

"Both. Usually both." The wheel turned a spoke. "Why does a cartographer carry maps the government says don't exist, to an expedition that officially isn't there, on a boat captained by a woman whose name half the magistrates on this river keep in a drawer?"

Sena set down her pencil. "You know my name's in drawers too, Captain. Voss. My brother Tomas surveyed the Aldermere Reach eleven years ago. First team in after the flooding. They went up in June. The river gave them back nothing but one boot and a field notebook with the last forty pages torn out." She kept her voice steady the way she kept all her voices steady, cost be damned. "I've spent eleven years mapping a place the government erased, because somewhere in that drowned valley is whatever it was my brother found that made forty pages worth tearing out."

The lamp hissed. The river slid black beneath the bow.

"I lost my name to a courtroom," Ivo said quietly. "You lost yours to a flood. Maybe that's why neither of us trusts paper."

"Paper's fine," Sena said. "It's the hands that sign it."

That earned her something — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, surfacing around the corner of Ivo's mouth like a fish rising. It changed her whole face, and Sena understood suddenly why the crew worshipped this woman, and understood also that she was in a certain amount of trouble.

---

They argued about charts until midnight — depth soundings versus dead reckoning, whether the marker at Halloway Point had drifted, whether Sena's contour intervals were honest or optimistic. Then they argued about coffee, because Ivo made it strong enough to strip paint and defended it as a navigational necessity, and Sena — who had drunk survey-camp coffee brewed in a tin over peat fire, coffee that could qualify as geological — declared it cowardice to call that strong.

"You put *two* spoons," Ivo said, scandalized, pouring a third cup at one in the morning because neither of them had mentioned sleeping.

"You drink it like it owes you money."

"It does owe me money. Everything on this boat owes me money." But she drank Sena's version, grimacing, and poured her own over it, and the resulting blend was terrible and perfect and they finished the pot arguing about whose fault it was.

Somewhere past two, the argument ran out of material, the way arguments do when they were never really about charts. The lamp burned low. Rain had started somewhere upstream — Sena could smell it coming down the valley, green and mineral — and the wheelhouse windows streamed with the first fat drops.

"Why cartography?" Ivo asked. Her voice had changed register, gone quieter, the voice of a person asking something that matters more than it sounds. "Of all the trades. Spending your life drawing places you may never stand."

"Because a map is a promise," Sena said. "That a place exists even if no one's looking. That it can't be erased just because someone powerful wants it gone." She turned her cup slowly on the bench. "My brother believed that. He used to say the river keeps everything, it just files it badly. I'm trying to build the index."

Ivo was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked against the glass. When she spoke again, her voice was rougher.

"When I was on trial, they took my father's river almanac into evidence. Handwritten. Forty years of soundings. They called it 'material support' — evidence I'd falsified channels to run contraband." Her thumb moved along the wheel rim, back and forth, a small unconscious motion like soothing an animal. "They burned it afterward. Disposal of seized materials, the notice said. I signed for the ashes." She looked over, and in the low lamplight her face was stripped of all its captain's armor. "So yes. Maps are promises. I know exactly what it costs when someone breaks them."

Sena reached across the chart table — slowly, giving her time to refuse — and rested her hand over Ivo's where it gripped the wheel. The captain's hand was scarred and rope-hard and very still under hers, and then, by degrees, not still at all.

Neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say that the rain wasn't already saying better, ticking down on the roof, filling the dark with the sound of the river keeping its accounts.

---

Dawn came up grey-gold over the water, and Sena woke in the chart bench's corner with a blanket over her that smelled of diesel and someone else's life, her neck stiff, her map case serving as a pillow. Across the wheelhouse, Ivo stood at the helm, hair tied back, steering into the morning as if she'd never slept anywhere else.

But there were two cups on the chart table, rinsed and drying side by side. And pinned above the chart shelf, where the official folio had been, Sena's map hung in full view of the helm — unrolled, corrected in Ivo's own pencil in a dozen places, soundings added, hazards noted, the blank reach above Kessel Bend now annotated in that careful copperplate hand:

*Local knowledge confirmed. Verify all soundings. Trust the cartographer.*

Sena read it twice. Below it, smaller, almost grudging, as if written before the writer could reconsider:

*Trust the captain too.*

Upstream, the sky over Kessel Bend sat low and bruise-colored, and the river ran high with snowmelt, brown and muscular, pushing against the season. Somewhere beyond that bend, a camp had gone silent, and two men with the wrong boots were whispering over a radio at dawn.

Sena folded the blanket, took up her pencil, and went to stand at the rail beside the woman who had just, in ink, promised to trust her.

The *Kestrel* pointed her bow at the bend, and the current took them both.
