## 4. The Magistrate's Toll

Kessel Bend announced itself the way bad news does—gradually, then all at once. First the smell: coal smoke and tannery reek riding the wind off the bluff, cutting through the green breath of the river like a knife through canvas. Then the sound: a mill wheel groaning somewhere behind the levee, and beneath it the thinner music of the town itself—hammers, a church bell gone sour with rust, dogs arguing over something dead. Last came the sight of it, stacked up the hillside in tiers of gray stone and peeling clapboard, crowned by the courthouse with its copper dome gone the color of old vomit, and beside it, taller than any church spire, the flagpole from which Magistrate Corven Hale's jurisdiction flew its colors.

Ivo brought the *Maren Lee* around the bend at half throttle, hands easy on the wheel, face arranged into the pleasant blankness that had kept her alive for six years. Below decks, her cargo sat in crates she'd signed for sight unseen—twelve of them, stenciled with a university crest and a manifest that read *survey instruments* in handwriting too fine to belong to anyone who'd ever carried an instrument through mud. Up on the hurricane deck, the two surveyors who weren't surveyors stood at the rail watching Kessel Bend grow, and Ivo watched them watch it.

The tall one—Brack, he'd given as his name, though his boots said otherwise—had shaved that morning. She'd heard the scrape of the razor through the bulkhead at dawn, careful and unhurried, a man tidying himself for someone whose opinion mattered. Surveyors headed into unmapped country didn't shave for the country's benefit.

"Town looks prosperous," Brack said when she climbed down to check the fuel gauge she'd already checked twice.

"Towns always look prosperous from the water," Ivo said. "Water doesn't show you the debt."

He smiled at that, thin and correct, and turned back to the rail. The short one, Pell, hadn't spoken above ten words since Vellacourt, but his eyes moved constantly, cataloguing—the depth marks on the pilings, the number of constables visible on the landing, the position of the customs shed relative to the boat. Ivo had spent three days feeding them false river gossip to see which pieces traveled downstream. She'd invented a story about a gold strike in the Sable Forks, told it loudly to her engineer, and watched Pell write a note that night by lamplight, fold it small, and pay a deckhand two bits to carry it aboard the downstream packet when they'd tied up at Miller's Landing. A gold strike nobody had heard of, reported within four hours. Whatever these men were surveying, their findings went south by fast post, and somebody south was paying for speed.

That somebody had a name. Ivo just hadn't let herself say it yet.

* * *

They took on fuel at the lower wharf under a sky the color of wet slate. Rain had been threatening since morning without committing, and the air hung thick enough to chew. Ivo stood on the dock supervising the hose connection, keeping her hands busy because idle hands on this particular stretch of river felt like an invitation.

She saw the launch before she understood what it meant—a black-hulled government steam launch coming up from the town landing, flying the magistrate's pennant, moving with the unhurried authority of something that had never once been made to wait. Her stomach did a slow, cold roll, the way it did when the keel found a sunken log: not pain yet, just the knowledge of it coming.

"Captain Thornbury." The man who stepped onto the dock wore a dove-gray coat cut close to a body kept trim by fencing or vanity—Ivo guessed both—and carried a walking stick he clearly did not need. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned, with a face built for portraits: strong jaw, level brow, eyes the pale gray-green of river ice. Handsome the way a hook is handsome, all clean lines until you're on it. "Corven Hale. Magistrate of this district." He offered his hand.

Six years, and the name still landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. But the face—she'd have known the face anywhere. It had looked down at her from the witness box every day of her trial, patient, faintly sorrowful, the face of a man forced to be severe by the wickedness of others. She had hated him so completely for so long that the hatred had worn smooth, like a stone in a current, and now picking it up again she found it lighter than expected and far more dangerous.

"Magistrate." She shook his hand once, briefly. His grip was dry and precise. "You know my name."

"I make it my business to know every captain working this water." He said it pleasantly, but his eyes stayed on hers a moment too long, and there it was—not recognition building, recognition *arrived*, banked and glowing like coals raked over for the night. He knew exactly who she was. He'd known before the launch left the town landing. Possibly before she'd left Vellacourt; the expedition contract would have crossed his desk, permits being his to grant or withhold. "And yours especially, Captain. Your reputation precedes you. Both reputations."

Down the dock, Pell had gone very still by the fuel hose. Brack had drifted closer without appearing to move at all. So they answered to Hale. Good. Now she knew the shape of the leash even if she couldn't see the hand.

"The *Maren Lee* is under charter," Ivo said. "Resupply run for the Halvorsen expedition. My papers are in order if you want to inspect them."

"Oh, I'm sure they are." Hale gestured upriver with his stick, an expansive motion that took in the whole gray length of the water. "That's rather the point of you, isn't it? A captain with immaculate papers and nothing left to lose. Do you know how rare that combination is? Most men with your history either stop running or stop caring. You've managed neither. It's admirable. Genuinely." He smiled, and the smile had warmth in it, which was the worst thing about it. "Walk with me, Captain. Ten minutes of your time. Consider it a toll—levied in conversation rather than coin. I hear you're short of coin."

Every instinct she owned said *step back, cast off, put the river between you*. But refusing a magistrate on his own dock, in front of his own men, with twelve uninspected crates and two informants aboard—that refusal would cost more than ten minutes. And some cold practical corner of her mind noted that a magistrate who sought her out wanted something, and a man who wants something can be learned.

"After you," she said.

* * *

They walked the length of the wharf and out along the levee path, where the town fell away below them in roofs and smoke and the launch idled at its mooring like a dog told to stay. The rain finally arrived, fine and needling, and Hale opened an umbrella without breaking stride, tilting it to cover her as well—an old-fashioned courtesy performed with such ease it felt rehearsed, and maybe it was. Maybe he'd practiced this walk. She wouldn't have put it past him.

"Do you remember your trial?" he asked.

"No," Ivo said.

He glanced at her, amused. "Truly?"

"I remember the dock. I remember the sound my chains made. I don't remember faces. Faces are what people use to lie to you."

"A hard philosophy for a woman who lives by reading water." They reached the end of the levee where a cannon from some forgotten war pointed futilely at the far bank, and Hale stopped, letting the umbrella drip between them. "For what it's worth—and I understand it isn't worth much—I reviewed your file after the verdict. Out of diligence. The evidence was compelling. The barge logs, the testimony of your first mate, the manifest discrepancies. Compelling," he repeated, softly, "and incomplete. There were questions I raised at the time. I was overruled. These things happen in courts, Captain. The machinery grinds toward a conclusion, and sometimes the conclusion arrives before the truth does."

Ivo stood very still. Six years she had imagined this conversation—in fevered, 3 a.m. versions where she screamed at him, where she produced the proof that never existed, where the courtroom rose up and reversed itself like a flock of starlings. In none of those versions had he been gentle. Gentleness from Corven Hale was worse than cruelty. Cruelty she could fight.

"You signed the sentencing recommendation," she said.

"I did. As the law required." He turned the umbrella slightly, shedding water. "But here is what I have learned since, Captain, and why I've come out in the rain to say it: the law is a current, not a compass. It moves everything in its reach, but it points nowhere. Men who mistake one for the other end up wrecked on shoals they never charted. You, I think, learned that lesson early. It's made you careful. Careful captains live long lives on this river." He smiled again. "Careful captains also notice things. Which brings me to my toll."

"There it is."

"There it is." He seemed pleased that she'd seen it coming, as if her intelligence were a compliment he intended to collect. "The Halvorsen expedition holds permits issued under my seal. Its findings, whatever they prove to be, will pass back through this district on their way to the capital—to journals, to ministries, to men in marble rooms who decide what rivers get dammed and what towns get drowned and what histories get written about it afterward. I am asking you, Captain, as a courtesy between professionals, to keep me informed. What they find. What they destroy. What they try to smuggle past my inspection stations. In exchange—" he paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over the incision— "your file could be misplaced. Files do go missing. Clerks are careless. A fire in the records annex, perhaps. Six years of exile, ended by an accident of paperwork. You'd be Ivo Thornbury again, unencumbered. Free to hold a license in your own name. Free to marry, own property, walk into a courthouse as a witness instead of a defendant."

The rain ticked against the umbrella. Somewhere below, the mill wheel groaned on, patient as the river.

*Free.* The word went through her like brandy through cold blood—warmth first, then the burn, then the knowledge of what it cost. She thought of her father's compass in her coat pocket, the needle that pointed nowhere useful anymore. She thought of six years of signing other women's names to manifests, of sleeping with her boots by the door, of the word *convicted* sitting in every ledger line of her life like a stain no lye would lift. And she thought: *he knows exactly what to offer me, which means he knows exactly what I lost, which means he has read my file the way I read water—line by line, looking for the rocks.*

"And if I decline?"

Hale sighed, genuinely regretful, a performance so good she almost applauded. "Then nothing, Captain. Nothing at all. You'll carry your crates upriver, the expedition will do whatever expeditions do, and I will continue to administer this district with the diligence my office demands. Though—" he tilted his head, considering— "I will confess to a certain anxiety. Rivers in flood are dangerous. Uncharted reaches more so. Expeditions go silent. Boats are lost with all hands, and the paperwork that follows is so very tedious. Insurance inquiries, next-of-kin notifications. One hates to think of a capable captain ending as a footnote in someone else's tragedy." He lowered the umbrella a fraction, and the rain touched his shoulders without his seeming to notice. "Of course, a captain who cooperated would find the river remarkably well marked for her. Soundings updated. Hazards flagged. Friends in every lock office between here and the delta."

"And the artifacts? If the expedition finds something worth finding—something the capital would want to see—what happens to it then?"

Something moved behind the ice-colored eyes, quick as a fish turning under the surface. Gone before she could name it.

"Artifacts," Hale said mildly, "are the property of the Crown's survey office, and the survey office reports to the ministry, and the ministry reports to men who value stability above sentiment. If Professor Halvorsen has dredged up something inconvenient from the bottom of this river, Captain, then the kindest thing—for him, for his people, for everyone—is for it to be handled properly. Quietly. By men experienced in handling things quietly." He extended his hand again, and this time she noticed the signet ring, dark stone, worn smooth. "You needn't answer today. Think of it as a standing offer, like credit at a chandlery. Available whenever you find yourself in need."

"I find myself in need of nothing," Ivo said, "except to cast off before the stage drops."

"Of course." He didn't withdraw the hand immediately; he held it there, patient, letting the refusal hang, and when she finally took it his grip was warm and firm and entirely without offense taken. That frightened her more than anger would have. Angry men were predictable. This man filed things away. "One more thing, purely professional. Your two passengers—the surveyors. Brack and Pell, I believe they signed as. Fine men. Thorough. They'll be sending me regular reports, so you needn't trouble yourself with the burden of correspondence." His smile deepened by a single degree. "Unless, of course, you'd prefer their reports to contain something specific. A well-placed word in a well-read letter can accomplish wonders, Captain. Even for a woman with a sealed file. Think on it."

He tipped his hat, turned, and walked back along the levee with the umbrella riding his shoulder like a standard, and Ivo stood by the useless old cannon and watched him go and understood, with the flat certainty of a sounding lead hitting bottom, that she had not been offered a bargain at all.

She'd been fitted for a hook. And the bait wasn't her freedom—it was her hope, which was worse, because hope was the one cargo she'd never figured out how to jettison.

* * *

Aboard, the rain had settled in for the evening, drumming on the texas roof and streaming down the windows in ropes. The *Maren Lee* sat heavy with fuel and unsaid things. Ivo ate supper alone in the pilothouse—hard bread, smoked fish, coffee boiled twice—and watched the lights of Kessel Bend smear in the wet glass. On the wharf below, the black launch still idled at its mooring, its running lamp burning steady, and twice she caught the glow of a lantern signaling from the customs shed toward her boat: short-short-long, short-short-long. Pell answering from the main deck, casual as a man lighting a pipe.

Reporting already. Of course. The toll had begun collecting itself.

Sena Voss appeared at the pilothouse door near midnight, wrapped in an oilskin too big for her, hair damp, carrying two cups of coffee and the expression of a woman who had been listening at bulkheads and wasn't going to pretend otherwise. She set one cup on the chart table without asking and leaned against the doorframe.

"He came aboard the dock for you specifically," Sena said. "Brack shaved."

"I noticed."

"Hale doesn't leave his courthouse for fuel inspections. My brother wrote about him—before." Her voice did the small catch it always did around the word *brother*, and smoothed itself over by force. "He called him the river's undertaker. Everything upstream of Kessel Bend that disappears, Hale signs the certificate."

Ivo drank her coffee and said nothing. Through the streaming glass, the launch's lamp burned on, patient as a hooked fish's eye.

"What did he offer you?" Sena asked quietly.

"My file," Ivo said. "Lost in a fire. My name cleaned. All I have to do is tell him what the expedition finds." She turned the cup in her hands. "And I told him no."

Sena was silent a moment. Outside, the rain thickened, and far upriver—so far it might have been thunder, or might have been imagination—came a long, low rumble that neither of them remarked on, because remarking on it would have made it real.

"That's the wrong answer," Sena said at last. "Isn't it."

"It's the only honest one." Ivo set down the cup. "Which on this river is usually the expensive one."

Above the town, unnoticed by either of them, the river gauge at the Kessel Bend bridge slid another hand's width up its painted scale, and the water coming down from the high country ran brown and fast and full of everything the mountains were letting go.
