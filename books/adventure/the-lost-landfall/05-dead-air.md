## 5. Dead Air

The river rose while we slept.

I knew it the way a sailor knows weather in her bones—by the sound of it. The current had changed its voice overnight, from that low conversational mutter to something deeper, more insistent, like a man talking in his sleep who is beginning to dream of running. I lay in my bunk beneath the wheelhouse and listened to the hull flex against the mooring lines at Grayling Landing, and I thought: *flood stage by Thursday.*

Which meant three days, maybe four, before the narrows upstream went from difficult to suicidal.

Which meant whatever was waiting for us at the Halvorsen camp, we would be going into it with the water climbing at our back.

I got up before dawn because sleep had become a negotiation I kept losing. The galley stove took its usual argument with the damp kindling, and I stood over it with my hands out, watching the flame catch, watching it throw orange light across the pots and pans I'd bought secondhand from a widow in Pell's Reach who'd cried when she handed them over—not for the pans, but because her husband had hung them, and now they were leaving too. Everything on this boat had a history of leaving. That was the deal you made with the river. It carried things away and called it work.

The coffee was terrible. I drank two cups anyway.

By full light we were casting off, and the two surveyors—I'd stopped thinking of them as passengers somewhere around the second day—appeared on deck with the punctuality of men on a schedule set by someone else. Marsh and Quill, their papers said. Papers that said nothing else at all, which was itself an answer if you knew how to read it.

"Morning, Captain," Marsh said. He was the taller one, gray at the temples, with hands too soft for survey work and boots too new. "We'll make good time today, I hope."

"River decides that," I said. "Not me."

He smiled the way a man smiles when he's decided not to be annoyed. Behind him, Quill stood at the rail with his binoculars—not looking at the water, not looking at the banks, but upriver, always upriver, as though he expected something to come down the current toward him and wanted to see it first.

They hadn't talked to each other all morning. That was the thing about them that had begun to itch at me like wet wool. Real partners talk. They complain, they argue about rations, they argue about nothing, because that's what people do when they've spent weeks together in the field. Marsh and Quill moved around each other like strangers in a train station, polite and weightless, and every so often one of them would glance at the other with an expression I could only describe as *checking*.

Last night I'd tested them. I'd sat down at supper and told them, casual as passing the salt, that the Kessel Bend lock had washed out its north gate in the spring flood and wouldn't reopen till autumn—which was a lie; I'd helped patch it myself in June. Marsh had nodded along, filed it away, no questions. But Quill had gone below decks within the hour, and through the bulkhead I'd heard the crackle of our own radio being keyed, transmitting on a frequency that wasn't mine.

Reporting downstream. To someone. About a lie.

You don't report lies unless you're paid to report everything.

I finished my coffee and climbed to the wheelhouse, and put the lie behind us along with the landing.

* * *

The morning ran long and green. The river widened here between bluffs furred with pine, and the mist burned off slow, and for an hour or two it was possible to pretend this was just work—the honest, muscle-sore work of pushing cargo against current, which was the only kind of work I'd ever been any good at. Deckhand Ruiz sang under his breath while he checked the lashings, some ballad in his grandmother's language that I didn't understand but that made the crates seem less like crates. The engine settled into its rhythm. The wheel answered my hands.

At noon I allowed myself the luxury of believing the day might stay ordinary.

Then the radio spoke.

It came in at 12:40, which was right—it was supposed to come in at 12:40, the Halvorsen expedition's scheduled check, noon and dusk, standard protocol for contracted resupply runs. What wasn't right was the voice.

"—copy, *Merrow*, do you copy—" Static, thick as wool. Then, underneath it, a voice I recognized from the contract negotiations: Professor Halvorsen himself, old and precise, the kind of voice that had spent forty years lecturing rooms into silence. Except now it was stripped down to wire. "—do not—repeat, do not—" A burst of static ate half a sentence. When he surfaced again he was saying numbers. Coordinates, maybe. Or a warning spelled out the only way left to him. "—they know—we can't hold the—"

Gone. Just the river's own hiss filling the band, that white noise that sounds like nothing and is actually everything—the whole world's weather compressed into a whisper.

I keyed the transmitter. "*Merrow* to Halvorsen camp. Say again your last. Halvorsen camp, say again."

Nothing.

"*Merrow* to Halvorsen camp, acknowledge."

Nothing. I tried for ten minutes, working down the frequencies, watching the needle swing through dead air the way a diver watches for a body. Ruiz came up and stood in the wheelhouse doorway without speaking, because he'd heard it too, and because Ruiz was the kind of man who understood that some silences are louder than alarms.

Behind me, boots on the ladder. Both of them. Of course both of them.

"Turn back."

Marsh said it flatly, without preamble, without even pretending it was a request. He stood with his hands clasped behind him like a man inspecting troops. "The expedition has gone dark. Protocol in a communications failure is withdrawal to the last confirmed contact point. You're contractually—"

"I'm contractually obligated to deliver supplies," I said. "That's what I'm paid for. That's what this boat is for." I turned back to the radio, keyed it again, let the static answer for me. "And that transmission didn't sound like a radio failure. Radios fail politely. That sounded like a man shouting over something."

"It sounded like interference," Quill said. He'd positioned himself near the chart table, and I noticed—he was very careful about how he did it—that he was standing where he could see the course I'd penciled in. "Storms build fast in these reaches this time of year. Atmospheric conditions alone could account—"

"You were listening to the same broadcast I was," I said. "So tell me why a professor whose permits are clean, whose camp is legal, whose only visitors in six weeks have been fish and weather—tell me why that man says *do not* and *they know* and *can't hold*."

Neither of them answered. Which was an answer.

"Captain Thornbury." Marsh's voice dropped into a register I remembered from a courtroom, years ago—a lawyer's voice, patient, reasonable, built to make you feel like the unreasonable one. "Consider your position. Rising water behind you. Unknown situation ahead. Two paying clients advising withdrawal. If anything happens to this vessel above Grayling, the liability—"

"The liability," I said, "is mine. Has been since I signed the articles. Now get off my wheelhouse."

For a moment nobody moved. The engine thudded below us, steady as a heart. Then Marsh smiled—thin, correct, a door closing rather than opening—and went down the ladder, and Quill followed, and I heard them talking on the aft deck in voices pitched exactly low enough that I couldn't make out words but high enough that I knew they wanted me to hear them talking.

Ruiz lingered. "Cap," he said quietly, in Spanish first and then English, because he always gave me the choice, "the water's coming up fast. Whatever we choose, we should choose it soon."

"I know."

"And those two—"

"I know."

He nodded and went back to his lashings, and I stood at the wheel and looked upriver at the green distance and felt the moment arrive the way weather arrives—not suddenly, but all at once, the pressure dropping, the air going strange, the knowledge settling into the chest that there is no version of this day anymore in which you turn around.

Because here was the arithmetic I'd been doing since dawn, the ugly sum I couldn't stop carrying: a garbled transmission naming *they*. Two passengers who reported to someone downstream. A magistrate at Kessel Bend who had smiled at me like a man holding a hook already set, who had offered to lose my file—my file, eleven years of ruin in a folder—in exchange for reports on what the expedition found. And now the expedition, finding *something*, screaming fragments into the static.

Corven Hale had put those men on my boat. I would have bet the hull on it. And whatever Hale wanted from Aldermere—or wherever it was the Halvorsen dig had really gotten to—he wanted it badly enough to burn a professor's voice out of the airwaves to get it.

If I turned back, I delivered my passengers to their employer, and whatever happened upstream happened without witnesses, and I went on being the woman Hale owned on paper. A file in a drawer. A hook with nothing on it yet.

If I went on—

I looked at my hands on the wheel. Scarred knuckles, rope-burned palms, the hands of a woman who had spent eleven years learning that the law was a building with Hale's name on the door. There was no justice in that building. There never had been. But there was a camp upriver full of people who had trusted me with their mail and their medicine and their flour, and there was a voice on the radio saying *can't hold*, and there was a rising river, and I had a boat.

A boat was not much. But it was more than most people had, and it was pointed the right way.

* * *

I rang for full ahead at two o'clock.

The *Merrow* leaned into the current and the current leaned back, and the old girl grumbled the way she always did when I asked her for everything she had, but she gave it. Below, I heard Marsh's voice rise in protest and Ruiz's answer, calm and immovable: *"Captain's boat, señor. Captain's river."*

By four the banks had narrowed and the water had gone from green to the brown-yellow of stirred silt, and driftwood came down in pieces big enough to matter—one whole birch, roots and all, spinning slowly past us like a drowned woman's hair. I posted Ruiz on the bow with a boathook and kept us in the channel by eye and memory and the small stubborn faith that the river forgives attention.

Quill appeared beside me at five, without invitation, and stood watching the water.

"You'll go aground in the narrows at this stage," he said. Not threatening. Almost gentle. That was worse. "Flood-stage water in the narrows eats boats, Captain. Eats crews. Nobody would ever find the wreck until autumn, and by then—" He shrugged, a small economical motion. "Whatever you think you're sailing toward, it isn't worth drowning for."

"Is that what you'd tell your employer?" I asked. "That I drowned? Would that be convenient?"

Something flickered across his face—surprise, quickly mastered, but surprise nonetheless, the first true expression I'd ever seen him wear. He looked at me for a long moment, and the river filled the silence between us with its deepening voice.

"Everyone serves someone, Captain," he said finally. "The only question worth asking is whether they'd serve you back."

He went below. I watched him go, and then I watched the water, and I turned his sentence over like a stone found in a net, examining what lived underneath it. Because it hadn't been a threat. It had been an offer. A door held open a crack, by a man who reported to someone downstream and had just discovered, perhaps, that the someone downstream was not a man he wanted to drown for.

I filed it away. The river teaches you to file things away; the current brings them back around when it's time.

Dusk came early under thickening cloud, and at 6:40 I sat at the radio for the evening check that didn't come. I called every quarter hour until nine. I called until my voice went hoarse and the static became the only voice I had, that vast indifferent whisper, the sound of a world not answering.

At nine-thirty, Ruiz brought me coffee I didn't ask for and stood looking out the wheelhouse glass at the black water sliding past, flecked with foam now, the current visibly faster, the river gathering itself for the narrows.

"There's a light," he said.

I followed his gaze. Far upstream, where the gorge pinched down and the hills crowded close, a point of yellow flickered through the rain-haze. Steady. Deliberate. Not lightning, not a farmhouse—there were no farmhouses in the gorge, not since the government bought out the valley thirty years ago and drowned what it didn't buy.

A signal. Or a trap. On this river, with these passengers, the two had stopped being different things.

I put the wheel over and aimed the bow at it, into the rising water, into the dark, and the *Merrow* answered, and there was no going back that I cared to take.

Behind me, on the aft deck, I heard Marsh keying the radio again—transmitting downstream, telling whoever held his leash that the captain had chosen the wrong direction.

Let him report it. By the time anyone downstream could do anything about it, we'd be through the narrows or at the bottom of them, and either way, we'd be past the point where other people's orders reached.

The light blinked once, twice, and held.

"Hold on, Professor," I said to the dark. "We're coming."
