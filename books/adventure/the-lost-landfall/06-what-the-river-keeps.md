## 6. What the River Keeps

The moon came up the color of a coin left too long in water, and Sena Voss thought: *good*. She had spent her whole life learning to read light, and this was the light she trusted least and loved most—the kind that made the river lie beautifully.

She stood at the rail behind the pilothouse with her satchel of maps pressed to her hip like a child she was afraid someone would take from her. Below, Ivo Thornbury worked the wheel in shirtsleeves despite the cold, her hands moving on the spokes with an ease that looked almost lazy and wasn't. Sena had watched those hands for three days now. They never hesitated. Even when the channel did.

"You're going to want to bear off," Sena said. "Port side. There's a bar forming where the creek comes in."

Ivo didn't turn her head. "There's no creek marked."

"There is if you're looking at my charts instead of the government's."

A pause. The engine throbbed beneath them like something alive and mildly annoyed. Then Ivo eased the wheel over, and thirty seconds later the bow swung wide around a long brown tongue of silt that had not existed when any surveyor worth his license had passed this way.

Ivo glanced back at her then, just once, and said nothing. But it was the kind of nothing that had weight to it, and Sena felt it settle somewhere under her ribs.

* * *

They had argued about the charts on the first night, properly argued, the kind of argument that leaves both parties hoarse and secretly pleased. Ivo had spread the official navigation sheets across the chart table—beautiful things, printed in three colors, confident in every line—and Sena had laid her own work beside them without asking permission.

"Where did you get these?" Ivo had asked, in the voice of a woman deciding how much trouble she was in.

"I made them."

"Of what?"

"Of what's actually there."

The reach they were entering now appeared on the government sheets as a single placid ribbon between bluffs, annotated with a depth figure that Sena knew to be fiction and a note reading *UNIMPROVED — LOCAL KNOWLEDGE ADVISED*, which was the Survey's polite way of saying *we gave up*. Her own map of the same water covered four sheets and took up most of her satchel, and even it was honest only about its own ignorance. Half the channels in it were drawn in pencil. Pencil meant *I think*. Ink meant *I know*. The river above Kessel Bend was mostly pencil, and the moonlight tonight was going to make her guess or make her wrong, and she could not yet tell which.

Because that was the thing about the flood. The whole valley was rising. She'd seen it in the willows first—green trunks standing to their knees in brown water that should have been a field away—and then in the current itself, which had gone from a river's patient crawl to something with intent in it. Debris ran past the boat in the dark: fence rails, a door still on its hinges, a drowned hen folded neatly into an eddy like a paper boat. Somewhere upstream, rain was falling on ground that had already forgotten how to hold it.

"We shouldn't be running this at night," Ivo said—not to her, exactly; to the wheel, to the river, to herself. "Not at this stage. Not on pencil lines."

"No," Sena agreed. "We shouldn't."

"But you're not going to ask me to turn around either."

It wasn't a question, so Sena didn't answer it. They had already had that conversation, or rather they had had the shape of it without the words, back at the fuel stop, when the garbled radio check had come through and the two silent surveyors had begun saying *turn back* in the flat voices of men reciting instructions. Ivo had pointed the bow upriver instead, and Sena had understood, in the way you understand a thing in your body before your mind consents to it, that she had chosen the right boat.

Now the right boat was feeling its way up a drowned valley by moonlight, and the silence between the two women at the wheel had changed texture. It was no longer the silence of strangers being careful. It was the silence of people who had run out of reasons to pretend.

* * *

At half past eleven Ivo brought her alongside a gravel bar in the lee of a bluff and shut the engine down, and the quiet that rushed in was enormous. River-quiet at night isn't silence; it's a hundred small sounds agreeing to speak at once—water worrying the gravel, willows combing the current, the boat's timbers ticking as they cooled. Sena stood on the afterdeck and let it all in through her skin.

Ivo came out carrying two tin mugs and handed her one without ceremony. Coffee, hours old, tasting of tin and woodsmoke. Sena drank it anyway.

"You knew that bar was there," Ivo said. "Before we saw it."

"The creek told me." Sena nodded toward the dark mouth of the tributary, invisible now except as a colder breath of air moving across the main channel. "Water coming in that fast pushes its own delta. You can smell the difference in the current if the wind's right. And the bluff shadow—" She stopped. "You don't want the lecture."

"Keep talking," Ivo said, and leaned against the rail, and looked at her with an attention that Sena found more disarming than hostility would have been. "I've been running this river eight years off other people's guesses. I'd like to hear how a person actually knows it."

So Sena talked. About reading the water's surface the way a page is read—the smooth glassy tongues that mark deep channel, the boiling confused water that marks a snag, the long V of a wake that means the current has found something to argue with. About how a flooded river is a different animal wearing the same skin, and how everything you learned at low water becomes a lie at high. About her father, who had been a lockkeeper, and had taught her to draw before he taught her to write, because *the river doesn't sign anything twice, girl; learn to put down what you saw, not what you hoped.*

"He sounds like he'd have gotten along with my mother," Ivo said. "She used to say the river keeps better books than any bank."

"What happened to her?"

The question was out before Sena could weigh it, and she braced for the wall to go up—the wall she'd seen Ivo raise twice already, once over the matter of Kessel Bend, once over why a captain with her skill was running resupply contracts at rates that said *desperate*. But Ivo only turned her mug slowly in her hands, watching the coffee circle.

"She drowned," Ivo said. "When I was nineteen. Ferry accident at Marlow Cut. Overloaded, bad trim, night crossing." A pause. "The inquiry called it pilot error. The pilot was me."

Sena kept very still. "You were nineteen."

"I'd run that crossing forty times. That night the load shifted. Two people went in the water. My mother was one of them." Ivo's voice was level in the way that ice is level—flat, and bearing more than it looks like it can. "That's the part of the file that's true. Everything else in it—" She stopped, and shook her head once, sharply, like a horse refusing a bit. "Everything else is Corven Hale's handwriting."

The name landed between them like a stone into still water. Sena had heard it twice since boarding—once from the deckhand, spat, and once from Ivo herself, in the tone reserved for weather and disease. She knew the shape of the man from reputation: the magistrate whose jurisdiction began at Kessel Bend and ended wherever his gunboat got bored, who owned the warehouses and the judges and, rumor insisted, the Survey office that had declared a certain reach of the valley unimprovable and closed to inquiry.

And there it was. The door she had been standing outside for three days, waiting to see whether it opened.

"Hale," Sena said carefully. "He's why you took this contract. Through his jurisdiction. With crates you weren't allowed to inspect."

Ivo looked at her for a long moment. Moonlight did strange favors to a face—it erased the wariness, left only the bone of the thing underneath, and what was underneath was tired, and proud, and younger than the river had any business letting her be.

"You're doing it too," Ivo said. "Asking sideways. You've been circling something since the fuel stop. So here's the trade: I'll answer one question straight, and you'll answer one straight. No cartographer's pencil. Ink."

Sena's heart was going hard enough that she was sure Ivo could hear it over the water. "Agreed."

"Your turn first. Why does a woman carry maps of a reach that officially doesn't exist, and pay passage on a supply boat to get to them? What's up this valley that's worth drowning for?"

Sena set down her mug. Her hands wanted to shake and she forbade them to. Some doors you have to walk through while your courage is still warm, or you never walk through at all.

"Aldermere," she said.

The word went out over the water and did not come back.

* * *

"My brother was on the first survey team," Sena said. "Twenty-nine years ago. Before the dam. Before the flooding. Tam Voss—you won't find him in the records either, which was the first thing that taught me the records were lying."

Ivo had gone very still. Sena took that as leave to continue, and once begun, she found she could not have stopped if the boat caught fire.

"Everyone knows the story they printed. The Corven Dam project—flood control, they called it, electrification, progress. The valley above Kessel Bend was condemned, the towns bought out, the gates closed in the spring of 'sixty-one. Three towns. Harrow's Ford, Millbrook, and Aldermere." She said the names slowly, laying them down like cards. "Eleven hundred people relocated. That's the number in the report. Eleven hundred, tidy as arithmetic."

"And the truth?"

"The truth is that the buyouts weren't finished when the gates closed. The truth is that the reservoir filled faster than the schedule said it would—because the spring melt came early, and because someone ordered the sluices shut ahead of plan, and the order was signed by a junior land commissioner named Corven Hale, whose signature everyone downstream was very motivated never to look at too closely. The truth is that Aldermere didn't empty. Aldermere drowned with people in it." Sena's voice had gone quiet and hard, and she let it. "Forty-one souls, by the count my family kept. The official record says the town was evacuated on the ninth of April. My grandmother stood on the bluff on the tenth of April and watched the church spire go under with the bell still hanging in it."

The river ran dark beneath them, patient, keeping its books.

"They called it a tragedy," Sena went on. "An act of God. There was an inquiry, and the inquiry was managed, and the managing was Hale's first great work—he was twenty-six years old and he buried a valley and built a career on the grave. And a year later, when some of the displaced families wouldn't stop petitioning, the Survey sent a team up to 'assess residual structures.' Four men. My brother was the youngest. Twenty-two. He wrote me a letter from Kessel Bend the night before they went upriver, and the letter said—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish. "It said, *They're not sending us to assess anything. They're sending us to see what the water gives back, and to decide who gets to know. If I'm slow writing next time, it's because I'm being careful.*"

"That was the last letter?"

"That was the last letter. The team went up in June. The Survey reported in August that the assessment was complete and the findings sealed pending review. In September the team was listed as having resigned their posts and dispersed. Four resignations, four signatures, filed the same day." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Tam couldn't even spell *resignation* without asking me. He was twenty-two and he drew better than any man in the office and he signed his own disappearance with a hand that wasn't his."

Ivo hadn't moved through any of it. Now she said, quietly: "Halvorsen. The expedition. That's what they're looking for. The town."

"The town, and what's in it. The courthouse records, the dam company's correspondence, whatever the first team found and hid before they vanished. Halvorsen spent nine years and most of his reputation getting permits to survey the drawdown zone—because here's the joke, Ivo, the beautiful rotten joke. The dam was built crooked. Bad siting, bad concrete, and everyone too well paid to say so, and for thirty years the reservoir has been silting up and the engineers have been quietly lowering the operating pool to keep the structure from failing. This drought finished the job. The water's dropped fourteen feet." Sena turned and pointed upriver, into the black. "Somewhere up that valley, right now, the top of Aldermere's bell tower is standing in open air for the first time in a generation. Streets. Foundations. Maybe the courthouse archive, if the mud was kind. Everything Hale drowned, coming back up to breathe."

For a long moment Ivo said nothing. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, stripped of its captain's briskness, carrying something raw.

"The crates I hauled for you people. Sealed. Manifests that said *instruments* and *provisions* and one crate I'd have sworn held paper by the weight of it. Paper and iron." Her jaw tightened. "Hale offered me my file. At Kessel Bend. My mother's death and my trial and every year since, wrapped up and lost, if I'd report what the expedition found. He smiled when he said it. Like the hook was already set." She looked at Sena. "He wasn't fishing for artifacts, was he. He was fishing for proof. Because he knows what's surfacing, and he needs to know how much of it we have."

"Yes."

"And your brother—"

"Is why I'm here." Sena picked up her mug again just to have something to hold. "Twenty-nine years, Ivo. Everyone who might have spoken is dead or bought or frightened, and the paper trail dies at Kessel Bend like a road washed out. Except the river kept everything. Rivers are the worst liars in the world—they give back whatever you sink in them, eventually, on their own schedule. Tam knew that. It's why he went. And if there's anything of him up that valley—a note, a cache, a carving, his actual bones—I am going to stand where he stood and read what he left, and I am going to put it down in ink."

The words hung in the cold air. Sena realized she was crying, not dramatically, just steadily, the way a roof leaks, and that she had not cried about Tam in years, and that it was Ivo's stillness that had allowed it—that the captain had given her, without a word, the one thing no one else had: a silence safe enough to fall apart in.

Then Ivo reached over and took the mug from her hands before she dropped it, and set both mugs on the rail, and did not touch her otherwise, and somehow that restraint was the kindest thing anyone had done for Sena in a decade.

"One question straight," Ivo said at last. "My turn. You said the first team was sent to decide *who gets to know*. Do you think they decided? Do you think there's a cache up there—records, evidence—deliberately hidden?"

"I think," Sena said, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, "that my brother was the best field surveyor the office ever produced, and that he had six weeks and a head start on anyone hunting him, and that if a man wanted to hide the truth of a drowned town, the one place no magistrate would ever dare dig is the town itself. Under the water. Where Hale's own crime would have to be dug through to reach it." She managed something that was almost a smile. "The river keeps its secrets. But it keeps them *findable*. That's the difference between a river and a man like Hale. The river never intends to keep them forever."

Ivo nodded slowly. Then she looked upriver, into the dark where the bluffs narrowed and the current quickened, and Sena watched the change come over her—not softening, exactly. Hardening into purpose, the way wax hardens into a seal.

"Then we'd better get you there," Ivo said. "First light, we run the Narrows Reach. After that, nothing on any chart, yours or theirs. Just what the water tells us."

* * *

They raised the anchor at dawn, and the fog came off the water in ropes, and Sena stood at Ivo's shoulder in the pilothouse calling depths from soundings she barely trusted while the valley closed in around them—bluffs steepening, current sharpening, driftwood running thicker. Twice the lead line came up short and Ivo threaded them through gaps that Sena would afterward swear had not existed until the moment the boat needed them.

Near midmorning the fog tore open for half a minute, and Sena, squinting upreiver, saw something that stopped her breath.

On the eastern bluff, above a fold of drowned orchard, stood a stone chimney. Alone. Whole. Its hearth faced the water like an open mouth, and around its base the flood-stripped bank showed raw clay and the pale scatter of foundation stones, and beyond it, far out in the middle channel where no island belonged, a dark shape broke the current—a ridge of masonry, weed-hung, with something slender rising from its center that might, at this distance, in this light, have been a tower.

Ivo saw it too. Sena knew because the engine note changed—the barest easing, the captain's instinct to give strange water room—and because Ivo's hands had gone white on the spokes.

"Aldermere," Sena whispered. It was not a question. Nothing in her life had ever been less of a question.

But Ivo was frowning at the near bank, at the chimney, at something below it that the fog was still hiding, and when she spoke her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman reporting damage.

"Sena. Look at the landing below the chimney. Look at the ash."

The fog shifted. The landing came clear: a shelf of gravel where boats had tied up for a hundred years before the water took it, and on it, unmistakable even at distance, the black sprawl of a burned fire ring—and beside it, arranged with terrible neatness, a row of supply crates.

Intact. Unopened. Abandoned.

Nobody builds a campfire that size and walks away from full crates. Nobody, Sena thought, unless they left in a hurry—or were made to.

Ivo rang for more speed.
