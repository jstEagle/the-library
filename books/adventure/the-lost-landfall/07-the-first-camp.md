## 7. The First Camp

The smoke told us before the bend did.

I smelled it first—old smoke, days old, the sour ghost of it that clings to a riverbank like a mourner who won't leave. Then Sena saw it from the bow, her hand rising without a word, pointing at the gray smudge hanging in the cedars where the Halvorsen camp was supposed to be. Nobody said anything for a long minute. The engine talked enough for all of us, knocking and muttering in the current, and the two surveyors stood at the rail with their wrong boots planted wide, watching the shore like men reading a verdict.

"Could be a cook fire gone bad," Renn said at my shoulder. My deckhand had a talent for hope the way some men had a talent for cards—he could make it appear out of nothing when he needed it.

"Cook fires don't smell three days dead," I said.

I brought us in slow, against every instinct I owned. A captain learns early that smoke on a bank is a door you don't open until you know what's behind it. But there were eleven people upriver who'd stopped answering their radio, and one of them had a name that made Sena Voss go quiet in a way I was beginning to hate, and so I opened the door.

The channel into the camp's landing was narrow and silted, and I took it with the current pushing at my stern like a hand between my shoulder blades. The *Maren Lee* grounded soft in the shallows with a sigh, as if she too would rather have kept going. I dropped the gangplank myself.

* * *

The camp had burned from the east end outward. That was the first thing I noticed, standing in the ash with the wind moving the cedars overhead, dropping gray flakes on my shoulders like dirty snow. Fire spreads hungry and stupid; it takes what's nearest and follows the wind. This fire had started at the radio tent—the char pattern fanned out from it in a clean semicircle—and then it had stopped. Stopped, at the edge of the supply lean-to, where a line of scorched earth ended as neat as a plowed furrow.

"Flood did this?" Renn asked, though his voice said he already knew better.

"Floods don't burn one tent and apologize to the next." I crouched and pressed two fingers into the black. Cold through and through, packed hard by rain since. Three days, maybe four. "Somebody set this. Somebody who wanted the radios dead and nothing else touched."

The supply lean-to stood untouched under its tarpaulin. Crates of flour, salt pork, lamp oil, rope—enough to winter a dozen people, sitting in the open not fifty feet from a burned tent, unlooted. Even the lanterns hung on their pegs. Whoever came here hadn't come to rob. They'd come to cut the expedition's voice out of the world and leave its body breathing.

I felt the two surveyors behind me before I turned. They'd come ashore without being asked, which they never did, and they were standing at the edge of the burn looking at the radio tent with an attention that sat wrong on them. The tall one—Verrick, he'd given as his name, though I'd have bet the boat it wasn't—was studying the ground around the tent's foundation, reading it the way I read water. Looking for footprints. His own kind's footprints.

"You two stay by the landing," I said.

"We're hired hands same as anyone," Verrick said. "Surveyors. We should assess—"

"You'll assess the riverbank." I kept my voice flat as a chart. "This is expedition property and I'm responsible for it. Go count driftwood if you need work."

Something passed between him and the short one, Doss—a flicker, quick as a fish turning—and they went. Not far. Far enough to look obedient, close enough to listen.

Sena was already inside the ruins of the radio tent, moving through the ash on her toes like a woman walking through a church. She crouched at the center of the burn and lifted something with a stick: the radio's backing plate, warped and melted, its vacuum tubes burst into little glass flowers. She turned it over twice, then set it down with a care that made my chest tighten.

"They smashed the tubes first," she said quietly. "Look—the casing's burned after. Someone put the fire on top of a wrecked machine. If they'd only wanted it destroyed by flame, they'd have left it whole."

"You read wreckage like your brother read maps," I said, and regretted it the moment it left me, because her face did something complicated and then went still.

"Somebody taught us both," she said, and kept digging.

It was Renn who found the note. He came running from the cook shelter holding a strip of oilcloth like it might bite, and when I unfolded it my hands were steadier than I expected them to be. Inside, written in pencil in a cramped, hurried hand:

*Radios gone before we knew. Men came by boat at night, no lights, took nothing but our voices. H. knows what we found. Do not trust the magistrate. Do not bring this downriver. We've gone to the high ground past the narrows. If you are reading this, turn back or come armed.*

No signature. But the handwriting slanted hard to the right, and the *H* was crossed with two strokes, impatient, and I thought of a man in a white suit at Kessel Bend who smiled like a hook already set.

"Halvorsen wrote this," Sena said over my shoulder. Her breath stirred the paper. "I know his hand. He annotates everything—his margins are half mad with it." She pointed at the *do not trust*, where the pencil had pressed through the oilcloth. "That's him. That's exactly him."

"Do not trust the magistrate," I read aloud, because saying a thing out loud makes it more real, and I needed it real. "Corven Hale boarded my boat and offered to lose my file if I reported what the expedition found. I told him no. And now someone has burned their radios and left a warning with his initial on it."

"That doesn't prove—" Renn started.

"It proves enough." I folded the note into my coat, against my breastbone, where I keep the things I can't afford to lose. "It proves we're being watched by people who move at night without lights, and that the professor knew it, and that he ran for high ground rather than wait for whoever burned his voice to come back and take the rest."

I looked upriver. The water was rising—I'd been marking it against a root since morning, and it had climbed two fingers' width—and somewhere beyond the narrows, past channels that officially did not exist, eleven people were camped on high ground guarding whatever it was that made men burn radios.

"Captain," Sena said. It was the first time she'd used the word, and she used it deliberately, the way you plant a flag. "Whatever you're deciding, decide it knowing the surveyors heard every word."

"I know," I said. "That's why I'm deciding it out loud."

* * *

We didn't linger. I gave the camp an hour—Renn and I walked its full perimeter while Sena sketched the layout into her field book with quick, economical strokes, marking the burn, the lean-to, the landing, the game trail leading north toward the high ground. She drew the way she argued: precisely, and faster than you could follow.

There were boot prints along the trail. Several sets, weeks old, heading north. And near the landing, half-erased by rain, a single set heading south toward the water—narrow heels, deep toe-off, a man carrying weight. Carrying something heavy down to a boat in the dark.

I stood over those prints a long time. The river lapped at the mud six feet away, patient as a debt collector.

Back aboard, I posted watches. Double watches. I told the crew we'd anchor midstream overnight, well off the bank, engines cold but boiler warm, and nobody asked why, because riverfolk don't ask why about things that smell like smoke and lies.

The evening came down gray and thick. Rain started just after dark, a fine persistent drizzle that beaded on the deck boards and made the cedars along the bank whisper among themselves. I ate standing up at the wheel, chewing without tasting, and watched the shoreline dissolve into blackness. Somewhere out there, men who moved without lights knew exactly where we lay.

Sena found me at midnight. She didn't announce herself; she just arrived at my elbow with two cups of coffee, and handed me one, and leaned against the wheel housing beside me like she'd been doing it for years.

"You're not sleeping," she said.

"Neither are you."

"I slept on your chart table. It's very comfortable if you fold your arms correctly." She blew across her cup. Steam curled away into the drizzle. "Your watchman is asleep too, by the way. The one on the stern. He's got his coat over his head and he's snoring into his own collar."

"He's earned it."

"And the surveyors?"

I sipped. The coffee was terrible, burnt and bitter, the way coffee gets when it sits too long on a stove, and it was the best thing I'd tasted in days. "Verrick is awake. Has been since ten. He's lying in his bunk with his boots on."

"You checked?"

"I listened. Boots on a bunk frame sound different than boots off. Off, you get the thump of them hitting the floor, one, then two. On, you get nothing, and a man who sleeps in silence is a man waiting to move."

Sena looked at me sideways. In the low lamplight spilling from the wheelhouse, her face was all angles and shadow, and there was something in her expression I couldn't chart—respect, maybe, or the wariness of a woman realizing she'd tied herself to a captain who noticed everything, including her.

"My brother used to say the river keeps better records than any court," she said. "He'd have liked you."

"Where is he? Your brother. Truly."

She was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence, ticking on the roof above us. "Officially? Lost with the first survey team, seven years ago. Unofficially—" She turned her cup in her hands. "Unofficially, the last letter he sent me was postmarked from a town that isn't on any map, and it said, *the water went down further than they ever meant it to, Sena, and there are streets under there with laundry still strung.* That's all. That's the whole letter. I've read it four hundred times."

"Laundry still strung," I repeated.

"People don't string laundry in cities that were evacuated properly and mourned honestly." She said it simply, without heat, and somehow that was worse than if she'd shouted it. "Aldermere wasn't drowned by accident, Captain. And neither was my brother. And I think your magistrate knows it, and I think that's why he wants eyes on this expedition, and I think you know I'm right because you hid that note in your coat instead of reading it to the crew."

I said nothing. The river said plenty. It had risen another finger's width against the root I'd marked; I could see it even in the dark, the way the current had fattened, the way it pulled at the mooring line with a new insistence, like a dog that scents a storm coming.

"Get some sleep, Voss," I said finally. "Tomorrow we run the narrows."

"The narrows at rising water?" Her eyebrows went up. "You've seen the charts. There are no charts."

"There are no charts because somebody paid for there to be no charts. Which means the narrows are exactly where we'll find the truth." I drained my cup. "And tomorrow night, I want double eyes on that crate the surveyors have been sleeping beside. The one they brought aboard at Kessel Bend and never let out of arm's reach."

"You've been counting their crates?"

"I count everything on my boat. It's how I stay alive." I looked at her, and let her see all of it, the fear and the fury and the thing underneath that was starting to feel dangerously like trust. "They're going to run. Tonight or tomorrow. Men like that always run when the country gets honest. And when they do, I intend to know what they're carrying."

* * *

I was right about the hour and wrong about the night.

It came at the black end of dark, that hour when even the river seems to hold its breath. I was in the wheelhouse, pretending to study Sena's sketch of the narrows, actually listening—listening the way my father taught me, with my eyes shut and my whole skin turned into an ear. A boat this size talks in its sleep. Hull timbers tick as the temperature falls. Lines creak. Crewmen shift and mutter. Every sound has its place, and when a sound moves where it shouldn't be—

There. Forward, near the cargo hatch. The soft scrape of a boot sole on wet decking, placed with care, the walk of a man who has practiced being quiet.

I was out the wheelhouse door and across the deck before I'd fully decided to be, barefoot, in my shirt, the rain fine as spun glass on my arms. The forward hatch stood open a hand's width. Lamplight leaked from within, low and yellow.

Inside, Verrick knelt beside the crate, and the crate was open, and the lamplight showed me what it held: oilcloth bundles, tight-wrapped and sealed with wax, stacked with the neatness of a man packing something precious—or something damning. Beside the crate lay a canvas sling, rigged for carrying on the back. A load-out pack. He wasn't inspecting the contents. He was preparing to move them.

He heard me the same instant I understood him. He came up fast, taller than me by a head, and for one suspended moment we stood in the lamplight looking at each other across the open crate, two liars caught in each other's honesty.

"Can't sleep either?" he said.

"Where's your partner?"

"Asleep."

"In boots?"

Something flickered in his face—the first true thing I'd seen on it. "You listen to boots, Captain Thornbury. Hale said you would."

The name landed in the middle of the hold like a dropped anchor. Neither of us moved. The rain whispered on the hatch above us, and the river breathed against the hull, and somewhere aft a crewman snored on, innocent, dreaming of nothing.

"Say it again," I said quietly.

But he was already moving—not at me, past me, through the hatch, and I grabbed for him and got a fistful of slick oilskin and lost it, and he went over the rail into the black water with barely a splash, a man who had clearly done this before. By the time I reached the rail he was thirty feet out, swimming hard and silent for the northern bank, and the crate sat open in the lamplight, and I counted the bundles.

Six. There had been eight. I remembered, because I count everything.

Two bundles gone, and one man in the water, and a name spoken aloud that I had spent five years trying to forget.

Behind me, a match flared. Sena stood in the companionway in her coat, hair loose, the lit lamp trembling just slightly in her hand. She looked at the open crate, at the empty rail, at my face.

"Hale," she said. Not a question.

"Hale," I said. And the fear that had been living in me since the burned camp finished its work and hardened into something else, something with edges, something I could steer by. "Wake the crew. All of them. We run the narrows at first light—no, before first light. Before he can tell anyone which way we went."

Sena set the lamp down on the hatch coaming, and its light steadied, and so did she. "Then I'd better finish drawing you a chart," she said, "because Captain, nobody alive has ever taken the Maren Lee through those narrows in flood."

"Then we'll be the first thing the river remembers," I said, and went to get the anchor up.
