## 8. Aldermere Rising

The river told them before the charts did.

Sena felt it in the way the current changed its mind—slowing, spreading, losing its argument with itself—and then she saw it in the water's color, going from brown to a strange bruised green where silt gave way to something older underneath. She stood at the bow rail of the *Merry Adeline* with her brother's map open against her chest, held down by both hands as though the wind might steal it back.

"There," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended.

Ivo Thornbury stood at the wheel behind her, one hand resting easy on the spokes, and followed Sena's pointing finger with eyes that had gone narrow and still. Ahead, rising out of the low evening water like the rib of some drowned animal, stood a bell tower.

Not the top of it. Not a spire poking through like a hazard on a chart. The whole tower—arched belfry, louvered openings, even the iron crossbar where the bell had once hung—all of it above the surface, crusted white with thirty years of mineral stain, gulls nesting in the arched windows where the bell should have been. The drought had taken the river down farther than anyone alive remembered, and the river had given something back.

"Aldermere," Sena said.

"I know," Ivo said quietly. "I can read your face, Voss."

* * *

They anchored in the lee of what had once been the town's grain warehouse, its roofline just breaking the surface downstream, and took the skiff in through streets that existed now only as darker lines beneath two feet of clear water. Sena knelt in the bow with the lantern turned low, watching cobbles slide past under the hull, watching doorsteps rise out of the green gloom, watching a cast-iron fence stand in a front yard where the house had been carried off whole by the flood and never found. A child's tricycle lay on its side in the shallows, rusted to lace.

She had drawn this town from memory for eleven years. Her brother Tomas had drawn it from life, six weeks before the water came, and his survey notes—the ones she'd spent a decade pretending were only academic interest—described a place of four thousand souls, two churches, a tannery, a schoolhouse with a slate roof that caught the light like fish scales. The government maps said *reservoir*. The government maps said *uninhabited at time of inundation*. Tomas's maps said otherwise, in his cramped left-slanting hand: *Mrs. Okafor refuses evacuation, third house past the mill. School term ends Friday; families waiting to move after.*

The water had not waited for Friday.

Now the schoolhouse stood in eight feet of water with its bell tower twin to the church's, and Sena put her palm flat against the cool stone of the church wall as Ivo held the skiff steady, and she did not say anything at all for a long time. Ivo, to her credit, let the silence be. That was one of the things Sena had begun to notice about her—that the woman knew when silence was a kind of company rather than an absence of it.

"Your brother walked these streets," Ivo said finally. Not a question.

"Six weeks before they closed the gates." Sena traced a mason's mark in the stone, a compass rose cut by some long-dead craftsman. "He wrote that the town smelled like bread in the mornings. He wrote that in his last letter home." She looked up at the tower, at the gulls arguing in the belfry. "He thought he was documenting a scandal. He didn't know he was documenting a grave."

Ivo's jaw tightened. Sena watched her look downstream, back toward Kessel Bend, toward the jurisdiction of Magistrate Corven Hale, and saw the captain do arithmetic she clearly hated.

"Camp's upstream," Ivo said. "Past the tower?"

"Past the millrace. They'll be on the high ground—the old cemetery hill." Sena climbed back over the thwarts. "If they're still there."

"If they're still there," Ivo agreed, and gave the engine fuel, and the skiff slid up out of the drowned streets into the living dark.

* * *

Sena smelled the camp before she saw it: woodsmoke, yes, but under it the sourness of hunger, of bodies too long unwashed, of meat stretched past its natural life into salt and suspicion. The hill rose black against the stars, terraced with headstones, and among the graves someone had raised tents, strung tarps, built cook fires in stone rings that glowed like coals in a banked forge.

A voice challenged them from forty yards out—"Boat! Name yourselves!"—and the voice cracked on the second word, and Sena knew the speaker was young and frightened and armed.

"Sena Voss!" she called back. "Cartographer. Tomas Voss was my brother. I've come upriver with supplies."

Silence. Then a murmur passed along the hillside like wind through wheat, voices repeating her name, and someone laughed—a short, disbelieving bark—and someone else began, horribly, to weep.

"They know my name," she said to Ivo under the sound.

"Tomas talked about you," Ivo said. "Halvorsen's letters mentioned a sister who argued with him about everything."

"He was right to argue with me about everything. It kept him honest." Sena's throat hurt. "Come on. Before they decide we're a mirage."

The picket was a graduate student named Ellery Marsh, nineteen years old, holding a shotgun nearly as tall as herself, and when the lantern light reached Sena's face the girl made a sound like something breaking and did not lower the gun so much as forget it entirely. Behind her, figures were emerging from tents—hollow-cheeked, wrapped in blankets against the river damp, moving with the careful slowness of people who had learned to ration even their movements.

"Professor Halvorsen," Ellery managed. "You should—you need to see him first. Before anyone else. Please."

* * *

The professor's tent sat apart, near the cemetery's eastern edge, and it smelled of camphor and illness. Halvorsen lay propped against a crate of survey equipment, and Sena barely recognized him. In conference halls he had been a mountain of a man, loud, bearded, impossible. Now the beard had gone gray at the edges and the mountain had eroded down to bones and bright, feverish eyes.

"Voss," he rasped, and then, seeing Ivo behind her, "And you'd be the boat captain. Thornbury. Yes. Yes, I remember the name." Something moved behind his eyes at that—recognition, calculation, guilt—and Sena filed it away.

"You're starving," she said. "All of you. Why? You had supply drops scheduled. You had the radio."

"The radio's dead. Smashed, actually—every set in camp, the same night, three weeks ago." Halvorsen coughed, wet and deep. "And the supply drop never came because we sent it away. We told them the expedition was concluded. We told them to stop sending boats."

Sena went cold. "Why would you do that?"

"Because of what we found." The old man's hand crept across his blanket and closed around a canvas-wrapped object, and he held it to his chest the way a child holds a stolen thing. "Because everything we found is worth killing for, Miss Voss, and I have twenty-three people on this hill who are still alive, and the only reason they are still alive is that no one knows yet what we have. No one downstream. No one in the capital. No one." His eyes fixed on Ivo. "No one at all."

"Professor," Sena said carefully, "we came to resupply you. We came because you went silent. There are people downstream who think this expedition is lost."

"Good," Halvorsen said. "Let them think it."

* * *

They fed the crew of the *Merry Adeline* what the camp could spare—a thin barley soup, half a loaf of hard bread each—and Sena ate hers sitting on a headstone dated 1889 while the camp gathered around the largest fire to look at them. Not hostile, exactly. Worse than hostile: desperate, measuring, the way starving men look at a stranger's pack. She counted faces. Twenty-three, as Halvorsen had said, and every one of them had the same shadow under the eyes, the same flinch when a branch cracked in the dark.

Ellery Marsh sat down beside her, shotgun across her knees like a doll she no longer knew how to play with.

"We take turns on watch," the girl said. "Four hours. Everyone, even Professor Halvorsen, even sick. Because twice now someone's come up the river at night in a boat with no lights, and both times they turned back when we fired warning shots. And the second time—" She stopped. Started again. "The second time they left something on the shore. A note. It said, *Deliver the collection or the next boat won't be empty.*"

Sena's hands went still around her tin cup. "Signed?"

"No signature. But the paper was good paper. Government stock." Ellery stared into the fire. "Dr. Anselm says it's Hale's people. Everyone says it's Hale's people. But saying it doesn't make us less hungry, does it? We can't eat being right."

Across the fire, Ivo stood talking with a broad-shouldered woman Sena placed as Dr. Anselm, the expedition's field director, and their conversation had the flat, clipped rhythm of two professionals establishing terms. Sena couldn't hear the words, but she could read the architecture of it: Anselm gesturing at the tents, at the dig site below, at the crates stacked under tarps; Ivo shaking her head once, slow and final, and then again, and Anselm's shoulders setting like a door being barred.

They were refusing the supplies' escort down. Or refusing to let the artifacts go. Or both. Sena finished her soup and went to find out which.

* * *

The dig site lay in the old market square, now a basin of ankle-deep water and exposed mud, and by lantern light it looked like the floor of the world had been peeled back. Wooden crates stood in rows on pallets, each stenciled with catalog numbers. Trenches ran between the foundations of vanished stalls. And everywhere, everywhere, the artifacts: amphorae furred with river silt, a shop sign in painted letters—*HEDSTROM & SONS, DRY GOODS*—that someone had carefully crated like a Rembrandt, church ledgers swollen into bricks of pulp and dried into tablets, a wedding photograph in a water-stained frame showing a couple squinting into a sun that had set thirty years ago.

"This isn't treasure," Sena said quietly. Anselm had come down to walk her through it, and the woman's face in the lantern light was gaunt and fierce. "This is evidence."

"That's exactly what it is." Anselm lifted a ledger tablet with both hands, reverent. "Parish records. Property rolls. Evacuation notices with dates on them, Miss Voss—dates proving those notices were posted *after* the dam engineers reported the reservoir schedule to the ministry. Names of families who petitioned for relocation assistance and were denied. Denied in writing, by an office that answers to—" She stopped herself, jaw working.

"To Corven Hale," Sena said.

Anselm looked at her for a long moment. "Tomas's sister," she said at last. "Of course. He figured it out too, didn't he. Near the end." She set the ledger down gently, as if laying it in a coffin. "We have proof here that Aldermere wasn't evacuated. That the gates were closed on schedule anyway, because delaying cost money and the compensation fund had already been skimmed. Four thousand people, Miss Voss. The official record says eleven hundred relocated. We have school attendance registers that say otherwise."

"And Hale wants it all at the bottom of a lake."

"Hale wants it in his own vault, is my guess—close enough to bury, far enough to bargain with." Anselm's laugh had no humor in it at all. "Either way, it never reaches daylight. So yes. We refuse. We refuse everyone. We will sit on this hill and starve before we hand it to any boat that comes up that river, including yours. Including hers." She nodded uphill toward where Ivo stood. "I don't care what deal she thinks she has. I don't care whose name is on her papers. Everything on this hill stays until it goes somewhere that isn't controlled by the men who drowned this valley."

"She's not working for Hale," Sena said.

"Everyone is working for Hale. That's what Hale is *for*." Anselm picked up the lantern. "Get some sleep, Miss Voss. Tomorrow I'll show you the tower crypt. There's something there you should see—something Tomas marked on his last survey. And then you can tell me honestly whether your captain is the exception to the rule of the world, or just the best liar ever to row up to our door."

* * *

Sena didn't sleep. She climbed instead to the highest terrace of the cemetery, where the headstones gave way to open grass and the whole drowned valley spread out below in the moonlight—a silver plain with streets ghosting under it, the two towers standing sentinel, the dark line of the old millrace, and far off, the drowned orchards reaching up their dead white branches like drowned men waving.

Tomas had stood somewhere on this hill. She was sure of it. He had stood here with his theodolite and his notebook and he had looked down at this exact view, and he had written in his cramped hand whatever he had written, and then he had gotten into a boat with five other people and gone downriver to report, and the river had kept him.

She spoke to him sometimes. Out loud, when no one could hear. *I found your map, you idiot. I'm using it. I'm standing on your hill.* The wind moved through the grass and said nothing back, the way it always did, and she let it.

Footsteps behind her—soft, deliberate, unhurried. She knew the tread by now. Ivo settled onto the grass beside her without asking, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the valley breathed below them.

"Anselm showed me the crates," Ivo said eventually. "And the note they got on the shore. And the smashed radios." She turned a small stone over in her fingers, flicked it out into the dark. "Twenty-three people up here, starving, guarding a graveyard full of paper. And downstream there's a magistrate with a gunboat and a grudge against me personally, and two men on my boat who I'm fairly sure report to him." She exhaled. "I keep trying to find the version of this where everybody lives and nobody drowns, Voss. I keep coming up short."

"Maybe there isn't one."

"Maybe." Ivo was quiet a moment. "There's a version where I take the ledger and run for it alone. Fast boat, storm cover, I've run worse water. There's a version where I leave all of this and pretend I never came. And there's a version where I stay and fight for a hill full of strangers and lose my boat and probably my neck." She looked sideways at Sena, moonlight along the edge of her jaw. "Funny thing is, none of those used to be hard choices. I used to be very good at choosing me."

"What changed?"

Ivo didn't answer right away. Below them, a night bird cried over the drowned streets, and the bell tower held its silence.

"Ask me again when we're off this hill," she said, and stood, and offered Sena her hand.

Sena took it. Ivo's palm was warm and rope-hardened, and she did not let go immediately, and neither did Sena, and the moment stretched between them like a bridge over deep water.

Then a shot cracked across the valley—from the water side, from the dark below the millrace—and a second answered it, and the camp erupted into shouting and lantern light, and Ivo's hand went from warm to iron as she pulled Sena up and started downhill at a run.

At the shoreline, where the skiffs were tied, a figure was pushing off from the bank in one of them—one of her own crew, one of the two silent surveyors, a crate hauled aboard and a long oar driving the little boat out into the silver dark toward the main channel and the downstream current and whatever waited there.

"Cut the other skiff loose!" Ivo roared, already wading into the shallows. "Somebody cut it loose—we're going after him!"

Sena stood knee-deep in the cold black water of Aldermere, watching the traitor's wake unspool toward the open river, and understood that the siege had just stopped being a standoff.

It had become a race.
