## 9. Professor Halvorsen's Bargain

The fever came at dusk, the way it had come every dusk for three days, and with it Professor Eirik Halvorsen's mind began to wander the drowned streets of Aldermere as though the water had never come down at all.

Ivo found him propped against the bell tower's fallen stones, wrapped in a survey tarp gone grey with mildew, his beard matted, his hands—those famous hands that had drawn the first honest maps of the upper reach—twitching against the blanket as if still turning pages. The expedition's camp was a ring of lean-tos among the ruins, and the ruins themselves were a ring of black water, and beyond the water the narrows ran high and loud, carrying the sound of the flood up through the broken streets like a congregation murmuring in a language only the dead could read.

He opened his eyes when her boot scraped the stone.

"Captain Thornbury." His voice was a dry leaf. "Or should I say—the river's own daughter."

"You're delirious, Professor."

"I am *dying*, Captain. There's a difference, and it's the difference I've come to discuss." He tried to smile and got halfway there. "Sit. Humor a man whose legs have resigned their commission. I've been rehearsing this speech since the fever took me, and I'd hate to waste it on the crows."

Ivo looked back toward the camp. Sena was at the waterline with the long glass, watching the narrows, watching for the gunboat's smoke the way she'd watched for three days without saying the gunboat's name aloud. The others were rationing rice by candle-count. Somewhere below, in the flooded plaza where the expedition had made its first dive, the collection sat in its crates, guarded by starving scholars with more conviction than ammunition.

She sat.

* * *

"You knew my father," Halvorsen said.

It was not a question, and it landed in Ivo's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She kept her face still. She'd had eleven years of practice keeping her face still.

"Everyone knew my father. He piloted half the river's freight."

"He piloted the *Meridian*," Halvorsen said. "The night of the ninth of Frostmonth, thirty years ago. The night Aldermere went under."

The flood-sound filled the silence between them. Ivo counted her breaths the way her father had taught her to count them in white water—slow, even, the body's way of lying to the mind.

"Say what you came to say, Professor."

"What I came to say is that I have spent thirty years being paid to forget, and I am done with the wages." He pushed himself up higher against the stone, and the effort cost him; she saw the sweat stand out on his brow in the last of the light. "But I'll start at the beginning, because you've earned the beginning. You've earned it by running my supplies through a magistrate's jurisdiction on a hull with your name on the manifest, when every sensible captain on this river would have turned back at Kessel Bend."

"Every sensible captain was broke."

"Yes." His eyes were clear now, terribly clear, the fever having burned away everything but the truth. "Broke and framed and name-poisoned, and still you came. That's why it has to be you. That's why it could only ever have been you."

* * *

He told it in pieces, the way men tell the things that have rotted in them longest.

The Aldermere Valley, thirty years past, had held four towns. Not ruins—*towns*. Aldermere itself, with its bell tower and its market square and its schoolhouse with the children's names still chalked on the slates. Three smaller settlements strung along the banks like beads. Eleven hundred souls, give or take the season.

The dam was sold to the government as a flood-control project for the lowland cities. The surveys said the valley would fill gradually, that the towns would be emptied over three seasons, that the water would rise slow and merciful.

The surveys were forged.

"The original hydrology was mine," Halvorsen said. "Honest work. I mapped the valley's water the way you map a channel—every depth, every current, every place where the river could be made to turn. And then Corven Hale came to my office in the capital, young then, hungry then, already smelling of other men's money, and he put a contract in front of me with a figure on it that would have funded my work for twenty years. All I had to do was amend the timeline. Change *three seasons* to *one week*. Change *gradual* to *immediate*. Sign the permits that said the flooding was an emergency measure, unavoidable, regrettable."

Ivo's hands had gone still in her lap. She was aware of them the way a helmsman is aware of the wheel in a breaking sea.

"They told the valley it would be a slow drowning," Halvorsen went on. "They told the towns they had until spring. And then, on the ninth of Frostmonth, with the first hard frost on the ground and the roads already mud, they opened the sluices all at once. No warning that mattered. No time that counted. The water came down the valley at eight knots. Do you understand what that means, Captain? Eight knots of cold water through a valley full of people who had been promised a season."

The bell tower stood above them in the failing light, its crown black against the sky, its bell still hanging—she could see the rope's rotted end trailing from it, thirty years under water and the rope still there, still tied, as if someone had meant to ring it and never got the chance.

"My father," Ivo said. Her voice came out flat. "The *Meridian*."

"Was the only boat on the water that night." Halvorsen closed his eyes. "Your father heard the sluices open—heard it, the sound carries up a valley like the end of the world—and he ran his boat against the current, upriver, into the flood face, and he pulled people off rooftops and out of trees until the *Meridian* foundered under him with her boiler rooms full. He saved eleven people that I have been able to count. Probably more. The ones who wouldn't give their names, because they'd seen what happened to witnesses in that valley afterward."

"And the ones he didn't save."

"Two hundred and six," Halvorsen said. "I have the list. I have had the list for thirty years, in a ledger, with dates and names and the permit numbers that made each death a legal fiction. Your father saw the sluices opened early. Your father could name the men who gave the order. And so when the inquiries came, Corven Hale did not need to destroy your father. He needed only to destroy his daughter—because a man watching his child be ruined will keep his mouth shut forever, and a dead martyr makes martyrs, but a disgraced pilot makes only silence."

The last light went off the water. Somewhere in the camp a pot lid clanged, and a voice called for a second ration of nothing.

"They charged me with falsifying cargo manifests," Ivo said. "A cargo I never carried. A trial where the surveyor who testified against me was on Hale's payroll before he ever set foot in my hold."

"Yes."

"Eleven years, Professor. Eleven years I couldn't take a berth at a legitimate dock."

"Yes." He said it without flinching, and she hated him for it, and she hated herself for the small, filthy relief of hearing it said plainly at last, by someone, out loud, after eleven years of the river's polite fiction that her name was simply an unlucky one.

* * *

The fever took him again near full dark, and she held the cup of willow bark to his mouth while he shivered, and when it passed he gripped her wrist with a strength that surprised them both.

"The collection," he said. "You've seen what they found. The dive teams, the crates."

"I've seen crates."

"Then let me tell you what's in them. The original hydrology surveys—mine, the honest ones, before I amended them, with my annotations showing every change Hale's office required. The dam authority's internal correspondence, recovered from the records office in Aldermere before the water took it—the orders for the early sluicing, signed, dated, initialed. And the ledger." His breath caught. "The ledger is the heart of it. Every payment. Every forged permit. Every witness paid, threatened, or drowned, with dates. Hale's own hand appears in it more times than God appears in scripture."

"Why hasn't it gone to the capital, then? Why has your expedition been sitting on it in a hole in the water, starving, for a season?"

"Because every route out runs through Hale." Halvorsen laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent him double. "Because I forged his permits, Captain, and he has owned me ever since, and when I tried to buy my way free by funding an honest excavation, he sent his surveyors to ride your boat and his enforcers to burn our radios and his patience ran out the day you arrived. Because anything I carry, he can bury. Anything I *sign*, he can dispute—I'm a known forger, that's the elegance of it, my own crime is the lock on his. It has to be carried out by someone with nothing left to lose and a boat fast enough to make him afraid. It has to be carried by the one witness he can't discredit without reopening everything."

"I have a crew," Ivo said. "I have a duty to bring them home alive. You're asking me to make my boat the most hunted thing on this river."

"I'm asking you to make it the most *necessary* thing on this river." He fumbled beneath the tarp and brought out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with surveyor's cord, and pressed it into her hands. It was heavier than its size. Everything true is. "The ledger. The survey annotations. Three letters from witnesses still living along the reach—ferrymen, a lockkeeper's widow, a man who tended the sluice gates and has been drunk for thirty years out of shame. Names, Captain. Corroboration. Enough to hang him in any court that isn't already bought."

"And if every court is bought?"

"Then it goes to the river," Halvorsen said. "Read it aloud at the lock fair. Nail it to every pilot house from here to the delta. A secret this size doesn't need a courtroom, Captain. It needs a current."

She looked down at the oilcloth packet in her hands, and the flood-sound rose in the dark, and thirty years of water seemed to pass through her, all of it asking.

"If I take this," she said, "Hale doesn't stop at my name this time. He comes upriver with guns."

"He was always coming upriver with guns. The only question was ever whether anyone would be holding something worth the trip." Halvorsen's grip tightened on her wrist. "I'm not asking you to be brave. I watched brave people die in that valley, secondhand, in the reports I falsified. I'm asking you to be *angry* in the right direction. You've had eleven years of the other kind."

* * *

She stood at the tower's broken stair afterward, the packet inside her coat, and Sena came up out of the dark with the long glass slung on her shoulder and frost forming in her breath.

"You were with him a long time," Sena said.

"He confessed."

"To what?"

"To everything." Ivo looked at her—at the woman who had come aboard with maps of a place that didn't exist, who had a brother somewhere in the list of the lost, who had kissed her once under a burning camp's smoke and once more tonight at the waterline, brief and fierce, like a promise made in a language neither of them had time to translate. "Sena. Your brother. The first survey team. Halvorsen says they found the records office. He says there are names."

Sena went very still. "Whose names?"

"Everyone's." Ivo's hands ached around the packet's hidden weight. "Including the men who drowned the valley. Including the man who ruined my father. Including, if Halvorsen is right, the order that sent your brother's team up here and let them vanish."

Below them the camp fires burned low, and beyond the fires the water lay black and patient over the streets of Aldermere, and somewhere downriver—too far to see, too near to forget—a gunboat was burning coal toward them through the night.

"Then we take it out," Sena said. Not a question either. The Voss voice, Ivo was learning, the one that read channels by starlight.

"The narrows are at flood stage."

"I know what the narrows are at. I've been charting them in my head since we arrived."

"It will kill us."

"It might," Sena said. She put her hand flat against the oilcloth packet through Ivo's coat, over the heart, as if taking a pulse. "But we'll be the first thing in thirty years to come down this river carrying the truth instead of running from it. Whatever else happens—that's a channel no one's ever run."

In the lean-to below, Professor Halvorsen's fever broke one last time, and he began, very quietly, to recite the names of the drowned, in order, alphabetically, as he had every night for thirty years—like a man keeping a light in a window for a town that was already under the water.

And downriver, at the mouth of the narrows, the first light of the gunboat came around the bend.
