## 10. The Traitor's Crate

I heard the boat before I saw it—a sound like a wasp caught in a tin cup, carrying wrong against the wind that should have drowned it out.

I was elbow-deep in the *Marrow's* bilge at first light, bailing the last of the seepage with a bucket that had seen better decades, when the engine note drifted down through the mist off the flooded streets of Aldermere. Not Halvorsen's launch. Not the flat putter of a fisherman's skiff. This was a heavy-hulled workboat running hard against the current, and there was only one man on this river who could afford to burn fuel like that on a morning nobody had asked him for anything.

"Voss." My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Boat coming."

Sena was already awake—she'd slept badly, curled in the chart room with her brother's maps spread around her like a woman building a nest out of paper and grief. She came up the companionway with a pencil still tucked behind her ear, and when she reached the rail beside me, she went very still the way she did when she was reading something. Reading water. Reading distance. Reading the shape of trouble before it finished arriving.

"One boat," she said. "Maybe forty yards of open water between us and the tower causeway. They'll have to come in slow."

"They'll come in however they want," I said. "Men like that always do."

The mist was burning off the drowned city in long silver ribbons, and through it I watched the boat resolve: black hull, canvas canopy over the stern where men could crouch out of sight, and at the bow, standing with one hand on the rail like he owned the river itself, Rennick—the surveyor who'd slipped away three nights ago with a crate under his arm and no word to anyone. He'd left his partner behind, dead-eyed Danner who now stood at the edge of our camp holding a rifle he hadn't earned the right to carry, looking like a man doing arithmetic about which side of the coming fight would keep him breathing.

Rennick raised an arm. A greeting. A summons. Hard to say which, and it didn't matter, because behind him in the shadow of the canopy I counted shapes—four, five, six—and the glint of glass on at least two of them.

"Hale's enforcers," I said. "He led them straight here."

"He led them to the collection," Sena corrected quietly. "There's a difference, and we're going to need that difference before this day is done."

* * *

By the time the black boat ground its nose against the causeway stones, we had arranged ourselves as best we could. Professor Halvorsen lay in the bell tower's lower chamber, too weak to stand, propped against the wall with a blanket over his knees and the ledger—I had insisted—wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat. The surviving expedition members, seven of them, hollow-cheeked and half-starved after weeks guarding ruins they refused to abandon, took up positions among the fallen columns with whatever the camp offered: two rifles, a shotgun older than I was, and a fisherman's gaff that Oskar Brandt held like it might apologize for being inadequate.

The artifacts themselves were crated and stacked beneath the tower's arches—thirty years of Aldermere rising out of the water piece by piece. Door lintels carved with names. A schoolhouse bell green with age. Jars of preserved documents, their wax seals intact, their contents legible despite the drowning. And stacked apart from the rest, wrapped in sailcloth, the thing Hale actually wanted: the survey records, the correspondence, the ledger pages that proved the dam had not failed. It had been opened. Deliberately. On a Tuesday, with the valley full of people who were told it wouldn't be.

Rennick stepped onto the causeway alone. That was theater, and I knew theater when I saw it—he wanted us counting heads, wanting us to think negotiation was possible. Behind him the enforcers stayed in the boat, casual as Sunday fishermen, rifles laid across their knees.

"Captain Thornbury." He smiled. He had a good smile; I'll grant the man that much. It was the smile of a church deacon, warm and practiced and entirely detachable from anything happening behind his eyes. "You look well. River life agrees with you."

"Rennick." I kept my voice level, my hands loose at my sides. "You took a crate belonging to these people and disappeared into the night. That's theft where I come from."

"That's logistics," he said pleasantly. "Magistrate Hale extends his compliments to Professor Halvorsen and requests the immediate surrender of all recovered materials. In exchange, the professor will receive medical transport downriver, the expedition members will face no charges regarding permits—which we both know were irregular—and you, Captain, will find your file has developed a regrettable case of water damage."

Beside me, Sena made a small sound. Not fear. Something colder.

"And if we decline?"

Rennick sighed, genuinely regretful, a man burdened by other people's stubbornness. "Then the magistrate's offer expires, and what happens next happens without terms. Captain, look at your people. Look at them." He gestured gently at Brandt, at the others, at the tower where Halvorsen sat. "They're starving. They've been starving for weeks guarding doorframes and a bell. For what? For stones? The past is past. Let the river keep it—that's what rivers are for."

"The river didn't drown Aldermere," Sena said. Her voice carried across the causeway clear as a struck bell. "Your magistrate did."

Something flickered across Rennick's face—annoyance, mostly, but underneath it, briefly, something that looked almost like agreement, quickly smothered. I filed that away. Men who agree with you and serve him anyway are either cowards or assets, and both kinds can turn.

"The lady is misinformed," Rennick said. "You have until noon."

"We don't need until noon," I told him. "The answer is no. It was no when you crept out of here with that crate, and it's no now, and it'll be no tomorrow. Tell Corven Hale that Ivo Thornbury said so, and tell him I remember every word he said to me at Kessel Bend, and I've been keeping them somewhere safe."

His smile thinned by a degree. "That's unfortunate, Captain."

"It usually is," I agreed.

He turned back toward the boat, unhurried, and that unhurriedness frightened me more than any hurry could have. Men rush when they're afraid. Men stroll when the outcome is already arranged.

* * *

They gave us four hours, and they used every minute of them.

We spent the morning moving crates deeper into the tower, up the spiral stair, stacking them in the belfry itself where the old bronze bell hung silent above the flood line. Halvorsen directed operations from his blanket, coughing between orders, insisting on the document jars first—"the papers, Captain, the papers, stone can wait, stone has already waited thirty years"—and I obeyed him because he was right. Stone survives. Paper drowns. So does truth, if you let the water get to it.

It was during the third trip up the stairs, a jar of town council minutes in each arm, that Sena pulled me aside into the shadow of the archway. She had the survey maps rolled under one arm—the originals, her brother's hand in every margin—and she pressed them against her chest like a shield.

"Ivo. The ledger."

"What about it?"

"If they take the tower, they take everything in it. Halvorsen can't run. Most of them can't. But the ledger doesn't have to be here." Her eyes were bright and hard and fixed on mine. "It doesn't have to be anywhere they'd think to look."

I understood her before she finished speaking, the way you understand a channel opening in fog—you see it whole, all at once, or not at all. The *Marrow*. My boat, moored in the flooded street below, sitting low and unremarkable among the drowned rooftops, her hull full of bilge water and spare parts and twenty years of my own neglect. Nobody searches a riverboat's engine housing. Nobody wants to put their hands in there. Even I didn't want to put my hands in there.

"Help me get it down," I said.

We went together, down the spiral stair while the enforcers' boat idled audibly beyond the rooftops, down to the waterline where the *Marrow* knocked gently against her lines. Inside the engine room it was dark and smelled of diesel and rust and old rain. I knelt on the deck plates, wrenched loose the inspection panel over the fuel sediment chamber, and cleared out the sludge with my bare hands while Sena unwrapped the oilcloth.

The ledger looked smaller than it had any right to. A fat book, water-stained at the corners, filled with Halvorsen's cramped handwriting and worse—signatures, dates, transfer payments, a record of every coin that had changed hands between a university's funding office and a magistrate's private account, and the single line item that had started everything: *Valley clearance, expedited. No relocation required per C.H.*

My father's name was in those pages too. As a witness statement, taken down the week before the flood, the week before he stopped writing letters home. I had read it once, by lamplight, and never again.

"Give it here," I said, and wrapped it in fresh oilcloth, and sealed it in a tarred canvas bag meant for tools, and slid it down into the sediment chamber where it sat snug against the hull plates, surrounded by twenty years of grease that no enforcer alive would volunteer to touch. Then I bolted the panel back down and smeared the seams with bilge scum until even I couldn't tell it had been opened.

Sena watched me work, kneeling close in the dark, and when I sat back on my heels she said, very quietly, "You just hid the only thing keeping you innocent—or the only thing that could hang you—in the dirtiest hole on your own boat."

"That's generally where I keep things worth keeping."

She laughed—a short, startled sound, gone as fast as it came, but real. And then, because the light through the engine room hatch was thin and gray and the sound of that idling boat was getting louder in my chest if not in my ears, she did something else. She reached out and took my hands—both of them, slick with grease, scraped knuckles and all—and held them.

"Ivo." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "When this goes wrong—and it's going to go wrong, we both know it—I need you to hear this while there's still time to hear things. I didn't come upriver for the maps. The maps were the excuse. I came because my brother vanished on this reach and because every person I met downstream who'd known you said the same thing: *find Thornbury, she doesn't leave people.*" Her grip tightened. "I'm not leaving either. Whatever happens on that causeway today. I'm not leaving."

The engine ticked as it cooled. Water lapped the hull. Somewhere above us, a bell that hadn't rung in thirty years hung waiting in its tower, and I thought: *this is what ringing sounds like, then. This is the note.*

"Sena Voss," I said, "you picked a hell of a place to say that."

"I'm a cartographer. I go where the terrain demands."

And I kissed her—grease and all, in the dark, with the smell of diesel and the threat of guns hanging over us—because some declarations shouldn't wait for better weather, and there wasn't going to be any better weather. Not today. Maybe not ever, on this river.

When we broke apart, her forehead against mine, she whispered, "That wasn't a no."

"No," I said. "It wasn't."

* * *

Noon came in on a change of wind, and with the wind came the boats.

Not one this time. Three—Rennick's black hull plus two more, packed with men, fanning out across the flooded street so that the tower stood ringed by water and rifles. They grounded on the causeway in sequence, deliberate as a drill, and Rennick came ashore again with a paper in his hand this time, a warrant by the look of it, though whose seal authorized a warrant in a drowned city I couldn't say.

"Professor Halvorsen!" he called up at the tower. "By authority of Magistrate Corven Hale, acting for the district court, I am instructed to seize all materials of archaeological and historical significance recovered from this site, and to detain all persons obstructing—"

Oskar Brandt answered him with the shotgun, firing high, blowing a chip of masonry off the archway six feet over Rennick's head. It was a warning shot, and everyone on that causeway knew it, and everyone knew what came next anyway, because warnings only work on men who intend to be warned.

What came next was everything at once.

They rushed the causeway in a wedge, eight of them, rifles up, and our seven answered from the columns and the stairwell, and the morning tore open with noise. I fired from behind a fallen lintel, choosing my shots, putting one man's rifle in the water and another man flat on the stones clutching his shoulder—not dead, I made sure of that, I needed none of them dead, dead men make martyrs and martyrs make magistrates—but the arithmetic was bad and getting worse. Seven starving scholars against twelve armed professionals is not a defense. It is a delay.

They got inside the arches. They got to the crates. And in the churn of it, in the smoke and the shouting, I lost sight of Sena.

I found her at the base of the tower stair, where she had planted herself with the survey maps hugged to her chest and her body squared in the doorway like she was made of masonry herself. Two enforcers were on her—one wrenching at the maps, the other raising a rifle butt—and I was too far, ten yards of broken ground I couldn't cross in time, watching it happen the way you watch a capsize in slow water, knowing exactly how it ends.

The butt came down. She turned with it. It caught her across the temple instead of the skull—a graze, a stripe of blood, her knees buckling—and she *still* didn't let go of the maps. The man tearing at them ripped the roll case free and she grabbed his wrist with both hands and screamed, wordless, furious, alive, and that scream did what bullets hadn't: it brought Brandt and two others off the stairwell in a countercharge that broke the enforcers' formation and bought back ten feet of tower.

I got there on the next breath. I got my arm around her and hauled her behind the lintel, and her blood was on my collar, hot and shocking, and her eyes were still fierce and focused and *hers*.

"The maps?" I demanded.

"Gone. One roll." She pressed her palm to her temple and looked at the red on it with clinical annoyance. "The duplicates are in the belfry. He got the field copy. Ivo—the field copy has the channel notes. The safe approaches. If they're planning to run anyone downriver—"

"Then they'll earn their pay the hard way." I tore a strip from my shirt sleeve and bound her head with hands that shook more than I liked. "You stay behind me now. That's not a request."

"It wasn't obedience," she said. "It was agreement. There's a difference."

"There's always a difference with you."

Outside, the enforcers were pulling back—not routed, regrouping, dragging their wounded toward the boats with the discipline of men following a plan rather than fleeing a fight. Through the archway I watched Rennick standing on the causeway consulting with a tall man in a gray coat I hadn't seen before, and the tall man was looking not at the tower but at the *Marrow*, riding at her moorings in the drowned street, and he was looking at her the way a man looks at a thing he intends to burn.

"They'll fire the boat," Sena said, following my gaze. "Tonight. Cut off escape and destroy anything hidden aboard."

"Then we don't wait for tonight."

I helped her up the tower stair, past the crates, up to the belfry where Halvorsen waited with the last of his strength and the whole story folded in his failing chest. Below us the enforcers' boats withdrew to the far shore, settling in like a siege, cooking fires already smoking. Above us the great bell hung in its frame, green with thirty years of river, patient as stone.

Halvorsen looked from my face to Sena's bandaged temple and understood, the way dying men understand things, faster than the living.

"You've moved it," he said. "The ledger."

"It's where no one will look," I said.

He closed his eyes. When he opened them again they were wet, and calm, and terribly tired. "Then listen carefully, Captain, both of you, because I haven't much voice left and less time, and there's a part of this I haven't told anyone. Not even Voss." He gripped my wrist with fingers light as paper. "The ledger isn't the whole of it. There's a page missing. Rennick has it—it's why Hale trusts him—and that page names the order's author. Not Hale. The man who *signed*."

Below us, across the darkening water, the first torch flared on the far shore.

And I understood that whatever we ran tomorrow, we would not be running from Hale's guns alone. We would be running toward a name.
