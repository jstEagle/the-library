## 11. Siege at the Bell Tower

The first shot came at dawn, and it did not come from the river.

Sena heard it as a flat crack rolling across the water, the sound arriving a half-second after the sight of spray leaping off the bell tower's western stair—a chip of old limestone spinning away into the flood. She had been sleeping against the tower wall with her brother's survey notes folded under her jacket like a stone against her ribs, and she came awake all at once, the way you wake when the ground itself has changed its mind about holding you.

Below, in the drowned street, Ivo was already moving. Sena watched her from the arched window of the belfry—watched her wade chest-deep through water the color of weak tea, one hand on a submerged fence rail, her head low, her other hand signaling the expedition's survivors back toward the tower with small, economical gestures that wasted nothing. The enforcers' boat sat at the mouth of the channel where the river ran out toward open country, a black-hulled gunboat riding low and patient, its engine idling with the sound of something digesting.

They had cut the exit in the night. Sena had heard them do it—the grind of an anchor chain being dragged across the shallows below the narrows, the deliberate noise of men who wanted to be heard, who wanted the people in the tower to know that the door out of Aldermere had closed.

"Down," she called softly, and the word went from mouth to mouth along the tower stair: *down, down, down*, until eleven survivors were pressed into the belfry's stone throat, breathing through their mouths, listening to the water lap at walls that had not felt weather in thirty years.

Professor Halvorsen lay on a pallet of expedition tarpaulins near the bell itself, too weak now to lift his head, his breath whistling through him like wind through a cracked pane. His eyes were open, though. They were always open. He had not truly slept since Ivo had read him the first page of his own ledger aloud, and Sena suspected he would not sleep again in this life.

"They're not storming us," he whispered, when the second shot rang out and struck nothing but gulls. "Not yet. Hale wants us to starve politely. It's cheaper than bullets."

"He doesn't know Ivo," Sena said.

"No," Halvorsen agreed, and something moved behind his eyes that might have been hope or might have been fear for what hope would cost. "No one ever does."

* * *

The tower of Aldermere had been a church once, before the dam, before the water. Its builders had raised it on high ground because churches go where the people are, and the people of the valley had lived along the river terraces, close to the flood line, trusting the water the way farmers trust weather. When the engineers closed the gates thirty years ago, the town had nine days' warning and no choice. The water had risen up the nave like a congregation filing in for a final service, and then it had kept rising, over the pews, over the altar, over the roof, until only the bell tower stood above the surface—a gray finger pointing at a sky that had done nothing to deserve it.

Now drought and flood had conspired to lower the waterline just enough. Streets stood exposed in long black ribbons of mud. Doorways opened onto air instead of river. And the bell tower rose forty feet from the shallows, its lower courses furred with thirty years of silt and weed, its upper windows still glazed with glass that caught the morning light and threw it back in broken coins across the flooded square.

Sena knew this place from maps that officially did not exist. She had drawn it herself, from her brother's notes, from the fragments of the first lost survey, from dreams she refused to call dreams. But knowing a place on paper was nothing like standing in it. Paper did not smell of river rot and old stone. Paper did not have gulls nesting in the belfry louvers, screaming at every boat that moved below.

She spent the morning doing what cartographers do when there is nothing else to be done: she measured. From the belfry window she took bearings on the gunboat, on the tree line, on the drowned rooftops breaking the surface downstream like the backs of sleeping animals. She marked the depth of the water over the market street by watching Ivo wade it—Ivo's shoulders above the surface, then her chin, then only her hand holding a knife aloft as she crossed the deeper channel by the old granary. She drew it all in her head, because her field case had gone into the river two nights ago with the first assault, and her hands remembered anyway. Hands always remember.

By noon the pattern of the siege was legible. Two enforcer boats held the channel mouth in relays, trading off so that one was always awake. A third crew had landed upstream and built a fire on the levee—arrogant, visible, meant to be seen. Snipers worked the eastern bank where the willows gave cover. They were not trying to take the tower. They were trying to make the tower a tomb with a view.

"We can't stay," said Marta Okafor, the expedition's quartermaster, a broad woman whose patience had been burned down to the stud by three weeks of short rations and longer fear. She said it quietly, which was worse than shouting. "We've got four days of food if we share like saints. Five days of water if it doesn't rain. And they've got a steam launch coming—I heard the whistle past the narrows last night. That's not a patrol boat. That's a supply boat. They can sit on us until spring."

"Then we don't sit," Sena said.

Marta looked at her. "You've got a route out of here that forty men with guns haven't thought of?"

"I've got a map," Sena said, "that nobody else has ever seen. That's better."

It was half true and she knew it. Her brother's notes described channels through the drowned town—streets that had been deep enough to float a loaded skiff even in drought years, running north behind the granary row toward a gap in the levee where the old millrace had cut through. But the notes were thirty years old, written by a man who had been twenty-six and certain of everything, and rivers do not honor the certainty of the drowned.

Still. It was more than they'd had yesterday.

* * *

Ivo came back up the tower stair in the late afternoon, soaked to the collar, her boots squelching, her face carrying the particular blankness Sena had learned to read as either very good news or very bad. With Ivo Thornbury you learned quickly that the range between those two conditions was narrower than anyone believed.

"There's a boathouse," Ivo said, wringing out her sleeve without looking at anyone. "Behind the granary row. Stone-built, roof's mostly intact. There's a work skiff inside—lapstrake, maybe eighteen feet. Hull's sound. Oars are gone, but there's a sweep pole and enough canvas to rig a shelter." She paused. "And there's writing on the wall."

Sena's heart did something complicated. "Whose writing?"

Ivo finally looked at her, and whatever Sena saw there made her forget, briefly, how to breathe.

"It's your brother's name," Ivo said. "Carved into the beam over the boat slips. And six others. Dated the week the water came up."

* * *

They went down together, which was against every rule Ivo had established since the shooting started, and which neither of them pretended to regret. Ivo led; Sena followed with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, one hand on Ivo's shoulder whenever the bottom dropped away beneath them. The flooded streets of Aldermere at dusk were a country of thresholds—doorways passing overhead, chimney pots brushing the surface, a child's iron hoop leaning against a wall where some hand had left it thirty years ago and no hand had touched since. The water was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. It was cold the way a locked room is cold.

The boathouse stood where Ivo had said, its stone walls rising straight out of the flood, its slip doors agape. Inside, the air was dry and smelled of creosote and mouse nests and time. The skiff sat on its trolley in the dark like a patient animal. And above the boat slips, where the lantern light could reach it, the beam bore seven names cut deep into the oak with a knife that had been sharp and a hand that had been steady:

**E. VOSS — R. CALLOWAY — T. MERCER — J. OKONKWO — P. LINDQVIST — S. BREMNER — A. HALE**

Sena read the names twice. Then a third time. Her lantern hand was steady because she made it be steady, the way she made her pen lines be true, by deciding it and refusing otherwise.

"That's the first survey team," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "All seven. Officially they never got here. Officially they turned back at Kessel Bend because of the weather."

"The magistrate's brother," Ivo said quietly. She was looking at the last name. "Aldous Hale."

"Aldous." Sena tasted the name. "Corven's older brother. He was the team's engineer. My Elias wrote to me about him—said he was the only honest man the commission employed. Said he'd found something in the dam surveys he couldn't live with." She stopped. The next part came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh. "Elias's last letter said they were going to document the villages. All of them. The ones the commission said were already empty. He said Aldous had proof the gates were being closed early. Before the evacuation orders. Before anyone—" 

Her voice failed on the arithmetic of it. Seven names. Seven people who had paddled into a drowning town to write down the truth, and who had written their own names into the wood of a boathouse the way travelers sign a guestbook, because they believed—or needed to believe—they were coming back.

Beneath the names, smaller, almost shy, a single line had been added in a different hand, shallower, hurried:

*The water is faster than the paper. If anyone reads this, we went up the millrace. Tell them what we found.*

Sena put her fingers on the letters of her brother's name. The oak was cold and hard and real, and Elias Voss had touched this exact grain of it with his living hands, and she stood in the dark of a drowned town with her fingertips on the last physical thing he had ever made, and she did not cry, because crying was a luxury the besieged could not afford, and because somewhere underneath the grief a harder thing was setting like cement.

"They went up the millrace," she said. "That's the channel. That's the way out. They tried to run it."

"And didn't make it," Ivo said. Not cruelly. Just truly.

"Or they made it," Sena said, "and something else happened after. Ivo—Aldous Hale carried proof the drownings were deliberate. Corven Hale has spent thirty years making sure that proof stayed underwater. He didn't send men upriver to guard artifacts. He sent them to finish what the water started."

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. In the lantern light her face was all planes and shadow, a face carved by the same knife that had carved the beam—something sharp and steady and decided.

"Then we run the millrace," she said, "and we carry what they died carrying. All of it. Every page."

* * *

They fortified the tower through the night, because a plan for escape was not yet a plan of escape, and because Halvorsen could not be moved and would not be abandoned. The survivors worked in silence and near-darkness, shuttering the belfry windows with planking pried from the old pews below—pews that had waited thirty years underwater for a purpose and found one at last. Marta organized the rations down to the ounce. The two young students, Pell and Anselm, hauled water from the clean seep on the tower's north side and stacked stones across the stair door in a pattern Ivo dictated, loose enough to scatter underfoot, heavy enough to matter. Sena drew the millrace route from memory onto the back of Halvorsen's permit papers, marking depths from her brother's notes, hazards she could only guess at, and the gap in the levee where the race met the open river.

At midnight she climbed to the belfry alone, because someone had to keep the watch and because she needed, for ten minutes, to be the only person awake in the world.

The moon was up, three-quarters full, laying a silver road across the flood. Downstream, the gunboat rode at anchor with a single lamp burning at its stern, patient as a spider. Upstream, the fire on the levee had burned to embers. Between those two points of light, the drowned city lay silent, its rooftops and chimneys and bell tower keeping their thirty-year counsel.

Sena thought about her brother's handwriting on the beam. *Tell them what we found.* Seven people had died trying to carry the truth out of this valley, and the truth had sunk with them, and the man who had helped sink it now sat on a gunboat at the mouth of the channel with his family name carved beside her brother's, wearing the law like a coat over the same rotten timber.

Well. The river had kept their secret long enough.

A soft footfall on the stair, and Ivo came up into the belfry carrying two cups of the terrible coffee Marta stretched from grounds used three times over. She handed one to Sena without asking, which was its own kind of sentence, and stood beside her at the window, shoulder almost touching shoulder, looking out at the water road.

"Marta says five days of food," Ivo said. "I say we go tomorrow night. Storm front coming down from the north—the barometer's falling faster than I like and exactly how I need. Rain kills visibility, and visibility is the only weapon they have that outranges ours."

"The millrace in flood, in the dark, uncharted for thirty years," Sena said.

"You forgot 'under fire.'"

"I didn't. I was saving it."

Ivo almost smiled. In the moonlight the almost was worth more than most men's whole smiles. Then her expression settled back into that grave, working calm, and she nodded at the dark shape of the drowned streets below.

"When I was down there today," she said, "standing in that boathouse, reading those names—I kept thinking about my father. About what he saw, that night the water came up. What they made him carry for the rest of his life because he wouldn't lie about it." She turned the cup slowly in her scarred hands. "Seven people carved their names in that beam because they thought the truth would need witnesses. It did. It does. We're what came after, Sena. We're the ones they were writing to."

Sena looked at her—at this woman who had lost her name and her future to the same corruption that had taken Elias, who stood in a besieged tower planning a suicide run through a drowned town, and who spoke of it all in the level voice of a captain reading soundings. She thought: *I could love this person entirely, and it will probably cost us both everything.* She thought: *do it anyway.*

"Tomorrow night, then," Sena said.

Below them, far out in the flood, a second lamp kindled at the gunboat's bow. And with it, thin and clear across the water, came the sound none of them had wanted to hear: the deep-throated whistle of the steam launch rounding the narrows, hauling Hale's reinforcements—and, lashed to its deck under tarpaulin, something long and heavy that caught the moonlight along a bright steel edge.

The siege was about to become something worse.
