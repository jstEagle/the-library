## 12. Hale Comes Upriver

The gunboat *Verity* — Magistrate Corven Hale had chosen the name himself, thirty years ago, and had never once laughed at the joke aloud — pushed against the current at a steady eleven knots, her bow wave folding back in two long white wings, her smoke trailing downriver like a confession no one was left alive to read.

Hale stood at the rail under the awning with a cup of coffee he had let go cold, because the cold coffee reminded him of the morning everything began, and he liked to be reminded. A man who forgot the shape of his sins was a man who would eventually make new ones out of carelessness rather than necessity, and Corven Hale had never in his life done anything out of carelessness.

The river was rising. He could read it the way other men read scripture: the water had gone the color of strong tea, thick with the red clay of the upper reaches, carrying whole trees with their roots still clutching the banks they'd been torn from. Fence rails. A rain barrel, spinning slowly. The flood was coming down out of the hills like an army with no flag, and every ferryman between Kessel Bend and the Narrows had tied off and gone home to watch the water climb their steps.

Which meant the river was empty, and empty rivers were where inconvenient things happened.

He heard the boots on the deck plates before he saw the man. Dree. His senior enforcer, if the word *enforcer* could be printed on a government payroll, which it could, because Hale had written the payroll classifications himself. Dree came to the rail and stood at a respectful distance, which was to say close enough to be spoken to and far enough to be thrown overboard, though only one of them had ever considered that possibility and it was not Hale.

"Report," Hale said.

"Boat's still at the tower. The one that ran the supply run. She's anchored in the lee of the bell tower with the expedition's wreck of a steam launch. They've fortified. Sandbags, the churchyard wall, the tower itself. They're not moving."

"And the crate?"

"Still aboard. Our man says the captain hid it somewhere in the engine spaces. He hasn't been able to get near it. She sleeps on the deck beside the engine hatch like a dog on a grave."

Hale turned his cup slowly in his fingers. "Of course she does. She was always a careful woman. It was the one thing about her I respected, back when I was ruining her."

Dree said nothing. Dree was very good at saying nothing; it was why Hale paid him what he paid him, which was more than the magistrate's own salary, drawn from accounts that existed on paper only in the way that rivers exist on maps — as lines, as conventions, as things agreed upon.

"Anything from the camp?"

"One shot fired yesterday, no hits. The professor's people have a rifle in the tower and a woman up there who can use it. We lost the use of the landing stairs. They burned the skiff we left below the falls."

Hale nodded, unsurprised. Halvorsen had been a fool in most ways but never in the matter of hiring. The old man had always been able to find people who would stand on a wall for him, which was the entire art of an expedition, and the entire art of an empire, and Hale had told him so once, years ago, over brandy, before any of this. *You find the ones who'll stand on the wall, Halvorsen, and I'll find the ones who'll sign the papers, and between us we'll build a country.* The professor had laughed. Hale had not.

"Any word from Kessel Bend?"

"The warehouse fire's been written up. *Pirates*, the paper says. The town council's issued a statement. There's talk of a bounty."

"There will be a bounty by tonight. Fifty crowns. Make it sixty; fifty looks like spite, sixty looks like civic concern." Hale set the cup down on the rail's flat cap. "And the witnesses?"

"Three on the list are dead already. The ferryman at Two Rivers drowned in the spring — genuinely drowned, before you ask, I checked twice. The lockkeeper at Marrow Gate is old and frightened and has agreed to remember nothing. That leaves the widow at Candle Ford and the girl who used to work the toll office at Kessel Bend."

"The widow first. She's the one who kept the letters."

"She's the one who kept everything," Dree said. "That's the problem."

Hale looked out at the water. A dead sheep went by, bloated and serene, riding the current with its legs folded under it like a sleeping dog, and he watched it until the current took it around the bend, because it was important to watch such things. A man who could not watch such things had no business doing what needed doing, and what needed doing had needed doing for thirty years, and Corven Hale had done it, all of it, every day, without flinching, and he intended to go on doing it until the river or the grave took the job off his hands.

* * *

People imagined, when they imagined men like him — if they imagined him at all, which they mostly didn't, which was the point — that a man who drowned a town must feel something about it. That he must wake at night with the water in his ears, the way the novelists wrote it, the way the preachers promised it. That guilt was a debt that collected itself.

Hale had stopped waiting for that debt to collect a long time ago, and what he had learned instead was worse and simpler: guilt was a luxury of the innocent. He had never been innocent. He had been twenty-nine years old and already a magistrate's clerk with a magistrate's debts, and the men from the capital had come down the river in a white launch with a briefcase full of money and a map full of towns, and they had explained it to him over dinner at the Kessel Bend hotel as one explains arithmetic to a clever child.

The valley had to flood. That was decided. The dam was decided. The towns — Aldermere, Lowmarsh, the ferry hamlets, eleven of them, four hundred and six souls by the last census — were not decided, exactly; they were *scheduled*. Compensation had been allocated. The compensation had been reallocated. The towns had been told to leave by a date, and most of them had not left by the date, because people do not leave their churches and their orchards and their mothers' graves because a paper says to, and so the date had been moved up, and the gates had been closed early, and the water had come down the valley in the night.

He had not opened the gates. He wanted that understood, if only by himself, in the privacy of his own skull where no one could cross-examine him. He had signed the order that *authorized* the early closure, and he had signed the order that sealed the records of the protest petitions — four hundred and six souls, and every one of them had petitioned, he had read every petition himself, he could still recite the handwriting of half of them — and he had signed the order that declared the drowning an act of God, an unprecedented flood, a tragedy no one could have foreseen.

But he had not opened the gates. The engineers had opened the gates, on orders from the capital, on the strength of his signature. There was a chain of custody in every crime, and Corven Hale had spent thirty years making sure his link in the chain could not be lifted out and held up to the light.

He had been paid, in the way that such men are paid: not in money, though there was money, but in *permanence*. In a jurisdiction that answered to no one upstream. In a courthouse with his name over the door. In thirty years of a river that ran quiet because everyone along it knew, in the wordless way that animals know, which bank was safe to stand on.

And it had worked. It had worked for thirty years, and it would have worked for thirty more, except for two things.

The first was an old man with a conscience that had rusted through late in life, who had gone up the river to dig up the past with a government permit that Hale himself had forged, in a fit of what he had believed at the time was prudence — better to control the dig than to let some stranger's spade find the church bell with the town's name still on it. Halvorsen. Hale had known the man was weak. He had not known he was *brave*. Weak men with money were manageable. Weak men with a cause were dynamite with the fuse already lit.

The second was a riverboat captain named Ivo Thornbury, and here Hale's reflection always snagged, the way a hull snags on a sunken piling, because Thornbury had never been part of the plan at all. She had simply been there, twenty-two years old, standing on her father's deck, and her father had been there, at Candle Ford, on the night the water came down, and her father had seen the lamplighters on the dam road, the lanterns moving gate to gate, hours before any flood could have been natural — and her father had told people, and the people he told had started asking questions, and so the Thornburys had needed to become a cautionary tale.

The smuggling charge had been elegant. Hale had written it himself, and he was not ashamed of the craft of it. A crate of contraband tobacco in the *Thornbury Girl*'s hold, planted by a customs man who owed Hale more than money. A trial in a courthouse where the jury pool had been winnowed by a clerk who owed Hale more than that. A conviction, a stripped license, a family name dragged through the mud of the Kessel Bend gazette until the mud was all anyone remembered. The father had drunk himself into the river within two years — Hale had read the coroner's report, and the report had been honest, which was rare — and the daughter had gone to prison and come out with a boat and a debt and a face that Hale had never been able to forget, because it was the face of a woman who had looked at him across a courtroom, twenty-two years old and ruined, and had not wept, and had not cursed him, and had simply *watched* him, the way you watch a man to learn where he keeps his knife.

He had told himself, in the years after, that it had been necessary. That it had even been — he reached for the word and found it and used it, because he did not flinch from words — *kind*, in the way that amputation is kind. Better one family broken than four hundred and six graves left unexplained. Better one girl's name in the mud than the whole valley's truth in the light. The arithmetic was not complicated. He had done harder arithmetic. He had done the arithmetic of the gates.

But arithmetic was not the same as forgetting, and Corven Hale forgot nothing. It was the discipline his whole edifice stood on. He remembered the smell of the valley the week after the water rose — the sweet rot of drowned orchards, apples floating in their thousands like a second harvest, the whole river downstream of the dam running cider-sweet for a month. He remembered the bell of Aldermere, which the salvagers had been unable to lift, tolling once, they said, when the water first reached it, a sound heard three miles off, though the engineers insisted it was impossible. He remembered the girl in the courtroom. He remembered all of it, every day, and he had made his peace with all of it, and his peace was this: that he would do it again. That he *was* doing it again. That the past was not behind him but below him, like the riverbed, and that a man's whole life was the work of keeping the water from digging it up.

* * *

The rain started an hour before dusk, a soft grey curtain marching upriver to meet them, and the deck crew rigged the canvas and the *Verity* went on pushing north with her running lights hazed in spray. Hale took his dinner in the cabin — river perch, bread, the last of the decent wine — and ate alone, as he always ate alone, because a man in his position could not afford the intimacy of a table companion, and because he had never wanted one, which he told himself was the same thing.

Afterward he stood at the aft rail and watched the water, and Dree came and stood where Dree always stood, and for a while the only sound was the engines and the rain on the canvas.

"She'll run," Dree said at last.

"I know she'll run."

"The narrows at flood stage. Nobody runs the narrows at flood stage."

"Nobody ran the narrows at flood stage," Hale said, "until a woman with a ruined name and nothing left to lose decides the alternative is worse than drowning. She's been drowning for twenty years, Dree. The water will feel familiar. She'll run it."

Dree was quiet. Rain ticked on the canvas. Somewhere upriver, faint under the engine noise, thunder was working the hills like a man prying boards off a barn.

"The professor," Dree said. "If she takes him off that tower—"

"She won't take the professor. The professor is dying; I've seen the cough. She'll take the ledger and the ones who can walk." Hale turned from the rail. In the lamplight his face was composed, courteous, the face that had signed four hundred and six petitions into oblivion. "Which is why we go now, tonight, ahead of the flood. We take the camp before she runs. We take the collection. And everyone at that tower — the professor, the students, the woman with the rifle, every soul who's seen what's in those crates — stays at the tower. Permanently. The river's coming up. There will be a flood. There was always going to be a flood. The river takes people, Dree. It's the one thing the river has never needed my help with."

"And the captain? Thornbury?"

Hale looked upriver, into the dark where the rain was coming from, where a bell tower stood in drowned streets with a dying man in it and a woman who had watched him for twenty years across the width of a courtroom.

"Captain Thornbury," he said, "has been a dead woman since the night her father saw the lanterns on the dam road. She's simply been slow about it. We'll be quick."

He went below to write the orders, and the *Verity* drove on north through the rain, her bow wave folding back in two long white wings, and behind her the flood came down out of the hills to meet them both.
