## 13. The Ledger

The engine housing was warm as a sleeping animal.

Ivo knelt on the deck plates with a lantern hooded by her cupped hand, and for a long moment she simply listened—to the river sliding past the hull, to the rain beginning somewhere far off in the dark like a crowd applauding politely, to her own heart doing its stubborn work. Then she reached into the cavity beneath the fuel line, past the rag she'd wound there three days ago, and drew out the ledger.

It was smaller than it had any right to be. A book the size of a hymnal, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with a length of copper wire that Halvorsen himself had twisted shut with dying fingers. She'd carried it against her ribs through the narrows, through the storm, through the moment when the *Maren Lee* took water over the gunwale and old Pietro went into the flood with his arms around a crate he thought held maps. The ledger had survived all of that dry. It seemed almost obscene.

She carried it aft to the wheelhouse, where Sena sat wrapped in a blanket with her bandaged arm cradled like something breakable, and where the survivors of the Aldermere camp slept in a tangle of limbs and borrowed coats across the deck below—eleven souls who should have been fourteen, if you counted the professor, and Ivo did count him, every hour.

"Is this wise?" Sena asked quietly.

"No," Ivo said. "But we're past wise."

She set the ledger on the chart table, weighted its corners with two brass fittings and a cold cup of coffee, and cut the wire.

* * *

The first pages were almost ordinary. That was the thing that unsettled her most, later—the ordinariness of it. Columns of figures in Halvorsen's small, precise hand: diesel, flour, lamp oil, quinine, wages paid to men whose names meant nothing yet. Dates going back eleven years. It looked like what it pretended to be, the account book of a working archaeologist, a man who counted his shovels and his sacks of rice with the same care.

Ivo turned the pages slowly, and the lantern hissed, and the rain came closer.

Sena stood beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, though neither of them acknowledged it. Her eyes moved down the columns faster than Ivo's; she had spent her life reading other people's marks on paper, and she read them the way Ivo read water—looking not at the surface but at what moved underneath.

"There," she said, on the ninth page. "The handwriting changes."

It did. Subtly. The figures stayed neat, but the pressure of the pen deepened, and certain entries were written in a different ink entirely—a brown-black, older, the kind that came from the cheap iron-gall bottles sold in every government office on the river. And the dates clustered. Three entries in one week, seven years ago. Nine entries the following spring. Payments, all of them, but payments to no supplier Ivo had ever heard of. The payee column said only: *H.*

"Hale," Sena breathed.

"Not yet." Ivo's voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were speaking through her. "Keep turning."

They kept turning.

The middle of the book was where the pretending stopped. Folded between pages forty and forty-one was a letter, brittle now, written on magistrate's stationery with the seal of Kessel Bend pressed into the wax—and Ivo knew that seal the way a dog knows the boot that kicked it. She had stared at it across a courtroom for six hours while men in good coats explained to her, patiently, kindly, what she had done wrong.

*Professor,* the letter began, *the arrangement stands as discussed. Funding continues contingent upon documentation. You understand what is required. The province cannot afford another inquiry. What happened at Aldermere must remain an engineering failure, nothing more. — C.H.*

Below it, in Halvorsen's hand: *Received and understood. Permits drawn as instructed. First payment acknowledged.*

"Engineering failure," Sena repeated. The word came out of her like a splinter coming out of skin. "Thirty-one people lived in the lower town when they opened the sluices early. My brother's survey team was still in the valley. They called it an engineering failure."

"I know what they called it."

"Do you?" Sena turned on her, and her eyes were wet and furious and lit from inside by something worse than grief. "Because I have spent nine years calling it that too. Nine years telling myself the river rose, the dam was badly built, the government was incompetent but not—" She stopped. Her voice cracked and reassembled itself. "Not *deliberate.*"

Ivo didn't answer. She couldn't. Because the page under her thumb had stopped being about Halvorsen at all.

* * *

The entries from seven years ago ran on, and then, near the back of the book, wedged between an inventory of recovered ceramics and a list of men paid to keep silent—Ivo recognized none of the names, and understood all of them—there was a single loose sheet, different paper, different year. A receipt of some kind. Dated the autumn before her trial.

And at the bottom, signed in a hand she would have known anywhere in the world, in any light, dead or alive:

*Elias Thornbury.*

Her father's name. Her father's signature, unmistakable, the long crossbar on the T, the way he always hooked his E backward like a man writing left-handed out of habit and shame. He had witnessed something. Signed as witness to something. The body of the document was Halvorsen's drafting, a formal attestation—*I, the undersigned, being present at the lower gates of the Aldermere works on the night of the release, do attest that the order to open was given verbally by—*

The rest was torn away. Deliberately, cleanly, a strip ripped out and gone.

But the signature remained. And above it, in the margin, in pencil so faint she had to hold the page to the lantern and nearly touch flame to paper to read it, someone—Halvorsen, it had to be Halvorsen—had written four words:

*Witness secured. Leverage held.*

Ivo sat down. Not gracefully; her knees simply stopped being interested in holding her. The deck plate was cold through her trousers. Somewhere below, one of the survivors murmured in sleep, and the river went on talking to itself the way it always did, patient, indifferent, carrying everything downstream eventually.

"Ivo." Sena crouched beside her. "What is it? What did you find?"

"My father," Ivo said. Her mouth tasted of copper. "My father was at Aldermere the night they opened the gates."

"That's not possible. Your father was a lockkeeper at—"

"Marlow Lock. Forty miles downstream. Yes." She heard her own voice as if from the far bank. "He ran cargo sometimes. Off the books. Small runs, grain, timber, things that didn't want weighing at the toll stations. The autumn before my trial he took a run upvalley. He told my mother it was a favor for a man who'd helped him once. He came home and he didn't talk for a week, and then he talked too much, drinking, saying things like *they'll never prove it* and *a man signs his name once and pays forever*, and I was nineteen and I thought he was just drunk and old." She laughed, and hated the sound. "Six months later I'm standing trial for theft of government cargo I never touched, and the evidence against me is a manifest with my boat's name on it in handwriting nobody can trace, and the magistrate presiding is Corven Hale, and my own lawyer barely looks at me because somebody has already bought him, and my father won't testify because—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish. "Because testifying would mean explaining why Hale needed him silent in the first place."

Sena had gone very still. "He framed you to hold your father."

"He framed me to hold my father, and he held my father because my father saw the gates open." Ivo pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until sparks bloomed. "Seven years. Seven years I thought it was about money, about a shipment, about some clerk covering a shortfall. It was never about the cargo. It was about a witness. I wasn't even the point, Sena. I was the *hook.*"

"And your father?"

"Gone before my second year inside. River took him, they said. Drunk, they said." She lowered her hand. "I used to believe the river part."

The lantern hissed. Rain arrived properly now, drumming on the wheelhouse roof, running in bright threads down the glass, and beyond the glass the black water slid past, enormous and unbothered, keeping everything it had ever been given.

* * *

Sena did not say *I'm sorry*. Ivo was grateful for that. Sorry was a coin people threw at you so they could walk away feeling paid.

Instead Sena took the torn page from her hands with a care that undid her more than sympathy would have, laid it flat on the chart table, and studied it the way she studied channels—with total attention, as if the missing piece might be coaxed back into existence by sheer looking.

"The tear is clean," she said. "Whoever removed it wanted the name Hale gave the order. Not the fact of the order—the *name.*" Her finger hovered over the gap. "Which means Halvorsen tore it out himself. After. When he understood what he was sitting on."

"Why keep the signature, then?"

"Leverage cuts both ways." Sena's voice was quiet and terrible. "A witness attestation naming Hale's order, even half of one, proves Elias Thornbury was there. Proves Hale had a witness. Proves the whole machinery of silence had a hinge—and the hinge had a daughter taking the fall for cargo theft the same season." She looked up. "He wasn't protecting Hale with this page. He was insuring himself. If Hale ever decided the professor had outlived his usefulness, Halvorsen could show him that his oldest secret had a living signature attached."

"And instead," Ivo said, "he showed me."

The thought landed in her chest like an anchor dropping.

Because that was the shape of it now, wasn't it. Halvorsen, dying among his starving students in a drowned city, had chosen what to put in her hands. Not the artifacts—those he'd guarded like a madman, those he'd let people nearly die for. This. A hymnal-sized book of accounts and a torn page and a pencil note in the margin. He had pressed it into her hands and said, in that ruined voice, *take it out, take it out even if it means he hunts you forever*—and she had thought he meant the collection, the proof of the drownings in general, the great abstract truth.

He had meant her. Specifically her. He had known—God knew how long he'd known—that the captain he'd hired to run supplies upriver was the daughter of the man whose signature lay in his ledger. Maybe he'd hired her *because* of it. Maybe the whole contract, the generous pay, the crates she wasn't allowed to inspect, had been a message traveling upstream in the safest vessel Corven Hale would never suspect: the woman he'd already broken once, the last person on the river who'd ever carry anything to him willingly.

"He used me," Ivo said. "Again. Everyone uses me. Hale used me to leash my father, and Halvorsen used me to smuggle his conscience downstream."

"Yes," Sena said. No flinching, no softening. That was one of the ten thousand reasons—she catalogued them helplessly, furiously, in the back of her mind—one of the reasons she loved this woman. "And it worked. So the question isn't whether you've been used. The question is what you do with the use."

Ivo looked at the ledger. At the columns of hush-money, at the letter with Kessel Bend's seal, at her father's backward-hooked E holding the whole rotten architecture together like a keystone.

For seven years she had wanted one thing, and she had wanted it the way drowning people want air: her name cleared. The file burned. The word *thief* struck from every record between here and the capital. She had imagined it a thousand times—walking out of the magistrate's office with the paper in her hand, walking down to the dock, hearing the riverfolk say her name without spitting. She had traded years of her life to that want. She had let it decide which rivers she worked and which towns she anchored in, which jobs she took, which faces she avoided. Hale had built his offer on exactly that foundation—*report what they find, and I lose your file*—because he knew the size of the want. He had measured it himself, seven years ago, with his own hands.

And now she sat in her own wheelhouse with the true key in her lap, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the file was never the point either.

Hale hadn't feared her. He had feared Elias Thornbury's signature, and he had kept it buried by burying her. Which meant the exoneration she'd dreamed of was worthless on Hale's terms—because a pardon granted by Corven Hale was just another entry in his ledger, another column of hush-money, another way of owning her. He would clear her name the way he'd cleared the valley: officially, thoroughly, and with the truth drowned underneath forever.

Justice outside the law, Halvorsen had asked her. She hadn't understood until now that there was no law left to be outside of. There was only the ledger and the river, and one of them told the truth.

"We don't trade," Ivo said. Her voice had changed. She could hear it herself—something burned out of it, something else forged in the space. "Whatever he offers. Whatever he threatens. We don't trade the location, we don't trade the artifacts, and we don't trade this." She put her hand flat on the ledger. "We read it aloud."

Sena blinked. "Read it—where? To whom? Hale owns the courts, the papers, the toll offices—"

"Not the courts." Ivo stood, and the exhaustion fell off her like a coat. "The river. Every ferryman between here and the delta owes somebody a debt or holds somebody a grudge, and every one of them has heard of the Aldermere drownings the same way they've heard ghost stories—half-believing, half-afraid. Thirty-one people went into the water because a magistrate wanted a valley empty before the ink on the compensation claims dried. You think the widows don't know? You think the lockkeepers haven't wondered for thirty years why the official story never quite floated?" She was pacing now, three steps fore, three steps aft, the old rhythm of a mind making way. "Hale's power is silence. Paper silence. Sealed files, lost manifests, witnesses who sign and vanish. But he can't seal a hundred voices at a lock fair. He can't subpoena a river."

"It'll get you killed."

"It was always going to get me killed. That was the deal the day I pointed the bow upriver." Ivo stopped at the window. Rain silvered the glass; beyond it, the dark reach unrolled, and somewhere behind them, patient as rot, a gunboat was surely making way. "He framed me because my father saw the water rise. Fine. Then let every soul on this river see it too. Let them see the ledger, the letter, the seal, the signature. Let them see what he does to people who witness things." She turned, and found Sena watching her with an expression she couldn't name—fear and pride and something warmer underneath, all of it held very still, the way you hold a match in wind.

"You understand what this means," Sena said softly. "Once you start, there's no anchoring out. No more hiding in the backwaters. He will come for you openly, and he will not stop at your record this time."

"I know."

"And the others. Eleven people on this deck who trusted you to bring them home."

Ivo's jaw tightened. That was the barbed end of it, the hook inside the bait. She had sworn an oath on the dock at Marlow—*everyone I take upriver, I bring back down*—and she had already broken it once, losing Pietro to the narrows, and she would carry that to whatever grave received her. Now she was proposing to steer all of them into open war with a magistrate.

So she went below, barefoot on the cold deck, and woke them one by one—not gently, they deserved better than gentle—and she told them. All of it. The ledger, the letter, the drownings, her father, her trial, the plan, the price. She told it plainly, without asking for anything, and when she finished she said: "I can put you ashore at the next landing, tonight, and you can walk away clean. Or you can stay aboard, and from here on you're not passengers. You're witnesses. And I will not pretend that's safe."

The rain drummed. The lantern swung.

Old Marta, the camp cook, who had watched her husband sicken in the flooded streets of Aldermere guarding pots he refused to eat from, spat into the bilge—a riverwoman's oath, older than any court—and said, "I've been a witness since the day they drowned my town, girl. Somebody finally asked."

One by one, the others answered the same way. Even young Pell, seventeen and gray-faced with fear, even him.

When Ivo climbed back to the wheelhouse, Sena had spread the river charts across the table and was marking landings in red pencil—ferrymen, lockkeepers, the annual fair at Two Rivers Lock, eleven days downstream, where the whole working river gathered under bunting and lanterns and the indifferent eye of the provincial guard.

"Two Rivers," Sena said. "If we make the fair, we make it public. But Hale knows the calendar too. His gunboat will be there."

"Then we'd best be louder than his guns." Ivo took the red pencil from her hand, and their fingers stayed together a moment longer than the task required. Outside, the rain was easing, and far downstream—she could feel it the way you feel weather in an old wound—the current was already running toward the fair, toward the lanterns, toward the reckoning.

She opened the ledger to the first page, and began, carefully, to copy out the names.
