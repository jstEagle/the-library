## 14. Running the Narrows

The storm came up the valley like something with intent.

Ivo felt it before she saw it—a pressure in her back teeth, a smell of iron and wet stone rolling down from the high country—and she stood at the wheel of the *Marrow's Grace* and watched the sky over the narrows turn the color of a bruise going bad. The river had been rising for two days, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees in its grip like a man carrying firewood. Every hour it climbed another finger's width up the pilings at Aldermere's drowned streets. Every hour, Hale's gunboat sat downstream in the channel like a cork in a bottle.

"Storm's early," Pell said beside her, squinting into the wind. He had a rope burn across one palm from the fortification work and he kept flexing the hand as if testing whether it still belonged to him. "Season's wrong for it."

"Season's been wrong since they drowned a town," Ivo said.

Behind them, the bell tower of Aldermere stood out of the floodwater like a raised finger—*we were here, we were people, remember us*—and around its base the last of Halvorsen's survivors were loading what could be loaded: crates of letters, the professor's field journals, Sena's rolled charts wrapped in oilcloth, and nothing else. The artifacts stayed. Ivo had made that bargain with herself on the tower stairs at midnight, holding the ledger against her ribs where it had lived for three days now, warm as a second heartbeat inside her coat. Some things belonged to the drowned. She was only stealing back the truth.

Sena came down the tower ladder two rungs at a time, hair plastered flat by the first fat drops, and pressed a folded chart into Ivo's hands without ceremony. Her fingers were ink-stained and shaking—not from fear, Ivo thought, but from having drawn the same channel four times by lantern light until the lines stopped arguing with each other.

"Narrows chart," Sena said. "It's guesswork above the old mill race. Nobody's ever run it at this stage. Nobody's ever *wanted* to."

"Then we'll be the first thing the river learns from in thirty years." Ivo unfolded the chart. Pencil lines crossed and recrossed; soundings marked in Sena's small precise hand; a question mark at the S-turn where the flooded quarry cut bit into the north bank; and at the bottom, in letters smaller than any other note on the page: *I trust you.* Ivo looked at that longer than she looked at the soundings.

"The gunboat will see us the moment we clear the tower," Sena said. "There's no cover on the water."

"There is," Ivo said, and nodded at the sky. "He's got a searchlight and a deck gun and six men who signed on to frighten starving scholars. I've got forty feet of riverboat and a storm God seems to be running personally. I like my odds better than they look."

Thunder answered her, long and rolling, like the river clearing its throat.

* * *

They cast off at dusk, or what passed for dusk under that sky—which was to say the world simply gave up pretending there was light. Rain came sideways. The *Grace* wallowed heavy in the current, riding low with eleven souls aboard and every drum of fuel Ivo had begged, borrowed, or traded for, and Ivo took her up through the drowned streets with the bell tower sliding away astern and the rain hammering the cabin roof like applause from a crowd that wanted very much to see how this ended.

She had given the crew their orders on the tower steps an hour before, in the flat voice she used when the truth needed to stand up straight without decoration. "We go through the narrows at flood stage, in the dark, in this weather, past a gunboat that has orders to stop us by any means. Anyone who wants to stay behind and take their chances with Hale, say so now and I'll think no less of you." Nobody said so. Old Marta, who had cooked for the expedition since before Ivo was born, had looked at her over the lamplight and said, "Captain, I've buried a husband and two sons to this river's moods. It doesn't get to have me sitting down." And that had been the whole vote.

Now the narrows opened ahead like a door into a darker room.

In ordinary water the narrows were a half-mile of channel between the quarry cliffs, deep enough, honest enough, with a lighthouse keeper's cottage rotting on the shelf above. At flood stage they were something else entirely. The river had swallowed the banks whole; the cliffs rose sheer out of churning brown water, and the current funneled between them with a sound Ivo felt in her sternum more than heard—a continuous roar, like the mountain itself breathing out. Whirlpools spun off the eddy lines, black and patient. Debris rode the surface: fence rails, a door still hanging in its frame, a drowned orchard's worth of branches.

"Sena!" she shouted over the storm. "Talk to me!"

Sena braced herself against the wheelhouse doorframe, chart held under an oilskin hood, reading by the lightning's strobe. "Quarter mile to the entrance! Keep left of the sunken mill—the millstone shows at this stage, you'll see white water breaking over it! Then the channel dog-legs right around the quarry point—there's an eddy behind it big enough to hide a cathedral, but don't linger, the backwash will spin us!"

"And after the point?"

"After the point—" Sena's jaw tightened. "After the point is the part I drew four times because I didn't believe it myself. The quarry cut. It's flooded to within eight feet of the rim. If we can hold the channel along the north wall, the cliff breaks the gunboat's line of sight for maybe ninety seconds. Ninety seconds to make the bend and be gone."

"Ninety seconds." Ivo put the wheel hard over. "I've done more with less."

The millstone appeared exactly as promised—a boiling knot of white in the middle dark, the river tearing itself apart over sixty tons of drowned machinery. Ivo went left of it so close she could hear individual stones grinding beneath the hull, and then the narrows took them.

It was like being swallowed. The cliffs closed in, blacker than the sky, and the current seized the *Grace* by the keel and *hurried* her—Ivo had been on rivers her whole life and had never been hurried by one before, never felt a boat move like a leaf, like a thing the water was deciding about. She fought the wheel in short, brutal corrections, reading the surface the way her father had taught her before the world taught her everything else it knew: the V of a submerged rock, the smooth glass tongue of a whirlpool's eye, the place where the current piled up green and mean against the south wall. Lightning lit the gorge end to end, once, twice—a frozen photograph of roaring water and driving rain and her own hands white-knuckled on spokes worn smooth by three generations of Thornburys—and in that flash she saw the quarry point rushing toward them and the eddy beyond it, wide and black and waiting.

"Right rudder—hold—*hold*—" The stern swung. The bow dug into the backwash and for one endless moment the *Grace* stood nearly still while the river tried to rotate her like a toy, and Ivo stood at the wheel with her boots planted wide and her teeth bared and *refused*, hauling the wheel spoke over spoke, until the boat shrugged free of the eddy's grip and slid into the shadow of the quarry cliff.

And the world went quiet.

Not quiet, exactly—but muffled, walled. The cliff stood between them and the open channel, and the rain fell softer here in the wind's lee, and ahead the flooded quarry cut stretched away into darkness like a canal built for ghosts. Eight feet of freeboard to the rim, Sena had said. It looked like less. It looked like the water was thinking about the rim the way a man thinks about a window.

"Pell! Sound the bilges!"

Pell's answer came back hoarse from below: "Taking water! Slow—maybe an inch every ten minutes—old seams are working in this chop!"

"Inch every ten minutes buys us an hour. We need twenty minutes. Pump anyway." She turned. "Sena. Where does the cut come out?"

"Into the lower channel, half a mile above the ferry ruins. After that—" Sena spread her hands, and even soaked through, even gray-faced with cold, she managed something that was almost a smile. "After that it's your river, Captain. I just draw it."

* * *

The gunboat found them anyway. Of course it did.

They were halfway down the quarry cut, running easy for the first time since the tower, when the searchlight beam swung across the rim rock ahead—white, enormous, sweeping the flood like the eye of something waking. Ivo killed the engine to a whisper and let the current carry them, and everyone aboard went silent in a way that had weight, eleven people not breathing at once.

The beam swept past them. Swept back. Stopped.

The light pinned the *Grace* to the water like a moth to a board, and across the channel, faint through the rain-hiss, came the sound of a whistle—one long blast, then two short. *Heave to. Heave to or else.*

"Hale's boat," Pell said quietly, coming up the ladder with a boathook in his fist as if a boathook would matter.

"He'd be a fool to bring it anywhere near this water in the dark," Ivo said. But her eyes were measuring the distance to the cut's mouth, and the angle of the searchlight, and doing arithmetic she didn't like. A deck gun didn't need to come close. A deck gun needed only to be patient, and Hale had always been patient—that was the whole architecture of him, patience wearing a magistrate's chain of office.

The whistle sounded again. Then a voice, amplified, distorted by rain and echo into something barely human: "*Thornbury!* Last chance! Come alongside or we sink you at the mouth!"

At the mouth. Where the cut narrowed. Where a single well-placed shell would bring the quarry rim down into the channel and bury them under a hundred tons of rock, and the river would keep them the way it kept Aldermere, and no one would ever call it murder.

Ivo looked at the searchlight blazing off the water. She looked at the ledger's shape beneath her coat. She thought of her father standing on a bank somewhere upstream thirty years ago, watching towns drown, knowing what he knew, saying nothing because saying it had already been arranged to cost everything—and she found that the fear in her chest had burned down to something else, something with a cleaner heat.

"He won't sink us blind," she said. "He needs the ledger. He needs to know it's even aboard. He's bluffing." She turned the engine over. "But we're not going to stand here and let him decide when to stop bluffing. Sena—how far to the mouth?"

"Three hundred yards. Maybe less."

"Then we're going to give him ninety seconds of the worst seamanship of his life. Everybody hold onto something that loves you back."

She rang the engine full ahead and drove the *Grace* straight out of the cliff's shadow into the searchlight's glare.

What followed lived afterward in Ivo's memory as fragments, the way disasters and miracles both refuse to arrive in order. The gunboat's silhouette downstream, low and black, its light burning into her eyes until the world was all glare and roaring water. The crack of the deck gun—once, twice—shells falling long, throwing up columns of spray and shattered rim-rock, Hale's gunners firing blind against rain and current and a target moving faster than anything had a right to move. The *Grace* threading the falling water. A branch as thick as a man's thigh rising out of the flood dead ahead and Ivo putting the wheel over so hard the boat lay down on its side and Marta screamed and something heavy went overboard with a splash nobody had time to mourn. The radio mast—she saw it go, saw the stays part with a sound like piano wire, saw thirty feet of steel and copper cartwheel away downstream trailing wire like a severed nerve. No more radio. No more calling for help, no more being called to. Just the boat, the river, and whatever waited at the mouth of the cut.

And then the mouth itself—the channel pinching down between the collapsed ferry pilings, water stacking up green and furious, the gunboat's searchlight swinging wildly as Hale's pilot realized too late that his deeper-drafted vessel could not follow where a riverboat could thread—and Ivo stood at the wheel with rain in her teeth and her heart going like a drum at a hanging and *laughed*, actually laughed, because terror at this pitch has nowhere to go but out, and the *Grace* shot the gap with a foot of water on either side of her planks and burst through into the open channel beyond.

Open river. Black, swollen, running like a spilled sky, but open. Behind them the searchlight shrank to a star, then a rumor, then nothing. Ahead the storm was already thinning toward the eastern hills, rain slacking to silver threads in the last of the lightning's glow.

Pell came up the ladder grinning like a skull. "Bilge's at six inches, Captain. Mast's gone. Cook's fine, the crate that went over was empty, and I have never in my life—" He shook his head, wordless.

"Never in your life what?"

"Been glad to be on a sinking boat. Felt wrong to say it."

"Don't apologize to the river," Ivo said. "It gets ideas."

Sena appeared at her elbow, soaked to the skin, one hand pressed flat against the wheel housing as if to feel the boat's heartbeat directly. She didn't say *that was impossible*. She didn't say *we almost died*. She looked at Ivo—at the wheel, at the river, at the woman holding both—and said, "Ninety seconds. You gave yourself ninety seconds and took eighty."

"I'm getting sloppy in my old age."

Sena laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, bright as the lightning—and in the thunder that followed, Ivo thought: *whatever comes next, whatever this costs, I have heard that sound, and they cannot take it back out of me.*

Then Pell's voice came down from the bow, flat and careful, the way men speak when the news is bad and getting worse:

"Captain. Bilge's at ten inches and climbing. Seams have opened up aft. We're not pumping fast enough—and there's shoal water ahead. Lots of it. We need ground under this boat soon, or the river's going to choose it for us."

Ivo looked downstream into the dark, at the open river she had bought with a mast and nearly a hull, and began, grimly, to hunt for a place to land.
