## 15. The Price of the Crossing

The engine died a mile below the narrows, and the river took us the way a thief takes a purse—quietly, and all at once.

I heard it before I understood it: a change in the boat's voice, the deep diesel stutter swallowed by water. Then Ivo's shout from the pilothouse, raw and stripped of everything but command. "Bilge! Get the bilge pump running! Sena—life vests on everyone, now!"

I was already moving. The storm had followed us out of the narrows like a debt collector, rain coming sideways in gray sheets, and the deck was slick as oiled glass under my boots. Behind us, through the curtain of weather, I could just make out the gunboat's searchlight sweeping the flooded timber where we'd slipped past it—a pale blade swinging through the dark, finding nothing.

We had made it. That was the thing I couldn't stop thinking about, even as the water rose around my ankles in the companionway. We had actually made it.

The engine room told a different story. I got the hatch open and looked down into a churning black well. The river had come in through the exhaust line when we shipped that wave in the narrows—the one Ivo had threaded us through with her hands white-knuckled on the wheel, the one that had lifted the whole stern like a toy in a child's fist. Water had poured down the stack while the engine gulped and coughed, and now it had found its way into the cylinders. Hydrolock, Ivo would say later, in the flat voice she used for things she refused to feel. The pistons had tried to compress a river, and the river had won.

"She's done," Ivo said behind me. She stood in the hatchway, rain streaming off her jaw, and there was no panic in her at all. Only arithmetic. "We drift until we ground out. Everyone topside. Life vests."

"Where do we hit?"

She looked downstream, reading the water the way I read contour lines—the boils and eddies, the V of smooth current marking the channel, the standing waves that meant something buried and hard beneath. "Sandbar, if the river's kind. Snags, if it isn't."

"And if Hale comes back down?"

"He'll be looking upstream for us by now." She almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Nobody runs the narrows at flood stage. He'll assume we drowned."

*Somebody did,* I thought. *You did.* But I said nothing, because the crew was crowding up onto the deck—Marsh and Odile and young Tam with his split lip from the narrows, and old Piet who'd been running this river since before either of us was born—and they needed to see calm, not conversation.

So we drifted.

It should have been terrifying. In some ways it was—the storm moaning through the drowned timber along the banks, the boat wallowing beam-on to the current, the black water sliding past only inches below the gunwales. But there was also something almost gentle in it, after the violence of the narrows. The river had tried to kill us for an hour, and now it simply carried us, indifferent as sleep. I stood at the rail beside Ivo and watched the lightning walk along the far hills, and neither of us spoke, because there was nothing to say that the silence wasn't already saying better.

We grounded sometime after two in the morning, with a long shuddering sigh rather than a crash. Sandbar, then. The river's kindness. The hull settled into the shallows with a list to port, and the current pushed us snug against the bar like a hand tucking in a blanket, and then we were still—truly still—for the first time in two days.

Ivo counted heads twice. Marsh, Odile, Tam, Piet, me, herself. All present. All alive.

"All right," she said. "Piet, get the tarp over the cargo hold. If that ledger takes another soaking, Halvorsen's ghost will haunt us all. Tam, bail what you can. The rest of you—rest. We're aground until the water drops or help finds us, and I don't intend to spend whatever strength we have left panicking about it."

They moved to obey, slow and sodden and grateful. And that was when the second boat came out of the dark.

I never saw where it launched from. Later, piecing it together, we decided it must have been a patrol skiff stationed below the narrows—Hale's insurance, in case the gunboat missed us. It came alongside silent as an eel, riding low with four men in oilskins, and the first any of us knew of it was Marsh shouting a warning from the stern.

What happened next happened fast, the way terrible things do, each moment distinct and none of them connected.

They wanted the crate. Not the whole collection—we hadn't risked carrying that—but the ledger, Halvorsen's ledger, wrapped in oilcloth and lashed inside the crate marked *spare parts*. They knew exactly which crate. Someone had told them. And when Ivo refused—when she stepped between them and the hold with nothing in her hands but her own fury—they came for it anyway.

There was a struggle on the wet deck. I remember Odile screaming, and Piet swinging a boathook, and one of the oilskinned men going into the water with a splash that seemed impossibly loud. I remember Ivo taking a boot heel across the knuckles of both hands as she gripped the crate's rope handle, and I remember the sound she made—not a scream, something worse, something bitten off and swallowed.

And I remember Marsh.

He had the ledger. He'd snatched it up in the chaos—I never learned whether he meant to defend it or surrender it—and he backed away from the men with it clutched to his chest, backing toward the rail, backing toward the dark water, and then the skiff swung wide and its gunwale caught him at the hip and he went over.

He went over holding the ledger.

The river took him the way it takes everything—without malice, without pause, without ever once looking back. His lamp went down glowing green through the black water, and then the glow shrank, and then it was gone, and the current folded over the place where he'd been as smoothly as a page turning.

Ivo went in after him.

I have to write that plainly or I'll soften it in memory forever: she went straight off the rail into flood-stage water in the dark, with hands already broken, after a man who was already past saving. Piet and I caught her twenty yards downstream, both of us half-drowned ourselves hauling her into the shallows, and she fought us the whole way—fighting to keep swimming, fighting toward the place where the green light had gone out, saying his name over and over in a voice I didn't recognize. *Marsh. Marsh. Marsh.*

The men in the skiff were gone by then. Whether they'd gotten what they came for or fled from Piet's boathook, I couldn't tell you. It didn't matter. Nothing mattered except the empty water and the woman kneeling in the shallows with the river still trying to pull her back out, sobbing like something torn open.

The ledger washed ashore an hour later, snagged in the roots of a dead willow fifty yards downstream. Oilcloth is good gear. The river reads labels too.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and the storm had spent itself somewhere east of the hills. The boat lay heeled over on the bar with her decks awash in wrack and rainwater, and nobody aboard had slept, and nobody suggested trying.

Ivo sat on the cargo hatch with her back against the collapsed mast step, and she had not spoken in three hours. Her hands lay in her lap, and they were ruined—the boot heel had fractured two knuckles on the right, maybe three, and the left was wrenched and swelling, and beneath all of it the skin was scraped raw from the rail and the rocks and the desperate climb out of the current. She held them palms-up, the way you'd hold something you'd been handed and didn't know how to put down.

I knelt in front of her with the medical kit, and she didn't look at me.

"Sena."

"Yes."

"I let go of the crate." Her voice was sanded flat. "When the boot came down. I let go, and Marsh picked it up, and if I'd held on—"

"You'd have gone over the rail with it."

"If I'd held on, he'd be—" The sentence broke. She stared at her hands. "He had a daughter in Kessel Bend. Did you know that? He showed me a drawing she sent him. A boat. She drew him a boat, because he worked on one." A breath that shook going in and came out steady, forced steady, the discipline of a woman who had spent years teaching herself not to break in public. "I'm going to carry him. Every mile from here. I'm going to carry him the way I carry my father, and the way I carry the trial, and eventually I'm going to be so heavy with the dead that I sink, and that will be justice of a kind, I suppose."

"No," I said.

Her eyes came up. Gray-green, red-rimmed, furious with grief that had nowhere to go.

"It doesn't work," I said. "Believe me. I've been carrying my brother for six years, and I can tell you exactly how much good it does. None. It doesn't bring anyone back and it doesn't make you lighter and it doesn't honor them. It just makes you slower, and slower people miss things, and the things you miss are the things Hale is counting on you to miss." I took her right hand, gently, and turned it over. She flinched but didn't pull away. "He saved the ledger. Do you understand? Of every person on this boat, the river gave it to *him*, and he kept hold of it all the way down. That was his choice, Ivo. His. You don't get to steal it from him by making it your sin."

"I ordered him topside. I put him on that deck."

"You put him on a deck he'd have defended anyway. He loved this boat. He loved *you*." I unwound the first bandage strip. My own hands were shaking; I made them stop. "Now hold still, because I'm bad at this and you're going to swear at me."

She swore at me. It was almost companionable.

I splinted what needed splinting with slats from the kit and strips cut from Piet's spare shirt, and bound the left wrist tight, and cleaned the scrapes with spirits that made her go white-lipped and silent. She endured it the way she endured everything—jaw set, eyes fixed on some middle distance where the river ran. But somewhere in the middle of wrapping the right hand, she stopped looking at the middle distance and started looking at me.

"Why?" she asked. Not hostile. Genuinely asking. "Why are you doing this?"

"Because you hauled me upriver into a siege for my brother's maps," I said. "Because you ran the narrows at flood stage for strangers' names. Because somebody has to hold your hands together for a few weeks, captain, and it might as well be someone who's read enough charts to know where they're pointed." I tied off the bandage. "And because you're wrong about how the carrying works. You don't carry the dead alone. That's the whole trick of it. You share the weight and somehow it gets lighter instead of heavier. I don't know why. Nobody knows why. But it does."

The wind moved through the willows along the bank. Downstream, the river ran high and brown and indifferent, already polished smooth over the place where Marsh had gone down, already carrying leaves and branches and the ordinary debris of the world as though nothing had ever stopped in it at all.

"I can't feel my hands," Ivo said finally. "How am I supposed to pilot?"

"Poorly," I said. "For a few weeks. Fortunately for you, I've been studying your river charts since Kessel Bend, and I intend to be insufferable about it."

Something happened to her face then—something small, exhausted, and true. Not quite a smile. Closer to the moment when a knot gives way, when a rope you've been hauling goes suddenly slack. She leaned forward until her forehead rested against mine, bandaged hands hanging useless between us, and stayed there, breathing, while the morning came up around us and the crew pretended very hard to be busy elsewhere.

"We deliver the letters," she said against my mouth. "Every survivor's letter. Every name. We finish what Marsh started."

"We finish what Marsh started," I agreed.

* * *

We floated the boat off the bar at noon, when the flood crested and began, at last, to fall. The engine was beyond salvage short of a machine shop, so Piet rigged the spare sail from the cargo tarp—a crooked, ugly thing, half jib and half prayer—and the *Maren* limped downriver under canvas at a walking pace, listing, leaking slowly, magnificent in her ruin.

Ivo stood at the helm with her hands bound and useless, and I steered by her voice. *Two points to starboard. Ease her. Watch the boil off the point.* Between commands she talked—more than I'd ever heard her talk—about Marsh's daughter, about the drawing of the boat, about the letter in the hold addressed to a girl named Lissie in a town called Ferrow's Landing. When we reached Ferrow's Landing, she said, she would deliver it herself. On foot. To the door.

Toward evening, Piet climbed down from the bow with a strange expression and a piece of oilskin in his hand, fished from the wrack tangled in the anchor chain.

"It was lashed to the chain," he said. "Somebody wanted it found."

Inside the oilcloth was a single sheet, water-spotted but legible, written in the cramped official hand I'd last seen on warrants and toll notices. It was signed with a name that made Ivo go very still beside me.

It was orders. Dated three days ago. *Take the ledger. Leave no witnesses.*

And it was countersigned—not by Hale, but by the man who had slipped away from our camp two nights past with a crate under his arm. The surveyor. The traitor.

Except the ink told a different story now. Beneath his signature, in fresher pen, in a hurried scrawl, someone had added four words:

*I did not sign this.*

Ivo read it twice. Then she looked up at me, and despite the bandages, despite the grief, despite everything the river had taken in the last twelve hours, there was a light in her face I recognized from the charts of Aldermere—the light of a passage opening where a map shows only stone.

"The ferry landing at Millbrook," she said. "That's where he'd run. It's the only place between here and Kessel Bend where a man can disappear and still hear news." She turned her ruined hands over, wincing. "Sena—how fast can this sail go?"

"Not fast," I said. "But the wind's changing."

And it was. All down the valley, the storm's aftermath was swinging the air around to the northwest, filling our crooked canvas, pushing us toward Millbrook and the man who had written four words that might hang a magistrate—or damn us all.
