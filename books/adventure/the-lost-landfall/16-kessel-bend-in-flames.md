## 16. Kessel Bend in Flames

We smelled the town before we saw it.

Smoke has a grammar all its own, and I have read it my whole life on this river—the green sweetness of leaf piles burning in autumn, the tarry bite of a lamp knocked over in a cabin, the fat gray reek of a cookstove left unattended. What came down the current to meet us at the last bend before Kessel Bend was none of these. It was heavy and chemical and old, the smell of creosote and pitch and stored paper going up all at once, and beneath it something sweeter that I refused, for as long as I could, to name.

Sena stood beside me at the wheel, her arm still bound from the graze she'd taken at the tower, her brother's decoded notes wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat. She lifted her chin into the wind and breathed it in, and I watched her do the arithmetic I was already doing.

"That's not a chimney fire," she said.

"No."

"That's a warehouse."

I didn't answer. There was nothing to say that the river wasn't already saying. The *Maren Lee* came around the bend with her hull groaning, patched in three places, half her rail gone where the narrows had chewed us, and Kessel Bend opened below us the way it always did—the long curve of the levee, the mooring chains, the cranes, the tin roofs catching the late light like fish scales.

Except where the expedition's warehouse had stood, there was no roof at all. There was a black wound in the line of buildings, still smoking, still steaming where they'd played water on it too late, and the char had spread to the cooper's shop next door and taken its sign with it. A crowd stood along the levee in the way crowds do after a fire—not helping anymore, just watching, the way people watch a grave being filled.

I brought us in slow. My hands were steady on the wheel, which surprised me, because everything else in me had gone very quiet and very cold, the way the river goes cold under its skin of fog.

"They'll have posted notices," Sena said. "He'll have been ready for us since the narrows."

"He's been ready for me for eleven years," I said. "This is just the first time he's spent money on it."

* * *

They met us at the mooring—Hale's men, four of them, standing on the planks with their boots planted wide and their faces arranged into expressions of official sorrow. Behind them, nailed to the warehouse wall that hadn't burned because it belonged to someone else, hung a broadsheet, fresh ink, still curling at the edges.

I read it from the wheelhouse before we ever touched the fenders. The type was large enough to read across water, which was the point.

**WANTED — PIRATES AND ARSONISTS.** The crew of the steamboat *Maren Lee*, wanted in connection with the destruction of the Halvorsen Expedition's stores at Kessel Bend and the murder of Professor E. Halvorsen, archaeologist. Reward offered by order of Magistrate C. Hale, District of the Upper Reaches. All lawful persons are commanded to detain—

I stopped reading. It wasn't the lie that made my vision go red; I had expected lies. It was Halvorsen's name. They had put his name on it, the dying man who had spent his last breath handing me the truth like a man passing a coal hand to hand so it wouldn't go out, and they had written *murder* next to it, and somewhere in the district clerks were already copying it into ledgers, and in twenty years it would be history.

"They killed him themselves," Sena said quietly behind me. "Slowly. With debt and permits and a supply line cut off. And now they've written it on you."

"I know how he works," I said. "I sat through it once already."

I took us in anyway. There was nowhere else to take us. That was the whole design of it, and I could admire the craftsmanship even while it closed around my throat: every landing between here and the delta would have the same notice, every lockkeeper paid or frightened, every ferryman warned. The *Maren Lee* was not a boat anymore. She was a target with a smokestack.

The four men on the dock didn't board. One of them, a thick-necked fellow I remembered from Hale's gunboat, held up a folded paper sealed with the magistrate's stamp and called across the gap between hull and planks in a voice pitched to carry to the crowd.

"Captain Thornbury. You're ordered to surrender your vessel, your cargo, and yourself to the district authority. Refusal will be treated as piracy on the navigable waters."

"And if I comply?" I called back.

He smiled without warmth. "Then you'll be held pending inquiry."

Pending inquiry. Eleven years ago those two words had eaten three of the best years of my life before the trial even started. Pending inquiry meant a cell in the Kessel Bend courthouse, and the Kessel Bend courthouse meant Hale, and Hale meant that whatever happened inside those walls would happen exactly once, in the shape he wanted, with the paperwork already dry.

"Tell your magistrate," I said, "that the *Maren Lee* answers to the river, and the river doesn't take orders from him."

It was a fine thing to say. The crowd along the levee shifted, and a few of them—ferrymen, mostly, and the lockkeeper's widow, and old Toma who ran the fuel barge—looked at their feet or looked at me, and I couldn't tell yet which of them were ashamed and which were afraid, and I suspected they couldn't either. That was the other thing Hale understood about towns: he didn't need them to believe the notice. He only needed them to believe it might be true, and that believing otherwise had a price.

The enforcers withdrew up the levee, unhurried, and I felt the eyes of the whole waterfront settle on my boat like crows settling on a carcass.

* * *

We went ashore after dark, in ones and twos, hooded and quiet, because there were things we needed that a boat can't give. Brine for the wounded. Bread. Word of what had actually burned.

What had burned, it turned out, was everything.

The expedition's warehouse had held more than crates. It had held the expedition itself—Halvorsen's thirty years of field notes, the first survey team's records, the photographs of Aldermere before the water rose, the affidavits the professor had been collecting from lockkeepers and dredge crews since before I was born. Sena's brother's original charts had been stored there, the ones that mapped the drowned reach in loving, forbidden detail. All of it had gone into the fire in a single afternoon, and the fire had been set by two men in a skiff who had been seen by everyone and stopped by no one, because by the time anyone understood what was burning, understanding it had become dangerous.

I stood in the ruins with Sena while the timbers ticked and settled around us, still warm underfoot even now, three days cold. Char to the waterline. The river lapping at the wreckage as if it wanted it back.

"My brother's charts," Sena said. Her voice was flat, which is how I knew what it was costing her. "There were copies. He always made copies—he used to say paper drowns but ink swims, if you put it in enough boats." She pressed her bandaged arm against her ribs. "But the originals. Thirty years of his handwriting. It smells like him in here. Did you know that? Burned paper smells like—" She stopped. She didn't finish, and I didn't ask her to.

I put my good hand on her shoulder, the one that wasn't wrapped in the bandages she'd tied herself at the grounding, and we stood together in the ruin while the river breathed against the pilings.

"He did this because we're coming," I said finally. "Not despite it. Because of it. We're carrying the ledger, and the ledger names him, and he can't burn the ledger, so he's burning everything around it. Every corroboration. Every witness who kept papers. He wants us arriving with a story and nothing to prove it."

"Then he's failed," Sena said, "because he doesn't know what else we're carrying." She touched the oilcloth at her chest. "Dek's notes. The witness list. Names, dates, dredge manifests—things copied out before the warehouse ever existed to burn."

"He'll learn," I said. "He always learns. That's what makes him dangerous. He's not a fool, Sena. He's worse. He's patient."

* * *

The betrayal, when it came, didn't come from Hale. It came from the town.

I should have known. I did know—I had grown up on this levee, bought my first rope here, learned to read water off the old pilots who drank at the Anchor Line—but knowing a thing and feeling it are different cargo entirely.

It was the Anchor Line where it showed itself. I went in alone, hooded, past midnight, because the Anchor Line was where the pilots gathered and the pilots were where the truth lived, and I needed to know which of them would still speak to me. For eleven years I had avoided this room, and walking into it felt like walking into my own funeral, and part of me had hoped—God help me, part of me had actually hoped—for the rough warmth of it, the pipe smoke and the argument and somebody banging the table about channel markers.

The room went quiet when I came in. Not hostile-quiet. Worse. Careful-quiet. The quiet of men checking where the windows are.

Toma was there, and the lockkeeper's widow, and half a dozen pilots whose hands I had trusted my life to in fog and flood. Old Brandt, who had taught me the narrows when I was nineteen and fearless and stupid. He was the one who spoke, and he wouldn't look at me while he did it.

"You shouldn't be here, Ivo."

"The notice is a lie, Brandt. You know me. You've known me since I was—"

"I know what I've known," he said heavily, "and I know what's printed, and I know that Magistrate Hale has closed the locks to any boat out of this district without his seal, and I know that the last pilot who argued with him about a survey lost his license inside a week, and I know that you're asking me to choose." He turned his glass on the bar, around and around. "I'm sixty-eight years old, girl. I've got a pension. I've got a granddaughter."

"You've got a river," I said. "You taught me it was worth more than a pension."

His jaw worked. For one moment—one long, terrible moment—I thought I'd reached him. Then the door opened behind me and two of Hale's constables came in out of the rain, not hurrying, not threatening, just arriving, the way weather arrives, and Brandt looked at them and then at the floor, and whatever had lived in his face for that one moment went back into its hole.

"Go home, Ivo," he said. "Or go anywhere. Just don't be here."

I walked out past the constables with my head up, because that much pride they weren't going to take from me, and I heard the room come back to life behind me—low, careful, resuming—and I understood, walking down the wet street with the rain starting, that I had just lost my hometown. Not to fire. To fear. Hale hadn't needed to burn the Anchor Line. He'd only needed to let everyone in it do the arithmetic Brandt had done, and the arithmetic always came out the same, and it always would, until somebody changed the numbers.

That was the deepest cut of it, deeper than the notice, deeper than the burned warehouse. Not that the law was against me—I had known the law was against me since I was twenty-four years old in a courtroom with Hale's signature moving through the evidence like a tapeworm. But I had always believed the river was mine. The riverfolk, the ferrymen, the people who lived by water and knew what water costs. I had built my whole exile on the belief that if I ever came home telling the truth, the river would recognize me.

And the river had looked at a piece of printed paper and looked away.

* * *

Sena found me at the boat. I don't know how long I'd been sitting in the wheelhouse in the dark with the rain on the roof. Long enough that my coat had soaked through at the shoulders. She climbed the ladder without a word, set a lantern on the chart table, and put a mug of coffee in my hands, and the coffee was terrible, scorched, made over a spirit stove by a woman who had never once in her life made good coffee, and I drank it like it was sacrament.

"They wouldn't listen," I said.

"No."

"Brandt looked at the floor, Sena. Brandt. He held my helm in the narrows when I was nineteen. He looked at the floor."

She sat down across from me, and for a while she let the silence do its work, which is a cartographer's habit, I think—to wait until the terrain stops shifting before you draw it.

"Then we stop asking the town," she said at last.

"The town is the jury. It was always the plan. The fair, the reading, the—"

"The fair is in nine days, and every road to it runs through Hale's seals and Hale's notices and Hale's constables, and every person we counted on just discovered what it costs to count for us." She leaned forward. "Ivo. Listen to me. He thinks he's won tonight. Look at what he's done—burned the records, branded us pirates, locked the locks, frightened the pilots. From where he sits, the ledger is a dead document in the hands of a dead woman. He's already writing his report."

"So?"

"So he's stopped watching the way he watches when he's afraid." Her eyes caught the lantern light, and there was something in them I'd seen on the river at night, right before the moon came out from behind cloud—a kind of silver certainty. "A cornered man guards every door. A victorious man guards the doors he remembers. Dek's list has fourteen names on it, Ivo. Ferrymen. A dredge foreman. Two widows of the drownings. A lockkeeper at Vair's Cut who signed a manifest he was told to burn and kept a copy instead. Fourteen people scattered along ninety miles of river, each of them thinking, all these years, that they were the only one who kept something."

I stared at her. The rain drummed. Somewhere down the levee, a dog was barking at the smoke smell that would hang over Kessel Bend for a month.

"You want to go get them," I said. "One by one. On an outlawed boat, past sealed locks, with a reward on our heads."

"I want to build something he can't burn," she said. "He can burn a warehouse. He can burn a courthouse full of files, if it comes to that. He cannot burn fourteen separate fires already lit in fourteen separate houses. He doesn't even know where they all are." She pulled the oilcloth packet from her shirt and laid it on the chart table between us, and her fingers, unwrapping it, were steadier than mine had been all night. "My brother spent his last year doing exactly this. Copying, scattering, hiding truth in small boats. He called it seeding the current. He died before he could harvest it." She looked up at me. "Harvest it with me."

I looked down at the pages. Dek Voss's handwriting, cramped and urgent, names and dates and little marginal maps in a code Sena had cracked by lamplight while I slept. And beneath the grief and the fury and the cold ash smell that was never going to leave my coat, I felt the old thing stir—the thing that had put me on a boat at sixteen, the thing that eleven years of exile had not managed to drown. Not hope exactly. Hope was too soft a word for it. It was more like a heading. A bearing you fix and hold regardless of weather.

Every road home was closed. Fine. I had never once in my life gotten anywhere by road.

"All right," I said. "Vair's Cut first. The lockkeeper with the manifest. It's forty miles upriver, the lock is sealed, and we'll be running it in a boat that's held together with pine tar and prayer."

Sena almost smiled. "Pine tar and prayer got us through the narrows."

"Pine tar and prayer, and a cartographer who reads water better than any pilot on this stretch." I stood, and my knees ached, and my hands ached under their bandages, and none of it mattered even slightly. "Get the chart. If we leave before the fog lifts, we can be past the first patrol line by dawn."

She was already unrolling it. And as her brother's inked channels spread across the table under the lantern—the drowned reach, the hidden cuts, the small secret country that existed on no official map—I understood that Hale had made one mistake, the same mistake every dam-builder makes.

He thought he could control the river by holding back the water.

He'd forgotten what a river does with what it keeps. It waits. It gathers. And then, all at once, when the ground can hold no more—

it rises.
