## 17. The Witness List

The lamp burned low before Sena found the cipher.

She had been at it since dusk, hunched in the *Marrow's* cramped aft cabin with her brother's survey notes spread across the chart table like the scattered bones of a bird—forty-one pages of field observations that read, on their surface, as the most tedious document ever produced by the River Survey Office. Water depths. Silt samples. Bearing corrections. Aldous had always been meticulous to the point of madness; she remembered him at their mother's kitchen table, fourteen years old, ruling pencil lines so fine they seemed drawn by a spider's leg.

But meticulous men do not hide things in plain sight unless they are afraid.

And Aldous Voss had been afraid. She knew that now the way you know a room has been searched—not from any single sign but from the whole of it. The pages were dated across eleven weeks, the spring and early summer of the year the dam gates closed. Eleven weeks of routine soundings along a reach of river that, according to every official map printed since, did not exist.

Sena rubbed her eyes and looked out the porthole. Beyond the glass, Kessel Bend lay under a curfew smoke, embers still rising off the ruins of the expedition warehouse like slow orange moths. Somewhere downriver, Hale's gunboat patrolled with its searchlight sweeping the banks in long white strokes, patient as a lighthouse that hated everything it illuminated.

On the bunk above her, Ivo slept badly. Sena could hear it—the shift of weight, the half-caught breath, the way a person who has spent years sleeping with one ear open never quite stops. Ivo's hands were bandaged where the rope had burned through them during the crossing, and twice in the night she had murmured something Sena couldn't catch, some name or number from the drowned dark of the narrows. Pell, maybe. The deckhand who had gone into the water holding the ledger case above his head until the river took everything but the case itself.

Sena turned back to the pages. She would not think about Pell yet. Grief was a current too, and if you let it take you mid-task you drowned just as thoroughly.

* * *

The trick, when she finally saw it, was so simple she laughed—a dry, cracked sound in the empty cabin.

Aldous's depth soundings were wrong. Not randomly wrong: systematically wrong. Every sounding taken within sight of the old town boundaries ran three feet deeper than the surrounding readings, and three feet was not instrument error. Three feet was a choice. And when she wrote the anomalous depths out in sequence—seventeen, nineteen, twenty-three, twenty-one, seventeen—and mapped them against the standard survey alphabet she and Aldous had invented as children for passing notes under the schoolmaster's nose, the numbers resolved into letters.

Q-S-W-D-Q. No. She transposed again, checked her key, and felt the hair rise on her arms.

It wasn't one message. It was dozens. Each day's soundings carried a fragment, and the fragments only assembled when laid end to end across all forty-one pages, the way a river assembles itself out of rain.

She worked until the lamp guttered, feeding wick and oil without looking up, her fingers black with pencil lead, copying the decoded text onto the back of an old cargo manifest in her smallest hand:

*Day 9 — Corven Hale visited the site office Tuesday. He brought the closure schedule forward. He says the valley will be flooded "regardless of evacuation progress." He said it smiling.*

*Day 14 — Marta Ellery refuses to leave the ferry house. Her husband's grave is on the bluff. Six families remain in Lower Aldermere proper. The relocation notices were never delivered — I checked the post ledgers. They were signed for by someone at the magistrate's office.*

*Day 22 — The church bell came down today. Old Tomas wept. Hale's men watched from the levee and made no move to help load the carts.*

*Day 30 — They have begun closing the upstream coffer dams EARLY. I have written to the Commission. My letter came back unopened, marked RETURNED TO SENDER. Someone at the Commission is his.*

*Day 37 — Evacuation incomplete. 200 souls at least still in the valley, maybe more — the count is impossible because the parish rolls were removed from the church two weeks ago. Removed, not lost. I saw the empty shelf.*

*Day 41 — Water at the gates. God forgive us all. I am writing down every name I can find and hiding this book where no clerk will look. If I drown with it, let someone smarter than me find it.*

Below that, the final page—the one whose soundings had defeated her for six hours—resolved into a single line, and Sena sat very still when she read it:

*The list follows. Learn the river's grammar and it will speak its dead.*

Then, in ordinary ink, in Aldous's ordinary hand, came the names.

Not two hundred. Not the parish rolls, which were gone forever. But thirty-one names, each with a place attached, written in the margins of the survey pages over the following weeks in the same disguised soundings-code, as though he could not bear to put them anywhere a stranger might stumble. *Widow Ellery, ferry house, Kessel Bend.* *The Ostrander brothers, lockkeepers, Mile 44.* *Halfdan Rusk, eel fisher, the oxbow below Tarn.* *Preacher Anselm's daughter, now married name Cobb, Tarn landing.* On and on, thirty-one threads of the living stitched through the valley of the dead.

And beside four of the names, a small mark Aldous had used only once elsewhere in the entire notebook: a circle with a dot, his childhood shorthand for *confirmed, seen with my own eyes.*

Four witnesses he had personally verified as alive after the flood. Four people who had stood in the valley when the water came down and lived to walk away.

Sena set down the pencil because her hands were shaking too hard to hold it.

* * *

Ivo found her there at gray dawn, sitting in the same position, surrounded by paper like a woman building a nest out of evidence. Sena heard her pause in the doorway—heard the particular silence of Ivo Thornbury assessing a room before entering it, a habit Sena had once mistaken for coldness and now understood as scar tissue.

"You haven't slept," Ivo said.

"I found it." Sena's voice came out rusted. She held up the manifest sheet. "Aldous hid the truth in his soundings. Depths for letters. He documented the whole drowning, Ivo—the undelivered notices, the missing parish rolls, Hale moving the closure date up. And then—" She stopped, swallowed. "Then he listed the witnesses. Everyone he could name who survived and where they went. Thirty-one people."

Ivo crossed the cabin and read over her shoulder. Sena felt the warmth of her standing close, smelled engine oil and river water and, beneath it, the soap they'd bought together in Tarn like ordinary people doing ordinary things in another life. Ivo read for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was very quiet.

"Your brother was on the first survey team. The one that vanished."

"The one Hale sent upriver to make sure nothing surfaced." Sena touched the last page, the final coded line. "He knew what he was walking into. He hid the book anyway. He thought—" Her throat closed. She tried again. "He thought someone smarter than him would find it."

"He was right," Ivo said simply.

Sena hadn't known she was crying until Ivo's thumb caught the tear at her jaw, careful of her own bandaged hands, turning her face gently toward the gray light. Neither of them said anything for a moment. There was no need. Whatever they had declared to each other under enforcer fire at the bell tower needed no restatement in daylight; it had settled into the structure of things, like a keel.

"Eat something," Ivo said at last. "Then show me the names. All of them."

* * *

They spread the map on the chart table after a breakfast of hard bread and coffee strong enough to strip varnish, with Halvorsen's surviving students crowded at the edges—young Wren with her arm still splinted from the siege, the geologist Okonkwo who had not spoken more than ten words since the tower, and the boy Tam, fifteen and hollow-eyed, who had carried water under fire and aged a decade in nine days.

Sena placed a brass weight on each witness location. Thirty-one weights, glittering on the chart like a constellation fallen into the river.

"Look at the distribution," she said, and her voice steadied as it always did when she taught. "They're not random. Aldous grouped them. Here—" her finger traced the lower reaches, "—eleven names between Kessel Bend and the sea. Ferrymen, lockkeepers, dock workers. People whose livelihoods depend on knowing exactly what the river does and when. You don't fool a lockkeeper about water levels. They *knew* the gates closed early."

"Hale's jurisdiction," Ivo said. "He'll have been watching them for thirty years."

"Watching, yes. But look here." Sena moved upriver. "Nine names in the middle reaches—Tarn, the oxbows, the eel grounds. That's outside his writ, or nearly. And here, the upper river past the narrows, another eight. Fishers, a schoolteacher, a woman who runs the trading post at Cold Larder. The farthest-flung survivors drifted upriver, away from the dam, away from him. He never bothered much with the upper river. It's poor country. Nobody there reads the gazette."

Okonkwo leaned in, squinting. "Thirty-one names. How many can still be alive? It's been thirty years."

"Some," Sena admitted. "Widow Ellery would be past ninety, if she lives. But look at the ages Aldous noted—he recorded them, of course he did. A dozen of these people were children in the valley. Children in 1931 are in their forties now. In their prime." She tapped the circled names, the four Aldous had verified. "And these four he saw alive *after* the flood. Halfdan Rusk the eel fisher. The Ostrander brothers. Preacher Cobb's wife. If even half the list holds..."

"Then you don't have a ledger anymore," Wren said slowly. "You have a congregation."

"A jury," Ivo corrected, and there was something in her voice that made Sena look up from the chart. Ivo was staring at the constellation of brass weights the way she stared at water before running rapids—reading it, measuring herself against it, finding the line through. "Hale framed me because my father saw the drownings. Because a witness with a name and a face is dangerous in a way no document ever is. Documents burn." She touched the manifest, Aldous's careful coded depths. "People remember."

"People can also be frightened into silence," Okonkwo said. "Or bought. Or disappeared, like your brother."

"My brother was disappeared," Sena said, "because he worked alone." She straightened, and the exhaustion fell away from her like a coat. "That's the lesson. That's why he died and why the ledger sat hidden for thirty years. One witness is a problem to be erased. Thirty-one witnesses, gathered in one place, in front of a crowd—" She met Ivo's eyes. "That's something else entirely."

"The lock fair," Ivo said.

Sena nodded. She'd seen it in Ivo's face already, days ago, the idea forming behind her eyes like weather. "Every boat on the river comes to Kessel Bend for the fair. Hundreds of craft. Every ferry family, every lockkeeper, every trader for two hundred miles. And it's *their* fair—riverfolk's law, riverfolk's custom, older than any magistrate's commission. Hale can't clear the ground the way he cleared the warehouse. Not with the whole river watching."

"It's in nine days," Tam said quietly. He had been silent so long that his voice startled them all. "My father took me every year. There's a bell they ring at noon. The old ferry bell." His jaw worked. "It came out of the valley. Out of Aldermere. They rang it the day the water came, warning the lower farms. Somebody saved it when the town drowned, and nobody remembers who."

The cabin went very still.

Sena looked down at the chart, at the little brass weight marking Widow Ellery's ferry house at Kessel Bend, and felt the pieces click together with an almost audible sound. The ferry house. The widow who refused to leave her husband's grave. The woman Aldous had listed first of all.

"She kept the bell," Sena whispered. "Or she knows who did. Ivo—if she's alive, if that house still stands—"

"Then the fair opens with the ringing of a bell that watched the drownings," Ivo finished, "and we read the ledger to a crowd standing in its shadow."

Outside, dawn was coming up properly now, burning off the river mist, and somewhere downstream the gunboat's searchlight swept the bank one last time and went dark, surrendering to the morning. Sena began copying the witness list onto three separate sheets, dividing the names by reach, her pencil moving fast.

"We can't gather thirty-one people in nine days," Okonkwo protested. "Some of these places are a week's travel apart."

"We don't gather them," Sena said. "We *summon* them. Riverfolk talk faster than any boat travels—that's how Aldous's generation survived, word passed lock to lock. We send word ahead at every stop: tell the families of the drowned that the truth surfaces at the lock fair, noon, at the ferry bell. Let the river carry the invitation." She blew the pencil dust from the finished sheets and slid them across the table to Ivo. "You once told me the river keeps what it keeps. Well. It kept my brother's list. It kept your father's memory. It kept thirty-one names."

Ivo picked up the sheets, and Sena watched the last hesitation go out of her—the final flinch of a woman who had spent seven years believing she had nothing left to fight *for*, rather than only something to hide *from*.

"Then we'd better start talking to locks," Ivo said. "Tam—how soon can you raise steam?"

The boy was already moving for the engine room.

* * *

They cast off within the hour, slipping downriver on the ebb with the warehouse ashes still hanging faintly in the air behind them. Sena stood at the rail with the manifest sheet folded inside her coat, over her heart, and watched the banks begin to slide past—mile 44 coming up ahead, where two lockkeeper brothers had once confirmed a crime with their own eyes and presumably spent thirty years wondering if anyone would ever ask.

At the lock, when they came to it, Sena stepped ashore with a copy of the list pressed into the elder Ostrander's calloused hand, and watched an old man read his own name in a dead surveyor's code and begin, silently, to weep.

By nightfall, word was moving up and down the river faster than any gunboat could follow.

Hope, Sena thought, watching lamplight kindle in windows that had been dark for a generation, was assembled the same way her brother built his ciphers: piece by piece, in plain sight, waiting for someone patient enough to read the water.

Two hundred miles downstream, a bell waited in a ferry house to be rung again.

And ninety miles beyond that, unseen in the gathering dark, the gunboat turned upriver.
