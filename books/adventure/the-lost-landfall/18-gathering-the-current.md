## 18. Gathering the Current

The last coin in Ivo Thornbury's pocket was a silver half-crown, worn smooth as a river stone, minted the year before her trial. She turned it over in her palm on the morning they left Kessel Bend behind, feeling the old king's profile rubbed nearly featureless by thirty years of hands. Her father had given it to her once, when she was small, telling her to keep it for luck and never spend it.

She spent it that same afternoon, and she did not regret it.

* * *

They worked downriver in the *Magpie*'s slow way, engine mended enough to grumble but not to sing, stopping at every landing, every lock, every leaning shack where a lamp burned late. Sena rode at the rail with her brother's decoded notes spread across her knees like scripture, cross-referencing names against the ledger Halvorsen had died protecting. The ledger itself stayed hidden in the engine housing, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from the machinery, closer to Ivo's heart than anything she owned.

The first witness was a ferryman at Tallow Landing named Brack, who poled travelers across the narrows for coppers and remembered everything he'd ever seen, because seeing was all there was to do between crossings.

Ivo bought him a bottle of plum brandy and sat with him while the light went amber over the water. She didn't show him the ledger. She didn't have to. She only asked him what he'd seen the spring the dam gates closed.

Brack drank, and looked at the river a long time, and talked.

"Saw them walk out," he said. "Whole families. The company said the valley'd be empty by the equinox, but folk don't leave their houses just because a paper says so. They waited. Stood on the ridgeline watching the water come up their own streets. There was a woman—" His voice caught, restarted. "There was a woman carrying a clock. A mantel clock, big as a loaf of bread. Carried it above her head like an offering when the water took her yard. Never made a sound. None of them did. That's the part nobody believes. It weren't screaming. It were quiet."

"Would you say that to a crowd?" Ivo asked. "If it mattered?"

"To a crowd," Brack said, "to a magistrate, to God Himself if He'll stand still long enough. Thirty years I've carried that woman and her clock. You're the first soul ever asked me to set it down."

He wouldn't take payment. Ivo pressed the half-crown into his palm anyway and closed his fingers over it. "Not for the testimony," she said. "For the keeping of it. You held it longer than anyone should have to."

* * *

Word traveled the way word does on a river—faster than any boat, riding the current of gossip from lock to lock. By the third day, people were waiting for them at landings where Ivo hadn't planned to stop. An old lockkeeper at Weir Nine who'd signed the gate logs and knew the dates didn't match the official record. A widow whose husband had been a dam engineer, drowned conveniently before he could testify, leaving behind a toolbox full of drawings marked with dates and initials. Two brothers who'd hauled freight for the dam company and could recite, from memory, the manifest of crates labeled FARM EQUIPMENT that had weighed like machinery and smelled like gun oil.

Sena sat with each of them in the *Magpie*'s cramped cabin, taking notes in her precise cartographer's hand, building something that wasn't quite a map and wasn't quite a list. It was a chart of memory. Every name a sounding line dropped into thirty years of silence, coming back up with mud on it—and sometimes gold.

"They were paid to forget," Sena said one night, her pencil hovering over the page. "Every one of them. Small sums, mostly. Enough for a winter's coal. And it worked, because forgetting is easier when you're hungry. But look—" She turned the notebook toward Ivo. "Look how many kept something anyway. A drawing. A logbook page. A letter they never sent. People hide the truth the way squirrels hide nuts. Instinct. Even when they think no one will ever come digging."

"Someone came digging," Ivo said.

"Yes." Sena looked at her across the lamplight, and there was something fierce and tender in her face at once. "You did. With your last coin."

* * *

It was true, more or less. The half-crown was gone, and after it went the rest—the brass compass Ivo had used since she was twelve, traded to a pawnbroker in Millbrook for coin enough to buy ink, paper, and passage-money for witnesses who needed to travel when the time came. She kept nothing but the boat, the crew, the ledger, and the woman who refused to leave either.

"You realize we're financing a revolution with pawnshop money," Rennick said, the evening he found her counting coppers on the deck. Her boatswain had come through the siege at the bell tower with a scar across his forearm and a silence about the man who hadn't come out—the young deckhand, Pell, who'd gone into the flooded archive street for the ledger and hadn't come up. Ivo saw Pell every time she closed her eyes: his hand breaking the surface, then not.

"Not a revolution," Ivo said. "A reading."

"At the lock fair. In front of Hale's gunboat."

"He wants a public reckoning. He's spent thirty years making sure everything happened quiet—behind doors, under water, inside sealed files. So we do the opposite. Sunlight, a crowd, and every voice the river's got." She stacked the coppers into a little tower and knocked it flat with one finger. "Hale can shoot me. He can't shoot three hundred witnesses at once. Not in front of the fair. Not without becoming exactly what the ledger says he is."

Rennick chewed on that, working his scarred arm the way he did when thinking. "And if the crowd's afraid? If they scatter?"

"Then we'll know the river's forgotten how to speak," Ivo said. "But I don't believe that. Do you?"

Rennick looked downstream, toward Kessel Bend, toward the burned warehouse and the brand of piracy Hale had painted on them all. "No, Captain," he said slowly. "I've been listening to it talk all week. It's got plenty to say."

* * *

The defection came at dusk on the fifth day, at a reed-choked inlet below Weir Eleven, and it came in a stolen skiff paddled by a man so exhausted he could barely hold the oars.

Fen. The second surveyor. The one who'd boarded at Millbrook with wrong boots and a silent partner, who'd demanded Ivo turn back at Dead Air, whose partner had slipped away in the night with a crate and come back wearing Hale's colors. Fen had stood in the standoff at Aldermere with a rifle in his hands and his eyes anywhere but on Sena's bleeding arm. Then he'd vanished again when the siege broke, and Ivo had assumed he'd gone home to his paymaster.

Instead he'd spent nine days finding his way downriver, dodging Hale's patrols, to find her.

She met him at the rail with a boathook and no welcome. "Give me one reason I shouldn't leave you for the reeds."

"Because I brought you this." He lifted an oilskin packet with shaking hands. Inside: orders. Signed orders, on Kessel Bend magistracy letterhead, bearing Corven Hale's seal. *Secure the collection. No witnesses.* Dated the week before the expedition's radios went dead. Beneath them, expense disbursements—payments to informants, to the men who'd burned the base camp, to a clerk in the records office for "file maintenance." File maintenance. Ivo's file. Her father's file.

"My partner took these orders straight from Hale's own desk," Fen said. "I copied them before he did. I've been carrying them ever since, waiting for my spine to grow in." He laughed, and it broke in the middle. "It never did. Not really. I'm not brave, Captain. I'm just done."

"Why now?" Ivo asked. "Why not stay bought?"

Fen looked past her, at the river running dark and patient toward the sea. "Because I was at Aldermere. I walked those streets when the drought dropped the water. I saw the doorframes. The height marks where parents measured their children, cut into the stone, right where the flood line runs." His voice went thin. "Hale told us it was ruins. Abandoned. Nobody home. But nobody measures their children in an abandoned town, Captain. Somebody lived there. Somebody was told to leave and didn't believe the water would really come. And Hale signed the order that made sure it did."

Ivo took the packet. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; inside her chest, something vast and cold was moving, the way ice moves on a river in thaw—slowly, and then all at once.

"You'll testify," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I'll testify." Fen swallowed. "Will it matter? One coward's confession against a magistrate?"

"It won't be one," Ivo said. "Come below. There are people you need to meet—a lockkeeper, a widow, two freight brothers, and a ferryman who watched a woman carry a clock above the flood. You're not a coward anymore, Fen. You're the eighteenth voice. And voices are like current." She nodded at the river, at the whole brown-gold weight of it sliding seaward, gathering every creek and seep and spring along a hundred miles. "One drop, nothing. But enough drops—" 

"—and even a dam can't hold," Fen finished.

* * *

That night, anchored in the willow shadow below Weir Eleven, Ivo climbed to the roof of the wheelhouse and sat with the ledger open on her knees, though she no longer needed to read it. She knew it now the way she knew the channel markers of her childhood reach—every entry, every date, every signature. Including the one that had started everything. Her father's name, written as witness to the water releases, dated three days before the drownings, three days before he drowned too—or was made to look drowned—and six years before his daughter stood in a courtroom and heard herself convicted of a theft she'd never committed, on evidence a records clerk had quietly maintained.

Below her, the boat murmured. Rennick checking lines. Sena's lamp burning late in the cabin, her shadow bent over the witness chart, adding Fen's name in her careful hand. Somewhere upstream, a gunboat lay at anchor with its orders and its secrets, and somewhere downstream, the lock fair was assembling itself out of bunting and rumor and the annual hunger of riverfolk for spectacle.

Let him come, Ivo thought. Let him bring his seal and his soldiers and his thirty years of practiced calm. The river had been keeping testimony longer than any magistrate had been keeping files. It had kept the drowned towns and the drowned names and the drowned truth, and now, drop by drop, voice by voice, it was giving it all back.

She closed the ledger and looked at the stars, and for the first time since the trial, the future didn't feel like a sentence. It felt like a current. Strong, and rising, and pointed somewhere.

Two days later, rounding the last bend before the fairgrounds at Harrow Lock, they saw the bunting strung bank to bank—and saw, moored midstream in the fairest anchorage, black and patient as a verdict, the gunboat *Corven's Pride*, flying the magistrate's pennant.

Hale had arrived first.

Sena came up beside her at the wheel, followed her gaze, and said quietly, "So. He means to watch."

"He means to stop it," Ivo said. She felt the old dread rise and, beneath it, something new and harder, something forged in bell towers and flooded streets and the long slow labor of gathering voices. She put the wheel over and steered the *Magpie* straight toward the fair. "Then we'd best make certain there's too much happening for even a gunboat to hold."
