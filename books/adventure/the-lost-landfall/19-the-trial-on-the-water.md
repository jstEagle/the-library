## 19. The Trial on the Water

### Ivo

The lock fair at Marrow's Gate began, as it had for a hundred years, with the ringing of the great bell above the lock gates—a cracked old thing that sounded less like a bell than like a warning, which Ivo had always thought fitting. By midmorning the cut was jammed bow to stern: grain barges lashed three abreast, painted excursion steamers with their brass rails polished, log rafts from the upper camps, houseboats hung with laundry and children. The smell was diesel and frying doughnuts and wet rope and the green breath of the river itself, high and brown with spring melt.

Ivo stood on the deck of the *Marrowbird*—her boat, her name burned into her by Hale's clerks as *pirate vessel* now, though no one at the fair seemed to care—and watched the crowd gather below like water finding its level. Word had traveled faster than she had. That was the way of rivers; that was the way of grief. Ferrymen she'd paid with her last coin had told their passengers. Lockkeepers had told the barge crews. A widow named Ostrander had told everyone within earshot of the fish market, and what she'd said, Sena reported, was simply: *"They drowned living towns, and they blamed a girl for knowing."*

That was enough. On a river, that was always enough.

"You don't have to do it this way," Sena said beside her, maps rolled under one arm, the graze on her shoulder still bound beneath her coat. "You could hand copies to the magistrate at Corven. Let the paper do the work."

"The paper did the work thirty years ago," Ivo said. "It went into a drawer and the drawer went into a fire. No. Paper needs voices. It needs a hundred of them, all saying it at once, where it can't be quietly filed."

Below them, the crowd had thickened along both quays. She counted faces she knew—the ferryman Brask with his burn-scarred hands, the lockkeeper's widow in her gray shawl, two of Halvorsen's gaunt survivors sitting upright on crates like witnesses already sworn in. And more she didn't know. Hundreds. Maybe six hundred, maybe eight, spilling off the quay onto the moored decks of strangers' boats, because on the river a stranger's deck during fair week was common ground.

"They're waiting," Sena said.

"Then let's not keep a jury standing."

Ivo climbed to the wheelhouse roof, took the reading stand they'd built from a chart table, and opened the ledger.

* * *

### Sena

Sena had decoded her brother's notes twice more in the night, by lamplight, until the numbers swam. They matched the ledger. They matched it line for line—the gauge readings from the first survey team, taken before the dams closed, showing the valley towns still inhabited, still lit at night, still petitioning the government for protection. Petitions that were received and buried. Signatures acknowledging receipt. One of those signatures belonged to a young clerk named Corven Hale.

She stood at the rail now and watched Ivo read, and understood that she was watching something older than law happen. Not a trial—there was no bench, no bar, no oath. Something the river had been doing since before there were courthouses: weighing a man in front of his neighbors.

Ivo's voice carried the way river voices carry, low and even, pitched under the wind rather than against it. She read the ledger entries plainly, without theater. *Received, 14 March, thirty years past: report of occupied dwellings in the Aldermere basin, population 1,140. Disposition: no action. Endorsed: C. Hale.* Then the next. *Compensation schedule deferred indefinitely.* Then the entry that made the whole quay go quiet, so that Sena could hear the water slapping hulls and a child asking its mother, too loudly, what *occupied dwellings* meant.

*Flood scheduled ahead of evacuation. Roads out to be closed for "maintenance."*

"They closed the roads," Ivo read. "They closed the roads, and then they opened the dams."

A sound moved through the crowd—not a gasp, nothing so clean. A low murmur, like current under ice. Somewhere a man began to swear, steadily, without stopping, the way men swear when they've run out of anything better.

Sena found her brother's name rising in her throat and swallowed it. Tam Voss had been nineteen when he rowed into the Aldermere basin with the first survey team and never rowed out. For thirty years the official record said *lost in a surveying accident, waters unseasonably high.* Now she knew the waters had been seasonably, deliberately, administratively high. She knew who had signed the order to close the roads. She had it in her brother's own handwriting, corroborated by a dying professor's confession, and today, in front of six hundred people, it was going to stop being a secret.

That was when she saw the smoke.

Not chimney smoke. Funnel smoke, black and oily, coming downriver fast against the current, and under it the gray shape of a gunboat riding low with the weight of engines and armament no river patrol needed for smugglers.

"Hale," she said, and Ivo looked up from the ledger, and their eyes met across ten feet of deck, and Sena watched her lover do the arithmetic every captain does—distance, speed, time—and then set the arithmetic aside and keep reading.

* * *

### Hale

Magistrate Corven Hale stood on the gunboat's foredeck with his hands clasped behind him and let the river come to him, because it always did. Thirty years he had served this waterway, and in thirty years he had learned the single truth that governed it: the river forgave nothing, but it forgot everything, if you were patient and thorough. Valleys forgot. Records forgot. Even widows forgot, eventually, or died, which amounted to the same thing in a file cabinet.

He had built a career on being patient and thorough. He had ruined a riverboat pilot's father with an audit, and ruined the daughter with a conviction, not out of cruelty—he wished people would understand this—but out of hygiene. Some truths, left lying about, infected everything they touched. The drownings were a wound; the Thornburys were a splinter; you removed the splinter or you lost the arm. He had lost nothing. The valley had filled with water and silence, and the silence had held for three decades, and he had slept well.

Until a half-drowned expedition came down out of the hills carrying a dead professor's ledger.

His enforcer Dree stood at the rail beside him, saying nothing, which was unlike Dree. Hale glanced over. The man was watching the fair crowd with an expression Hale had never seen on him before—something loose around the mouth, something unsettled behind the eyes.

"Six hundred witnesses," Dree said finally. "Maybe more. You going to arrest her in front of all of them?"

"I'm going to restore order in front of all of them," Hale said. "There is a difference, and the difference is paperwork. She's a convicted pirate in possession of stolen property, sheltering fugitives. Every word out of my mouth will be procedurally correct."

"And if they've heard the ledger?"

Hale looked at the black funnel smoke streaming ahead of them, at the fair brightening as they rounded the last bend—the bunting, the barges, the bell tower of the lockhouse, the crowd dense as spawn on both quays. He felt, briefly and unwelcome, the memory of the valley at night before the water came: lamplit windows, forty of them, fifty, a whole constellation of ordinary evenings that his signatures had extinguished. He had told himself for thirty years that the constellation had been dim anyway. Failing towns. Doomed valleys. Progress.

He had been thorough. He had almost been patient enough.

"Then we learn," Hale said, "whether a river full of boats can outrun the law."

He gave the order for flank speed, and the gunboat's wake climbed white and angry behind them, and far ahead, thin over the water, he could hear a woman's voice reading names.

### Ivo

She let the last words sit on the air. Below her, the crowd had stopped being a crowd and become something else—a congregation, a jury, a single animal with six hundred pairs of eyes. An old woman near the fish stalls had begun to weep without any noise at all, tears running into the creases of her face while her mouth stayed firm. Two barge captains had linked arms. Brask the ferryman stood with his burn-scarred fists clenched at his sides, and Ivo remembered paying him her last coin for his testimony and thought: *best money I ever spent, and it wasn't the money.*

"My father," she said, and her voice held, mostly, "was a lockkeeper at Aldermere town. He watched the water come. He wrote letters for eleven years afterward. Every letter was answered by the same office, and every answer was signed by the same clerk, and that clerk grew up to be Magistrate Corven Hale. When my father wouldn't stop writing, Hale destroyed him with an audit. When I wouldn't stop asking why, Hale destroyed me with a conviction. I have spent nine years running from a crime I didn't commit, and the man who committed the real one is coming up that channel right now, aboard a gunboat, to tell you all that none of this happened."

She turned the ledger around and held it high so the sun caught the pages.

"But it happened. And here are the names of everyone who signed it."

And then, because the moment demanded it and because she had promised herself she would not flinch, she began to read the witness list—her father's name first, then the surviving families, then the scattered witnesses Sena had decoded from her brother's notes, ferrymen and lockkeepers and a dredge operator and a schoolteacher, names called out over the water like a roll at muster. With each name, somewhere in the crowd, someone answered. *Here. Here. My mother, rest her. Here—that's me.*

The answering voices were what broke it open. Ivo felt it happen the way you feel a flood stage change: not all at once but everywhere at once, a deep shift in the current. Six hundred people discovering they were not alone in what they knew.

Which was when the gunboat came round the bend.

* * *

### Sena

It came on fast, black smoke rolling, its bow wave slapping the moored barges so hard that hawsers groaned and children stumbled. A loudhailer crackled. Hale's voice, smooth as silt, rolled across the fair:

*"This gathering is unlawful. The vessel *Marrowbird* is seized under warrant. Disperse to your homes. Anyone obstructing officers of the river court will be charged."*

Nobody dispersed. That was the thing Sena would remember all her life—nobody moved. A thousand people on the quays and decks stood exactly where they were, and the silence they returned to the loudhailer was worse than shouting. It was the silence of people who had just heard their own history read aloud and were measuring it against the man who'd erased it.

Ivo stepped to the rail. "Magistrate Hale!" she called, and her voice carried without any machine at all. "Come aboard! Bring your warrant! We'll read it right after the ledger—you can check our citations!"

A ripple of grim laughter ran the crowd. Hale's gunboat slowed, circling, and Sena saw the problem land on him visibly, the way trouble lands on a careful man: he could not board without wading through a wall of linked barges and linked arms, and everything he did now would be done in front of witnesses who had *just* heard his signature read out in his own hand.

Dree appeared at the gunboat's rail with a boarding hook, and the crowd pressed forward instead of back, and Sena's heart went up into her throat—

—and Dree stopped. Stood there with the hook in his hands, looking at the faces. Looking, Sena thought, at the old woman weeping silently. At the child on his father's shoulders. At the roll call still echoing: *here, here, here.*

"Dree," Hale said, sharp, through the loudhailer's off-switch. "The hook."

Dree looked at the hook. He looked at Hale. And then, in a voice that carried across the water better than any loudhailer, because it was quiet and therefore everyone strained to hear it, he said:

"I was on the salvage crew. After the flood. Thirty years back." He turned the hook over in his hands as if seeing it for the first time. "We pulled things out of the water for six weeks. Furniture. Livestock. People. We were told it was a natural dam failure." His voice didn't rise at all. "I've been on this man's payroll eleven years, and I never once asked him why a natural disaster needed a magistrate to guard its records."

The whole fair had gone still enough to hear the lock gates dripping.

"So before anybody boards anybody," Dree said, and set the hook down on the gunboat's deck with a click that sounded, to Sena, exactly like a cell door, "somebody ought to ask him."

Every head on the river turned toward Corven Hale.

Hale stood on his foredeck with the spring light on his face and six hundred witnesses arranged around him like water around a stone, and Sena saw him understand, in the slow terrible way of a drowning man, that the current had changed direction—and that it was not finished with him yet.

Behind her, Ivo's hand found hers and gripped it hard.

"He'll try to run," Ivo murmured. "Watch the wheel."

The gunboat's engine note changed.
