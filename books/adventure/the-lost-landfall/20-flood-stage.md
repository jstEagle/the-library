## 20. Flood Stage

The lock fair had turned the river into a town.

Ivo had never seen so many hulls packed into the wide water below Kessel Bend—ferry barges lashed three abreast, fishing smacks with their nets hung out like banners, houseboats with laundry strung from railings, log rafts, oyster dredges, a floating chapel with a painted stern and a bell that had been ringing since dawn. Every craft that could float and half that couldn't had come up from the lower reaches and down from the headwaters, drawn by the rumor that moved faster than any telegraph: *the Thornbury woman has the ledger. She's going to read it on the water. Today.*

She stood at the wheel of the *Magpie's Rest* and felt the crowd the way she felt a shoal—through the soles of her boots, through the hull, through some animal sense older than seamanship. The river was high with the week's rain, brown and muscular, running a full fathom over the fair-season marks. Banners snapped. A fiddler on a coal barge was playing something fast and sour. Somewhere a child was selling roasted chestnuts off a skiff, working the crowd of boats like a street corner.

Sena stood beside her at the rail, her arm in a sling that Ivo had tied that morning, twice, because the first knot hadn't been good enough and Sena had made her do it over. The maps were below, wrapped in oilcloth. The ledger was in Ivo's coat, against her ribs, where it had lived for eleven days like a coal that wouldn't cool.

"They came," Sena said quietly. "All of them. Look at the lockkeepers. That's Brannic's ferry on the north shore—the one you bought testimony from with your last coin."

"I didn't buy it," Ivo said. "I bought the *trip*."

"You bought the truth, and everyone knows the difference. That's why they came."

Ivo didn't answer. She was looking south, downriver, where the water opened wide and gray toward Kessel Bend proper, toward the burned warehouse and the magistrate's court with its green dome, toward the place where the law lived. The crowd of boats had left a lane there—unwillingly, nervously, the way water leaves a lane around a rock.

Into that lane, at half past nine, came the gunboat.

* * *

She had known he would come. She had counted on it, in the way you count on a rapid having a drop. But knowing did not prepare her for the sound—the diesel throb of the *Corven's Pride*, named for himself, of course, everything on this river named for him whether he owned it or merely frightened it—and the sight of the forward gun, a real one, a naval surplus thing with a canvas cover that two of Hale's men were pulling off even as the bow came on.

The crowd did not part so much as tighten. Ivo watched it happen with a feeling she had no name for: forty boats, sixty, drifting by inches, by oar-stroke, by the simple refusal of helmsmen to give way—closing the lane behind the gunboat even as it opened ahead of her. Brannic's ferry slid left. The coal barge slid right. The chapel bell began to ring faster.

"He's going to order them aside," Sena said. "He'll order them aside and they'll obey, because they always—"

"They won't," Ivo said. "Not today."

She didn't know it for certain. She said it the way you say a heading: choosing it, and then steering for it.

Hale's voice came across the water through a speaking trumpet, polished and carrying, the voice of a man who had spent twenty years making rooms go quiet. "Ivo Thornbury! By authority of the magistrate's court of the Kessel Bend district, you are under arrest for piracy, theft of protected antiquities, and the murder of Professor Edvard Halvorsen. Strike your wheel and prepare to be boarded."

The lie about Halvorsen landed in the crowd like a stone in a well. Ivo heard the murmur go around—*murder, he's saying murder*—and she felt the old cold open in her chest, the cold of the trial, of the courtroom where her father's name had been dragged through mud she'd never been allowed to wash off. Eleven days she had carried the ledger against her ribs. She had thought she was ready to read from it. She discovered, in that moment, that she had not been ready at all—she had only been armed.

She took the speaking trumpet from its bracket. Her hands were steady. She noticed that with a strange, floating detachment: the hands that had shaken for six years after the trial, steady now, at the end.

"Professor Halvorsen died of his heart, in his own camp, with his students around him," she called back. "I was three days' run downstream when it happened, and forty people on this water can swear to where I was. But you know that, Magistrate. You know everything about that expedition. You sent your men to it. You sent them twice."

The trumpet in Hale's hand lowered a fraction. On the gunboat's deck, the two enforcers by the gun had gone very still.

"Board her," Hale said, not into the trumpet—into the voice-tube to his helmsman—but the river heard it anyway, because the river had gone quiet. Sixty boats, a thousand people, and the only sounds were the bell and the water and the diesel throb.

The gunboat came on.

* * *

What happened next, Ivo would spend the rest of her life failing to describe properly, because it did not happen the way events happen, in sequence, with causes. It happened all at once, the way a river goes over a ledge.

Brannic's ferry moved first. The old lockkeeper put his wheel hard over and his heavy beam slid across the gunboat's bow line like a gate swinging shut, and his ferryman's pole came down into the water with a crack that carried, and he shouted—not words, just a sound, the sound ferrymen make when they refuse a crossing—and the sound was taken up. The coal barge closed from the east. The oyster dredges, ugly and stubborn, closed from the west. The floating chapel swung its stern across the southern gap, and the priest stood on its roof in his vestments with his arms out, and Ivo heard him say, in a voice built for small congregations but given, this once, to a thousand people: "There is no lawful arrest on this water today. There is only testimony. Let it be heard."

The gunboat's horn sounded, three long furious blasts. The forward gun's crew looked at their officer; the officer looked at the chapel roof, at the priest, at the hundred small boats now packed so tight around them that a shell fired anywhere would land among children and chestnut sellers and grandmothers with oars.

Hale stood at his rail in his gray magistrate's coat, and Ivo watched him understand, in real time, the thing she had understood at the burned camp: that the river was not a road through his jurisdiction. The river was a country. It had never been his.

"Thornbury!" he shouted, without the trumpet now, his voice cracking on the second syllable, and it was the first time in six years she had heard him sound like a man and not an office. "Whatever you think you have, it proves nothing. Paper burns. Witnesses drown. Ask your father."

The crowd made a sound Ivo had never heard a crowd make—a low, collective intake, a thousand people inhaling together—and she felt Sena's good hand close on her arm, hard, holding her to the deck, because her body had already decided to go over the rail and her mind caught up a half-second later.

"Ask him," Hale said again, softer, and the trumpet found his mouth once more, and the polish came back like a mask settling. "Ask her, riverfolk, what her father was. A drunk. A trespasser. A man who drowned in flood water because he was too stupid to come off the river. That is the court's finding. It has been the finding for thirty years. Nothing she carries changes a finding."

And that was when the enforcer stepped to the gunboat's rail.

* * *

Ivo knew him. Of course she knew him. Dree Halvard—the name he'd given her at the dock, one of the two silent men with the wrong boots, the surveyor who'd slipped away from the first camp with a crate and come back with Hale's guns. He had stood in her wheelhouse for six days and drunk her coffee and lied to her with a flatness she had almost admired. He was a big man, gray at the temples, with the stillness of someone who had done violence and expected to do more.

He stepped to the rail and he did not look at Hale. He looked at the water.

"Magistrate," he said, and his voice carried the way big quiet voices do, "I'll ask you one question, in front of all these people, and I'll ask it as a sworn officer of your district. When you ordered us upriver to the expedition camp—was the order to *secure* the collection, or to *sink* it?"

The diesel throb filled the silence. The chapel bell had stopped ringing. Even the fiddler on the coal barge had gone still, bow raised, a held breath of rosin and string.

"Dree," Hale said, gently, the way a man speaks to a dog he may have to put down, "step back from the rail."

"Because I have the second order in writing," Halvard said. "In your hand. And I kept it, Magistrate, the way you taught all of us to keep things—because you said paper protects the man who holds it." He reached into his coat, slowly, and the whole river flinched, a thousand people moving as one body—and brought out not a gun but a folded paper, gone soft at the creases, handled many times. "You said that at my swearing-in. Eleven years ago. I believed you."

"Arrest that man," Hale said to his officer.

The officer did not move. Ivo watched him not move—the small, enormous refusal of it, a man standing on a deck with a gun's lanyard in reach and choosing, visibly, deliberately, to look at the sky instead. The second enforcer turned and walked to the stern and sat down on the bollard and put his face in his hands.

Halvard unfolded the paper and read it aloud. Ivo had read those words herself, eleven days ago, in a dying professor's ledger, and hearing them again in a sworn officer's voice over open water was like hearing a bell she'd only ever seen: *the collection is to be denied to all parties; if removal cannot be effected, the site and its contents are to be made unrecoverable; no personnel of the expedition are to be permitted to depart the reach; the captain of the supply vessel is to be handled at the magistrate's discretion.* Signed. Sealed. Dated four days before the camp burned.

"Made unrecoverable," Halvard read again, and lowered the paper. "That's a burning, Magistrate. That's what we were sent to do. I burned the stores because you told me to. I did not burn the people, though you'll note the order permitted it. I want that noted." His voice did not rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "I want it noted that I drew that line, and that you drew the other one, and that I have carried your paper in my coat for a month waiting to find out which of us I was."

"Thornbury put you up to this," Hale said. "She's a convicted thief. She'll say anything—"

"I was there before she ever reached the camp," Halvard said. "You know that. You sent me." He looked down at the water again, at the brown flood running hard and high between the boats, and Ivo saw him swallow. "I've been on this river twenty-two years, sir. I've pulled eleven bodies out of it. Every one of them was found downstream of something you signed." He looked up. "I'm not drowning for your secrets. I never was going to. I just needed to hear you say them first."

Hale's face, in that moment, did the thing Ivo would remember longest. It did not rage. It *rearranged*—the magistrate folding away, the man underneath surfacing for one unguarded second, a man looking out at a thousand small boats and understanding that the water had risen past the top of everything he had built. His hand went to the trumpet and stopped. His officers were looking at Halvard. His gun crew was looking at the chapel. His helmsman, Ivo saw, had quietly put the engine to half ahead *away* from the *Magpie's Rest*, as if the gunboat itself had decided something.

"You have nothing," Hale said, but it came out as a question, and the river answered it.

Brannic's ferry horn blew. Then the coal barge. Then a dozen horns, a hundred, the ragged joyous terrible chorus of a river deciding—and under it, rising, the voices, not words at first, just sound, and then words, and the words were her name, and then, worse and better than her name, they were his: *Corven Hale, Corven Hale,* chanted flat and slow like a tolling bell, like a count, like the beginning of something that would not stop at the waterline.

* * *

"Take us in," Ivo said.

Sena turned. "Ivo—"

"Take us alongside. Now, while he's frozen. If he gets his wheel back he'll run for the Bend and the court and we start over with a roof over his head and a judge he owns." She was already moving, untying the ledger's oilcloth with her scarred hands, the hands Sena had bandaged, the hands her father had taught to read water. "Halvard's order, the professor's ledger, the witness list. On the water, in front of all of them. That was always the plan."

"The plan was to read it," Sena said, following her down the deck. "The plan was not to hand yourself to a mob."

"It's not a mob." Ivo looked back at her—at the sling, at the graze healing along her jaw, at the woman who had come aboard with maps of a place that didn't exist and stayed for a country that did. "It's a jury. It's the only one I ever got. Six years ago twelve men in a room read a file Hale wrote and called it justice. Today the river reads the file itself." She touched the rail. "I'm not handing myself to anyone, Sen. I'm handing *this* to everyone. There's a difference. It's the whole difference."

Sena held her eyes for a long moment. Then she went to the wheel, and the *Magpie's Rest* leaned into the turn, sliding through the packed boats toward the gray coat and the frozen gun, and Ivo climbed onto the cabin roof with the ledger open against the wind.

She read. She read the permits forged in Hale's hand. She read the payments. She read the line item, dated thirty years back, that had made the crowd go silent again—the one that proved the sluice gates had been opened on a rising river with the towns still on it, *for schedule, for budget, for the magistrate's signature*—and she read the name of the man who had witnessed the gates opened and been paid to forget, and then been framed and drowned when he tried to remember.

She read her father's name.

She read it aloud, into the speaking trumpet, over the water where he had lived and on the water that had taken him, and the name went out across a thousand boats and came back changed—no longer a drunk, no longer a trespasser, no longer a finding in a file, but a witness, a ferryman, a father, *hers*—and Ivo Thornbury stood on the roof of her own boat and let the river hear it and did not look away from a single face.

When she finished, the chanting had stopped. The whole reach was quiet, the flood running strong and brown under the hulls, carrying everything downstream the way it always had, except that today it had carried the truth upstream instead, against the current, against the law, all the way to Kessel Bend.

And that was when Ivo saw the smoke.

It rose from the green dome at the heart of the town—thin, then thick, then black—rising from the magistrate's court itself, and along the shore she saw figures running, and on the gunboat Halvard said, very softly, to no one and to everyone:

"He'll have a fire in every file cabinet in the district by noon. He's burning the archives." The big man turned to her across the closing water, and his face was grim as a lock gate. "Captain—everything that proves your father's case on *paper* is in that building. We've got the ledger, and we've got my order, and we've got the witness list. But the court records, the old survey filings, the dam commission minutes—" He looked at the smoke, and then at her, and finished it. "If we don't get there before his clerks do, thirty years of paper becomes ash, and your father's name rests on what we can carry in one boat."
