## 22. Landfall

The river came up in the night the way it always did in spring—not all at once, but with a patience that felt deliberate, like something old turning over in its sleep. Ivo heard it before she saw it: a low, muscular sound beneath the hull of the *Maren Lee*, the water pressing against the planks as if testing them for worthiness. She lay in her bunk and listened, and for the first time in longer than she could reckon, she was not afraid of what the river wanted.

She rose before dawn out of habit rather than need. The wheelhouse was cold enough to fog her breath, and she stood at the glass watching the last stars dissolve over the drowned country—over Aldermere, invisible now beneath thirty feet of brown spring water, its bell tower swallowed again as if the whole affair had been a rumor the river had decided to end. Somewhere down there, streets she had walked dry-shod in autumn lay dark and silted. Somewhere down there, a name carved into stone: *E. Voss*, and four others, cut by men who had known they were going to die and had wanted the water to remember.

The water remembered. That was the thing Ivo had learned, the lesson that had cost her a crewman, a pardon, and most of her illusions: the river kept everything. It only chose when to give it back.

Behind her, bare feet on the deck plates. Sena came into the wheelhouse carrying two tin mugs and the particular silence of someone who has been awake long enough to have thought everything through twice.

"You're up early," Ivo said.

"The river's up early." Sena handed her a mug. Coffee, black, bitter enough to strip paint—the way they'd drunk it that first dawn at the fuel stop, arguing about charts neither of them had fully trusted. "High water by noon, if the gauge at Kessel Bend is honest."

"Kessel Bend." Ivo said the name carefully, the way you touch a scar to check whether it still hurts. "Nothing from Kessel Bend is honest."

"Then we'll find out ourselves." Sena leaned against the rail beside her, shoulder almost touching Ivo's shoulder, not quite. They had crossed that last small distance weeks ago, in the aftermath of the crossing itself, when Ivo's hands were still bandaged and grief sat between them like a third passenger; but some distances, once crossed, you still liked to stand at the edge of and look down. "Letters are sorted. Forty-one. I counted twice."

Forty-one letters. Survivors' letters, written in the tower by lamplight while enforcers circled outside—letters to widows and sons and sisters scattered along three hundred miles of river, letters that had waited through winter in an oilskin packet under Ivo's bunk because delivering them meant putting names to faces, and faces to what Hale had done, and what Hale had done was still, officially, nothing at all.

Ivo drank her coffee and watched the light come up gray-gold on the water. Her hands had healed crooked. The burn scars across both palms pulled when she gripped the wheel, and the ship's doctor in Marlow's Landing had told her she was lucky to have kept the fingers. She didn't feel lucky. She felt like a woman who had paid exactly what things cost, no more and no less, and who was done pretending the ledger balanced any other way.

* * *

They cast off at first light with the current already strong under them, the *Maren Lee* riding high and light, her cargo hold nearly empty except for forty-one letters, a crate of engine parts, and one sealed box that rode amidships like a passenger nobody spoke about.

The box held copies. That was all anyone would say about it, and only in whispers. After the trial on the water—after the lock fair, after four hundred riverfolk had stood in their boats and listened while the ledger was read aloud, after Hale's own enforcer Brannic had turned on him with his testimony still dripping wet from the swim he'd barely survived—after all of that, the original ledger had gone into the keeping of the riverfolk themselves, distributed page by page among ferrymen and lockkeepers and widows until no magistrate's fire could gather it all in one place. But Halvorsen had made copies. And one copy, by agreement that had taken three nights of argument to reach, traveled with Ivo Thornbury, to be delivered wherever the story next needed telling.

"Where does a story need telling?" Sena had asked, the night they loaded it.

"Wherever somebody's still pretending," Ivo had said. "That should keep us busy."

Now the boat ran easy down the swollen channel, past willows standing waist-deep in floodwater, past fence lines that vanished mid-field, past the roofs of farmhouses where families watched them pass from second-story windows with the calm of people who had lived beside this river long enough to know that high water was not a disaster but a season. Smoke rose from chimneys. A dog swam a fence line and shook itself off on higher ground, unbothered. Spring on the river was the world remade, and everyone along it knew the terms.

At Tenmile Lock, the lockkeeper waved them through without toll. He was a broad, gray-bearded man named Ostrow whom Ivo had known slightly before her trial and who had looked through her afterward for six years like she was a window with nothing behind it. Now he leaned on his gate arm and grinned at her like she was a festival.

"Heard you're running letters," he called across the water.

"Forty-one of them. One's got your name on the outside of it somewhere, if my map-reader's honest."

"It's honest." Sena appeared at the rail with the oilskin packet already open, sorting. "Ostrow, Carew at the grist mill, and the Widow Ansel at the bend below. Three for this stretch."

Ostrow's grin faded into something older and quieter. "Ansel," he repeated. "Her man was on the first survey team."

"No," Sena said gently. "Her man was on the second. The first team never came back far enough to have a widow. Just a name on a tower."

Ivo watched the lockkeeper take that in—the way a man takes in weather that means work. He nodded slowly, once, and stepped back from the gate. "Bring her the letter yourselves," he said. "Don't send a boy. Some doors you knock on personal."

They did. That evening, moored below the bend where the willows grew thickest, they walked the letter up a muddy lane to a low house with a garden already drowning and a woman waiting on the porch as though she had been waiting for thirty years and had gotten very good at it.

Ivo let Sena hand it over. This was Sena's country, these griefs; Ivo carried her own, and knew better than to stand between a sister and a brother's last words. But she stood at the foot of the steps, hat in her burned hands, and she watched the widow read, and she watched the reading change the woman's face the way water changes stone—not breaking it, but showing what had been inside it all along.

When it was done, the widow folded the letter along its worn creases—Sena had copied it before delivery; the originals went home with the families—and looked at Ivo with eyes gone bright and level.

"They say you found the tower," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"And the names."

"Yes, ma'am. All five. Cut deep. The water hadn't touched them."

The widow nodded, and pressed the letter flat against her chest with both hands, and said, "Thirty years I was told he deserted. Thirty years they let me believe my own husband ran." Her voice did not rise. That was the terrible part of it, Ivo thought—the quietness, the way grief that old had burned down to coals you could hold in your bare hand. "You gave me back my husband's honor. Whatever else comes of it, captain—that debt is yours to spend."

Walking back down the lane in the blue dusk, Sena slipped her arm through Ivo's, careful of the bandages even now, even months on.

"That's the ninth one who's said that," Sena observed.

"I'm not counting."

"You're absolutely counting."

Ivo huffed something that was almost a laugh. "All right. Nine. What do I do with nine debts?"

"Same thing you do with everything," Sena said. "You run them downriver and see where they land."

* * *

They fell into the rhythm of the river the way you fall into sleep—gradually, then completely. Mornings, Ivo took the wheel while the mist still stood on the water in columns, and Sena worked her charts at the little table Ivo had built for her over the winter, updating the survey of the drowned reach with soundings they took whenever the mood struck, because the maps of Aldermere belonged to whoever might come looking someday, and Sena Voss did not intend that whoever to be another Magistrate Hale. Afternoons, they traded places, and Ivo learned the pleasure of sitting idle in a chair while someone else steered her boat well—a pleasure she had not known she was missing until it arrived. Evenings, they moored wherever the current suggested, cooked whatever the river or the bank offered, and read aloud from the growing stack of replies that came back upstream with every northbound barge: answers from the widows, from the sons, from the lockkeepers and ferrymen who had become, without ever holding a meeting, the custodians of the largest archive on the river.

Word traveled ahead of them, as word does on water. At Marlow's Landing, a crowd met them at the dock—not hostile, not anymore, but hungry. They wanted the story told whole, and Ivo had learned to tell it plainly: the dam, the drownings, the forged permits, the towns that had been living towns right up until the sluices opened, the professor who had traded truth for funding and spent his last months trying to buy it back, the magistrate who had framed a riverboat captain's father for witnessing what he'd helped bury, and the daughter who had spent six years wearing that frame like a coat sewn onto her skin.

"My record's sealed," she told them, the same thing she told everyone. "Not cleared. Sealed. There's a difference, and don't let anyone sell you the first when you're owed the second."

A young ferryman in the front row raised his hand like a schoolboy. "Why not take the pardon? Word is they offered it. Full restoration, in exchange for the location of the collection."

The dock went quiet. Sena, at the gangway with the manifest, went very still.

Ivo looked at the young man for a long moment. Then she looked out at the river, high and brown and patient, holding its city.

"Because some things belong to the drowned," she said. "And because a pardon bought with a location isn't a pardon—it's a price tag. I've been priced before. I'm done shopping." She picked up her hat. "The collection stays where it is. If the day comes when it can do its work in the open—when there's a court that wants the truth instead of a magistrate who wants leverage—it'll surface. Until then, the river keeps it. The river's kept worse secrets better."

She walked off the dock through a silence that broke, halfway up the street, into applause—not the polished kind, but the rough, stamped, whistled kind that comes off working boats and working hands, and which Ivo found she could bear only by pretending it was for someone else.

* * *

It was Sena who finally asked the question, three days later, in the wheelhouse, in the hour when the light goes long and gold and makes even wrecked country look kind.

"Where are we actually going?" She had the chart open, the one with the whole river drawn in her own fine hand, and her pencil hovering over the empty space below the last marked town. "We've delivered the letters. The trial's done, or as done as trials get on this river. Hale's in custody pending a hearing that everyone knows will go badly for him, because Brannic's testimony won't stay quiet and neither will four hundred witnesses. There's nothing left to run toward." She tapped the blank paper. "So. Where?"

Ivo stood at the wheel and considered it honestly, because Sena deserved honesty and because, for once, she had an answer that wasn't a deflection.

"When I was a girl," she said, "my father used to ask me that every time we cast off. Where to, Ivo? And I never had an answer, so I got in the habit of saying *downriver*, and he got in the habit of laughing, because downriver isn't a destination, it's a surrender. You let the current decide. He said a captain who lets the current decide is either a coward or a saint, and the river sorts them out eventually." She turned the wheel a few degrees, unnecessary, just to feel the boat answer. "I spent six years letting other people decide for me. The court decided. Hale decided. The creditors decided. Even running the narrows—I decided that, but I decided it against something. Everything I've done since the trial, I've done against something. Against Hale. Against the seal on my record. Against the lie."

"And now?"

"Now—" Ivo looked downriver, where the channel bent away through flooded timber toward country she had never bothered to chart because it had never mattered where she went, only that she kept moving. The compass on the binnacle swung lazily with the turn and settled, pointing south-southwest, steady as a heartbeat. "Now there's nothing left to run against. So I suppose I get to find out what it's like to run toward."

"Toward what?"

"Toward whatever's down there." She nodded at the blank edge of the chart. "Toward the towns that lost people and haven't heard yet. Toward the hearing, if they'll have us testify. Toward spring, and then summer, and then however many springs this old girl's got left in her hull." She put her hand flat on the wheel, over the scars, and found that they didn't pull today. "Toward the place where the story surfaces next. That's a heading. It's the only one I've ever really wanted."

Sena looked at her for a moment across the wheelhouse, in the gold light, with the chart forgotten under her hand. Then she rolled the map closed, tied it, and set it aside.

"South-southwest it is," she said. "I'll draw as we go. Somebody has to make sure the next person who comes looking finds more than we did."

* * *

They made ten miles that evening and moored in the lee of a bluff where the floodwater ran slow and deep, and Ivo climbed to the roof of the wheelhouse with the last of the coffee to watch the stars come out over the water. Below her, through the deck, she could hear Sena moving in the cabin, the small domestic sounds of lamp and kettle and chart weights, and the sound did what it always did now—loosened something in her chest that six years of running had cinched tight.

Her father had been dead eleven years. She had spent six of them believing he had died a criminal, and the rest of that time believing she'd die one too, and both beliefs had been lies laid down by the same man for the same purpose. The ledger had given her back her father's name. The river had given her back everything else. And the price—the drowned crewman whose face she still saw sometimes in the spray off the bow, the sealed record, the collection sleeping under thirty feet of spring water—she carried all of it openly now, the way you carry ballast: not gladly, but knowingly, because it was what kept her upright in a current.

Far downstream, past the last of the visible channel, a light blinked on the horizon. Another boat, or a town, or a farmhouse on high ground with someone awake in it. It didn't matter which. It was ahead of her, and it was lit, and for the first time in her life the direction she was pointed was the direction she had chosen.

Ivo finished the coffee, set the mug down, and put her hand on the compass housing, feeling the faint tremor of the needle through the brass, faithful as a pulse.

"Downriver," she said softly, to her father, to the river, to no one. "But this time it's not a surrender."

Below her, the cabin lamp went out, and Sena's voice came up through the deck boards, warm and unhurried: "Coming to bed, Captain? We've got current to catch at dawn."

Ivo looked once more at the light on the horizon, and the stars above it, and the black water carrying her toward all of it.

"Yeah," she said, and went down the ladder into the lamplit dark, into the life she had finally stopped running from. "Yeah. Let it decide."

And the river, which keeps everything, kept that too.
