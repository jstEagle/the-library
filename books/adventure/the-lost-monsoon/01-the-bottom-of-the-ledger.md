## 1. The Bottom of the Ledger

The wreck of the *Cormorant's Pride* sat in forty feet of green-black water off the breakwater at Sallow Point, and I was the only person alive who wanted anything to do with her. That wasn't romance. That was arithmetic.

I'd been down eleven minutes when my gauge told me the truth about my morning: 1,400 pounds of air left, and a hull to strip before the tide turned and the current through the channel went from rude to murderous. The creditor who owned this dive — a soft-handed man named Pell with a ledger instead of a soul — had promised me forty crowns for the brass fittings and whatever copper pipe I could cut free. Forty crowns was two weeks of food, half a month's mooring fees on my boat, and one-third of what I owed the chandlery for the tank of air currently hissing against my back.

That last number is the one that should tell you everything about my life. I was borrowing breath to earn money to pay for the breath I'd already borrowed. Somewhere below me, the drowned don't complain about their arrangements, but I suspected they'd have found mine funny.

I hooked my pry bar under a brass porthole frame and leaned into it. The *Pride* had been condemned eleven years ago after she burned at anchor — no dead aboard, thank God, just a hull full of char and silt and enough salvageable metal to keep a diver in debt another season. Her superstructure had collapsed into a lean-to of blackened steel, and the whole wreck groaned when the current pushed it, a long low complaint like an old man shifting in his sleep. You learned not to take it personally.

*Three more frames,* I told myself. *Then the pipe aft. Then up.*

I worked by feel mostly. The water at Sallow Point carried so much suspended grit that my torch was less a light than a rumor of light, a brown-gold glow that died two feet from its source. My gloves were worn through at the fingertips, which meant I could feel every edge and also meant that someday soon I'd cut a finger to the bone and have nobody to notice but the crabs. Diving solo is illegal in most charter jurisdictions. It's also the only kind of diving I could afford, and the only kind, if I'm honest, that suited me. A buddy underwater is a witness, and witnesses ask questions, and questions are how you end up explaining your life to someone over coffee and watching their face do the slow arithmetic of pity.

My father taught me to dive. Not the way fathers in stories teach — patiently, on sunny mornings, with praise — but the way a man teaches when he believes the sea will eventually come collecting and wants his daughter to be able to argue her case. He put me under at eight years old in a flooded quarry with a mask too big for my face and told me, when I surfaced crying, that fear was fine but panic was a choice. I've been making that choice daily ever since, usually correctly.

He vanished when I was seventeen. Boat found empty and adrift off the Teeth, compass still lashed to the binnacle, no body, no note, no wreckage worth the name. The harbor office wrote *lost at sea* in a hand so bored it might have been filling out a grain tariff. Everyone else moved on inside a year. Grief, I've learned, has a shelf life for everyone except the person carrying it.

The compass came home to me eventually — salvaged by a scrap dealer who recognized the name Nystrom stenciled on the old chart case and sold it back to me at a markup he probably considered sentimental. It sits now in a wooden box under my bunk, brass case gone green at the seams, glass cracked across the northeast quadrant. The needle still swings true. I check it sometimes the way other people visit graves.

I got the second porthole frame free and bagged it. My gauge said 1,100. The current had started its afternoon argument with the channel, tugging at my lines, plucking at my harness like a child demanding attention. Time to move aft.

Here is the arithmetic of my life as of that Tuesday, and I keep it the way misers keep coin:

Owed to Pell, principal and interest, four hundred sixty crowns, due in full by season's end or he took the boat. Owed to the chandlery, ninety crowns, rolling, because air tanks and line and lantern oil don't stop being necessary just because you're poor. Owed to Marta at the dockside canteen, twelve crowns and an apology, because she fed me through the storm week on credit and I'd been avoiding her eyes ever since. Owed to the harbormaster, thirty-one crowns in back mooring fees. And owed — though no ledger held this line — to a father who'd taught me everything except how to stop diving wrecks that should have been left alone.

Against that: forty crowns from the *Pride*, maybe sixty if the copper ran long. A boat worth perhaps three hundred, if anyone would buy a twenty-year-old salvage ketch with a bad winch and worse luck. And myself, which the market priced at roughly nothing, since a woman with debts and no family and a reputation for asking inconvenient questions can be rented cheap and discarded cheaper.

Total position: drowning, slowly, at a rate of about five crowns a week.

People think drowning is dramatic. It isn't. It's bookkeeping.

* * *

The pipe aft was good — six feet of uncorroded copper running along what used to be the galley bulkhead, and I was halfway through cutting it when I saw the thing that ruined my tidy morning.

Not treasure. Nothing so kind. A boot.

It protruded from the silt drift under the collapsed companionway, leather gone stiff and gray, and attached to the boot was a leg, and attached to the leg was a man in a rotted diving suit with a brass helmet I recognized because I'd priced one just like it at auction two years ago and couldn't afford it. Old-school hardhat rig, surface-supplied, the kind of gear that went out of fashion before I was born. His air hose ran up into the dark toward the broken deck above and simply ended, severed, trailing like a cut umbilical.

I hung there in the brown water and looked at him for a long time. Long enough that my gauge dropped fifty pounds without my noticing.

Divers find bodies. It comes with the trade; we go where ships go wrong, and ships go wrong with people aboard. But those are modern dead, usually — fishermen, ferry passengers, the occasional smuggler whose cargo weighed more than his judgment. This man had been down here decades. His suit had fused with the silt. Something had been building a home in his chest cavity, and I decided firmly not to identify what.

The strange part — the part I turned over all the way up and all the way home — was the tag. Hardhat divers wore brass tags on their harnesses, stamped with their name or their company, so the tender topside could log them down and bring them back up. His tag was polished clean. Bright. Someone had been down here recently enough to clean it, or the current kept it scoured, or —

Or someone visited him. Regularly.

I know what you're thinking, and yes, I thought it too: leave it alone, August. Cut your pipe, collect your forty crowns, go home. There is a version of me that does exactly that, and that version sleeps better and dies poorer.

But the tag had letters stamped into it, and my gloves were worn through at the fingertips, and I reached out and brushed the silt away with my thumb before the sensible part of me finished filing its objection.

*N. VESSANIA SALVAGE CO.*

Two things about that. First: Vessania. There is no Vessania. There hadn't been a Vessania since before my grandmother was born — a nation swallowed by border wars and then by maps, erased so thoroughly that schoolchildren learned the word only as a geography riddle. Second: the stamp was fresh. Not decades-fresh. *Seasons*-fresh. Somebody had cut that tag recently, or re-stamped it, or —

My gauge said 700. The current had stopped tugging and started hauling, and somewhere above me the tide was turning with the indifference of all large indifferent things. I wrapped the tag once around my wrist — I'll tell myself forever that I meant to show it to the harbormaster, report it, do the lawful thing — and cut my pipe and kicked for the surface with a stranger's name riding my arm like a bracelet.

* * *

Topside, Pell was waiting on the charter skiff with his ledger open and his mouth already going, because Pell's mouth and his ledger were the same organ.

"Forty minutes," he said. "You were down forty minutes. I bill wait time, Nystrom."

"You bill wait time to yourself," I said, hauling the bag of fittings over the gunwale. "Nobody else is stupid enough to pay it."

He weighed the bag with a fish scale and pronounced a number that was somehow nine crowns less than the weight justified, and I let him, because arguing with Pell cost more than nine crowns in lost daylight. While he counted, I dried the brass tag on my shirt hem and looked at it properly for the first time in real light.

Up close it was worse. Better. Worse-better. The stamping was machine-cut, crisp, done on equipment nobody had manufactured in eighty years — unless somebody had gone to the trouble of restoring a period press, which raised the question of why anybody would, and the further question of what else they'd restored. And stamped beneath the company name, smaller, almost an afterthought: a serial number. *VS-114.*

I don't know why that number itched. Numbers itch me sometimes; it's the one inheritance from my father I'd have declined. He used to say figures were honest the way weather is honest — they don't care whether you believe them. He'd run salvage charts for the harbor office before he went independent, and he had a habit of tapping numbers twice when he didn't trust them, like he was checking them for a pulse.

I tapped VS-114 twice on the skiff rail while Pell counted out my forty crowns minus nine.

"Something on your mind?" Pell asked, with the flat suspicion of a man who assumed every silence was costing him.

"Just admiring the view."

"The view is a condemned wreck and a woman in debt."

"One of us is having a good day, then."

He almost smiled. Credit where due; even Pell had a sense of humor, buried deep, like ore under worthless rock. He shoved off, and I stood on my own deck — the *Kestrel*, twenty years old, bad winch, worse luck, mine until season's end and Pell's after that — and watched the breakwater lights come on as the light went off the water.

* * *

That night I did what I always do with problems too big to solve: I made tea and laid them out on the chart table like specimens.

The tea was the cheap kind that tastes like hot regret. The chart table held, in order: my account book, open to the page I've privately titled *The Bottom of the Ledger* because that's what it is — the floor beneath which there is no further floor; the brass tag; and my father's compass, brought up from its box because some part of my brain had connected *Vessania* to something and wouldn't say what.

I read the account book first, because misery prefers to be thorough. Four hundred sixty, ninety, twelve, thirty-one. Against forty a week if the work held, and the work never held. Season's end was fourteen weeks away. Even if I stripped a wreck every week — even if my body held, even if the weather held, even if Pell didn't find new fees to invent — I'd land short. Not dramatically short. Just short enough. That's how debt works on people like me: not the axe, but the tide. You never see the moment it takes you. You just notice, one evening, that the water is at your chest.

I closed the book. Then I picked up the compass.

It fit my palm the way it had fit his — he'd had bigger hands, but the case was worn to a hollow that seemed to accept any Nystrom. Brass, green at the seams, glass cracked northeast. On the back, engraved so faintly you needed raking light to read it, was a mark he'd never explained: a small circle crossed by three lines, like a wheel seen edge-on, or a trident lying down. I'd spent years assuming it was a maker's mark, some defunct instrument house.

Under the chart lamp, with the brass tag lying beside it, I noticed for the first time that the engraving matched the stamp on the tag. Not the words — the *style*. The same hand. The same press, maybe. The same era of deliberate, official, institutional certainty.

A nation that no longer existed had stamped a diver's tag on a wreck that shouldn't have been dived, and my father's compass wore its signature.

I want to tell you I felt excitement. What I actually felt was colder and older: the specific dread of a woman who has just heard her own name called from an empty room. Because here's the thing about my father's disappearance that never made it into the harbor office's bored little file — the thing I've never said aloud, not to Marta, not to anyone. The week before he vanished, he came home late with river mud on his boots. We lived on the coast. There is no river mud within a day's sail.

He'd looked at me across the galley table — I remember the lamp between us, and his hands around a cup he never drank from — and he'd asked me a strange question. He'd asked if I knew what happened to countries when they lost a war. Not the men. The *countries*. Where did the flags go, the ledgers, the treasuries, the debts? Who collected?

I was seventeen. I said something clever and dismissive, because seventeen is fluent in dismissive. He'd smiled like I'd passed a test I didn't know I was taking, and kissed the top of my head, and gone to bed, and eleven days later the sea handed back his boat empty.

I set the compass down. My tea had gone cold, which saved me from drinking it.

Outside, the harbor slapped at the pilings, and the *Kestrel* ticked and settled around me the way old boats do, talking in their sleep. Fourteen weeks. Four hundred sixty crowns. One brass tag with a dead man's serial number and a dead nation's name. One compass pointing, as far as I could tell, at nothing.

I should have thrown the tag in the harbor. I've replayed that night a hundred times, and every version where I'm smart ends with the tag going over the rail, splash, done, back to the arithmetic, back to Pell and the tide and the slow drowning that at least has the decency to be familiar.

Instead I wrapped it in oilcloth, put it in the box under my bunk next to the compass, and blew out the lamp.

And that — I'd learn soon enough, sooner than any sane person would wish — was the exact moment my quiet life ended. Because the man who'd cleaned that tag, whoever he was, had been coming back for it.
