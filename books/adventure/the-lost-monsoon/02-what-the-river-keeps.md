## 2. What the River Keeps

The river at dawn looks like something that's already made up its mind about you.

I was in it by six, because the tide tables said six and the tide tables are the only authority I've ever trusted without reservation. The Kessarin doesn't care about your feelings. It cares about weight and current and how long you can hold your breath before it decides to keep you. Every diver I've known who talked about the river like a friend is dead. Every one who talked about it like a creditor — *here's what you owe me, here's when I want it* — is still breathing. Mostly.

My name is August Nystrom, and I owed the river nothing yet that morning. That was about to change.

The job was simple, which should have been my first warning. Salvage work is never simple; "simple" is the word creditors use when they mean *cheap*. Halvard & Sons had a condemned paddle-steamer rotting on a restricted stretch of the lower channel — the *Coriander*, forty years of honest freight service followed by ten years of dishonest neglect — and they wanted her hull cleared of anything with resale value before the demolition crews came through and turned her into scrap and paperwork. My cut: enough to cover two months of interest on my tank rental, my berth fees, and the small personal loan I'd taken out against a boat I didn't technically own anymore.

The arithmetic of my life fits on an index card. It has to. Anything bigger and I'd have to look at it straight-on, and nobody looks at their own drowning straight-on if they can help it.

* * *

Diving the *Coriander* was like exploring the corpse of something that had died of embarrassment. Her paddles hung off her flanks like broken wings. Silt had claimed everything below the main deck, soft and gray and patient, the way silt always is. I worked my way along the cargo hold with a hand light cutting cones through water the color of old tea, prying loose brass fittings, copper pipe, anything that would weigh enough on a scale to matter.

Here's what nobody tells you about salvage diving: ninety percent of it is inventory. You spend your time underwater doing math. *Brass fitting, maybe four marks. Copper length, six. Tank pressure, down to half.* You count your debts in air. I've done more accounting at forty feet than most clerks do in a career.

My father taught me the other kind of counting — the kind where you find something nobody else saw. Elias Nystrom could read a wreck like other men read weather. He used to say the river keeps everything, it just files things badly. *You don't search a wreck, August. You ask it questions until it answers wrong.*

He'd been missing eleven years. Officially drowned, unofficially never found, and the space between those two words is where I've lived most of my adult life. All he left me was a brass compass with his initials scratched inside the case and a needle that pointed true north with a stubbornness I've come to think of as genetic. I keep it in my dive bag's inner pocket, which is either sentiment or superstition, and I've stopped asking myself which.

By mid-morning I'd stripped the hold clean. Two hundred marks of metal in the lift bag, maybe three hundred once Halvard's scale-man finished shaving his percentage off. Not enough. Not close. And the tide was turning, which meant my window was closing, which meant I had a choice: surface with what I had, or push into the restricted stretch upstream for one more sweep.

The restricted stretch is restricted because of a collapsed rail bridge, some bureaucratic fiction about structural hazard zones, and the fact that whatever sinks there tends to stay sunk and unexamined. Divers who work it without permits lose their licenses. Divers who work it without permits and get caught also tend to have very short conversations with men from the Harbor Authority.

I did the math on the index card in my head. Then I swam upstream anyway.

* * *

The bridge wreckage came out of the murk like the ribs of something enormous that had knelt down to drink and never gotten back up. Twisted iron trusses, concrete pylons furred with weed, and between them a graveyard of small history: a rowboat, a cart axle, a child's bicycle fused into rust and riverstone. The river keeps everything. It just files badly.

I swept the shadow line under the third pylon, where the current eddies and drops whatever it's tired of carrying. That's where the chest was.

Not floating free — wedged, half-buried, tucked into the collapse like something deliberately hidden rather than accidentally lost. Iron-bound oak, straps gone to lace but the wood sound underneath, and on the lid, stamped deep enough to survive decades of silt, a seal I didn't recognize and then, all at once, did.

A crowned wave over a key.

Vessania.

You learn the dead nations young if you grow up around harbors, the way you learn dead languages from gravestones. Vessania had been a coastal state two hundred miles south, annexed, dissolved, and scrubbed from the atlases within a single generation — its ports reassigned, its archives dispersed, its name kept alive mainly by historians and people selling forged antiques. A drowned nation, people called it, though the country itself stood dry as bone. Only its treasury had ever actually gone under, somewhere in these waters, according to stories divers told each other after the third drink. Stories. The kind you file next to mermaids and honest salvage brokers.

Except here was the seal, under my hand, real as rust.

I should have surfaced. I should have marked the location, reported the find per the salvage code, let the lawyers and the Harbor Authority and whoever-claims-what descend on it like gulls. That's the procedure. Procedure is what keeps people like me from disappearing.

Instead I stayed down there for another ninety seconds, breathing borrowed air, running my thumb over the crowned wave, and asked the wreck a question. *Who put you here?*

It answered wrong. Wrecks always do. But the answer it gave was this: the chest hadn't fallen. It had been placed. The silt scoured away from its leading edge in a pattern no current makes — deliberate scouring, a channel dug by hands, a hiding spot engineered by someone who understood this river the way I understand it. Which is to say: someone who counted tides.

My gauge read a quarter tank. I got the lift bag on the chest, walked it up the slope with my legs burning, and broke the surface into gray daylight with my heart going like a bilge pump.

* * *

I didn't open it on the water. Rule one of finding things: never open them where anyone can see you do it. I ran the skiff back to my berth with the chest under a tarp and my pulse under only slightly better control, tied off, checked the dock both ways the way you check a street you've been robbed on, and carried it below decks of the *Magpie* — my boat, or the bank's boat that I lived on and pretended was mine.

Inside, the lock had long since surrendered to the river. The lid came up with a groan and a breath of trapped smell — old iron, wet cedar, time.

Ledgers. Dozens of them, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, and beneath them a strongbox tray of documents in wax-sealed folios, and beneath *that*, at the bottom, a false panel that even I almost missed, holding a single slim volume apart from the rest.

I'm not proud of what I did next. I'm not ashamed of it either. Both of those feelings are luxuries, and I've never been able to afford them.

I took the slim volume, wrapped it in my shirt, and stowed it in the compartment under my bunk where I keep the things that would ruin me if found: the compass, my father's last letter, and now this. Then I resealed the chest, reported the find to Halvard & Sons exactly per procedure — *iron-bound chest, contents unknown, presumed commercial records, recovered from restricted stretch adjacent to the Coriander site* — and watched from my deck as their men hauled it away, already arguing about whose signature went on whose line.

That was the first lie. Not the pocketing — the pocketing was just theft, and theft is honest work compared to lying. The lie was standing in front of Halvard's clerk with my face arranged into bored professionalism while my whole body screamed *mine*. The lie was saying *contents unknown* when I knew exactly what the contents were worth, or at least suspected, in the way you suspect a storm coming when your teeth ache.

That night, moored in the dark with a lamp turned low, I finally opened the slim volume.

It was a ledger. Not a merchant's ledger — the handwriting was too fine, the columns too strange. Figures in a script I didn't know, Vessian script, dense and slanted like rain on glass. But numbers are numbers, and I've spent my life reading other people's accounts, so I did what I do. I counted.

Weights. Repeated weights, in patterns. Tonnages that made no sense for cargo and perfect sense for coin. Dates spanning years. And names — a column of names, each with figures beside them, each name written in that same beautiful foreign hand except one.

One entry near the end was in different ink. Newer. The letters cramped, hurried, written by someone cold or afraid or both.

I couldn't read the name. The script defeated me. But the date beside it I could read, because dates don't change languages, and the date was the year my father vanished.

I sat in the lamplight for a long time after that, listening to the water slap the hull, feeling the river's patience reach up through the pilings into the bones of my boat. Somewhere downstream, Halvard's men were cataloguing a dead nation's secrets without knowing what they held. Somewhere south, a country that no longer existed was keeping its books balanced in a chest at the bottom of a channel. And somewhere in the arithmetic of my life — on the index card, in the interest schedules, in the column of debts I'd been carrying since I was nineteen — a new figure had just entered that changed every sum above it.

The compass sat in its compartment an arm's length away. I took it out. The needle swung, settled, pointed north with its usual stubbornness, and I swear — I *swear* — it felt less like a tool that night and more like a question.

*What did you do, Papa? And what does the river still have of yours?*

I didn't sleep. Around three, a navy launch went past my berth, slow and unlit, searchlight playing along the docks like a finger running down a list of names. It paused — I'd say imagined it, except I don't believe in coincidence anymore, not since the ledger — it paused across from the *Magpie* for the length of four heartbeats. Then it moved on.

In the morning there was a man in a naval uniform waiting at the end of my pier, and he was asking the harbor master questions, and the harbor master was pointing.

At me.
