## 3. Six Tides

The navy came at low tide, which told me everything I needed to know about how seriously they took me. Serious people arrive when they can't be ignored. The navy arrived when my boat was sitting in the mud like a beached whale, keel exposed, rudder useless, nowhere to go even if I'd had the sense to run.

I was on deck scraping barnacles off the hull with a putty knife when their launch rounded the breakwater. Two of them, actually — a grey patrol cutter hanging back at the channel mouth like a chaperone, and a small rigid-hulled boat cutting across the flats toward me, throwing a bow wave that was frankly showing off in six inches of water.

Three sailors and one officer. The officer stepped onto my deck without asking permission, which is a thing only two kinds of people do: drunks and naval intelligence. He was neither drunk nor apologetic about it.

"August Nystrom." He said it like a fact he'd already verified, not a question. Mid-forties, maybe. Grey coming in at the temples like frost on a railing. A face that had been arranged for patience but kept slipping into something sharper underneath. His uniform was immaculate in that way that isn't vanity — it's a message. *I have never once been caught unprepared.*

"Depends who's asking," I said, and kept scraping barnacles. It's hard to look dangerous with a putty knife. That's exactly why I held onto it.

"Commander Corvane. Naval Salvage Directorate." He produced a leather folder and didn't open it. Just held it, the way a card player holds a hand he likes. "You're licensed for restricted-zone survey work."

"I'm licensed for a lot of things."

"You're also," he went on, mild as milk, "eleven thousand crowns in arrears to Meridian Credit, with a lien against this vessel, three outstanding citations for diving without a spotter, and a disciplinary flag from the Harbor Authority dated last spring." He finally opened the folder and turned it so I could see my own life printed out in bureaucratic typeface. "You've been busy, Miss Nystrom. Just not profitably."

The barnacle came loose under my knife with a wet crack. I thought about all the ways this conversation could go, and none of them ended with me keeping the chest hidden under my bunk where it currently sat, wrapped in oilcloth, humming its secret through the floorboards like a bad conscience.

"What does the Salvage Directorate want with my credit history?" I asked. "Thinking of lending me money? Because your interest rates would have to beat Meridian's, and those are criminal."

Something flickered across his face. Not quite amusement. Recognition, maybe — the look of a man cataloguing a specimen that was behaving unexpectedly. "We're conducting a recovery operation in the Serrat Channel. Restricted waters. There was an incident there recently."

"Was there?"

"A diver was seen." He let that hang. "Unlicensed. Alone. In a zone closed to traffic since before you were born."

Here's what nobody tells you about lying to a naval officer: they don't catch you by what you say. They catch you by watching what you don't say. So I gave him nothing to measure. I shrugged, wiped my hands on my rag, and leaned against the rail like a woman with nowhere better to be, which happened to be true.

"Lots of divers out there," I said. "River's full of them. Half the harbor dives condemned wrecks for scrap. You going to interview all of us, or just the ones with debts?"

"Just the ones," Corvane said, "who pulled something out."

* * *

He invited himself below decks. Of course he did. I stood aside because standing aside was the only move that didn't look like a move, and watched him duck through my hatch into a cabin I suddenly saw through his eyes: a kettle crusted with scale, a chart table buried under unpaid invoices, a hammock that hadn't been strung straight since the day I bought this boat with money I didn't have.

And under the bunk, wrapped in oilcloth, a sealed chest stamped with the crest of a country that hadn't existed in sixty years.

Corvane looked at everything except the bunk. That was the tell. Innocent men look everywhere; guilty ones too; but a man conducting a search pretends not to search, and Commander Corvane pretended very well indeed. His gaze snagged on the chart table, on the compass lashed beside it — my father's compass, brass worn to gold at the edges — and stayed there half a second longer than it should have.

"Family piece?" he asked.

"It was my father's."

"Elias Nystrom." He said the name gently, the way you'd set down something fragile. "Diver. Lost with the *Merrow*, twenty-two years ago. Body never recovered."

The cabin got very quiet. Somewhere above us, a gull screamed like it was being paid to.

"You've done your homework," I managed.

"It's my job." He turned from the compass and faced me, and for a moment the patience slipped entirely and I saw the thing underneath: a tired man doing arithmetic he hated. "Miss Nystrom, I'm going to speak plainly, because I think you're smart enough to know when someone's speaking plainly. Sixty years ago, a nation called Vessania existed on these charts. It doesn't anymore. Its government fell, its records were burned, its fleet was scuttled, and its treasury—" he paused, "—was lost. Officially. Unofficially, everyone from smugglers to salvage syndicates to three separate governments has spent six decades looking for it. Last week, a diver entered restricted waters and found artifacts bearing the Vessian seal. I'm here because whatever she found has started a clock."

"I wouldn't know anything about that."

"No," he agreed. "You wouldn't." And then, quietly, almost kindly: "Miss Nystrom, the questions I'm asking are questions no salvage diver survives answering wrong. Not because of me. Because of who comes after me. Do you understand the difference?"

I understood. I'd grown up understanding. My father used to say the sea keeps what it takes, but men take what the sea keeps, and they don't leave witnesses. I nodded anyway, slow, letting him read caution instead of guilt.

"Six tides," Corvane said. "That's how long until the Directorate's recovery fleet reaches the site. Six tides until every syndicate gunboat in the archipelago converges on the same coordinates, because word travels fast in the gray market and faster among thieves. Six tides, Miss Nystrom, in which a smart diver with nothing left to lose could get very far away. Or very dead." He closed the folder. "I'd prefer you were neither. Consider that a professional courtesy."

"Is that what this is? Courtesy?"

"This is me telling you the tide tables are public knowledge." He moved to the hatch, then stopped, one hand on the frame, and didn't turn around. "Your father was a good diver. Better than good. The *Merrow*'s loss wasn't weather." Another pause, weighted like a lead boot. "Think about what survives a wreck, Miss Nystrom. And what doesn't."

Then he was gone, down my ladder, across my deck, into his boat, and the patrol cutter at the channel mouth turned away like a dog called off, unhurried, certain it would see me again.

* * *

I sat below decks for a long time after they left, listening to the mud suck at my hull as the tide began to come back. The chest sat under the bunk where I'd left it, patient as a debt.

Six tides. I did the math the way I do all my math now — in air tanks and borrowed time. Twelve hours per tide if I counted slack water generously. Three days. In three days, a naval fleet would crawl over the wreck site with sonar and grapples and forty men who got paid whether they found anything or not. In three days, the smugglers who sold me the tip in the first place — the ones whose "routine sweep" coordinates had felt wrong from the start, whose generosity had the specific smell of bait — would realize their fish had swallowed the hook and start asking why. And somewhere between those two forces, there was me, eleven thousand crowns underwater, holding a ledger I couldn't read and a document I'd stolen from a drowned nation.

I should have reported the find. That was the law, and more than the law, it was the sensible thing. Hand over the chest, collect whatever finder's fraction the Directorate paid — probably enough to clear Meridian, maybe enough to keep the boat — and go back to scraping barnacles and counting air tanks. Safe. Legal. Slowly poor forever, but alive.

Instead I pulled the document back out from under the bunk and spread it on the chart table next to my father's compass.

It was a page of the ledger, heavy paper gone soft as cloth from sixty years underwater. The Vessian script crawled across it in columns — numbers, mostly, and I couldn't read a word of it. But ledgers are honest in any language. Columns of figures. Dates. And at the bottom of the page, in a different hand, cramped and hurried, a line of characters that repeated twice, like a man signing something twice to make sure it took.

I held the compass over the page. The needle swung, settled, pointed — not north. It pointed at the page. At the handwriting. At that cramped signature.

Compasses don't point at ink. Except mine had always been a little wrong. My father's compass had never found north reliably in its life; I'd assumed the magnetism was shot, some old damage from a life spent around engines and iron hulls. Every chart I'd ever plotted with it needed correcting by a few degrees, always the same few degrees, always the same stubborn pull toward open water east of the Serrat Channel.

I'd thought it was broken.

Standing in my cabin with a dead nation's ledger under my lamp, I realized it might be the only honest instrument I owned.

* * *

The tide came up around the hull and the boat woke, rocking, groaning, remembering it was a boat. I climbed topside and looked east, toward the Serrat Channel and the restricted water beyond it, where a wreck older than my grief sat holding a treasury everyone wanted and no one had found.

Six tides. The first would flood tonight.

I thought about Corvane's face when he'd said my father's name. He knew something about the *Merrow*. Knew the loss wasn't weather. Which meant the navy had files, and files meant names, and names meant that somewhere in a drawer in the Directorate there was a page with Elias Nystrom written on it — connected, somehow, to a sunken treasury and a compass that couldn't find north.

My father taught me to dive. Taught me to read water, to count my bottom time like a miser counts coins, to trust the gauge over the gut and the gut over anyone else. He'd been dead twenty-two years, and he was still teaching me things. Right now the lesson was: *when everyone converges on the same point, the only winning move is to be there first, or to not be there at all.*

I couldn't afford not to be there first. The finder's fraction, the salvage rights, the ledger itself — any of it might clear my debts. All of it might explain my father. And if the navy reached it first, I'd get nothing but questions, and if the syndicates reached it first, I'd get nothing at all, because dead women keep no salvage rights.

There was a third option, of course. Walk away. Sell the document to Odile Marchetti, the fence who bought anything and asked nothing, take the money, disappear upriver. I'd survive. Survival was the one skill I'd truly mastered.

But survival wasn't living, and I was so tired of the difference.

Below decks, the chest waited. Above, the first stars came out over the channel, and beneath them, invisible, the tide began its long slow climb toward the wreck.

One tide down. Five to go.

I went below, pulled out a chart, and started plotting a course I had no crew, no money, and no right to sail.
