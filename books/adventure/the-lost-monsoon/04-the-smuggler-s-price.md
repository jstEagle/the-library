## 4. The Smuggler's Price

Odile Marchetti kept shop above a chandlery that smelled of tar and old lies, which is to say it smelled like every honest business in Port Sallow. You reached her by climbing a staircase so narrow you had to choose between your shoulders and your dignity, and I'd long since stopped bringing the dignity.

I stood at the top of those stairs with a stolen page folded into my jacket and told myself the walk over had been casual. It hadn't been. I'd taken three different routes, doubled back twice, and once stepped into a doorway to let a pair of navy shore patrol pass, my heart hammering like a bilge pump running dry. Corvane's questions were still lodged in me like shrapnel. *Where exactly did you recover the chest? Did anything else come up with it? Anything paper?*

Anything paper. He'd asked it too gently, the way you ask someone if they've seen a dog you've already shot.

So here I was, doing the one thing a smart diver never does: going deeper instead of coming up.

* * *

The door at the top of the stairs was painted a green that had given up sometime in the last decade. I knocked the pattern — two, pause, three — because Odile changed the pattern every season and Wren-the-dock-boy network had told me this season's before I even knew I'd need it. That's the thing about Port Sallow. Everyone sells everything, including information about where to sell things.

"Enter," said a voice like a knife being drawn slowly from silk.

Inside was not what first-timers expect. No smoke, no shadowed corners, no muscle by the door. Odile Marchetti ran her operation out of what looked like a retired schoolteacher's parlor: good light through clean glass, a tea service for two, shelves of ledgers bound in leather gone soft as earlobes. She sat behind a desk that had probably been looted from an admiral's cabin, small and silver-haired and neat as a pin cushion, mending a glove.

People always expected a fence to be a man with rings. Odile was a woman with spectacles on a chain and hands that could tell a forged bill of lading by touch in the dark. The rings would have been a liability. Everything about her was designed to make you underestimate her right up until she owned you.

"August Nystrom." She didn't look up from the glove. "You're early."

"I'm on time."

"You're early for someone who hasn't decided yet whether to show me what's in that jacket." Now she looked up, and her eyes did the thing they do — went warm and sharp at the same time, like sunlight off a blade. "Sit. Tea?"

"I can't stay long."

"Nobody who says that ever stays short." She poured anyway, two cups, because refusing her tea required more energy than I had. "Now. You look like a woman carrying something heavier than salvage. Which is interesting, because the harbor talk says you pulled up a chest stamped with a country that hasn't existed since before you were born, and that the navy has been asking questions with a very particular flavor of politeness."

Word travels fast in Port Sallow. Word travels fast everywhere; it just travels fastest where people are paid to carry it.

"The chest went to the harbormaster's bond store," I said. "Per regulation."

"Mm." She set down the glove. "And the regulation of August Nystrom? What came up under that one?"

I should have lied. I want that on the record, wherever records go when drowned women drown them. I should have fed her some story about brass fittings and sold her the fittings and walked out clean. But Odile Marchetti had been buying and selling the truth of this coast for thirty years, and there is no lie you can bring her that she won't recognize as secondhand.

I took out the page and put it on the desk.

It was the document I'd pocketed from the chest — the one I'd told myself was insurance, or curiosity, or nothing at all, depending on which hour of the night you asked me. Heavy paper, water-stained but sound, written in a tight columnar hand in a script I didn't read, with columns of figures in a numeration I didn't recognize. At its head, pressed in faded wax-and-foil, the seal of Vessania: the triple wave beneath a broken crown.

Odile didn't touch it at first. She looked at it the way you look at a snake you thought was extinct.

Then she put on her spectacles, and picked it up, and the room got very quiet — quieter than a room with harbor noise outside has any right to get. Her thumb moved along the columns. Her lips moved slightly, counting. And I watched thirty years of professional composure develop a hairline crack straight down the middle.

"Where," she said, without looking up, "did you get this."

"You know where."

"I know where the *chest* came from. I'm asking how *this* survived." Her voice had changed register entirely — all the silk drawn back off the knife. "This is a Vessian treasury ledger. Disbursement record. Do you understand what you pulled out of that river?"

"A ledger."

"A ledger of a nation's *money*, girl. Not taxes. Not tariffs. The reserve. The gold Vessania moved out ahead of the annexation and then lost, because the convoy carrying the final transfer went down in the Saltreach and everyone who knew the coordinates died in the war or shortly after it, in ways I will not describe over tea." She set the page down with exaggerated care, as if it might detonate. "There have been syndicates, governments, and at least two royal pretenders looking for this book for twenty-five years. People have been killed over rumors of rumors of this book."

I drank my tea. It was very good. My hands were steady, which surprised me.

"So it's valuable."

"It is a loaded weapon lying on the seabed with the safety off." She took off the spectacles and polished them, which I would learn was what she did instead of wringing her hands. "And you brought it to *me*. Why?"

Because you're the only fence in Port Sallow with the reach to move it. Because every other buyer would sell me to the navy inside the hour. Because you have a reputation for keeping your partners alive, which in your line of work is the same as a reputation for honesty.

"Because I need it appraised," I said, "and I need it quiet, and I hear you're the best at both."

"And what do you imagine it's worth?"

"Enough to clear my debts."

She laughed. It wasn't unkind, which somehow made it worse. "August. This page is worth enough to buy the harbor master, the admiralty board, and a small island with a temperate climate. If we're talking about the whole ledger — and this is one leaf, one leaf, of a multi-volume disbursement record — then we are talking about the largest unrecovered sovereign fortune on this coast. Enough money to fund a war. Or prevent one. Depending on whose hands—" She stopped herself. I watched her decide to say the next part anyway. "Depending on whose hands it lands in."

"Yours, presumably."

"My hands are old and full." She stood, went to the window, looked down at the street where ordinary people sold rope and paint and fish with no idea the century had teeth. When she turned around, she'd rebuilt the composure, but I'd seen the crack, and she knew I'd seen it, and that changed the arithmetic between us permanently. "Here is what happens now, and you may choose between two doors. Door one: I report that you came to me with Vessian material. Certain parties will take it from you. You will be compensated modestly, briefly, and posthumously."

"That's not a door, that's a wall."

"All doors are walls until you're through them. Door two." She sat back down and folded her hands. "Partnership. You found it; that gives you standing. But you cannot dive this alone, you cannot finance this alone, and you cannot survive the attention this alone. You need a boat crewed by people who don't ask questions, equipment that doesn't appear on any manifest, and someone with the connections to learn what the navy knows before the navy learns what you know. That is me. In exchange: forty percent."

"Sixty."

"Thirty-five, and I don't sell you."

"Forty-five, and you don't sell me *or* anyone I sail with."

Something flickered across her face — not offense. Interest. The look of an appraiser finding an unexpected hallmark under a tarnished plate.

"Fifty-fifty," she said, "because I like you, and liking people is expensive, and I want you to remember I paid for it." She extended her hand across the desk. Small hand, dry grip, calluses in places that told stories her ledgers wouldn't. "Do we have terms?"

I looked at her hand and understood precisely what I was doing. I was shaking hands with a woman who had just offered to have me killed as door number one, who counted betrayal among her services rendered, and whose eyes had gone hungry in a way I recognized because I saw it in mirrors whenever rent came due. Every instinct I owned — and diving had sharpened most of them — said *swim away*.

But instincts keep you alive. They don't pay your debts. And somewhere under the river, in a sealed chest stamped with a dead nation's crest, was the rest of that ledger, and the coordinates it pointed to, and the only version of my future that didn't end with me diving condemned wrecks until the sea collected what the creditors had already bought.

"One condition," I said. "If you ever plan to sell me, you tell me first. I want the chance to be worth more to you breathing."

Odile smiled, and for a moment the smile was almost young. "That," she said, "is the most honest thing anyone's said in this room in years. Terms accepted."

We shook. Somewhere below us, the chandlery bell rang for a customer, and the ordinary world went on selling rope.

* * *

She talked business for another hour, and I learned fast why her reputation for keeping partners alive was earned rather than advertised. She drew charts from a locked drawer — charts of the Saltreach that showed channels no hydrographic office admitted existed. She named names: which naval officers took bribes, which took bribes *and* reported them, which syndicate captains were currently fighting each other and might be turned. She asked precise, surgical questions about the wreck site — depth, current, visibility, what else the sweep had touched — and I answered some of them and deflected others, and she noticed every deflection and filed it without comment, which was somehow more unnerving than if she'd pushed.

At the door, with the ledger leaf locked back in her drawer and my tea finished and my fate signed, she stopped me with one finger raised.

"A question, partner to partner. Off the books."

"Off the books."

"The chest you recovered. The seal was intact?" 

"Intact. Unbroken. Twenty-some years underwater and the lock hadn't been touched."

Odile nodded slowly, and there was something in her face I couldn't read — grief wearing appraisal's clothes, maybe, or the other way round. "Intact," she repeated softly. "Then whoever sealed it expected to come back." She took off her spectacles and looked at me through naked, tired eyes. "Be careful, August Nystrom. Things that wait twenty years for their owners tend to attract the owners' enemies too."

I climbed down the narrow stairs with fifty percent of a fortune and zero percent of a plan, and the evening air of Port Sallow tasted like tar and old lies, and for the first time in years it tasted like possibility too.

Behind me, above the chandlery, a light burned late in a green-painted room, and I chose not to wonder whom Odile Marchetti was writing to.

Some debts you count in air tanks. Hers, I was beginning to suspect, were counted in something darker — and I'd just made myself collateral.

Two tides later, a naval cutter anchored in the outer roads flying the pennant of an officer who asked questions no salvage diver survives answering, and I understood that whatever Odile had written, the clock on all our lives had started ticking.
