## 5. A Crew of Castoffs

*August*

The thing nobody tells you about needing a crew is that you have to ask people for things. I'd spent eleven years perfecting the art of asking no one for anything. Debts were fine — debts were transactions, impersonal as tide charts. But asking a human being to stake their neck on my judgment, on a document I'd stolen and a wreck I could only half locate? That required a different kind of arithmetic, and I was bad at it.

I made a list anyway, sitting in Odile's back office with her ledger open between us like a card game neither of us would show our hands to.

"Engineer," she said, tapping her pen. "Someone who can keep that rust bucket of yours breathing when the navy's shooting at it."

"She's not a rust bucket."

"August. I've seen your boat. She's a rust bucket with delusions." Odile lit a cigarette and didn't offer me one, which was its own kind of message. "You want Reyes. Tomas Reyes. He's at the Meridian Yard, doing contract work they don't let him sign his name to."

"Why not?"

"Because three years ago he certified a ferry as seaworthy, the ferry went down in calm water, and forty-one people drowned. The inquiry found the hull plates were swapped out after his inspection. Found it *eventually*. By then the yard had buried him instead." She exhaled smoke through her nose. "He was right. Everyone above him was wrong. Guess which fact mattered."

I wrote the name down. "And the second slot?"

"You need someone young. Someone cheap who'll climb rigging in weather that would make a grown man cry, and who doesn't ask what's in the boxes you're hauling." Odile smiled with all the warmth of a fishhook. "I know just the girl. But she costs you a promise, not wages. Runaways are paid in futures."

"I can't pay anyone yet."

"Of course you can't. That's why they're available to you." She stubbed out the cigarette half-smoked, an extravagance that told me more about her finances than any ledger. "Nobody good works for money they've already got, August. Good people work for money that means something. Go be meaningful."

* * *

Tomas Reyes worked under a crane that groaned like it was composing an opera about its own death. He was broad through the shoulders and narrow through the future, a man built for engine rooms and clearly aware the world had decided he didn't get one anymore. When I said his name he didn't turn around.

"Whatever it is, I didn't do it."

"That's the spirit. I'm offering you a job where you won't do anything either."

That got him to turn. Up close he had the eyes of a man who'd read every accident report about himself so many times he'd memorized the typos. "Who sent you?"

"Odile Marchetti."

"Worse." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last year. "Look, lady — whatever she's selling, I'm not buying. Last time I took a job off Marchetti's board, I spent eight months in a customs cell learning that 'salvage' has a very flexible definition."

"This isn't salvage. It's treasure." I said it flatly, because flattery would bounce off him and honesty might not. "A Vessian treasury wreck. Real coordinates, real documentation, real shares. And a navy fleet that reaches it in five tides if we don't."

He laughed once, without humor. "Vessania. Sure. Next you'll tell me there's a chest of dragon teeth."

I put the ledger page on his workbench. Not the whole document — I wasn't stupid — but enough. The seal, the script, the column of figures in a dead nation's hand. He looked at it longer than a skeptic should have. His thumb hovered over the crest without touching it, the way you hover over something hot.

"My grandmother was Vessian," he said quietly. "Came out before the floods. Used to sing songs in a language nobody else on the dock spoke." He straightened up, and something in his face closed like a valve. "No. You know why? Because the last time I trusted a hull, forty-one families got a funeral and I got a name nobody hires. If I go down with your rust bucket, that's the story forever. *Reyes sank another one.*"

"It's not a ferry full of passengers. It's four people who know exactly what they signed up for."

"Four." He almost smiled. "Confident number."

"Tomas." I waited until he met my eyes. "They took your name away. I'm offering you a chance to take it back. Not clean — nothing's clean — but *yours*. An engine room where your signature actually means what you say it means."

He looked at the ledger page again. At the crane. At the yard that paid him in cash and contempt. I watched him do the same arithmetic I did every morning, the crushing kind, the kind where every column ends in zero.

"One condition," he said. "I inspect your boat before I commit. If she's a coffin, I walk."

"She's a coffin," I agreed. "But she's *my* coffin, and I intend to die old in her."

He shook my hand like he was testing whether it would hold weight. "Five tides?"

"Four, now."

"Then stop talking to me and start walking."

* * *

*Tomas*

Here is what they don't put in the inquiry reports: I loved that ferry. I loved her the way you love anything you've made safe with your own hands. Forty-one people, and every plate, every weld, every rivet was mine to answer for — except the ones somebody else quietly replaced while I slept, which turned out to be the only ones that mattered.

Three years of hauling scrap since. Three years of foremen saying *Reyes* in a tone usually reserved for wet rot. So when the Nystrom woman walked into the yard with a dead language in her pocket and asked me to be brave again, I should have said no.

Instead I spent my afternoon crawling through her boat like a doctor giving a terminal diagnosis, and the terrible thing — the thing I'll deny if anyone asks — is that I fell a little bit in love.

She was a coastal tramp once, sixty feet of bad decisions, re-engined twice by people who thought cable ties were a marine technology. Her fuel lines were a crime scene. Her bilge pump was held together by hope and electrical tape. But her keel was honest, laid down by somebody who cared, and her frames were sound beneath the rot, and underneath thirty years of neglect there was a ship trying to get out.

"Well?" Nystrom stood on the dock with her arms crossed, pretending not to watch me the way cats pretend not to watch birds.

"She'll float," I said. "Whether she floats *where you want* is a different question. Your fuel system is a fire waiting for an excuse. Your pumps are decorative. And whoever wired this panel should be tried at The Hague."

"Can you fix it?"

"I can fix anything. That's the entire tragedy of my life." I pulled myself out of the engine room and stood up in the grey afternoon light. My hands were shaking. They always shook near open water, near boats, near the memory of a list angle and screaming I still heard in quiet rooms. "One more condition. Nobody calls me the engineer who sank the ferry. On this boat, I'm just Reyes. New name, new record. Agreed?"

"Agreed," she said. "On this boat we're all nobody. It's very freeing."

I believed her, which should have been my first warning. Instead it felt like the first deep breath I'd taken in three years.

* * *

*Wren*

The harbor master's office had a roof, which was why I slept behind it, in the gap between the wall and the stacked crab pots where the wind couldn't find me. Six months I'd been in this port. Long enough to learn which chandlers counted stock honestly (none), which taverns threw you out gently (the Anchor, if you tipped the cook), and how long a person could live on gull eggs and stubbornness (indefinitely, but it wears on you).

So when the dark-haired woman came looking for me — Odile Marchetti's errand girl, or so I assumed at first — I had my knife out and my exit planned before she finished saying my name.

"Easy," she said, hands visible, palms out. Diver's hands, scarred and steady. "Odile said you'd run. She also said you'd listen, because you're smart enough to know running gets old."

"Odile can say a lot of things. She sold me a deckhand job once and then sold the *ship* out from under it."

"I know. I'm not here for Odile." She crouched down to my level, which nobody did, ever. Grown-ups talked down at you like weather. This one crouched. "I need crew. One voyage, maybe two. Honest wages — well. Wages. Eventually honest."

"Eventually." I kept the knife where it was. Habit. "What's the cargo?"

"Treasure."

"Liar."

"Probably," she admitted, and that surprised a laugh out of me before I could stop it. "But it's a specific lie with a map attached, and I need someone who can go up a mast in weather that would make a grown man cry."

"Odile's words?"

"Word for word."

"Then she's already picked my price." I sheathed the knife, mostly. "Everyone thinks runaways want coin. Coin gets stolen, coin gets spent, coin gets you noticed. What I want is a berth. A place where my name isn't the one the orphanage sold, and nobody checks papers, and if a navy cutter comes alongside I'm just some captain's niece with sea legs."

"Nystrom," she said. "August Nystrom. And sure. Niece. Why not." Something crossed her face when she said her own name — quick, like a fish under ice — but I was fifteen going on forty, and I knew better than to ask. People's names were their business. Mine had been *Girl, Section C* for nine years, and I hadn't enjoyed explaining it either.

We shook on it. Her grip was strong and her palm was calloused in the pattern of rope and rail, working hands, and I thought: *this one's broke too.* Broke people hire broke people. It's the only charity anybody trusts.

* * *

*August*

Wren came aboard at dusk carrying everything she owned in a canvas sack that weighed less than my dive kit, and she looked around the deck of the *Magpie* with the expression of someone checking whether a miracle came with a catch.

"Bunk's aft," I said. "It was the mate's. There's a leak over it when it rains hard, so don't keep anything paper there."

"A leak I can fix," Reyes called from the engine room hatch, voice muffled and already proprietary. "If someone buys me sealant that isn't older than the girl."

"I'm not *that* young," Wren said.

"You're younger than my sealant. That's the bar."

I stood at the wheel and watched them orbit each other — the disgraced engineer cataloguing my ship's sins aloud, the runaway stowing her sack with the economy of someone who'd learned that everything can be taken — and felt something in the hull of me shift, some plank settling into a new position. The *Magpie* had been quiet for eleven years. Quiet the way an empty church is quiet. Tonight she creaked and muttered and had opinions again, and so did I.

I cooked. That was the other thing nobody tells you: a captain feeds her crew or loses them. Salt cod, hard bread, tea brewed strong enough to strip paint. We ate in the cockpit with our knees knocking, and Wren ate like food might be legislated against tomorrow, and Reyes ate slowly, savoring, a man relearning that meals could be occasions.

"So," Reyes said, pointing his fork at me. "Treasuries. Dead nations. Navies. Want to explain to the new hire why we're all going to die?"

"Hey," Wren said.

"He's right, though. Odds aren't great." I turned my cup in my hands. My father's compass sat in my pocket, and I touched it through the cloth without meaning to, the way you touch a bruise. "There's a wreck. What's in it is worth killing for — people already have been killed for it, and more will be. We have four tides to reach it before a naval salvage fleet does, and I can't pay you what you're worth, and I lied to a naval officer yesterday and I'll probably lie to him again."

Silence. Rain started somewhere over the harbor, soft as static.

"And the shares?" Wren asked.

"Equal. Four ways."

"Four?" Reyes raised an eyebrow. "You're taking the same cut as the hired help?"

"The hired help is diving into black water with me. Yes. Equal."

Wren looked at Reyes. Reyes looked at Wren. Some silent negotiation passed between them, the kind crews conduct without captains, and I pretended not to see it, which is most of leadership.

"Equal shares," Reyes said finally, "from a treasure that may not exist, captained by a woman who lies to navies." He raised his tin cup. "To the *Magpie*. May she outlive our reputations."

"To the leaky bunk," Wren said, clinking.

"To debts," I said, and drank, and meant it in every direction.

Later, when they'd gone below — Reyes muttering over my wiring like a priest over a heretic, Wren curled in the mate's bunk already asleep, fast, the way the poor sleep — I stood my first watch alone and let the harbor lights blur.

Two days ago it had been one pair of hands on this boat. Mine. Now there were six, and every one of them was a debt I'd chosen, which was different from the debts I'd been assigned. Creditors owned pieces of me. These two — the man the industry buried and the girl the orphanage sold — they weren't creditors. They were something I didn't have a word for yet. Family was too big a word for nightfall of the first day. But it was pointed in that direction, the way a compass points, wobbling, insisting.

I took out my father's compass and opened it under the binnacle light. The needle swung, settled, swung again — restless, like everything else in my life. Tomorrow I'd sit down with the ledger and the tide tables and try to make sense of coordinates that matched no chart I owned. Tomorrow Corvane's shadow would get longer. Tomorrow the syndicate would start asking Odile questions she'd answer badly.

Tonight, though, the boat was warm and full, and the rain played its small music on the deck above sleeping people who had, incredibly, said yes.

The compass needle steadied at last, pointing north-northeast — out toward open water, toward the marshes, toward whatever my father had left me besides a childhood of silence.

"Hold that thought," I told it, and went below to plot a course.
