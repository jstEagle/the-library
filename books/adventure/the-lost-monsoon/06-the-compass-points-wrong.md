## 6. The Compass Points Wrong

The problem with stolen secrets is that they don't come with instructions.

I'd spent three days learning this. Three days of sitting in Odile Marchetti's back room — or my own cabin, or the galley of the *Marrow*, depending on which version of hiding I was pretending to do — staring at the Vessian ledger until the numbers started rearranging themselves in my sleep. Which, to be fair, was still better than the dreams where Corvane's patrol boat found me, or the dreams where my creditors did, or the dream where my father stood at the end of a dock and I woke up before I could reach him. My subconscious had a full dance card.

The ledger itself was a beautiful thing, which felt like a personal insult. Vessian craftsmanship, Odile called it, running her thumb along the spine like she was greeting an old lover. Brass corners, oiled canvas boards, pages of a paper stock so dense it had spent decades in a river and come out merely damp instead of dissolved. The ink was iron-gall, faded to the color of old bruises. Columns and columns of it. Cargo manifests, mostly. Grain weights, tar quantities, rope lengths. The bureaucratic afterlife of a nation that had drowned.

And then, on the third page from the back, tucked between a manifest for salt pork and a note about hull caulking that someone had annotated with what I hoped was a complaint and not a prophecy, the coordinates.

I say coordinates the way Odile did, with reverence. What they actually were was a string of figures in a cipher that wasn't a cipher at all, or wasn't only a cipher — numbers in base something that wasn't ten, arranged in groups that refused to behave like latitude and longitude no matter how I bullied them. I tried every conversion I knew. I tried assuming the river delta was the origin point. I tried assuming the old Vessian capital was. I tried assuming the cartographers of a drowned country had simply been drunk.

Nothing landed on a chart. Any chart. I checked the modern hydrographic surveys, the pre-war admiralty charts Odile kept behind a locked drawer like they were contraband — which, knowing Odile, they might have been — and the hand-drawn salvage sketches I'd inherited from three different dead divers. The numbers pointed everywhere and nowhere. They pointed, mostly, at open water forty miles off the shelf edge, which is the ocean's way of saying *nothing for you here, go home*.

"You're doing the face," Odile said from the doorway. She had a talent for appearing exactly when I was least composed, which I suspected was a professional skill and not a coincidence.

"What face?"

"The one where you're losing an argument with a book." She set down two cups of coffee that smelled like it had been roasted during the war, and not the recent one. "The book is winning. Books usually do."

"It's numbers," I said. "Numbers are supposed to be honest. That's the entire selling point of numbers."

Odile lowered herself into the chair across from me with the slow care of a woman whose knees had opinions. She looked at the ledger, then at my charts, then at me, and I watched her decide not to say something. She'd been doing a lot of that since I'd brought her the page I'd lifted from the chest — the one page, the one I'd sworn to myself was a bookkeeping error and not theft. She knew. Of course she knew. Odile Marchetti had forgotten more about theft than I would ever learn, and she'd forgotten it on purpose.

"The Vessans didn't chart from the coast," she said finally. "Everyone assumes they did, because everyone charts from the coast. But Vessania was a nation of river people before it was anything else. Their zero point wasn't a harbor. It was a spring."

"A spring."

"A freshwater spring, offshore, in the delta shallows. The one their first settlement was built around. When the sea took the country, it took the spring too. There's no landmark to measure from because the landmark is under forty feet of silt and salt." She sipped her terrible coffee and watched me over the rim. "Their coordinates are true. They're just true in a language the maps stopped speaking."

I sat with that. It was, I'll admit, the first time in my adult life that a piece of information made me want to both throw something and hug someone, and Odile was not a hugging candidate, so I settled for gripping the table.

"So I need the spring."

"You need the spring," she agreed. "Or you need someone who knows where it was. Which is a shorter list, and every name on it is either dead, expensive, or lying."

I thanked her for the encouragement. She waved it off like a queen dismissing a courtier, and I went back to my cabin with the ledger under my coat and the particular heaviness of a person who has just learned that the treasure map requires a second, older treasure map.

* * *

Here's the thing about my father's compass: I've been trying to get rid of it for eleven years, and I can't.

Not can't in the sentimental way — well, not *only* in the sentimental way. I mean it physically refuses. When I was nineteen and broke in a way that made today's broke look like a vacation, I pawned it. The pawnbroker looked at it, looked at me, and handed it back across the counter with an expression I've thought about more times than I'd like. "Brass case's cracked," he said. "Movement's shot. Needle wanders." He was right. The needle wanders. It doesn't point north so much as it points *somewhere*, with the confidence of a drunk giving directions, and no amount of tapping or swearing or, on one memorable night, prayer has fixed it. It was worthless as an instrument the day I got it, and it's worthless now, and it has outlived every pawn ticket, every attempt to sell it, every year I told myself I should let it go the way I'd let him go.

It was in his kit bag. The one they shipped back to me, or rather the one the salvage company shipped back to me with a form letter and a check that didn't cover the funeral. *Lost with all hands during a commercial dive operation, vessel *Coriander's Luck*, position unremarkable.* Eleven words about a man who taught me to swim in floodwater. The compass was in the bag's inner pocket, wrapped in an oilcloth, like he'd been protecting it from the water. Like he'd known he was going somewhere wet.

I kept it because it was the last thing he'd touched that wasn't me. I told myself that was the reason, anyway. Eleven years is a long time to keep a lie that small and that enormous.

That night, back on the *Marrow*, I took it out. I don't know why that night and not one of the previous three. Maybe because Odile had said *the landmark is underwater*, and something in the back of my skull had started turning over like an engine on a cold morning.

The compass is brass, dented, about the size of my palm. The face glass is original, scratched to a frost. The needle wanders. On the back, Elias Nystrom is scratched in a hand I'd know anywhere, because I inherited it — the same impatient downstrokes I write my own name with.

I'd never opened the case. It was cracked along the hinge, and I'd never opened it, and I want to tell you this was out of reverence but the truth is I was afraid that opening it would make it finally, completely dead, and then it would just be an object, and then I would have to decide what to do with an object.

I opened it.

The hinge gave with a small click that sounded, in the empty cabin, like a door.

Inside, the movement was exactly as shot as advertised — gears furred with corrosion, the card loose on its pivot. But the inner face of the case back, the side that had been pressed against all that oilcloth for eleven years and who knows how many years before that, wasn't bare.

It was engraved.

Lines. A grid of them, fine as hair, cut into the brass with a steady hand — and not a machine's steady hand. A person's. I've spent my life around engraving, around stamped plate and maker's marks and the serial numbers we divers use to identify wrecks, and this was none of those. This was someone sitting down with a graver and cutting a map into the inside of a compass they expected no one but themselves to ever see.

A map of a coastline I didn't recognize, except that I did, because I'd been staring at its ghost for three days in the ledger's stubborn numbers. The proportions were wrong for any modern projection, the scale arbitrary, the features reduced to suggestions — but there, unmistakable, a delta. A river mouth branching like a cracked window. And in the shallows off the eastern channel, a small circle, and inside the circle, a dot.

A spring. A freshwater spring, offshore, in the delta shallows.

I sat there for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above me, Wren was pacing the deck in the rhythm she paced everything, quick and restless, a girl who slept like the boat might leave without her.

Here's what I understood, in the slow, cold way you understand water coming into a boot: the coordinates in a nation's lost ledger and the engraving in my father's compass were the same map. Not similar. The same. The same delta, the same channel, the same dot in the same shallows. Either my father had copied the ledger — which meant he'd had access to a Vessian state document that had been sitting in a sealed chest at the bottom of a river for decades — or someone had copied the compass into the ledger, which made no sense at all, or —

Or my father had known. Before the *Coriander's Luck* went down, before the form letter, before eleven years of me doing arithmetic with air tanks and borrowed time. He had known where the spring was. He had known what the spring was for. He had cut it into the one object he wrapped in oilcloth and carried on his last dive.

I did the math I didn't want to do. The ledger's dates ran back forty years, to the last year of Vessania. My father was a child then. A river kid from a family of divers, in a port town full of Vessian refugees and Vessian money and Vesian secrets, if Odile was to be believed, and Odile was many things but she was rarely wrong. He could have heard the stories. He could have heard the numbers. He could have spent his whole life with a map in his pocket and a nation's ghost in his head, and then one day he'd loaded his gear onto the *Coriander's Luck* and gone down to look.

*Position unremarkable.*

The wreck of the *Coriander's Luck* sits eleven miles northeast of where the chest came up. I know because I looked it up years ago, the way you look at a scar. Eleven miles northeast. And the compass's little dot in the delta shallows sits — I measured it four times with dividers, hands not as steady as I'd have liked — eleven miles northeast of the chest.

The chest that was stamped with a nation's crest and sealed like it mattered and sunk like it didn't.

My father's last known position and the Vessian chest, eleven miles apart, and a spring between them, and a map engraved in secret on the inside of a broken compass that had spent a decade in my pocket pointing at nothing.

The needle chose that moment to swing. It does that, wanders, but it had never done it while I watched with the case open, and I'd like to tell you it pointed north, or at the delta, or at anything useful. It pointed at the cabin wall. At the hook where my dive gear hung. At the water beyond the hull, which is to say it pointed everywhere I was about to go.

I closed the case. The click sounded different the second time. Less like a door and more like a lock.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the galley at two in the morning with the ledger and the compass side by side under the one working lamp, matching lines, matching angles, and the match held. It held the way things hold when they're true, which is to say quietly, without any effort at all, the way a keel holds a boat.

Wren found me there. She does that — materializes in doorways like a cat that's decided you're hers. She looked at the compass, at the ledger, at my face, and instead of asking what any normal person would ask, she said:

"That's Vessian work."

I looked up. "What?"

"The engraving." She leaned in, and her finger — quick, certain, the same finger that could splice a line in the dark — traced the delta without touching the brass. "The way the channels are drawn. Vessian charts use that convention, the branching lines with the dots at the sources. Freshwater marks. They're the only ones who did it that way." She glanced at me, and for a second the wariness in her face slipped and there was something younger underneath, something that had learned this the way she'd learned everything: in a place that had no business teaching children anything. "Where did you get a Vessian chart?"

"It's not a chart," I said. "It's a compass."

"Whose?"

And there it was, the question I'd been circling all night, the one with eleven years of grief and a form letter and a check that didn't cover the funeral wrapped around it. I could have lied to her. She was a runaway deckhand I'd known three weeks; lying to her would have been easy and cheap and I'd already done my first lie with the page I lifted from the chest, and lies are like debts, they compound.

"My father's," I said.

Wren nodded slowly, and then she said the thing that took the floor out from under me, in the flat, matter-of-fact tone she used for weather and other forces beyond anyone's control:

"Then he was Vessian. Or he worked for them." She tapped the dot in the spring. "Nobody else would know to mark the source. That's not navigation. That's *belonging*."

I opened my mouth to say that my father had been born in Harrow's Bend to a family of nothing-special salvage divers, that his name was in no ledger I'd ever seen, that he was the least belonging-to-anything man I'd ever known, and that was the whole point of him.

But the ledger was open on the table in front of me, and I'd spent three nights with its columns, and somewhere in the back of my mind a list had been forming — a roster I hadn't let myself read as a roster, because it was just names, just the dive crews of a drowned nation's last salvage office, just history.

I turned to the page. The one with the crew lists. The names ran down in that faded iron-gall ink, forty years old, and I'd skimmed them a dozen times without seeing, the way you skim a stranger's family photos.

I saw it before I let myself see it. Fourth from the bottom. A name in a hand that wasn't the clerk's — added later, the ink a shade darker, squeezed in like an afterthought or an amendment.

*Nystrom, E.*

Dated the year my father vanished.

The lamp hissed. The needle in the closed compass, I would have sworn, clicked against its glass, once, like something knocking to be let out.

"August," Wren said quietly. "Whose year is that?"

I didn't answer. I was doing arithmetic again — the only kind I'd ever been good at, the kind where you count what you've lost — and the numbers were coming out wrong. Or coming out right, in a way that was so much worse.

My father hadn't stumbled onto the Vessian secret at the end of his life. He'd been on the roster. Forty years of ledger, and his name was in it, and his map was in my pocket, and the chest had been waiting in the river like a letter addressed to no one, and I had opened it.

Someone had to know I had this. Someone had already died over less — I'd seen what the smugglers did to the last crew that talked. And somewhere out there, Corvane was asking his questions no salvage diver survives answering, and the fleet was coming, and the tide was running out in every sense the word has.

I closed the ledger and put my hand flat on the compass, and Wren watched me with those eyes that missed nothing, waiting for an answer to a question I couldn't say out loud yet:

If my father was on that roster — what else was he on?

And who else was still looking for him?
