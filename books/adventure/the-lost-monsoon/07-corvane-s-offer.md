## 7. Corvane's Offer

I have interrogated smugglers, mutineers, and one customs clerk who tried to sell an entire naval registry to the Vessian remnant for the price of a fishing boat and a pardon. I like to think I know how to read a person across a table. So it was a professional humiliation that August Nystrom read me first.

She came aboard the *Vigilant* at my summons — came willingly, which told me she was either innocent or very good. She climbed the pilot ladder with the unhurried economy of someone who had done it a hundred times in worse weather, stepped onto my deck, and looked at my ship the way a butcher looks at a cow. Not with fear. With assessment.

"Officer Corvane." She said my rank the way you'd say *mister* to a man selling brushes door to door.

"Miss Nystrom. Thank you for coming."

"You didn't leave much alternative." She glanced at the two marines flanking the gangway, then at me, and something almost amused crossed her face. "Though I notice you asked rather than ordered. That's interesting."

Everything about her was interesting, which was precisely the problem. Interesting people in salvage zones are either corpses or complications, and I had buried enough of the former and been blamed for enough of the latter.

* * *

My cabin aboard the *Vigilant* is not designed for hospitality. It is designed for charts, reports, and the particular brand of insomnia that comes with command. I cleared a space on the desk anyway, poured coffee from the pot my steward keeps scalded past the point of cruelty, and watched her take in the room.

She noticed the chart first. Of course she did. The restricted zone, marked in red, the search grid we'd been running for nine days. Her eyes moved over it without lingering, which is how trained people look at things they've already memorized.

"You've been busy," she said.

"We're conducting a survey."

"A survey." She wrapped both hands around the coffee cup — I noted the calluses, the rope burns half-healed along her palm, the diver's habit of holding warmth close — and did not drink. "In a river stretch condemned since before I was born. With two hydrographic launches and a mine-sweeper tender. What are you surveying for, Officer? Fish?"

"I could ask what you were doing in it."

"Working. A creditor hired me to strip scrap off the *Marguerite*. You can verify that. His name's Doss; he'll confirm anything if you lean on him correctly." She finally sipped the coffee and her expression flickered — surprise, then a kind of grudging respect, as though the navy had achieved something by ruining a bean properly. "The chest wasn't on my manifest. It wasn't on anyone's. I reported it within the hour. Ask your own patrol log."

"I have. Your report was exemplary." I let that sit. "Almost too exemplary. Most divers who find something like that chest either run or start making phone calls. You wrote a report with a serial number, a seal impression, and a condition assessment. That's not a diver's report, Miss Nystrom. That's an archivist's."

"My father taught me documentation," she said, and there it was — the first crack, hairline-thin, gone as soon as it showed. "He believed if you write everything down honestly, nobody can hang you with your own pen."

"And where is your father these days?"

"Gone." Flat. Final. A door closing so smoothly I almost missed that it was a door at all.

* * *

Here is what I knew going into that room, and what I want recorded plainly, because afterward everyone involved decided the record should be vague: the Admiralty believes Elias Nystrom participated in the theft of the Vessian state treasury twenty-two years ago.

Not suspected. Believes. There are files — sealed files, the kind that require three signatures and a bad conscience to open — containing dive manifests from the last months of Vessania's existence, when the government-in-exile was frantically moving its gold ahead of the annexation. One of those manifests lists a contract diver named E. Nystrom. The same E. Nystrom whose boat was found adrift and empty eleven years later, whose daughter was raised on charity and harbor work, and who has spent every year since diving other people's wrecks to pay other people's debts.

The official theory is elegant: Nystrom helped move the treasury, kept a key, vanished when his partners started dying, and left his daughter as unwitting cover — a grieving orphan too poor and too honest-looking to ever be examined. The theory has one flaw, and its name was sitting across my desk drinking terrible coffee.

Because grief like hers doesn't perform well. I've seen manufactured grief; it arrives on schedule and leaves on schedule. Hers lived in her like ballast. Either she genuinely believed her father dead, or she was the finest actor the Service never recruited.

"The Vessian material," I said, deciding to spend some of my ammunition. "You held the document up to the light. Checked the watermark. Most people wouldn't know to do that."

"People who grow up around paper learn paper." A shrug. "Harbors are full of documents. Bills of lading, mostly. Lies with stamps on them."

"What did the seal mean to you?"

"Nothing. A dead country's stamp on a dead ship's cargo." She met my eyes, and I will grant her this: she never looked away, not once in ninety minutes, and liars who hold eye contact that steadily usually believe their own story, which is the most dangerous kind. "Why does a hydrographic survey need to ask me about seals, Officer? Why does the navy care about a drowned nation's stationery?"

Because, Miss Nystrom, the treasury of Vessania contained roughly four million crowns in bullion and bearer instruments, because at least three governments and two syndicates believe they have a claim to it, because my own superiors would prefer it never surfaces at all, and because in the last month two independent salvage crews working the delta have stopped answering their radios. But none of that goes in the opening bid.

"What it means," I said instead, "is opportunity."

I slid a folder across the desk. Inside: a single typed page, unsigned, unletterheaded, deniable in six ways. Immunity from prosecution for salvage violations, customs irregularities, and any discoveries arising from the *Marguerite* recovery. Full salvage rights to ordinary wreck material in the delta for five years. In exchange: cooperation. Anything she found, everything she found, reported directly to me, and any further sites indicated by the Vessian materials disclosed before she dove them.

She read it twice. I watched her lips move slightly on the second pass, and I understood then that I had misjudged the arithmetic of her life. She wasn't reading the immunity. She was counting what five years of salvage rights would clear against her debts. I have seen men do that math with their lives; she did it with her future, quickly, and arrived at an answer she didn't like.

"That's generous," she said slowly.

"It is."

"Which means you're frightened." She set the page down and aligned its edge with the desk's edge, a small compulsive neatness that I suspected she'd inherited alongside the documentation habits. "Navies don't offer immunity for fish surveys. You're frightened of whatever that chest points to, and you need someone expendable to go get it first. Someone who dives condemned wrecks for creditors and won't be missed if the water stays closed over her."

I have been called many things in my career. Accurate is the one that stings most.

"The alternative," I said, "is that I detain you as a material witness, impound your boat, and you spend the duration of this operation in a naval brig answering questions from people considerably less patient than I am."

"You could." She considered it with what appeared to be genuine calm, which unsettled me more than any outburst would have. "But you won't. Because if you thought I knew anything worth locking me up for, you'd have done it already, marines first and questions after. This—" she tapped the folder "—is you telling me you don't have enough. Which means whoever else is hunting this thing knows more than you do. And you want me because I'm the only lead you have that isn't a corpse."

The cabin was quiet except for the ship's bones creaking and, somewhere above, a bosun yelling at a winch.

"Miss Nystrom—"

"August." She said it like a correction, not an invitation. "If we're going to lie to each other, we may as well use names."

* * *

I want to be fair to her here, because history — my small, personal history, the kind that never makes files — requires it. In that moment I liked her. That was the trap. Every officer who has ever been played was played by someone he liked.

"August, then." I leaned back. "Let me tell you a story instead of an offer. Twenty-two years ago, a nation died. Vessania. You won't find it on maps because three treaties agreed to erase it — borders redrawn, name struck, archives dispersed or burned. But nations die slowly, and while it was dying, someone had to move its money. The treasury was loaded onto barges on the delta. Some of it reached the exile government. Some of it reached thieves. And some of it simply... stopped appearing in anyone's ledger."

Her face did nothing. Perfectly nothing. And yet the stillness itself was information — a woman holding herself motionless the way you hold a lantern steady in wind.

"One of the contracted divers on that movement," I continued, watching her, "was named Nystrom."

"Common name on the delta."

"Elias Nystrom."

For one heartbeat — I counted it, I count everything, it's my curse — something moved behind her eyes. Not shock. Recognition. The look of a woman hearing a song she hasn't heard since childhood and discovering she still knows every word.

"He died eleven years ago," she said quietly. "His boat was found empty off Grayhaven Point. I identified his effects myself. I was fifteen."

"I'm sorry."

"Are you?" And there was the wry edge again, armor going back up plate by plate. "Because it sounds like you're building toward something. Dead fathers make excellent levers. Everyone thinks so. They're wrong, but everyone thinks so."

"If your father was involved—"

"He wasn't." Sharp now, the first true heat she'd shown. "Whatever he signed, whatever they say he hauled — he was a contract diver, Officer. He moved cargo for pay because I needed shoes. He didn't steal nations. He fixed engines and wrote everything down and came home smelling of bilge, and he's dead, and using him to pressure me is the cheapest trick in your file, and I expected better from someone who ruins coffee this thoroughly."

I sat with that. Partly because she deserved the silence, partly because I was recalculating everything.

Here is the truth I did not tell her: I don't believe Elias Nystrom stole anything either. I've read the manifests. He was hired muscle, low on the chain, and when the syndicate began executing the dive team one by one — and they did, four bodies in five years, all ruled accidents — Nystrom did the only sensible thing. He disappeared. Faked his death, most likely, or simply walked into a new name somewhere far away. The navy's files call him a thief. My private judgment calls him a survivor. The distinction matters to me because I have spent my career watching institutions confuse the two.

It also matters because if Nystrom is alive, he knows where the treasury is. And if he knows, then the moment anyone starts pulling Vessian threads in this delta, he will move — and every wolf in the water will follow him straight to it.

Which meant the girl across my desk was not a lead. She was bait. And I hated my superiors a little more for making me the hook.

"Last question," I said. "The document you recovered. The ledger fragment. When you held it, before you reported it — did you read it?"

"No."

"Any part of it?"

"No." She stood, set the cup down with a click of finality, and pushed the immunity folder back across the desk with one finger. "And no to this, too. I dive scrap for creditors, Officer. That's my whole empire. Whatever your dead nation lost, it's not mine to find, and I'd like to keep it that way. My life expectancy improves dramatically when I stay ignorant."

"You understand I can compel you."

"You can detain me. You can't compel knowledge I don't have." At the cabin door she paused, hand on the frame, and looked back — and for just a moment the wry mask slipped and I saw underneath it something tired and fierce and young. "You said the navy believes my father was involved. Believe. Not know. Whoever wrote your files never met him. I did. Whatever he was part of, he was running *from* it, not with it. If you want the truth about Vessania, stop digging through his daughter and start asking who killed his crew."

Then she was gone, down the ladder and into her battered skiff, and I stood at the porthole watching her cross the gray water back toward the harbor, moving with the unhurried certainty of a woman who has just lied to an intelligence officer and gotten away clean.

Except.

Except that when she said *no*, her thumb had moved against her forefinger — once, a small rolling press, the unconscious gesture of someone feeling the ghost of paper between her fingers. I've seen it in couriers. In card counters. In people who have recently handled a document they memorized and destroyed.

She had read it. Every word, probably. And she had walked out of my cabin carrying coordinates in her head and immunity refused, which meant she intended to reach whatever that ledger pointed to before my fleet did — alone, or with whatever ragged allies a bankrupt diver could scrape together in forty-eight hours.

I should have detained her. The file I eventually wrote says I considered it and lacked grounds. The truth is simpler and worse: I let her go because I wanted to see what she'd do, and because some part of me — the part that joined the Service believing it protected people instead of burying them — wanted her to outrun us all.

I returned to my desk and pulled the sealed file on the dive-team deaths. Four accidents. Four bodies. And a fifth name on the original manifest, marked *status unknown*, that my superiors' elegant theory had somehow never pursued:

*O. Marchetti.*

I sat with that a long time, listening to the winch complain above me, and then I did the thing I would spend the rest of this affair neither regretting nor confessing: I opened a private channel and ordered my patrol boats to widen their grid. Two miles east. Away from the harbor. Away from her.

A survey, after all, needs room to breathe.

By nightfall the harbor master would tell me a patched-up salvage ketch had taken on engine parts, a disgraced engineer, and a deckhand too young to sign her own articles — and that a fence named Odile Marchetti had been seen buying provisions in quantities suggesting a long voyage.

The tide, as they say, turns whether you consent or not. Six of them, I'd learn later. We all had six.
