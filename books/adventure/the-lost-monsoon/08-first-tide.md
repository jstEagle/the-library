## 8. First Tide

There are three kinds of fear, in my professional opinion. The kind that makes your hands shake — useless. The kind that makes you sick over the rail — also useless, but at least it's honest. And the kind that goes quiet and cold and starts doing arithmetic.

I was deep into the third kind by the time the sun started going down on the day we ran the blockade.

The arithmetic went like this: one barge, forty years old, held together mostly by paint and stubbornness. One decoy raft loaded with scrap metal and a cheap transponder Odile had bought off a man who smelled like kerosene and regret. One engine room that I was personally responsible for keeping alive long enough to get us past three naval patrol cutters. And one disgraced engineer named Tomas Reyes, whose last official act aboard a vessel had been to flood one.

You'll notice the arithmetic doesn't include a way out. That's because there wasn't one. There never is, in my experience. The math always comes out to *go forward or go back*, and going back has its own costs, and I've never once been able to afford them.

"Reyes." August's voice through the engine room hatch, flat as a chart table. "How's she running?"

"She's running," I called up, which was true, which was the most you could ever honestly say about any engine, and which August, to her credit, accepted as a complete engineering report.

* * *

Let me tell you about the disgrace, since apparently we're all telling each other our worst stories now, like sailors trading scars. It happened four years ago. I was chief engineer's assistant on the *Meridian Star*, a coastal freighter, and I signed off on a hull patch I should have refused. My boss told me it would hold. The sea told us both otherwise, forty miles out, at two in the morning, in water cold enough to stop your heart before it could drown you.

Eleven men got off that ship alive. Two didn't. I stood on the deck of the rescue cutter in my socks and watched the *Meridian Star* go down stern-first, her lights still on, glowing under the water like something alive being swallowed, and I made myself a promise with the kind of clarity you only get once or twice in a life: never again. Never again open water. Never again responsibility for other people's breathing.

Four years of harbor work followed. Dry dock. Pier-side repairs. Boats that never left sight of land. I got very good at engines and very good at excuses, and I told myself the two skills were unrelated.

Then a woman with a compass and a debt walked into my workshop, looked at my hands, and said, "I hear you're the best engine man on this coast who can't get hired."

"I hear you're the diver who found something the navy wants," I said.

We stared at each other like two people recognizing the same disease.

She offered me wages she admitted she couldn't pay. I asked what the job was. She told me. I laughed, because it was either laugh or start doing the arithmetic again, and then I heard myself say yes, and I've been trying to figure out why ever since. Best theory: she looked at me the way nobody had looked at me in four years — like a man who might be useful rather than a man who had failed. You'd be amazed what people will do for that look. I was.

* * *

Dusk came down over the channel like a lid closing. From the deck of the *Vagrant Grace* — Wren had named her, don't ask, the girl names everything, the dinghy is called *Sir Reginald* — the naval station at Point Alder showed as a smudge of lights to the north, and the patrol cutters were black shapes with white wakes, working their lanes like dogs working a fence line.

Odile stood at the rail with binoculars she absolutely did not need, since she'd already memorized the patrol schedule from sources she declined to name. "Two cutters on the inner lane, one on the outer sweep," she said. "They change stations at full dark. There's a gap of about eleven minutes when the outer boat swings wide around the shoal marker."

"Eleven minutes," August repeated.

"To cross nine miles of open channel at that barge's top speed? No." Odile lowered the binoculars and smiled her smile, the one that was all business and no warmth, though I was starting to learn there was a real person behind it, buried deep, like cargo under ballast. "Which is why the barge doesn't cross. The barge *is*. We set the decoy raft drifting on the inner lane with the transponder squawking, the cutters converge on it, and we take the outer gap while they're busy being heroes."

"And if they don't take the bait?"

"They'll take the bait. Navy salvage fleets are like cats. Show them something moving where it shouldn't be, and they cannot help themselves." She handed the binoculars to August. "It's not foolproof. Nothing is. But it's the best odds anyone's offering tonight."

Wren appeared at my elbow, silent as always, holding a thermos. She did that sometimes — materialized next to whoever seemed worst off and offered coffee without a word. Sixteen years old, maybe, runaway, sharp-eyed, and the only person aboard who treated me like an uncle instead of a cautionary tale.

"You're pale," she said.

"I'm always pale. I'm an engineer. We're cave creatures."

"You're paler than usual." She poured coffee into the cap and held it out. "My mum used to say fear is just your body voting no. Doesn't mean you have to listen to it."

"Your mum sounds wise."

"She sounded wise. She worked for the syndicate." Wren shrugged, and the shrug carried more weight than most people's confessions. "Drink your coffee, Uncle Tomas."

I drank the coffee. It was terrible. It helped.

* * *

The engine room of the *Vagrant Grace* was my cathedral, my confessional, and my coffin, depending on how things went. I'd spent three days in her guts getting the old diesel sorted — cleaning injectors with a toothbrush, chasing a fuel leak that turned out to be a hairline crack in a line some previous owner had "repaired" with what appeared to be chewing gum and prayer. She was a bad old boat. But she was *our* bad old boat, and there's a difference between loving something because it's good and loving it because it's yours.

At 1940 hours, Odile launched the decoy. We watched from the rail as the little raft drifted away into the dusk, its transponder chirping out a false signature — a salvage tender, unregistered, alone. For ten minutes nothing happened. The channel lay flat and gray-violet, and I stood there doing breathing exercises my doctor had taught me after the *Meridian Star*, the ones for panic, the ones I'd sworn I'd never need again.

Then the outer cutter's searchlight snapped on, swept the water, and locked onto the raft.

"There they go," Odile murmured, satisfied as a banker. "Cats."

Both cutters broke formation and converged on the decoy. The outer boat swung wide around the shoal marker exactly as predicted, and for eleven minutes — I counted every one of them against my own pulse — there was a hole in the navy's fence, and we steered straight into it.

August took the wheel herself. Wren stood lookout on the bow, hand raised to signal course corrections. Odile watched our wake like a woman watching for knives. And I went below, because if the engine died in those eleven minutes, none of the rest of it mattered.

Here is what nobody tells you about fear: it doesn't leave. Four years of dry dock, four years of safe harbors, and the moment the hull started to slap open water — real water, deep water, water with nothing underneath it but more water — my body voted no, loud and clear, exactly as Wren's mother would have put it. My hands wanted to shake. My stomach wanted to stage a revolt. Somewhere in the back of my skull a voice that sounded suspiciously like my old chief engineer started reciting the casualty report from the *Meridian Star*, name by name, the way it does when I'm tired or scared or both.

So I did what engineers do. I gave the fear a job.

"Talk to me, sweetheart," I told the engine, and put my palm flat against the block, feeling her run. Two hundred RPM steady. Oil pressure good. Temperature climbing a degree, then settling. Every machine tells you the truth if you listen, which is more than I can say for people, including me. I checked the fuel line twice. I checked the raw water intake. I wiped down the bilge with a rag even though it was clean, because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

And then, at 1953 hours, the engine coughed.

One cough. A single missed beat, like a heart skipping. On any other night I'd have logged it and moved on. Tonight, with nine miles of open channel under the keel and a naval cutter somewhere behind us, it was the most terrifying sound I'd ever heard.

I pulled the injector inspection cover with hands that were, I noticed distantly, completely steady. That's the strange mercy of crisis, if you want to know the truth. Fear stops being weather and becomes fuel. The shaking stopped the moment there was something to fix.

Fuel starvation. A partial clog, sediment stirred up from the tank by the chop — the *Grace* hadn't been properly fueled in months, and her tank had a bottom layer of sludge older than Wren. In five minutes it would clog fully, and we'd drift dead in the water inside a naval patrol lane, and everything August had built, everything Odile had schemed, everything Wren had hoped for would end with us in irons or worse.

I couldn't fix it while running. I had to switch tanks, bleed the lines, and pray the reserve tank was cleaner than the main. Ninety seconds of dead engine. Maybe less if my hands stayed steady.

"Nystrom," I said into the voice pipe. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "Engine's dying. Switching to reserve. Ninety seconds, maybe two. Keep her pointed at the gap."

A pause. Then August's voice, flat and calm as ever: "Do it."

No panic. No second-guessing. Just *do it*, as if ninety seconds of dead engine in a blockade run were a normal Tuesday. Later I understood that this was the whole secret of August Nystrom, the thing that made people follow her into stupid, beautiful danger: she trusted you to do your job, completely, and trust is a load-bearing structure. You find yourself holding things up you didn't think you could hold.

I switched tanks. Bled the primary line — crack the fitting, watch the foam, wait for solid fuel, tighten. Crack, foam, fuel, tighten. The engine turned over, caught, coughed twice, and settled into her rhythm like a sleeper rolling over. Total downtime: eighty-one seconds. I know because I counted them against my pulse, and my pulse was going like a drumline.

Through the hull I heard the searchlight beam pass overhead — I swear you can hear a searchlight, or maybe you can just feel being seen — and then fade astern, chasing the decoy raft, chasing cats.

* * *

We cleared the channel at 2006 hours. Nine miles of open water behind us, the naval cutters still circling a raft full of scrap iron, and the *Vagrant Grace* running clean into the dark toward the coordinates that had gotten us all into this mess.

I stayed in the engine room longer than I needed to. Partly to monitor the fuel pressure. Mostly because I needed a minute with my back against the bulkhead, in the smell of diesel and hot metal, to understand what had just happened to me.

I had been in open water. Deep water. The exact water that killed two men whose names I still keep in a notebook in my footlocker, because forgetting them felt like a worse crime than remembering. And I hadn't frozen. I'd fixed the engine. Eighty-one seconds, and my hands had been surgeon-steady, and the voice in my skull with the casualty list had gone quiet — not because the list was wrong, but because for the first time in four years, I'd had something worth drowning out.

Wren found me there, eventually. She stuck her head down the ladder, saw me sitting on my toolbox, and climbed down without asking. She sat on the opposite toolbox. We looked at each other across the engine block like two parishioners in a very greasy church.

"You did it," she said.

"The engine did it. I just listened."

"You know what I mean." She picked up a rag and started wiping grease off her fingers, though her fingers weren't dirty. Nervous habit. The kid had a hundred of them. "August says we make the dive site by tomorrow noon. First dive's tomorrow afternoon. She wants everyone who can swim to be ready to work the wreck."

Everyone who can swim. Which included me, technically. Which meant, translated out of August-speak, that the disgraced engineer who hadn't touched open water in four years was expected to go down a line into a drowned archive vault, in tidal current, on a wreck nobody had surveyed.

"I'm the engine man," I said. "Engines don't work underwater."

"Tomas." Wren looked at me with those too-old eyes of hers. "You ran an engine through a naval blockade tonight. You bled the lines with the searchlight on your back. You think anyone on this boat believes you're afraid of a little water?" She stood, tossed the rag at me, and paused at the ladder. "Besides. If the pump fails down there, you're the only one who can fix it. So maybe come along and don't let it fail."

She went up. I sat there a while longer, listening to the engine run, doing the arithmetic one more time.

Fear of open water: total. Record as an engineer: ruined. Odds of the dive going wrong: high. Odds of these people — the woman with the compass, the fence with the secrets, the kid with the hundred nervous habits — becoming the closest thing I'd had to a family since the *Meridian Star*: higher still, and rising with every mile.

I'd spent four years believing my record said what I was. Sitting there in the dark with a good engine humming under my hand, it occurred to me that records are written by disasters, and disasters are written by moments, and moments can change.

Tomorrow I'd dive. Not because I was brave. Because they needed the pump to work, and I was the man who kept pumps working, and it turns out that's a kind of bravery — the kind that doesn't feel like anything except showing up.

I climbed the ladder, told August I'd be on the dive team, and watched her nod like she'd known all along. Probably she had. Women like August always know.

What none of us knew — what I couldn't have guessed standing there in the salt wind with my fear finally quiet — was what we'd find at the bottom of that archive. Not gold. Not the treasury. Something smaller, and heavier, and older: a brass dive tag with initials scratched into it, initials that would turn August's whole hunt inside out by morning.

But that was tomorrow's tide. Tonight, for the first time in four years, Tomas Reyes slept on open water — and slept well.
