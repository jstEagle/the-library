## 9. The Drowned Archive

There's a particular kind of silence that lives forty feet down in black water, and it isn't silence at all. It's the sound of your own heartbeat amplified until it becomes a metronome you can't argue with, a small drumming voice insisting *you are alive, you are alive, you are alive* — which is either comforting or accusatory, depending on how the last hour has gone.

Mine had gone like this: four hours of grid searches over a featureless bottom while Wren called bearings through the comm line and Tomas muttered about silt density like a man complaining about the texture of his porridge. The ledger's coordinates put us over a shelf of drowned rock off the old Vessian coast, a stretch of sea the charts marked with the maritime equivalent of a shrug. Somewhere under that shrug, supposedly, was a national treasury.

What we actually found was mud. Miles and miles of ambitious mud.

"Anything?" Wren asked, for the ninth time.

"Fish," I said. "Judgmental fish. They keep looking at me like I owe them money."

"Everything looks at you like you owe it money," Tomas said over the line. "It's your defining quality."

He wasn't wrong. My debts had a collective weight now, a gravity of their own. I'd started thinking of them as a crew member — the one who never dove, never hauled line, never stood watch, but who came along everywhere anyway and ate first.

I was halfway through a sweep of the shelf's eastern lip when the bottom changed. Not gradually, the way seabeds usually lie to you, but all at once, like a page turning. The mud gave way to dressed stone — cut blocks, mortared, laid in courses too regular for nature and too deliberate for a shipwreck. I hovered over it, my light carving a yellow tunnel through the murk, and felt the specific cold that has nothing to do with temperature.

"August?" Wren's voice, careful. "Your breathing just went funny."

"I found architecture."

A pause. Then Tomas, all business: "Describe it."

"Cut stone. Load-bearing walls, or what's left of them. There's a doorway — a real one, hinged frame, though the door's long gone." I drifted lower, gloved fingers brushing a lintel furred with a decade of soft growth. Letters were carved into it, deep enough to survive the silt. I didn't need Wren to read them for me. I'd seen that alphabet once before, stamped in wax on the inside of a dead man's chest lid, in a river at the bottom of my old life.

*Vessania.*

"An archive," I said. "Or a vault. Something with a door worth carving."

The silence on the comm line had a different texture now. Three people, one boat, and a shared understanding that we had just crossed a line marked on no chart. Corvane's fleet was out there somewhere, six tides from this spot — five, now. The smugglers who'd sold me the ledger's existence were out there too, and they didn't send polite inquiries.

"Go in or go home?" Wren asked.

I looked at the doorway. Doorways are honest things. They don't promise what's behind them is good; they only promise it's on the other side.

"Go in," I said, and kicked through.

* * *

Inside was worse than outside, in the way that ruins always are. Outside, at least, the water moves and the light pretends. Inside the archive the silt hung suspended like weather that had forgotten how to be weather, and my lamp beam bounced back at me off a hundred thousand drifting particles, so that I was diving inside my own light, a moth in a jar.

The vault was bigger than the doorway suggested. Long hall, side chambers, ceiling lost in gloom above the reach of my lamp. Shelving — stone shelves, built to last, running the length of the walls in ranks. And on the shelves: nothing.

Not collapsed-nothing, not scattered-nothing. *Swept* nothing. The shelves were clean in the way that only deliberate human effort makes things clean. Someone had come here, years ago by the growth on the fixtures, and taken everything. Document cases, seal boxes, whatever a drowned nation kept in its memory — gone. In their place, a fine grey sediment had settled evenly, a snowfall of time, undisturbed.

"Empty," I reported. "Stripped clean. This happened a long time ago."

"Years?" Tomas asked.

"Ten. Fifteen. Maybe more." I ran my light along a shelf bracket. The iron was rusted to lace, but the stone beneath it was scoured in long parallel arcs, like a floor that had been mopped. "Somebody was thorough. Somebody was *organized.*"

"Treasury's still supposed to be here, though," Wren said. "The ledger points here."

"The ledger points *somewhere* here," I said. "An archive isn't a treasury. It's a library. Somebody emptied the library and left the vault for—" I stopped.

Because my light had found the floor.

The silt on the floor of the central hall wasn't undisturbed after all. There were tracks in it — old, half-healed by settling, but tracks. Boot prints, dozens of them, overlapping, crossing, a choreography of people who had come and worked and left. And near the base of the far wall, where the shelving gave way to a recessed alcove — a cluster of debris that wasn't sediment at all.

I swam to it. The cold came with me.

It was equipment. Old diving equipment, rotted past function, crusted into the silt like fossils. A weight belt with the leather gone to black lace. A helmet — an old hard-hat rig, the brass gone green, the faceplate fogged from inside by twenty years of dark. And scattered among it, small and bright as coins in a beggar's cup: dive tags. Lead discs, stamped with numbers and letters, the kind salvage crews and navy divers wore on a lanyard so the surface could identify what they brought up.

The original team. Whoever stripped this archive had come down in the old way, brass and canvas and brute courage, and they'd left their dead men's shoes behind. Or their living men's spares. Or —

My lamp caught on one tag apart from the others, half-buried at the edge of the alcove where the silt thinned against stone. It was smaller than the rest. Stamped more finely.

I brushed the grey away with my thumb, one slow stroke, and the letters came up out of the dark like bodies rising.

*E.N.*

Elias Nystrom.

My father's initials. My father's name. Stamped on a dive tag lying in a stripped Vessian archive at the bottom of a sea he'd never told me he'd sailed.

The metronome in my ears went wild. *You are alive, you are alive* — but whose life, whose heartbeat, whose voice was I hearing down here, forty feet under a lie twenty years deep? I'd built a whole person out of his absence. A father lost to weather, to debt, to the sea's ordinary cruelty — a story I'd told so many times it had worn smooth as sea glass. And here was the rough edge under all that polish: he hadn't been lost. He'd been *here.* He'd worn this tag on a lanyard and breathed through this brass and swum these halls, and then he'd gone home to a daughter and said nothing, or he'd never gone home at all, and either way —

Either way he'd lied. Or died. Or both.

"August." Wren's voice, distant, underwater in every sense. "Your air. You're at a third."

I looked at the gauge. She was right. I'd been down here burning air like a woman with money to burn, which was the one thing I had never been.

"I'm coming up," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's.

I took the tag. I know that was theft, technically — evidence, salvage rights, all the careful legal fictions we were hiding behind. I took it anyway. Some things you don't leave in the dark, even when the dark is where you found them.

* * *

On the surface, the *Magpie* — Odile's money, Tomas's engines, Wren's charts, and my name on a charter nobody respectable would honor — rocked on a sea gone glassy and gold with late afternoon. I came up the ladder with the tag in my fist and didn't let go until Wren pried my fingers open one by one, gentle as a nurse, and turned the disc to the light.

"E.N.," she read. "That mean anything?"

I told them. All of it — the compass, the ledger, the alias I'd recognized on the chest document back when this was still a crime instead of an excavation, the father who'd walked into weather one October and become a story I told creditors. I'd kept pieces of it back from them, the way you keep a room of your house locked even from people you love. Standing on my own deck with my father's dive tag in my hand, the locked rooms seemed suddenly stupid. Secrets are just debts you pay in loneliness.

Tomas listened the way engineers listen, which is to say he watched the machinery of the story for load-bearing failures. When I finished he was quiet a moment, then said, "So he was a diver. A salvage man."

"Or a thief."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive. I've been both." He turned the tag over. "This rig's twenty years old, maybe twenty-five. Navy pattern, but the stamping's civilian — a contract crew, not military. Your father dove with a private outfit working a Vessian site, back when nobody was supposed to know Vessania existed."

"And then the archive gets stripped clean," Wren said slowly, "and the treasury — if there ever was a treasury — stays lost. And the crew scatters. Or dies." She looked up at me. "August. What if he didn't vanish? What if he *left?*"

The question went into me like a hook. I'd spent twenty years grieving a man the sea took. It had never once occurred to me — not honestly, not in the place where you keep the thoughts you don't say out loud — that the sea might have been his accomplice instead of his killer. That a man could drown himself on purpose, in paperwork and distance, and leave a daughter holding a compass and a bill.

"I need to know," I said. Which was true, and insufficient, and we all knew it. Needing to know is what gets divers killed. You go down for answers, and the water charges you in air and time and sometimes blood, and it doesn't give change.

Tomas set the tag on the chart table, weighted it with a brass fitting, and stepped back like a man leaving flowers on a grave. "The archive's stripped, but the floor tells a story. Those boot tracks converged on that alcove. That's a work site, not a storeroom. They were doing something *at* the wall." He tapped the chart, the shelf of drowned rock, the little pencil X Wren had drawn. "And archives have foundations. Foundations have sub-levels. If there's a treasury, I'd bet the engine room it's not on this floor."

"Your engine room is the only one you've got," I said. "Bet something you can afford to lose."

"Fine. Bet the coffee."

"Deal."

Wren wasn't laughing. She was staring at the tag, at the fine civilian stamping, and I watched her arrive at the thing I'd been avoiding. "The people who stripped this place," she said. "The syndicate didn't exist twenty years ago, not like it is now. But the men who *became* the syndicate — they were around. Odile said her people have been in this water for generations." She looked up. "What if your father's crew and Odile's people are the same story? What if that's why she recognized the ledger?"

I thought of Odile Marchetti on her gilded barge, all lacquer and arithmetic, the way her eyes had gone still and old when she'd seen the Vessian seal — not greedy-still, but *grieving*-still, a woman looking at a photograph of a fire she'd survived.

"Then we're not the first crew to chase this," I said. "We're just the first one she hasn't sold yet."

"Comforting," said Tomas.

"I'm not in the comfort business. I'm in the diving business."

I picked up the tag. It was lighter than it should have been, the way all the things that turn your life over turn out to be — a wax seal, a stamped disc, a compass with worn engraving. Small objects, enormous gravity. I put it in the pocket over my heart, where I keep the things I'm not ready to look at, next to the folded page of the ledger I'd stolen from a dead man's chest.

Below us, through forty feet of gold-green water, the archive waited with its swept shelves and its boot prints and its wall that somebody had once worked at with their hands. Above us, five tides out, a navy fleet steamed toward the same coordinates with better sonar and worse intentions. And somewhere between, on a barge full of silk and secrets, a woman who'd known my father — or of him — waited for my report.

I owed her a call. That was the arrangement: her money, my findings, her cut. And now I had a finding that was also a question, and the question was also a weapon, and I had no idea which end of it I was holding.

"Make for the rendezvous," I told Wren. "We'll report the archive. Empty, stripped, decades old. That part's true."

"And the tag?"

I closed my hand around it. "The tag stays ours. Some debts you settle in private."

Wren put the helm over and the *Magpie* came around, slow and stubborn, like everything else on this boat, like everyone. The sun was going down the color of old brass. Somewhere behind us, in the dark under the dark, my father's initials lay in the silt where I'd found them, and the wall he'd once worked at held whatever it held, patient as stone, keeping its one secret for one more tide.

I just didn't know yet that the next voice out of that dark wouldn't come from the water at all.

It would come from the radio — Odile's voice, silk over wire, saying four words that turned my father from a ghost into a suspect:

*"I knew Elias Nystrom."*
