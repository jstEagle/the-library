## 10. Odile's Arithmetic

There are three kinds of ledgers in this world, and I have kept all of them.

The first is the honest kind — columns of numbers that add up or don't, and when they don't, you pay the difference or you die trying. The second is the kind the syndicate keeps on you: what you owe, whom you owe it to, and how many of your fingers remain as collateral. The third is the one nobody teaches you to keep, the one written in a hand you don't recognize until it's too late — debts of the other sort. Favors. Loyalties. The price of a stranger handing you coffee because they noticed your hands were shaking.

I was taught arithmetic by a man named Brannoc Hale, who ran numbers for the Vessian exiles out of a fish cannery in Port Sallow, and who taught me that every kindness is a loan and every loan is a leash. I was nine years old. I have been doing his math ever since, and I am very good at it, which is precisely the problem.

* * *

The *Vela's Mercy* sat at anchor off Gullwatch Point with her running lights doused, looking from the water like any other derelict waiting for the scrapyard to remember her name. Below decks, in the cabin I'd claimed for myself — the one with the door that locked, which had cost me an argument and a look from August Nystrom that could strip paint — I spread the document flat on my bunk and lit the lamp low.

The ledger page was Vessian work, no question. The paper stock, the iron-gall ink gone brown at the edges, the column headers in that cramped bureaucratic script the Chancellery used right up until the Chancellery stopped existing. I'd spent four years of my youth transcribing documents like this for men who paid in coin and threats in equal measure. You don't forget the handwriting of a dead country. It's like forgetting your mother's voice.

And there it was, in the margin, in a different hand entirely — a notation I'd last seen twenty-two years ago, on a night I've tried very hard not to remember and have failed at with impressive consistency:

**TREASURY MANIFEST — REVISED. SITE TWO CONFIRMED. SEE MARSH TABLES.**

Site two. There had been three sites, originally. Everyone who knew that was supposed to be dead.

I sat with the lamp turned down and did what I do best, which is count.

What the find was worth to me if I sold it clean: the syndicate would pay forty thousand crowns for verified coordinates, maybe sixty if I could confirm the archive vault hadn't been stripped. Enough to buy back my boat outright. Enough to settle the interest that had been compounding against my name since before August Nystrom learned to swim. Enough, if I was careful, to disappear somewhere warm where nobody knew the word *Vessania* and the only thing that ever sank was the sun.

What it was worth to the crew if we saw it through: more than that. Considerably more. Assuming we lived, which the odds did not favor, and assuming the navy didn't take it, which they would try to, and assuming the syndicate didn't take it first, which — well.

That was the flaw in the second column, wasn't it. The syndicate already knew something was moving. They'd known since the chest came up the river, because nothing comes up the river without someone selling word of it, and the someone in this case had been me, or rather the man I'd bought the salvage rights tip from, who reported to people who report to Hale. The machine doesn't sleep. It just waits for its operators to feed it.

I could feed it now, tonight, while the crew slept ten feet above my head. Or I could hold the information back, gamble everything on the crew reaching the treasury first, and pray that when the syndicate came — and they would come — I'd be standing close enough to the gold to negotiate.

Or there was a third option, which was to burn the page, tell them all it was worthless, and sail away poor and alive.

I laughed at that one. Quietly. The walls are thin.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being old in this trade: you start liking people.

It's a professional hazard. When you're young, everyone is a mark or a threat or furniture, and the arithmetic stays clean. But you work with someone long enough — share a deck, share bad weather, watch them do something stupid and brave for no reason you can price — and the columns start bleeding into each other. The ledger gets smudged. You catch yourself doing favors at cost, and then at a loss, and then you wake up one morning realizing you've been keeping two sets of books, and the secret set is full of entries you'd be ashamed to show anyone.

I'd known August Nystrom for eleven days. Eleven days. In eleven days she had lied to my face twice — badly, both times, she has a tell, she touches her left cufflink when she lies, someone should tell her — and hired me anyway, because she needed a fence and I was the only one who wouldn't sell her route inside a week. She trusted me exactly as far as she could throw the *Mercy*, which is the correct amount of trust for this business, and I respected her enormously for it.

And in eleven days she had also done the thing that ruined me, which was this: on the third night, coming back from the naval office with Corvane's questions still hanging over us all like weather, she'd found me awake in the galley at two in the morning and hadn't asked why. She'd just made tea. Two cups. Set one down in front of me without a word and sat across the table working on charts until dawn, and neither of us said anything, and it was the closest thing to company I'd had in six years.

Two cups of terrible tea. I could probably have bought the same comfort in Port Sallow for a few coppers from a woman whose name I'd never learn. Instead it went into the secret ledger at a value I couldn't calculate, which meant, by Brannoc Hale's arithmetic, that it was priceless, which meant it was dangerous, which meant I should get off this boat at the next port and never look back.

I didn't get off the boat.

I told myself it was because of the money. That's the story I'll use if anyone asks. It's even mostly true.

* * *

The courier came aboard at slack water before dawn, the way couriers do — a fishing skiff, a boy of maybe nineteen with salt-cracked hands and the flat eyes of someone who'd been owned longer than he'd been employed. I met him at the rail with the prearranged signal, a lantern shown twice, and passed down the sealed tube with the page copied out in my own cipher hand.

"Marchetti," he said, taking it. Not a greeting. A confirmation. They always say your name like they're entering it into a book.

"Tell the broker the site is real. Tell him the archive was stripped years ago but the secondary reference points to marsh country, south of the Serrat line." I kept my voice level, bored, the voice of a woman discussing freight rates. "Tell him I want terms renegotiated before anyone moves. My percentage doubles if I deliver the location complete."

The boy wrote nothing down. He'd memorize it; they train them young. "And if the broker says the old terms stand?"

"Then tell him Marchetti remembers 1809, and so does he, and I'll take my business to the navy office in Harrowgate before he can spell *defection*."

Something flickered behind those flat eyes — respect, maybe, or the memory of what happened to the last person who'd threatened the broker. "He'll hear it."

The skiff pulled away into the gray. I stood at the rail watching it shrink until the fog took it, and I felt the familiar sensation of having just done the correct thing and the wrong thing in the same motion, which is the only way I know how to do things anymore.

Because here's the part of the arithmetic I left out of the message, the entry that exists only in the secret ledger: I told him the archive was stripped. True. I told him the ledger pointed to marsh country. Also true. What I did *not* tell him — what I held back with both hands, sweating — was the specific coordinates, the tide tables, the fact that the final site was reachable only at the sixth tide through a seamount cave that half the smugglers in the archipelago believe is a sailor's ghost story.

I sold them the shape of the treasure and kept the door.

Brannoc Hale would call that the oldest trick in the trade and he'd be right. He'd also call it suicide, and he'd be righter. The syndicate does not forgive partial deliveries. Partial deliveries are how they know you're thinking. And thinking, in their arithmetic, is theft.

But I'd kept the door back for another reason too, and the reason had a face, and the face had been drinking my terrible tea and calling me *Odile* like I was a person instead of an instrument, and touching her father's compass the way other people touch reliquaries.

Twenty-two years ago I made a choice between people and survival, and I chose survival, and I have been paying for it in a currency that isn't printed anywhere. Tonight I'd made the same choice again, halfway. Sold enough to stay safe. Kept enough to stay human. Balanced on the knife-edge between the two ledgers like a woman walking a spar in a storm, except storms end, and this walk doesn't.

* * *

August found me at dawn. Of course she did. The woman sleeps four hours a night and spends the rest cataloguing everything, including me.

"You were on deck at four," she said, dropping into the chair across from my desk without invitation. She had the chart case under her arm and shadows under her eyes and that look she gets when she's decided not to say the thing she's thinking, which is most of the time. "Talking to a skiff."

"Fishing boat. Buying bait." I didn't look up from the false logbook I was pretending to update. "You run a tight boat, Captain, but I'm allowed to conduct trade."

"We're anchored off a rock with no harbor."

"Then I got excellent bait."

She watched me for a long moment. I could feel her doing the thing she does — weighing, auditing, running her own quiet arithmetic on whether Odile Marchetti was an asset or a liability this week. I let her. There's a strange freedom in being accurately suspected. Nobody expects sincerity from a woman they already believe is a traitor, so whatever I actually feel stays safely invisible.

"The document," she said finally. "The page you recognized. You knew more than you said. At the meeting."

"I knew some things. I said some things. That's called negotiation, Captain, and it's the only reason you can afford Tomas's engine parts."

"And the rest?"

I closed the logbook. Met her eyes, and gave her the truth, or the portion of it that was truest: "The rest is worth more than either of us can carry alone, and less than you think, and I'm still deciding who I'm going to betray with it. When I decide, you'll be the second to know."

"Who's the first?"

"Me." I smiled, and it even reached most of the way up. "I'm always the first to know. It's the advantage of being the least trustworthy person on this boat."

Something moved behind her eyes — not quite a smile, but adjacent to one. "Wren thinks there's writing on the ledger pages that isn't numbers. Marginal script. She says it looks like Vessian shorthand." She stood, tucking the chart case under her arm. "She wants to try translating it tonight. If you know anything about Vessian script, Odile—"

"I know everything about Vessian script," I said, before I could stop myself, and then watched the words land, watched August file them away in whatever ledger she keeps on me, the one I suspect has fewer damning entries than mine does on myself.

"Tonight, then," she said, and left.

I sat alone in my cabin with the lamp burning low and thought about marginal script. Thought about the notation in the corner of the page upstairs in my bunk — *see marsh tables* — and about who had written it, in that hurried hand I'd pretended not to recognize. Because I had recognized it. That was the other entry in the secret ledger, the one written in ink that never dries.

The handwriting belonged to a diver named Elias Nystrom, who had been twenty-six years old and laughing the last time I saw him, standing on a dock at Serrat Point telling me to run, Odile, run, and I had run, and he had vanished, and the world had agreed to call him dead.

His daughter was on the deck above me right now, asking a runaway orphan to translate her dead father's handwriting.

I poured myself the last of the brandy and drank it neat and did the only sum that mattered: if Wren decoded those tables, August would learn her father might be alive. And if August learned that, she'd tear the sea apart to reach him — and the syndicate would follow her straight to the treasury, following the trail I'd already started laying for them.

The knock came at the cabin door before I could finish the thought. Wren's voice, thin and excited through the wood:

"Odile? August says come up. I think I've got it. I think — I think these aren't coordinates. I think they're *tides*."

I looked at the empty brandy glass. Somewhere out past Gullwatch Point, a courier skiff was carrying my name toward men who kept the second kind of ledger, and here was a child about to open the third.

"Coming," I said, and went up to meet my arithmetic head-on.
