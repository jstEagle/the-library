## 11. Wren's Map

I learned to read the way most kids learn to swim — by nearly drowning in it.

The orphanage called itself Saint Verity's Home for Foundlings, which was a lie twice over, since there was no saint and nobody there had ever been found by anybody who wanted them. What it actually was, was a sorting house. The matrons took in children the way a fishmonger takes in whatever the tide brings, and then they sorted us. The pretty ones went one direction. The strong ones went another. And the ones with quick eyes and quicker memories — the ones who could be taught to look at a page of squiggles and tell a man what it said without understanding why he cared — went to a small room off the laundry with a slate, a stub of chalk, and a tutor who smelled like lamp oil and never gave his name.

He taught us languages the way other people taught arithmetic. Vessian was his favorite. He said it was a dead language, which was funny, because everything he taught us was for the benefit of very much alive men who paid by the page.

I was nine. I was good at it. I didn't know yet that being good at a thing is just another way of being useful, and being useful, at Saint Verity's, was the whole of your worth. I thought I was being given a gift. That's the cruelest trick they ever pulled on us — making the exploitation feel like education, so you'd sit up straight and thank them for it.

So when August spread that water-stained ledger across the chart table in the *Kestrel's* cramped cabin and asked, for the third time, if anybody could make sense of the columns of angular script crawling down the margins, I kept my mouth shut and my eyes on my boots.

Not out of modesty. Out of fear. There's a difference, and I've had twenty years to learn it.

* * *

The trouble with secrets is that they don't stay bought. You pay for them up front and then they keep sending you invoices.

I'd been aboard nine days. Nine days of Tomas Reyes muttering at the engine like it was a horse that had wronged him, of Odile Marchetti drinking tea like it was a weapon she was choosing not to use, of August Nystrom counting air tanks the way other people counted rosaries. Nine days of sleeping in a bunk narrower than a coffin and liking it, because a coffin at least is yours.

I'd run from my last berth with nothing but a seabag and a forged discharge paper, and I'd told the crew I was fourteen going on fifteen, which was true in the way that a half-empty glass is full. What I hadn't told them was the rest of it. The sorting. The tutor. The pages I'd read for men whose faces I was paid never to see.

And now here was a book full of the dead language of a drowned country sitting on the table, and everyone staring at it like it was a wall, and me knowing it was a door.

"You keep looking at it," August said. She didn't look up when she said it. She had a way of talking to the middle distance that made you feel like the middle distance had been promoted. "You've been looking at it since we left the harbor. Like it owes you money."

"Everything owes me money," I said. "It's called being young."

Tomas snorted from the doorway, a wrench in one hand and engine grease up to both elbows, which was his version of formal dress. Odile sat in the corner doing something quiet and precise with a ledger of her own, because of course she had a ledger. I was starting to suspect the woman dreamed in double-entry.

August turned a page. The old paper crackled like a fire catching. "The numbers in the main columns I can read — they're in trade cipher, standard salvage notation, the kind any wreck crew used forty years ago. Depths, headings, tonnage. But the margins—" She tapped a column of sharp-angled characters, all vertical strokes and slashes, like a fence someone had built out of knives. "This is Vessian. I know that much. I can't read a word of it. Neither can Tomas. Odile claims she can't."

"I claim many things," Odile said pleasantly, not looking up. "Most of them are even true."

"So either this is decoration," August said, "or it's the part that matters. And in my experience nothing gets written down twice unless the second writing is the one somebody wanted hidden."

The cabin was quiet except for the tick of the lamp and the sea doing its slow percussion against the hull. I looked at the margin script. I could read it the way you read your own name — without effort, without thinking, the shapes just *meaning*, and that was the problem. The moment I read it out loud, I wouldn't be the deckhand anymore. I'd be the girl from the laundry room. The useful one. And useful things, in my experience, get used up.

But there was another thing, and this is the thing I'd been chewing on for two days: the ledger was pointing at a wreck. The wreck was pointing at a treasure. And somewhere in the margins, I was almost sure, was pointing at my father's compass — no. Not mine. August's. The one she wore on a cord like a saint's medal, the one she'd shown us in the cove when the coordinates wouldn't sit straight on any chart. The engraving inside it. She'd said it matched nothing.

I'd looked at it too. And I'd known, the way you know a face across a crowded market, that I *could* read it.

Saint Verity's taught me a lot of things. The first and best of them was that information is the only currency nobody can take off you by force. The second was that you spend it when the price is right, and not a moment sooner.

The price, I decided, watching August's jaw tighten as she stared at letters she couldn't crack, was right.

"It says the site moves," I said.

Everyone turned. I kept my eyes on the ledger, because looking at people while telling the truth is a skill I've never managed.

"The main columns are a dive log — depths, headings, like you said. But the margins are annotations. Vessian script. It's a working document, not a record. Somebody was using it." I pulled it toward me, careful of the wet corners, and put my finger under the first line. "This says: *The door opens on the fifth and the eleventh hour of the ebb, at the turning of the middle tide, and not otherwise.*"

"Opens," August repeated. "A door."

"That's what it says. And this—" my finger moved down, "—this is a table. Tide tables. Days and hours, matched to a place. Except the place isn't written as a name. It's written as a description." I read it slowly, translating in my head the way the tutor had drilled into us, sound first, sense after: "*Beneath the salt marsh, where the old channel drowned, under the teeth of the drowned bell.*"

Silence. The lamp ticked. Somewhere above us, Wren's — my — the *Kestrel's* rigging sang its one long note.

"Salt marshes," Tomas said slowly. "The Vessa Delta. The whole coast there is marsh. Charts show six feet of water over forty feet of mud over God knows what."

"Charts show what people bothered to measure," August said. Her voice had gone flat and fast, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "The ledger's coordinates put the archive vault where we already dove. But this—" she tapped the margin, "—this is a second site. A working site. The archive was the library. This is the vault."

"Or it's a wild goose chase written in a dead language by dead men," Odile said, "designed to send fools into a marsh where the tide eats them." She finally looked up, and her eyes were doing that thing they did, weighing everything and committing to nothing. "The girl reads Vessian. Convenient."

"I'm not convenient," I said. "I'm cheap. There's a difference."

"How," August said, and it wasn't really a question, but I knew she wanted the answer anyway, because August collected truths the way other people collected debts — compulsively, and against her better judgment.

I could have lied. I'd had a lot of practice. But there was something about the way she'd said *neither can Tomas* — like she'd already gone down the crew, one by one, and filed us all under *can't help me* — and something about the fact that she'd asked three times instead of giving up after two. August didn't waste questions. If she'd asked three times, it was because the answer mattered more than the asking.

So I told her the truth. Most of it.

"I was raised in a foundling home that wasn't a home. Saint Verity's. They taught the ones of us with good memories to read dead languages. Vessian, old trade cipher, a couple of others. Then they rented us out. Merchants who wanted contracts read that their rivals couldn't. Collectors who wanted inscriptions translated without questions. Men who wanted letters read in front of them so they could watch your face and see if you understood what you were reading." I shrugged, and the shrug came out steadier than I felt. "I was good at it. Being good at it was the problem. So I left."

"Left," Odile repeated softly, in the tone of a woman who knew exactly how many ways there are to leave a place like that, and how few of them are clean.

"Left," I confirmed.

August was quiet a long moment. Then she did something I didn't expect. She didn't ask how old I really was, or what I'd read for those men, or whether the syndicate had a file with my name in it. She just slid the ledger the rest of the way across the table, until it sat in front of me like a plate of food.

"Then read it," she said. "All of it. Every word. And Wren—" she waited until I looked up, "—whatever it says, however bad it is, I'd rather hear it ugly from you than pretty from anyone else."

And that, more than the wages she'd promised me, more than the bunk and the hot food and the fact that nobody aboard had yet tried to sell me, was the moment I decided the *Kestrel* was mine. You can be bought with coin. You can only be kept with trust. She'd handed me the trust first and hoped I'd notice.

I noticed.

* * *

We worked through the night, the four of us bent over the table like surgeons over a patient, and by the small hours I had the shape of it.

The ledger, it turned out, was two documents wearing one cover. The trade cipher in the main columns was the salvage company's own record — the Vessian treasury recovery operation, forty years back, the one that officially never happened because officially there was no Vessania anymore, just a drowned nation and a lot of embarrassed governments. Depths. Headings. Manifests. And in one column, chilling in its blandness, a list of names: the dive team. I copied them out carefully. Names are important. Names are how the dead stay owed.

The Vessian margins were something else. They were annotations by a second hand — smaller, sharper, angrier. Whoever wrote them hadn't been part of the recovery. They'd been watching it.

"The main vault was emptied," I told them, somewhere past midnight, my throat raw from reading aloud. "That's what the annotations say. The recovery team took what they were told to take. But the margins say the *real* treasury was never in the archive at all. It was moved before the recovery ever started. Moved to a second site. And the second site—" I tapped the phrase I'd already translated three times, wanting to be sure, "—is under the salt marsh. The old channel. The drowned bell."

"What drowned bell?" Tomas asked.

I looked at August. August looked at the chart pinned above the table — the big coastal chart, the one with the Vessa Delta crawling across it like a spill of ink, all those braided channels and mudflats and the little printed warnings: *shifting shoals. unreliable soundings.*

And there, on the oldest edition of the chart — the one Odile had produced from her cabin with a flourish and no explanation, because Odile owned things she shouldn't — there was a marking. A tiny bell symbol, half worn away, printed on the delta's edge where no modern chart marked anything at all. A navigation bell. The kind they used to moor at channel mouths a century ago, before the channel silted and the bell went under and everyone forgot.

"Under the teeth of the drowned bell," I said. "It's not a figure of speech. It's an address."

August sat back. I watched her do the thing she did — the counting thing, the arithmetic of survival, air and hours and tides and debts. I was getting good at reading her. It was like reading Vessian: the shapes looked like nothing, and then all at once they meant everything.

"Six tides," she said quietly. "The navy reaches the wreck in six tides. And if this ledger's right, the wreck was never the prize. The prize has been sitting under a marsh the whole time, and every fleet and every syndicate gunboat on this coast is racing toward the wrong address."

"Then we're ahead of them," Tomas said.

"We're ahead of them," August agreed. "Which means we're alone out front. Which means whatever's under that marsh, we get to it first — and so does whatever comes out of it after us."

Odile smiled her small closed smile and poured herself more tea, and I noticed — because noticing is the other thing Saint Verity's taught me, the thing they never knew they'd taught — that her hand wasn't quite steady.

I didn't say anything. Not yet. I filed it away with the other invoices.

* * *

Near dawn I went up on deck because the cabin had gotten too warm and too full of other people's hope. The sky was doing its gray-to-pink trick over the water, and the wind had a new taste in it, metallic and heavy, the taste of a season turning.

August came up a while later. She didn't say anything at first, just leaned on the rail beside me and watched the water, her father's compass hanging against her chest, turning slowly on its cord with the roll of the boat.

"The tutor who taught you," she said at last. "The one from the laundry room. Did he ever say why Vessian? Why a dead language for a drowned country?"

"He said dead languages were the safest place to keep secrets," I said. "Because the only people who can read them are the people you taught yourself."

August huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Sounds like a man who'd have gotten along with my father."

"Maybe," I said. And then, because the truth was already half out of me and the rest of it wanted to follow, I said the thing I'd been holding since the cove. "The engraving inside your compass. The one that matched no chart. I read it. That night. I could read it and I didn't say anything, and I'm sorry."

She turned. The compass swung between us on its cord, catching the first pink light.

"It's not coordinates," I said. "It's the same phrase from the ledger. *Under the teeth of the drowned bell.* Your father's compass was pointing at the marsh the whole time."

For a long moment August didn't move. The wind came off the water carrying that metallic taste, stronger now, and far to the south — down toward the delta, down toward the marsh — the sky had gone the color of a bruise.

"Then he knew," she said finally, very quietly. "Whatever's down there — he knew. Twenty years ago, he knew."

The barometer on the bulkhead behind us gave a small, sick click, and began to fall.

* * *

By noon the wind had swung a full quarter and the swells had that long, organized rhythm that Tomas, squinting at the horizon over his engine, called "the sea clearing its throat." The *Kestrel* ran south with the marsh chart spread below and the ledger's tide table copied out in my own hand, pinned above the wheel, and every hour the weather took another step toward ugly.

We'd need the tide table to find the drowned bell. We'd need the tide to drop to find the channel mouth. And the tide, the weather, and our luck had all apparently agreed to arrive at the same moment, which is the sea's idea of a joke.

August stood at the wheel with her eyes on the bruised southern sky, and I stood beside her with the ledger's list of names copied in my pocket, and behind us Odile wrote something small and quick in her own book and hid it, and ahead of us the marsh waited with its black channels and its drowned bell and whatever — whoever — had been keeping its secrets for twenty years.

The first drops hit the deck before the marsh was even in sight.

They were warm, and they tasted of salt, and they did not stop.
