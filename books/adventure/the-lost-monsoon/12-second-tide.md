## 12. Second Tide

The storm didn't announce itself. That was the thing about the Vessian coast nobody put in the charts — the weather here had learned to lie from the rocks below it.

I'd been on deck for an hour watching a cloud bank that looked like nothing, and then it looked like everything, all at once, the way a debt collector looks like a neighbor until he's standing in your doorway with paperwork. The barometer fell so fast I could almost hear it. Wren came up the companionway with her hair full of static and her eyes full of questions she'd learned not to ask in the orphanage, because asking got you answers you didn't want.

"That's a wall," she said, pointing at the horizon, which had stopped being a horizon and started being a rumor of one.

"That's a wall," I agreed.

Tomas appeared behind her, gripping the doorframe like it owed him money. He'd been below for six hours coaxing our engine back into a mood, and he emerged looking like a man who'd lost an argument with machinery and knew there'd be a rematch. "If we're thinking about outrunning that," he said, "the engine has opinions."

"What kind of opinions?"

"The kind where it seizes forty miles offshore and we find out how everyone swims."

I looked at the chart, then at the sky, then at the chart again, as if staring hard enough would make the coastline produce options it didn't have. It did have one. A notch in the cliffs about three miles south — the ledger called it *Calder's Teeth*, which is never a name that promises hospitality, but names like that are usually given by people who survived them.

"There," I said, and put the wheel over.

* * *

The cove opened out of the cliff line like a held breath. You'd never find it from seaward unless you already knew — a slot maybe sixty meters wide, guarded by two rock spires that would gut anything drawing more than two meters, with a channel between them narrow enough that I took us through at dead slow with Wren on the bow calling depths and Tomas below decks praying to whatever saint engineers pray to when their handiwork is about to be tested by geology.

Inside, the water went glass-still. The wind was still screaming over the top of the cliffs, but down in the bowl of the cove it was like being inside a cupped hand. Green water, black rock, and — this was the part my brain took a moment to file — a beach. An actual beach, gray shingle, tucked under an overhang, with a stone slipway running down into the water like something built by people who intended to come back often.

And on the beach: a camp.

"Salvagers," Wren said, shading her eyes. "Tents. Look — a compressor. They've got a dive setup."

"Then we've got neighbors." I brought us around to anchor in the lee, and the relief of still water after four hours of green hell was so physical it felt like being set down by someone gentle. Tomas killed the engine and just sat there listening to it tick, the way you listen to a friend breathe after a bad fever.

It wasn't until I was coiling lines that the wrongness arrived. Not all at once. In pieces, the way it always does.

The tents were up. Nobody leaves tents up when they break camp — canvas rots, and salvagers are too poor to rot their own gear. The compressor sat under a tarp, tied down properly. There was a cook fire ring with a kettle beside it. And there were no boats. Three tents' worth of crew, a working camp, and no tender, no skiff, no dory hauled above the waterline.

"Camps don't stay put without boats," I said.

Wren followed my gaze. She was nineteen and had grown up learning to read rooms the way other kids learned to read, and I watched her arrive at the same arithmetic I had. "Maybe they're diving."

"In this?" The storm was still throwing spray off the clifftops in ropes. "Nobody dives in this."

We took the dinghy in anyway, because the alternative was sitting on our boat staring at a question, and I've never been able to afford questions. I left Tomas aboard — he volunteered, loudly, to guard the engine, and I let him have the dignity of the excuse. Wren rowed. She rows like she does everything, quietly and better than she admits.

The beach crunched under our boots. Up close the camp resolved into details, and the details were worse than the shape of it.

A mess table under the overhang, set for a meal that had never been finished. Plates. A tin of biscuits, open, gone soft with weeks of damp. A logbook lying face-down where someone had dropped it mid-sentence.

And blood. Old blood, weathered to a brown-black stain on the shingle, more of it than one body makes. More of it than two.

Wren stopped walking. I heard her breath change.

"Stay by me," I said, which was a stupid thing to say, because staying near me had historically not improved anyone's odds. But she moved closer anyway, and I registered that — filed it away in the small warm drawer I was keeping for things I couldn't examine yet.

The first man was behind the tents. He'd been dead long enough that I'll spare both of us the description; the gulls and the weather had done their accounting. What was left told me what I needed: he hadn't drowned, hadn't fallen, hadn't died of any accident the sea keeps in stock. Someone had done this to him, deliberately, and then left him for the birds.

There were four more. I counted because counting is what I do instead of feeling — air tanks, debts, bodies, it's all arithmetic if you hold your nose. Four more, scattered between the camp and the slipway, arranged like they'd been driven toward the water and cut down short of it. Like someone had wanted them to see the sea they wouldn't reach.

"August." Wren's voice had gone flat, the way voices do when they're holding a lot of weight very carefully. She was standing at the mess table, looking at the logbook. "This is the *Marrow*. The salvage tug out of Corvane's own harbor. I've seen her crew at the fish market. There was a woman who used to give me orange peel—"

She stopped. I let her stop. Some sentences you don't make people finish.

"Weeks ago," I said, crouching by the stain nearest the slipway. Rain had worked at it, tide had licked at it, but the age was readable if you knew stains the way I knew debts. "Three weeks. Maybe four. Before we ever found the chest."

"So it's not about us." Wren said it like she was testing whether the sentence would hold weight. "They didn't even know about us."

"No. This is older than us." I picked up the logbook. The last entry was coordinates — a stretch of the salt marshes, written in a careful hand, with a note beside them: *Second site confirmed. Tell no one until we've lifted.* Underlined twice. The handwriting stopped there.

Someone had told someone. And the someones who'd been told had come here and made sure the telling stopped.

I stood there with rain coming off the overhang in a beaded curtain and thought about Odile Marchetti, who had looked at our ledger document with an expression I'd catalogued as greed and filed away. Greed I understood. Greed spends money. Whatever had happened on this beach, it hadn't spent money. It had spent people, casually, the way you spend matches.

"You're thinking about her," Wren said.

"I'm thinking about everyone," I said. "That's the problem."

* * *

We searched the rest of the camp because leaving without searching is how you die later. Salvage habit: know what's here, know what's missing, know what doesn't fit. Most of the gear was intact — compressors, tanks, tools, a decent winch. Thieves who kill for treasure don't leave treasure-adjacent equipment. Whoever had done this hadn't robbed the camp. They'd erased it.

What didn't fit: a locker at the back of the overhang, padlocked from the outside, and inside it — I broke the hasp with a pry bar, apologizing to no one — a strongbox, empty, its lid hanging open, and a child's shoe.

Just the one. Small. Salt-stiffened. Sitting in the corner of the locker like it had been placed there rather than lost.

Wren made a sound I've only heard once before, from a woman identifying a body, and I closed the locker and put my hand flat on it and said, "Hey. Hey. Look at me." She looked at me. Her eyes were doing the thing where they go somewhere else entirely. "Whoever that belonged to isn't here. This crew was five adults. Five. No children on a salvage contract. That shoe came from somewhere else, and I don't know where yet, but it wasn't this beach."

"You can't know that."

"I can't," I admitted. "But I'm going to choose it, because the alternative is standing here until the tide comes in." Which was, I realized as I said it, exactly what I'd been doing my whole life — choosing the version of events I could carry. Maybe that's not delusion. Maybe that's just ballast.

We took the logbook, the charts from the tent, and a photograph I found pinned inside the galley cupboard: five people squinting into sun on the deck of the *Marrow*, arms around each other's necks, grinning like people who'd found something. One of them was the orange-peel woman. I folded it into my jacket next to my father's compass, because apparently that's where I keep other people's ghosts now. The pocket was getting crowded.

Back at the dinghy, Wren paused with one foot on the shingle and looked back at the camp.

"They were going to tell no one," she said. "Until they'd lifted. And they're dead anyway."

"Yeah."

"Then whoever killed them didn't need them to talk. They just needed them to have known." She rowed us back to the boat in silence, and I watched her shoulders and thought: the orphanage taught her to survive people, and every year the lessons get more useful, and someday I'm going to burn that building down. I added it to the list of things I'd do if I ever had money. The list was getting long enough to be funny.

* * *

Tomas met us at the rail, read our faces in about two seconds, and asked no questions, which is his particular genius. Then he asked one question, because he's also incapable of leaving an engine — or a mystery — unexamined.

"Was it the navy?"

"No," I said. "The navy kills you with paperwork first. This was fast, and it was local, and whoever did it left the equipment. You don't leave a compressor worth eight months' wages unless taking it would tell someone you were here."

"So they wanted the camp found eventually," Tomas said slowly. "Or they didn't care either way. Which means they're not hiding." He rubbed his jaw. "People who aren't hiding are people who think no one's left to hide from."

The storm spent itself against the cliffs through the afternoon, and we sat in the cupped-hand calm of the cove with a dead crew's camp fifty meters away, and I called a crew meeting on the aft deck because I refuse to make decisions in whispers. Whispers are how crews rot.

"Here's where we are," I said. "The ledger gives us a second site under the salt marshes. The *Marrow* found it first, three or four weeks ago, wrote it down, and got killed for knowing it. The navy is hunting the same trail and thinks we're small fish. And somebody else — somebody who isn't the navy, isn't the syndicate's usual muscle, or maybe is exactly the syndicate's muscle and I'm flattering myself — is killing every crew that gets close, and they're ahead of us, and they're thorough."

"Odile knows our route," Wren said quietly.

"Odile knows our route," I agreed. "And I'm going to look her in the eye tonight and ask her about this camp, and watch her hands while she answers, because hands are honest even when mouths aren't. But hear me clearly: I'm not turning this boat around. We are four tides from a treasury that three separate groups of people think belongs to them, and the only group it actually belongs to is dead on that beach. So we finish it. For them, if for no better reason."

Tomas cleared his throat. "For the record, I'd like it noted that 'for no better reason' is carrying a lot of weight in that sentence."

"Noted."

"And that I'm terrified of open water, and this cove is the first place in a month I haven't felt like the sea was reading over my shoulder, and you're asking me to leave it."

"Noted twice."

He sighed the sigh of a man whose objections exist purely for the historical record. "Fine. But I'm rigging the engine compartment with a tripwire. If anyone boards us in the night, they're going to meet a very surprised engineer and a bucket of bilge water."

"That's the spirit."

Wren hadn't spoken. She was sitting on the rail with her arms wrapped around her knees, looking at the beach, and when she finally turned to me her face had settled into something older than nineteen.

"The shoe," she said. "You said it came from somewhere else."

"I said I chose to believe that."

"Vessania emptied its orphanages when it fell," she said, in the tone of someone reciting something they'd memorized young and hated forever. "Nobody talks about where the children went. The good ones got trafficked west. The ones nobody paid for—" She shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "Ledgers have columns for everything, August. Even the ones nobody reads."

I looked at the compass in my pocket, at the beach, at the two of them — an engineer afraid of water and a girl who read dead languages and dead camps with equal fluency — and felt the strange fierce arithmetic of it settle into place. I had spent my whole life counting what I lacked. Somewhere in the last month, without noticing the transaction, I'd acquired a crew.

Which meant I now had things to lose. That was the catch. That was always the catch.

"Tide turns in ninety minutes," I said. "Eat something. Sleep in shifts. Tomorrow we dive the marshes, and we do it faster and quieter than anyone expects, because the people who did that—" I nodded at the shore "—are expecting everyone to be slow and loud. Being neither is the only edge we've got."

They went below. I stayed on deck with the wheel under my hands and the dead crew's logbook open on the chart table, and I read their last entry again, and again, until I noticed what I'd missed.

The coordinates were right. I'd checked them against ours twice already. But the depth notation was different — theirs read four fathoms shallower than the ledger's figure, with a correction scrawled beside it in that same careful hand: *Silt shifts after storms. Trust the bottom, not the paper.*

Trust the bottom, not the paper.

I was still staring at it when Wren's voice came up through the deck plates, thin and sharp from below:

"August. Come look at this. There's someone in the engine room."
