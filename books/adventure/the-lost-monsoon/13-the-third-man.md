## 13. The Third Man

I want it on the record — my record, the one that already has a stain on it the size of a collapsed dry dock — that I checked the engine room twice that night. Twice. Before I even thought about sleeping.

The first time was habit. The second time was because Wren had said, on her way to the fo'c'sle, "Tomas, you've got a face like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like." And she was right. I'd been doing arithmetic all evening. Fuel burn against distance. Distance against tide tables. Tide tables against the fact that somewhere behind us, a navy patrol boat was pretending not to exist and somewhere ahead of us, people were killing salvage crews in coves.

The numbers didn't come out clean. They never do.

So I went down again, third time, lamp in my teeth, and stood in the engine room of the *Magpie's Debt* listening to her heartbeat. Every boat has one. Ours is a forty-year-old diesel that Tomas Reyes personally rebuilt with his own hands after the maritime board took his license, his reputation, and very nearly his will to live. She runs rough. She runs loud. But she runs, and when she runs right, there's a rhythm to her — four beats and a shudder, four beats and a shudder — that I could follow in my sleep.

That night, at 0300 by my watch, the rhythm was wrong.

Not broken. Wrong. A half-beat dragged where it shouldn't drag, a vibration up through the deck plates that felt less like machinery and more like something *loose*. Something that hadn't been loose six hours ago.

I'll tell you what fear feels like for a man like me. It isn't the dramatic kind. It's cold and specific and it starts in the hands. My hands knew before my head did. They were already reaching for the inspection panel over the raw-water intake before I'd finished the thought, and when I pulled it open and put the lamp inside, the light came back to me red.

Water. Not a trickle. Not seepage. Water moving with intent, sheeting down from a joint that had been dry as a sermon that morning, pouring through a fitting whose packing nut had been backed off — backed *off*, three full turns, deliberate as a signature — until the pressure had walked it loose enough to let the sea start making its argument.

Sabotage.

I did what any sensible engineer does. I swore, comprehensively, in two languages. Then I stopped swearing and started working, because that's the other thing about men like me: we're only useful in exactly the moments when everything is going wrong.

* * *

Here's what you need to know about me, so the rest of this makes sense.

Three years ago I was Chief Engineer Reyes, licensed, respected, employed on a passenger ferry with nine hundred souls aboard. Three years ago I signed off on an inspection I should have failed. Not because anyone paid me. Because I was tired, and the paperwork was due, and the bearing *sounded* fine, and I told myself the sea would forgive a man one optimistic signature.

The sea doesn't forgive. The sea keeps books.

The bearing seized forty minutes into open water. Fire in the engine room, dead ship in a shipping lane, nine hundred people evacuated into lifeboats in weather that had no business being survivable. Nobody died. That's the sentence I cling to at three in the morning when the arithmetic gets bad: *nobody died.* But the board didn't care about nobody-died. They cared about the signature. They took the license, and the industry took its cue, and I learned that a disgraced engineer is a thing the world steps around like a puddle.

August found me in a harbor bar arguing with a man about fuel prices, and she didn't ask about the ferry. She just said, "I hear you can keep a dying engine alive," and offered wages she didn't have, on a boat I hadn't seen, for a job she wouldn't describe. And I said yes, because a drowning man doesn't interview his rope.

Now the rope was leaking.

I got the packing nut hand-tight, then jammed it with a wedge and a hose clamp, then stood there in the rising slosh watching the flow slow from a pour to a seep to a grudging dribble, and I understood two things simultaneously. One: whoever did this knew engines. Backing off a packing nut is not amateur work; an amateur opens a seacock and drowns everyone. This was calibrated. Enough water to kill us slowly, or disable us at the worst possible hour. Two: whoever did this was still aboard. You don't back off a fitting and walk ashore.

We were four hours from anywhere, running dark through coastal water, and there was a saboteur on my boat.

I climbed the ladder with my heart going like a piston and woke August first, because that's the chain of command, and then Wren, because Wren sleeps light and wakes faster than anyone I've ever seen, and then Odile, last, deliberately, because I am not a good enough liar to look at Odile Marchetti with this news on my face and keep my opinions out of my eyes.

August heard me out standing at the chart table in her oilskins, hair still wet from sleep, and I watched her go somewhere cold and quiet behind her eyes. When she came back she said just one word: "Sweep."

* * *

You sweep a sixty-foot boat for a stowaway the way you'd clear a building: methodical, top to bottom, lamps and hands and no talking except the necessary kind. August took the bow sections. Wren took midships. I drew the lazarette and the spaces aft, which suited me fine, because it kept me near the engine room and away from Odile, who stood in the main cabin doorway with her arms crossed, watching us tear apart her investment, wearing the expression of a woman who finds the whole exercise faintly insulting.

I didn't like that expression. I want to be fair here — I have no proof of anything, and what happened next complicated every theory I'd built — but I didn't like it.

Wren found him.

I heard the shout come out of her — short, sharp, wordless — and I was moving before I processed it, and I came around the deckhouse in time to see the strangest wrestling match I have ever witnessed. Wren had a hold on a bundle of tarp and netting stacked against the rail by the anchor windlass, and the bundle was *moving*, and then the bundle unrolled itself into a boy.

Not a man. A boy, seventeen maybe, soaked to the skin, lips blue, shaking so hard his teeth chattered like dice in a cup. He'd been wedged between the tarp and the rail since — God knew. Since the cove? Since before the cove? Long enough that the seawater on him wasn't spray. He was hypothermic, half-drowned, and he'd been riding our wake through the same waters where someone had been butchering salvage crews.

Wren had him by the collar with a knife she hadn't been holding five seconds earlier. August arrived a breath behind me, and I saw her take him in — the age, the state of him — and I watched something shift in her stance. The gun stayed holstered. Barely.

"Please," the boy said. Or tried to. It came out mostly as steam and stutter. "Please, I — the water — I couldn't—"

"Who put you aboard?" August asked. Flat. Cold. The voice of a woman counting exits.

He shook his head. His teeth kept chattering. Wren tightened her grip and he made a sound like a kicked dog, and August said, "Wren," quietly, and Wren stepped back a pace but kept the knife where everyone could admire it.

"Below," August said. "Get him warm. Get him talking. Tomas — check everything he was lying against. Everything."

* * *

What he was lying against, it turned out, included a waterproof satchel strapped flat against his ribs under three layers of soaked clothing. I found it while Wren and August stripped him into blankets in the main cabin and Odile — Odile, who had materialized with hot broth from a galley I'd never seen her use — fed it to him spoonful by spoonful with the patience of a saint or a spider.

The satchel was oilskin, military surplus, sealed with waxed cord. Inside: a canvas-wrapped packet, dry as bone. Inside *that*: two sheets of paper and a brass token stamped with a coiled serpent.

The first sheet was a manifest. Cargo codes, weights, delivery points along the coast — syndicate logistics, plain as a laundry list. The second sheet was different. It was written in a cipher — columns of paired characters, grouped in fours — and at the bottom, small and neat and unmistakable once you'd seen it once, a mark.

A circle bisected by a line, with a dot offset inside the upper arc.

I knew that mark. I'd spent a week watching Odile Marchetti stamp it onto her correspondence, her receipts, her charter amendments. It was her personal cipher mark. Her private seal. The thing she told us meant "authenticated, aboveboard, Marchetti business."

It was on orders carried by a stowaway courier working for the people who had slaughtered a camp full of salvagers in Salt Marsh Cove.

I brought the papers up to the chart table without saying a word to anybody, which was cowardice, and I set them down in front of August, which was not. She looked at them for a long time. Then she looked at the mark. Then she looked at me, and I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd been doing since 0300, and arrive at the same ugly sum.

"Get Odile," she said.

* * *

I'll say this for the confrontation: it was quiet. I'd expected shouting. What I got was worse.

Odile came to the chart table drying her hands on a towel, saw the papers, and went very still. Not guilty-still. *Calculating*-still, which from Odile is somehow more frightening, because guilt at least means the answer surprised her.

"Where did you find this?" she asked.

"On the boy," August said. "Strapped to his ribs. Along with your seal."

"My seal." Odile picked up the second sheet, turned it toward the lamp, and studied it with an expression I couldn't read, which from me, given my history, should tell you something about how well I read expressions generally. "This is my mark. It's also a forgery, or it's theft, or—" She stopped. Set the paper down. For the first time since I'd known her, Odile Marchetti looked genuinely uncertain, and it aged her ten years in a second. "August. I need you to understand something. My cipher mark isn't secret. It's *registered*. The syndicate keeps copies of every partner's mark on file. Anyone in their communications office could reproduce it."

"That's convenient," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because both women turned to look at me, and I'm an engineer, not a diplomat.

"It's *true*," Odile said. "Which is different." She straightened, and the uncertainty folded itself away somewhere, packed up neat as tools in a roll. "I sold your route. I admitted that. I did not sell this crew to be killed, and I did not send a half-drowned child aboard this boat carrying my name. Someone is using my mark, August. Which means either the syndicate is cutting me out—" her jaw tightened around the words like a bearing seizing "—or someone inside it wants me to hang for whatever happens to you."

Silence. The engine shuddered underneath us, four beats and a drag, four beats and a drag, my temporary repair holding the way a tourniquet holds.

"The boy," August said finally. "Can he talk yet?"

"He can talk," Odile said. "He's been talking. To me." She met August's stare without flinching. "His name is Pell. He's a courier, third run, terrified, and he was told if the package got wet he was to destroy it and swim for it. He fell off a tender during the transfer at the cove — the night we left. Swam for our anchor light because it was the only light. He's been hiding in the cold ever since, too scared to show himself, too frozen to move."

"You got all that from broth?"

"I got all that from being the person on this boat he wasn't afraid of." Something flickered across her face — too fast to name. "Ask him yourself. But ask him gently. He's a tool, August, not a blade. Somebody else holds the handle."

August looked at the cipher sheet a long moment. Then at Odile. Then at me.

"Tomas," she said. "How long on that repair?"

"Honest answer? Twelve hours if the sea stays kind. Twenty-four if it doesn't. And either way I don't trust that fitting past a day. I need parts I don't have."

"Then we have a day." She rolled the cipher sheet closed and held it like a knife she hadn't decided whether to use. "And somewhere out there, someone is signing my crew's death warrant with Odile's name."

She looked at the mark one more time — the circle, the line, the offset dot — and I saw her arrive at the question that had been sitting in my chest since 0300, the one neither of us wanted to say out loud:

If the syndicate was cutting Odile out — or framing her — then the person giving the orders knew this boat, knew this route, knew this crew.

Knew us from the *inside*.

Underneath our feet, the engine beat on, four beats and a drag, faithful and wounded, carrying us toward the sixth tide with a stranger's name sewn into our sails.
