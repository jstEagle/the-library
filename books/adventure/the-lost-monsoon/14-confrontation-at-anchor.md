## 14. Confrontation at Anchor

I found out the way you find out most things that ruin your week: from a kid who reads too much and trusts too easily.

Wren came up the companionway at dawn with the courier's satchel in both hands, like it was something that might bite. Which, in fairness, it had already bitten all of us.

"August," she said. "You need to see this before Odile does."

"Too late for that," Tomas said from the engine hatch, wiping his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last year. "She's been awake since four. I heard her moving around. People who sleep fine don't move like they're defusing something."

The satchel was waterproofed canvas, salt-stiff, with a brass clasp that had been picked — recently, and not by an expert. Inside: oilskin packets, a cipher wheel the size of a pocket watch, and three sheets of paper covered in a cramped hand that resolved, once Wren held it at the right angle to the rising sun, into orders.

Route confirmed. Vessel *Magpie's Debt*. Departure through the eastern channel, blockade run at dusk, second tide. Site believed to be the old archive vault. Confirm salvage and hold position for retrieval.

Stamped at the bottom, small and neat as a signature: a coiled serpent eating its own tail.

Odile's mark.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about betrayal: it doesn't arrive like a knife. It arrives like arithmetic. You sit down with everything you know about a person — every favor, every laugh, every time they paid for your fuel without being asked — and you start subtracting, and the sum comes out uglier than any of the individual numbers.

I did my arithmetic in the galley while the kettle worked itself up to a scream and nobody drank anything.

Odile Marchetti had bought us the decoy barge. Odile Marchetti had fronted the diesel, the dive tanks, the medical kit with actual morphine in it. Odile Marchetti had also, apparently, sold our route to the syndicate before we'd ever run the blockade — which meant the gunboats that nearly took Wren's head off at the tender line hadn't found us by luck. They'd found us by invoice.

Tomas wanted to put her off at the next port in a borrowed rowboat. Wren wanted — and this surprised me — to hear her side first. "People lie less when they're cornered," Wren said, with the flat pragmatism of someone who'd been cornered professionally by age nine. "They just lie differently."

And me? I wanted the thing I always wanted, which was for the world to be simple enough that a person was either good or bad, and for Odile to come down firmly on one side so I could stop carrying the weight of her on both shoulders.

The world declined to cooperate. It usually does.

We waited until she came up on deck with two cups of coffee, because of course she did, because Odile Marchetti has never once entered a room empty-handed, and I have learned that the generosity of people who owe debts is never free.

"Morning," she said, offering me a cup.

"Set it down."

She set it down. She looked at me over the rim of her own cup, and I'll give her this — she didn't play dumb. She read the room the way she read ledgers, fast and without sentiment, and then she sighed and sat down on the hatch cover like a woman settling in for a long negotiation, which is exactly what she was.

"The boy talked," she said.

"He didn't have to. Your cipher mark talks plenty."

"Mm." She sipped. Somewhere below, Tomas made a noise like a man restraining himself from violence through sheer mechanical interest. "Before you do anything dramatic, I want you to understand that I saved your lives twice this week, and you're only aware of one of those times."

"That's a hell of an opening."

"It's the truest thing I'll say today, so I thought I'd lead with it." She set the cup down and folded her hands, rings clicking against rings. "Yes. I sent word about your route. Yes, the syndicate knew where you'd be running the blockade. And yes, if I hadn't also told them the *Magpie's Debt* was carrying plague quarantine papers — a fiction I invented specifically for the occasion — they'd have boarded you at the channel mouth instead of skirmishing at the edge of it. You lost a tender. You didn't lose a crew. That distinction cost me a favor I'd been sitting on for eleven years, and I'd appreciate some acknowledgment."

"You sold us."

"I sold our *route*, August. Not our destination." Her voice didn't rise, but it sharpened, the way a blade sharpens — same metal, finer edge. "There is a difference, and the difference is the whole point. The syndicate knows we're hunting Vessian material. They've known since the day I took your ledger to my contact in Port Sallow, because that's how partnerships work — you show goods, you get backing. What they do *not* know is where the wreck actually lies, because I kept that back. Kept it back through the blockade run, through the archive vault, through every night you've slept ten feet from me trusting me with your coordinates. The location is still ours. Still mine. Whatever else you think of me, that page of the ledger has never left my body."

I stared at her. "You're telling me you're proud of this."

"I'm telling you I'm *precise*. Pride is for people with clean books." For a moment — half a moment, no longer — something moved behind her eyes that wasn't calculation. Something tired. "You think I don't know what this looks like. A smuggler sells information; water is wet; news at eleven. But look at the arithmetic, girl, because you're better at arithmetic than you let people believe. If I wanted to sell you out entirely — wreck, crew, coordinates, the lot — I could have done it in Port Sallow for more money than this boat earns in a decade, and I'd be drinking something better than this coffee right now on a beach that isn't haunted by navies. Instead I spent my own capital keeping the worst of them off you, and I'm sitting here letting a twenty-six-year-old with negative net worth point a finger at me."

"Because the wreck's worth more than we are."

"Because the wreck is worth more than *anyone*," she said, "and because — " and there it was again, that flicker, gone before I could name it — "because not everything is the wreck."

The kettle screamed. Nobody moved to take it off the heat. It screamed itself hoarse and settled into a rattle, and the sound filled the silence that none of us knew how to fill with words.

* * *

We voted in the cockpit, because the *Magpie's Debt* is a democracy the way a lifeboat is a democracy — everyone gets a say, and then someone has to row.

Tomas went first, arms crossed, jaw set. "Put her ashore. First harbor, whatever harbor, I don't care if it's a fishing village with one dock and a dog. We rig the engine room twice now — once sabotage, once her courier — and both times the thread leads back to syndicate business, and she's the syndicate business we invited aboard. I've been disgraced once for a mistake I didn't even make. I'm not going under again for one somebody made on purpose."

"She didn't rig the engine room," Wren said quietly.

"We don't *know* that."

"We don't know she did, either." Wren sat cross-legged on the deck with the courier's papers spread in front of her like tarot cards. Thirteen years old, and she chaired the argument better than either of us. "Here's what I know. The courier's orders name our route but not our site. The sabotage flooded the bilge but missed the fuel lines — sloppy, or careful, depending on who you think did it. And Odile stood anchor watch alone last Tuesday and had six hours alone with the ledger and copied nothing, because I checked the pages against my memory and every figure's untouched. If she were selling us whole, we'd already be dead. If she were selling us in pieces, we'd be deader. What she actually did was sell the part that was cheapest to give away and keep the part that keeps us alive. That's not loyalty. But it's not betrayal either. It's — "

"Bookkeeping," I said.

"Yeah," Wren said. "It's bookkeeping."

Tomas threw his hands up. "Wonderful. We're keeping a woman aboard because her treachery is *well-organized*."

"We're keeping her aboard because she's the only one among us who can read the syndicate's moves before they make them," I said. "Because she fronted this expedition and can sink it with a telegram. Because putting her ashore anywhere with a telegraph office is how we all end up at the bottom of the channel. And because — " I hated the next part, hated it the way you hate stepping on glass you can see perfectly well — "because she's right. About the route. About the difference. I don't like it. I don't trust it. But I checked the courier's orders against what she told them, and the story holds. She gave them enough to keep her own standing, and not one fathom more."

"So what's the vote?" Tomas said. "Keep her, chained to usefulness?"

"That's the motion," I said.

"On the record," Wren said, "I want it noted that 'chained to usefulness' is the most honest job description anyone's ever had on this boat."

It went two to one. Tomas abstained rather than vote against, which in Tomas-language meant *I'll comply and resent it fluently*, and we shook on it the way you shake on anything these days — briefly, and watching each other's hands.

* * *

I told Odile the terms at dusk, alone, because some conversations shouldn't have an audience.

"You stay," I said. "You keep the location back — all of it, every coordinate, every tide table, every scrap of Vessian script Wren hasn't decoded yet. You don't transmit, you don't signal, you don't leave messages in fish markets or dead-drop buoys or whatever charming method you people use. The next thing you send to the syndicate will be a decision we make together, out loud, on this deck. And the moment I believe you've broken that — not suspected, *believed* — I put you off wherever we happen to be, and I won't lose sleep."

"And if we're anchored off a rock?" she asked.

"Then you'll have excellent company."

Something that was almost a smile touched her mouth and withdrew. "Fair terms. Harsh, but fair — harshness is just fairness with the padding stripped off." She looked out at the water, at the last light going copper along the swells, and for a long moment she wasn't a fence or a fixer or a woman with a serpent stamped on her correspondence. She was just tired, in the specific way of people carrying something heavy for a very long time. "You should know one more thing. Not as leverage. As — context."

"I'm listening."

"When I was younger than Wren, I ran messages for men like the ones hunting us now. I was good at it because I was small and quick and nobody counts a child as a witness. And the first time I sold information that got people hurt, I told myself the same thing I'm telling you now: *I kept the worst of it back. I was precise.*" She turned the ring on her little finger, once, a full rotation, like winding something. "Twenty years later I'm still telling myself that. So when you chain me to usefulness, August — understand that the chain is older than this voyage, and you're not the first hand to hold the other end. You're just the first one honest enough to call it a chain."

I didn't have an answer for that. I'm not sure one exists. So I did what I do instead of answering, which is work — I went forward and checked the anchor rode, which needed checking the way a healthy man needs checking, and I let the dark come down over the deck between us like a curtain neither of us had to acknowledge.

* * *

Wren found me at the bow near midnight. She didn't ask permission; she never does. She just sat down beside me and put the ledger open across both our knees, and pointed at the column she'd been chewing through since the courier came aboard.

"New page," she said. "Deeper in the cipher than we've gone before. It's not cargo weights. It's names."

"Names?"

"Divers." Her finger traced the cramped Vessian script. "A roster. Dates of certification, dates of last dive, and — this column here — disposition. Most of them say the same word. It translates roughly as *lost*. Some say *paid*. Two say *fled*." She paused, and in the moonlight I saw her swallow. "And one entry near the bottom says *active*. The name attached is Nystrom. A. Nystrom. Dated the year my charts say your father's boat went down."

The wind came off the water and moved nothing in me at all, which is how I knew it had hit somewhere deep enough that the surface didn't register.

My father's name. In a drowned nation's ledger. Marked *active* — not lost, not paid, not fled — in the same ink, the same year, the sea supposedly swallowed him.

"August?" Wren said softly. "Your face is doing something I haven't seen it do before."

"Hold onto the ledger," I managed. "And wake Tomas. Change of plans — we're not waiting for the sixth tide to find out what else this book knows."

Because if A. Nystrom was *active* — then somewhere out past the salt marshes, past every chart and every grave I'd mourned, my father's story wasn't closed.

It was still being written. And I intended to read every line.
