## 15. Third Tide

I learned to read twice. Once at the orphanage in Salt Harbor, where Sister Verity taught us letters with a ruler across the knuckles for every mistake — the alphabet came with bruises, so I never forgot it. And once in the linen closet behind the chapel, where a dying old man named Pell hid from the sisters and taught me Vessian script out of a smuggled prayer book, because he said a language was the only inheritance nobody could take off you.

He was wrong about that, mostly. People took things off me all the time. But the language stayed, folded up small in my head like contraband, and for eleven years it had been about as useful as contraband too — something you carry and can't spend.

Until now.

* * *

The ledger sat on the chart table under the lamp, and I sat opposite it, and we regarded each other like two animals deciding whether to fight or mate. It was the third night of the third tide. Outside, the sea did its usual impression of a giant turning over in bed. Inside, the boat smelled of diesel, Tomas's burnt coffee, and the particular staleness of paper that has spent decades underwater and come back up angry about it.

August had handed it to me an hour ago without ceremony. "Wren says she can read it," she'd told the others, in the tone of a woman reporting weather. "So Wren reads it." Then she'd gone topside to stand her watch, because August Nystrom dealt with hope the way other people dealt with hornets' nests: carefully, from a distance, with smoke.

Tomas hovered. He was bad at hovering — a big man, engine-greased, built for standing still next to large machinery — but he hovered anyway, drying his hands on his rag for the hundredth time and pretending to organize tools that were already organized.

"You don't have to babysit me," I said.

"I'm not babysitting. I'm supervising." He set down the rag. Picked it up again. "Also there's nothing else to do on this boat and if I go near the engine one more time tonight I'll start fixing things that aren't broken."

"Fine. Supervise quietly."

He supervised quietly for about four minutes, which for Tomas was a personal record.

The script was Vessian High Register — the formal hand they used for official documents, contracts, church records, the paperwork of a country that had been on maps for six hundred years and then wasn't. The water had gotten into the binding and swollen the pages, so some entries bloomed into illegibility, ink feathered out into grey ghosts. But the ledger had been sealed well enough, and whoever had kept it had written small and neat, the way accountants do when they know the numbers will outlive them.

It was a dive ledger. That much I'd worked out the first night. Columns of dates, depths, durations. Names. Payments. A second column of payments marked with a symbol I didn't recognize yet — a circle with a bar through it, repeated, always in a different hand than the entry beside it.

Names first. I started there, because names are easy and because I wanted to warm up before I got to whatever the circle-bar meant.

* * *

Reading someone's drowned paperwork is a strange kind of eavesdropping. You get their handwriting, which is more intimate than their face. The first diver wrote with a heavy hand, pressing hard enough to emboss the next page: **M. Kessler**. He appeared forty-one times over two years and then stopped appearing, and the payment column beside his last entry was marked not with the circle-bar but with a different symbol, a small diagonal stroke, and after that his name was written once more by a different hand, smaller, in the margin, with what looked like a date and no other comment.

I decided not to think about what the diagonal stroke meant yet. There would be time to think about it later, probably at three in the morning, staring at the deckhead.

The second diver, **L. Okafor**, wrote her own entries in a slanted, hurried script. She dove deeper than Kessler — the depth column climbed past numbers that made Tomas, reading over my shoulder despite explicit instructions, make a low noise like a kettle starting.

"That's past safe air," he said. "Those are past safe air even on good mix."

"She knew," I said. "Look — she writes the times shorter as the depths go longer. She's rationing herself."

Tomas went quiet in the way he went quiet around anything broken beyond repair. He'd told me once, drunk on two sips of the terrible plum brandy Odile kept aboard, about the inspection that ended his career — how he'd signed off on a hull he'd had doubts about, because the yard foreman had a daughter in hospital and the whole crew needed the payday, and how the ferry had held together long enough to dock and then come apart at the pier with everyone already ashore, so officially nothing happened, except officially everything happened, and Tomas Reyes's name went on a list that follows you through every port office in the world. He understood ledgers. He understood being an entry in one.

"Keep going," he said finally. "If she rationed herself, she thought it was worth it. People don't ration themselves for nothing."

The third name entered the ledger fourteen months after the first. The handwriting was different again — quick, confident, letters leaning forward like they were in a hurry to be somewhere. The abbreviation for the first name was just an initial. The surname was longer.

**A. Nystrom.**

I stopped.

Not dramatically. I didn't drop the book or cry out or any of the things people do in stories. I just stopped, the way you stop when you put your foot down on a stair that isn't there. My whole body did the arithmetic before my head caught up, and my body apparently already knew the answer, because my heart was going like I'd swum the channel.

"Wren?" Tomas said.

"A. Nystrom," I said.

"Yeah?"

"The diver. Her name is August Nystrom."

Tomas stood very still. Somewhere above us, boots crossed the deck — August on watch, pacing her thoughts into order, the way she did. The lamp hissed. The sea said its one long word.

"It might not be—" Tomas started.

"It's dated." My finger was on the entry and I hadn't told it to move. "Here. Look at the year notation. Vessian calendars ran eleven days ahead of ours after the Reckoning, everyone knows that, Pell drilled it into me because he thought calendar drift was fascinating, which tells you everything about Pell." I did the conversion in my head, the little translation machine whirring, and the number it produced was a number I knew. Everyone who'd ever heard August talk about her father knew that number. The year Elias Nystrom's boat went down with all hands. The year August stopped being somebody's daughter and started being everybody's problem, including eventually mine.

The entry was dated three weeks before the wreck.

"He was alive," I said. "Three weeks before the boat sank, he was diving here. On this crew. In this ledger."

* * *

Here is what I knew about fathers, before tonight: mine sold me to the orphanage for a debt and a bottle, in whichever order the debt and the bottle came. I was four. I remembered his hands and the smell of tar and nothing else, and for years I'd built him a whole personality out of those two details, the way you build a fire out of wet wood — lots of smoke, no heat. Eventually I'd let the construction collapse. It was healthier. Sister Verity said so, right before she hit me for asking whether my father ever asked about me.

So I am not sentimental about fathers. I want that on the record. Whatever I felt sitting at that chart table with a dead nation's payroll open under the lamp, it wasn't the soft thing. It was the sharp thing. The thing that says: *here is proof that a man walked into the water and did not walk out, and a girl grew up doing arithmetic with air tanks, and neither of them was told about the other.*

Because that was the part that snagged. Not the grief — grief I keep in good condition, oiled and ready. The part that snagged was the ledger itself. Because A. Nystrom's entries didn't stop.

Kessler's entries stopped. Okafor's entries stopped. Divers in this book stopped, and stopping came with a diagonal stroke in the margin, and I had already decided not to think about that. But A. Nystrom dove eleven times, twelve, thirteen — the dates marching forward through the season, through the year of the wreck, past it, into the next year and the next, the handwriting steady, the depths careful, the durations sensible. Whoever A. Nystrom was, he was not dead in this book. He was employed.

And then, on the final page that still legibly held entries — the page where the ledger simply ended, mid-season, the way books end when the people keeping them stop keeping them — A. Nystrom's last entry had no payment mark at all. No circle-bar. No diagonal stroke. Just the dive data, and beneath it, in that same hurrying hand, a single line of text instead of a number.

I read it four times before I trusted myself to say it out loud.

"What does it say?" Tomas asked. He'd gone pale, which for a man whose face is mostly beard takes effort.

"It says—"

And here's the thing about translating under pressure: your mouth wants to run ahead of your certainty. I made myself go slow. Word by word. The Vessian came up out of the deep storage of me, Pell's voice overlaid on it, *careful, girl, a mistranslation is worse than ignorance.*

"'The vault is moved,'" I said. "'All names to the marsh record. Tell no one the new bearing. If I do not surface, the compass knows.'"

The lamp hissed. Tomas sat down slowly on the tool chest, which complained.

"The compass," he said. "August's compass."

"The compass," I agreed.

"Her father's compass. The one she—"

"Yes, Tomas."

"And the vault is moved, and the new bearing is in the marsh record, and the marsh record is—"

"The salt marshes. Where the second site is supposed to be. The coordinates in the front of the ledger that don't match any chart." I turned back to the earlier pages, the ones I'd skimmed past in favor of the juicy human drama, and there it was in the header of the first section, in the formal register, the phrase I'd translated the first night and filed without understanding: *the marsh record.* I'd assumed it was accounting jargon. A record of marshland assets. Which, technically —

Which technically it was. The asset was a treasury. The marshland was underwater.

"Oh," I said, out loud, to nobody, feeling the whole architecture of it assemble itself in my head with an almost audible click. "Oh, you beautiful paranoid dead nation."

* * *

August came below an hour before dawn. She did it the way she did everything — no announcement, just suddenly present, filling the doorway with that look she wore like armor, the one that said *I'm fine* in forty languages none of them true.

"Well?" she said.

She'd been checking on us every hour. She thought we didn't notice. She noticed everything and thought nobody noticed her noticing; it was the saddest magic trick I'd ever seen, and I'd seen Pell palm a coin for dying children.

"Sit down," I said.

"I'll stand."

"August. Sit down."

She sat. That alone should have warned her. I turned the ledger around on the table so it faced her, and I put my finger on the name, and I watched her read it.

I want to describe her face accurately, because people get it wrong in the retellings. They say she went white, or her eyes filled, or some other theater. What actually happened was smaller and worse. Nothing happened. Every part of her face that could move decided, simultaneously, not to. She became a portrait of herself. For maybe five seconds she was the most still living thing I have ever seen, stiller than the drowned, and then she breathed out through her nose, once, and said:

"That's my father's initial."

"It's more than an initial." I turned the pages to the last entry. "Read the dates. Read all the dates. He didn't stop diving when his boat sank, August. His boat sank and he kept showing up to work. Thirteen more dives over two years. And then the vault gets moved, and he writes this —" I tapped the line, and translated it aloud again, slowly, watching each word land on her like weather: "*If I do not surface, the compass knows.*"

"The compass points wrong," she said. Automatically. Like a prayer she'd been saying her whole life without knowing it was instructions. "The engraving inside. The coordinates that aren't anywhere. We figured — I figured —"

"You figured it matched the site. It does. But it's not the site, it's the *fallback*. It's what he left in case he didn't come up. It's a message, August. Your father wrote you a message twenty years ago and addressed it to a compass, because he couldn't write it to you."

"Why not?" And there it was — the crack, finally, running through the stillness, her voice coming out stripped of all its rigging. "Why wouldn't he just — if he was alive, if he was diving here while I was — why wouldn't he *come home*?"

And I didn't have an answer. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is supposedly my function on this boat — the kid who reads the dead languages, the kid who tells you what the writing says. I didn't have an answer. I had a ledger full of divers who stopped, and a margin full of diagonal strokes I still hadn't let myself think about, and a line about telling no one the new bearing, and a picture assembling itself that I liked less and less the clearer it got.

What I said was: "Maybe home wasn't safe. Maybe *he* wasn't safe. Look at the others, August. Kessler stops. Okafor stops. Half these names stop, and the ones that stop have a mark in the margin, and I haven't decoded the marks yet, but I've seen enough paybooks to know what gets recorded instead of wages when someone doesn't clock out."

Tomas made the kettle noise again.

"So either he ran," I said, "or he was taken, or he hid — and every one of those means the same thing, which is that the people who moved this vault were willing to kill the men who found it. And your father knew it. That's why the message is in a code only a compass could carry. That's why it says *tell no one*. He wasn't hiding treasure from the world, August. He was hiding *you* from the list."

August looked at the ledger for a long time. The lamp burned. Above us the sky was going the color of dishwater, the third tide slackening toward its turn, and somewhere out in all that grey water a navy fleet was steaming toward the same arithmetic we'd just done, and a syndicate, and God knew what else, all of them chasing gold, none of them knowing — none of them *knowing* — that the real cargo of this wreck had been a message in a bottle, and the bottle was brass, and it had been riding in August's pocket for years.

"My father's alive," she said. Quietly. Testing the weight of it, the way you test ice.

"Or was," Tomas said gently. "Twenty years is —"

"Or was," she agreed, and I watched her take that too, fold it away, add it to the crushing column of debts she carried — the woman counted everything, that was her whole tragedy, she counted costs other people hadn't even incurred yet. "Either way, the marsh record comes next. The second site. That's where the trail goes."

"That's where the trail goes," I said.

"And Wren —" She looked up at me then, and for one unguarded second there was no armor at all, just a girl my age, roughly, who'd been orphaned by a lie and handed the receipt. "You did this. You read a dead country back to life in one night. Nobody taught you —"

"Pell taught me. In a linen closet. He's the reason I can do any of this, and he died owing the sisters four months' laundry fees, so if anyone asks where I got my education, tell them it was expensive."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her, which had been the point. She took the ledger, carefully, like it might bruise, and carried it to her cabin, and I heard her bunk creak as she sat down with it, and I knew she'd be reading until the light came up, running her fingers over her father's handwriting, learning his letterforms the way you learn a face.

Tomas squeezed my shoulder on his way aft — one pump, engineer-style, calibrated to the exact pressure that says *well done* without saying anything embarrassing — and then it was just me and the lamp and the margin full of diagonal strokes.

I let myself think about them, finally. All of them. I copied out every name that had stopped, and every stroke beside it, and the dates, and I laid them against the history I knew — the Reckoning, the dissolution, the years when Vessania's ships went down and its archives burned and its people scattered to ports that didn't want them. The strokes clustered in the bad seasons. The strokes came after dives to the deepest columns. The strokes came, sometimes, within days of a payment marked in the circle-bar hand — the second party, the paymaster behind the paymaster, the hand that never dove but always paid.

A crew of divers found a nation's treasury. A second party paid them to move it. And the divers, one by one, stopped surfacing.

Except one. Except A. Nystrom, who saw the pattern early, moved the vault himself, wrote his warning in a compass, and vanished so completely that even the syndicate's ledger lost him.

He hadn't abandoned her. He'd done the only thing the ledger said a smart man could do.

I blew out the lamp and went up on deck, because I needed the wind, and because I'd learned young that some knowledge sits in your body better in motion. The tide was turning. The water in the channel ran backwards, confused, the whole sea briefly undecided — and that was when I saw it, far off to the northeast, hull-down against the greying horizon: running lights in a formation. Not fishing boats. Fishing boats don't sail in lines.

Four lights. Five. Six, as the light grew.

I stood there counting them, doing the newest arithmetic, the one where I converted lights into hours, and I decided — privately, in the linen-closet part of myself where I kept the things that were only mine — that I would decode every remaining page of that ledger before anyone reached us, if I had to read it by lightning.

Behind me, below decks, August was meeting her father for the first time in twenty years, in ink.

Ahead of me, the navy was coming to meet him too.
