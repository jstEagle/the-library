## 17. Salt Marsh Descent

The salt marshes at dawn look like the world giving up on itself. Grey water braided through grey grass, a sky the color of dishwater, and a silence so complete you can hear your own doubts breathing. Wren stood at the bow with the decoded ledger in her hands and said it looked like home. I decided not to ask what that said about her childhood.

"Second site's under the old channel marker," she'd told us the night before, her finger tracing tide tables only she could read. "The Vessans didn't trust vaults. They trusted mud."

"Mud I can do," Tomas had said, and then spent the rest of the evening checking his dive gear three times, because Tomas checks things when he's afraid, and he is always at least a little afraid.

Now we idled into the marsh proper, engine down to a whisper, the hull kissing silt that smelled like centuries of rot doing slow business. The channel marker was a stub of blackened timber leaning out of the water like a drunk leaning on a lamppost, and beneath it, according to Wren's tables, a shaft dropped forty feet into a flooded archive of some kind — not the treasury itself, but its antechamber. Its waiting room. Treasure, it turns out, has bureaucracy.

I suited up in the stern, and my father's compass sat in its pouch against my chest where it always sat, and I tried not to think about what it meant that a dead man's coordinates matched a drowned nation's ledger. There wasn't time to think about it properly. There was never time to think about anything properly; that was the whole shape of my life, an arithmetic problem with no paper.

"Line check," Odile said, taking up position at the reel. She'd been quiet since the cove, since the slaughtered camp, since all of us had started sleeping in shifts with a knife under our pillows. Quiet for Odile was louder than most people's shouting.

"Line check," I confirmed, and gave the tether three hard tugs, and she tugged back, and that was the whole liturgy of trust between divers: *I hold the rope, the rope holds me.*

Wren leaned over the gunwale. "August. The tables say slack water gives us maybe fifty minutes before the current turns. After that, the shaft fills with a silt wall you couldn't cut with a chainsaw."

"Fifty minutes." I spat over the side, which is diver for *understood and unimpressed.* "Plenty."

It was not plenty. It was never plenty. But I rolled off the gunwale into water the temperature of a grudge, and the marsh closed over my head like a book.

* * *

Black water is a lie the ocean tells. People imagine darkness as an absence, but this was a presence — a thick, mineral, absolute dark that pressed on the mask like a hand. My lamp carved a cone perhaps two feet deep before the murk ate it. Below six feet, I was diving by feel and faith, and I've never owned much stock in either.

The shot line went down past the marker stub, weighted with a chain scrap, and I followed it hand over hand while the world narrowed to rope, breath, and the small percussion of my own heartbeat in my ears. Somewhere above, the boat was a rumor. Somewhere below, per Wren and her orphanage-taught script, was a Vessian strongbox full of answers I wasn't sure I wanted.

At thirty feet the rope ended in soft bottom, and I swept my lamp across a plain of silt dotted with the ribs of something wooden — a barge, maybe, or a pier, or just the marsh eating another tree. Crabs scattered from my light like bad ideas. I found the shaft by falling into it.

That's the honest version: I felt the bottom drop away under my fins and suddenly there was nothing beneath me but more dark, and the walls of the shaft whispered past my shoulders, brick or stone furred with weed, going down and down. Forty feet, Wren had said. It felt like four hundred. It felt like the shaft went all the way through the world and came out somewhere worse.

I checked my gauge. Twelve minutes in. Plenty.

At the bottom, the shaft opened sideways into a corridor — a real one, mortared stone, the kind of work you don't find in a marsh by accident. Somebody had built this. Somebody had wanted whatever was down here to stay down here, or stay hidden, or both. My lamp found a lintel carved with characters I now recognized from watching Wren work: Vessian script, tight and angular, like handwriting written by someone angry.

I don't read Vessian. But I've read enough ledgers to know a door marked with an official seal when I see one, and this lintel had one — the same crest as the chest from the river, the eagle-and-wave of a country that maps no longer admitted to having existed.

The corridor ran twenty feet and ended in a collapsed chamber. Part of the ceiling had come down years ago — decades, judging by the silt stratification, which is a fancy way of saying the muck layers looked old — and half-buried under the rubble was exactly what Wren's tables promised: an iron strongbox, chest-high, strapped with bronze bands gone green as pond scum.

My heart did something stupid and hopeful. I told it to sit down.

Digging in zero visibility is archaeology performed by a panicked mole. I worked by feel, clearing silt from around the box's base, testing the straps. One had rotted through. The other held. The box shifted when I pulled — heavy, heavier than empty, heavy the specific way a thing is heavy when it's full of metal or truth. I got my lift bag rigged under it, fed air, and watched the box rise an inch, two inches, freeing itself from the mud with a sound like a kiss being withdrawn.

And that's when the light caught it.

A glint in the rubble beside the box. Small. Metallic. I brushed the silt away and came up with a brass dive tag on a corroded lanyard, stamped with initials: **E.N.**

Elias Nystrom. My father. Stamped in a style I knew better than my own signature, because he'd made mine to match when I was nine, at the kitchen table, with a nail set and a hammer, telling me *a diver who can't be identified is a diver who won't be missed.*

He had been here. In this shaft, in this black water, decades ago, touching this same box, or one like it. The ledger listed him. The compass matched the coordinates. And now his tag lay in the silt like a coin left on a dead man's eyes.

I stood there in the dark with my lungs ticking and let myself feel it for exactly four seconds. Then I clipped the tag inside my suit, took the lift bag line in both fists, and started the long haul back up the corridor toward the shaft, dragging sixty pounds of iron and inheritance behind me.

Twelve minutes gone. Thirty-eight left. Plenty.

* * *

The shaft again. Up is harder than down when you're towing weight — every fin kick buys inches, and the silt I'd disturbed on descent had come back down around me like weather. My lamp showed nothing but suspended clay, a brown blizzard. I followed the guideline by touch, counting knots — Tomas ties a knot every ten feet, because Tomas believes in systems the way other men believe in gods.

Knot one. Knot two. Knot three.

Then the line went slack.

Not heavy-slack, not tangled-slack. *Cut* slack. The end of the rope whipped past my glove with a frayed whisper, and I knew — the way you know a knock at the door is bad news — that this was not chafe. Chafe frays. This was severed, clean, deliberate, a blade's work, done while I was twenty feet underground playing in the mud.

The lift bag dragged at my hands. The dark pressed in. And somewhere above me, at the mouth of the shaft, whoever had cut my line was either leaving or waiting.

Panic is a diver's first drowning. I've watched it happen — good divers, strong swimmers, gone in ninety seconds because their bodies voted without consulting them. So I did what my father taught me at that same kitchen table, over hot chocolate, in the only year of my life I remember him being gentle: *When the water turns on you, August, stop. Breathe. Count. The water gets bored before you do.*

I stopped. I breathed. I counted to ten, and the water, bored, receded a little.

Options, then. Swim blind up a forty-foot shaft with no line, in current that would turn in under half an hour, towing a strongbox. Or drop the box — drop the answers, drop the proof, drop the thing my father had touched — and swim up light and fast and probably alive.

I want to tell you I hesitated for noble reasons. The truth is dumber: I was furious. Twenty years of poverty, of creditors, of a mother who died counting coins, of a father who was a name on a headstone with no body under it — and somebody in the dark thought they could take this from me too. I looped the lift bag's line twice around my forearm, put the box's weight on my shoulder like a debt I refused to refinance, and started climbing the shaft wall by feel, brick by slimy brick, kicking myself upward through a brownout so total I could have been ascending toward heaven or sinking toward hell and had no way to check which.

Ten feet. My regulator rasped. Fifteen. The box swung and nearly tore my arm from its socket. Twenty. The current had begun to notice me, a cold finger pulling sideways, the tide starting to think about turning.

Thirty feet, my lamp — God bless Tomas and his triple-checked everything — guttered, flickered, and died.

Full dark. Full weight. Rising current. A dead man's strongbox chained to my arm.

I laughed. I actually laughed, alone, underwater, in the dark, because the alternative was screaming and screaming costs air. Then I went back to bricks and kicks, one and one and one, my free hand walking the shaft wall like a blind woman reading a very hostile letter.

Thirty-five feet. My shoulder screamed. The box caught on a lip of collapsed brick and I hung there, pinned in the throat of the earth, air ticking down, and I thought — clearly, calmly, the way you think at the end — *so this is where the arithmetic stops balancing.*

Then hands. Hands on my harness, hauling, and a shape resolving out of the murk with a lamp blazing — Wren, half in the water at the shaft mouth, tethered to the boat, small and ferocious, pulling with everything her underfed frame had. Behind her, Tomas's voice carried down the shaft, cracked and terrified and absolutely steady in the way that mattered: "I've got the winch! AUGUST! ON THREE!"

They hauled. I kicked. The box came loose with a grinding shrug, and together — the runaway, the coward, and the fool — we brought all three of us up out of the marsh like a bad omen being born.

* * *

I came over the gunwale coughing half the marsh out of my lungs and lay on the deck while the sky wheeled overhead, grey going gold at the edges, morning arriving late and unapologetic. Tomas stripped my gear off with shaking hands, checking me for damage the way he checked engines — methodically, thoroughly, refusing to accept "fine" until he'd verified it himself.

"The line," I managed. "It was cut. Clean cut. Someone was down there, or on the surface, while I was under."

Odile crouched beside me. Her face did something complicated. "The reel never moved," she said. "Nobody approached the boat. I'd have seen them."

"Then they were in the water."

Silence went around the deck like a passed flask. Wren knelt and wrung out her sleeves. "There's a skiff," she said quietly. "Or there was. When I was pulling you, I saw wake sign near the marker — a hull print in the grass, still settling. Somebody idled out of here not five minutes before you surfaced."

"They knew we were coming," Tomas said. "They knew the site. They waited for you to go down and—" He stopped, because finishing the sentence was worse than leaving it.

I sat up. My arm was going to be a spectacular bruise. My everything else was going to be a spectacular bruise too, given time. From inside my suit I pulled the dive tag and set it on the deck boards between us, and watched them all look at it, and watched Odile look at it longest.

"E.N.," Wren read. "That's—"

"My father." I kept my voice level, which cost me. "He was part of the original team. He dove this shaft. And twenty minutes ago, somebody tried to make sure I never came back up out of it."

The strongbox sat on the deck, streaming marsh water, green-strapped, silent. We all looked at it. None of us reached for it. Some boxes you open right away. Some you stare at first, the way you stare at a door you know is going to change the room behind you.

Tomas fetched the pry bar. Odile said, "Wait," and didn't say why, and nobody waited, because waiting is for people whose lives aren't scheduled by tides.

The lid came up with a groan of twenty-year hinges.

Inside, packed in tarred cloth, were bars of dull yellow bullion — maybe forty pounds of it, a fortune, a war chest, a reason for every corpse we'd found in that cove. And on top of the bars, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, a photograph.

Five people standing on a dock, squinting into a sun two decades dead. Four men. One woman, young, sharp-faced, dark-haired, a cigarette tucked behind her ear like a pencil.

I knew the man second from the left instantly — younger than any photo I owned, whole in a way I'd forgotten he'd ever been. My father, smiling like a man who hadn't yet learned what his treasure would cost.

And the woman with the cigarette, the one with her hand resting on his shoulder like she had every right to it —

I turned the photograph toward Odile, whose face had gone the color of the marsh water.

"That's you," I said.
