## 18. Fourth Tide

There's a particular kind of silence that happens on a boat when everyone is pretending not to stare at a box.

I'd heard it before, once, in a courtroom in Cádiz when the verdict was read out — that held-breath quiet where forty people are all thinking the same thought and none of them will say it. The *Magpie*'s cabin had it now. August at the table with the strongbox between her forearms like she was arm-wrestling it. Wren perched on the chart locker with her knees pulled up. Odile by the door, doing an excellent impression of a woman who had nowhere else to be, which for Odile is the most suspicious thing a person can do.

And me, Tomas Reyes, engineer of nothing, holding a pry bar I'd fetched from my own toolbox because apparently we were going to open this thing with tools instead of sense.

"Nobody's touched it since the marsh?" Odile asked. Fourth time she'd asked. I'd started counting.

"Nobody's touched it," August said. "You were there."

"I was there when you *said*—"

"Odile." August didn't look up. "Sit down or go topside."

That's the thing about August Nystrom that took me a while to understand: she doesn't raise her voice because she doesn't have to. She just states where the floor is, and you either stand on it or you fall off it. Odile sat. It cost her something visible, like watching a cat fold itself into a shoebox.

The strongbox had come up from the black water of the salt marshes wrapped in twenty years of silt and one very bad memory — mine, specifically, the memory of August's dive line going slack and her voice cutting out of the headset mid-word. We'd hauled her up by hand, all four of us on the rope, and for eleven seconds that felt like a fiscal quarter she hadn't been breathing. Then she'd coughed up half the marsh and said, "Got it," and I decided right there that I would follow this woman into genuinely stupid situations for the rest of my life, which is not a decision I recommend to anyone with a functioning survival instinct.

Someone had cut her line. Deliberately. With a blade. That fact sat in the cabin with us too, uninvited, like a relative at dinner.

"Corrosion on the hasp is old," I said, mostly to have something useful to say. "Lock's brass, marine-grade. Whoever built this wanted it to survive salt water for a long time." I turned the pry bar over in my hands. "Which means whoever built it expected someone to come back for it eventually. Or never."

"Engineer's assessment," Wren said solemnly, "is that it's a box."

"I'm being professional."

"You're stalling because you want August to do the dramatic part."

I was. I admit it freely. There are moments in a salvage operation that belong to other people, and a man who spent two years rebuilding engines in a disgrace-shed after his certification board called him *a hazard to himself and others* learns to know which moments those are. This one belonged to August. Her father's initials were somewhere under that water. Her father's name was in a ledger written in a dead language. And whatever was in this box had been worth killing a rival salvage crew over, weeks ago, in a cove we'd sailed out of with our lights doused and our stomachs in our throats.

August set the pry bar against the seam. Looked around the cabin once — at Wren, at me, last and longest at Odile, who met it without flinching, which should have told us something and didn't yet — and leaned her weight in.

Brass shrieked. The hasp gave. The lid came up with a suck of trapped air that smelled like cold iron and old money.

Bullion.

Not coins, not plate, not the romantic stuff of ballads. Bars. Stamped bars, packed in wool gone grey with age, maybe forty of them, each one the size of a brick and heavy as a sin. Vessian hallmarks, the double-crown-and-wave crest pressed deep enough to still be crisp under the tarnish. Even tarnished, even in the flat grey light through the cabin glass, they looked like they were humming.

Wren made a sound I'd never heard from her before — small, involuntary, like a kettle starting. She put both hands over her mouth.

"That's—" I did the arithmetic automatically, because numbers are where I go when feelings get large. Forty bars, call it twelve kilos each at Vessian standard, gold at any port price you liked — "that's more than every debt on this boat multiplied by every year we'll live."

"It's a nation's ransom," Odile said quietly. "Or what's left of one."

August wasn't looking at the gold.

She'd gone still in a way I'd learned to read as *bad*. She'd lifted the top layer of wool aside, and underneath the bars, flat against the bottom of the box, was a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord that had kept its color because it had kept its darkness. She unwrapped it the way you'd handle a bird with a broken wing.

A photograph. And papers. But the photograph first, because August's hands stopped moving entirely when she saw it.

We crowded in. Nobody pretended not to anymore.

It was a dive team. Seven people standing on the deck of some vessel I didn't recognize — a workboat, utilitarian, the kind of boat that exists to be worked and forgotten. They were in old-generation hardhat and early free-dive gear, a museum of equipment I'd only seen in textbooks, and they were arranged with the stiff informality of people who'd been ordered to pose and were doing their best to make it bearable. Someone had written on the border in pencil, faded but legible: *Vessania Recovery Expedition — final survey — [date smudged] — all present.*

Seven faces.

Third from the left was Elias Nystrom.

I knew him from the compass, from the way August's whole posture changed whenever his name came near the conversation, from the single photograph she kept rolled inside her bunk lining that she thought nobody knew about. Younger here. Twenty years younger, obviously — dark-haired where I'd imagined him grey, lean in the way men get when work feeds them better than kitchens do. He was laughing in the picture. Not smiling. Laughing, head tipped back, caught mid-sound, one hand on the shoulder of the woman next to him like they'd been sharing the joke a half-second before the shutter fell.

The woman next to him was Odile Marchetti.

Young. I want to stress *young*, because the Odile standing by our door is a woman assembled entirely out of angles and arithmetic, lacquered and armored and priced by the pound. This girl in the photograph was round-faced, sunburned, hair tied back with what looked like a strip of torn shirt, grinning at Elias Nystrom like he was the best thing the ocean had ever produced. Her diving knife hung off her hip on a lanyard knotted in a style I recognized, because I'd seen the same knot on Odile's gear a hundred times. People don't change knots. Knots are the one thing about a person that stays true.

"Odile," Wren said. Not a question. A confirmation, spoken into the silence like dropping a stone down a well to measure its depth.

Nobody answered her. Odile stood by the door with her arms crossed and her face doing absolutely nothing, and her face doing absolutely nothing was louder than screaming.

August turned the photograph over. On the back, in the same pencil, seven names in a column. *E. Nystrom.* Third from the top. And fourth: *O. Marchetti.*

Below the names, smaller, pressed hard enough to tear the paper slightly, someone had written a single line:

*If anything happens to us, it was the syndicate. Tell no one why.*

The cabin got very small.

I've been in engine rooms during blowbacks. I know what it sounds like when air goes where it isn't supposed to go. That's what the silence was. Pressure in a space built for less.

"Twenty years," August said finally. Her voice was level. That was the frightening part. "Your cipher mark was on the courier's orders. You sold our route. And you were on the original team." She laid the photograph on the table, facing Odile, the way you'd lay down a card you'd been holding for a long time. "You've known since the ledger. Since the fence shop. Maybe since before I ever walked through your door."

"Yes," Odile said.

Just that. No defense mounted, no story deployed, no charm offensive — and coming from Odile, the absence of a performance was more damning than any confession could have been. She always had a story. Stories were her inventory.

"You knew my father," August said.

"I knew your father." Something moved behind her eyes then, the first crack in the lacquer, quickly plastered. "Everyone on that boat knew everyone. That's what a crew is."

"*Was* it a crew? Or was it a job?"

"Is there a difference?" Odile asked, and it came out bitter, and bitter was honest, and honest was new. "Ask your own crew in ten years."

"We're asking now," Wren said softly. She'd come down off the chart locker without anyone noticing — she does that, moves like a rumor — and she was standing beside August, shoulder to shoulder, seventeen years old and immovable. "About you. Right now."

For a moment — I'd swear to this later, and I'd swear it under oath if anyone ever asked — Odile Marchetti looked afraid. Not of exposure. Of something worse. Of the question itself.

"The syndicate killed them," she said. "Five of the seven. The ones they could find." She nodded at the photograph, at the laughing man, at her own younger face. "The paper says tell no one why. I'm the one who made sure no one could. I wrote half those reports myself — the false ones, the ones that sent the searchers to empty water. I buried my own crew so thoroughly that the sea got the credit." Her jaw tightened. "And then I got rich doing it for other people's secrets, because grief is expensive and I'd already sold everything else I had."

"Why tell us now?" I asked. It came out rougher than I meant. "Why not let the box stay shut?"

"Because you were going to open it anyway." She looked at August. "And because a man cut your line in that marsh, and it wasn't navy, and it wasn't mine, and the only people left who care what's in that box are people who were *there* — twenty years ago, on that deck, in that picture." She tapped the oilcloth packet, the papers beneath the photograph that August hadn't unfolded yet. "Read the rest. Then decide what I am."

August's hands hovered over the packet. I watched her weigh it — I watched all three of us watch her weigh it — trust against curiosity, anger against need, the girl in the photograph against the woman at the door.

Outside, the tide was turning. I could feel it in the hull, that subtle shift in the water's grip, the *Magpie* swinging lazy on her anchor as the current reversed. Fourth tide down, two to go, and somewhere out past the point a naval fleet was steaming toward coordinates that might or might not still be secret, and a syndicate with gunboats was doing the same math.

August unfolded the papers.

They were tide tables — Wren leaned in fast, hungry, already reading the Vessian script like it was her mother tongue, which in every way that mattered it was — but beneath the tables, in the same handwriting as the warning on the photograph's back, in Elias Nystrom's hand, was a set of instructions. Final coordinates. A seamount. A cave mouth that opened twice a day and closed like a throat.

And one more line, underlined twice, the pencil pressed clean through the paper:

*Sixth tide. If I am not there, do not come looking. Some doors are meant to stay shut from the inside.*

August read it three times. I watched her lips move on the third pass.

"He's alive," Wren whispered. "He wrote *if I am not there*. That's a living man's sentence. Dead men don't leave conditions."

"Or it's a dead man's vanity," Odile said, but gently, for her.

"No." August folded the papers along their original creases, precise, careful, a woman putting something back together that had been torn for twenty years. She looked up, and her eyes were dry and bright and terrible. "It's a warden's sentence. Somebody's been keeping that door shut from the inside for a long time."

She stood, tucked the photograph into her jacket over her heart like a plate of armor, and gave orders — course, watches, Wren to the tables, me to the engine room to get us ready to run at slack water. All of it calm. All of it exact.

Then she paused at the cabin door, next to Odile, close enough that the two of them could have touched, and didn't look at her.

"When we're clear of the blockade," August said, "you're going to tell me everything. From the beginning. Every name, every report you filed, every lie you buried them under." A breath. "And Odile — you're going to start with how you let me believe, for twenty years, that my father drowned."

She went topside. The hatch banged.

Odile stood alone in the middle of the cabin, in the smell of old wool and older money, and for the first time since I'd known her, Odile Marchetti looked like a woman doing arithmetic she couldn't win.

I picked up the pry bar, said nothing, and went to light the boilers — because whatever came next, we'd need steam, and I've only ever known how to fight with heat and pressure anyway.

Two tides left. One door, waiting to open.

And somewhere ahead of us, a man who'd been dead for twenty years was either waiting at a cave mouth — or making sure no one ever reached him again.
