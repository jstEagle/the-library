## 19. Odile's Confession

There is a particular kind of silence that comes after a photograph has been passed around a table. Not the comfortable kind, not the kind that means everyone is thinking their own thoughts in peace. The other kind — the kind with weight, the kind that presses down on the deck planks and makes the coffee go cold in its cups.

I had been dreading this silence for twenty years.

The photograph lay between us on the chart table of the *Magpie's Debt*, which was, I will note for the record, an absurd name for a boat and exactly the sort of name August Nystrom would choose without ever noticing she'd chosen it. Four faces in the picture. A dive dock somewhere on the old Vessian coast, back when there still was a Vessian coast to stand on. Three men and one woman, all squinting into a sun that no longer existed over a country that no longer existed on any map worth buying.

Elias Nystrom, young, already going grey at the temples even then. The courier whose name I never learned because he died before I had the chance. And me. Odile Marchetti, twenty-four years old, holding a tank valve like it was a bouquet of flowers, smiling like a woman who believed the best thing she would ever do was still ahead of her.

It was. That's the joke. It was ahead of her, and then she did the worst thing instead, and everything after that was just interest accruing on the principal.

"You knew him," August said. It wasn't a question. She'd been looking at my face in the photograph, and at my face above the photograph, doing the arithmetic that divers do — the same arithmetic I'd watched her do over ledgers and air tanks since the day we met. "You knew my father."

"I knew all of them," I said.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about confession: it doesn't get easier with practice. It gets easier to *plan*. You rehearse it in the dark hours, you arrange the sentences like cargo in a hold, heaviest at the bottom, and you tell yourself that when the moment comes you'll simply open the hatch and let it out. And then the moment comes, and you discover that the heaviest things are always the ones you packed first, twenty years ago, and they've settled, and they've fused, and they don't come out clean.

They come out in pieces. So bear with me. I'm an old fence; I know how to move stolen goods, but I have never once managed to move the truth in a single load.

"Vessania wasn't drowned by accident," I said. "You know that much already, or you wouldn't be sitting here. The war took the coast, the treaties took the rest, and the maps were redrawn by men who found the whole business embarrassing. But a country isn't its coastline. A country is its people, and the people didn't drown. They scattered. Refugees, mostly. Tens of thousands of them, washing up in ports that didn't want them, in camps that smelled like rust and despair, while the new governments sat on the old treasury and called it reparations."

Tomas had stopped pretending to work on the engine room checklist. Wren sat cross-legged on the bench with her arms around her knees, making herself small the way orphans learn to make themselves small, except Wren was watching me with those flat, unblinking eyes that saw far too much for her age. August stood at the head of the table with her hands flat on the charts, as if the boat might pitch otherwise.

"The treasury was supposed to fund resettlement," I went on. "That was the treaty language. Gold bullion, bearer bonds, currency plates — enough to rebuild a nation's worth of lives. Instead it sat in a naval vault for six years while committees argued about whose refugees counted as real. So five people decided to stop waiting for committees."

"The dive team," August said.

"The dive team." I touched the edge of the photograph. "Your father planned it. He was the only one of us who could read the vault schematics — he'd done salvage survey work for the navy before the war made him choose sides, and he chose wrong, or right, depending on which government you ask. The courier moved the documents. The engineer — that was Marta, she's not in the picture because someone had to hold the camera — Marta cut the seals. And I was the fence. I was the one who was supposed to turn gold into passage tickets, medicine, land grants, safe houses. That was my whole job. I was very good at it. I am still very good at it, which should tell you something about how often I've used that skill honestly since."

"And the syndicate," August said quietly. "The Meridian Combine. They found out."

"They didn't find out," I said. "We told them. Or — I told them."

The cabin got very still. Even the sea seemed to lower its voice.

"There were four hundred thousand lives in those camps, August. And the Combine came to me with numbers. Real numbers. What they'd pay for the location of the vault, what they'd pay for the seals, what they'd pay for the schedule. Enough to move every refugee family out of the camps and onto dry land with papers and futures. That's what they promised. That's what I told myself they'd do. And I believed it, because believing it was cheaper than the alternative, and I have always been a woman who shops for the cheapest version of the truth."

"You informed," Wren said. Not accusing. Just naming it, the way she named things. It was somehow worse than if she'd spat.

"I informed," I agreed. "I gave them the schedule. I gave them the rendezvous point. I kept back the final coordinates of the cache site, because even then some animal part of me understood that once the Combine had the treasure itself, the witnesses would become a line item. And I was right, God help me, I was right. They took the delivery. They killed the courier on the dock. They put two rounds in Marta through the engine room door of her own boat. Your father dove that night and never surfaced where they were watching, and the world wrote him down as dead, and the Combine closed the file and paid me half of what they'd promised, because that's what combines do — they combine your hope with their profit and hand you back the difference."

August hadn't moved. Her hands were still flat on the charts, but her knuckles had gone white, and I thought — with the strange clarity that comes when you finally stop running from a thing — *she's going to kill me, and I will deserve it, and the tragedy is that she'll be the one who has to live with it.*

"Why," she said. Her voice was level. Leveler than mine deserved. "Why are you telling me this now. Why not on day one, when I brought you the ledger page. Why not before I chained you to usefulness and voted to keep you aboard."

"Because on day one you were a client," I said. "And clients are arithmetic. But then—" and here my voice did something humiliating, something wobbly, and I hated it, and I kept going anyway, because a confession that stops halfway is just another lie with better posture— "then you went and assembled a crew of castoffs and fed them wages you couldn't pay and taught a runaway how to read tide tables, and I watched you build in three weeks the exact thing I spent twenty years telling myself I'd destroyed. A crew. A family that chose itself. And I realized I'd been keeping the truth back not to protect the mission. I was keeping it back because I couldn't stand for you to look at me the way you're looking at me now."

"That's not a reason," August said. "That's a symptom."

"Yes," I said. "Welcome to my entire character. It's symptoms all the way down."

* * *

Wren spoke next, which surprised me. She hadn't uncurled from her bench. Her voice came out small and precise, like a child reading aloud in class.

"The orphanage. Saint Verity's. In the marsh provinces."

I closed my eyes. Of course. Of course she'd gotten there first. Children always find the bodies fastest; they're low to the ground.

"The Combine ran six of them," I said. "Officially charities. Unofficially — labor, trafficking, and a place to disappear children whose parents had asked too many questions about missing money. Your parents, Wren, if you had parents in the records, were probably listed as dissidents. Which in Combine bookkeeping meant 'account receivable.'"

"My mother was a schoolteacher," Wren said. "In Vessania. Before."

"Then she was exactly the kind of person the treasury was meant for," I said, "and exactly the kind of person it never reached. I'm sorry. I know sorry is worthless currency. I've been printing it for twenty years and it has never once held its value."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Then she said, "I'm not forgiving you. I just want you to know I heard it." And she uncurled, and left the cabin, and the door clicked shut behind her soft as anything, and I understood that I had just received the most honest transaction of my career, and that I had come out of it poorer than I'd gone in, and that this was correct.

* * *

Tomas stayed. Tomas, who feared open water more than he feared God, who had a disgrace in his service record the size of a ship's hull, sat down heavily across from me and poured himself the last of the coffee without asking, which in his personal economy was a form of intimacy.

"The syndicate gunboats," he said. "The courier with your cipher mark. The camp we found slaughtered. That's not cleanup. That's a sweep pattern. They're not chasing the treasure anymore, Odile. They're erasing the history of it. Everyone who could testify."

"Now you understand why I sold your route," I said. "Not proudly. But understand it. The Combine gave me a choice twenty years ago and I chose wrong, and they have been reminding me of the terms ever since. When they contacted me after the archive vault turned up, the message wasn't a request. It was a statement of account. My debt is older and darker than yours, August. Yours is measured in credits. Mine is measured in graves, and the interest compounds daily, and the bank holds my ledger, and I have been trying to buy my way out of it for two decades with the only currency they accept, which is more betrayal."

"So stop paying," August said.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out cracked and ugly, like a hull breach.

"Just like that. Stop paying. August, sweetheart, you cannot default on these people. There's no bankruptcy court for what I owe. There's a cove in the salt marshes full of dead salvagers who defaulted."

"Then there's a third option," she said, and her eyes had gone bright and hard in the lamplight, the way they went when she looked at a wreck and saw not a grave but a door. "You said you kept the final coordinates back. Twenty years ago. From the Combine."

"I did."

"And they've never had them."

"They've never had them."

"Then they need you alive," August said. "Which means, for the first time in twenty years, Odile Marchetti, *you* are the one holding collateral." She straightened up off the charts. "So here's what happens next. You're going to send them a message. In your cipher. You're going to tell them the final site is confirmed and that you'll deliver the coordinates yourself, in person, at the sixth tide — because you're a careful woman who doesn't trust ciphers with that kind of value. And then we're going to use you as bait."

The cabin was quiet again, but it was a different quiet. Tomas had stopped breathing. I could hear the sea working against the hull, patient as a creditor.

"You're proposing to sail me into the middle of a Combine fleet," I said, "and trade my life for a tactical advantage."

"I'm proposing to spend you," August said, "the way you've been spending everyone else for twenty years. Except in my version, you get a vote."

And that — that absurd, terrible, generous offer of a vote — is the thing that finally broke me. Not the accusation. Not Wren's flat little sentence. The vote. Because in twenty years of informing and fencing and surviving, no one had ever once offered Odile Marchetti a say in her own expenditure.

I looked down at the photograph one more time. Young Elias, squinting into the sun. Young me, holding that tank valve like flowers, like a future.

"All right," I said. "I'll write the message. But August — when the Combine comes, and they will come, and they will come angry — you should know the last thing. The thing I've never said out loud."

She waited.

"The night they killed the courier and Marta, your father wasn't running from them. He was diving *toward* the cache. To move it. To bury it deeper. He knew what I'd done — I never learned how, but he knew, because he left a message for me in the vault, in Vessian script, and it said: *The debt collector always comes for the one who owes least.*" I slid the photograph across the table to her. "He's been hiding that treasure from the Combine for twenty years, August. And if he's alive — and we both know now that he's alive — then the man who buried it deepest is the man who trusted me least. And I am about to lead his daughter straight to his door."

* * *

Outside, the weather was turning. You could smell it coming off the water — that green, metallic tang of a monsoon building its case. Tomas went aft to check the lines twice, which is what Tomas does instead of praying. Through the porthole I watched August stand at the rail with the photograph in her hand, holding it up against the darkening horizon as if she could make the young man in it step forward out of the clouds.

I sat alone in the cabin with my confession finally spent, feeling precisely nothing like absolved. Lighter, yes. The way a boat rides lighter after you've jettisoned ballast. But ballast exists for a reason, and I had a sudden, sickening intuition that the storm bearing down on us was going to test exactly what kind of vessel I'd left us all in.

Somewhere out past the shoals, a gunboat was reading my cipher. And by morning, everything I loved — and I had finally, stupidly, unforgivably allowed myself to love things again — would be sailing toward it.
