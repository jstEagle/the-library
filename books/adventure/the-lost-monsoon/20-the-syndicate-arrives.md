## 20. The Syndicate Arrives

**AUGUST**

The storm broke an hour after sunset, and the gunboats came with it.

I was on deck when I saw them — three sets of running lights where there should have been none, sliding out of the rain like teeth. No fishing boat runs dark-to-dark in weather like this unless it's hunting something, and nothing hunts in the Saltrow cove except us and whatever Odile's old masters had sent to collect.

"Tomas," I called down the hatch, keeping my voice level the way you do when the people below you are already scared enough. "Kill the lanterns."

He killed them. The cove went black except for the lightning, which was generous with its work that night — strobing the cliffs white, then the water, then the three hulls, closer now, maybe four hundred meters off our stern. Gunboats. Not the navy kind, all angles and authority. These were working boats with working guns bolted to their bows, the kind of vessels that don't exist on any registry because their whole business model depends on not existing.

"Wren." She appeared at my elbow, small and damp and steady, which is more than I could say for my own hands. "Get below. Stay with Odile."

"I can help on deck—"

"That wasn't a request."

She went. I heard her boots on the ladder and then I heard Odile's voice, low, saying something I couldn't make out, and Wren saying something back, sharp, and I decided I didn't have time to parse either of them.

Tomas came up beside me, squinting into the rain. He'd tied his hair back and put on the tool belt he wore even when there was nothing to fix, which I'd come to understand was less about tools and more about having something to hold onto. "Three of them," he said. "We've got one engine that hates us, a tender, and whatever Odile keeps locked in her cabin that she swears isn't weapons."

"Is it weapons?"

"It's absolutely weapons."

"Then wake it up." I put my hand on his shoulder. "And Tomas — the tender first. Get it ready to cast off. If this goes wrong, the tender is how we leave."

"The tender's a dinghy with delusions."

"Everything we own is a dinghy with delusions. That's the crew motto. Go."

* * *

**WREN**

Odile was sitting up when I got below, one hand pressed to the bandage on her side, the other holding a pistol like she'd been born knowing what it weighed. Maybe she had. She looked at me and did the thing she does — that quick arithmetic behind her eyes, weighing me against the situation, deciding what I was worth and where.

"Under the chart table," she said. "There's a case. Bring it up. Carefully."

I brought it up. Inside: two more pistols, a box of shells, and a flare gun, sitting on top of folded papers covered in numbers I recognized. Tide tables. Our tide tables. In her handwriting.

I must have gone still, because she said, "Not now, Wren."

"When, then?"

"When we're not about to die." She took the pistol, checked it, held it out to me grip-first. "Do you know how to use this?"

"No."

"Then hold it anyway. Sometimes the shape of a thing matters more than its function." Her voice was even, but her hand wasn't quite, and I understood suddenly that Odile Marchetti was afraid — actually afraid, in a way I hadn't seen since the marsh — and that frightened me worse than the gunboats did, because Odile knew exactly what those boats were capable of. She'd probably commissioned some of them.

"Those men out there," I said. "They're yours. Your cipher mark was on the courier's orders. So tell me true: will they take prisoners?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Lightning came through the porthole and lit her face white, and for once she didn't look like a woman doing sums. She looked tired. Old, even, though she couldn't have been much past forty.

"They'll take anything they can carry," she said. "Including answers. Including you." She pushed the pistol into my hands and closed my fingers over it. "So don't be carryable."

* * *

**AUGUST**

The first shot came at half past nine, and it wasn't aimed at us. It was aimed at the cliff above us — a warning, or a demonstration, rock splintering down into the water close enough to pepper our deck. Then a voice through a loudhailer, flattened by rain and distance but perfectly legible in its meaning:

"*Marchetti. You know the terms.*"

Odile came up on deck then, pale, one arm wrapped around her ribs, and stood next to me in the dark like we were two women watching a fire across the harbor. "That's Brannoc's voice," she said. "He runs the syndicate's wet work in this stretch. We've done business. He'll have orders to bring the ledger and everyone who's read it."

"And you?"

"He'll have orders about me too." She smiled without any humor in it at all. "Twenty years ago I was worth saving. Now I'm a loose end with excellent bookkeeping."

The second shot took our tender.

No warning that time — just a flat crack from the lead boat and the dinghy, tied astern, coming apart in a bloom of orange flame that lit the whole cove like a stage. The heat of it reached us across thirty feet of water. Tomas made a sound next to me that I felt rather than heard, and I understood: that ugly little boat with its patched hull and its engine that started on the third prayer was our way out, and Brannoc knew it, and had removed it in one shot with the calm of a man crossing an item off a list.

"There goes the crew motto," Tomas said hoarsely.

"We were never going to outrun three gunboats in a tender anyway," I said, and it was almost true, and I let myself believe it because the alternative was screaming.

They came alongside ten minutes later. Grapnels over the rail, boots on our deck, six of them with rifles and rain-slick jackets, moving with the bored efficiency of men who had done this many times and found it dull. Brannoc himself stayed on his boat — a big man in a yellow slicker, face shadowed under the hood, only his voice reaching us, unhurried as a harbormaster's.

"Ledger," he said.

"Ledger's not aboard," I said.

"Girl." A sigh. "Everyone lies. It's the first thing we check for."

What happened next happened fast, and I've turned it over so many times since that it plays in pieces now, out of order, like cards shuffled by a bad dealer. One of the boarders grabbed Odile by the collar and she went with him, compliant, murmuring something — and then the flare gun was in her hand from somewhere inside her coat, and she fired it point-blank into the man's chest, and the world went red and screaming. Another boarder swung his rifle toward her. Toward her, past her —

— toward Wren, who had come up the hatch behind them with no plan and no invitation, carrying the pistol Odile had given her, and I saw the muzzle track past Odile's shoulder and find the smaller target, the easier one, the girl.

I've been in fights before. Diving teaches you a certain economy of violence — everything underwater is a fight, against current, against panic, against the simple murderous indifference of pressure. But I had never moved the way I moved then. I crossed that deck and got between the rifle and Wren and drove my shoulder into the shooter's chest, and we went down together onto the boards, and the shot went high and took a fist-sized hole out of our mainmast instead of out of a sixteen-year-old girl.

After that it was noise. Tomas came up the hatch swinging a pipe wrench with a war cry that would have embarrassed a librarian. The burning tender lit everything in stuttering orange. Odile fired her second flare into the rain for no tactical reason whatsoever, pure theater, and Brannoc's men — professionals, professionals hate chaos — began to lose their formation. Someone cut our grapnel lines. Someone else fell overboard with a splash and a curse. And then Brannoc's voice again, from the darkness beyond the rail, still unhurried, which was somehow the worst part:

"Keep the boat tonight. Keep the girl too, if she matters to you. But the tide turns at dawn, Nystrom, and after dawn there won't be a cove for you to hide in. We know where they're going. We've always known." A pause, and then, softer, almost kind: "*Ask Marchetti how we know.*"

The gunboats withdrew into the rain. The tender burned itself down to a black skeleton. And we stood on our scarred deck among spent flares and scattered shell casings, five people breathing hard in the lightning, and nobody said anything for a long time.

* * *

**TOMAS**

I hit a man with a pipe wrench tonight. Full swing, both hands, the way you'd swing at a rusted coupling, and he dropped like cargo, and I stood over him shaking, and the strange part — the part I'll never tell anyone sober — is that for one clear second I wasn't afraid.

Me. Tomas Reyes, whose naval career ended because his hands froze during a flooding drill while a man named Okonkwo drowned twenty feet away from him. They gave me a discharge and a diagnosis and a lifetime of open water turning my stomach, and tonight I swung a wrench at a armed man on a pitching deck in a firefight and my hands did not freeze.

I keep thinking about why. Best answer I've got: Okonkwo died alone, in the dark, while I watched. Nobody on this boat is going to die alone in the dark if I can help it. Not August, who carries debts like ballast and pretends they're nothing. Not Wren, who is sixteen and has already survived more than I did in thirty years. Not even Odile, who sold us out and saved us in the same hour and is currently sitting against the mast looking like a woman doing final arithmetic on herself.

August found me afterward, coiling line in the rain because my hands needed a job. She didn't say anything about the wrench. She just worked beside me for a while, and then she said, "You were braver than your record tonight."

"My record's accurate," I said. "Tonight was an outlier."

"Every brave thing anyone's ever done is an outlier. That's what makes it brave." She tied off the coil and looked out at the black water where the tender used to be. "We lost our escape. We lost the element of surprise. By dawn there'll be three gunboats and a naval fleet converging on the same coordinates, and the sixth tide is two days out, and I'm standing here realizing—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—realizing the fleet deadline is starting to look like the merciful option. At least Corvane wants to arrest us. Brannoc wants to erase us."

"So what do we do?"

"We run the gauntlet at slack water tomorrow. Fifth tide. Straight through both of them." She said it lightly, the way you'd announce a change in dinner plans, and I saw her hands weren't steady, and I saw that she was going to do it anyway.

"August," I said. "If I freeze out there—"

"You won't."

"But if I do—"

"Then I'll unfreeze you." She clapped me on the shoulder, hard, diver's grip. "Get some sleep, Reyes. Tomorrow we outrun the entire ocean."

* * *

**ODILE**

Below decks, alone with my ledgers, I finally read the courier's orders properly — the ones we took off him in the flooded engine room, the ones with my cipher mark that I swore I'd kept the wreck's location out of.

I had. Mostly. The route, yes, I sold — the cove, the timing, the shape of our run. That was the price of staying alive inside the syndicate's memory, and I paid it the way I've paid every bill for twenty years: quickly, and telling myself it cost less than it did.

But there was a second page I hadn't decoded. Brannoc's own addendum, in his hand, under my mark. And it said, in the flat language of men who move money and bodies:

*Subject M's cooperation assumed exhausted. On recovery of site contents, subject M is to be retired. Asset status: negative.*

Retired. Twenty years of information, of introductions, of looking the other way at the right moments, and the sum total of my account with these men is a bullet with my name pre-written on it.

I should feel betrayed. Instead I feel something worse: seen. Because the syndicate's arithmetic has always been my arithmetic, and tonight, watching that boy swing a wrench and that girl stand her ground and that stubborn Nystrom woman put her body between a rifle and someone else's child, I caught myself doing new math. The kind where the assets are people. The kind where I am very deep in debt.

Above me, footsteps crossed the deck — August, pacing, planning, already three moves ahead of a woman who taught her everything she knows about being three moves ahead.

Dawn comes early. And after dawn, the fifth tide.

I closed the ledger on my own name and went to tell her everything.
