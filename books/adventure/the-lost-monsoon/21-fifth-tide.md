## 21. Fifth Tide

Slack water is a lie the sea tells twice a day. For maybe twenty minutes, between the tide going out and the tide coming back in, the channel between the Serrat shoals goes almost calm — almost being the operative word — and every smuggler, navy man, and professional idiot with a hull knows it's the only window to run the gauntlet.

We were, by process of elimination, the professional idiots.

"Five minutes of slack left," Wren said from the bow, where she'd appointed herself human radar on the grounds that her eyes were younger than everyone else's and she liked having something to yell about. "Four if you count that cloud."

"I'm choosing not to count the cloud."

"You're counting the cloud."

I was counting the cloud. Behind us, the syndicate gunboats sat at anchor off Cape Verren like three black teeth in a gray jaw, close enough that I could see the glint of glass on their bridges. They'd lost us in the night skirmish two days ago — lost our tender, too, along with most of my savings and a fair chunk of Tomas's dignity — but "lost" was a generous word for what they'd done. They hadn't found us yet. There was a difference, and the difference had a name, and the name was slack water.

"Tomas," I called down the hatch. "Tell me something beautiful about the engine."

The engine answered first, a cough and a rattle that sounded like an old man clearing his lungs before a speech he didn't want to give. Then Tomas's head appeared, grease-streaked, one goggle pushed up into his hair. "She'll hold."

"She'll hold?"

"At cruising speed, absolutely. At the speed you're about to ask for, she'll hold *angrily*." He wiped his hands on a rag that was already more grease than rag. "There's a difference. The difference is what happens after."

"After we're through the shoals, she can fall apart all she wants."

"That's the spirit." He vanished below again, and I heard him talking to the engine in low, coaxing tones, the way you'd talk to a horse before asking it to jump something it had every right to refuse.

Odile stood at the rail amidships, arms folded, watching the syndicate boats with the flat expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. She'd been quiet since the confrontation at anchor — quieter than guilt usually makes people, which I'd learned meant she was still deciding something. I'd chained her usefulness, not her wrists; the crew had voted, and the vote had been closer than I told her. But a chain you can't see is still a chain, and she wore hers without complaint, which somehow made it worse.

"They'll have pickets in the channel," she said, not looking at me. "Not the gunboats. Smaller craft. Skiffs with engines and men with rifles who get paid whether they shoot straight or not."

"You'd know."

"I would know." No flinch. That was the arrangement now: she gave me the truth raw, and I swallowed it raw, and neither of us pretended it tasted fine. "Two pickets, probably. Maybe three if the syndicate's nervous, and the syndicate is nervous, because someone sank their tender and made them look foolish in front of the navy."

"We made them look foolish."

"*You* made them look foolish. I merely provided the intelligence that allowed it, which is the sort of distinction that gets people killed in my former profession." She turned then, and there was something underneath the dryness — not quite apology, not quite fear. Something older. "August. If we don't make the sixth tide window—"

"We'll make it."

"If we don't, there isn't another for eleven hours, and by then this whole coast will be crawling with every gun that floats. So whatever happens in the next twenty minutes, the coordinates matter more than the boat. You understand? The boat is replaceable. The ledger—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, as if she'd said one word too many.

"The ledger isn't the point, Odile."

"No," she agreed softly. "But it's the map to the point. And maps are the only things I've ever been any good at keeping safe."

Wren yelled from the bow: "Slack! It's slack NOW!"

I put the wheel over.

* * *

The Serrat channel is a mile of water that hates you personally. The shoals rise on either side like the ribs of something enormous that lay down here centuries ago and never got up, and the sandbars shift with every storm, so no chart older than a season can be trusted. We went through at full throttle with Wren calling depths off the bow and Tomas begging the engine in two languages and me steering by a combination of instinct, memory, and the specific flavor of dread that tells you a sandbar is coming before your eyes find it.

The first picket was exactly where Odile said it would be — a skiff tucked into the lee of the northern shoal, engine idling, two men aboard pretending to fish. Nobody fishes the Serrat channel. The fish there are mostly bones and grudges.

"Hold course," Odile murmured. "They're watching for a boat that turns away. A boat that comes straight at them looks like a local running home before the tide turns."

"A local," I repeated. "In a salvage tug with a patched hull and a crew of four criminals."

"Every boat out here is a criminal's boat. That's what makes the disguise work."

The skiff grew. Fifty yards. Thirty. One of the fishermen stood up, unhurried, and shaded his eyes, and I saw him take in our silhouette against the graying dawn — and I saw the moment he decided we were somebody else's problem. His hand came down. He sat back down. Twenty yards later we were past him and his rifle stayed in its case, and I let out a breath I felt like I'd been holding since chapter one of my life.

The second picket was less polite.

It came out of the southern fog bank at speed, a low black skiff with three men and no pretense of fishing, and the man in the bow had a rifle that was not in its case. Bullets skipped off the water ahead of us — warning shots, or bad aim, and with hired guns you never know which is worse.

"Wren! Get down!" I hauled the wheel hard to starboard, putting the shoal between us and them, which meant putting the shoal between us and *not dying on rocks*, which is the kind of trade you make at speed and audit later.

"They're flanking south!" Wren shouted, flat on the deck plates, voice muffled. "They know these waters better than we do!"

"Nobody knows these waters! That's the entire point of them!"

Tomas came up the hatch like a man ascending into weather he'd been promised wouldn't happen. "If you keep turning like that, the engine—"

"Will hold *angrily*, yes, I remember!"

A bullet rang off the rail six inches from his hand. He looked at it, then at me, with the wounded expression of an engineer whose specifications had been violated. Then he did something I will credit him for until the day I die: he went back down the hatch, and thirty seconds later the engine made a sound I had never heard it make, a deep tearing roar, as if Tomas had reached inside and reminded it of something it used to be.

We pulled away. The skiff tried to follow through the shallows and paid for it — I heard, rather than saw, the grind of a hull kissing sand, and then a great deal of profanity carrying across the water in a dialect I didn't recognize and Wren, from the deck, translated with relish: "He says the tide is a thief. He says several other things about the tide's mother."

"Fog bank's ahead," Odile reported. "Once we're in it, we're smoke."

We were smoke. The fog took us the way fog does on that coast — all at once, like a door closing — and the gunfire behind us became a rumor, and then not even that.

* * *

We ran blind for an hour, dead slow, listening. That's the part nobody romanticizes about running blockades: after the adrenaline spends itself, there's just the fog, and the engine ticking as it cools, and four people on a small boat discovering how loud silence is when you've nearly died in it.

Wren made coffee. Bad coffee — she made it the way she did everything, with more enthusiasm than technique — and we drank it in the cockpit while the compass swung lazy circles under its glass.

That was when I noticed it wasn't swinging lazy. It was holding steady, needle angled off true north by a stubborn fifteen degrees, pointing southeast into open water. Pointing, when I checked it against the ledger's decoded coordinates spread out on the chart table, exactly along our heading.

"It's done that before," Wren said quietly. She'd seen me looking. Of course she had; the girl missed nothing except social cues, and only sometimes those. "Back at the archive site. I thought it was the iron in the wreck."

"There's no iron out here. Nothing but water and us."

"Then it's not pointing at iron." She wrapped both hands around her mug, which was too big for her, the way everything was too big for her except her opinions. "My father had a watch like that. Broken, he said. Except it always knew when the boats came back to the harbor. He'd stand on the dock and it would tick different, right before the fleet came in." She shrugged, embarrassed by her own sentiment. "Probably nonsense."

"Probably," I said, and didn't believe it, because I'd spent twenty years telling myself the same kind of probably-nonsense about the compass my father left me, and because the needle was pointing at a seamount cave that existed on no chart, that could only be entered at the sixth tide, that my father had engraved into the case back when I still believed he'd come home.

Elias Nystrom had known where this treasure was before anyone alive should have known. And the compass — his compass, the only thing of his I owned — apparently wanted me to go there as badly as I did.

"August." Odile stood at the chart table, and her voice had changed. Not the fence's voice, not the confessor's. Something careful. "Fog's thinning. And we have company."

Company. My stomach dropped toward the bilge. "Syndicate?"

"No." She tilted her chin toward the fog wall off our port quarter, where a shape was resolving — long, low, gray, riding high and purposeful. A naval patrol boat, running lights dark, moving parallel to us at a respectful half-mile. "Navy."

Corvane.

* * *

Here's what I knew about Lieutenant Corvane, and it wasn't much, but it was strange enough to be useful. He'd offered me immunity for cooperation in a room that smelled of mildew and official menace, and when I refused, he'd let me walk. He'd had every reason to detain us at the blockade and instead his patrol boat had been conspicuously elsewhere. Twice now he'd found us and twice he'd fired nothing. In my experience, a hunter who doesn't shoot is either incompetent or thinking, and Corvane was many unflattering things, but I'd sat across a table from him, and incompetence wasn't among them.

"He's herding us," Tomas said. He'd surfaced again, wiping his hands, squinting at the gray shape in the thinning fog. "Or escorting. With that type of vessel, the difference is classified."

"Why would the navy escort us?" Wren asked.

"Because the navy doesn't want us," Odile said slowly, and I watched her arrive at the thought the way you watch someone step onto ice and hear it hold. "The navy wants the treasury. But Corvane's superiors want the affair buried — I've dealt with governments before; burial is their favorite form of recovery. If the crew dies at sea, the questions die with them. If the crew lives and delivers..." Her mouth thinned. "Then Corvane becomes a loose end too."

"So whose side is he on?" Wren asked.

"His own," Odile and I said together, and despite everything, despite the fog and the rifles and the eleven-hour clock ticking toward the sixth tide, I almost laughed. It was the first time Odile and I had agreed on anything without a negotiation, and it turned out what it took was mutual cynicism.

The patrol boat held its distance all morning. It shadowed us through the fog breaks, fell back when we altered course, closed again when we steadied — never closing enough to board, never falling far enough back to lose us. Once, briefly, I raised my binoculars and caught a figure on the bridge, tall, hands clasped behind his back, watching us through his own glasses. Even at half a mile I could feel the weight of it. Corvane wasn't hunting us. He was *waiting* to see what we'd become.

"Should we run?" Wren asked.

"On what? We're making eight knots and he makes twenty-two." I lowered the glasses. "We ignore him. If he wanted us dead, we'd already be a report."

"And if he wants us *found*?" Odile asked softly. "Followed to the cave, so his government can take what we bleed for?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Every mile we ran, we ran for him too, laying our wake straight toward the last secret my father had kept. I looked at the compass on the chart table, needle steady, patient, certain, and I thought about Elias Nystrom engraving coordinates into brass twenty years ago, trusting some future version of me to read them. He'd trusted the wrong daughter, maybe. Or the right one. I hadn't decided yet.

"Then we lose him at the seamount," I said. "Fog, current, and a cave entrance that exists for ninety minutes a day. If Corvane follows us in, he follows us into water even his charts don't admit exists."

"And if he shells the entrance after we're inside?" Odile asked.

I met her eyes. "Then he'd better hope we find what we're looking for, because I intend to come back out, and I hold grudges the way other people hold assets."

Something flickered across her face — approval, maybe, or its guilty cousin. "Spoken like a woman with nothing left to lose."

"I have plenty left to lose. That's the problem. I've recently acquired a crew." I nodded at Wren, at Tomas leaning in the hatchway, at the whole improbable, unpaid, unbought collection of them. "Which is why we're going to do this cleanly. Fifth tide got us through the gauntlet. Sixth tide opens the door. Between now and then, nobody talks to anybody, nobody trusts anybody, and everybody watches the horizon."

"What about me?" Odile asked. Dry as driftwood.

"You especially."

* * *

The final coordinates put us off the Vessian Shelf by late afternoon, over water so deep it went from green to black in the space of a breath, as if the sea had simply given up pretending to be shallow anywhere nearby. The seamount rose out of that blackness on the sonar like a fist — a drowned volcano's shoulder, charted by no one, rising to within forty feet of the surface at its crown.

And on its seaward face, according to the ledger's tide tables, at the peak of the sixth tide, a cave mouth would stand open. Ninety minutes. Then the sea would close it for another day, and after that, if the tables were honest, for a season.

We anchored in the lee of the shelf as the light went copper, and I stood at the stern and watched the patrol boat do the same, five miles out, a gray sliver against the copper, holding its position like a promise or a threat.

Tomas cooked. Actual food — rice and tinned fish and the hot sauce he hoarded like bullion — and we ate on deck because none of us could stand the belowdecks silence. Wren spread the ledger pages out and traced the Vessian script with one finger, mouthing the words, building her translation of the final passage piece by piece. Odile sat apart, as she always did now, but not far apart, and once I caught her watching Wren work with an expression I couldn't price.

"Tomorrow," Wren said, around a mouthful of rice. "High water's at 0640. Cave's open by 0700, shut by 0830. After that—" she tapped the tide table, "—nothing. Eleven days of nothing."

"Then tomorrow morning we go in," I said.

"And whatever's in there," Tomas said quietly, "has been waiting twenty years."

"Not the treasure," I said. The compass sat in my pocket, and I found I was gripping it through the fabric. "Someone's been living in that cave. The ledger's margins — Wren's translation — the supply notes, the dates. Someone's been *keeping* it."

"You think it's your father." Odile said it without inflection, which was her kindness, when she chose to offer any.

"I think the compass points at that cave like it's homesick. I think my father's initials are on a dive tag in a stripped archive. And I think tomorrow morning I'm going to swim into a hole in a seamount and find out whether I've been chasing gold or a ghost." I looked out at the darkening water, at the gray sliver of Corvane's ship holding its vigil, at the black line of the shelf where the cave waited behind forty feet of ocean. "Either way, at 0700, the door opens."

Below us, the fifth tide turned, and the sea began its long pull toward the sixth — and somewhere in the dark, patient as brass, the compass pointed the way home.
