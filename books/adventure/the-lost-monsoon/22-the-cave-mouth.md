## 22. The Cave Mouth

The sixth tide didn't announce itself. It just started breathing.

We hung off the seamount's shoulder at slack water, engines idling low enough that the whole boat felt like it was holding its breath with us. Below us, maybe forty feet down on the seaward face, a fissure ran into the rock like a crack in a tooth — invisible unless you knew to look for it, and we only knew because Wren had spent two days arguing with a dead Vessian accountant's handwriting until the ledger gave up its secret.

"There," she said, pointing past the rail at a smear of darker blue in the cliff face. "The mouth opens at high stand. The ledger says the door breathes twice a day and swallows anyone who argues with it."

"Cheerful book," Tomas muttered. He'd been cheerful in that particular Tomas way all morning — which is to say he'd checked the dive lines four times, rechecked them, and then quietly retied one knot nobody had asked him about.

I was already suited up. I'd been suited up since dawn, mostly because taking the suit off felt like admitting we might turn back, and I'd decided somewhere around the third tide that turning back was no longer a thing this boat did.

"Rules," I said, and the crew gathered the way crews do — half listening, half watching the water. "Flooded cave, zero natural light past the entrance, single line in and out. Nobody touches anything. Nobody wanders. If your light dies, you follow the line with your hands and you don't panic, because panicking burns air, and air is the only currency down there."

"You've given this speech before," Wren said.

"I've given cheaper versions of it to people who didn't come back. Consider yourselves upgraded." I clipped my gauge console where I could see it. "Tomas, you're topside. Don't argue."

His jaw did the thing it always did when his record got mentioned without anyone mentioning it. Since the blockade run he'd been diving anyway — short dives, shallow, white-knuckled, but diving — and every time he surfaced he looked faintly astonished at himself, like a man who'd found money in an old coat. But a cave dive was a different animal, and we both knew it, and I wasn't going to spend his courage for him when I might need it later.

"Someone has to mind the boat," he said finally. "And someone has to mind the charges."

"And you're the only one who can do both without blowing us into weather. That's not a demotion, Reyes. That's trust."

He grunted, which from Tomas was practically a blessing.

Wren kitted up beside me, smaller frame swimming in borrowed gear, her braid stuffed down the back of her suit with the grim efficiency of someone who'd learned young that loose hair and machinery don't negotiate. She was sixteen going on forty, and she'd been quiet all morning in a way that wasn't fear. It was something closer to recognition.

"You okay?" I asked.

"My orphanage was funded by Vessian money," she said. "Odile told me. The ledger lists it — a disbursement, year after year, like clockwork. Somebody stole a nation's treasury and some of it bought beds for kids like me." She cinched a strap. "So whatever's down there, it's not just gold. It's receipts."

I thought about that all the way down the ladder.

* * *

The water at the cave mouth was cold in the way deep water is cold — not hostile, exactly, just indifferent to your plans. We dropped along the cliff face with the surge tugging at us like a dog on a leash, and there it was: a vertical fissure, six feet wide at the lip, narrowing as it went down, exhaling a slow pulse of colder water as the tide pushed through some hidden lung deeper in the rock.

The ledger hadn't lied. The door breathed.

I went in first, lamp beam cutting a cone through water so clear it felt rude, like reading someone's mail. The fissure angled down and then leveled out, and the walls opened around us by degrees — and then, all at once, we weren't in a crack anymore. We were in a cathedral.

The beam found stone pillars, flowstone curtains hanging like frozen waterfalls, a ceiling lost in black above us. And below, sloping gently upward out of the dark, an air pocket — a vast one, its surface trembling silver in our lights, reflecting the vaulted rock back at itself so the cave seemed to go on forever in both directions.

Wren's hand found my arm and squeezed. Even through neoprene I felt it.

We surfaced inside the mountain.

Our lamps swept across still black water, and the sound of our own breathing came back off the walls a half-second late, like the cave was considering whether to repeat us. Then the beams climbed the far wall and stopped being interested in geology.

Crates. Dozens of them, stacked in careful ranks on a stone shelf above the waterline, each stenciled with the same crest I'd pulled out of the river weeks ago — the crowned wave of Vessania, a nation erased from every map but apparently not from every warehouse. Beyond the crates, sealed strongboxes in rows. Beyond those, under oilcloth, shapes I couldn't name but my gut could: the long silhouettes of crated rifles, cases stamped with ordnance marks, the kind of cargo that doesn't get stolen to be admired.

A treasury. Not coins in a chest. A war, packed for shipping, twenty years late.

"Holy hell," Wren breathed, and the cave took her voice and gave it back smaller.

I should have felt rich. Standing in a room containing more value than every debt I owed multiplied by every debt I'd ever heard of, I felt — nothing much. Cold. Tired. The arithmetic part of my brain, the part that had kept me alive through ten years of condemned wrecks and creditors' arithmetic, went strangely quiet, the way it does when the numbers stop fitting on any page.

Then my lamp swung left and found the shack.

* * *

It stood on a shelf of flat rock near the air pocket's edge, and it was, absurdly, a home. Timber frame scavenged from wreck planking, roofed with patched canvas and sheet copper gone green at the seams. A rain-catchment gutter fed a cistern. A cot with a real mattress. Shelves of jars. A wood stove, vented through a crack in the ceiling that must have taken someone months to cut, its pipe running up into darkness.

And on the stove — I want to be precise about this, because precision is what saved my sanity in the next sixty seconds — sat a kettle.

Not a rusted relic. Not a prop. An enamel kettle with a dented lid, sitting on a stove whose firebox, when I crouched and held my palm near the damper, still breathed warmth.

"Wren," I said. My voice came out flat, which was the best I could manage. "How fresh?"

She crossed to the stove, and I watched her do the thing she did — the survivor's inventory, cataloguing exits and threats before beauty. Her eyes went from the kettle to the firebox to the floor, and stopped on the floor.

"August."

Footprints. In the fine silt dusting the stone shelf, dozens of them, overlapping — old ones faded to ghosts, new ones sharp-edged, a man's boots, large, the tread worn smooth on the outside edge the way boots wear when their owner favors one leg. The trail led from the waterline to the shack and back, over and over, years of it compressed into a single palimpsest. Someone walked this shelf every day. Someone had walked it recently enough that the prints hadn't finished softening.

"Hours," Wren said. "Maybe less. The kettle's warm, August. There's tea in the cup." She pointed, and there was — a tin mug on the crate that served as a table, a skin of steam still ghosting off it.

My heart was doing something complicated that I chose to interpret as anger, because anger was simpler than the alternative.

Nobody lives in a cave for twenty years. Nobody keeps a kettle warm on a stove they built from wreck timber unless they intend to stay, and stay, and stay. And nobody — my brain supplied, unhelpfully, in my father's voice — nobody learns to build a stove vent through living rock except a man who'd taught me, on his knee, in a boathouse that smelled of tar and coffee, how a flue draws.

"Elias Nystrom dove this site," I said aloud, because saying it made it a fact instead of a hope. "The ledger lists him. A. Nystrom — that's what Wren found. But the A—"

"The A was for Augustus," Wren said softly. "Your grandfather's name. He signed the crew manifest with it. Your father hated being called it — you told me, that night at anchor, remember? You said he'd sooner answer to a boot than to Augustus."

I remembered. I remembered everything suddenly, all at once, the way you do when the ground shifts: him teaching me knots with names like songs. Him leaving on a salvage contract when I was nine, waving from the rail of the *Cormorant*, promising a doll from port. The letter. The flag. The grave with no body in it that my mother had insisted on anyway because grief needs furniture.

Twenty years. Twenty years of a warm kettle.

"He's alive," I said. It didn't sound like joy. It sounded like an accusation, and I suppose that's what it was.

Wren put her hand on my shoulder again and didn't say anything, which is the most useful thing anyone said to me that day.

* * *

We did what professionals do, which is work while shaking. We photographed the stacks. Wren counted crates and matched stencil numbers against the ledger's manifest, her lips moving, her finger tracking, and every match landed like another brick in a wall between the girl who'd been sold by an orphanage and the nation that had paid for it. Two hundred crates of small arms. Bullion strongboxes — I pried one lid a quarter inch and saw the dull yellow promise inside and shut it again fast, the way you'd shut a door on a room where something was crying.

The treasure was intact. That was the thing I couldn't get past. Every faction on the coast had hunted this place for two decades — navy, syndicate, every salvager with a debt and a dream — and here it all sat, ordered and oiled and guarded, guarded so well that the guarding had grown a garden. There were tomato plants. Actual tomato plants, in salvaged buckets under a canvas skylight, ripening in lamplight. Whoever lived here had decided, at some point, that if he was going to hold a door against the world forever, he was going to eat well doing it.

That detail undid me more than the rifles. You don't grow tomatoes for a vigil. You grow tomatoes for a life.

"August." Wren's voice, low, from the shack doorway. "There's a journal. On the cot."

I crossed the shelf feeling like I was walking on someone's grave and someone's kitchen at the same time, which, I suppose, I was.

The journal was a ship's logbook, repurposed, its spine cracked, its pages filled with a hand I knew better than my own — because it was the hand mine imitated, the slanted capitals, the way he crossed his sevens. Dive entries. Tide tables. Maintenance notes on the stove, the cistern, the crates. And dates. Page after page of dates, marching forward year after year, the same patient handwriting growing slightly less steady, the ink changing as pens changed, the entries shortening as though the writer were rationing words the way he rationed everything else.

The last entry was dated three days ago.

*Surfaced a skiff at dusk, north shore. Syndicate scout, alone, photographing the cliff. Watched him leave. They're close now. Closer than the navy, and worse.*

*If you're reading this, then either I'm dead or I've been careless, and I've tried very hard to be neither.*

*I'm sorry about the doll.*

I read it three times. The third time, Wren read it over my shoulder, and neither of us spoke, because what do you say? *I'm sorry about the doll.* Twenty years compressed into five words, and somehow it was the truest apology I'd ever been handed, and I wanted to burn it and frame it in the same breath.

The lamp flickered. My gauge said I had time; my chest said otherwise. Somewhere behind us, the cave mouth was breathing its slow tide-breath, and somewhere beyond that, Tomas was minding a boat full of explosives, and somewhere beyond *that*, Corvane's patrol boat was shadowing us without firing, and Odile's syndicate was closing like a hand.

But none of that mattered for one long moment, because the silt on the floor of the shack had shifted, and the newest set of boot prints — the ones with the worn outer edge, the ones made hours ago — didn't lead out toward the waterline.

They led up. Toward a second passage at the back of the cavern, a crack in the rock I'd taken for drainage, wide enough for a man, climbing into the dark.

He wasn't coming back. He was here. He'd been here minutes ago, close enough that the kettle hadn't cooled, and he'd heard us come in — of course he'd heard us, twenty years of silence makes a man's ears into instruments — and he'd done what he'd trained himself to do. He'd withdrawn. He'd watched.

Or he was watching right now.

I turned slowly, lamp raised, and swept the upper gallery where the beam couldn't quite reach, the flowstone columns rising into blackness like the pipes of some drowned organ.

"Hello, Papa," I said, and my voice cracked down the middle, and the cave carried it up into the dark and gave it back to me twice.

For a moment: nothing. Just water dripping somewhere, counting seconds the way caves do.

Then, from the shadow between two pillars, a lamp clicked on — an old one, brass, the kind they stopped making before I was born — and a voice answered, roughened by two decades of salt and disuse but unmistakable, unmistakable, the voice from every dream I'd ever had about the *Cormorant*'s rail:

"Hello, August. You got tall."

Behind me, Wren's breath caught. Behind all of us, far away, the tide began to turn, and the door behind us started, patiently, to close.
