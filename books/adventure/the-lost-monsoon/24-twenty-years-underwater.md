## 24. Twenty Years Underwater

I had rehearsed this conversation for twenty years, which is a long time to rehearse and still get every line wrong.

She stood in the doorway of my shack — my shack, God help me, the word *home* had crept in somewhere around year nine and I'd stopped fighting it — dripping cave water onto the floorboards I'd planed smooth myself, holding the compass I'd given her the day she turned twelve, looking at me like I was a wreck she'd been sent to salvage and had found, instead, a man.

"Say something," she said.

"I put the kettle on," I said. "It's probably gone cold."

August Nystrom did not cry. I knew that the way I knew the tide tables of this seamount, the way I knew which rungs of the ladder had rusted through. She had not cried at the funeral — the closed casket, the flag, the whole theater of it — because she was eleven years old and someone had told her big girls don't, and she had taken that instruction as a life sentence. So she didn't cry now. She sat down on the stool across from me, the one I'd carved for a daughter who was never supposed to find me, and she said:

"You're going to tell me why. And you're going to do it without lying, because I've spent three weeks being lied to by professionals and I can smell it now."

* * *

Here is what I told her, and for once it was all true.

The Vessania sank in '61, but nations sink slower than ships. The government had been drowning for a decade before the water came — coups, sanctions, the currency worth less than the paper it was printed on. When the end finally arrived, there were men whose job it was to make sure that when Vessania died, it died rich enough to be reborn. A treasury. Bullion, bearer bonds, sealed accounts. Enough money to fund an army, or a government-in-exile, or — and this is the version they told us divers — a refugee fleet. Forty thousand people scattered across three seas who would need to eat while the world decided whether they existed.

Six of us on the dive team. I was the youngest. Odile Marchetti was the second youngest, though you'd never have guessed it; she carried herself like a woman who had already survived something, and I later learned she had.

We raised it over eleven weeks. Eleven weeks of black water and bad air and a foreman named Kessler who counted every bar twice. And when it was done — when the last strongbox came up and the manifest balanced to the gram — we learned what the money was actually for.

Not refugees. Not rebirth. There were men in the syndicate — the same syndicate that runs guns through these waters today, the same one whose gunboats are probably circling this cove right now — who had bought the dive team's contract through three shell companies, and the treasury was going to buy rifles. Ten thousand rifles, ammunition, patrol boats. A private war, funded by a dead nation's savings, fought over the corpse's shipping lanes.

Kessler told us this at a celebration dinner, raising his glass, because he thought we'd be proud. He thought we'd been building something.

Odile was the one who understood first what happened next. Men who steal forty million in bullion don't leave six witnesses standing. She came to my bunk that night — I remember her knuckles rapping the frame, quick, like a code — and she said, "Elias. We are already dead. We just haven't been notified."

So we stole it back. All of it. Six divers, one barge, and the single most insane night of salvage ever attempted: putting treasure *back*. We scuttled it here, in the seamount, in a cavern deep enough that no magnetometer short of a naval survey would flag it, and we split the knowledge six ways so no one man could sell the location. Then we scattered, and waited to see who would die first.

Vasquez died in eight months. Boiler explosion, officially. Okafor lasted two years before the car went off the road. Lindqvist made it four and then simply vanished between a dock and a taxi. By the time August was five, I was the only diver from the original team still breathing, and I understood that staying alive wasn't luck anymore. It was a job.

Because there was one more thing. The syndicate hadn't stopped looking. Twenty years, and they were still looking — because ten thousand rifles keep, and a buyer's market always comes around. Every few years a survey ship would pass too close. Every few years a salvager with more ambition than sense would start asking about Vessian manifests. And every time, if I didn't move, if I wasn't *here*, being the warden, being the ghost in the machine — it would come up. All of it. Into other hands.

So I stayed. I faked my death because a dead man has no debts anyone can collect, no family anyone can squeeze, no address anyone can burn down. I let them bury a casket full of ballast stones because a man with a daughter is a man with a lever, and levers get pulled.

Distance was protection. That was the whole architecture of it. As long as I was nowhere, they were safe. As long as I loved them from far enough away, the loving couldn't hurt them.

I believed that for twenty years. I want that on the record. I believed it the way you believe a hull is sound — right up until the water comes in.

* * *

August listened to all of it without moving. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere above us, muffled by sixty feet of rock and water, the sea was doing whatever the sea does when it isn't trying to kill you.

Then she said, quietly, "Do you know what a pair of boots cost in Harrowgate, winter of '72?"

I didn't answer. There was no answer that wasn't a wound.

"Because I do," she said. "I know exactly, because Mama wrote it in the margin of the rent book, and I found the rent book after she died, and I read every page. Boots, four crowns. Coal, seven. Doctor, when I had the fever — nine, and she paid it late, and the man made her feel like dirt for it. Do you know how I learned you were dead? I was eleven. A woman from the naval office came to the house and handed Mama a form and said the word *presumed* like she was sorry about it, and then she left, and Mama sat down at the table and didn't move for a long time, and I stood in the doorway and decided right then that I was never going to need anybody ever again." She looked at her hands. "Took me twenty years to break that promise to myself. Took me a crew of strangers and a smuggler I don't trust and a runaway kid who reads dead languages. You know what fixed it? Not you. People who showed *up*."

"August—"

"I dove condemned wrecks for creditors." Her voice stayed level, which was worse than shouting. Level meant rehearsed. She'd rehearsed this too, maybe. Maybe we'd both been giving our speeches to empty rooms for two decades. "Three hundred feet down on structures the survey office had red-flagged, because the pay was double and double was what the interest needed. I've counted my life in air tanks, Dad. Literally. Fifty minutes of bottom time equals X amount of debt. I used to do the arithmetic on the boat ride out. How many dives till I'm free. The number never got smaller. And the whole time — the *whole time* — there's a cave under the ocean with forty million in it, and my father is sitting on a stool next to it, carving furniture."

"The money was never mine to spend."

"No," she said. "But you were mine. And you spent yourself on a vigil nobody asked you to keep." She leaned forward. "You want to know the worst part? It worked. Your logic is *good*, Dad. Dead men can't be leveraged, the treasury stayed hidden, the syndicate didn't get its rifles. Twenty years, and your math checks out. But you protected a nation that doesn't exist better than you protected a girl who did. You guarded gold from thieves and left your daughter alone with poverty, and grief, and every winter after '72, and you call that distance, and I call it the same thing the syndicate would have done. They'd have taken me hostage. You just did it slowly, and called it love."

The words went into me like a hook into soft flesh. I didn't flinch, because she deserved to see them land.

"I told myself you'd be safer hating a memory than mourning a target," I said. "That if I ever surfaced, they'd find you inside a week. That every birthday card I didn't send was a bullet I didn't draw."

"And was it worth it?"

And there it was. The question. Twenty years of tide tables and canned food and talking to myself in a hole in a rock, all of it balanced on one scale pan, and my daughter's childhood in the other, and she was asking me to read the needle out loud.

"No," I said. "It was a calculation, and calculations are how you survive, and it was wrong. I chose the treasure over you without ever once letting myself say those words, because saying them would have meant leaving. And I couldn't leave. Because if I left, who would guard it?" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Me. It was always me. The great Elias Nystrom, irreplaceable. Vanity wearing duty's coat. That's all the vigil ever was, past the fifth year. After that it was just easier to stay than to come home and find out what I'd done."

She was quiet for a long time. Outside, far off, I heard it — a low thrum through the water column, engines, more than one set. My ears had gone native down here; I could read the sea like a ledger. Two boats, maybe three, holding position beyond the shoal. Not navy spacing. Navy ships announce themselves. These were loitering.

August heard it too. Her head tilted, that little bird-motion she got from her mother, and some cold practical diver part of her brain started running numbers even while the rest of her was still bleeding on my floor.

"We're not done," she said. "This conversation — I'm not done with it, and you're not getting forgiven by sundown, so don't arrange your face like a man awaiting absolution. But the tide turns in four hours, and Wren decoded the last table this morning, and the entrance seals for nine hours after that. Which means whatever's coming is coming *now*."

"It's been coming for twenty years," I said. "That's rather the point of me."

"Well," August said, standing, checking the seal on her dive knife with a motion so familiar it hurt, because I taught it to her at a kitchen table in another life, "it can bloody well queue up behind everything else."

At the door she stopped. Didn't turn around.

"Mama kept your compass on the mantel," she said. "Face down. For nineteen years. I used to think she couldn't look at it. Now I think she kept it face down so it couldn't point at anything — so it couldn't pretend to be true." She glanced back, just once. "It pointed me here, Dad. First thing it's gotten right since you died. Let's hope your judgment's improved, because we've got a syndicate outside, a navy behind them, a crew upstairs voting on whether to trust you, and about four hours to decide what forty million in stolen money is actually *for*."

She walked out into the dark of the tunnel, and I sat alone in the shack I'd built instead of a life, listening to the engines multiply beyond the shoal, and understood at last that the vigil was over — not because I'd ended it, but because the world had finally come to collect.
