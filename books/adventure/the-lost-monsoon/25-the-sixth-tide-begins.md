## 25. The Sixth Tide Begins

**AUGUST**

The water told us first.

It always does, if you're listening. The sea doesn't announce itself with trumpets; it clears its throat. I was standing at the cave mouth with a mug of Elias's terrible coffee when the current changed direction — not gradually, the way tides usually turn, but all at once, like a door swinging. Cold water shoved past my ankles, headed inland, into the dark.

"Sixth tide," Elias said behind me. He said it the way other men say *incoming artillery*.

"How long?"

"Four hours to flood. Then the mouth closes for six more." He came to stand beside me, and up close I could see what twenty years underwater had done to him — salt in the creases of his face, eyes that had gone the color of the deep channel and stayed there. "After that, nobody goes in or out until it opens again."

"And outside?"

He didn't answer, which was an answer. Outside, the syndicate gunboats were converging on the cove like sharks that had smelled blood three reefs away, and somewhere beyond them, Corvane's patrol boat was shadowing us with a silence that felt worse than gunfire. Two predators, one seal hole, and us inside it with a fortune in Vessian gold and no way to spend it fast enough.

I went below to tell the crew, because that's the job now. Not diving. Not decoding. Telling people true things they don't want to hear, and watching them decide who they are anyway.

Tomas took it the way Tomas takes everything — by immediately listing the ways we could die, ranked by probability. "The entrance narrows to maybe eight meters at half-flood," he said, chalk squeaking against the bulkhead where he'd drawn the cave profile from memory. "We could run it now, at slack, before the current builds. After that—" he drew a line across the throat of the passage, "—we're corked."

"Run it into what?" Wren asked. She was sitting cross-legged on the chart table, which she'd claimed as personal territory sometime around the second tide, and nobody had been brave enough to evict her. "Three gunboats and a navy frigate? At least in here we're a hard target."

"We're a *sealed* target," Tomas said. "There's a difference between hard and buried."

They looked at me. That was the thing about this crew I still hadn't gotten used to — they looked at me. Twenty years of looking out for myself, and now four faces turned my way like I was a lighthouse instead of just another wreck waiting to happen.

I laid it out flat. "Option one: we run now, loaded light, and pray the syndicate's gunners are as hungover as their captain. Option two: we stay sealed in here for six hours with enough gold to buy a small country and no way out, and hope Corvane keeps playing both sides. Option three." I paused, because option three was the one I hadn't said out loud yet, and saying it would make it real. "Option three is what we came here to do."

Wren understood first. Of course she did. "Sink it," she said quietly. "Collapse the cavern."

Elias made a sound like a man being gutted slowly. I didn't look at him. If I looked at him, I'd see twenty years of vigil cracking down the middle, and I wasn't strong enough for that and the tide at the same time.

"We vote," I said. "But vote fast. The water isn't waiting on our feelings."

* * *

**CORVANE**

I have written reports that ended careers. This one, I suspected, would end mine, and I wrote it anyway, because there comes a point in every officer's life where the uniform fits worse than the truth.

*To Fleet Command, re: Vessian recovery operation.* My pen hovered. Through the porthole of my cabin, the cove lay black under a moon going in and out of cloud like a bad conscience. Three syndicate gunboats holding station two kilometers out, running dark except for their cooking fires — arrogant, or certain. And beyond them, on the horizon, the running lights of the fleet proper: *Vigilant*, *Sable*, and the tender *Merrow*, carrying enough marines to take a city and enough explosives to bury one.

The order had come down an hour ago, encoded, signed by an admiral whose name I'd have to look up because he'd spent his career behind desks. *Upon confirmation of treasury location, deny recovery to all parties. Collateral assessment secondary.*

Collateral assessment secondary. In the language my superiors actually spoke: whatever's inside that cave when the shells land, count it as acceptable loss.

Including, presumably, one disgraced salvage diver, one engineer struck off the rolls, one runaway child, one smuggler with a price on her head in three ports, and one man the Republic had officially mourned for two decades — a man whose death notice I had, in another life, helped file.

I burned the draft. Then I sat with my hands flat on the desk and did the arithmetic they'd trained me for, the cold kind, the kind that makes admirals. Six hours until the tide sealed the cave. The fleet would arrive in five. Which meant the order to fire would come at high water, when the crew inside couldn't run even if they knew to try.

They weren't meant to know. That was the elegance of it. Shell the cave at flood tide, report subsidence, file the Vessian affair in the drawer where inconvenient history goes to die. No witnesses. No trial. No awkward questions about how a nation's treasury walked out from under a naval escort twenty years ago, and whose signatures were on the manifest.

My signature, as it happened. Junior lieutenant, logging clerk, the lowest pen in the machine. I had watched those crates go aboard a transport that never reached its destination and written *lost at sea* in a hand so neat it had earned me a commendation.

Twenty years later the sea was giving it back, and my government wanted to drown it again along with everyone who'd touched it.

I took out a fresh sheet and began a different report. This one would go nowhere official. It would go, by way of a fishing skiff and a boy I'd bribed with fuel, to a woman named Nystrom, and it would say only: *Fleet arrives within five hours. They will not offer terms. The tide will close before they open fire. Whatever you mean to do, do it before the water does.*

Then I poured myself the last of the brandy I'd been saving for a promotion toast, drank it to the death of my career, and went up to the bridge to lie to my own executive officer with a straight face.

It's remarkable, the things you find you're good at too late.

**ELIAS**

I built my life around a door, and now the door was closing.

That's the thought that kept circling while the girl — my daughter, God, *my daughter*, and I still couldn't say the word without it catching like a fishhook — moved through the cave tallying charges with Tomas. Demolition points. Structural weaknesses. Twenty years I'd mapped this place the way a priest maps his parish, every chamber, every fault line, every coffer stacked and catalogued against the day it might matter. And in one afternoon they'd found the seams I'd spent two decades pretending weren't there.

The gold sat in the inner vault the way it had always sat: patient, indifferent, beautiful the way a loaded gun is beautiful. Eleven hundred bars of Vessian mint, plus the plate, plus the regalia, plus the paper bonds that had once been a nation's promise to itself. Enough to arm the syndicate for a generation. Enough to buy every port official from here to the equator. Enough, if it ever got loose, to make the war that drowned Vessania look like a skirmish.

So I stayed. That was the whole of my philosophy, compressed: *so I stayed.* I let them bury me in the newspapers. I let August grow up on stories with no body in them. I told myself distance was armor, that a dead father was safer than a compromised one, that love could be paid out in silent installments across twenty years and cashed in full at some future date I never quite examined.

She was examining it now. Every time she passed me in the lantern light, I felt the examination happening — not hatred, which I could have borne, but accounting. She was counting what my vigil had cost, and the ledger ran through her childhood, and there was no column in it for my reasons.

Wren found me at the vault door. The girl moves quiet as bilge water; I'd stopped hearing her coming days ago, which should have worried me more than it did.

"You'll die for it," she said. No preamble. She'd learned conversation from ledgers, apparently — straight to the figures.

"I've guarded it twenty years."

"That's not an answer." She put her back against the opposite doorpost, mirroring me, small and immovable. "You'll die for it tomorrow, or tonight, or whenever the shells come. And the gold will still be here, under the rock, and the syndicate will dig, because they dug at the marsh site after your team died, and they'll keep digging, because men like that don't stop when the treasure sinks. They stop when it stops existing." She looked past me into the dark of the vault, at the pale gleam of stacked metal. "My orphanage was bought with money like this. I know what it costs after it's sunk. It doesn't sink. It floats. It floats up into people."

I wanted to tell her she was wrong. I opened my mouth to say something about permanence, about the deep channel, about the difference between hiding a thing and ending it — and heard, underneath my own voice, the sound of a man defending a wall he'd hidden behind so long he'd mistaken it for a fortress.

"The tide's turning," I said instead.

"It always is," Wren said. "That's rather the point."

**AUGUST**

The note arrived folded inside a fish, which is a sentence I never expected to think, let alone write.

One of Odile's old contacts ran a skiff out of the far side of the cove — a boy of maybe fifteen with the nerves of a card sharp — and he came alongside our anchorage inside the cave long enough to toss a wrapped bundle and vanish again before Tomas finished swearing at him. Inside the fish, oiled paper. Inside the paper, naval stationery. Inside the stationery, five lines in a hand I recognized from the margins of confiscated charts.

*They will not offer terms.*

I read it twice. Then I carried it to the others, and we stood around the engine room lamp like mourners, and the truth settled over us with the weight of the water rising outside.

"They won't board," Tomas said. "They'll shell. At flood tide, when we can't move." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Corvane's telling us because he's decided to hang separately."

"He's telling us because he's decided we're worth more than his career," Odile said. She'd been quiet since the confrontation at anchor — quieter than I'd ever known her, chained to her usefulness and watching us with those appraiser's eyes that had started, somewhere around the fourth tide, to look less like appraisal and more like grief. "Don't mistake it for sentiment. Men like Corvane break ranks for their own reasons. But the information's clean. He wants us gone before the fleet arrives, because if we're alive and free, there's no tidy story to file."

"So we run," Wren said. "Now. Slack water. Tomas said the throat's passable for another hour."

"And carry out how much?" Elias's voice came from the shadows by the vault, and I made myself turn and face him. "A boatload? You'd run the gauntlet of three gunboats and a frigate carrying what — forty bars? Fifty? The rest stays here for the syndicate to excavate at leisure, and everything we bled for buys the next war instead of preventing it."

"Then we sink it," I said. "All of it. Properly."

The word dropped into the room and kept dropping.

"Not hide it. Not seal it and trust the rock to keep our secret. We bring the vault gallery down onto the deep channel side — Tomas has already found the fault — and the sea finishes what the war started. No coordinates. No ledger. Nothing left to kill each other over." I looked around the lamplight, face by face. "I know what I'm asking. Dad, I know exactly what I'm asking. But you said yourself the syndicate digs. Hiding it just sets the table for the next twenty years of this. Ending it is the only inheritance nobody bleeds for."

Elias looked at me for a long moment, and in the lantern light I saw the warden and the father fighting for the same territory, and neither winning.

"The vote," he said finally, hoarse. "You said we vote."

"We vote."

* * *

**TOMAS**

Four to one.

I want it noted, in whatever log survives this, that I voted yes with my stomach in my boots, because rigging demolition charges inside a flooded limestone cavern while the tide climbs toward your exit is not engineering, it's prayer with extra steps. But I voted yes. Reyes, the man who froze in the *Cormorant*'s engine room while eleven shipmates drowned — voting yes.

Wren voted yes before anyone finished asking. Odile voted yes with a strange little smile, like a woman settling a debt older than any of ours. August voted yes the way she does everything, which is to say like the outcome was already true and the universe was merely catching up.

And Elias — Elias stood there with his hand flat on the vault door like a man taking a pulse, and then he said, "Yes," and the single syllable cost him more than the rest of ours combined, and I understood that I was watching a man bury himself alive on purpose, and that he was doing it because his daughter asked.

The tide gave us our deadline. Four hours to place the charges, three to clear out, and then the mouth would shut and the fleet would arrive and none of it would matter anymore.

I started drilling at the primary fault line while the water rose around my knees, and outside, faint through the stone, I could hear engines. More than three boats' worth. The syndicate had stopped being patient.

Above us, August stood watch at the cave mouth with her father's compass open in her palm, though it pointed at nothing useful anymore — it hadn't since the marshes, as if it had finished its work. The water climbed. The light died. And the sixth tide began to close the only door in the world.
