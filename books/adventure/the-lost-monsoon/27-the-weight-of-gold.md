## 27. The Weight of Gold

The treasury of Vessania smelled like a church and a coin purse had drowned together.

That was my first thought, standing ankle-deep in black water inside the seamount cave, lantern light crawling across the stacked strongboxes, the bullion bars wrapped in oilcloth gone to lace, the ceremonial plate green with twenty years of patience. My second thought was simpler and worse: *this is enough money to buy a small country, and it is going to get every one of us killed.*

"August." Tomas's voice came through my earpiece, thin and crackling from the tunnel mouth. "Tide's turning. We've got maybe ninety minutes before the entrance sills up. Whatever we're doing, we do it now."

I didn't answer right away. I was doing arithmetic. It's what I do. It's what I've always done — tanks against minutes, wages against debts, breath against depth. I'd spent my whole adult life counting things I couldn't afford, and here, finally, was the sum at the end of all those columns, glittering in the lantern light like an answer.

Except it wasn't an answer. It was a question with teeth.

Wren crouched beside one of the open chests, her gloved hands hovering over a ledger bound in eel-skin, not touching. Even now, even after everything, she treated the Vessian script like something holy. Maybe it was. It had certainly been treated like contraband often enough.

"How much do you think?" she asked quietly.

"Enough," I said. "More than enough."

"That's not a number."

"No," I agreed. "It's a problem."

Elias stood at the far end of the cavern, near the shack he'd built out of salvage and stubbornness — the cot, the kettle, the shelves of tinned food arranged with the neatness of a man who had nothing to do but be orderly. Twenty years of being orderly. He was watching me the way he'd watched me since the reunion curdled: carefully, like I was a wreck he hadn't finished surveying.

My father. Alive. Thinner than the man in the photograph, grayer, with diver's hands and a warden's eyes. A man who had chosen a cave full of dead men's gold over a daughter's childhood, and who probably — I could see it forming behind his face — thought that choosing had been noble.

We were going to have that fight eventually. But first we had to have this one.

* * *

The loading went fast because greed is efficient.

Tomas rigged a block and tackle at the shelf where the water shallowed, and we passed crates hand to hand along the line: Wren in the shallows, me on the ledge, Elias sorting and selecting with the terrible competence of a man who knew exactly which boxes held what. The tender couldn't come inside — the mouth of the cave was too narrow, too shallow at this tide — so everything had to go out by sling and swim, load after load, into the boat riding the swell beyond the rocks.

Bullion first. Then the plate, because Odile had said — before the bullet, before the blood on the deck plates below — that the syndicate's appraisers paid best for identifiable pieces, and if we were going to buy our way clear of her people, we needed the pieces they'd recognize as real.

I tried not to think about Odile lying in the hold with a compress on her shoulder and Tomas's field kit doing its inadequate best. I tried not to think about the gunboats I could hear, faintly, through the rock — engines idling out past the point, patient as sharks. Corvane's patrol boat was out there too, shadowing without firing, which was either protection or a very elaborate trap, and I genuinely could not tell which.

Load. Pass. Swim. Load again.

By the fourth trip my arms were shaking and the arithmetic had started whispering to me, the way it always does when the numbers are bad.

Here's the problem nobody tells you about treasure: it's heavy. That sounds stupid until you're the one hauling it. Gold doesn't care about your escape plan. Every kilo we loaded bought us speed going out and slowness getting away, and the syndicate boats were faster than us empty. Fully laden, we weren't a salvage crew anymore. We were a piñata.

"Tomas," I said into the mic, on the fifth trip. "If we take all of this, how long to reach deep water?"

Silence. Then: "You already know."

"I want to hear you say it."

"We don't reach deep water. Not laden, not with two gunboats working the channel. They don't even have to catch us. They just have to wait for us to get slow." A pause. "August, we can carry maybe a third of it. Enough to pay off the boat, settle everyone, disappear. If we run light and lucky."

"And the rest?"

"The rest sits here until the sixth tide ends and the cave seals, and then it sits until someone comes back with better equipment and worse intentions." Another pause, longer. "Which will be soon. Everyone knows the coordinates now. Odile sold the route; Corvane fed his fleet something; the syndicate has the marsh site. This place has a shelf life measured in days."

I looked at the cavern. At the wealth of a drowned nation, stacked and waiting, enough to end every debt I'd ever counted, enough to buy Wren a life no orphanage could ever claw back, enough to give Tomas his name back, enough to make Odile's sacrifice mean something in the only currency her world respected.

Enough to make us rich.

And enough to make us corpses, because you cannot outrun a gunboat carrying a nation's ransom, and the men in those boats did not want a share. They wanted all of it, and us at the bottom of the channel as a rounding error.

* * *

I called a halt on the seventh load.

Wren noticed first — she notices everything, it's the orphanage in her, that hypervigilance dressed up as cleverness. She straightened up in the shallows, water streaming off her, and looked at my face, and whatever she saw there made her go still.

"August?"

"Everyone in. Tomas, kill the winch. Dad—" The word came out before I decided to use it, and I watched him flinch, and I didn't take it back. "Get out of the water. All of you. We need to talk."

We gathered on the dry shelf by the shack, five people and a dying fire and a fortune. Elias sat apart, the way he'd sat apart for twenty years, guarding. Wren wrung out her sleeves. Tomas kept glancing toward the tunnel mouth like the tide itself might come early out of spite.

"Here's the situation," I said. "We can't take it all. Taking most of it kills us. Taking some of it means leaving the rest for the syndicate or the navy or whoever swims in next week, and any one of them uses it to fund everything we've been fighting — the guns, the orphanage pipelines, the whole rotten trade. Odile bled out half her shoulder to buy us hours. Corvane burned his career to feed us false coordinates. And if we leave this cave holding a fraction, then what they bled for buys nothing but our funeral costs."

Nobody spoke. The tide spoke instead, a low breath through the tunnel, rising.

"So there's a third option," I said, and heard my own voice go flat, the way it does when I'm about to say something unforgivable. "We sink it. Permanently. Tomas brings down the cavern roof onto the deep channel side, the whole vault goes into the scoured-out trench under it, and the sea closes over it at a depth nobody salvages. Not us. Not them. Nobody. Ever."

The silence afterward had texture. You could have cut it into strips and sold it.

"You're joking," Tomas said finally.

"I never joke about tonnage."

"August." He took his cap off, which is what he does when he's scared — I'd learned that in the engine room during the sabotage, watching him work with his hands steady and his cap coming off and on like a nervous tic. "That's — do you know what you're describing? Charges inside a limestone seamount, at flood tide, while we're still inside it? The math alone—"

"The math works," I said. "You told me yourself the trench under the east wall runs past forty meters. The rock above the vault is shot through with old blast seams from whoever mined the approach channel a century ago. You said it was a miracle the roof was still up."

"I said that about the *engine room* too, and look how that went."

"It held."

"Barely!"

"Then you're the only man alive qualified to bring it down properly." I turned to Wren. "And you — everything the syndicate bought with money like this. Your orphanage. The girls they trafficked out under shipping manifests. That's what this gold funds next, if anyone gets it. You know that better than anyone."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice was steady in the way that cost her something. "I know. I just — I spent three weeks reading that ledger thinking it was a door out. For all of us." She looked at the chests, and I watched her say goodbye to something. "But doors out aren't worth much if the house burns down behind you anyway. Do it."

Then there was one person left, and she wasn't talking, and neither was he.

Elias hadn't moved. He sat on the edge of his cot with his hands flat on his knees, looking at the vault the way other men look at graves. Which, I supposed, was exactly what it was — twenty years of his life entombed in limestone, every cold night and silent morning and tin of food arranged just so, all of it in service of keeping this gold from the world. And now his daughter was proposing to drop the entire ocean on it and call it done.

"No," he said.

"Dad—"

"You don't understand what this is." He stood, and for the first time since the shack, there was iron in his voice, the voice of a man who had argued with tides and won. "This isn't a payday. It's a *warden's charge*. I have spent twenty years making sure this never arms another syndicate, never buys another election, never sinks another refugee boat — and you want to solve it with explosives? You want to bury it and walk away and call that an ending?"

"Yes," I said. "Because the alternative is you keep guarding it forever. Is that the plan, Dad? You die in this cave, on schedule, and the gold waits for the next person with a compass and a grudge? Because that's not guardianship. That's just a longer line."

"This is not yours to destroy!"

"Neither is it yours to hoard!" The words came out of me hot and fast, twenty years of them, all the Christmases and funerals-without-bodies and creditors' letters addressed to *the widow Nystrom* when there was no widow, just a girl and a hole shaped like a man. "You buried yourself with it! You let me grow up poor and grieving so you could play lighthouse keeper for dead men's money, and now you want me to leave it sitting here so you can keep doing it? No. No more. Either it goes to the bottom of the trench where no one — *no one* — can ever spend a life on it again, or I start loading every bar onto that tender and we take our chances with the gunboats. Pick one."

He stared at me. In the lantern light his face did something complicated, grief and fury and — underneath, barely visible, the thing I'd been looking for since the shack — recognition. He was looking at his own stubbornness wearing my face, and he knew it, and it horrified him.

"You sound like your mother," he said quietly.

"She'd have voted to sink it too."

* * *

It was Wren who broke the standoff, though not the way I expected. She didn't argue. She walked to the nearest chest, lifted out a single bullion bar, small enough to palm, the kind they pressed for courier runs, and set it on the crate between Elias and me.

"One," she said. "We keep one. Not to spend. To sell, once, to the appraiser Odile trusted, quiet, split six ways — enough to settle the boat, the debts, the hospital bill. Enough that nobody on this crew ever has to dive a condemned wreck for a creditor again. And then the rest goes into the trench, and we don't speak of it, and nobody ever bleeds for it again."

Elias looked at the bar. Then at her — this skinny runaway kid his daughter had collected off a dock somewhere, who read dead languages and carried old wounds — and something in him finally, visibly, gave way.

"One," he said hoarsely. "One bar. And the ledger goes with it — the original. History shouldn't drown twice."

"Done," I said, before he could change his mind, before any of us could breathe wrong and shatter it. "Tomas. How fast can you rig it?"

Through the earpiece, crackling with distance and disbelief: "Give me forty minutes and everybody's prayer of choice. August — if I'm wrong about the seam lines, we'll know immediately, and briefly."

"Then be right," I said. "You usually are."

I looked around the cavern one last time — at the wealth of nations, at the shack with its warm kettle, at the man I'd mourned for two decades standing among coffers he'd guarded with his whole life. Outside, through the rock, I could hear the gunboats idling, closer now, patient as arithmetic.

Ninety minutes until the tide sealed the door. Forty to rig the charges. Which left exactly enough time for something else to go wrong — and out past the point, through the lap of water against stone, I heard the unmistakable grind of a hull touching rock, and voices.

A boarding party. Coming in through the tunnel mouth.

They'd found us.
