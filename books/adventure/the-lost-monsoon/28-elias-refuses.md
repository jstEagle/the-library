## 28. Elias Refuses

I have held a gun on better people than these.

That is not a boast. It is an inventory. Twenty years in a flooded cave gives a man time to count, and I have counted everything — the bars of bullion stacked like cordwood in the dark, the crates of Vessian crown coin, the ledgers nobody will ever read, and the list of names I have pointed this pistol at to keep all of it sleeping at the bottom of the world. Navy divers. Syndicate cutters. A pair of treasure hunters out of Corvane's own fleet who thought the sixth tide was a rumor. Every one of them armed. Every one of them willing.

The girl holding the detonator wires is not willing. That is what makes her dangerous.

"Step back from the charges, Tomas," I say, and my voice comes out steadier than I have any right to expect. Cave air. Cold and mineral, tasting of stone and old water. I have breathed it so long it no longer feels like breathing. "All of you. The demolition stops now."

August stands between me and the main coffer, lamp light catching the wet line of her jaw, and she has her mother's stubbornness and my own worst habits, and she says nothing at all. She just looks at me the way she must have looked at every locked door in her life — like she was already deciding which hinge to break.

My daughter. Twenty years dead to me by my own design, standing forty feet away with salt water dripping off her gloves, asking me — no, *telling* me — to let the treasury go under for good.

"No," I say again, because apparently once wasn't enough for either of us.

* * *

You should understand something about twenty years underwater.

It is not dramatic. Nobody writes ballads about it. It is a kettle, a cot, a rain catchment rigged off a drip line, and four thousand mornings of checking seals that don't need checking because checking them is the only prayer I know. It is talking to yourself until your voice goes strange, then talking to the gold instead, because at least the gold doesn't answer back with your father's accent.

It is arithmetic, too. August learned hers counting air tanks; I learned mine counting threats. One navy salvage fleet. One syndicate with longer reach than any government. One daughter who believed her father drowned when the *Meridian* went down, because I paid good money to make the lie official and then never once — not once in twenty years — spent anything cheaper than money to undo it.

I told myself distance was protection. I told myself the treasury had to stay guarded, that if it ever surfaced it would buy guns and grudges and orphanages full of nothing. I told myself a man who abandoned his family to guard dead kings' coins was a warden and not a coward.

Twenty years is long enough to believe almost anything, if you never say it out loud where the echo can find you.

And now here is the echo, wearing my face younger and angrier, holding a coil of det cord like it owes her money.

"You're pointing a gun at your own crew," August says. Not her crew. Ours. She said *crew*, and something in my chest turned over like a hull taking water.

"They're not my crew."

"No," she agrees. "They're mine. Which means if you shoot one of them, you'd better be a very good shot, because I'm the second."

Tomas Reyes has his hands up, which is the correct response to being drawn on, but his eyes keep flicking to the charge he'd set against the cavern's eastern pillar — the one that would bring the ceiling down into the deep channel and bury four centuries of Vessian treasury under ten thousand tons of rock, permanently, unrecoverably, gone. He built the rig fast and clean. Disgraced engineer or not, the man knows his work. That's rather the problem.

Wren stands closest to me. Of course she does. She's maybe nineteen and she has been closer to more danger than anyone should survive, and she is looking at my pistol the way you look at weather — assessing, unimpressed, already planning around it.

"Mr. Nystrom," she says.

"Don't." My voice cracks on it. "Don't call me that like we're negotiating a charter."

"What should I call you? The man who's going to shoot us over metal?"

"It's not—" And there it is. Twenty years of the same sentence rising up my throat like floodwater. *It's not about the gold.* Except it is, and it isn't, and I have never once managed to say what it actually is about, because what it's actually about is that I gave everything else away and this is all that's left of the man I was supposed to be.

"The Vessania treasury funds armies," I say instead. "Syndicate gunboats. Bribed admirals. Whole wars, bought wholesale. You sink it, fine — but you sink it knowing what it buys if it ever floats. I've kept it dark for two decades. Two decades, girl. You think that was free?"

"I think," Wren says, "you should put the gun down before someone does something everyone regrets."

"And I think you're nineteen years old and standing in front of a loaded weapon because you haven't yet learned what fear is for."

"Oh, I've learned what fear is for." Something moves behind her eyes, quick and cold as fish under ice. "Fear is for keeping you alive long enough to get even. Put it down, Mr. Nystrom."

* * *

Here is what I remember about the year the treasury came down to us: rain that wouldn't stop, and seven divers who laughed too loud in the staging camp because laughing quiet felt like lying.

We were Vessian naval salvage, the last of it, working a nation that was dissolving under us even as we worked. The government was collapsing; the syndicate was moving in like water finding a crack; and our orders came down through three different channels, each contradicting the last, until finally Captain Sera Ilves gathered us in the mess tent and told us the truth: the treasury would be seized within the month, sold, scattered, converted into exactly the kind of power that had drowned Vessania in the first place. Unless we moved it first.

So we moved it. Seven of us, black water, zero visibility, hauling crown after crown down into a seamount cave that appeared on no chart because we made certain no chart survived showing it. We were going to hold it in trust. For the refugees. For the rebuilding. For whatever nation might grow back from the wreck of ours.

The syndicate found four of us within two years. Odile Marchetti survived by informing — I learned that later, much later, from a courier's intercepted letter I still keep in a waterproof sleeve like scripture. Elias Nystrom survived by dying. Best decision I ever made, and I made it without asking anyone whose life it would cost.

The Meridian went down with a body in it that wasn't mine. A drowned sailor, nameless, paid for by the syndicate's own bounty on my head — irony so thick you could dive it. My wife got a flag and a coffin of seawater. My daughter got a compass and a childhood of creditors.

I got a cave.

* * *

"Last chance," August says quietly. "Tomas fires the charge in ninety seconds. After that, the cave closes forever and nobody — syndicate, navy, nobody — ever gets this gold. Including you."

"Including me," I repeat. "That's the part you don't understand. I can't—"

"You *can*. You just won't."

"Won't and can't are the same thing after twenty years!" The words come out louder than I intend, bouncing off wet stone, and the pistol shakes now, genuinely, and I hate that they can see it. "You think I stayed down here because I wanted to? You think I liked watching my daughter grow up through a periscope of other people's letters? I stayed because if I leave, this room becomes a *target*, and everything those men died for becomes ammunition! Sera died for this. Pieter died for this. All of them—"

"All of them are dead," August says, "and you're alive, and the gold is still here, and none of it brought a single one of them back. Dad." The word lands like a depth charge. "Dad. Look at me. It didn't work. Whatever you were guarding against — it happened anyway. People bled for this before you ever got here, and they'll bleed for it after, unless it goes under tonight."

"And what do you get?" I hear myself ask, vicious with grief. "A clean conscience? You dive condemned wrecks for creditors, August. You know what debt is. This is *my* debt, and I pay it my way."

"No," says Wren.

Everyone turns. She hasn't moved. Her hands are loose at her sides, and her voice, when it comes again, is steady in a way that makes my skin prickle, because it is the steadiness of someone reciting something they have had to memorize.

"You want to talk about what the gold costs? Fine. Let's count it."

"Wren—" August starts.

"No. He asked." She takes one step toward me, and Tomas's hand twitches toward her, and she waves him off without looking. "You said it buys wars. Armies. Orphanages full of nothing. I'm going to tell you about an orphanage."

The lamp hisses. Water drips somewhere in the dark, patient as a clock.

"There was a place called Saint Aldhelm's, on the estuary coast. Gray building, gray porridge, gray sisters. They took in kids nobody claimed. War orphans, mostly. Dock children. Kids whose parents drowned doing honest work or dishonest work, didn't matter, the paperwork was the same." Her voice doesn't shake. That's the worst part. It doesn't shake at all. "I lived there from age six to age eleven. And I want you to understand, Mr. Nystrom — that orphanage was funded. Well funded. New roofs, warm coats in winter, meat twice a week. The sisters used to say God provides. God didn't provide. The Marchetti syndicate provided. Clean money, laundered through charity, skimmed off shipments like yours. Like the ones your dead friends died moving."

"That's not—" My mouth is dry. "That's not the treasury. That's smuggled goods, that's—"

"It's the same water," Wren says. "It all flows downhill into the same accounts. And here's the arithmetic you've been avoiding for twenty years: every crate you guarded, every shipment your crew died protecting, some fraction of it trickled out sideways and bought roofs and coats and meat for children whose parents were dead because of the trade that money came from. You weren't keeping the gold pure. You were keeping the pipeline intact. You were the best thing that ever happened to the syndicate — a warden who works for free."

"Stop."

"Saint Aldhelm's sold me," she says. "Age eleven. To a ship's master who needed small hands in his rigging. The sisters signed the papers with the same pens they used to write thank-you letters to their donors. So when you stand there with your gun and tell me the gold has to stay guarded — guarded *from whom*, Mr. Nystrom? Guarded for whom? Because I'm the only person in this cave who's actually touched what it does when it circulates, and I'm telling you: it doesn't buy wars. Wars are expensive. What it buys is *small things*. Coats. Meat. Children. Cheap, quiet, ordinary evil, paid for on time, every quarter, for decades. Your vigilance didn't stop that. Your vigilance *was* that. You were the vault that made the whole scheme credible."

The pistol is very heavy. When did it get so heavy?

"Sera Ilves didn't die for the syndicate," I manage.

"No. She died for a plan that stopped working the day the syndicate figured out where you were. Everything since has been you guarding a corpse and calling it duty." Wren tilts her head, and for a moment she looks terribly young and terribly old at once. "You want to honor them? Sink it. Sink it the way they couldn't, because they still believed it could be saved. Be the first one in twenty years to actually finish the job. Otherwise you're just the last lock on a door that should have been bricked up, and locks, Mr. Nystrom, are how the syndicate knows where to knock."

* * *

Silence, afterward. The kind that has weight and temperature.

Tomas's hand rests on the firing mechanism, not touching it. August hasn't moved, but her eyes are shining, and she is looking at Wren with an expression I recognize from mirrors — the look of someone revising everything they thought they knew about another person, upward.

I lower the pistol. It takes both hands. I am not ashamed of that. Or I am, but I am more tired than ashamed, and tired wins for the first time in twenty years.

"The eastern pillar," I say hoarsely. "If you're bringing down the ceiling, the eastern pillar is load-bearing into the channel fault. Set the charge there and the whole chamber drops clean. Anywhere else and you'll half-do it, crack the roof, leave the upper galleries exposed to salvage inside a season."

Tomas blinks. "You're... helping?"

"I've had twenty years to study how to destroy this place," I say. "Might as well spend the knowledge before the tide spends me."

"Dad." August crosses the space between us, boots splashing shallow water, and stops an arm's length away — close enough to touch, far enough that neither of us quite dares. "The gold goes under. But you come up with us. Those are the terms. Both or neither."

Above us, faint through the stone, comes a sound I have dreaded for two decades and know instantly, the way you know your own heartbeat: engines. More than one. Deep-throated, military-spaced, closing on the cove from two directions at once — the syndicate running one line, and behind them, slower, heavier, the navy coming to collect what its government sent it to bury.

The sixth tide is beginning to rise. And outside the mouth of the cave, the sea is filling with men who want what's in this room, and none of them — not one — knows that in ninety seconds, it may already be gone.

"Both or neither," I agree, and start walking toward the charges.
