## 29. The Standoff

The tide had turned, and so had the night.

I heard the boarding party before I saw them — the particular wet slap of boots on stone that no honest person makes. Honest people knock. Honest people shout across water. These were the sounds of men who had been paid to arrive unannounced, and I had spent enough years in the salvage trade to know that sound the way a child knows its mother's footsteps.

"Tomas." I didn't whisper. Whispering is for people who still believe they might be ignored. "They're in."

He was already at the tunnel mouth, a lantern shuttered down to a coin of light, his face the color of wet paper. "How many?"

"Enough." I counted the echoes. "Six. Maybe seven. They're moving slow — the passage narrows past the third bend. That's ours for maybe four minutes."

Four minutes. In four minutes I could have loaded a third of the strongboxes. In four minutes Wren could have finished marking the demolition points on Tomas's schematic. In four minutes my father could have done what he had spent twenty years doing, which was stand in front of the treasury with his arms crossed as though his body were a seawall.

Instead we spent them arguing. Families do that. It's the one thing we'd all learned to do together, and we did it beautifully.

"We blow the tunnel now," Elias said. "Seal it. They can't get in, we can't get out, and in three hours the sixth tide opens the upper channel and we leave through the roof like smoke."

"The upper channel's a crawl," Tomas said. "With the charges primed, with the water rising, with *her*—" he jerked his chin at Odile, who sat on a strongbox with her wrists still chained to its handle, looking, I will grant her, remarkably serene for a woman whose syndicate had come to collect.

"I can crawl," Odile said.

"Nobody asked you."

"Nobody ever does. It's why I've learned to answer anyway."

I put my hand flat against the stone. Through it, faint as a pulse, I could feel the water rising — the sixth tide beginning its slow, patient work of closing the door behind us. Somewhere above, out in the black, Corvane's patrol boat was either our shield or our witness or our executioner. I had stopped trying to predict which.

"Nobody blows anything yet," I said. "Tomas, the charges stay on the treasury side. If they breach the last bend, we blow the *inner* passage and drop the ceiling on the vault approach. We lose the escape route. We don't lose the option."

Elias turned on me, and I saw twenty years of vigil in his face, worn smooth as sea glass. "August. That gold is the only thing standing between the syndicate and a fleet of gunboats. If they get it—"

"If they get it, they get it over my corpse, same as they'd get it over yours. But we're not dying for it, Da. We're deciding what it's *worth*." I looked at him until he looked away. "That's the lesson you never learned. Everything's worth something. Nothing's worth everything."

He didn't answer. Behind him, in the lantern light, the treasury gleamed — the Vessian crest on every coffer, the drowned nation's whole weight in gold and paper, patient as a debt. Somewhere in that pile was the ledger that had started all this, and somewhere in that ledger was a boy's name written in a dead language, and somewhere in that boy's future was an orphanage that had sold her like salvage.

Wren sat apart from all of us, knees drawn up, the compass — my father's compass, the one I'd given her because inheritance should be a choice — open in her lap. She wasn't reading it. She was just holding it, the way you hold a thing that reminds you who you decided to be.

The boots got louder.

* * *

They came through the third bend at 2:14 by Tomas's watch, and they came the way syndicate men always come: in a wedge, guns first, a lantern held high by the man in the middle so that everyone could see how many of them there were. Seven. I'd been close.

The leader was a broad, wet-looking man with a syndicate cipher tattooed on his jaw — I'd seen the same mark on the courier's papers, on Odile's old correspondence, on the manifest of every dirty hull in the archipelago. He looked at us the way a creditor looks at a ledger. Like arithmetic.

"Nystrom," he said. Not a question. My father's alias had traveled further than my father had.

"That's me," Elias said, stepping forward, and I swear to you the man had the audacity to look *relieved*, as though twenty years of guarding had earned him some professional respect.

"Vessar sent us. He wants the vault inventory. He wants the woman." The leader's eyes slid to Odile, and something in them went flat and hungry. "He especially wants the woman."

"Vessar," Odile said pleasantly, "can stand in line with everyone else who wants me."

"Ma'am, with respect, you're in chains."

"Yes. It's my best look."

The leader's mouth twitched. Then he raised his lantern higher, and I saw what I'd been afraid I'd see: behind the wedge of gunmen, more men, and behind them, at the mouth of the tunnel, the gray gleam of a rigid hull — they'd brought a skiff *into* the cave passage. They hadn't swum. They'd motored. Which meant the syndicate had mapped the approach, which meant the courier's intelligence had been better than Odile had promised, which meant—

Which meant I'd have time to be furious about that later, if there was a later.

"Here's how this goes," the leader said. "You step away from the vault. Nobody's family gets a funeral. Vessar's feeling generous — he'll even let the girl keep her little book." His gaze found Wren, and I felt the temperature of the room change. "The orphanage girl. He remembers her. Says she owes him a debt of instruction."

Wren stood up. She's small, and she stood up the way a wave stands up — all at once, with the whole ocean behind her.

"I don't owe him anything," she said. "I *paid*. Everybody in that house paid. You just never gave receipts."

"Wren." I put a hand out, low, easy. Don't spook her, don't spook them. "Behind me, sweetheart."

"I'm not going behind you."

"I know. It's a figure of speech for people with sense."

And that — God help me — that's when the shooting started, because the leader's second man got nervous at the word *receipts*, or maybe at Wren's tone, or maybe because nervous men with guns are just accidents looking for a place to happen. The lantern went over. Darkness, total, and the muzzle flashes turning the cavern into a lightning storm underground, gold glinting in the strobes like the whole drowned nation was blinking awake.

I hit the stone behind a coffer and the world became sound: Tomas screaming something about the charges, Elias's pistol cracking twice with the unhurried rhythm of a man who had done this before and hated it, Wren's breathing beside me — she'd come to me after all, sense being contagious — and Odile.

Odile, who was chained to a strongbox in the open.

"Odile, get down!" I shouted into the dark.

"I'm *seated*," she shouted back. "This is me getting down. Do you know how hard this is in irons?"

A muzzle flash lit the vault, and in that half-second I saw the geometry of the whole disaster: the leader's wedge advancing on the strongboxes, one gunman peeling left — peeling toward Wren, toward the small shape crouched beside me, toward the girl with the compass — his barrel swinging, his finger already committed.

I was too far. Elias was too far. The whole world was too far, the way it always is, the way it had been my entire life — every rescue arriving one second after the need.

Odile moved.

She came off the strongbox like a woman diving, chains and all, and she put herself between that barrel and that girl the way you'd put your body between a door and a storm. The gunshot was very loud and very small, the way they always are when they matter.

She went down across Wren's legs, and the dark took her.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and I've had time since to be proud of it, which is not a thing I say lightly.

Tomas blew the inner passage.

I heard him key the detonator — two clicks, the way we'd drilled it, two clicks meaning *ceiling, not tunnel* — and the vault approach came down in a roar of dust and stone, sealing the syndicate's advance into a dead end and burying their skiff under a hundred tons of the seamount. The gunfire stopped the way a clock stops. In the ringing dark, Elias was shouting the crew's names like a roll call — "August! Wren! Reyes!" — and we were answering, one by one, the oldest prayer there is.

Then: "Odile," I said, and nobody answered, and I crawled toward where I'd seen her fall with my hands out in front of me like a beggar's.

I found her by the chains. They were still attached to the strongbox, and she was still attached to them, sprawled across Wren, who was already pressing both hands against the wound below Odile's collarbone and saying, in a small, ferocious voice, "No. No no no, you don't get to — you *don't*—"

"Language," Odile murmured.

"Shut up. Shut *up*."

I got my knife under the chain lock — syndicate iron, good iron, it took three cuts — and while I worked, Odile looked up at me with an expression I had never seen on her face in all the months I'd known her. She'd worn a hundred faces: the broker's smile, the fence's shrug, the arithmetic look she got when she was pricing a man's loyalty. This was none of them. This was just a woman, fifty-some years old, bleeding out on the floor of a cave full of gold she'd spent twenty years trying to buy her way out of.

"August," she said. "The oldest debt."

"Don't talk."

"Oldest currency." A wet, terrible laugh. "I told you once. Everything's worth something. Nothing's—" She coughed. "You stole my line. I want it back."

"You can have it," I said, and the lock gave, and I gathered her up, and she weighed nothing, which frightened me more than the blood. "Tomas! Light! *Now!*"

The lantern came up. The dust was still settling, hanging in the beam like the cave was full of ghosts, and maybe it was. Elias stood at the edge of the rubble with his pistol still in his hand and his face gone gray, looking at Odile in my arms with the expression of a man seeing the past stand up and take a bullet for the future.

He'd known her. Of course he'd known her. The photograph in the strongbox — the young dive team, his face beside hers, twenty years younger and already lying to each other about what the gold was for.

"Vessar's men are still in there," Elias said quietly. "Behind the rockfall. Trapped, but not gone. And the sixth tide won't wait for our grief."

"Then it can wait for nothing," I said, "because we're leaving. All of us. Now." I looked down at Odile. Her eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling, doing sums, probably. She was always doing sums. "Can you hear me, Marchetti? You're not paying anything tonight. You're *coming with us*. That's an order from your captain."

"I chained myself to a strongbox," she whispered, "and you made me captain of nothing."

"I know. Worst crew I ever sailed with."

She smiled. It cost her. She smiled anyway.

* * *

We carried her through the upper channel in relays — Tomas first, hauling the line, his fear of open water drowned out at last by something louder; Wren behind him with the compass open in one hand and Odile's wrist in the other, as if the needle might need both of them; Elias breaking trail with a strength that twenty years of cave-living had banked like coal. I came last with Odile across my shoulders, her blood warm through my shirt, her breath a small torn sound against my neck.

The crawl was everything Tomas had promised and less. Twice the water took us to our chins. Once the passage narrowed until I had to turn sideways with a dying woman on my back and trust the rock to hold us both. Odile talked the whole way, because Odile would have talked at her own funeral, and probably will.

"Ledger's in my coat," she said. "Inner pocket. Everything. Every account, every name, every debt Vessar thinks he's collecting. He thinks he owns me. August. *August.* He thinks he owns me, and I'm going to die owning *him*."

"You're not dying."

"Everyone says that. It's the one lie nobody improves on." A pause, and then, smaller: "The girl. Wren. Did I—"

"You saved her."

"Hm." I felt her think about that, felt the weight of it settle on her like ballast. "Twenty years I've been paying that debt. To a house I burned down from the inside. I never thought—" Her breath caught. "I never thought I'd get to pay it *forward*. Only back. You only ever pay it back."

"Odile—"

"Tell her I said the receipts are settled." Her voice was going somewhere I couldn't follow. "Tell her the orphanage girl is the richest person I ever met. Tell her—"

The passage opened, and the night air hit us, and the sky was there, enormous and indifferent, and the sixth tide was standing at full flood around the seamount like a wall. Below us, in the cove, lights were converging — syndicate gunboats from the east, and from the west, cutting across them with every lamp burning and no flag flying, a navy hull moving like it had somewhere to be and nothing left to lose.

Corvane. I'd know that reckless, honorable, doomed wake anywhere.

I got Odile onto the deck of our own boat, into Tomas's arms, into Wren's hands, and I stood there in the rain that was starting to fall, and I made the last decision of the night, the one that had been waiting for me since the ledger came up out of the river with my father's alias written in it.

The treasury was still down there. Intact. Sealed behind a hundred tons of rockfall, guarded by trapped men, ticking down toward the tide that would close the cave for good.

And I was done letting it cost what it cost.

"Tomas," I said. He looked up from Odile's wound, his hands red to the wrist. "How much of the demolition charge is still wired on the vault side?"

He understood immediately. I watched him understand, and I watched him be afraid, and I watched him — my disgraced engineer, my terrified, brave friend — begin to grin.

"Enough," he said. "Barely enough. If we go back in."

"Then we go back in."

Below us, the first shell from the syndicate gunboats lit the water green, and the sixth tide began, at last, to close the door.
