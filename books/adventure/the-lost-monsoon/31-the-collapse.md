## 31. The Collapse

The world ended the way most things end underwater: slowly, then all at once.

Tomas's charges went off in sequence — three sharp concussions I felt in my teeth more than my ears, a percussion rolling through the rock like a fist knocking on a door from the inside. The water around me shivered. Silt bloomed from the walls in gray curtains, and somewhere above, deep in the throat of the cave where it met open sea, something structural gave up its argument with gravity.

I was forty meters from the tunnel mouth when the ceiling started to come down.

Not all at once. First a sound like a whale clearing its throat, vast and wet and wrong. Then a rain of grit against my hood. Then the light from the entrance — that pale coin of daylight we'd been swimming toward for twenty minutes — began to shrink.

*Shrink.*

Caves don't close. That was the thought my brain kept offering up, useless as a broken compass. Caves are stone and stone is patient and patient things do not move. Except this one had two hundred tons of Vessian treasury collapsing through its floor into the deep channel Tomas had rigged, and physics does not care what you believe about stone.

I kicked for the light.

Behind me, the treasury was doing exactly what we'd designed it to do. The charges had cracked the shelf of rock beneath the coffers, and now gold — actual gold, bars and coins and the weight of every bad decision anyone had ever made about money — was sliding into the channel in a slow, glittering avalanche. It should have been beautiful. Wren would have called it beautiful. All I could think was: *we bled for that. Odile took a bullet for that.* And now it was leaving us the only way it ever should have gone — down, into the dark, where no one could spend it on guns or grief.

The light shrank again. Maybe half its size now. My dive computer screamed against my wrist, a thin electronic wail that sounded absurdly small against the grinding of a mountain.

And then I remembered who wasn't behind me.

I stopped. I actually stopped, finning hard enough to arrest my momentum, and turned back into the blackening water like an idiot, like a woman with a death wish and poor impulse control, because Elias Nystrom had been at the far wall when the charges blew.

He'd said he'd be right behind us. He'd said it the way he said everything — flat, certain, a man who'd spent twenty years alone in a flooded cave and had lost the habit of being contradicted. He'd been gathering the last of his things. A kettle. A cot. Twenty years of a life packed into a canvas bag, and he'd been slow about it because he'd never had to hurry anywhere in his life, and now the mountain was hurrying for him.

The sensible thing — the thing any salvage diver worth her salt would tell you, the thing I'd have told Wren or Tomas in a heartbeat — was to keep swimming. The tunnel mouth was closing. Going back meant dying. Full stop. Arithmetic so simple even a creditor could do it.

I swam back.

Because here's the thing about arithmetic: I'd been counting debts my whole life, and I'd finally learned the difference between what you owe and what you choose to pay. Nobody was making me turn around. That was the whole point. That was the only point.

* * *

The silt was total now. I swam by feel and memory, one hand trailing the guide line we'd run through the cavern, counting the knots Wren had tied at ten-meter intervals the way she counted everything — obsessively, gratefully, like a girl who'd learned that numbers were the only promises that kept. Ten meters back. Twenty. My lamp was useless, a coin of glow swallowed by the murk. Somewhere ahead of me the ceiling groaned, a long tectonic complaint, and a chunk of rock the size of a rowboat dropped past my shoulder and vanished into the deep with a boom I felt in my chest.

My air gauge read nine minutes. I'd started with thirty.

*You're going to die here,* said the part of my brain that had kept me alive through every condemned wreck and crooked contract of the last decade. *You're going to die in your father's cave, and he'll outlive you by four minutes, and the universe will find that very funny.*

"Shut up," I said into my regulator, which is not a sentence that accomplishes anything, but you say these things.

Then a hand closed on my ankle.

I nearly shot upward out of my skin — out of my harness, anyway, which amounts to the same thing down here. I spun, knife already in my fist, and found myself face to face with a shape in the murk. Older gear than mine. A homemade rebreather, jury-rigged, the kind of rig a man builds when he's been diving alone for two decades and has stopped caring whether the navy would approve.

Elias Nystrom looked at me through his mask, and even in the brown-out I could see his eyes go wide with fury.

He shoved me toward the exit.

I shoved back.

We did this — two grown adults, pushing each other through a collapsing cave like children fighting over the last biscuit — until he grabbed my wrist and pointed, emphatically, at the shrinking light, then pointed at himself, then shook his head.

No. Absolutely not. He was staying. The vigil-keeper to the end, guarding a treasure that was currently avalanching into the abyss without his permission, ready to go down with coffers that no longer existed.

I grabbed his collar with both hands and hauled myself nose-to-mask with him, and I shouted into my regulator — a wasted gesture, words don't carry, but some things need saying even if they only live in your own skull:

*I am not leaving without you. I have buried you once already. I dug you a grave with my own hands when I was eleven years old because they gave me an empty box and told me to grieve, and I will not — I will NOT — stand on the surface and watch this cave close over you too.*

Maybe he read my lips. Maybe he just read me — twenty years of absence notwithstanding, there are things a father knows about the set of his daughter's jaw. Because something in him changed. I watched it happen, right there in the killing dark: the warden flickering out and the man flickering on. Twenty years of duty losing, in the space of one exhaled bubble, to twenty years of guilt, and guilt — God help us — is a much stronger swimmer.

He took my hand. Actually took it, the way he must have done when I was small, crossing some street in some port town I barely remembered. And we swam.

* * *

The tunnel mouth was a slit now. Where the entrance had been a generous twelve meters wide, the rockfall had chewed it down to maybe two, and it was still eating. Chunks of ceiling came down in ones and twos, each impact punching shockwaves through the water, each one a little reminder that the cave had opinions about our departure and they weren't kind.

Elias went first. His frame was narrower than mine, his gear stripped down to essentials, and he folded himself through the gap like a man threading a needle he'd threaded a thousand times. Then the gap pulsed — another slab shifting above — and I saw him hesitate on the far side, turning back for me, and I screamed at him through the water, *go, GO,* and he went, which was the correct answer, which was the first time in this entire disaster that Elias Nystrom had chosen forward over backward.

I hit the gap three seconds later.

It's strange, the things you notice while squeezing through a closing wound in the earth. I noticed the barnacles on the lip of the rock, still alive, still filtering their little meals, oblivious. I noticed my tank housing scraping stone with a shriek I felt in my fillings. I noticed that I was laughing — actually laughing, a hysterical little hiccup of bubbles — because of course. Of course this was how it went. Not the syndicate's guns, not Corvane's fleet, not the tide itself. Geometry. We were going to be defeated by geometry.

I got stuck.

One heartbeat. Two. My tank wedged against the narrowing arch, fins kicking uselessly behind me, and the light beyond Elias's retreating silhouette dimmed as another section of the mouth sagged inward, and I had time to think, with perfect clarity: *Wren will blame herself. She'll think she decoded the ledger wrong. Someone has to tell her it wasn't the math, it was me, it was always me—*

Then I did the only thing left. I shrugged out of my tank harness, twisted sideways through the gap on residual breath, and let the cave take the tank instead. Fair trade. The tank had served me well; the cave could have it.

I shot through into open water, into the green-gold afternoon light of the sixth tide, and Elias's arms caught me before I'd traveled five meters — catching me the way fathers catch daughters, badly, gracelessly, both of us nearly drowning for the effort, and I didn't care. I hung onto him in the bright shaking water while behind us the cave mouth finished its meal with a roar I heard through my chest and my teeth and my bones, a sound like the sea swallowing a cathedral.

Dust rolled out of the entrance in a great gray exhalation, and then the water cleared, and then there was nothing there at all. Just cliff face. Just rock, seamless and ancient, as if the cave had never existed. As if the treasury had never existed. As if twenty years of my father's life had been spent guarding a room that the ocean had just decided to unmake.

We surfaced together, gasping, in the lee of the seamount, and the first thing I saw was the *Vagabond Grace* — our boat, our ridiculous, debt-ridden, hard-won boat — swinging at anchor fifty meters out with Wren standing at the rail, and the sound she made when she spotted us was not a word. It was older than words. Tomas was beside her, and beyond them, wrapped in blankets below decks where Odile lay healing or not-healing, and Corvane's patrol boat holding station at the edge of the cove like a sheepdog that had bitten its own shepherd.

Elias treaded water beside me, coughing, his jury-rigged rebreather hanging dead around his neck, and he looked at the cave — at the absence of the cave — and I watched twenty years leave his body all at once. Not gracefully. Like a man setting down a load he'd forgotten how heavy it was.

"It's gone," he said. His voice was wrecked — salt and disuse and something else. "August. It's gone."

"Yeah," I said.

"I guarded it for—"

"I know."

"Twenty years, and it's—" He stopped. Started again. "Was it worth it? Was any of it—" He couldn't finish. He was looking at me the way you look at a bill you can't pay.

I thought about answering honestly. About telling him that no, it hadn't been worth it, that a childhood of empty boxes and borrowed time doesn't balance against a vault of metal at the bottom of the sea, that he'd chosen a dead nation's gold over a living daughter and no amount of heroism retroactively fixes that. All true. All waiting to be said, someday, over bad coffee in better light.

But we were treading water in the wreckage of everything, and the syndicate's gunboats were regrouping beyond the point, and Corvane was burning his career for us even now, and Wren was screaming something joyful and furious from the rail, and my father — my impossible, maddening, resurrected father — was looking at me with an expression I'd only seen in mirrors: the face of someone hoping, against all evidence, to be let off the hook.

"You're alive," I said. "That's the only entry in the ledger that matters today. Everything else — we'll settle later. At length. With interest."

He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, rusty and crooked, and it was the first one I'd seen from him since I was nine years old, and I hated how much it undid me.

"Interest," he repeated. "Your mother used to say—"

"Don't." My voice cracked. "Don't do the nostalgia yet. Swim first. Grieve later. In that order."

We swam for the boat.

Halfway there, the first shockwave from the deep channel reached the surface — the treasury hitting bottom, tons upon tons of Vessian gold settling into the trench where no navy, no syndicate, no desperate diver would ever reach it. The water bucked under us once, gently, like the sea turning over in its sleep, and then went still.

Wren hauled me over the rail and held onto me hard enough to crack ribs, and Tomas was shouting about the engine and the tide and the gunboats, and none of it mattered for exactly four seconds, during which I stood dripping on the deck of the *Vagabond Grace* between a runaway and a disgraced engineer, watching my father climb aboard a boat he'd never seen, crewed by strangers who were not strangers, and I understood — finally, completely — what we'd salvaged.

Not the gold. Never the gold.

Then Tomas grabbed my shoulder and pointed past the headland, and the joy drained out of the morning like blood from a wound.

Three gunboats had rounded the point, running dark flags, and they weren't slowing down.
