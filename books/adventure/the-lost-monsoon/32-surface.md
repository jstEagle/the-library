## 32. Surface

**August**

The sea has opinions about what we just did, and it is shouting them.

We come up through the channel mouth in a wall of spray, the *Magpie* riding swells that are still arguing with each other, and behind us the water over the seamount is doing something I have no vocabulary for. It isn't boiling exactly. Boiling implies heat. This is the ocean itself flinching — a vast circular shudder, white water standing up in ridges, and beneath it a sound like the world clearing its throat to say something final.

Tomas has the wheel and both hands on it, knuckles gone the color of old rope. Wren is lashed to the rail beside me because I refused to let her work deck with her arm in that state, and she's screaming something at the collapsing cave mouth that might be triumph or grief or both, which, if you spend enough time around Wren, you learn is usually both.

"Is it down?" she shouts. "Is it actually down?"

I look back one more time. The last of the cavern roof goes with a sound I feel in my teeth rather than hear, a deep structural groan that travels through the water and up through the hull and into my spine, and then the sea closes over the place where twenty years of gold and one man's whole life of vigil used to be. A ring of foam marks the grave. In an hour even that will be gone.

"It's down," I say.

I should feel triumphant. That's the thing nobody tells you about winning — sometimes it arrives wearing the wrong clothes, looking suspiciously like loss, and you stand there wondering whether to cheer or check its pockets. We sank a treasury worth more than every debt on this coast combined. We sank it on purpose. And somewhere under all that moving water is the shack my father built, the kettle he kept warm, the bed he slept in for two decades while I counted air tanks and borrowed time and assumed he was bones somewhere.

He's aboard. He's in the wheelhouse right now, dripping seawater, wrapped in a blanket Wren gave him without being asked, sitting next to Odile's empty spot at the chart table because nobody has had the heart to move her kit off it yet.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The surface, first. The chaos. Let me do this in order, because order is the only inheritance I've got left.

The syndicate boats see it before we do — or rather, they feel it, the shockwave rolling out under their hulls, and whatever nerve the gunners had left snaps clean through. There were four of them when we ran the gauntlet at slack water: two fast cutters and a pair of converted trawlers bristling with more ambition than armor. Now they're turning. All four. Not retreating in formation, not falling back in good order — fleeing, engines wide open, cutting across each other's wakes like men running from a fire they started themselves. One of the trawlers takes a swell broadside and nearly broaches, and I watch men scramble on her deck, and I feel precisely nothing.

That's a lie. I feel something. I feel tired in a way that sleep won't fix.

"They're running," Wren says, wonder in it. "They're actually—"

"They think we blew the treasure," I say. "Which we did. Which means there's nothing left to kill us for."

Except there's always something left to kill people for. Revenge. Reputation. The principle of the thing. The syndicate didn't get rich by letting insults slide, and we didn't just insult them — we drowned their retirement plan in front of witnesses.

Then Corvane's ship comes around the headland, and everything gets complicated again.

* * *

**Corvane**

There is a particular silence that falls on a warship's bridge when her captain commits treason in real time. I had heard it once before, years ago, when a subordinate refused an order he should have followed and I'd backed him anyway. I did not appreciate then how loud that silence could get.

"Sir." Lieutenant Hask, at the signals board, holding the fleet's latest transmission as though it might bite him. "Admiralty again. They want confirmation of our firing solution on the cove."

"Tell them the target no longer exists."

"Sir, the seismic station already logged the subsidence event. They're asking why our guns never fired."

"Tell them," I said, "that we arrived too late."

It was even true, in the way most useful lies are true. We had arrived too late — too late to stop the demolition, too late to save the treasury for the men in the Admiralty who wanted it quietly disappeared into pension funds and political favors, too late to do anything except what I had spent three days discovering I was going to do regardless: put my ship between armed men and unarmed ones.

Because that was the other development. The syndicate cutters, in their panic, had done something unforgivably stupid. One of them had opened fire on the salvage vessel — the little green boat, the *Magpie* — as she cleared the channel. Two rounds, maybe three, wild in the heavy sea but unmistakably live. And my orders, the orders I had been receiving all morning from a fleet commander who had stopped pretending his interest in the Vessian affair was legal, were to let the syndicate handle "salvage disputes among civilians."

Civilians. As if Nystrom's crew hadn't been carrying my false coordinates in their pockets like a rosary.

"Helm," I said. "Come about to intercept. Put us between those cutters and the green hull."

Hask looked at me. Young man, decent, ambitious in the ordinary way that ambition is ordinary — which is to say, fatally. "Sir, those are syndicate vessels operating under letters of—"

"I know exactly what they're operating under. Helm, if you please."

The *Vigilant* came about with the ponderous grace of her class, and I watched the syndicate cutters understand, all at once, that the arithmetic had changed. Four boats against a corvette with real guns is not a fight; it's a eulogy with extra steps. They scattered. One went north, two went south, and the one that had fired on the *Magpie* tried to run straight out to sea, which told me everything about its captain's judgment and nothing about its chances.

"Signal the green vessel," I said. "Ask if they require assistance."

A pause on the comms. Then the reply came back, and I confess I laughed — actually laughed, alone on my own bridge, in front of my officers, which is the sort of thing careers have ended over.

The reply read: *No assistance required. But thank you for the escort.*

* * *

**Wren**

Here's what nobody tells you about almost dying: your body keeps doing it afterward, in installments. My arm throbbed where the splinter caught me during the skirmish, and my lungs kept taking these small practice breaths, like they didn't trust the big ones yet. And my hands wouldn't stop shaking, so I held onto the rail and pretended I was just holding onto the rail.

Elias Nystrom came and stood beside me.

I'd known him for less than a day. He was a legend-shaped hole in August's life, and now here he was, actual size, salt-crusted and gray-bearded and looking at the horizon like a man reading a letter. I didn't know what to say to him. He was August's father, which made him either the most important person on this boat or the least welcome, depending on the hour.

"You're hurt," he said.

"Splinter. Tomas already yelled at me about it."

"Tomas seems like a man who yells carefully."

"He does. It's his best quality." I looked at him sideways. "You really lived down there? Twenty years?"

"Nineteen. The first year I still had a boat." He said it flat, no self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "You learn to keep a garden of things that don't need daylight. You learn the tide schedule better than your own name."

"The ledger said divers' names. Yours was first."

Something moved through his face then, quick as a fish under ice. "You read Vessian."

"The orphanage taught us. They liked us literate — literate orphans fetch better prices." I shrugged, because it was either shrug or cry, and I'd done my crying quota for the season. "Turns out the worst year of my life was training for the best one."

He was quiet a moment. Then he said, "She built a good crew. August. I've been watching boats work this coast for nineteen years, and I know a good crew when I see one."

"She didn't build it. She collected it. Like strays." I nodded toward the wheelhouse, where August stood talking to Tomas, both of them gesturing at the chart table. "Mostly by refusing to give up on people who'd already given up on themselves. It's infuriating, actually. You'll see."

And then the shouting started, because Odile's fever broke bad.

* * *

**August**

We found her in the aft cabin, and I will tell you honestly that part of me had been waiting for this since the tunnel, since she took the bullet meant for Wren and smiled about it, because Odile smiling about anything was always a bill coming due later.

The bullet had gone through and through, which Tomas said was lucky. What wasn't lucky was the water — she'd been in the flooded lower passage before we pulled her out, and the marsh water on this coast carries things, and by the time we surfaced she was burning up and talking to people who weren't there.

Now she was gray as old canvas, propped against the bulkhead, and Elias — who apparently knew field medicine the way he knew tides, another item on the long invoice of things my father learned while I grew up — was cleaning the wound with the last of our medical alcohol.

"How long has she been cold?" he asked.

"An hour, maybe. She was sweating through the blanket at dawn."

"That's not dawn sweat leaving. That's the body giving up the argument." His hands were steady. Mine weren't. "Get me the needle and thread from the galley tin. And someone hold the lamp."

Odile's eyes opened. They found mine with the terrible accuracy she brought to everything, arithmetic included.

"Don't make that face," she rasped. "It's unbecoming. You look like Tomas."

"Tomas looks fine."

"Precisely my point." She coughed, wet and long, and Elias worked without looking up, and I held the lamp steady because that was the one job I couldn't fail at. "Nystrom. Listen. Before the fever takes the good parts of me."

"Odile—"

"No. No speeches. Speeches are for funerals and I intend to attend mine as a heckler." Her hand found my wrist, grip weaker than her voice, which was itself saying something. "The assets. Papers in the strongbox under my bunk, the real one, not the decoy. Everything signed over. Boat, accounts, the warehouse in Port Sable, the — god, the awful paintings. It's yours. All of yours. Split it however the crew votes."

"Why would you—" But I knew why. I'd seen her ledger face down in chapter after chapter of her life. Debts. She'd spent twenty years paying the wrong ones. "Odile, you're not dying. You don't get to skip out on the vote."

"Wouldn't dream of it." Her smile was the old one, sharp and crooked, but it flickered like a lamp low on oil. "Just... settling accounts in advance. Habit."

Elias tied off the last stitch and sat back on his heels, and I watched him look at her — really look — and I remembered the photograph from the strongbox. A young Odile Marchetti, twenty-some years old, standing beside my father on the deck of some dead boat, both of them laughing at something outside the frame. Both of them still believing the treasury would buy safety instead of graves.

"Elias," she said, and her voice changed, dropped its armor. "You old ghost."

"Hello, Odile."

"You look terrible."

"You look worse."

"Good." Her eyes closed. Opened again. "I informed on them, Elias. On all of them. You know that."

"I know."

"And you stayed down there anyway. Guarding it. Twenty years guarding the thing that got everyone killed, because someone had to." A breath that rattled. "Stupid, noble man. The worst kind."

"The only kind you ever tolerated."

Her hand slipped from my wrist. For one horrible second I thought that was it, right there, mid-sentence, unfinished business stacked to the rafters. But her chest kept moving, shallow and stubborn, and Elias pulled the blanket up over her shoulders with a gentleness I had never once seen in him and had now seen twice in an hour.

"She needs warmth, quiet, and luck," he said quietly. "In descending order of availability."

* * *

**Corvane**

By late afternoon the sea had remembered its manners, mostly, and the *Vigilant* stood off the *Magpie* at half a mile, close enough to watch, far enough to pretend we weren't hovering. I had filed my interim report — *target site suffered catastrophic subsidence, no recoverable assets observed, syndicate vessels dispersed* — and I had burned, in the incinerator behind the signal office, the folder containing the original survey charts, the Admiralty's private memoranda, and the list of names that explained why a drowned nation's treasury had been worth killing over for twenty years.

My career now rested on a geological fiction. I found I slept better for it.

The launch came alongside at dusk. Not navy — civilian, patched paint, a woman at the tiller with her arm bound in a sling, and beside her a young man whose posture announced *engineer* the way a uniform announces rank.

"We brought coffee," the girl called up. "It's terrible. Captain Nystrom says you've earned terrible coffee."

I looked at my executive officer. I looked at the boarding ladder. Somewhere south, the fleet was steaming toward coordinates I had falsified, and somewhere north, the syndicate was licking wounds and counting losses, and somewhere below decks of a patched green salvage boat, a woman was dying of a bullet she took for a child she'd helped traffic, in the great irony of a life that had run on worse ones.

"Mr. Hask," I said. "You have the ship."

"Sir?"

"I'm going to drink terrible coffee. Log it as a diplomatic courtesy."

He hesitated, and I saw him weigh the question every officer eventually faces — whether loyalty means following a man or understanding him. He settled, visibly, on understanding.

"Aye, sir. Diplomatic courtesy."

* * *

**August**

Night came down soft for once, the wind backing off to a whisper, the sea going glass-dark around the anchor light. Below decks, Odile slept — actual sleep, fever-bound but breathing, and Tomas sat outside her cabin door on an upturned crate because he'd appointed himself sentry and none of us had the heart to argue. Above decks, the rest of us drank terrible coffee on the stern while Corvane, out of uniform jacket, listened to Wren explain Vessian numerals with the intensity of a man discovering a new continent.

My father sat beside me. Not touching. Not speaking. Just present, the way he'd been absent for twenty years, as though presence were a skill he was relearning from the ground up.

"You could have taken some of it," he said finally. "Enough to disappear on. Enough to be safe."

"Safe." I turned my cup in my hands. "You know what I figured out down there, watching you guard that room? Safety's just debt with better collateral. Somebody always holds the note."

He absorbed that. He was good at absorbing; it was the expressing that needed work. "Your mother used to say—"

"I know what she used to say. I was there. Alone, mostly, but there."

The words landed harder than I'd aimed them, and I watched them hit, and I didn't take them back. Some debts you collect. That's the whole point of them.

But then Wren laughed at something Corvane said — actually laughed, bright across the dark water — and Tomas called up from below that Odile's fever had broken a degree, one degree, a single degree that felt like a country changing hands. And my father reached over, slow and careful, and put his hand on the rail next to mine, not quite touching, an offer shaped like proximity.

"One degree," he said.

"One degree," I agreed.

Below decks, in the lamplit cabin, Odile stirred against her pillows and beckoned me down with two fingers, and when I bent close she whispered, hoarse and urgent, like a woman passing along the combination to a safe:

"There's something you need to know about the ledger. About the sixth name on the diver list. Before the fever takes it back."

I froze, lamp in hand, the whole dark boat rocking gently around us.

"Tell me," I said.
