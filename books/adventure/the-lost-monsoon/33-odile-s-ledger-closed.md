## 33. Odile's Ledger Closed

There is a particular arithmetic to dying that no fence ever taught me. I have spent forty years pricing things — contraband, loyalty, silence, the occasional human being — and I thought myself fluent in every currency the world minted. But nobody quotes you a rate for your own blood leaving your body. Nobody tells you what a bullet through the belly costs until you're paying it in installments, each one colder than the last.

I lay on a bunk in the *Magpie's* hold — August's boat now, whatever the paperwork says; boats belong to whoever keeps them floating — and I did what I have always done when frightened. I counted.

One: the wound. Through and through, the ship's doctor said, which is doctor-speak for "through you and through your luck." Two: the blood loss, measured in the puddle they'd sopped up and the paleness of my own hands, hands that had never once trembled over a signature and were trembling now. Three: the fever, arriving on schedule like a debt collector. Four: the faces at the edge of my vision — Wren, bandaged and alive because I'd had the wit to be wide rather than quick; Tomas hovering with a kettle he didn't know what to do with; Corvane standing awkward as a man in a church of the wrong denomination.

And five: August Nystrom, sitting where she'd been sitting for six hours without moving, watching me with her father's eyes, which is to say with eyes that refuse to look away from a thing once they've decided to see it.

"Odile," she said, for perhaps the ninth time. "You should rest."

"I should do a great many things," I said. "Rest has never been among them. Rest is for people whose ledgers balance."

My voice was doing something embarrassing. Going thin. Going honest. Dying strips you the way salt water strips paint — slowly, then all at once, and everything underneath is the color you actually are.

* * *

Let me tell you about ledgers, since it seems I'm to be remembered by one.

The Vessian treasury was never gold to me, not originally. It was people. Twenty-two years ago I was a courier for a nation that was drowning — not metaphorically, though eventually also literally — and the dive team Elias Nystrom assembled was the last act of a government that had already ceased to exist on any map worth consulting. We stole a treasury from a collapsing state to feed refugees the world had decided to forget. That was the romance of it. I was nineteen and I believed in the romance.

Then the syndicate came, and the romance died the way most romances die: badly, in an alley, while I watched.

They killed Marat first. Then the Kessler twins. Then old Brandt, who'd taught me cipher work, who'd called me *kleine Füchsin*, little fox. They killed them one by one across three years, and each time they came to me first, because I was the survivor who could be bought, and I let myself be bought, and I told myself each name I sold was the last name, and it never was, because "the last name" is the oldest lie in commerce. It sits right next to "this shipment is clean" and "of course I'll pay you back."

Elias I never sold. Not because I was loyal — I want that understood clearly, even now, especially now. I didn't sell him because he was already dead as far as anyone knew, and dead men are worthless on the market. The one decent thing my cowardice ever accomplished was accidental. That is the shape of my whole life, if you're asking. Accidental decency, deliberate ruin.

So when I met his daughter in my shop two seasons ago, holding a scrap of Vessian ledger paper like it might bite her, I recognized two things at once. One: the coordinates were real, and there was more money under that water than I would see in ten lifetimes. Two: God, or fate, or whatever bookkeeper runs the universe, had finally sent me an invoice.

I intended to pay it. I also intended to profit. These are not contradictory impulses if you've lived my life; they're just two columns in the same book.

* * *

The fever broke toward evening, or rather it crested and left me wrung out and clear-headed, which is worse. Clear-headed, I could feel exactly how much of me remained, the way you can feel how much air is left in a tank if you've spent enough time underwater. Enough for a conversation. Maybe enough for two.

"Wren," I said.

She came over carefully. She'd taken shrapnel along her arm in the skirmish and wore the bandage like a trophy, which I approved of. At nineteen I'd have worn mine the same way.

"You read Vessian," I said. "Who taught you?"

Her chin came up. She wasn't going to give me the orphanage story, not to me, not after everything. Good. I hadn't earned it.

"I know why you're asking," she said instead. "August told us. About the team. About—" She stopped herself, which was kind, and continued anyway, which was braver. "About what you did."

"What I did," I agreed. "Yes."

"The orphanage sold kids to ships that needed bodies," Wren said. "The syndicate ran the contracts. So." She looked at me with those flat harbor-water eyes. "You helped run the machine that made me. And then you took a bullet meant for me. I don't know what to do with both of those facts."

"Neither do I," I said. "That's what makes it arithmetic instead of accounting. Some entries don't balance. You carry them forward."

She frowned. "That's not how carrying forward works."

"No," I said. "It isn't. But it's how guilt works, and guilt is the only ledger I've ever kept honestly."

She almost smiled. Almost. From her, that was a fortune, and I banked it.

"Tomas," I said next, and he appeared with the kettle still in his hands like a man holding a life preserver in a desert. "Reyes. Your disgrace. The *Corvina* inquiry."

His face went gray. "You wouldn't—"

"I'm dying, not cruel. There's a difference, though it's narrower than people think." I made myself sit up against the pillows, which cost me something I pretended it didn't. "Your record is sealed in three archives. After tonight it will be sealed in none, because I am having it burned. Every copy. Including the ones the syndicate used to keep you desperate and employable."

"Why?" His voice cracked on it. "Why would you—"

"Because engineers who panic in open water but dive anyway are rarer than honest money, and I have spent my whole life around the counterfeit version of courage. I know the real thing when I finally see it." I waved a hand, weakly. "Consider it a refund. I shorted you years ago, along with everyone else."

Tomas set down the kettle, sat on the edge of Corvane's cot, and put his head in his hands. Men cry differently than women — quieter, as if apologizing for it — and I listened to him apologize for crying and thought: *there goes another entry that will never balance.*

* * *

Night came down through the deck boards in slats. Corvane relieved himself from the sickbay and went topside to argue with the sky, or the navy, or whichever of his superiors he was currently betraying — the man has a talent for principled treason that I find genuinely moving. Tomas fell asleep sitting up. Wren stayed within earshot, pretending to mend a net, mending nothing.

Which left August.

She came and sat on the stool beside my bunk, and for a long time neither of us spoke, because we are, both of us, women who understand that the most expensive words are the ones you say before you've checked the exchange rate.

"I've drawn up papers," I said finally.

"I figured."

"Of course you figured. You always figure. It's insufferable. It's your father all over." I gestured, and she leaned close so I could speak softly and not spend myself on volume. "Everything. The warehouse in Port Serrat. The accounts under the nine names. The *Gilded Hour*. The safe-deposit holdings, the fences who owe me, the fences I owe — those cancel, don't worry, I've been tidy. It all goes to the crew. Jointly. Not to you — to all of them, jointly, so no one can ever be bought alone the way I was."

August was quiet a moment. "You're buying your way into heaven."

"Heaven doesn't exist. I checked the market thoroughly." I found her hand on the blanket and put the pen in it. "I'm buying my way out of hell, which does exist, and I've been living in it for twenty-two years at compound interest. Sign. Witnessed. Wren, put down that net and witness."

Wren witnessed. Tomas woke up enough to scrawl his name looking like a seismograph reading. Even Corvane came back down and signed with the neat, doomed flourish of a man destroying his own career in longhand.

"There," I said, when it was done. "The Marchetti ledger is closed. Every asset distributed, every debt either paid or converted to a scholarship fund for harbor orphans — yes, Wren, I know exactly which orphans, I've known for years, I funded that orphanage's competitors out of spite and it was the only spite I ever enjoyed."

"That's not closure," August said quietly. "That's just spending."

"It's what I have. Spending is what I have." My voice was going again, fraying at the edges like wet rope. "You want to know what I don't have? What no amount of it buys?"

"Don't," she said.

"Young lady, I am dying of a gunshot wound on a boat I gave away an hour ago. I will say what I please." I turned my head on the pillow to look at her straight on, and it hurt, and I was glad of the pain because pain is proof of attention. "Forgiveness. That's the item. The one thing in the world that isn't for sale, and I've spent my whole life assuming that meant it didn't exist. Everything can be bought. That was my religion. And here at the end I find the one commodity I need operates entirely outside my currency, and I am—" the word came out smaller than I wanted, "—broke."

"Odile."

"My name is on the syndicate order that reached the Kesslers' boat. My cipher mark. I didn't pull the trigger, I never pulled a trigger, I just drew the maps to everyone who did. Your father watched me do it and never once said my name aloud again, and he was right to, and I have thought about that silence every day for twenty years, and now you're going to sit there and be decent at me, and I would rather you shot me than be decent at me—"

"Odile." Her hand closed over mine, and it was steady, and I hated how much steadiness costs the person lending it. "Stop selling yourself short. For once in your life."

I stopped. Mostly because I'd run out of breath, but partly because no one had ever accused me of that particular failing, and it landed somewhere unarmored.

* * *

What she gave me was not forgiveness. I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only integrity I have left, and I'll not have the story softened after I'm gone.

"I can't forgive you," she said. "Not tonight. Maybe not ever. Marat and the Kesslers aren't mine to forgive — their families would have to do that, and I don't even know where their families are. And my father—" her jaw worked. "He gets a vote too, and he's not here to cast it."

"So I die unforgiven."

"You die *honest*," she said. "Which is more than you'd have managed a year ago, and more than most people manage ever." She was quiet a moment, choosing words the way she chose dive weights — exactly enough, nothing extra. "But here's what I can give you. You asked for the one thing you can't buy. Fine. But you got the terms wrong. Forgiveness isn't the only thing outside your currency. There's something cheaper."

"What."

"A place in the story." She said it simply, the way you'd state a heading on a chart. "When this is over — when we've settled everything, when the boat's ours outright — I'm going to write it all down. The treasury, the tides, all of it. And you'll be in it. Not the cipher mark. Not the informant. *Odile Marchetti*, who decoded the ledger, who outfitted a crew of nobodies, who took a bullet for a girl the machine built to be disposable. Both columns. The whole entry. People will read it and they won't get to decide you were only one thing, because the record won't let them."

I stared at her. Somewhere in my chest the ruined machinery of my heart did something complicated and painful that I choose to attribute to the wound.

"That's not nothing," I managed.

"It's not forgiveness," she agreed. "It's smaller. It's truer. Forgiveness is a feeling, and feelings evaporate. A story is a *record*. Records persist." A ghost of a smile. "You taught me that, actually. 'Paper outlives blood.' Your words, the night we met."

"Paper outlives blood," I echoed. My eyes were leaking, which I blamed on the fever, and everyone present had the grace to blame it on the fever too. "God help me, I taught a Nystrom something. Put that in the story. Make it humiliating."

"I'll make it accurate. Same thing."

* * *

Near dawn the pain changed registers, and I knew what the change meant, the way an old diver knows what the change in light means before the storm. I sent the others up — all except August, who refused, and I hadn't the strength to make her, and wouldn't have if I'd had it.

"One more piece of business," I said. "In the strongbox I gave you, beneath the bullion manifests. A letter. Sealed. It's addressed to a woman named Ilse Kessler, née Voss, living in Tarnholt under her married name. She's the widow of one of the men I sold. The letter confesses everything, names names, includes the location of the graves — I paid to have them marked, twenty years ago, anonymously. She deserves the truth from a living mouth, not a posthumous one. See it delivered. Don't open it. Don't soften it."

"I'll deliver it myself," August said.

"Of course you will. Insufferable." I closed my eyes. The counting had stopped. For the first time in forty years, I wasn't counting anything. It felt like falling and it felt like rest and it felt, unexpectedly, like the tide going out over a flat you've walked a thousand times — familiar ground, briefly visible, on its way to being sea.

"August," I said. "The story. When you write it. Give me a good death scene. I've earned at least *that* much vanity."

"You're not dead yet."

"No," I agreed, smiling at the deck beams. "But I've seen the books, and I know how this chapter ends."

She laughed — wet, unwilling, real — and held my hand, and outside the hull the sixth tide was turning, and somewhere above us Corvane was lying to his own fleet to keep us alive, and below us the treasury I had chased for twenty-two years was settling into the dark where it could never buy another life.

It was, all things considered, the best-balanced account I ever closed.

* * *

I did not die that dawn. I want that on the record too, since records apparently matter now. What happened instead was this: the fever broke for good near noon, the bleeding clotted where wounds clot when the body is too stubborn to consult the odds, and Doctor Ansel — who had given me two days with the confidence of a man quoting freight rates — revised his estimate upward with visible irritation, as though I'd personally inconvenienced medicine.

By evening I was propped on pillows, gray as driftwood, alive against all professional advice, listening to August read aloud from the charter papers Tomas had drafted — wreck documentation, survey work, honest salvage, Corvane consulting under the absurd alias "Mr. Corbin" — and hearing my own name included in the crew roster, in ink, witnessed, binding.

"You put me on the charter," I said. "I'm a dying criminal with a hole in her gut."

"You're a navigator with contacts in nine ports," August said, not looking up. "And dying criminals recover. It's in the story."

"What story?"

"The one I'm writing." She folded the papers and looked at me then, and her father's eyes had her mother's stubbornness in them, or maybe that was just hope, which looks the same from a distance. "Chapter thirty-three. You're going to want to stick around for the ending. I hear it's good."

Outside, the season's first monsoon wind was rising, and the *Magpie* pulled at her lines like something that remembered how to run, and I lay there in the cheapest bed I'd ever owned and the richest room I'd ever stood in, and began — very quietly, so no one would hear — to count the days I might yet have.

For the first time in my life, the number kept getting bigger.
