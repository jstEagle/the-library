## 35. Debts Paid

The lawyer Odile sent us was named Prakash, and he had the particular sadness of a man who had spent thirty years turning other people's sins into paperwork. He arrived at the dock on a Tuesday morning in a suit too good for the salt air, carrying a leather folio and an expression like a man delivering a eulogy to people who weren't sure the deceased was actually dead.

"She's still alive," I said.

"For now," Prakash agreed. "But Mrs. Marchetti is not a woman who leaves things to chance, or to her own recovery. She asked me to tell you—" he opened the folio with the reverence of a priest, "—that she has signed over everything."

Everything turned out to be a word doing a lot of heavy lifting. There was the counting house on Cordwainer Street, its ledgers full of debts owed *to* Odile rather than by her — a distinction that apparently made all the difference in the eyes of the law. There were three warehouses, two of them legitimate. There was a boat called the *Mercy of Numbers*, which Odile had bought years ago under a false name for reasons Prakash declined to speculate about, because speculating was how lawyers got thrown off piers. And there was a letter, sealed in wax, addressed to me in handwriting that shook only slightly.

I didn't open it at the dock. Some letters you need to read alone, and some you need to read after coffee, and this one required both.

"You'll want an accountant," Prakash said.

"I have a Tomas."

"Mr. Reyes keeps books?"

"Tomas keeps engines. But he yells numbers at them until they behave, and I've seen him do it. It's close enough."

Prakash looked at me the way men look at women who frighten their accountants, and left his card anyway.

* * *

The first creditor I paid was a man called Hobb, who ran the air-tank concession at the salvage yard and had spent two years charging me interest calculated by methods I'm fairly sure involved dice. He'd repossessed my regulator once, mid-season, and I'd finished a job breathing off a garden hose rigged through a bilge pump, so when I walked into his office and laid the full amount on his desk in banded notes, I confess I took a certain pleasure in watching his face do arithmetic it hadn't prepared for.

"That's everything?" he said. "Principal and—"

"Principal, interest, the late fees you invented, and the fee for the fee. I read your contract, Hobb. It's a work of fiction. I'd admire it if I didn't hate you."

He counted it twice, which was wise of him, and then had the decency to look faintly ashamed, which was more than I expected. "You always struck me as a woman who'd die owing me money."

"I planned to. Plans change." I picked up my regulator from his shelf — he still had it, tagged, waiting for a buyer — and set down its price beside the rest. "That one too. Consider us square."

"Square," he repeated, tasting the word like it was foreign coinage.

It was the strangest thing, walking out of that office. For two years I had measured every decision against Hobb's ledger. Take the condemned wreck or lose the tanks. Dive the restricted water or miss the payment. Every choice I'd made since the chest came up out of the river had been made inside a box with Hobb's name on the lid, and now the lid was open and I kept bracing for it to slam shut again.

The second creditor cried. Old Marta Voss, who'd lent me the money for Wren's boots and the galley stove and had never once mentioned interest, because she wasn't really a creditor so much as a woman who'd decided a half-starved diver and her feral deckhand needed feeding. I paid her back double and she pushed half of it right back across the table and told me to buy something warm for the girl, and I let her, because there are debts you honor by refusing to settle them completely.

The third one I paid in person, standing in a bank manager's office while he tried very hard not to look at the way my jacket still smelled of bilge. The Harrow Street Consolidated held the paper on the *Vesper*, my boat — our boat, though the deed hadn't learned that yet — and had held it since my father vanished and the payments became mine by grim inheritance. Twelve years of never missing a month. Twelve years of diving jobs I shouldn't have taken, in water I shouldn't have trusted, because the alternative was watching them tow her away.

The manager counted the payoff twice, stamped something, and slid a document across the desk.

"Free and clear," he said. "Congratulations."

I looked at the paper. *Registered owner: August Nystrom.* Just that. No lien, no encumbrance, no bank's name squatting in the margin like a squatter with a stamp. Twelve years, and it fit on one page.

"Is that all?" I asked, and heard how strange my voice sounded asking it.

"That's all, Ms. Nystrom. She's yours."

She'd been mine the whole time, in every way that mattered. But owning a thing and owing a thing can live in the same hull for years, the way a family can, and it takes a stamp on a page to sort out which is which.

* * *

Tomas found me that evening sitting in the engine room with the deed, reading it over and over like it might change if I blinked.

"You're doing the thing," he said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you hold a piece of paper like it's going to bite you. You did it with the compass. You did it with Odile's letter — don't think I didn't see you reading it four times in the head with the door locked." He lowered himself onto the toolbox across from me, which was his chair the way the whole engine room was his chair, and wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime last season. "So. We're rich."

"We are absolutely not rich."

"There's a warehouse."

"One warehouse, and it's full of smuggled brandy that we legally cannot sell without becoming the kind of people we just spent six tides running from."

"Two warehouses, then. One empty."

"Prakash says the second one has a structural problem."

"Everything has a structural problem. That's what makes it interesting." He leaned forward. "August. Say it out loud. What does the ledger say? Our ledger, the real one, the one I keep in my head because paper burns."

I said it, because he was right and saying it out loud was the only way to make it true. "Nothing. We owe nothing. Not the bank, not Hobb, not the chandlery, not the fuel dock. The *Vesper*'s ours outright. There's money in the account for wages — real wages, a year of them, for everyone — and the charter contract with the survey institute starts next month."

Tomas whistled, low and long. "And how does it feel?"

I thought about lying to him, because lying to Tomas was practically a crew tradition at this point, but the engine room has a way of stripping pretense. Maybe it's the grease. Maybe it's that no one can maintain a lie and a diesel engine at the same time.

"Quiet," I said.

"Quiet."

"It feels quiet. Like someone turned off an engine I've been listening to so long I forgot it was running. All those years, there was this sound underneath everything — the payment, the deadline, the number I was short. And now it's gone and I keep waiting for it to come back. I woke up this morning and lay there for ten minutes trying to remember what I was supposed to be afraid of, and there wasn't anything, and honestly, Tomas, it was terrifying."

He nodded slowly, the way he did when an engine made a noise he recognized. "First time I got a ship of my own to fix — after the disgrace, after everyone stopped returning my letters — I sat in the engine room all night with nothing broken. Nothing. Do you know what an engineer does in an engine room where nothing is broken?" He didn't wait for an answer. "He waits for it to break. Because that's the only language he knows. It took me a year to learn that silence isn't a fault in the system. Sometimes silence is just the system working."

"And sometimes it's the calm before the syndicate shows up with gunboats."

"Well. Yes. That too." He stood, and clapped me on the shoulder with a hand that could strip a bolt. "But tonight, nobody's shooting. Tonight the system works. Try letting it."

* * *

Wren took the news differently, which is to say she took it like a cat takes a bath: with suspicion, followed by grudging acceptance, followed by an attempt to pretend none of it had happened.

I told her at dinner — real dinner, meat that had been purchased rather than traded, bread from the bakery instead of the emergency stores — that her wages were paid through next year, that there was a fund set aside for whatever schooling she wanted, that the survey charter would need a reader of old scripts and dead languages and she was, as far as any of us knew, the only one aboard.

She pushed her food around her plate. "You don't have to pay me extra for the Vessian stuff."

"I'm not paying you extra. I'm paying you what you're worth, which I have been criminally underpaying since the day I fished you out of that harbor."

"I fished myself out of that harbor. You just happened to be there."

"Happened to be there with a boat and a wage and terrible cooking. Luck counts."

She almost smiled. Then the suspicion came back, because it always did, because the orphanage had built it into her the way shipwrights build in a keel. "What happens when the money runs out?"

"It won't run out in a year."

"It always runs out." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you report weather. "Money runs out, or people do. Usually both."

"Wren." I waited until she looked up. "When it runs out — and someday it will, because that's what money does — we'll go earn more. That's the whole arrangement. That's what a crew is. It's not a purse, it's a machine for making more of itself. You're part of the machine now. Stop looking for the day they throw you off."

She stared at me for a long moment, and I watched her do the thing she did — checking my face for the lie, finding the lie absent, being briefly and visibly unsettled by its absence.

"Okay," she said finally, and went back to her food, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough. With Wren, *okay* was a treaty signed in ink.

Later that night I found her in the chart room with the compass open in her lap, tracing the worn engraving inside the case with one finger. My father's initials. Her father, in every way that had ever mattered, which was none and all of them at once. She didn't look up when I came in, and I didn't stay, because some inheritances need privacy to settle.

* * *

Elias took it worst of all of us, though he'd never have admitted it. He'd been aboard three weeks, sleeping in the fo'c'sle like a guest who couldn't quite believe the invitation, fixing things nobody had asked him to fix. He was very good at fixing things. It was the only conversation he knew how to start.

I told him about the debt at breakfast, mostly as small talk, and watched him go still over his coffee.

"All of it?" he said. "The Harrow Street paper too?"

"Paid off yesterday. The *Vesper*'s mine. Ours." I corrected myself, and saw him flinch at the correction, because *ours* included him now and he was still learning what that cost. "Why?"

"Because I used to dream about that," he said. "Down in the cave. Owning the boat free and clear. I'd run the numbers in my head, twenty years of numbers, what it would take. I figured if I ever got out, that would be the first thing. Before seeing you. Before anything." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "Twenty years underwater dreaming of a bank stamp."

"Dad—"

"I know." He held up a hand. "I know what you told me. Slowly. Honestly. Starting with not pretending three weeks of fixing pumps makes up for twenty years." He looked out the porthole at the harbor, at the boats riding at anchor, all of them owned by somebody, all of them owed on by somebody else. "It's just strange, August. To spend your whole life guarding a treasure, and find out the thing you actually wanted was worth less than a year of honest wages."

"That's not what the treasure was for, though. Was it?" I'd asked him this before, in the cave, with the water rising. He'd answered then. But some questions you ask twice, because the answer changes as the person answering changes.

"No," he said quietly. "It was never for having. It was for keeping. There's a difference, and it took me twenty years and nearly losing you to learn it." He drained his coffee and stood, and paused at the door the way he always did, like a man still half-expecting a door to be a bulkhead. "The survey charter. The institute. Will they want divers with... history?"

"They want divers who can document a wreck without stealing it. You're overqualified."

He smiled — a real one, small and crooked, the first I'd seen that wasn't aimed at machinery — and went to fix something nobody had asked him to fix.

* * *

Odile died on a Thursday, eleven days after the papers were signed, which meant she'd known exactly how much time she had when she signed them, which meant the signing had been the last honest act of a woman who had spent forty years making dishonesty profitable. Prakash wrote to tell me. The letter was brief and professional, except for one line at the end, which I suspect he added on his own:

*She asked me to say that the accounts are settled, and that she hoped, in the end, to have been worth the trouble.*

We buried her — or rather, we attended the burial, because Odile had arranged her own funeral down to the flowers, and the flowers were lilies, and there were a hundred of them, because even dead she refused to do anything at less than extravagant scale. Half the harbor underworld showed up in mourning clothes tailored to hide knives. Corvane came too, in civilian clothes, standing at the back where no one would look twice at him, and caught my eye across the graveside with an expression I'd come to know: the look of a man keeping a private ledger of his own.

Wren cried, which surprised everyone including Wren. Tomas put a hand on her shoulder and left it there. Elias stood apart, because Odile had been his crew once, twenty years and a betrayal ago, and grief doesn't care which side of a ledger it falls on.

I didn't cry. I stood at the grave and thought about a woman who had sold our route to the syndicate and kept the wreck's location back, who had informed on her own team and spent the rest of her life buying things she couldn't feel, who had taken a bullet for a girl she'd known five months and signed her fortune to strangers she'd known less. There was no version of Odile that was simple. I'd stopped needing one somewhere around the fourth tide.

At the wake — because of course there was a wake, with brandy from the warehouse we legally couldn't sell, served anyway, because Odile would have insisted — Prakash pressed a final envelope into my hands. Inside was a single sheet, in Odile's hand, dated the week before she died.

*Nystrom. If you're reading this, the doctors were wrong about the timeline, which is typical. A few things I didn't say, because saying them felt like begging: Thank you. Don't waste it. And when you write your story — and you will, you have the face of a woman who writes things down — give me a better ending than I earned. I'd have done it for you.*

I folded the letter and put it in my pocket, next to the deed, next to the compass I no longer carried because it lived with Wren now.

"A better ending than I earned," I said aloud, to no one.

"You talking to ghosts?" Tomas asked, appearing with two glasses of contraband brandy.

"Just taking dictation."

* * *

That night I walked the dock alone. The *Vesper* rode at her moorings, riding light, no longer weighed down by anyone's claim but our own. I stood on the planks and listened to the harbor sounds — halyards ticking against masts, water slapping hulls, the distant clang of the night shift at the yards — and waited for the fear to come back.

It didn't. The silence just stayed, wide and open, like a sea room on a chart with nothing marked in it. No creditors. No deadlines. No navy fleet, no syndicate gunboats, no six-tide clock. Just a boat, a crew, a contract for honest work starting in three weeks, and a life that belonged to me for the first time since I was eleven years old.

Freedom, it turns out, is quiet. Nobody warns you about that. They tell you it rings like a bell, sings like a bird, all the metaphors for noise. Nobody tells you it's the absence of the sound you've been living inside so long you mistook it for your own heartbeat, and that the first few nights you'll lie awake listening for it, missing it, grieving it a little, before you learn to hear what's underneath.

Underneath, when I finally stopped straining, was the crew. Tomas arguing with the new generator in the engine room, a low mutter of profanity and affection. Wren reading aloud to herself in the chart room, Vessian syllables turning into English ones. Elias's footsteps on the deck above, unhurried, learning the boat's rhythms the way he'd once learned a cave's.

A machine for making more of itself.

I was still standing there, learning the quiet, when the harbor watch came down the pier with a lantern and a folded paper, calling my name.

"Ms. Nystrom? Message came in on the evening packet. Survey institute." He handed it over, apologetic about the hour. "Says it's urgent. Says the wreck site moved."

I broke the seal and read it twice, and the quiet, which I had only just begun to trust, shifted underneath me like a tide changing its mind.
