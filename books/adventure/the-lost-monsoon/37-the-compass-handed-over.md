## 37. The Compass Handed Over

The morning after I paid off the last of my creditors, I woke up at six out of habit and lay there staring at the deckhead with nothing to dread.

It was an unsettling experience. For eleven years my days had come pre-loaded with arithmetic — air tanks against hours, wages against interest, one man's name against every tide. Now the ledger in my head was blank, and it turned out a blank page is its own kind of vertigo. I'd spent so long being owed-to and owing that ownership felt like standing on a dock that had quietly unmoored itself.

The boat — *my* boat now, deed and all, a fact I kept testing like a loose tooth — rocked gently at her berth in Corvane's harbor. Well. Not Corvane's harbor. The harbor of a coastal town whose name I still couldn't say without smiling: Saltrest, population four hundred, two of whom had ever heard of Vessania, and both of them were currently asleep aboard my ship.

I climbed out of the bunk, made coffee bad on purpose because good coffee felt like someone else's life, and went up into a morning so clean it looked scrubbed.

Wren was already in the cockpit, cross-legged on the chart table with the Vessian ledger open in front of her, copying something into a notebook in her small, precise hand. She did this most mornings now. She said the script was like a language you'd stop speaking if you didn't use it, and she never explained why she wanted to keep speaking it, and I never asked, because some things you let people carry until they're ready to set them down.

"Morning," she said, not looking up.

"Morning."

"You're hovering."

"I'm standing adjacent."

"You're hovering *adjacent*." She glanced up. She was seventeen going on forty, all sharp elbows and sharper eyes, hair chopped by her own hand with what appeared to have been a fish knife. "You've got your serious face on. The one where you look like you're doing math about me."

"I'm always doing math about you. It's called payroll."

"It's called something." She closed the notebook but kept her hand flat on it, the way she did when a conversation was heading somewhere she hadn't charted. Wren navigated conversations the way Tomas navigated engine rooms — by feel, by sound, by the smell of trouble before it started. "Out with it, Captain."

So I took the compass out of my pocket.

I'd been carrying it there for three weeks — since the collapse, since the surface, since the hospital corridor where Odile's lawyer put a folder in my hands heavier than any strongbox. Before that it had lived in a tin box under my bunk for eleven years, and before that in my father's coat pocket, and before that — if the engraving inside the case meant what we finally learned it meant — in the hands of men who stole a nation's treasury to feed its refugees, and died for it in black water.

It was a plain thing, honestly. Brass gone green at the seams, glass scratched like a cat had raised it. On the back, worn nearly smooth, the crest of a country that no longer existed. Inside the lid, in letters you needed the right light and the right grief to read: coordinates that matched no chart, only a seamount cave only reachable at the sixth tide, guarded for twenty years by a ghost.

By my father. Who was currently ashore, awkwardly learning how to be alive in public, buying eggs.

"I've been thinking about inheritance," I said.

Wren's eyebrows performed their full repertoire. "That's a terrible opening line."

"It gets better."

"Doubtful."

"The way I grew up," I said, "inheritance was a dirty word dressed as a promise. My father left me a compass and a hole. That was the estate. Everything else I built myself or borrowed against, mostly borrowed." I turned the compass over in my palm. The morning sun caught the scratched glass. "And I used to think that was the whole story. Blood gives you a starting point and then it's on you. But that's not true either, is it. Blood didn't give me Tomas. Blood didn't give me Odile's last three weeks, or Corvane burning his career in a filing cabinet, or you reading dead men's handwriting at dawn because somebody has to remember them properly."

Wren had gone very still. She had a sailor's stillness, the kind you learn when moving means falling.

"I'm not following," she said, which was a lie, because Wren followed everything; she just liked to make you say it plainly first. Orphanage habit, probably. Nobody gives you anything in those places without attaching terms.

I set the compass on the chart table between us, next to her notebook, next to the ledger of a drowned country.

"I'm saying it's yours."

She looked at it. Then at me. Then at it again, longer, and I watched her do the math she thought I couldn't see — what it was worth, what it had cost, what accepting it might obligate.

"That's your father's," she said carefully.

"It was my father's. He gave it to me. And I'm giving it to you, which makes it mine to give, which is the entire point of ownership as far as I can tell. You don't get to keep things. You get to pass them on well or badly." I shrugged. "He passed it on badly. Took him twenty years underwater to figure out the difference. I'm trying to beat his time."

"August—"

"Hear the terms first. There are terms. I learned terms from a fence, so brace yourself."

That got the ghost of a smile. Wren and Odile had understood each other from the start, two survivors of institutions that chewed children and called it care. We'd scattered some of Odile's ashes off the salt marshes two weeks ago, at the second site, where the ledger's tide tables pointed and where a girl from a trafficking orphanage had once decoded her own history out of a dead language. Wren had read the committal herself, in Vessian, voice steady as a depth gauge. Then she'd cried in the head for forty minutes and come out and asked what was for dinner, and nobody mentioned it, because that's family too.

"Terms," she repeated.

"One: it's not payment. You've been paid. You'll keep being paid, actual money, because I ran a debt operation for a decade and I refuse to run a gratitude operation now. Two: it doesn't make you crew — you already were crew, that happened somewhere around the third tide and none of us voted on it. Three—" I stopped. This was the part I'd rehearsed at four in the morning, staring at the deckhead, and of course it came out wrong anyway, coming out sideways, the way true things do. "Three: identity isn't issued. I spent years thinking I was the daughter of a dead thief, and that name fit like a boot two sizes wrong, and I walked miles in it anyway because it was the only pair I had. Turns out you can build your own. Takes longer. Fits better. So this isn't blood handing down to blood. It's choice handing to choice. You chose us. In the marsh, in the cave, with the gunboats closing in — you stayed. Nobody stays for a paycheck. So."

I nudged the compass an inch toward her.

"So it goes to the person who can actually read what it points at. Literally and otherwise."

For a long moment Wren didn't touch it. Her hand hovered over the brass like it was hot, or holy, or possibly both, and I remembered what she'd told us once, drunk on half a cup of smuggled rum, about the orphanage teaching her Vessian script — not out of scholarship, but because stolen goods need fences who can read provenance. They'd made her a tool for handling other people's lost things. She'd been decoding ever since, one way or another, trying to find the message that was actually addressed to her.

"This is addressed to me?" she asked quietly. Not the compass. The question underneath it.

"Yeah," I said. "First-class postage."

She picked it up.

She held it the way Tomas held tools he respected — firmly, close to the body, aware of what damage it could do. She opened the lid. The needle swung, wobbled, settled north with a faint shiver, indifferent as ever to the weight of human meaning we kept loading onto it. Compasses are honest that way. They don't care what you've survived. They just point.

"The coordinates are still in here," she said. "Inside the lid."

"I know."

"We could grind them out. Make it clean."

"Could." I leaned against the rail. Below us, a fishing boat chugged out toward the bar, its wake unspooling behind it like a sentence finishing itself. "But I'm done pretending the past is a hole you fall into. It's more like ballast. You don't cut it loose — you just make sure it's sitting low and even, so it keeps you upright instead of capsizing you." I nodded at the compass. "Let it say what it says. You know what it cost. That's the difference between carrying a thing and being dragged by it."

Wren closed the lid with a click that sounded final in the good way, like a contract signed, like a hatch dogged before weather.

Then she did something I didn't expect. She stood up, crossed the cockpit, and hugged me — quick and fierce and slightly bony, over before I'd finished being startled by it.

"Thank you," she said into my shoulder, muffled.

"Don't make it weird."

"Too late. It's extremely weird. You gave me a haunted antique and a speech."

"The speech was load-bearing."

"It really wasn't." She stepped back, and her eyes were wet and her chin was up, and she tucked the compass into the inner pocket of her jacket — the pocket, I noted, where she kept the things that mattered: her notebook, a photograph of the original dive team that she'd insisted on keeping even though it hurt, a strip of paper with Odile's cipher mark drawn on it that she refused to explain. "I'm going to put it in the log," she announced. "Officially. 'Received: one compass, brass, haunted. Condition: sentimental.'"

"Log entries should be factual."

"That *is* factual." She was already climbing down the companionway, talking over her shoulder. "Also I'm making breakfast, and if you say anything about my eggs I'm feeding yours to the gulls."

"They're always perfect, Chef."

"Liar. Get down here."

* * *

Elias arrived at nine, carrying eggs, bread, and the particular hesitancy of a man visiting a place he used to own in a different life. Twenty years underwater will do that to you. Even above water, even dry and shaved and wearing a shirt Tomas had lent him that fit like a tent, my father moved through doorways like a guest — pausing at thresholds, asking permission with his shoulders.

We ate breakfast in the cockpit, the four of us, and it was almost normal, which for our family constituted a miracle requiring paperwork. Elias told a story about a supply run in '09 that had gone wrong in a way that was apparently funny now, involving a goat, and Wren laughed the real laugh, the one she rationed, and Tomas snorted coffee, and I sat there watching my father's face in daylight — really looking at it, the new lines, the old ones — and felt the strange double exposure of him: the legend and the man, the warden and the dad, superimposed and slowly, slowly resolving.

Afterward, while Tomas went below to argue with the freshwater pump — their relationship was long, bitter, and loving — Elias lingered at the gangway.

"Auggie." He still said it like an apology. Maybe he always would. We were working on it. "The charter papers. Reyes showed me the draft last night. Documentation work, survey contracts, the university partnership." He turned his cap in his hands. "You'll want a diver with deep-cave experience on the roster. Someone who knows the seamount approaches. I could—"

"Dad." I held up a hand. "We talked about this. Slowly. Starting with honesty. Remember?"

"I'm being honest. I'm offering my résumé."

"Your résumé is twenty years of classified treasure-guarding and faking your death. HR is going to have questions."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, but the tectonic precursor of one. "Fair."

"But," I said, because I'd thought about this too, at four in the morning, in the blank-page quiet, "there's a survey contract coming up. Merchant wreck off the cape, harmless, boring, exactly the kind of work we're rebranding around. And the client wants a consultant who knows the local water." I let him hang there for exactly as long as he'd once let me hang over a childhood. "Talk to Tomas. First mate handles hiring now. That's the structure. You start at the bottom like everyone did."

"At the bottom," he repeated, tasting it.

"Everyone starts there. Some of us just start there twice."

He nodded, slow, and I watched him accept it — really accept it, the smallness of it, the dignity of it — and he shook Tomas's hand through the hatch a minute later, and I heard Tomas say, gruffly, "You'll be hauling anchor lines same as anyone, old man," and Elias say, "Good," and mean it.

Some inheritances you hand over in ceremonies. Some you hand over in anchor lines.

* * *

Evening found me at the rail with the harbor going gold and pink, doing nothing, gloriously. The new charter agreement sat below decks in a folder, waiting for signatures tomorrow — Wren as navigator and archivist, Tomas as engineer and first mate, Elias as deckhand-diving-consultant-probationary, and a line item Corvane had insisted on, consulting services rendered under the name "Mr. Salt," which fooled absolutely no one and delighted everyone. A found family, formalized in a handshake and bad coffee. Tomorrow.

Tonight, though, there was one more piece of business, and it came walking up the dock with a canvas bag and a scowl.

Corvane — Mr. Salt, retired, allegedly — stopped at the gangway and jerked his chin at the boat. "Got your mail," he said. "Came to the harbormaster. Addressed to the captain of the vessel *Vessa's Wake*." He pronounced it carefully, having been coached. "You registered a boat name without telling me?"

"We rechristened her last week. Where were you?"

"Avoiding sentimentality. It's a full-time post." He handed down the envelope, and I saw the return address and my stomach did something complicated, because the return address was a law firm in the capital, and law firms in the capital had only ever brought me invoices.

I opened it on the spot. Corvane, shamelessly, read over my shoulder, because boundaries were a concept he'd jettisoned along with his career.

It was short. Two paragraphs. The first was legal boilerplate confirming the transfer of remaining assets per the Marchetti estate settlement — accounts, a warehouse, a fast little cutter named *Verity* that Odile had apparently owned under a fourth alias we hadn't found yet, because of course she had.

The second paragraph was handwritten, in a firm slanted script I recognized from a hundred margin notes on a hundred ledgers:

*"Auggie — if you're reading this, the lawyers finally caught up. Check the warehouse, bay three, under the tarp with the anchors. I left you something that isn't money, because you'd only give the money away, and we both know it. Don't argue with a dead woman. — O."*

Corvane whistled low. "She's still running jobs from beyond the grave."

"She was always three tides ahead." I folded the letter, and my chest was tight in the way that was equal parts grief and grin. "Bay three. Under the tarp."

"When do we sail?"

I looked out at the harbor, at my boat, at the lights coming on along the quay, at the season turning toward the monsoon that everyone said would be a bad one — early, heavy, the kind you either hide from or go meet.

"Tomorrow," I said. "Right after the signatures."

Because whatever Odile had left under that tarp, I knew one thing for certain: it wouldn't sit still, and neither, anymore, would we.
