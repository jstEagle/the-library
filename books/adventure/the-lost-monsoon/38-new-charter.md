## 38. New Charter

**August**

The harbor office smelled of mildew and old coffee, which felt appropriate, because so did we.

I stood at the counter of the Port Authority annex with a pen in my hand and a form in front of me that asked, in the cheerful bureaucratic language of people who have never been shot at, to describe the *nature of business* for vessel *Kestrel's Wake*, registration pending renewal.

*Nature of business.* I looked at Tomas. Tomas looked at Wren. Wren, who had spent the last ten minutes trying to teach Elias how to work the coffee machine bolted to the wall, looked back at me with the particular expression of a seventeen-year-old who has already decided this is going to be funny and is waiting for everyone else to catch up.

"Write 'wreck documentation,'" I said. "Marine survey. Salvage assessment for historical preservation."

"That's four businesses," said Tomas.

"It's one business with delusions of grandeur." I wrote it down anyway. Under *owner/operator*, I put my own name, and my hand did not shake, which was new. Under *crew*, I listed them all: Reyes, T.; Wren, no surname given because she didn't have one she trusted yet; Nystrom, E., listed as *deckhand, emeritus*, a title I invented on the spot and which he accepted with the gravity of a man receiving a knighthood.

And under *consulting surveyor*, after a pause long enough that the clerk behind the counter glanced up, I wrote: **Merric Vale**.

That was Corvane's name now. He'd picked it himself, which told you something about a man — that when offered the chance to become anyone in the world, he chose a name that sounded like a minor character in a shipping novel. He'd grown a beard for it. It did not suit him. He wore it anyway, the way he wore his civilian coat, like a uniform he was still breaking in.

"You're sure about this," he'd said to me last week, standing on the dock in the rain, hatless, looking younger without the insignia. "You're sure you want me anywhere near your boat. After everything."

"I'm sure I want someone aboard who knows where the navy keeps its wrecks," I said. "And I'm sure that a man who burned his own career rather than shell a cave full of civilians is exactly the kind of liar I want on my crew."

He'd laughed. It was the first time I'd heard him do it, and it sounded rusty, like an engine turned over after a long winter.

Now the clerk stamped the charter — *thunk*, the most satisfying sound in maritime commerce — and slid the papers across the counter, and just like that, the *Kestrel's Wake* belonged to us. Not to a creditor. Not to a syndicate. Not to the memory of a drowned nation. To a debt-free diver, a disgraced engineer, a runaway with a genius for dead languages, a retired warden of twenty years' standing, and a naval officer named after nobody.

"Well," said Tomas, as we walked out into the light. "We're legitimate."

"Don't say it like that," said Wren. "You'll jinx it."

"We've been shot at by two governments and a crime syndicate. What's left to jinx?"

Wren opened her mouth. I put my hand over her face before she could answer, because I knew her well enough by then to know she had a list.

* * *

**Corvane**

There is a particular humiliation in being good at something you are no longer permitted to do.

For eleven years I was a salvage officer in Her Majesty's Navy. I could read a tide table like scripture. I could look at a coastline and tell you what it was hiding. And now I stood on the deck of a converted fishing trawler wearing a false beard and a borrowed name, holding a mug of coffee that Wren had made, and I use the word *coffee* in its loosest possible sense, and I realized with a kind of quiet horror that I was happy.

"This is punishment," I told August. She was at the wheel, charting our first contracted course — a merchant wreck off the Sable Shoals, sunk forty years ago, no treasure, no secrets, no syndicate. A client wanted it surveyed before they laid a cable across it. Honest work. Boring work. Work that would not get anyone killed.

"What is?"

"The coffee."

"That's not the coffee's fault. That's operator error." She didn't look up from the chart. "Wren makes it the same way. You're both doing something wrong."

"Wren is fourteen."

"Fifteen next month. And she makes it worse than you, if anything, which is genuinely impressive."

I drank it anyway. That was the thing about this crew that I was still learning, the thing that kept catching me off guard like a wave from an unexpected quarter: they gave you things freely. Bad coffee, yes, but also trust, unearned and unconditional, handed over like a tool — *here, hold this* — with the expectation that you would hold it. In the navy, trust was a currency, rationed, audited, always with interest accruing. Here it was just... given. I kept waiting for the invoice.

"Merric Vale," I said, testing the name again against the morning air. "Do you know why I picked it?"

"I assumed you liked the sound of it."

"My first posting, thirty years ago. There was a clerk in the port office at Casterhal, a little man named Merric Vale, who falsified my leave paperwork so I could get home when my mother was dying. Never met him before or since. He signed a lie to buy me three days with her." I looked out at the water. "It seemed right. To carry the name forward. Names should mean something."

August was quiet for a moment. Then: "Elias is going to ask you to teach him navigation. Don't let him charm you into skipping the fundamentals. He'll tell you he learned from a Vessian tide-priest. He did not. He learned from a bar bet."

"Noted."

"And Corvane—"

"Merric."

"—Merric." She almost smiled. It was still a rare weather event, her smile, and I found I'd started keeping track of them the way sailors keep track of clear skies. "Thank you. For the report. For burning it. For walking away clean."

"I didn't walk away clean," I said. "I walked away into your crew. Everyone will assume that's dirtier."

She laughed then, properly, and steered us toward the shoals, and I drank my terrible coffee and thought: *yes. This. Whatever this costs, it's cheaper than what I had.*

* * *

**Tomas**

I fixed the bilge pump at six in the morning, re-caulked the aft seam at seven, argued with the fresh-water tank at eight, and at nine o'clock I sat down on the engine housing with a wrench in my lap and realized I had nothing left to fix, and that this was somehow the most frightening part of the whole day.

Old habits. On the *Meridian* — God rest her, and may whoever scuttled her step on every plug in every harbor for eternity — there was always something broken, and it was always my fault according to somebody, and the fixing never stopped because the breaking never stopped. Disgrace is a full-time occupation. It doesn't leave room for sitting down.

But here's the thing nobody tells you about disgrace: it doesn't survive contact with people who refuse to acknowledge it. August hired me knowing my record. Knew about the *Meridian*, knew about the inquiry, knew about the year I spent doing engine repairs on river barges because no ocean captain would touch me. Hired me anyway. First wage packet came on time. Second one came early. Third one came with a note in Wren's handwriting: *for the pump. it sounds better. — w.*

I sat there holding that note longer than I want to admit, even now.

"Reyes." Elias appeared in the engine room doorway, holding two mugs. I braced myself. The coffee on this boat is a controlled substance — controlled, presumably, by someone who hates us. "You have a minute?"

"For you? Always." I took the mug. It was terrible. I drank it. These are the terms.

"The main winch," he said. "The drum bearing. It sings when it turns."

"It doesn't sing. It's worn. I ordered a replacement; it comes Thursday."

"A Vessian dive-crew would have shimmed it with brass sheet and been diving by noon."

"A Vessian dive-crew also had a mortality rate you don't want printed in a brochure."

He smiled — that slow, weathered smile of his, the one that used to make me furious and now mostly just makes me sad, in the way you're sad looking at an old photograph of someone you love who isn't quite that person anymore. Twenty years underwater does something to a man. He's coming back, though. Day by day. Wren drags him into card games. August lets him take the dawn watch. Little mercies, measured out.

"Thursday, then," he said, and clinked his mug against mine like we were sealing a treaty, and went back up top whistling something old and probably Vessian and probably mournful, because everything old and Vessian was mournful, except apparently him.

I looked around my engine room — *my* engine room, and note how easily I write that now — at the clean tools and the greased fittings and the spare parts racked in labeled bins, and I thought about the man I was two years ago, terrified of open water, terrified of his own shadow's reflection in it.

Then I went up on deck, because the sun was out, and the water was flat and blue and going somewhere interesting, and I found I wanted to be on it. Not under it. On it. Looking down instead of looking away.

Progress. Somebody should write it down.

* * *

**Wren**

Here is what I know about families, having now technically been in several:

The orphanage called itself a family. That was the word on the brochure they showed the donors — *a family environment* — while they taught us Vesian script in the basement because literate children fetched higher prices from collectors, and fed us just enough, and lost us sometimes, the way you lose inventory. I believed it was a family because I was six and the brochure was glossy.

The syndicate called itself a family too. Odile used to say it — *the family looks after its own* — and she meant it, in her way, the way a spider means it. I was cargo there. Valuable cargo, eventually, once they figured out what the orphanage had put in my head. But cargo.

This — and I want to be careful here, because I am fifteen and prone to dramatics, and August says dramatics are fine at sea but insufferable in harbor — this is different, and I think the difference is the paperwork.

No, listen. I'm serious. Yesterday we signed a charter. Five names and a false one, on a government form, agreeing to be responsible for each other. Nobody cried. Nobody made speeches. Elias tried to make a speech and August threatened him with the winch. We just... signed. Like it was obvious. Like it had been true for months and the paper was only catching up.

Families you're born into don't come with terms. Families you choose come with a contract, and contracts can be read, and renegotiated, and — this is the part that gets me — *kept*. Kept on purpose. Every day. By people who could leave and don't.

Anyway. I'm the ship's navigator now. Officially. August put it on the charter — *navigator, junior* — and Corvane, sorry, *Merric*, has been teaching me proper hydrography to go with my dead languages, and between the two of us we can read any coast on this ocean, living or drowned.

Which is relevant, actually, because of the letter.

It came yesterday, forwarded through Odile's lawyers — she's stable, she's recovering, she writes to us in a hand that gets stronger every week and signs herself *your partner in retirement*, which none of us have corrected because correcting it would mean admitting we read it, and we all read it. The letter enclosed a clipping from a shipping gazette. Small item. A research institute up north, cataloguing wrecks from the Vessian era, had acquired a collection of documents at auction.

Including, per the clipping's footnote, a partial manifest. Divers' names. Dive dates.

One of the names was circled in Odile's red ink, and beside it she'd written: *thought you'd want to know there were others. some of them might still be alive. — o.*

Others. Other divers. Other names on other ledgers, in other archives, scattered across other oceans like seeds from a split pod.

I haven't shown August yet. I'm going to. Tonight, maybe, over dinner, after the bad coffee, when Elias is mid-story and Tomas is pretending not to listen and the light goes gold on the water the way it does. Because that's what families are for, I've decided. Not just the surviving. The finding-out. The *there were others* and the *we'll go look*.

I folded the clipping back into my jacket, next to the compass.

It points wrong, still. August says it always will — says some instruments are calibrated to a place that no longer exists. But I've noticed something she hasn't, because I'm the one who reads dead scripts and thinks in dead tides: it doesn't point wrong so much as it points *first*. North, then adjusted. Home, then onward.

Tomorrow we make for the Sable Shoals. A boring wreck, a cable route, honest pay.

And after that — well. After that, the season's charts are blank, and I'm the one who draws them now.

* * *

**August**

We sailed on the morning tide with a charter in the drawer, a consultant in a fake beard, a winch that sang until Thursday, and coffee that could strip barnacles.

At the harbor mouth I looked back once — habit, not longing — at the town where I'd been drowning in arithmetic for years. Debts paid. Boat owned. Ledger closed. The freedom was still strange, still quiet, like a room after a storm has passed through it. I kept reaching for the weight of the numbers and finding nothing there, and my hands didn't know what to do with the lightness yet.

But they'd learn. We'd all learn. That was the whole arrangement.

"Course?" Wren asked, pencil ready.

"Sable Shoals," I said. "Bearing east by northeast. And Wren—" I nodded at her jacket, at the small square shape of folded paper she thought I hadn't seen. "Whatever you're carrying. Bring it to dinner tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight."

She went pink. "How did—"

"I was you, once. Badly." I turned the wheel. "Besides. Family meeting. That's what the paperwork's for."

Behind me, I heard Elias laugh, and Tomas groan about the winch, and Corvane — *Merric* — quietly pour the coffee over the side where it could harm no one, and I set our heading for open water with a full crew and an empty ledger and the whole turning season ahead of us.

Somewhere north, in an archive, other names waited in other ledgers. Some of them might still be alive.

We'd find out. That's what the charter was for.
