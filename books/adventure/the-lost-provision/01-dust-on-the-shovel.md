## 1. Dust on the Shovel

The claim was dying before Elodie ever touched it, and they both knew it.

Six days of digging in the baked clay south of Corriander Flats had produced exactly what Elodie expected: nothing. A seam of colorless quartz that promised everything and delivered gravel. The assay man in town had looked at their samples the way a doctor looks at a patient he's already written off—kindly, briefly, already turning to the next name on his list. *Trace values*, he'd said. *Might be worth working if you had ten years and no sense.*

Elodie had smiled and paid for the assay anyway, because paying was cheaper than explaining, and explanations were the one currency they never carried.

Now it was late afternoon on the seventh day, and the sun sat low and orange over the border hills like a coin someone had dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up. Dust hung over the pit in a fine gold haze, settling into the creases of Elodie's knuckles, the folds of their collar, the wet corners of their eyes. They'd stopped tasting anything but dust three days ago. Their shovel bit down through another layer of hardpan with a sound like a tooth cracking, and Elodie paused, forearms burning, and listened to the silence the way other people listened to music.

That was the part nobody understood about this work—the listening. The town folk thought prospecting was about greed, about striking it rich, about the fever. But Elodie had learned young that the ground talked constantly, in the language of grain and color and weight, and most of what it said was *nothing here, nothing here, move along.* Most people couldn't hear it because they didn't want to. They wanted the ground to lie to them. Elodie had made a life out of believing it.

*Nothing here.* The hardpan agreed. Six days of agreement.

They leaned on the shovel handle and let themselves have one full minute of stillness, which was one minute more than they usually allowed. Below them, the pit was a rough oval maybe four feet deep, walls slumping at the edges where the clay gave up its pretense of structure. Their tent stood at the rim—a sagging wedge of canvas gone gray as dishwater, one pole splinted with wire. Beside it, the cook fire's ashes. The mule, Bess, dozed hipshot under the single scraggly juniper, ears twitching at flies.

A week's wages, the assayer had called it, meaning the week Elodie had sunk into this hole. Seven days of food and powder and candle and the mule's feed, all of it poured into dirt that would give none of it back. By any honest arithmetic, Elodie Marchetti was seven days poorer than they'd been last Sunday.

But arithmetic wasn't why they were here. Arithmetic was never why they were anywhere.

They'd come to Corriander Flats because it was the kind of town you could pass through without leaving an impression. Two streets, a dry goods store, a saloon with a false front leaning like a drunk against a wall, and a population that changed so fast nobody bothered learning names. The kind of place where a person could dig for a week, sell or not sell, and drift on before anyone thought to ask where they'd come from or where they meant to go. Elodie had a system for towns like this. Arrive late in the day. Pay cash. Talk about weather. Leave before the second Sunday, when the church crowd started comparing notes about who was new and who was strange and who might be *that* Marchetti kid, the one from the papers, the one whose father—

The shovel slipped in their sweating grip and clanged against rock, and the sound went out across the flats like a bell.

Elodie stood very still.

It wasn't the sound that had done it. It was the thought underneath it, the one they kept buried under six feet of routine: *the one whose father.* Because that was the truth of every town, every camp, every border crossing. Somewhere behind them, always, was the story. The expedition that went into the Sawtooth Range eleven years ago with nine men and came out with four. The provision cache those men had been hunting—the lost 1890s supply house, seed stock and deeds and enough stored wealth to make a family's fortune—and the way the mountain had eaten the search alive. And at the bottom of all of it, the name Marchetti, spoken in saloons and assay offices and sheriff's offices from here to the coast, usually followed by *dead*, sometimes by *cheat*, occasionally by *hero*, depending on who was telling it and how much they'd had to drink.

Elodie had spent ten years making sure the name stayed ahead of them, moving faster than gossip could travel. It was exhausting work, being a rumor's shadow. But it was the only inheritance their father had left that was worth carrying.

Bess lifted her head, ears forward, looking east toward town.

Elodie looked too, and saw the rider.

He was a long way off yet—a dark smudge on the white alkali road, raising a tail of dust, coming on at a canter that was too purposeful to be casual. Elodie's body understood before their mind did. Some old animal part of them, the part that had learned to read ground and read men in equal measure, went tight and cold under the ribs.

They watched the rider resolve. Broad-shouldered man on a dun horse, hat brim low, one hand resting easy near his thigh—not reaching for a gun, just *near* it, the way certain men rested their hands. He wasn't wearing a consortium badge. He wasn't wearing anything official at all. That was worse. Official men could be reasoned with, bribed, outrun through paperwork. Men in plain clothes riding straight at your claim on the seventh day, when you'd told no one in town where you were digging—that was a different category of trouble entirely.

Elodie did the math the way they always did, fast and cold. Tent: abandonable in ninety seconds. Mule: saddled in two minutes if she cooperated, five if she didn't. Wages: a week's provisions, tools, the good pick with the hickory haft they'd bought in spring, the copper pan worn smooth as a spoon. All of it replaceable. All of it *designed* to be replaceable. That was the whole architecture of a life like theirs—nothing owned that couldn't be left in a hole in the ground by sundown.

The rider was close enough now that Elodie could see his face, and their stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Hollis Brandt. They knew him from photographs more than from memory—he'd been twenty-five in the expedition pictures, square-jawed and grinning, standing beside their father with a hand on his shoulder like they were brothers. Eleven years later he was heavier, grayer, weathered down to leather, but the face was unmistakable. Hollis Brandt. One of the four who came out of the Sawtooths. One of the men who had spent a decade telling anyone who'd buy him a drink about the Marchetti expedition, about the cache they'd nearly found, about poor damn Silas Marchetti who died on the mountain with the secret still in his head.

Or so everyone believed.

Elodie's hands moved before the fear finished arriving. Shovel down—it wasn't even a decision, just a fact, like gravity. They kicked dirt over the coals of the cook fire, though there was no one to hide it from; habit did the thinking. The tin of assay certificates went into the coat pocket, because paper was light and proof was rare. Everything else stayed. The tent, the pick, the pan, the bedroll, the half sack of flour. A week's wages, abandoned mid-dig, exactly as the ledger of their life demanded.

"Bess," they said quietly, and the mule sighed the long-suffering sigh of her species and let herself be bridled.

The saddle cinch fought them. It always fought them at moments like this, some law of the universe that tack tightened only when fingers shook. Behind them, hoofbeats slowed to a walk, then to the deliberate crunch of a horse picking its way up the claim's tailings slope. Elodie didn't look up. Looking up invited conversation. Conversation invited recognition. Recognition invited the whole rotten story, unpacked in front of God and the juniper tree.

"Marchetti." The voice was gravel over gravel. "Lord above. I thought I was seeing ghosts."

Elodie swung up into the saddle. "Wrong person."

"Hell you say." Hollis Brandt reined in at the pit's edge and sat there looking down into the empty hole, then up at Elodie on the mule, and something moved across his face that wasn't quite a smile. "You've got her chin. Silas's chin, exact. I'd know it in a crowd of thousands. I spent two years eating bad beans next to a man with that chin."

"I don't know who you're talking about." Elodie gathered the reins. Bess, bless her stubborn heart, began to turn.

"He told us he had kids," Brandt said, and now the smile was fully there, and it was the worst thing Elodie had seen in years, because it was warm. Warm like a stove in a cold room, warm like a trap. "Two of them. Said they were back east with their mother. Said when he struck the provision house, he'd go get them and they'd never want for anything again." He took off his hat, and beneath it his hair was gray as the dust. "You look just like he described."

The afternoon had gone very quiet. Even the flies had quit. Elodie sat in the saddle with the reins loose in their fists and felt the old arithmetic running, the only arithmetic they trusted: *distance equals safety, speed equals distance, attachment equals weight, and weight is what kills you on a mountain.*

"You've got the wrong digger," they said. "I'm passing through. Claim's dead. You can have the hole."

"I don't want the hole." Brandt turned his horse sideways, casual as anything, blocking the trail down off the tailings. Not aggressively. Just *presently*. "I want to talk. Ten minutes. About your father. About what he really found up there before the mountain took him—and about who else has been asking after it lately." He settled the hat back on his head. "Because it isn't just me, Marchetti. There are men with survey instruments and consortium paper crawling all over the east approaches, and they've got field notes that shouldn't exist, and I'd take it kindly if somebody with your blood in them heard me out before they dig up what your father died keeping buried."

Every instinct Elodie owned screamed the same instruction it had screamed for ten years: *go.* Kick Bess into a run, cut downslope through the sage, put the flats between yourself and this man and his warm trap of a voice, and be two counties gone by moonlight. It had worked in Corriander Flats' twin towns a dozen times. It would work here. Abandon the wages, abandon the name, abandon the question. Questions were how people caught you. Questions were how you ended up staying somewhere long enough to be known, and being known was the first step toward being buried.

But Brandt had said *field notes that shouldn't exist,* and the words snagged in Elodie like a fishhook, because there was only one set of field notes in the world that phrase could mean, and their father had carried them into the Sawtooths, and four men had carried grief out.

Elodie looked at the road south, wide and empty and safe as a held breath.

Then they looked at Hollis Brandt, sitting his horse in the orange light with eleven years of secrets stacked behind his eyes, and heard himself say, in a voice they barely recognized as their own:

"Ten minutes. Then I'm gone."

Brandt grinned and dismounted, and the dust settled around them both like something being decided.

* * *

He talked for forty minutes, and Elodie let him, because every word was a hook and by the fifth one they'd stopped pretending they weren't caught.

He talked about the expedition—the nine who went in, the cold snap in September that shouldn't have come so early, the river crossing that drowned two men and half the gear. He talked about Silas Marchetti, and his voice went soft in a way that undid Elodie worse than shouting would have: how Silas had mapped every drainage in the east Sawtooths from memory and paper, how he'd traded a month of his own rations to a dying Kootenai guide for one hand-drawn sketch, how he'd stood at the mouth of a hidden valley on the eleventh day of October and said, *it's here, Hollis, I can feel it, we'll come back in spring with proper equipment and our names on the deed.*

He talked about the storm that came instead. About the descent, the whiteout, the cornice that went out from under four men at once. About how they'd searched for Silas Marchetti for six days in killing cold and found his pack, his spare coat folded on a rock like a man changing clothes, and never him.

"And here's the thing nobody prints in the newspapers," Brandt said, crouched at the edge of the dead claim, drawing lines in the dust with a stick. "Your daddy didn't carry his field book on his body that day. He'd left it cached. Wrapped in oilcloth, sealed in a tin, stashed high on the ridge above the valley mouth—standard practice, keep your records dry. We never found the cache. Never found the valley again either; snow took the landmarks. But two years back, a consortium survey crew out of Halvard bought a box of old expedition papers at an estate auction, and I'd stake my pension one of them was Silas's field book. Because those crews have been crawling the east approaches ever since, season after season, spending real money on a wild-goose range." He looked up, and his eyes were flat and serious as slate. "They're closing in, Marchetti. And whatever your father hid up there—deeds, letters, the location itself—it belongs to his blood before it belongs to any consortium. Before it belongs to me. I just want what's fair for my trouble. I figure you'd want what's yours."

Elodie sat very still while the sun went down behind the border hills and the cold came up out of the ground like a tide.

Everything in them said *run.* Run now, run tonight, run the way they'd run from a dozen towns and a dozen names, because wanting things was how mountains killed you, and this man was offering them the biggest want of their life gift-wrapped in their father's voice.

But underneath the fear, quieter than it had ever been, something else stirred—an image they hadn't summoned in years: Silas Marchetti at a kitchen table, drawing maps on butcher paper, saying *someday I'll show you, someday we'll go together.*

"Someday" had died on a mountain. Elodie had spent ten years making sure it stayed dead.

They should ride out tonight. Every rule they lived by said so.

Instead, Elodie heard themselves ask the question that would unravel everything:

"This granddaughter Brandt keeps mentioning—Amos Vane's girl. Where do I find her?"

And Hollis Brandt smiled, slow as sunrise, and said, "River ferry at Alder Bend. She works the crossing."
