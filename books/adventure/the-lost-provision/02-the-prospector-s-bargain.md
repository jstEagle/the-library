## 2. The Prospector's Bargain

The saloon at Corvid Creek had no name worth remembering, which suited Elodie Marchetti fine. Named things invited attachment, and attachment was a debt like any other—one that came due when you least could pay it.

They'd been in town four days, long enough to pan the tailings below the old stamp mill and short enough that nobody had started asking questions. The room above the assay office cost two dollars a night and smelled of lamp oil and other men's tobacco, and Elodie slept with their boots on out of habit older than memory. In the mornings they drank coffee that tasted of iron and watched the mountains north of town go from gray to gold to gray again, and told themselves—again—that this was just another stop. Just another season. Just another job.

The saloon itself was the kind of place where the piano had three working keys and the bartender watered the whiskey twice: once for profit and once for mercy. Sawdust on the floorboards, a moose head over the bar with one glass eye that caught the lamplight wrong, so it always seemed to be looking at whoever wasn't watching it. Elodie took the corner table by habit, back to the wall, sightline to both doors. A decade of running teaches you geometry.

It was there, on the third evening, that the old man found them.

He came in leaning on a stick of lodgepole pine cut down to crutch-length, and he crossed that sawdust floor like a man crossing a river he didn't trust—testing each step before committing his weight. He was gaunt the way winter is gaunt, all edges and hollows, with a beard gone the color of the ash at the bottom of a cold stove. But his eyes were clear. That was the first thing Elodie noticed about Amos Vane: everything else about him was failing, but the eyes were clear as meltwater, and they were fixed on Elodie with an intentness that made the hair on their arms stand up under the flannel.

"You're Marchetti's kid," he said, without asking. He lowered himself into the opposite chair with a sound like green wood splitting. "Don't bother denying it. I seen you work a claim outside Kestrel Gulch, oh, eight years back. You dig like him."

"I don't know who you mean." Elodie kept their voice flat and their hands visible on the table. Old lessons.

"Sure you do." The old man smiled, and it was not a friendly smile so much as an honest one—the smile of a man who had stopped pretending things were pleasant a long time ago. "Silas Marchetti. Best ground-reader I ever saw, and I've seen most of 'em. Man could look at a hillside and tell you where the water went before it did." He coughed then, deep and wet, into a rag he produced from his coat like it was part of the motion, and when he came up from it his lips were gray. "And I know what happened to him, too. So don't waste your breath lying to me, boy. Lying's for people with time."

Elodie said nothing. Outside, the wind worried the eaves of the building, and somewhere down the street a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything.

"I'm dying," Vane said, matter-of-fact, tucking the rag away. "Doc in Silverton gave me till the passes close. Maybe less. My heart's quitting like a miner with a full poke." He gestured at the bartender, two fingers raised, and the bartender brought two whiskeys without being asked, which meant this had been arranged, which meant Elodie had been watched, sought, chosen. They didn't like how that felt. Being chosen by strangers was how trouble found you.

"I'm not buying anything," Elodie said.

"Good. Because I'm not selling." Vane pushed one whiskey across the scarred wood toward them. "I'm trading. And the trade is this: you take something off my hands that I can't carry anymore, and you give me your word on a thing that matters more than the thing itself."

"Your word," Elodie repeated. "That's a strange currency in a place like this."

"It's the only currency here that holds its value." He drank, small sip, wincing. "You know what a provision cache is?"

Elodie knew. Every kid raised around old workings knew the stories—the great supply houses of the nineties, built by companies that staked whole ranges on paper and vanished before the mules ever arrived. Grain, tools, seed stock, timber rights, sometimes deeds, cached in stone buildings against the day the boom came. Most of them rotted or burned or got picked clean. A few were never found at all.

"There's one in the Sawtooths," Vane said. "1893. Halvard Provision Company, before Halvard turned into a consortium and forgot its own history. Sealed. Intact. I've spent thirty years finding it and twenty more making sure nobody else did." His clear eyes glittered. "Thirty years, boy. You know what a man gives up, spending his life on one hole in one mountain? I do. I can show you the receipts."

"Why me?" Elodie asked. "You said yourself I dig like my father. Why hand a treasure to a ghost's kid?"

Vane set down the glass. For a moment the bluster went out of him, and what remained was just an old man in a failing body, tired past the reach of sleep.

"Because you're the only person in five hundred miles who doesn't want it," he said. "I've watched the crews circle. Consortium surveyors sniffing up every drainage with their instruments and their stolen notes. Claim-jumpers. Men who'd gut each other over a rusted stove lid if they thought it was plated. And every one of them wants the cache *because* of what's inside. You—" he pointed a knuckled finger, "—you want to be left alone. That makes you the only honest broker in the territory."

"And what exactly am I brokering?"

Vane reached into his coat, slow as a man moving through deep water, and drew out an oilskin packet, soft-worn, tied with waxed cord. He laid it on the table between them but kept his hand on it.

"A map," he said. "Hand-drawn. Thirty years of corrections on it. Bearings, landmarks, the trick of the approach—it's not findable without this, I'll swear to that. It's yours. Free and clear. Sell it, dig it, burn it, I don't care. Once it leaves my hand it's your trouble, not mine."

"Nothing's free," Elodie said. "What's the price?"

"The price is a promise." The old man's voice dropped, and the saloon noise seemed to lean away from them, leaving a pocket of quiet. "I had a daughter. She died hating me, and she was right to. I lost our stake—the family claim, legal and proper—in the Sawtooths, back before I understood what I'd found. Lost it through pride and bad paper and a handshake I should never have trusted. Her girl—my granddaughter, Mara—grew up hearing her mother tell it one way, and the truth is worse than the story, and she deserves it straight. No varnish. From someone who isn't me, because she won't give me the hour it would take." His jaw worked. "You find her. She works the river ferry at Two Forks. You tell her what I did. All of it. Then you give her whatever's hers out of that mountain—and what's hers is more than gold, though there's that too."

Elodie looked at the oilskin packet. Something in their chest, the part that had learned to keep accounts, whispered the arithmetic: a map to a sealed cache against one errand to a ferry landing. Easy trade. Take it, sell the location to the highest bidder in Silverton, be over the next pass before thaw.

But they'd heard the word *promise* land between them like a coin on a counter, and promises were the one debt Elodie Marchetti had spent ten years refusing to carry. Debts to banks could be outrun. Debts to dead men followed.

"I don't make promises," they said.

"I know." Vane almost smiled. "Your father didn't either. Said words were weather. And look where keeping nothing got him—a hole in the ground and a name nobody says." He leaned forward, and the lamplight filled the hollows of his face. "I'm not asking you to keep it forever, boy. I'm asking you to carry it as far as a ferry dock. Even you can manage that much staying."

The wind slammed the door open; the bartender swore and wrestled it shut, and in the commotion Vane slid the packet across the table. Elodie's hand closed on it before they decided to let it—oilskin cool and dense as a sleeping animal.

"One condition," Elodie heard themselves say. "If I tell her, I tell her plain. No dressing it up to spare anyone. If she hates you after, that's between her and your grave."

"Fair terms." Vane raised his glass, and there was something in his face then—not peace, exactly, but the end of a long argument with himself. "To fair terms."

They drank. The whiskey was terrible and warm, and Elodie set the glass down and looked at the old man properly for the first time—at the tremor in his hands, the wet rattle waiting behind his breath, the clear eyes already dimming toward some interior horizon.

"When?" they asked.

"Soon," Vane said. "Winter doesn't wait and neither will the consortium. Their permit on the range expires come spring, and expired paper means the cache goes to whoever stands on it first—with lawyers or guns, whichever's closer." He pushed himself up on the lodgepole crutch, joints cracking like river ice. "Go see her, Marchetti. Tell her the truth. After that, what you do with the mountain is your own business, and I'll haunt neither you nor it."

He made for the door, slow as erosion, and paused with his hand on the frame.

"She has her mother's temper," he said, not turning around. "Mind how you open the letter."

Then he was gone into the dark, and Elodie sat alone with the oilskin packet and the dregs of bad whiskey, telling themselves the same lie they'd told in every town for ten years:

*Just another job.*

Outside, the first flakes of the season came down fine as sifted flour, and far north, unseen, the Sawtooths put on their white crowns early.
