## 3. What the Map Costs

The river gave Mara Vane her living and took it back twice a year, when the ice came down from the high country and ground the ferry landing to splinters. Between those seasons she worked the cable like a religion—grease the sheaves, check the anchor chains, count the passengers, take their coins, and never, ever ask them where they were going. People crossing a river wanted to be somewhere else. She understood that better than most.

It was a Tuesday in late September, the water running low and green-gold with tannin, when the stranger stepped off the morning stage and stood at the head of the ramp looking at her the way people looked at a door they weren't sure was locked.

Mara saw them before they saw her—or rather, she saw the way they held themselves. Weight on the balls of the feet. Eyes doing the arithmetic of exits: the ramp, the far bank, the treeline, the road back to town. She'd grown up around miners and drifters and men who talked too loud about what they'd found, and none of them ever counted their exits. Only people who expected to need them did that.

"You're the ferry operator," the stranger said. Not quite a question.

"I'm the whole operation." Mara set down the winch handle. "Crossing's two bits. Freight's extra."

"I'm not crossing."

"Then you're wasting my morning."

The stranger came down the ramp anyway, boots ringing on the planks, and stopped at a respectful distance—the distance of someone who had learned that getting close to strangers got them hurt or got them arrested. They were younger than Mara expected, maybe thirty, lean as fence wire, with sun-cracked hands and eyes the color of creek water over slate. A canvas pack hung off one shoulder like it lived there. Everything about them said *temporary*. Everything about them also said *tired*, in the deep way that sleep didn't fix.

"Mara Vane," they said. "Your grandfather sent me."

The name went through her like a fishhook. She kept her face still because the ferry taught you that—currents read faces—but inside, something old and rusted swung on its chain.

"Amos Vane," the stranger said, "is dying. He asked me to find you. He asked me to tell you—" and here the stranger hesitated, and Mara realized with a strange, cold clarity that this person had rehearsed this on the trail and still couldn't make it come out smooth, "—the truth about what happened to your family's stake. The Silver Ledge claim. He says he lost it, and he says it wasn't the mountain that took it."

The river slapped against the pilings. Somewhere upstream a kingfisher rattled. Mara heard all of it from very far away, because she was eleven years old again, standing in a kitchen doorway while her mother burned breakfast for the third time that week, staring at nothing, saying *he promised he'd come back by Christmas, Mara-girl, he promised.*

"He's been dying for twenty years," Mara said. "That's what we called it. Dying. Every letter came later than the last, every letter had an excuse in it, and then the letters stopped and my mother waited anyway until waiting killed her slow." She picked up the winch handle and put it down again. "So he finally managed it. Congratulations to him."

"I'm not here to defend him."

"Then why are you here?"

The stranger reached into their coat and drew out a folded square of oilcloth, tied with waxed string, and laid it on the winch housing between them like a card player laying down a hand they weren't proud of. "Because he paid me. Not in money. In a promise. I'm supposed to tell you the truth, and this—" they touched the oilcloth without untying it, "—is part of it. A map. To something in the Sawtooths. Something of his."

Mara looked at the little package and felt her mother's ghost standing behind her shoulder, and hated how much she wanted to reach for it.

"My mother died owing money on a boarding house," she said quietly. "She took in wash and rented beds to drummers and she died with forty-one dollars to her name and a photograph of a man in a mackinaw holding a pickaxe like he owned the world. You want to know what his truth is worth to me? Nothing. Less. It's worth the price of the words, which is free, which is exactly what everything else from Amos Vane ever cost."

The stranger nodded slowly, and there was something in the nod—not pity, worse, *recognition*—that made Mara want to throw them off her ferry.

"So take your message back delivered," she said. "Tell him his granddaughter heard it. Tell him she said fine. Tell him—"

"He can't hear anything," the stranger said. "He's got days. Maybe hours. I left him coughing blood into a tin cup in a camp saloon with a Bible he doesn't believe in propping up the leg of his cot." Their voice didn't soften; if anything it flattened, the way voices do when the speaker has decided feeling is a luxury. "I'm not asking you to forgive him. I don't think he's asking either. I think he just wants the truth to outlive him instead of dying in the bed with him. There's a difference. Most things people leave behind are debts. This is the other kind."

"And what do you get?" Mara asked. "Nobody walks a hundred miles to deliver bad news for free. What's your cut?"

Something flickered across the stranger's face—surprise, maybe, or the discomfort of being seen accurately. "My cut is keeping my promise so I can leave clean."

"*Leave.*" Mara laughed, and it came out ugly. "Of course. That's the whole shape of you, isn't it? Deliver the package, keep the promise, walk away. My grandfather spent his whole life walking toward mountains and away from us, and he found himself a courier with the same disease."

"You don't know me."

"I know the type. I married adjacent to the type." She hadn't meant to say that. The river knew her secrets and apparently so did her mouth, this morning. "I know what it looks like when a person treats every place like a campsite. You've got dust on you from six territories. You checked my exits before you checked my face. Whatever he gave you, whatever's under that oilcloth—you were already planning how to be gone before I finished hearing about it."

The stranger didn't deny it. That was somehow the worst part. They just stood there with the wind coming off the water pulling at their coat, and said, "Probably."

* * *

They ended up in the ferry house, because the wind picked up and because Mara told herself it was about the wind. She poured coffee from the dented pot on the stove, and the stranger wrapped both cracked hands around the tin cup like it was the first warm thing in weeks, and for a while neither of them talked.

The map lay on the table between them. Untied now. Mara had made them untie it—*if it's mine to hear about, it's mine to look at*—and the stranger had complied with the grim obedience of someone paying a toll.

It was drawn in pencil and iron-gall ink on the back of a flour sack, and it was beautiful, in the way old things done carefully are beautiful. Ridge lines rendered in fine hatched strokes. A trail marked in dashes. Elevations noted in a cramped, confident hand. And in the upper reaches of a hanging valley, circled twice, a symbol like a small house with a cross above it, annotated in letters gone brown with age: *PROVISION HOUSE — 1893 — SEE DEEDS.*

"What's a provision house?" Mara asked.

"Honest answer? I don't fully know yet." The stranger turned the map so it faced her properly, and their finger hovered over the circle without touching it. "Amos talked about it like it wasn't gold. He said the old supply companies built them—sealed caches, tools, seed stock, stores enough to winter a crew—and that some of them held paper along with the goods. Claims. Deeds. The legal bones of somebody's stake." Their finger moved to the margin, where smaller writing said: *VANE — ¼ INTEREST — WITNESSED — NEVER FILED.* "He says your family's quarter interest was witnessed and never filed. He says the man who filed it instead became rich, and the man who witnessed it drank himself to death in a cabin above the Salmon, and the man who should have inherited grew up thinking her father abandoned her for nothing."

The coffee had gone cold in Mara's cup. She hadn't noticed drinking any of it.

"He watched her die?" she said. "He knew where she was the whole time?"

"He knew where she was the whole time."

Mara stood up. She walked to the window and looked at her river, low and patient, going about its business the way it had through every grief she'd ever handed it. Behind her she could feel the stranger watching, not moving, giving her the room they'd probably wished people gave them once upon a time.

"Here's what happens now," she said, turning around. "You're going to tell me you'll carry the message, close the promise, and catch the northbound stage tomorrow. And I'm telling you that's not what happens. I'm coming with you."

"That's not—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't tell me it's dangerous. Don't tell me I'll slow you down. Don't tell me it's no life for—whatever you were about to assume about me. I've run this cable through two floods and a forest fire. I can read weather better than you can read exits. And that map has my name on it—not yours, *mine*, Vane, in ink older than both of us. Whatever's in that provision house, half of it is mine by blood and all of it is mine by right, because I'm the only Vane left to claim it."

The stranger studied her for a long moment. She watched them do it—watched the arithmetic run behind those slate-colored eyes, the weighing of burden against obligation, and she understood suddenly that they were not deciding whether she could come. They were deciding whether they could afford the company.

"It's a hard crossing," they said finally. "Snow comes early to the Sawtooths this year. There are other people after what's in that valley—men with permits and survey instruments and no particular love for witnesses. If you come, you come as a partner, not a passenger. You pull weight. You follow instructions on the rope work even when they don't make sense. And when it's done—" they paused, and Mara saw the flinch, small but real, "—when it's done, we go our separate ways. That's the deal. That's always the deal."

"Fine," Mara said. "Separate ways. After."

"After," the stranger agreed, and offered their hand across the table. "Elodie Marchetti."

"Mara." She shook it. The hand was rough as tree bark and careful as a surgeon's. "Marchetti. That name mean anything to anybody?"

"Not anymore," Elodie said, and something in the flatness of it told Mara there was a story there, and that it was a long one, and that it had ended badly in at least one town.

She began closing up the ferry house. Draining the coffee pot. Banking the stove. Writing the note for the stage driver who leased the crossing rights—*Gone to the Sawtooths on family business, back when the ice breaks, sell the drummers' beds to whoever pays*—and her hand only shook a little writing *family business*, because she could not remember the last time the phrase had applied to anything in her life.

Outside, Elodie stood at the rail of the empty ferry, looking north up the valley toward the blue wall of the range, and the wind pulled at their coat like it was impatient.

"One question," Mara said, joining them. "The promise. The one he bought you with. Once you've told me the truth and shown me the map—is that it? Are you free?"

Elodie was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence for them.

"I don't know," they said at last. "I've never stayed anywhere long enough to find out what I am when a promise ends."

"Well," Mara said, swinging her pack onto her shoulder and turning her back on the only home she'd ever known, "you can figure it out on the trail. Because I've got a grandfather to bury and a fortune to file, and the snow doesn't care which of us gets there first."

Far to the north, above the Sawtooths, a cloud the color of pewter was already building—and Elodie Marchetti, who could read weather in the set of a man's shoulders and trouble in the ring of a stranger's coin, went very still, watching it grow.
