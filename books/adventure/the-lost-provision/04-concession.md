## 4. Concession

The town was called Meridian Cut, though there was nothing meridian about it—no line of longitude ran through it that any surveyor would own, and the only cutting done these days was of prices at the general store, where everything cost twice what it had the year before. It sat in the last flat fold of country before the Sawtooths stood up out of the earth like a jaw of broken teeth, and its single street was mud to the hub-depths, rutted by ore wagons and frozen into gray ridges by a night frost that had come early and without apology.

Elodie Marchetti came down that street with Amos Vane's map folded against their ribs, inside the oilskin wrap they'd sewn for it, and felt the particular unease of a person carrying something valuable through a place built to strip valuables from passersby. Ten years of practice had taught them to read a town the way other people read weather. Meridian Cut read bad. Too many men standing in doorways with nothing to do but watch. Too many mules in the livery for a town this size, and too many of them shod fresh. Somebody was outfitting an expedition, and had been for days.

They told themselves it didn't concern them. They were here for flour, salt pork, rope, a second blanket apiece, and out again before anyone learned their face.

The general store smelled of kerosene and dried apples. Elodie kept their collar up and their voice low and asked for goods in quantities that said *prospector, alone, unremarkable*, and the storekeeper—a woman with forearms like dock rope and eyes that missed nothing—rang it up without comment until Elodie added a tin of condensed milk, and something flickered across her face. Pity, maybe. Or recognition of a kind.

"You'll want to buy that before Thursday," she said, sliding the tins across the counter. "Consortium's bought out my whole stock of provisions as of Thursday. Every can, every sack. Paying me triple to clear the shelves."

Elodie's hands went still on the counter. "What consortium?"

"Halvard." She said the name the way you'd spit a cherry pit. "They've got a survey permit on the whole range. Runs out end of the month—some legal wrangle back east, appeals and countersuits, nobody in this town understands it and nobody's meant to. Point is, they've got until the snows to find whatever they're looking for up there, and they're spending money like water to do it." She leaned in, lowering her voice, and her breath carried coffee and tobacco. "You've got that look. The look of somebody heading up. So I'll tell you for free: their crews came through three weeks ago asking after an old man named Vane. Whether he'd been through, whether he'd sold anything, whether he'd talked to anybody."

The name landed in Elodie's chest like a dropped pick. They kept their face doing nothing at all. Ten years of practice at that, too.

"Vane," they repeated, flat. "Don't know him."

"Mm," said the storekeeper, who clearly did not believe them and clearly did not care. "Point is, they're not asking anymore. Now they're just buying. Mules, men, food. Three crews, I heard. Going up the east fork, the low pass, and the river trail both directions." She straightened and rang the register with a bang. "That'll be four dollars sixty."

* * *

Outside, the light had gone thin and metallic, the way it does when the sun is already thinking about winter. Elodie stood on the boardwalk with their purchases in a canvas sack and made themselves breathe slowly while their mind did what it always did under pressure: mapped exits.

Three crews. East fork, low pass, river trail. Which meant Halvard wasn't guessing. Which meant they knew roughly where to look, which meant—

Amos's field notes. The ones he'd told Elodie about with such bitter satisfaction, the notebook he'd kept for forty years, the one with every bearing and every failed dig recorded in his crabbed hand. He'd said a man had taken it off him in a card game in Coeur d'Alene, years back, and he'd never gotten it back, and he'd laughed when he said it, a wet laugh, because by then he understood that losing the notes hadn't mattered much—the map was the thing, the map was in his head and then on paper and now against Elodie's ribs—but Elodie hadn't understood it fully then. They understood it now. The consortium hadn't stolen the notes recently. They'd held them for years, waiting for the permit, waiting for the window, and Amos had spent those same years dying slow in a camp above the river, keeping his secret the only way left to him: by telling no one, until, at the very end, he told a stranger.

And the stranger had walked into the one town in the territory where the enemy was provisioning.

Elodie laughed once, short and without humor, and a man loitering by the assay office turned his head. Elodie turned the other way and walked.

* * *

The saloon was called the Last Chance, which was either optimism or inventory, and Elodie chose a corner table with a wall at their back and a view of the door and ordered whiskey they didn't want, because a lone prospector nursing a drink attracted less attention than a lone prospector nursing nothing. The room was half full of the usual: drifters, a faro game running quiet, two teamsters arguing freight rates. And at the long table near the stove, five men in new canvas and city boots, eating like men on expense accounts, with a sixth man standing over a spread of papers, marking it with a grease pencil.

Elodie looked at their whiskey. Looked at the mirror behind the bar, old and foxed, the kind of mirror that gave back a smudged impression of the room, good enough for watching without watching. In it, the papers resolved into a map. A big one. Hand-drawn, or drawn from hand-drawn sources, with contour lines and little penciled crosses scattered across the high country like a rash—and one cross circled, over and over, the grease pencil worn shiny with re-marking.

A man came out of the back room wiping his hands on a rag, and the crew at the table shifted to make room for him the way men shift for a boss. He was big without being soft, gray at the temples, with a face that might have been handsome before someone had rearranged part of it, and he spoke quietly enough that the words didn't carry, but Elodie caught the shape of them in the mirror: *east fork first, then the low pass if the weather holds, and nobody goes up the river trail alone.*

Reyes. It had to be. Amos had never named the consortium's man, but he'd described him once, bitterly, as *the polite one, the one who smiles when he takes things*. This man didn't smile. But he handled the map the way you handle a thing you've taken from somebody, with a certain proprietary care, and Elodie felt their father's old lesson rise up in them like bile: *when a man handles your life like property, don't argue with him. Leave.*

Leave. The word fit so comfortably it was almost a physical relief, like a joint going back into socket. There was a southbound freight leaving tomorrow morning; there were towns south of here where nobody had ever heard of Vane or Halvard or the Sawtooths; there was a whole continent of dead-end claims and anonymous camps, and Elodie could be a ghost in any of them by Friday. They could even keep the map. Sell it quietly in some city, take a cut, vanish richer than they'd started. That had been the plan all along, hadn't it? Tell Mara, collect the girl, use the girl as cover, get the cache, get gone. Nobody had promised anything except a dying man, and dying men's promises were cheap as faro chips.

Except.

Except the storekeeper had said three crews, and three crews meant the low pass was covered, and the low pass was where the map went. Except the permit expired with the month, which meant the consortium had a clock too, and men with clocks get careless and cruel in equal measure. Except Amos Vane had pressed that map into Elodie's hands with fingers like dry roots and said *tell her the truth*, and whatever else Elodie Marchetti had run from in ten years, they had never yet run from a promise, because a person had to keep one thing, just one, or there was nothing left to be.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest, was the thought they refused to look at directly: that Mara Vane was waiting at the ferry landing two days north, expecting them, and that some traitorous part of Elodie had already begun to count on not being alone.

They paid for the whiskey and went out.

* * *

Night came down hard and clear, stars sharp as nail heads, and the frost took the street and made it ring underfoot. Elodie worked by feel in the livery loft, repacking the canvas sack, checking the rope, counting matches twice because counting once felt like tempting something. Through the wall, the consortium men's voices droned in the hotel next door—orders, logistics, the flat arithmetic of an operation that had already decided the range belonged to it.

Elodie sat back among the hay and did the sums they'd been avoiding since the store.

Two days north to the ferry. Two days back, if Mara came, which she would—she had that look, the hungry stubborn look of a person who'd been cheated by life and intended to audit it personally. Then the low pass, four days hard walking in good weather, five or six if the weather turned, and the first snows were due anytime after the new moon, and the new moon was Tuesday. Against them: three crews, better provisioned, mounted on the east fork and the river trail, holding field notes that contained every bearing Amos had ever failed at, which was nearly as useful as the ones he'd succeeded at, because a smart man could subtract.

It couldn't be done. Not both ways. Not fast enough, not clean enough. Any honest calculator would say leave now, go south, sell the map, survive.

Elodie lay back in the hay and stared at the rafters, where a barn owl sat regarding them with the flat indifference of a creature that had also chosen a hard living.

"Tell me I'm a fool," they said to it.

The owl blinked. Somewhere below, a consortium mule stamped, and the sound of it was like a knock at a door.

Because here was the thing Elodie had figured out in the dark of the loft, the thing that changed the arithmetic entirely: the crews were hunting a treasure. Gold, supplies, whatever legend had grown around the cache in forty years of garbled retelling—they were hunting wealth, and wealth could be abandoned, wealth had no face. But if the letters were real—if what Amos had hinted at was true, that the cache held more than goods, that it held proof, paper, standing, a legal inheritance that had been stolen from his family before Mara's mother was born—then this wasn't a treasure hunt at all. It was a race to decide who owned a wrong. And wrongs, unlike gold, couldn't be sold off and vanished from. Wrong followed you. Wrong waited in every town you fled to, sitting patiently at the edge of your vision for the rest of your life.

Elodie had spent ten years learning that lesson from the other direction. Their father's expedition had ended in a valley none of the survivors would name, and the debt of it—not money, worse—had chased Elodie across half the territory, and every time they'd stopped moving, it had caught up. Running hadn't saved them. Running had just spread the damage thinner.

So. North in the morning, before the consortium's east fork crew shook out its lines. Take the ferry route, collect Mara, and beat them all to the low pass by however many hours stubbornness could buy. And if the snows came early, well—the mountain had never cared what Elodie Marchetti planned, and it wasn't going to start now.

They slept three hours, woke in the black cold, and were on the north road when the sky went gray, moving fast, the map warm against their ribs and the whole white jaw of the Sawtooth Range rising ahead of them out of the dark like something waiting.

Behind them, faint on the morning air, they heard the east fork crew's bell mule start up its complaining, and knew the race had already begun.
