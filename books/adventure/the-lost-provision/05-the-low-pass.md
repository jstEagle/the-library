## 5. The Low Pass

The trail, when Mara finally found it behind the collapsed sluice gate, was less a path than a rumor—one the mountain had spent thirty years trying to take back.

She stood at its mouth with her pack straps cutting into her shoulders and watched Elodie study it the way she'd seen her grandfather study a river: not looking at what was there, but at what had been. Amos Vane had that same habit of reading ground like a letter from someone he didn't trust.

"You're sure this is faster," she said. It wasn't a question. She wanted him to say no, wanted an excuse to turn back to the wagon road with its graded switchbacks and its honest bridges.

"Two days faster." Elodie crouched and pressed two fingers into the dirt of the old tread, where a ghost of wheel-rut still shadowed the slope. "Freight trail, 1890s. They hauled the provision house timbers up this way before they built the tram. Nobody's used it since because nobody's needed to."

"And us?"

Elodie glanced up at her. In the flat morning light their face was all angles and economy—no expression wasted, nothing held longer than necessary. "We need to be somewhere the consortium isn't."

That was the whole of their conversation for the first hour. Mara had learned in three days of travel that Elodie Marchetti spoke the way other people spent money they were afraid of losing—carefully, in small amounts, and only when the alternative cost more. She'd decided to find it infuriating. She was disturbed to find instead that she'd started listening for it, the way you listen for water in dry country.

The trail climbed through lodgepole pine so dense the sky came down to slivers. Old blazes still marked the trunks, axe-cuts gone black and weeping resin, and every few hundred yards a rusted artifact surfaced from the duff: an iron ring, a shard of blue bottle glass, a horseshoe fused to its own rust. The mountain was digesting the century slowly, and Mara found herself cataloguing it without meaning to—the way her mother had taught her, back when her mother still talked about the Vane side of the family at all. *Your grandfather could look at a hillside and tell you what it was hiding,* she'd said once, and then had stopped saying anything about him for eleven years.

By midday the trees thinned and the pass opened around them like a held breath—a granite corridor with the old freight road cut into its eastern wall, forty feet above a talus field that fanned down toward a creek gone thin and bright with autumn. The rock overhead was banded red and gray, fractured into shelves the size of barn doors, and frost had been working at the seams all night. Mara could hear it ticking. The whole wall sounded like a clock winding down.

Elodie stopped when she stopped. That was new too. Three days ago they would have walked on and let her catch up or not.

"Frost-heave season," Elodie said, eyes on the wall. "We cross the open stretches one at a time. If I'm under it when it comes down, you don't dig. You go on to the cache, you do what you came to do."

"That's a hell of a thing to say casually."

"It's not casual. It's arithmetic." And Elodie stepped out onto the exposed tread first, moving fast and light along the base of the wall, boots finding a line Mara couldn't even see until she watched them take it.

* * *

It happened on the second open stretch, an hour later, when the sun had climbed high enough to warm the frost out of the cracks.

Mara heard it before she understood it—a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, deep and mineral, somewhere above and behind the sunlight. Then the pigeons went up off the wall in a gray burst, and Elodie, twenty feet ahead, turned and shouted something the wind took away, and the world came apart.

Not all of it. Not dramatically, not all at once. A shelf the size of a cabin door simply let go of the cliff above them, and for one long moment it hung there, rotating, shedding smaller stones like sparks—and Mara's body refused every instruction. Her legs locked. Her mind presented her, absurdly, with her mother's kitchen, the smell of coffee, a Tuesday morning ten years gone.

Then Elodie hit her.

Full weight, shoulder into her hip, driving her off the tread and down the slope—not falling, *steering*, both of them sliding on loose scree toward the talus fan while the shelf came down behind them with a sound like the end of the world being filed away for later. Rocks the size of skulls bounded past. Dust swallowed everything, orange and choking, and Mara was tumbling, arms over her head, Elodie's boot against her ribs keeping her rolling instead of stopping—because stopping, she understood dimly, was how you died under it.

Then it was over. Silence rang like a struck bell. Dust drifted down through the light in slow gold columns.

"Mara." Elodie's hands were on her shoulders, turning her, checking—quick, impersonal, thorough, the way a farrier checks a horse. "Mara. Look at me. Toes. Fingers."

"I'm—" She coughed dust. "I'm fine. I think I'm fine."

Elodie sat back on their heels and looked at her, and for the first time since she'd known them, their face did something unguarded. Not relief exactly. Recognition. As if they'd been waiting three days to find out whether she was the kind of person who broke, and the answer had cost them something to get.

"You froze," Elodie said. Not cruel. Just true.

"I know."

"Everyone does, the first time. The trick is surviving the first time." They got up, brushed scree from their knees, and looked up at the fresh scar on the wall where the shelf had been, already calculating. "There'll be more. That whole band is rotten. We move through the shaded sections only, and we move quiet."

"How many times have you done that?" Mara asked. "Steered someone off collapsing ground. Or been steered."

Elodie didn't answer. They picked up her dropped pack, checked it for damage, and handed it back to her with the straps already adjusted the way she'd mentioned liking them, on the ferry dock, four days ago, in a conversation she barely remembered having.

That frightened her more than the rockslide had.

* * *

They camped early, in the lee of a granite rib below the last shaded stretch, because Elodie said the afternoon thermals shook loose whatever the morning hadn't. Neither of them suggested pushing on. Neither of them said why.

The fire was Mara's doing. She gathered the wood without asking, knelt, and struck the match before Elodie could object—and Elodie watched her do it with an expression she couldn't read, something between protest and surrender.

"Cold's going to kill us before the consortium does," she said. "I'd rather lose the argument about smoke than win the one about hypothermia."

"They'll see it if they've made the pass."

"Then they'll see two people who aren't dead of exposure." She fed a branch to the flame. "My grandfather used to say a fire you're afraid to light is the only kind worth having. Something about how fear means you're actually somewhere, doing something that matters."

Something moved across Elodie's face at that—quick, quickly buried. "He say much else?"

"He left before I was old enough to remember. My mother did the remembering for both of us, and then she stopped." Mara pulled her knees in. The fire cracked and settled. Above them the scarred wall caught the last light, red as an open wound. "You knew him. At the end. What was he like?"

Elodie was quiet long enough that she thought they wouldn't answer. When they did, their voice had changed—lower, stripped of its usual rationing.

"He was a man who'd lost everything twice and kept the map to a third loss hidden in his boot for forty years. He was stubborn, and funny when he forgot to be bitter, and he was sorry. He was sorry the way a man is sorry who knows sorry doesn't fix anything but says it anyway, every day, because the alternative is worse." Elodie turned a stick in the flames. "And he trusted me, which should tell you how bad his judgment had gotten."

"That's not nothing. Being trusted by someone who's out of illusions."

"No," Elodie said quietly. "It isn't."

They sat with the fire between them, and Mara realized she was no longer cataloguing the mountain. She was cataloguing Elodie—the way they sat angled toward the slope, always, even resting; the way their hand had gone to her pack strap on the trail before their eyes had gone to her face; the practiced, terrible fluency of someone who had steered bodies off collapsing ground enough times that the motion lived in their spine instead of their memory.

"You've run from more than rockslides," she said.

Elodie looked at her across the fire, and for a moment she thought they'd give her the silence, the closed door, the arithmetic. Instead they said, "Ground collapses everywhere, Mara. Towns. Families. Promises. You learn the feel of it starting—the tick in the seams—and you learn to move." A pause. "I've never once stayed to see what the mountain does after."

"And tomorrow?"

Elodie fed the fire another branch, and the light jumped, and their shadow leaned huge against the granite behind them.

"Tomorrow we climb through the shaded stretch before the sun hits the seams," they said. "And tomorrow night, if the weather holds, we make the high bench—and from there you can see the valley where your grandfather buried his family's future."

Mara nodded. But she'd caught it—the deflection, smooth as a card slid off a deck—and she filed it away with everything else she was learning about the person the mountain had thrown her against.

Sleep came hard and shallow. Somewhere past midnight she woke to find Elodie sitting up, watching the pass below them, where—far down the darkening trail, small as sparks—a light was moving.

Two lights.

Someone else had found the low pass.
