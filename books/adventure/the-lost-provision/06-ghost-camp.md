## 6. Ghost Camp

The storm found them before the camp did.

It came down the western face of the range in a gray wall, eating the ridgeline behind them, and Elodie drove them off the trail and into the trees with the particular urgency of someone who had been caught in weather like this before and remembered exactly what it cost. Snow hissed through the pines in long horizontal sheets. The temperature dropped so fast their breath turned to frost on their collar before it could drift away.

"There," Mara shouted, pointing through the white.

Elodie saw it a half-second later: the corner of a roofline, sagging under years of snowload, black timber against black timber. A structure. A shelter. They put their heads down and broke trail toward it, boots punching through crust, and reached the treeline clearing as the last of the daylight failed.

It was a camp. Not a cabin—a camp. Three structures arranged around a dead fire ring: a cook tent with its canvas rotted to lace along one side, a supply lean-to collapsed into itself like a folded hand, and a sleeping hut built of rough-sawn planks, still standing, still square, stubborn against twenty years of mountain winters. A rusted stovepipe jutted from its roof at an angle, like a broken finger.

Elodie stopped walking.

They knew that stovepipe. They knew the particular lean of it, the way it listed east because some impatient man had never properly braced it. They knew the fire ring's stones, hauled up from the creek below because the man who built this place believed a fire should be made of stone you could see the water in. They knew the way the sleeping hut faced south—not for warmth, though the man would have claimed that was why, but so that whoever woke inside it would open the door to the whole valley at once, all of it laid out like a promise.

"Elodie?" Mara had her hood back, snow melting in her dark hair, watching them with that sharp river-ferry attention she gave to anything that behaved strangely. "What is it?"

"Nothing." Elodie's voice came out flat. "It's shelter. Get inside before you freeze."

* * *

The hut smelled of pine resin and mouse droppings and time. Elodie got a fire going in the little iron stove while Mara shook out the old bunk frames and spread their bedrolls, and for a while there was nothing but work—the good kind of silence, the kind that keeps a body from thinking. Elodie fed the stove slats pried from a crate that had once held blasting powder, the stenciled letters still legible: *STERLING EXPEDITION — SAWTOOTH SURVEY — 1899 REPROVISIONING.*

Mara read the stencil aloud. Not asking. Just reading, the way she did, letting the words sit in the air between them until they became a question whether Elodie answered or not.

"Sterling," she said again. "That's your name."

"It's a common name."

"You told me your father died in these mountains."

"I told you he died. I didn't say where."

Mara sat down slowly on the edge of the far bunk, across the stove from them, and the firelight moved over her face. She didn't push. That was the thing about Mara Vane that Elodie had learned in six days of trail and friction and grudging alliance—she didn't push. She waited. She had the patience of someone who'd spent years ferrying strangers across moving water, reading currents, knowing when to hold the oars still.

Outside, the storm thickened. Wind came down off the peaks and leaned against the hut, and the old timbers groaned but held, the way they'd been holding since before either of them was born.

"My father built this," Elodie said finally.

The words surprised them both. Elodie looked at the stove, at the fire, anywhere but at Mara.

"This was his last expedition. The Sterling Survey. He had a contract—mineral assessment, upper Sawtooth drainage. Four men and me. I was nineteen." Their hands were steady on their knees. They made sure of it. "We wintered here by accident. Came down late off the high country and the snow closed the pass behind us. We meant to wait it out. Two weeks, maybe three."

"And?"

"And we stayed too long." The wind pressed at the walls. "You want to know how he died? Everyone always assumes avalanche. It's cleaner. But it wasn't an avalanche. It was staying. We stayed, and the provisions went thin, and then they went gone, and my father decided the answer was to send two men down the pass in February to bring back help. Because leaving felt like action. Because sitting still felt like dying slow, and a man will do almost anything rather than sit still."

"One of them was him."

"He made it four miles." Elodie heard their own voice go distant, the way it did when they talked about things that had happened to someone else, some other person wearing their name. "We found him in the spring when the melt opened the trail. Sitting against a tree, facing the valley. Facing this camp. Like he was looking at the reason he'd left it."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse resumed its small industry.

"After that I stopped staying places," Elodie said. "That's the whole arithmetic of my life, if you want it. Staying is where people die. My father stayed and died. Every claim I've ever worked, every town—I leave before the ground gives out, before the money runs dry, before anyone gets close enough to be standing next to me when something collapses. You've seen how I dig. How many times have I pulled you back from bad ground this week? Twice? Three times? I know collapsing ground because I grew up inside it."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "That's not arithmetic. That's fear wearing arithmetic's clothes."

"Mara—"

"No. Hear me." She leaned forward, elbows on her knees, and the firelight caught the stubborn set of her jaw—the same jaw Amos Vane had, Elodie realized, the same jaw in a younger face. "Your father didn't die because he stayed. He died because he *left*. He left the camp, left the shelter, left the people who needed him, because he couldn't bear to sit still. By your own telling, staying was the safe choice. Leaving killed him."

"The provisions ran out. Staying killed us all, slowly—"

"So the lesson is that he provisioned badly. So the lesson is that he made a bad call in a bad month. Instead you've taken it as—" she waved a hand at Elodie, at all of them, at the decade of abandoned claims and vanished wages and tools left sticking in the dirt of a border town—"as scripture. *Never stay.* You've been running from a man who ran first."

Elodie stood. The motion was too fast, too sharp, and they knew it even as they made it. "You don't know anything about it."

"I know you traded a dying man a promise for a map, and every mile since, you've been looking for the exit." Mara didn't raise her voice. That was worse, somehow. "I know you told me you'd take me as far as the low pass and no further, and here we are above the low pass. I know you check the southern horizon every morning like you're measuring the distance home. I know you sleep with your pack buckled."

"Everyone sleeps with their pack buckled in the mountains."

"Not the way you do. You sleep like a man lying fully dressed on top of a bed in a hotel he's already paid to leave." She sat back. "I'm not mocking you, Elodie. My grandfather did the same thing, just slower. He ran from my mother for thirty years and called it providing. Ran from this range, ran from what he lost here. And what did running get him? A cot in a mining-camp sickroom and a stranger to carry his last message." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and she covered it by looking into the fire. "Running doesn't keep people alive. It just spreads the dying out thinner, so you can pretend it isn't happening."

The wind rose to a shriek and fell away. Snow sifted down through a gap in the roof boards, fine as flour, catching the firelight.

Elodie sat back down. Slowly, this time.

They looked around the hut—at the bunks their father had built, at the crate stenciled with his name, at the iron hooks by the door where four coats and a boy's smaller coat had hung through that terrible winter. Twenty years, and the camp had kept everything. The camp had stayed. It was still here, still standing, still offering shelter to whoever came in out of the storm. It had outlasted the man who built it and the son who fled it and every winter it had ever been asked to endure.

Staying, Elodie thought, was where people died. But it was also where things endured. Both things were true, and they had only ever carried the first one down out of these mountains.

"I used to dream about this place," they said quietly. "For years after. In the dream the stove's lit and he's at the table doing figures and he looks up when I come in and says, 'There you are. Where've you been?' Like I'm the one who left." They laughed, and it came out wrong. "I wake up with my pack buckled."

Mara said nothing. She reached across the space between the bunks and set her hand briefly on Elodie's forearm—there and gone, a touch light as the sifting snow—and then began laying out the map and the tin of cold beans as if nothing had passed between them at all, which was its own kind of mercy.

They ate. The storm hammered. And near midnight, when the fire had burned to orange coals and Mara slept in her father's grandfather's camp with her breath fogging faintly in the cold air, Elodie sat awake by the stove and heard it:

A sound beneath the wind. Rhythmic. Distant but not distant enough.

Voices. More than one. And the metallic knock of a climbing hammer setting anchors in frozen ground—somewhere down-valley, coming up.

The consortium hadn't waited for the weather to clear.

Neither, it seemed, had Reyes.
