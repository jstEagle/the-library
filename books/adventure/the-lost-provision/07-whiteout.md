## 7. Whiteout

The storm did not announce itself. It arrived the way bad news arrives—first a silence, then a pressure, then everything at once.

Mara felt it before she saw it: a stillness in the air above treeline, a hush like the whole range holding its breath. They had been climbing since dawn through scrub pine and scree, following the map's second bearing toward a saddle that Amos Vane had marked with a single word in his cramped hand—*shelter*. Elodie had gone quiet an hour ago, and Mara had learned by now that Elodie's silences were barometers. The quieter they got, the worse the weather coming.

"Snow," Elodie said now, and it wasn't a guess. It was a sentence being passed down.

Mara looked up. The sky had gone the color of old pewter, low and bruised, and along the western ridgeline she could see it forming—a wall of white, soft-edged and deceptively gentle, like something you might want to walk into.

"How long?"

"An hour. Maybe less." Elodie was already moving faster, checking their footing on the talus with that strange economy of motion Mara had come to recognize—the way they never wasted a step, never reached for anything twice. "We need to find the cave."

"The map says shelter at the saddle."

"The map says a lot of things." Elodie's jaw tightened. "Amos drew it forty years after he was last up here. Land moves. Trust your feet over his ink."

They climbed. The wind came first, a scouting party of cold gusts that tugged at Mara's collar and made her eyes water, and then the snow itself—not falling so much as arriving sideways, a horizontal blizzard that erased the world in strokes. Within twenty minutes Mara could not see ten feet. Within thirty she could not see Elodie, only hear them ahead, calling back in short clipped words: *Left. Rock. Up.*

Her hands were numb inside her gloves. Her boots had soaked through somewhere below treeline and her feet had passed from pain into a warm, lying comfort that she knew from her river winters was more dangerous than pain. She thought about the ferry, about the flat gray water and the honest work of pulling a rope, and wondered—not for the first time since leaving the low pass—what exactly she had traded it for.

Then Elodie stopped so suddenly she walked into their back.

"Cave," Elodie said. "Two o'clock. There's a cairn."

Mara squinted into the white. At first there was nothing, just snow and more snow—and then, ghostlike, a shape resolved out of the murk: a stack of stones, human-ordered, marking a dark seam in the rock face. Someone had built that cairn. Someone had needed this shelter badly enough to mark it for whoever came after.

They squeezed through the seam single file, and the storm shut behind them like a door.

* * *

The cave was deeper than its mouth suggested—a throat of granite opening into a chamber maybe thirty feet across, dry-floored with gravel and old sand. And it was not empty.

Mara knew it before Elodie did. She saw it in the way Elodie went rigid two steps inside, the lantern light catching their face, every muscle in their body going to stone.

There was a fire ring in the center of the chamber. It held coals that were still breathing—dim orange hearts under gray ash, no more than an hour cold. Bedrolls lay against the far wall. Gear was stacked with military neatness. And propped against the rock near the entrance, where someone had left it deliberately visible, stood a surveyor's transit on its tripod, gleaming wet with the first melt of the storm.

A consortium crew had beaten them here.

"Don't," Elodie breathed, barely a sound. "Don't touch anything. Don't say anything."

"I wasn't going to introduce myself."

"You'd be surprised." Elodie set down their pack without a whisper of noise and moved along the wall, reading the camp the way Mara read water—currents, hazards, depth. "Four people. Maybe five. They'll have a watch posted outside or they're fools, and Reyes doesn't strike me as a fool."

"You've met him?"

"I've read his reports." Elodie's mouth twisted. "He writes beautifully. That's how I know he's dangerous. Men who write beautifully always want something bigger than money."

The storm chose that moment to make the decision for all of them. A gust screamed down the seam of the entrance and the snow came in after it, a white hiss that filled the passage like smoke. Somewhere above, Mara heard a deep groan of wind through the high country, the sound of a mountain settling its weight. Whatever was out there now was staying out there, and whatever was in here was staying in here, together.

From the inner darkness, a voice said: "You can stop sneaking. We heard you at the cairn."

Lanternlight bloomed. Three figures rose from behind a screen of packs—one big man with a beard crusted with ice, one young woman with a rifle slung loose and easy, and between them, unhurried, a lean man in his fifties with gray at his temples and eyes the color of slate. He looked at Elodie for a long moment, and something like recognition moved across his face—not surprise, exactly. Confirmation.

"Elodie Marchetti," he said. "I wondered when you'd surface."

"And you're Reyes." Elodie's voice was level, but Mara saw their hand drift, casually, to rest near the hatchet at their belt. "Your field notes are better than your manners. You camped in my father's route without so much as a by-your-leave."

"It's public land." Reyes gestured, gracious as a host, to the fire ring. "Sit. Both of you. The storm's decided we're colleagues until morning. No sense spending it standing."

Mara looked at Elodie. Elodie looked at the entrance, where the white roar had sealed them in, and Mara watched something calculate behind their eyes—the exits, the odds, the weight of a rifle versus the reach of a hatchet—and then, slowly, they sat down across the fire from Reyes, pulling Mara down beside them.

"Marchetti," Reyes said, settling opposite. "That name used to mean something in these hills. Your father mapped half this range before anyone paid him to."

"My father died mapping this range."

"Yes," Reyes said softly. "I know. I've read the expedition report. All of it. Including the parts the company filed away." He fed a stick to the coals and watched it catch. "You know what I find interesting, Marchetti? Everyone who goes looking for Vane's cache thinks it's gold. The old-timers, the treasure men, even my own crew—they dig because they smell metal. But you don't move like a gold hunter. You move like someone who read the same report I did."

Elodie said nothing. The fire popped.

"So let's trade," Reyes went on. "Trader's honesty, since we're stuck with each other. I'll tell you what I know if you tell me why you're really here."

"And if I don't?"

"Then we spend a very long night pretending we're strangers, and tomorrow we race, and my crew is fresh and yours has been climbing for six days." He smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which somehow made it worse. "I'd rather know you, Marchetti. Enemies you understand are safer than enemies you don't."

Mara felt Elodie go still beside her—that particular stillness, the one she'd learned meant a door opening somewhere inside them whether they wanted it open or not.

* * *

What Elodie told him, in the end, was not the whole truth but a piece of it, laid down like a card.

"My father took a contract from Halvard's predecessor company," they said, staring into the fire. "Twenty-two years ago. Survey work, the northern Sawtooths. He found something up here—correspondence, records, a structure. Provision house, 1890s vintage. Mining syndicate stores. And papers with it." Their jaw worked. "Papers showing the original claim filings. Who staked what. Who got robbed."

"Vane's family," Mara said.

"Vane's family," Elodie confirmed. "Amos's father held legal title to a quarter-section that the syndicate's lawyers buried. The cache isn't a treasure chest. It's evidence. Deeds, letters, seed stock, tools—everything a family would need to prove standing and survive the proving." They finally looked up at Reyes. "My father sent word back down. Two weeks later he was dead in a crevasse the report calls 'unfortunate.' The company sealed his findings. I was eleven years old."

The cave was very quiet. Outside, the storm hammered the rock like something demanding entry.

Reyes nodded slowly, and to Mara's astonishment he didn't deny any of it. "That matches what I've reconstructed," he said. "Here's my side of the trade. Halvard's concession on this range expires in nineteen days. When it lapses, the claims data becomes contestable—any valid prior filing supersedes. Amos Vane's father's filing, if the originals exist, would supersede everything. The consortium didn't send me up here to find gold, Marchetti. They sent me to find those papers and burn them."

Mara's breath caught. Beside her, Elodie didn't move, but she felt the change in them, a coiling, like water backing up behind a dam.

"Burn them," Elodie repeated.

"That's the contract." Reyes spread his hands. "I'm telling you because I want you to understand what you're racing. Not a rival crew. An incinerator." He leaned forward, firelight carving his face into planes and shadow. "Now the last part of the trade. What would you burn, Marchetti? Because I've watched you for three hundred miles of paper trail, and you run from everything—claims, towns, your own name. Running is a kind of burning. So what is it you'd actually stand and fight for?"

Elodie was silent a long time. The storm howled. Mara realized she was holding her breath.

"There's a girl downstream," Elodie said finally, quietly. "Granddaughter of the man whose family got robbed. Her grandfather died owing her the truth, and I promised him she'd get it. That's what I'd burn for. Not the cache. The promise."

Reyes studied them for a long moment, and then he laughed—short, genuine, almost sad. "A promise. Twenty years in these hills and I finally meet someone fighting for a promise." He shook his head. "For what it's worth, Marchetti—I haven't burned anything yet. I read reports. I write them too. And I've been wondering for six weeks what I'd do if I found those papers first."

"And?"

He looked into the fire. "Ask me tomorrow. If the weather breaks, we both walk out of here and we find out which of us is lying." He glanced at the young woman with the rifle. "Serrano takes first watch. Nobody sleeps past shallow. Storm's supposed to run two days—but I've seen these mountains lie about that too."

Mara pulled her bedroll close to the fire, close to Elodie, and lay awake listening to the wind grind against the stone. Two days trapped in a hole in the ground with the man hired to erase her family. She should have been afraid. She was—of the storm, of Serrano's rifle, of the nineteen days ticking down somewhere beneath the snow.

But mostly, watching Elodie sit rigid watch beside her, staring into the coals with the look of a person doing arithmetic they hated, she thought about what Reyes had asked. *What would you burn?*

And she thought: *Tomorrow, we find out which of us is lying.*

Above them, the mountain groaned, and the snow kept falling, and falling, and did not stop.
