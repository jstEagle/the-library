## 8. The Provision House

The last mile was a lie the mountain told.

Elodie had learned long ago that maps never showed the truth of distance—the way a slope could double itself in the last hundred yards, the way a trail drawn in a dying man's careful hand could dissolve into scree and switchback and wishful thinking. Amos Vane had drawn the cache at the head of a hanging valley, beneath a granite shoulder he'd called the Sentinel, and for three days the Sentinel had grown ahead of them like a promise being broken slowly. Now, with the light going copper behind the western ridgelines and the air carrying that mineral bite that meant snow, they came around a wall of talus and Elodie stopped so suddenly that Mara walked into their back.

"There," Elodie said, and their voice came out strange, flattened, like sound in a room with the furniture draped in sheets.

It didn't look like treasure. That was the first thing. Elodie had spent a decade digging for other people's gold, had imagined this moment in a hundred camps and a hundred bad rooms, and in every imagining there had been a glint—a box, a seam, a shine. What stood instead at the head of the valley was a building. Low, squat, built of squared timber gone silver-grey with forty years of weather, its roof shingled in split cedar and furred with lichen. A sod-covered porch sagged at one corner. The door was iron, painted once, and over it, cut into a plank and nailed like a dare, were the words:

VANE & SONS — PROVISIONS — EST. 1893

The wind moved through the valley and the plank creaked, and Elodie felt something shift in their chest that had no name they wanted to give it.

"It's a house," Mara said. She had Amos's jaw—Elodie had seen it a hundred times now in firelight, the stubborn set of it—and her voice was careful the way voices get when they're carrying something breakable. "He said a cache. He said *cache*."

"Caches are boxes," Elodie said. "This is a statement."

* * *

The lock was a puzzle of Amos's devising, and it took Elodie twenty minutes and a broken knife blade to solve it: a hasp bolted from inside, a secondary pin through the frame, and a final trick where the whole door had to be lifted a half-inch before it would swing. Whoever built it had wanted it found, but only by someone patient. Only by someone who'd earned the irritation.

Inside, the air was dry as paper and cold as a held breath. Elodie struck a match and the flame found a room that made no sense at first—shelves to the ceiling, but not the shelves of a store. Crates stenciled with the same VANE & SONS mark, but packed like a life being saved: seed grain in sealed tins, labeled by hand in a copperplate that had faded to ghost-script. Tools wrapped in oilcloth and waxed string, the steel still bright beneath the grease. Barrels of nails, kegs of lamp oil gone to varnish, bolts of canvas, a crate of glass windowpanes packed in wool like eggs. And along the far wall, in a cabinet built like a church reliquary, paper.

Mara went to the paper first. Elodie had known she would.

"Deeds," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word. She was holding a leather folio, its string rotted, its contents fanned across her hands like a losing card hand that had turned. "Survey plats. The original claim filings. *Amos Vane and Vane Holdings, 1894*—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "There's a patent here. A federal land patent. It was granted. It was *granted*, and then—"

"Then someone made it un-granted," Elodie said.

They worked by matchlight while the light outside went from copper to ash. Elodie pried crates, catalogued, kept their hands busy because their hands were the only part of them that knew what to do. Mara read. She read for an hour, kneeling on the plank floor in her ruined boots, and the letters she read aloud in fragments were a story Elodie recognized in its bones, because it was the same story as their father's, told from the other side.

The Vanes had struck the Mother Lode country in '93 with a provision outfit meant to supply the small claims—the one-man operations, the widows and immigrants and fools with a mule and a dream. Amos's father had built the provision house as much as a business as a promise: the little miners would never have to sell out to the big companies to survive a winter. The Halvard Consortium had arrived in '95. There had been a fire at the county recorder's office in '96—convenient, the letters said, with a lawyer's careful venom. There had been a re-filing, a forged signature, a tax assessment levied on land the Vanes no longer legally owned because the paper that said they owned it had burned. Amos had spent forty years and every dollar the outfit earned trying to buy the claim back from the consortium's lawyers, and had died of it by inches, alone in mining camps, while his granddaughter grew up thinking he'd abandoned her mother for gold.

"He didn't leave us," Mara said. She wasn't crying, exactly. It was worse than that—she was reading, her finger under each line, her voice level, and the levelness was the sound of a woman holding a door shut against a flood. "He was fighting them. He was fighting them his whole life, and he lost, and he was too proud to come home with nothing, so he stayed out here with nothing." She looked up. The matchlight moved in her eyes. "Elodie. There's a letter here from the territorial surveyor. Dated 1897. It says the original patent was *valid* and the re-filing was *irregular*. It says the consortium's title is defective. It's been sitting in a box in a mountain for sixty years."

"It's not money," Elodie said slowly, and the words felt like pulling a tooth. "It's standing. It's the right to say *this is ours* in a courtroom, with the paper to prove it. Amos wasn't leaving you an inheritance. He was leaving you a weapon."

Mara laughed—a short, stunned sound with no humor in it, or all the humor in the world. "He was leaving us a *war*."

* * *

They made no fire. Elodie wouldn't allow it—the valley was a bowl, and smoke in a bowl at dusk was a signal fire to anyone with eyes on the ridgelines, and Elodie had spent the last three days watching the backtrail with a feeling in their gut like a wire tightening. Reyes's crew had been a day behind them at the pass. Storms slowed everyone equally, but Reyes struck Elodie as a man who'd been slowed before and hadn't liked it.

They ate cold beans and hard bread in the provision house with the door barred, surrounded by the ghost of a family's whole intention—every crate a winter somebody meant to see through, every seed tin a spring somebody meant to plant. Elodie sat with their back against the reliquary cabinet and found they couldn't stop touching it, the way you touch a sore tooth.

"What happens now?" Mara asked. She had the deeds wrapped in oilcloth, against her body like a child. "We take this down the mountain. There's a courthouse in Kessler Flats. We file, we—"

"We're two people with no money and no lawyer," Elodie said, "holding papers a consortium's lawyers have had sixty years to figure out how to burn. And the permit that lets *them* be up here expires in nine days, which means for nine days they can do anything on this mountain and call it survey work."

"So we run. Tonight. Down the east side before the snow."

Elodie looked at her. In the dark the provision house was all shadow and suggestion, and Mara's face was a pale smudge above the wrapped folio, and Elodie felt the old arithmetic start up in them like a machine they couldn't unlearn: *two people, one pass, storm coming, odds bad, move now.* It was the arithmetic that had kept them alive for ten years. It had never once asked whether the life was worth the keeping.

"Maybe," Elodie said.

"You're doing it," Mara said. "The thing you do. I've watched it the whole trip. The second a place gets real, you start pricing the exits."

"The second a place gets real, people die in it." It came out of Elodie harder than they meant, with a decade behind it. "I've told you about my father."

"You've told me about a camp and a collapse and a man who stayed one season too long. I've told you what I think of that story." Mara's voice was low and absolutely steady. "Your father stayed and died. My grandfather ran and died. It seems to me the mountain doesn't much care which mistake you make. So maybe stop calling one of them a strategy."

Elodie had no answer. Outside, the wind had changed—it had come around to the north, and it had a new sound in it, a fine dry hiss against the timber walls, and Elodie was on their feet before they'd decided to move, because they knew that sound the way they knew their own pulse.

They opened the door a hand's width. The valley had gone blue-black, and out of that darkness the snow was coming—not falling but *arriving*, in long slanting sheets, the first real snow of the season, thick as a curtain drawn across the world.

"Storm's early," Elodie said. "We're not going anywhere tonight. Maybe not for days."

* * *

They heard the crew an hour into the dark.

Not voices—Elodie would have heard voices first, and been less afraid. What they heard was a sound that didn't belong: a rhythmic metallic knock, far off down the valley, patient as a clock. Then a light, swinging low along the talus, and a second light behind it, and the knock again, and Elodie understood: they were sounding the talus slope with a hammer, moving by touch in the dark, coming on like something that had all night and no fear of the cold.

Mara was at the window slit, the deeds pressed to her chest. "Is it—"

"Reyes." Elodie blew out the last match. "Six of them, maybe. They found the valley."

The knocking stopped. In the new silence Elodie could hear the snow hissing on the roof, could hear their own heart, could hear, distantly, a voice carrying flat across the frozen air—Reyes's voice, they were almost sure, though the words were eaten by wind. Then, closer, distinct, almost conversational, pitched to carry:

"Vane! Marchetti! We know you're in the old house. We watched your smoke from the ridge this afternoon."

*We watched your smoke.* Elodie closed their eyes. The fire they'd never lit—the fire from the pass, two nights back, banked in a hollow. Of course. Of course.

"You've got paper in there that belongs to my employer," the voice went on, unhurried, and now Elodie could see the lights spreading out below the house, three of them, four, spaced wide like men who knew how to surround a building. "And I've got a permit that says everything on this mountain belongs to my employer until it doesn't. Nine more days, Marchetti. Let's not make the mountain decide for us."

Elodie's hand found Mara's shoulder in the dark. Beneath their palm she was trembling—but she was also, Elodie realized, moving toward the door, toward the sound, her chin up, the folio clutched like a shield, and Elodie thought: *she's her grandfather's blood after all.*

The first hammer-blow landed on the provision house door, and the old iron rang like a bell.

Outside, the snow came down harder, as if the mountain itself had begun, at last, to take sides.
