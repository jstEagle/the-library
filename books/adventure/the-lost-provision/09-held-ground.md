## 9. Held Ground

The provision house had been built to outlast a century, and it was going to have to.

Mara stood in the doorway with the wind screaming down off the ridge behind her, watching Reyes's crew come up the draw through the white murk—six figures, ropes strung between them, moving with the grim economy of people who had done this before. Snow was already pasting itself to everything, thick as wet wool, and the light had gone the color of old tin.

"Inside," Elodie said at her shoulder. Not a suggestion. "Now."

"They'll—"

"Inside *now*, or we die arguing about it in the open."

They got the door barred with the iron rod that had waited a hundred years for exactly this purpose, and then there was nothing to do but listen to the storm take the mountain apart around them and wait for the knocking.

It came an hour into the dark. Three blows, measured, almost polite. Then Reyes's voice, muffled through timber two inches thick: "Vane! Marchetti! We know you're in there. We've got one tent left standing and it won't stand till morning. Open the door or open your conscience—your call."

Mara looked at Elodie. Firelight from the little stove caught the side of their face, and she saw something there she hadn't seen on the trail—not fear exactly, but the animal stillness of a creature calculating distances to every exit in the room.

"There's no back way," she said quietly. "I checked."

"I know." A breath. "I checked first."

She crossed the room and lifted the bar herself before Elodie could stop her.

* * *

Six of them came in crusted white, stamping and swearing softly, folding into the provision house like men entering a church they intended to rob. Reyes last, shaking snow from her collar, her eyes going over the room the way Mara had watched her go over terrain—cataloguing, pricing. The shelves of sealed tins. The seed stock in its glass jars. The tool racks. And on the table where they'd laid it all out, the oilcloth packet: deeds, letters, the surveyor's affidavit in faded brown ink proving that Elias Vane's stake had been filed, recorded, and then swallowed whole by the company that became Halvard.

Reyes looked at it for a long moment. Then at Amos's granddaughter standing between it and her.

"Well," she said. "You found it."

"We found it," Mara said. "And it's not what you think it is."

"Everything's something when you dig far enough down." Reyes pulled off her gloves finger by finger. "Here's the shape of the night, Vane. Storm's got us all. Nobody's walking anywhere till it breaks. So we sit, we share your stove, and come clear weather, my crew walks out of here with that paper or without you being able to stop us. I'd rather it be the first way. Fewer bodies to explain to Denver."

"You don't have a permit," Elodie said from the shadows by the stove. "Your concession expired in March. I read the filing in Kessler's office while you were buying drinks for the clerk."

Something flickered across Reyes's face—the first honest thing Mara had seen her wear. "The filing's late. Paperwork catches up. It always does."

"Not if there are witnesses," Mara said. "Not if there are *deeds*. That's the thing you keep not looking at, Ms. Reyes. This isn't a cache. It's a record. Amos Vane didn't hide gold up here—he hid proof. That his family held legal claim to half this range before Halvard's predecessors buried them in a courthouse instead of a mine. You can steal paper. But you can't steal it twice, and you can't un-steal it once people know."

The stove ticked. Wind leaned on the walls hard enough to make the beams groan, and somewhere above them the storm peeled another layer off the world.

Reyes sat down slowly on a seed crate, which Mara understood suddenly was the most dangerous thing she'd done all night. "You want to hear how I see it? I've spent eleven years working for Halvard. Eleven years of survey crews and expiring permits and men in Denver who talk about mountains like line items. You know what I've learned?" She spread her hands. "Paper doesn't beat them. I've seen paper beat. I've *filed* the paper that beat. The only thing that company respects is cost. So either holding this house costs them more than walking away—or it doesn't, and you two are a rounding error."

"Then why are you here in person?" Elodie asked. "A rounding error doesn't get a crew leader."

Reyes smiled without any warmth in it. "Because I'm tired of being the hand, Marchetti. I wanted to see the face."

* * *

The blizzard owned the next two days.

They divided the provision house by an unspoken treaty—one end for the consortium crew, one for Mara and Elodie, the stove and its small heat shared like a border river. Mara slept in snatches against the wall, waking each time to the same sounds: wind, canvas-voiced and endless; the crew murmuring in shifts; Elodie awake, always, sitting with their back to the shelving and their eyes on the door.

On the second night she crawled over and sat beside them.

"She offered you something," Mara said. "Yesterday, when I was checking the seals on the north wall. I saw her lean in. What was it?"

Elodie was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. "A way out. She's got a rope team and a weather window tomorrow morning, maybe. She said I could be on it. Her and me, down the east chute, gone before the crew even knew. She figures I'm the kind of person who takes that door."

"And?"

"And I told her I'd think about it."

Mara turned to look at them. In the stove light Elodie's face was all planes and shadow, ten years of leaving carved into it. "You're actually thinking about it."

"I'm *always* thinking about it," Elodie said, and there was something raw under the flatness, something that surprised them both. "That's the whole of me, Mara. That's the engine. Every camp I've ever broken, every wage I've walked off, every grave I never went back to—it's because staying is where people die. My father stayed. One season too long, one slope he should have known better than to trust, and I got a letter instead of a father. Staying killed him. Leaving kept me alive for ten years, and I am *good* at it, and you are asking me to stand in a wooden box on top of a mountain and let six armed people decide whether I get to keep doing it."

"I'm not asking you anything," Mara said. "I'm telling you what I'm doing. At first light I put those deeds in my coat, and when the storm clears I walk down to the county seat and I file them under Elias Vane's name, and if Halvard wants to contest it they can do it in a courtroom with reporters outside. That's my grandfather's name getting restored whether or not anyone stands next to me while I do it." She paused. "But I'll tell you what I figured out in the ghost camp, since you were too busy being haunted to hear me. You've got the arithmetic backwards. Leaving didn't keep you alive. It just kept you *available* to keep leaving. There's a difference, and the difference is everything you haven't done for ten years."

Elodie stared at the fire. The wind screamed. When they finally spoke, their voice was so low she nearly lost it under the storm.

"If I stay," they said, "and this goes wrong—if it goes wrong the way things go wrong—"

"Then it goes wrong with you having stood for something," Mara said. "Which is more than running ever bought you. I promise you that. I'm the granddaughter of a man who ran from us for forty years. I know exactly what running buys."

* * *

Dawn came gray and sudden, the way storms break—wind dying between one hour and the next, the silence after so total it rang. Mara was already dressed, the oilcloth packet buttoned inside her coat, when Reyes rose from her bedroll and crossed the frozen floor to stand over them both.

"Window's open," Reyes said. "Four hours, maybe five, before the next system. East chute holds snow. After that, nobody goes anywhere for a week." She looked at Elodie. "Last time I'm asking. Rope's rigged. One slot."

Mara held her breath.

Elodie stood. For a moment they just breathed, looking around the provision house—at the shelves their hands had inventoried, the stove they'd fed, the table where a dead man's truth lay folded in oilcloth—and Mara watched something happen in their face that she suspected had never happened there before. Not calculation. Decision.

"No," Elodie said.

Reyes raised an eyebrow.

"You heard me." Elodie stepped forward, and their voice carried, pitched deliberately for the whole crew, every word placed like a stone in a wall. "No. And you should think hard about your own answer, because here's what happens now. She files those deeds this week. Legal claim, recorded, witnessed—by *you*, all six of you, because you slept two nights under this roof and saw exactly what's on that table. Halvard's permit is dead. Their claim is built on stolen field notes from a man they defrauded, and the fraud is documented in triplicate in a packet that's about to become public record. Your employer isn't facing two treasure hunters anymore. They're facing a lawsuit with a face on it." Elodie nodded at Mara. "Hers. And every day this drags, that face is in a newspaper."

One of the crew shifted. Another muttered something to the man beside him.

Reyes studied Elodie for a long, cold moment. "You could've taken the rope."

"I know."

"That's the part I don't understand."

"It's the part I finally do," Elodie said.

Reyes looked at her crew. Looked at the storm-light strengthening in the high window, the mountain holding its breath. Whatever arithmetic she'd carried up the draw, Mara could see it being redone behind her eyes—costs shifting, columns falling—and when she exhaled, it came out slow, almost relieved, like a woman setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying.

"Strike camp," she said. "We're done here."

The crew moved without argument, rolling bedrolls with the mechanical obedience of people who had already done their own math in the night. At the door Reyes paused, hand on the iron bar, and glanced back once.

"For what it's worth, Vane—your grandfather would've liked seeing this. Somebody holding ground." A thin smile. "Don't waste it."

Then the door swung open onto a world remade in white, blue-shadowed and blinding, and the six of them roped up and went down the east chute, smaller and smaller, until the mountain took them back.

* * *

Mara stood in the doorway a long time after they vanished, the cold pouring over her like clean water, the deeds a solid weight against her ribs. Behind her she heard Elodie banking the fire, feeding it, settling in—doing the thousand small domestic acts of a person who intends to be somewhere tomorrow.

"The county seat's three days down," Mara said, not turning around. "Trail'll be buried. We'll be breaking path the whole way."

"I know."

"Could take weeks before the filing clears. Halvard will have lawyers there before we are."

"I know that too."

Mara finally turned. Elodie was crouched at the stove, firelight on their face, and they looked up at her with an expression she'd never seen on them—unguarded, almost startled, like a person who has just set down a pack carried so long the shoulders don't know their own weight.

"So," she said. "You're staying."

Elodie looked at the fire. At the shelves. At her.

"Yeah," they said, and their voice cracked slightly on it, and they laughed—a short, disbelieving sound, the laugh of someone stepping off a ledge and finding ground. "Yeah, I guess I am. God help me."

Mara grinned and shouldered her pack. "Then help me break trail. We've got a name to file."

Outside, the sun climbed over the Sawtooths and lit the whole white range burning, and they stepped out into it together.
