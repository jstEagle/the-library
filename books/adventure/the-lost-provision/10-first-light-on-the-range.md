## 10. First Light on the Range

The thaw came the way forgiveness does—slowly, then all at once.

For three days after the storm broke, the Sawtooth Range wept. Snowmelt ran down every gully and seam in silver threads, gathering in the low places, filling the silence with a sound like distant applause. The provision house stood open to it, its door no longer barricaded but propped wide, as if the building itself had decided there was nothing left inside worth hiding.

Elodie stood at that door on the fourth morning, watching the light come up over the eastern peaks, and did not reach for their pack.

It was a small thing, that not-reaching. Anyone watching would have missed it entirely. But Elodie felt it the way a man feels a bone knit—the absence of an old ache more noticeable than the ache itself. For ten years the pack had lived by the door, straps loosened, ready. In ghost camps and rented rooms and the backs of borrowed trucks, it had waited for the moment when staying became dangerous. It waited now, sitting against the provision house wall where they'd dropped it during the siege, and Elodie looked at it and felt nothing pull them toward it at all.

Behind them, Mara was already working.

She'd been up since before dawn, sorting through the letters again by lamplight—the ones Amos Vane had carried for forty years in a oilskin packet, the ones that proved her family's stake had been filed and recorded and then quietly buried under Halvard Consortium paper. She read them the way she did everything: with her jaw set, her hands steady, as if tenderness were a weakness she'd outgrown. But last night Elodie had seen her fold one of the letters—her grandfather's handwriting, cramped and apologetic—and tuck it inside her coat, over her heart, without ceremony or comment.

"You're staring," she said now, without turning around.

"I'm thinking."

"Don't hurt yourself."

Elodie smiled. Ten days ago that voice would have sent them reaching for the map and the door. Now it sounded almost like home.

* * *

The county recorder's office in Copper Bend occupied the ground floor of a building that had once been a bank, then a saloon, then a bank again, and had given up pretending to be anything but what it was: a place where paper became power. The clerk behind the counter was a woman named Odette Fisk who had processed claims for thirty years and could smell a fraudulent filing from across the room.

Mara laid the deeds on the counter like a winning hand.

"Provision cache, Sawtooth Range, section fourteen," she said. "Filed originally in 1897 under Vane. I'm claiming under the same name."

Odette adjusted her spectacles. "Vane. There hasn't been a Vane filing since—" She stopped. Her eyes went from the deeds to Mara's face and back. "Amos Vane's granddaughter."

"You knew him?"

"Everybody knew Amos." Something moved behind the clerk's expression—regret, maybe, or the particular guilt of people who watch a man drink himself toward a cliff and never say a word. "He used to come in every spring. Every spring he'd ask about his father's claim, and every spring I'd tell him the records showed lapsed title, and every spring he'd nod like he expected it and walk out." She pulled the ledger toward herself. "The records were wrong. Or made wrong. That happens sometimes, when the right money talks to the right people."

"And now?"

"Now you've got original instruments, witness affidavits, and a consortium permit that expired eleven days ago." Odette began writing. "Now it's just paperwork, Miss Vane. The best kind of fight there is."

Elodie stood by the door while Mara signed—her name, then her grandfather's beneath it, restored to the record in ink after four decades of erasure. They watched her hand move and thought about how many times they had signed false names in rooms like this. How many towns held a version of them built entirely out of aliases. *J. Marlow. E. Crane. S. Whitlock.* A dozen ghosts, all of them wearing Elodie's face.

When Mara finished, she turned. "Your turn."

"I don't file anything."

"You do today." She slid a second form across the counter. "Site steward. Archaeological survey. Odette says the state requires a named party of record." Her mouth curved, just slightly. "I need your real name, Elodie. On paper. Where it can't be outrun."

The form sat there. One line, blank, waiting.

Elodie picked up the pen. Their hand did not shake, which surprised them. They wrote *Marchetti* in careful letters—the first time in ten years the name had touched official paper—and felt the word land somewhere deep, like a coin dropped down a well, still falling, still theirs.

"There," Mara said. "Now you exist."

"Been existing all along. Just quietly."

"Not anymore." She took the forms back from Odette, rolled them, and tapped them once against her palm. "You're on record, Marchetti. Consortium comes sniffing around again, there's a paper trail leading straight to you."

"Good," Elodie heard themselves say, and meant it, and marveled at the meaning of it.

* * *

They spent two weeks at the provision house before the first supply wagon could reach them, and those weeks were the strangest of Elodie's life, because nothing in them required running.

They catalogued. That was the work, and it turned out to be the work they were born for—though their father, digging for gold, had never known it. Seed stock in sealed tins, labeled in a prospector's careful hand. Tools wrapped in oiled canvas, still bright. Survey chains, plumb lines, a transit in its case. And documents, hundreds of them: correspondence, receipts, the whole fossilized bureaucracy of a 1890s venture that had died not from failure but from theft. Elodie handled each piece with cotton gloves and something close to reverence.

"This isn't a treasure," Mara said one evening, watching them work. "It's a testimony."

"It's both. Testimony's just treasure that takes longer to spend."

She laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the first Elodie had heard from her—and the sound filled the old provision house the way the lamplight did, pushing back corners that had been dark for a century.

At night they cooked over the same fire Reyes's crew had huddled around during the blizzard, and Elodie found they no longer flinched at the memory. The consortium had withdrawn, their permits dead, their lawyers suddenly very interested in settlement rather than litigation once Odette's filings went public and a newspaper in Boise ran the story under the headline *THE CHEATED HEIRS OF THE SAWTOOTH*. Reyes himself had come by on the second day of thaw—not with guns, but with a handshake and a warning that Halvard's parent company had deeper pockets and longer memories.

"They'll be back," Reyes said. "Not this year. Maybe not next. But men like that don't lose. They wait."

"Then we'll be here when they do," Mara said.

Reyes had looked at Elodie then—at the person who, ten days earlier, he'd offered a bribe and an escape route to—and whatever he saw in their face made him nod slowly, like a man confirming a rumor. "Huh," he'd said. "You stayed."

"I stayed."

He'd left them a crate of coffee as payment for the truth, which Elodie considered the fairest trade of their entire career.

* * *

On the morning the wagon came, Elodie walked up the ridge above the provision house one last time—not to leave, but to look. The range spread out below, white going patchy with green, meltwater singing in every draw. Somewhere down there was the low pass where a rockslide had nearly taken Mara, and the ghost camp where Elodie had finally spoken their father's name aloud, and the cave where two enemies had traded truths through a long storm night. The mountains held all of it now. They would hold it whether anyone visited or not.

Their father had come to this range chasing gold and died running from what he found. Elodie had spent ten years proving they were nothing like him, and had only now understood that they'd inherited the wrong lesson. He hadn't died because he stayed. He'd died because he stayed alone, with lies, holding everything too tight. Staying wasn't the danger. Staying empty was.

Below, Mara was directing the wagon team with the brisk authority of someone who had been born to command mules and simply hadn't known it. She looked up, caught Elodie on the ridge, and cupped her hands.

"You coming down or are you planning to haunt the place?"

"Haven't decided."

"Liar." She grinned and turned back to the work.

Elodie stood a moment longer. Then they shouldered the pack—one last time, though it didn't feel like leaving; it felt like carrying—and started down the trail toward the provision house, toward the catalogued century, toward the woman with her grandfather's name on fresh ink and a future that needed an archaeologist more than it needed a ghost.

The snowmelt ran bright beside the path. Somewhere below, Mara was laughing at something the teamster said, and the sound carried up the mountain clean as water.

Elodie Marchetti walked toward it, and did not look back, because there was nothing behind them anymore worth running from—and everything ahead worth stopping for.
