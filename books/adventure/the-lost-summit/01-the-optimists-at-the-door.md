## 1. The Optimists at the Door

The rain had been falling on Saltmere Quay for six days without conviction — a thin, grey drizzle that never quite committed to being a storm and never quite stopped long enough to let the world dry out. Beatrix Dunmore stood at the window of her boathouse office with a chipped mug of cold coffee in one hand and a ledger she was pretending to read in the other, watching two men in pale yellow raincoats come up the ramp like a pair of cheerful infections.

She knew them before they reached the door. Everyone on the Quay knew the Vane Syndicate's colors by now: that particular butter-yellow, chosen — Ilya would have told her later, though she didn't know him yet — because yellow is the color of optimism, and optimism is the most disarming weapon ever fielded by an accounting firm with teeth.

"Beatrix!" called the taller one through the glass, before she could decide whether to pretend she wasn't home. "Beatrix Dunmore! It's a miserable morning, isn't it? Wonderful weather for paperwork!"

That was Corvin. She remembered his name from the last visit, three months ago, when he'd sat in this same office drinking her tea and complimenting her charts while his partner photographed her instruments. Corvin had a voice like a man reading bedtime stories to children he genuinely loved, and hands that moved constantly, expressively, as though every sentence were a small gift he was unwrapping for you.

The shorter one was Pell. Pell didn't talk much. Pell smiled. It was the smile that frightened people more than any threat could have — wide, warm, untroubled, the smile of a man who had already seen how everything ended and found it all quite satisfactory.

Beatrix set down the mug. She checked, out of forty years of habit, that the storm shutters were oiled, that the barometer on the wall read steady-low, that her boots were where her feet expected them. Then she went to open the door, because the only thing worse than letting the Vane Syndicate in was making them stand outside smiling at your windows until they decided to come in anyway.

"Gentlemen," she said. "You're early."

"We're punctual," Corvin said happily, stepping past her into the boathouse like a tide coming in. "There's a difference, and it's my favorite difference. Oh — look at this place. You've done something with the shelving."

"I haven't."

"You've *done something* with it. It has intention now." He turned a slow circle, taking inventory with his eyes, and Beatrix felt each glance land on her possessions like a small cold hand. The winch. The cable spools. The anemometer mounted on the roof mast, its cups turning lazily in the drizzle. The pressure gauges in their felt-lined case. Dov's dredging rig, still dripping from yesterday's run, coiled on the deck of the *Vesper* like something sleeping.

Pell stood just inside the doorway and smiled at the rain.

"Thirty days," Corvin said, and produced a folder — cream-colored, heavy stock, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than Beatrix's weekly food budget. He laid it on her desk with both hands, reverently, like a priest setting down a relic. "Well. Thirty-one, if we count today, and we do count today, because we count everything. That's rather our whole profession."

"I know what thirty days means, Corvin."

"Of course you do. Of course you do. But it's polite to say it aloud, don't you think? Rituals matter. My grandmother used to say grace over meals she couldn't afford, and I've always thought there was something in that." He opened the folder. Inside were pages of neat columns, and Beatrix did not look at them, because looking at them meant admitting they described things she owned. "Fourteen thousand, six hundred and twelve marks, principal. Interest accruing daily at the rate we discussed. Which brings us, at day thirty-one, to approximately fifteen thousand nine hundred. Give or take a storm."

"Give or take a storm," Beatrix repeated.

"That's the charming thing about your line of work, Ms. Dunmore. Your collateral appreciates in bad weather." He beamed at her as if she'd said something clever. Perhaps she had. With Corvin it was hard to tell whether you were having a conversation or being processed.

* * *

They walked the boat together, because Corvin asked so nicely and because refusing would have told him exactly how much she hated what came next. Rain ticked against the *Vesper*'s canvas cover. The river slid past below the dock, brown and swollen with the season's first meltwater, carrying twigs and a child's lost shoe and, somewhere far upstream, the runoff of mountains nobody on this coast had ever bothered to name.

"Now this," Corvin said, laying a hand flat on the dredging winch the way other men touched horses, "is a beautiful machine. Do you know what I love about dredges? They bring things up. Things the water was keeping safe. There's a whole economy down there, Ms. Dunmore, under the silt. Whole histories. And here you are with a machine that reads the riverbed like a menu." He laughed at his own joke, warmly, and waited until she laughed too, and she did, because not laughing felt more dangerous than laughing.

"You've inventoried all this already," she said. "Three months ago. Photographs, serial numbers, the lot."

"We refresh our records quarterly. Best practice." He clicked his tongue. "Also, frankly, some of this is new since spring. The sonar unit. The spare propeller. You've been investing."

"I've been working."

"Working. Investing. In a healthy enterprise those are the same word." He made another note on a slate he carried — an actual slate, chalked, old-fashioned, though Beatrix suspected the chalk was theater. "Now. I want to be transparent with you, because transparency is a value at Vane. If day thirty-one arrives without resolution, we begin recovery proceedings. Recovery is such an ugly word, isn't it? What it means in practice is that Pell and I come back with a barge and a crew of movers, and we take the *Vesper*, the rig, the sonar, the gauges, and — this is the part that always makes me sad — the hull itself. We sell it all at auction. Auctions are efficient but they're never fair, and you'll get almost nothing back after fees. Almost nothing. I want you to hear me say that kindly, because I mean it kindly."

"And if I pay?"

"Then none of that happens, and we go back to being your favorite creditors." He smiled. "We are your favorite creditors, aren't you? We've never once shouted at you."

It was true. That was the obscenity of it. Four years of debt and not one raised voice, not one threat delivered in anger, not one moment she could point to and call cruelty. The Syndicate had learned what older empires knew: that fear burns out, but cheerfulness corrodes. A man who threatens you gives you something to fight. A man who smiles while he appraises your life gives you nothing at all except the slow humiliation of owing happy people money.

Beatrix leaned against the rail and looked out at the river instead of at him.

"My mother banked with the old Karvathi houses," she said, mostly to the water. "Before the war ate them. She said the clerks wore black and never smiled, and she preferred it. At least you knew they were robbing you."

Corvin's smile didn't flicker, which was itself a kind of skill. "Karvath," he said, tasting the word. "That's a name I haven't heard in years. Erased nation. Wiped off the maps in what, 'forty-one? Forty-two?" He shook his head with genuine, gentle wonder. "You know what survived of Karvath, Ms. Dunmore? Its debts. Paper doesn't drown. Somewhere in a vault in Vane City there are ledgers with Karvathi names in them, still accruing interest, waiting for heirs who no longer exist to inherit obligations they never signed." He sighed happily. "I find that beautiful. Don't you?"

"No."

"Well." He closed the folder. "Nobody's perfect."

* * *

Pell spoke exactly twice during the entire visit, and Beatrix catalogued both utterances the way she catalogued shifts in barometric pressure — small readings that meant large weather.

The first time was in the office, afterward, while Corvin gathered his folder and his slate and his inexhaustible good humor. Pell stepped close — closer than the transaction required — and looked up at her with that vast, contented smile, and said:

"Lovely compass."

He nodded toward the desk, where her brass compass sat beside the cold coffee. Her grandmother's compass, actually; the one thing in the room that wasn't collateral, that had never appeared in any photograph, because Beatrix had learned early never to let the Syndicate's cameras see the few things she loved. It was a plain instrument, dented, its needle housing fogged with age, its face engraved with characters in no alphabet used on this coast. She kept it in a drawer when strangers visited. Today, in the hurry of opening the door, she had left it out.

"It's broken," she said. "Doesn't point anywhere useful."

"Mm," said Pell, still smiling. "Funny. The old ones usually point somewhere very useful indeed. Just not north." And he tipped an imaginary hat and followed Corvin out into the rain.

The second time was from the bottom of the ramp, turning back with his coat collar up, calling cheerfully through the drizzle:

"Thirty days, Ms. Dunmore! Weather's coming! We're rooting for you!"

And then they were gone, two yellow shapes diminishing along the quay, unhurried, waving at the fishmonger, nodding to the net-menders, beloved by everyone, feared by everyone, indistinguishable from tourists.

* * *

Beatrix stood in the doorway until the rain had washed even the memory of yellow off the boards. Then she went inside, picked up her grandmother's compass, and held it in both hands.

The needle swung. It had always swung — lazily, drunkenly, settling on a bearing that matched no map she owned, drifting a degree or two year by year as though it were following something that was itself moving. As a girl she'd thought it broken. As a young woman chasing storms across three coastlines, she'd thought it romantic. Now, with fifteen thousand marks of cheerful arithmetic sitting on her desk and thirty days ticking down like water filling a hull, she looked at the fogged dial and the worn engraving and felt, for the first time, that the compass was not broken at all.

It was pointing somewhere. It had always been pointing somewhere. She simply hadn't known enough to ask where.

Outside, the drizzle thickened, and far upriver, where the brown water came down out of country that had been erased from every map for forty years, the river rose a hand's width against the pilings, patient as a creditor, bringing something with it from the drowned places.

Beatrix put the compass in her pocket, poured the cold coffee over the rail, and went to find Dov.
