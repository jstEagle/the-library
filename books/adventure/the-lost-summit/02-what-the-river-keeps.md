## 2. What the River Keeps

The storm left the river the color of old pewter, and it left Beatrix Dunmore with a headache that sat behind her eyes like a fist she couldn't unclench.

She stood on the deck of the *Vela* at first light, watching the water run high and brown and fast, carrying whole trees past them like matchsticks. The surge had come through in the night—two meters of angry water, a sky that split itself open over the hills, thunder rolling down the valley in long, indignant peals. Now the world was rinsed and dripping. Mist hung off the far bank in rags. Somewhere upstream, a landslide had let go; you could smell the raw earth of it, wet clay and torn roots, sweet as a grave.

"River's generous after a tantrum," Dov said, coming up beside her with two tin mugs of coffee that tasted like boiled tires. He handed her one anyway. "Always is."

"That's what I like about you, Dov. Your poetry."

"I have others. *Storm broke the net again.* *Fuel costs more than fish.* *We are all going to die poor.*" He blew across his mug. "Classics, all of them."

Beatrix almost smiled. Almost. The debt sat in her chest like ballast she couldn't jettison—thirty days, Merrit Vane had said, thirty days and then we take the boat, the gear, the winch, the sonar rig, everything with a serial number—and the only way out of it was to find something in this river worth more than the *Vela* herself. Which meant dredging. Which meant luck.

"Luck," she said aloud, testing the word.

"What?"

"Nothing. Get the winch warmed up. The surge will have moved the shoal—I want to run the grid again before the silt settles."

Dov looked at her the way he always did when she said something like that—a long, level look, the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't share—and then he shrugged and went aft, and the diesel coughed twice and caught, and the *Vela* turned her blunt nose upriver toward the shoals below Karvath Reach.

* * *

The shoal hadn't been there last season. That was the thing about rivers: they were liars. They gave you a channel one year and took it back the next, built islands overnight out of nothing, buried things and unburied them on whims no chart ever predicted. The storm had carved a new bar of gravel and gray sand maybe two hundred meters off the true bank, a pale tongue of land just breaking the surface, and the water around it ran shallow enough that the sonar read bottom clean and sharp.

And the bottom read back.

"There," Dov said, tapping the screen. A hard return, angular, sitting in the scour pool where the current curled around the new bar like a hand closing. "That's not rock. Rock doesn't make corners."

Beatrix leaned over the display. The shape was boxy, regular, man-made, roughly a meter by half a meter by half a meter, lying canted in the sand like something dropped rather than lost. Her heart did something stupid and hopeful against her ribs.

"Grapple," she said. "Slow pass. Don't tear it up—if it's what I think it might be, condition matters."

"If it's what you think it might be, we're rich." Dov was already moving, feeding the cable, his big hands gentle with the controls in the way they never were with anything else. "If it's not, we're still poor, but we'll be poor with a story."

The hook went down. The river held its breath. Then the cable came taut, the winch groaned, and the *Vela* leaned into her work, and for three long minutes there was nothing in the world but the sound of steel straining against the weight of whatever the flood had decided to give back.

It broke the surface at 7:40 in the morning, streaming water, swinging on the cable like a hanged man.

It was a chest. Bronze, or bronze-clad—the green of decades had bloomed across it in continents and archipelagos, verdigris mapping its own private geography—but under the scum the metal was thick, cast in ribs, banded at the corners with fittings that had been gilded once. It was heavy. It was intact. And on the lid, centered, stamped deep enough to survive forty years of sand and flood, was a seal.

Beatrix knew seals. She'd grown up around salvage men who could read a hallmark at ten paces, and she knew shipping marks, customs stamps, house crests, the whole dull alphabet of ownership. This was none of those. This was a national arms: a mountain with three peaks above crossed keys, ringed by a border of interlocking waves, and beneath the mountain, small and precise, an inscription in a script she didn't recognize—angular, confident, letters that looked like they'd been cut to last.

She knew it anyway. Not from study. From somewhere older than study.

Her grandmother's compass. The brass one that lived in her jacket pocket, the one with no maker's mark and a needle that had never once pointed north without argument. On its reverse, worn nearly smooth by a thumb that had rubbed it for fifty years, there was an engraving. Three peaks. Crossed keys.

"Dov," she said. Her voice came out strange. Flat. "What country is that?"

He squinted at it, coffee mug still in hand, and she watched the recognition move across his face like weather. "Karvath," he said slowly. "That's Karvath. My grandfather used to talk about it—there were Karvathi fishermen on this stretch when he was a boy. Whole villages. Then the war, the annexation, whatever you want to call it, and—" he waved a hand at the horizon, at the maps that weren't printed anymore, "—gone. Wiped off. Forty years now. Nobody's Karvath. It's just 'the Reach' now."

"The Reach," Beatrix repeated. The word tasted borrowed.

Karvath. She turned the name over and found it fit her mouth better than it should have. Her grandmother had sung songs in a language Beatrix had assumed was just lullaby nonsense, syllables made up to soothe a child, and had cooked dishes nobody else in any port town cooked, and had died holding a brass compass that pointed stubbornly at nothing anyone could name. Beatrix had inherited the compass and never asked the right questions, because by then there was no one left to answer them.

Now the river was answering instead.

"We should open it," Dov said.

"No."

"It might be full of water and rust, Trix. Or it might be full of coins. Either way we should know before someone else finds out we have it. You know how news travels on this river. By tonight every dock rat from here to the delta will know the *Vela* pulled up something heavy."

"And if we crack it open and it's coins, we sell them, and the syndicate hears we sold coins, and the syndicate decides coins mean we can pay early, and then they decide we can pay more." She set her palm flat on the lid, on the cold wet bronze, on the three peaks. The metal was river-cold and seemed to drink the warmth from her hand. "This stays closed until I say otherwise."

Dov looked at her for a long moment. "Since when do you get sentimental over scrap?"

Since my grandmother's ghost started talking, she didn't say. Since a dead country put its hand on my boat.

"Since it's mine," she said instead. "Salvage law. Open water, no registered owner, recovered in our waters by our gear. It's ours. All of it. That includes the decision of when to open it."

"Ours," Dov repeated, and there was something in the way he said it—not quite agreement, not quite question—that she filed away next to the headache and the debt and the growing list of things she'd worry about later.

They lashed the chest to the deck amidships, wrapped in a tarp, chained to the mast step. It sat there through the morning like a passenger. Like a guest. Beatrix kept finding reasons to walk past it, and each time she did, she laid her hand on the tarp for just a second, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure had finished dreaming.

* * *

By afternoon the mist had burned off and the light came hard off the water, and Beatrix sat on the cabin roof with the chest's photograph developing in her head—she'd taken six frames, angles, the seal, the corner fittings—and tried to think like a debtor instead of a daughter.

Sell it sealed. That was the smart play. A sealed chest with a national seal of a vanished nation was a curiosity, and curiosities sold to collectors for more than their contents sometimes. Let some rich fool in the capital pay for the mystery. Pay off Vane. Keep the *Vela*. Walk away clean.

Except.

Except that her thumb kept going to the compass in her pocket, and the needle kept doing what it always did, which was swing lazily southwest and stop, pointing at the high country where the maps went blank, where the old charts showed a summit called—what? She'd never known. Her grandmother had called it something soft, two syllables, a word like a hand closing over another hand.

Except that when she imagined a stranger's crowbar prying that lid, something in her chest clenched like a fist around a bird.

"You're doing it again," Dov said, climbing up beside her. He'd changed into dry clothes and failed to change his expression. "Talking to a box."

"I'm thinking."

"You're communing. There's a difference, and the difference is money." He settled himself, elbows on knees. "Trix. I love you like a sister, so I'm going to say the ugly thing. Whatever's in there—it belonged to a country that doesn't exist. Its owners are dead or scattered or don't legally exist either. Nobody's coming to claim it. Nobody's going to arrest us for selling it. And we have thirty days before people who smile too much take everything you've ever built. Sell it. Sell it sealed, sell it whole, sell it tomorrow. Buy yourself back."

"I know the math, Dov."

"Do you? Because your math has been wrong since the day you bought this boat with money you didn't have, chasing storms for insurance surveys because it was, quote, honest work. Your math is always wrong in the same direction. Toward the thing you want to be true." He wasn't cruel about it. That was the worst part. He was gentle, the way you're gentle with someone standing on ice. "I've been on this boat four years. I'd like to be on it four more. But I can't be on it if Vane takes it, and Vane takes it unless that chest turns into numbers."

Beatrix looked at the tarp. Underneath it, the three peaks. The crossed keys. The waves that interlocked forever around a nation that had drowned on dry land, erased by treaty instead of water.

"When I was eight," she heard herself say, "my grandmother told me we used to be from somewhere. Not a town. A somewhere. She said the river remembered even when the maps forgot. I thought it was a story. Old women tell stories." She turned the compass over in her hand, showed him the worn engraving. Dov went very still.

"That's the same crest," he said quietly.

"That's the same crest."

The wind moved the mist off the far bank, and for a moment the hills stood clear and green and ordinary, and Beatrix felt the strangest sensation, as though the landscape were a page and something written on it had just become legible.

"So it's not scrap," Dov said at last. "It's yours. Or you're its. One of those, and neither one pays a debt."

"Neither one pays a debt," she agreed.

They sat with that while the light went long and gold and the river ran on, patient, keeping its counsel. Somewhere downstream, in a city of smiling accountants, thirty days were already burning down like a fuse. Somewhere upstream, in hills that used to have a name, something waited under stone and water that a bronze key had been built to open—or so Beatrix would learn, though she didn't know it yet, not until a gaunt stranger with archive dust still on his coat came walking down the dock at dusk with recognition in his eyes and hunger in his voice.

For now there was only the chest, and the compass, and the needle swinging southwest and settling, faithful as a heartbeat, toward home.

"Trix," Dov said. "There's a boat coming up the channel. Running dark."

She looked. A low silhouette against the last of the light, engines muffled, no lights, no flag—moving like something that didn't want to be seen, straight toward the shoal where the *Vela* lay at anchor.

Beatrix stood up slowly, her hand closing around the compass until the brass bit her palm.

"Well," she said. "Let's see who the river sent."
