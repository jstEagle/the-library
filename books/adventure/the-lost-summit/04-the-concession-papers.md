## 4. The Concession Papers

Rain came in off the river that evening the way it always did in the shoulder season—not falling so much as arriving, a gray wall of it that turned the boathouse windows to smoked glass and made the whole world sound like it was breathing through wet cloth. I'd lit every lamp I owned, which was four, and hung them at corners of the workbench like a poor man's constellation. Under that amber light, the chest sat on two sawhorses where Dov and I had dragged it, dripping river, and Ilya Senn stood over it with his sleeves rolled and his hands hovering above the bronze the way a priest hovers over an altar he's not sure still believes in him.

He hadn't touched it since we'd carried him in from the rain. He'd looked at it for a long time—long enough that I'd started counting the drips coming through the roof—and then he'd said, very quietly, "May I?" as if the thing were alive and might object.

Now his fingers moved across the seal the way you'd read braille. The crest of Karvath: a mountain split by a river, crowned by a sun with nine rays. I knew it only because I'd spent two nights staring at it myself, tracing the stamped lines with a thumbnail, feeling something turn over in my chest that I couldn't name and didn't want to examine too closely. A dead country's face, pressed into metal forty years before I was born.

"Say it again," I said. "The part about the vault."

Ilya didn't look up. He had the kind of voice that seemed built for archives—dry, precise, with a rasp underneath like paper being turned. "Every nation keeps a copy of itself somewhere. Deeds. Rolls. Names. Karvath kept its heart in the mountains, at the old summit station, behind a door that opens to this." He tapped the crest once, gently, the way you'd knock. "The chest is not treasure, Ms. Dunmore. It is a key."

"And you know this how?"

"Because I was an archivist before I was nothing." He smiled without any humor in it. "I spent eleven years in the Provincial Records Hall in Corvane, cataloguing colonial concession files. Karvath was my specialty. My obsession, if you ask the people who fired me." His hands finally settled flat on the bronze lid, and I watched them tremble—not fear, I decided. Something closer to hunger. "They didn't fire me for asking about Karvath. They fired me for asking *correctly*."

Outside, the river muttered against the pilings. Dov was up on the deck pretending to re-coil lines that were already coiled, which was what Dov did when he wanted to listen without admitting he was listening. I let him. Two sets of ears against one stranger's mouth seemed like fair odds.

"All right," I said. "Convince me. Start wherever makes sense, because none of this makes sense yet."

Ilya pulled the crate stool closer and sat, folding his long frame down like a compass closing. Up close he smelled of mildew and ink, and there was a stain on his cuff that might have been rust or might have been forty years of somebody else's history bleeding through.

"The Concession Era," he said. "You'll have heard the word even if nobody taught you what it meant. When the colonial charter companies moved into the highlands, they didn't conquer Karvath—they *leased* it. Paper instead of powder. The Crown granted extraction concessions: timber rights here, mineral rights there, river tolls along the whole length of the Sere. And the companies, being companies, wrote themselves generous terms. Forty-year terms, in most cases. Renewable, of course. Renewal was never meant to be a question."

"But Karvath fell," I said. "Forty years ago. Before I was born."

"Karvath was dissolved. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire point." Ilya leaned forward, and lamplight caught the hollows under his eyes. "When a nation is erased from the maps, its treaties don't vanish. Its obligations don't vanish. The concessions remained valid instruments—held now by whoever bought the debt, inherited the charters, absorbed the contracts. In our case, that means the Vane Syndicate holds more paper claims on Karvathi land than any living government does. They've been quietly buying those instruments for decades. Every defaulted loan, every lapsed charter, every company that went under holding Karvathi rights—the Vanes bought the corpse's wardrobe."

Dov's boots creaked on the deck boards overhead. I pretended not to hear him stop moving.

"So the syndicate owns the leases," I said slowly. "On land that isn't a country anymore."

"On land whose *records* aren't anywhere the syndicate can reach." Ilya's voice dropped, and I found myself leaning in despite myself. "Here is what the concession papers say, Ms. Dunmore, in clause after clause that the drafters never imagined anyone would read closely again: the grants are conditional. They expire at the end of their term *unless* renewed by a claimant in good standing—and renewal requires presentation at the source registry. Not a courthouse in Corvane. Not a syndicate office. The registry of record for the highland concessions sits at the summit station itself. The vault."

My skin prickled. "And when does the term end?"

"When the monsoon peaks." He said it simply, the way you'd say *when the tide turns*. "That was written into the original instruments—a seasonal grace clause. Concessions lapse at monsoon peak unless claimed in person, at the registry, before the waters close the passes. The drafters assumed any legitimate holder would renew years in advance, the way you'd pay rent. Nobody ever imagined a scenario where the nation itself was gone and no one came."

"And this year?"

"This year, the peak falls—" he did arithmetic on his fingers, quick and certain, "—nineteen days from now. Give or take a day, depending on how the pressure systems stack up over the plateau. And you're a storm chaser, so you'll know better than I whether the season's running early."

I did know. The barometer in the boathouse had been falling for three days straight, and the clouds over the western ridge had that bruised, organized look that meant the real rains were loading up somewhere out past the horizon. Nineteen days. Maybe fewer.

"And if nobody claims it," I said, "if nobody stands at that registry before the peak—"

"Then every concession lapses. All of them. Timber, mineral, river toll, the lot." Ilya's hands had gone still on the chest. "And here is the clause that made me unemployable: upon lapse, all rights revert to the lawful successors of the dispossessed sovereign. To the Karvathi people, Ms. Dunmore. To the survivors. To the villages scattered along this river who've spent forty years fishing water that legally belonged to someone else. It would all come home."

* * *

For a while neither of us spoke. The rain filled the silence, drumming on the roof, hissing off the river, and I became aware of my own heartbeat the way you become aware of a clock only when it stops.

"That can't be right," I said finally. "If that were true—if it were that simple—the syndicate would have—"

"They did." Ilya's mouth twisted. "Three months ago, the Vane Syndicate filed a renewal claim. Full documentation, registered through their Corvane counsel. They intend to stand at the summit registry before monsoon peak and renew everything for another forty years. Another lifetime of owning a country that doesn't exist, harvested by men who never saw it." He paused. "There's one complication. One small, beautiful complication."

"The key," I said.

"The key." He nodded at the chest between us. "The summit vault doesn't open to lawyers. It opens to the sovereign instrument—the national seal-carrier, the chest that held Karvath's own copy of its records. Without it, the registry cannot verify a claimant's standing. The syndicate has been searching for this chest for forty years. They dredged the lake district. They bought salvage fleets. They interrogated archivists." His eyes flicked up to mine, and there was something raw in them. "I know because I was one of the ones interrogated. That's the polite word for it."

The lamp nearest the window guttered as a gust shoved rain against the glass. I thought about the two smiling men who'd stood on this same dock twelve days ago, complimenting my winch, appraising my boat with cheerful little notes in leather books. Thirty days, they'd said. Such lovely equipment, they'd said. We do hope you'll find a way.

Nineteen days until the monsoon peak. Thirty days until the Vanes stopped smiling.

The math sat down inside my chest and refused to leave.

"How much," I said, and my voice came out flatter than I intended, "is your knowledge worth? If I take you upriver. If I get you and that chest to the summit before the peak. What happens then?"

"Then we present the instrument. The registry verifies the seal, and the lawful claimants—the Karvathi successors—can contest the syndicate's renewal in person. Maren Osta's village alone could file. There are others. But someone has to carry the proof, and someone has to open the door, and both of those someones are currently sitting in a leaking boathouse owned by a woman who owes her boat to the very people trying to bury this forever." Ilya spread his hands. "I won't lie to you. This is not a rescue plan. It's a race."

"A race we're losing by three months' head start."

"A race they don't know has a second runner." He held my gaze. "They filed their claim quietly. They think the key is lost. They think the archive tradition died with the country. As far as Merrit Vane knows, she walks into that registry unopposed." A pause, and then, softer: "Unless a storm chaser who dredges shoals nobody else bothers with happened to pull a dead nation's heart out of the river."

* * *

I got up because sitting still felt suddenly impossible. I walked the length of the boathouse, past the hanging nets, past the storm rigging I'd rebuilt three times, past the chalkboard where Dov kept our accounts in his careful carpenter's hand. The bottom line was written there in numbers I knew by heart the way you know a scar: what we owed the Vanes, what the boat was worth, what was left over. Nothing was left over. Nothing had been left over for two years.

"You understand what you're asking," I said, without turning around. "If the syndicate finds out I'm carrying that chest toward their claim, it won't be smiling men with inventory lists next time. It'll be gunboats in the gorge. It'll be my debt called in full, my boat taken, probably worse. You're asking me to bet the only things I own on a legal technicality written by dead colonizers."

"I'm asking you to bet on a deadline," Ilya said. "Deadlines are the great equalizer. Money bends everything except time. The Vanes can buy judges, salvagers, archivists, silence. They cannot buy the monsoon. When the peak comes, the clause fires whether they like it or not—all they can do is be standing at the registry when it does. And so can we."

I turned. He was still seated, still gaunt, still looking like a man assembled from spare parts and stubbornness, but there was a light in him now that hadn't been there when Dov and I fished him out of the rain. Hope, I realized. Actual hope. I'd forgotten what it looked like on a person. I'd mostly forgotten what it felt like.

"Why me?" I asked. "Why tell me any of this? You washed up on my dock by accident. You could have gone to the diaspora villages directly—to these people, Maren Osta, whoever they are. People with a *right* to this. Why bring it to a debt-ridden salvager who's half-tempted to sell the chest and make her problems go away?"

It was the honest question, and we both knew it. Dov had asked a version of it last night, turning the chest's latch under his thumb: *One sale, Bea. One sale and we're clean.* And I hadn't said no. That was the truth I was carrying around like a stone—I hadn't said no.

Ilya considered me for a long moment. Rain ticked against the roof. Somewhere out on the black river, a barge horn moaned once and faded.

"Because I went to the villages first," he said. "Two years ago. I walked into Maren Osta's kitchen with my maps and my clauses, and I told an old woman that her country could be legally resurrected if someone carried a piece of bronze through a war zone of syndicate patrol boats during monsoon season." His voice roughened. "She poured me tea. She listened. And then she told me that everyone in that village who was young enough to make the run had already given enough to the river, and everyone old enough to remember Karvath was too old to survive it. She said, 'Find someone who owes the river nothing and the world everything. Find someone angry enough to row.' And then she gave me her grandson's boat, which sank off Cape Verren, which is why I was in the water you pulled me from." He almost smiled. "So you see, Ms. Dunmore. I didn't choose you. I was sent. By a grandmother, in a kitchen, with tea. It's how all the important missions in history have actually started, whatever the ballads claim."

I laughed. I hadn't expected to; it came out cracked and surprised, and it broke something loose in my chest that had been locked since the smiling men left. Ilya watched me laugh with an expression I couldn't read—recognition, maybe. Two lonely people in a lamplit shed, each holding a piece of a dead country.

"Nineteen days," I said.

"Perhaps eighteen. Perhaps seventeen, if the pressure system over the plateau does what I think it's going to do." He rose, unfolding joint by joint, and laid his palm flat on the chest one more time. "The syndicate's claim is filed. Their gunboats will start moving upriver within the week. Every hour we sit here, their advantage grows."

"And if we run, we run without permits of our own, on a borrowed engine, with a hull I haven't trusted since the ice season." I looked at the chalkboard, at the debt, at the chest gleaming dully under the lamps. Everything sensible in me was screaming the sensible sum: sell it, pay them, live. But underneath the sensible sum, another number had started ticking—the kind of number a storm chaser knows in her bones, the countdown before the cell goes tornadic, when every instinct says *run toward it*.

"I need to talk to my crew," I said.

"He's been listening through the floor for an hour," Ilya said mildly.

"I know," I said. "I raised the stakes on purpose."

Overhead, Dov's boots went very still.

I crossed to the ladder and put my hand on it, and paused, because there was one more question scratching at me, the one I'd been avoiding since the night we dredged the chest off the shoal. "Ilya. The crest. The nine-rayed sun. Why do I—" I stopped, hating how the sentence sounded, and tried again. "When I look at it, it feels like remembering something. Like a word on the tip of my tongue. That's not normal, is it?"

Ilya went quiet in a way that had weight to it. He studied me across the lamplight, and I watched him decide something.

"Where did your family come from, Ms. Dunmore?" he asked carefully. "Before the coast?"

"Nobody asks that," I said. "We've just always been river people."

"In my experience," Ilya said softly, "nobody is just anything. And rivers," he added, glancing toward the window, toward the dark water sliding past with the chest's twin secrets somewhere in its memory, "have longer memories than archivists do."

Above us, the roof groaned under the thickening rain, and far upriver, past the black ridges where the drowned summit waited, the monsoon began clearing its throat.
