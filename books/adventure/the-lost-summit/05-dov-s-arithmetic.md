## 5. Dov's Arithmetic

Dov Hark had learned arithmetic from his mother, who kept a fish stall on the Salt Quay and could count a crate of herring by weight alone, her thumb flicking through the scales like she was dealing cards. She taught him that every number was a promise: this many coins, this many mouths, this many days until the rent came due. Numbers didn't lie. People lied about numbers.

He was thinking about her hands as he sat in the stern of the *Magpie's Rest* with a tin cup of cold tea going bitter against his tongue, watching Beatrix Dunmore sleep badly in the wheelhouse. The lamp behind the glass threw her shadow long across the deck boards, and even her shadow looked tired — shoulders slumped, head bowed, a woman folded up small around something heavy.

The chest sat between them in the dark, under an oilcloth Dov had thrown over it three nights ago and hadn't touched since. He could feel it anyway. That was the trouble with a thing like that chest. You stopped seeing it and started feeling it, the way you felt a tooth that had gone bad, or a debt that had gone old.

Forty years drowned in a river shoal, and it still smelled faintly of bronze and river mud and — he'd swear to this if anyone asked, though no one would — of ink. Old ink, the kind that had dried in ledgers and never quite left the air.

*Numbers don't lie,* his mother said in his head, in her quay-voice, sharp enough to scale a fish. *So count.*

Dov counted.

He started, as always, with what they owed. The Vane Syndicate's paper was simple the way a knife is simple. Eleven thousand crowns, principal. Interest compounding monthly at a rate Dov had checked twice because he hadn't believed it the first time. Thirty days, as of yesterday morning, when the two smiling men had stood on his deck and inventoried his winch, his sonar rig, his spare propeller, and — with a little bow, as though complimenting him — Beatrix's storm kite, folding each item into their ledger with fingers that never once stopped looking pleased.

Eleven thousand, compounding. In thirty days it would be eleven thousand six hundred and change. Dov did the change in his head while the river slapped the hull in its patient way, slap and drag, slap and drag, like the water was also counting.

Against that: what they had. The *Magpie's Rest*, mortgaged to the syndicate already, so not really theirs. Four hundred crowns in the strongbox under the bunk, which was technically Beatrix's but which Dov had put there himself over six lean seasons, so he counted it as half his. A hull sound for maybe another season if nobody hit anything. And one bronze chest stamped with the seal of a country that had been wiped off the maps before Dov was old enough to read them.

That was where the arithmetic got ugly. Because a thing was worth what someone would pay for it, and the trouble with the chest was that somebody — somebodies — were already paying. Word moved downriver the way silt did, silently and everywhere. The antiquities broker in Corren's Landing had sent a boy with a note offering eight hundred crowns, sight unseen, which meant the broker knew something and expected to make ten times that. There had been a man at the fuel dock two mornings ago, too well-dressed for the fuel dock, asking questions about salvage in the middle shoals without ever using the word *chest*. And last night, while Dov walked the mooring lines, a launch had come downriver running dark, no lights, engine muffled, moving fast toward the delta and the syndicate piers beyond it.

Dov had stood in the dark and watched it go and felt the first cold drop of the thing he would spend all of today trying not to think about.

Because that morning, before dawn, before the gulls, a man had found him on the quay.

* * *

He'd been buying ice. That was all. Crates of it, chipped off the block cart, for the fish hold they no longer used for fish but which kept the beer cold and made the boat look legitimate. The man had been waiting by the ice cart with his hands clasped behind his back like a schoolmaster on holiday, and he'd smiled — and Dov had thought, with a lurch, *oh no, another smiler* — except this smile was different from the syndicate men's. Theirs had been the smile of people enjoying their work. This one was the smile of a man who had done the work and found it dull and moved on to the interesting part.

"Dov Hark," the man had said. Not a question. "You crew for Beatrix Dunmore."

"Six seasons," Dov had said, because it was true and because true things were safe things.

"Must be loyal work." The man had turned a coin across his knuckles, silver, worn smooth. "Or must be cheap work. Hard to tell, on these boats."

Dov had said nothing. His mother's voice again: *when a man offers you silence, price it before you take it.* She'd never actually said that. But she should have. It belonged in the same catechism.

"The Syndicate," the man had said, "is prepared to be generous with its friends." And then he'd named a number.

Dov had heard numbers all his life. Prices at auction, wages at the cannery, the cost of his father's cough medicine by the month and by the year until the year the medicine stopped working. He had a sailor's ear for figures the way other men had an ear for music. So he heard this number properly. He let it sit in him and ring.

It was more than the debt. More than the debt plus interest plus the boat plus four seasons of wages. It was, Dov realized with a sensation like stepping into cold water, roughly the exact sum of everything Dov Hark had ever wanted and never said out loud: his mother's stall bought freehold instead of rented, her knees saved from the wet stone; a berth of his own someday, a small honest boat with a small honest engine; and underneath it all, unspoken, unpriceable — the end of being the man who stood next to someone else's dream and held the rope.

"For your silence," the man had said, "about a certain item of salvage. That's all we ask. Silence. You needn't touch it, move it, open it, or lie about it. Just be somewhere else with your hearing when the time comes." The coin had stopped turning. He'd set it on the ice crate between them, and the cold had crept up through the silver into Dov's finger where it rested beside it. "There's no deadline. But there is a tide. Things wash downstream, Mr. Hark. Chests. Opportunities. Loyalties." Another smile, brief as a knife-flash. "We'll find you."

And he had walked away into the fish-smell morning, and Dov had stood there with the ice melting around the coin, and after a long time he had picked the coin up, because leaving it would have been an answer, and he wasn't ready to answer anything.

He still had it. It sat in his pocket now, against his thigh, cold as a river stone, and he could feel it the way you feel a splinter — not painful exactly, just present, just insisting on itself.

* * *

Here was the thing Dov could not stop doing, the thing that was eating him alive by inches: he kept running both sums.

Sum the first. Beatrix's sum. In it, the chest went back upriver to whatever drowned summit Ilya Senn talked about with his hungry archivist eyes — the vault, the permits, the expiry clause, all that beautiful doomed paperwork — and the chest turned out to hold whatever dead Karvath had left behind, and Beatrix got to hand it to ghosts and stand straighter than she'd stood in six years, and the syndicate got nothing, and Dov got — what? He tried to be honest, sitting alone in the dark with his tea. He got the pleasure of it. He got to watch her win. He got the particular warmth of having been the man who stayed. And warmth, his mother would have said, scaling her herring, doesn't buy freehold.

Worse: he ran the odds on Beatrix's sum and the odds were bad. Gunboats on the gorge. Monsoon coming down the valley like a debt collector. A syndicate that had already filed claim papers with lawyers whose names were verbs in this part of the world. If they ran Beatrix's sum and lost, they lost everything — boat, gear, the four hundred crowns, and probably Dov's freedom besides, because enforcers collected on cosigners too, and Dov's name was on the *Rest*'s papers right under hers. He'd signed it in a good season, laughing, because she'd asked him to and because the boat had felt like theirs. Six seasons later his signature was a leash.

Sum the second. The syndicate's sum. Take the coin. Say nothing. Let the current do what currents do. The debt gets paid — not by him, but it gets paid, or it gets forgiven, or it stops being his problem, which amounted to the same thing in the ledger of a practical man. His mother's knees get dry stone under them. He buys the small honest boat. And Beatrix —

Beatrix loses the chest. Loses the dream. Stands on a dock watching the syndicate's crane swing her country's inheritance aboard a barge, and she will look at Dov, afterward, and she won't even know to look for guilt, because he'll have said nothing, done nothing, merely *not helped*, and there is no law against not helping. That was the elegance of the offer. They weren't asking him to betray her. They were asking him to be furniture.

He drained the cold tea and wished it were something stronger. The oilcloth shifted in a breath of wind and lay still.

The worst part — the part he kept circling like a bird over a drowned thing — was that he understood the smilers. That was the corrosion, right there. Six years ago he'd have hated them cleanly. Now he sat in the dark doing their math for them, checking their figures, finding them, God help him, *sound*. Eleven thousand crowns against a nation forty years dead. A living woman's ruin against a dead country's paperwork. Any auction house on the coast would call it a fair trade. Any bank. Any sensible man with a coin cooling in his pocket.

Any sensible man. And Dov had always prided himself on being sensible. Sensible Dov, who checked the bilge twice. Sensible Dov, who kept the flare locker stocked and the log current and never, in six seasons, let Beatrix Dunmore chase a storm cell he hadn't first measured for escape routes. Sensibility was his job on this boat. Somebody had to be the ballast.

But ballast, he thought, sits below the waterline and keeps the ship upright and never sees the sky, and maybe that was fine for iron, and maybe it was fine for him, except that last week — he searched himself for it honestly, the way you search a wound — last week, when Ilya Senn had laid his thin hand flat on the bronze lid and said *this is a key* in a voice like a man describing his own funeral, Dov had felt something move in his chest that had nothing to do with money. Wonder, maybe. Or grief. Or the memory of his mother telling him, once, only once, drunk on festival wine, that her grandmother had come from upriver, from a place that wasn't on any map anymore, and that the family name had been different before the maps changed.

Hark. What had it been before? She'd never say. She'd laughed and called for more wine. Dead country, dead name, dead grandmother — what was that against eleven thousand crowns?

What was that against a coin?

Dov took the coin out and held it up to the wheelhouse lamp's spill. It was old silver, worn nearly blank, but there was a ghost of a stamp on one face — a crest, maybe, or a seal, eaten by thumbs and years. He tilted it. Light slid across the metal and for a moment he thought he saw a shape like a mountain under three lines of water.

He closed his fist around it. Cold. Patient. Exactly like the river.

*Count,* his mother said. So he counted the ways it could go wrong, and ran out of fingers, and started on his toes, and gave up somewhere past twenty. Then he counted the ways it could go right, and got to one, and the one was Beatrix's face when she talked about giving the dead their names back, and he hated how much that one weighed. It outweighed the coin. It outweighed, God help him, the freehold.

He wasn't going to take the money. He knew that already, had known it since the quay, since the ice, since the man's smile. But knowing wasn't deciding, and deciding wasn't saying, and in the space between knowing and saying a man could lose himself entirely. He pocketed the coin. He'd keep it. Not as payment — as evidence. Proof, later, when it mattered, that they had come to him and he had carried their offer around in his pocket like a stone and never spent it. He owed himself that much honesty, at least. A man should be able to show his own ledger clean, even if no one else ever read it.

He'd tell her. Soon. When the river was calm, when Ilya wasn't listening, when the words could be said without the wind taking them wrong. *They offered me this. I didn't take it. Here's the coin; throw it overboard yourself, if you want. I couldn't.* That was the speech. He rehearsed it against the dark, and it sounded brave, and he didn't believe it yet, and he told himself that believing could wait until morning.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of rain that hadn't arrived yet. Dov was on deck coiling lines that didn't need coiling when Beatrix emerged from the wheelhouse with her hair tied back and her jaw set in the particular way it set when she'd been awake all night making plans.

"We're taking on provisions," she said. "Full stores. Ilya thinks we can be at the gorge inside a week if the weather holds."

"The weather won't hold," Dov said. "It's monsoon season."

"Then we beat the monsoon." She looked at him, and there it was — the dream, lit up behind her eyes like a harbor lamp, and beneath it, thinner, the fear she thought she hid so well. "Dov. Are you with me?"

And here was the moment. Here was the clean water, the calm river, the opening he'd rehearsed all night. All he had to do was reach into his pocket and put the cold coin in her palm and say the words.

Instead Dov Hark heard himself say: "I'm with you. Someone sensible has to be."

She grinned — quick, grateful, already turning away — and went forward to wake Ilya, and the moment went downriver without him, and the coin sat in his pocket like a second heartbeat.

It was midmorning, sun burning off the gray, when Dov looked up from the fuel line and saw the launch.

It came around the bend at an easy cruising pace, white hull, brass rail, flying no colors at all, which on this river was its own kind of flag. Two figures at the rail. One of them lifted a hand in a small, courteous wave.

The other was a woman, and even at that distance Dov could see she was smiling.

"Beatrix," he said, quietly, to no one, to the coin, to the river. "Company."
