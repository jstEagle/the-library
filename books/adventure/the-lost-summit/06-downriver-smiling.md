## 6. Downriver, Smiling

The tea was Dov's idea, which was how Beatrix knew it would go badly. Dov's ideas were always practical in the way that a tourniquet is practical—necessary, ugly, and applied too late.

"They're coming up the channel," he'd said that morning, standing at the rail with his binoculars held steady, his voice pitched low and even, the way he spoke when the weather turned. "Black hull. Twin screws. Flying no colors at all."

No colors meant they didn't need to announce themselves. Everyone on the river knew what a Vane Syndicate boat looked like: lacquered black like a beetle's back, brass fittings polished to a shine you could read by, and a crew of men who moved with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no.

Beatrix had watched it come for twenty minutes before it arrived. That was the thing about the syndicate—they never hurried. Hurrying implied doubt. The black boat came up the channel at exactly the speed of inevitability, its wake spreading out behind it in long, polite ripples that rocked the *Magpie* against her moorings.

Now she stood in her own galley, arranging cups.

It was absurd. She knew it was absurd. She was thirty-one years old, she had outrun a derecho over the Saltmarsh flats with a hailstone the size of a fist coming through her windshield, she had once talked her way past a customs officer who had every legal reason to impound her boat, and here she was, straightening saucers for a woman whose organization owned the roof over her head, the engine under her feet, and—if the arithmetic went wrong—the next thirty years of her life.

"Which cups?" Dov asked from the doorway. He was holding two chipped enamel mugs and one porcelain cup with a hairline crack running through it like a river on a map.

"The porcelain," Beatrix said. "If we're going to be executed, we'll be executed politely."

"That's not funny."

"I know." She took the mugs anyway. "That's why I said it."

Dov lingered. He had been lingering for three days now, ever since the chest came up out of the shoal, and Beatrix had noticed—she noticed everything about Dov, had spent eleven years learning to read him the way sailors read water—but noticing and understanding were different instruments, and lately the gap between them felt wider than the channel.

"Dov." She didn't look up from the cups. "Whatever it is, it can wait until after tea."

"It might not be able to."

"Then it will have to learn."

He set the mugs down and left without another word, and Beatrix stood alone in the galley with the porcelain cup and the hairline crack and the sound of the black boat's engines dying against her hull.

* * *

Merrit Vane came aboard smiling.

Of course she did. They all did. But Merrit's smile was not the smile of the two enforcers who had inventoried Beatrix's storm anchors three weeks ago—that had been the smile of clerks, of men reading from a list. Merrit's smile was bespoke. It fit her face the way a well-cut coat fits a shoulder, and it reached all the way to her eyes, which were pale gray and entirely without warmth, like the sky before a dry storm.

She was smaller than Beatrix expected. That was the first ambush. She wore a dove-gray traveling suit with not a single wrinkle despite the river humidity, and her hair was pinned up in a style that belonged in a capital-city salon, and when she stepped onto the *Magpie*'s deck she did it lightly, almost daintily, one hand on the rail, as though she were boarding a friend's yacht for a Sunday outing rather than descending on a debtor's boat like weather.

"Miss Dunmore." Her voice was warm honey poured over cold iron. "Thank you for receiving me. I know my office called ahead only this morning, and I do apologize for the short notice. The river is so lovely this time of year, I couldn't bear to wait."

"We don't have tea worthy of you," Beatrix said.

"Nonsense." Merrit settled into the deck chair Dov had dragged out, crossing her ankles with the ease of a woman who intended to stay exactly as long as she pleased. "Tea is a ritual, not a quality. It's the company that matters." Her gray eyes moved across the deck—the coiled lines, the patched canvas, the storm rigging Beatrix kept immaculate because it was the only thing she owned that she was proud of—and then, briefly, toward the boathouse door. Toward the chest.

It was the briefest glance. A flicker. But Beatrix saw it land and settle, the way a dart lands in a board.

"Your crewman made a fine pot," Merrit said. "Dov, isn't it? Such a steady man. Loyal men are worth more than gold on the river. Don't you find?"

"He has his moments."

Behind her, Beatrix heard Dov pour the tea, and heard—she was almost certain—a small tremor in the sound of the pouring. She filed it away with everything else she'd been filing away for three days, in the drawer that was getting dangerously full.

Merrit accepted her cup with both hands, cradling it like something rescued from a fire. She sipped. She sighed with genuine pleasure.

"Oh, that's good. You have no idea what the office coffee is like. Swill. I tell them, we are in the business of comfort—we repossess homes, Miss Dunmore, we take boats and shops and sometimes whole futures—and the least we can do is make excellent coffee while we do it." She laughed, a light musical sound, and Beatrix felt the horror of it settle into her bones: the woman meant it. Every word. There was no mask slipping because there was no mask. This was simply who Merrit Vane was, all the way down—a person who had organized her entire soul around the principle that cruelty performed with impeccable manners was not cruelty at all.

"You said you wanted to talk about my account," Beatrix said.

"Accounts, yes." Merrit set down her cup and produced, from somewhere inside that immaculate suit, a leather folio. She opened it on her knee with the reverence of a priest handling scripture. "Let's see. Principal, interest, penalties for the late survey fees, the mooring arrears... You know, when I first read your file, I thought, this poor woman, she'll never climb out. And then—" she looked up, and the smile deepened by one precise degree, "—then you went and dredged up a bronze chest off the Kettle Shoal, and I thought, well. The river provides."

The galley seemed to tilt, though the *Magpie* was moored dead still.

"I don't know what you mean," Beatrix said.

"No, of course not." Merrit nodded sympathetically, as though Beatrix were a child insisting there was no monster under the bed. "A chest comes up out of the river bearing the seal of a country that hasn't existed in forty years, and it washes up, by pure chance, into the boathouse of a woman who owes my syndicate a very great deal of money. The odds against it are astronomical. I've had our actuaries look at it. They got quite excited." She sipped again. "I love my actuaries. They find meaning in everything."

Beatrix's heart was slamming against her ribs hard enough that she could feel it in her throat, in her teeth. Somewhere below, in the hold, the chest sat under a tarpaulin and a false floor and forty years of other people's greed, waiting.

"You've been misinformed," Beatrix said. "We dredge salvage. We dredge a lot of things. Bottles. Chain. Last month we pulled up a piano."

"A piano!" Merrit's delight seemed genuine. "Was it playable?"

"Half of it."

"And the chest? Is it playable?" The gray eyes came up, and for just a moment the warmth drained out of them completely, and Beatrix saw the thing underneath—not cruelty exactly, but appetite, vast and patient and utterly certain of itself. "Because here is my situation, Miss Dunmore. My syndicate holds your debt. Your debt is worth, let's be honest, nothing. You have a boat, some very nice storm rigging, and a talent for surviving weather that frankly impresses me. If you died tomorrow, we'd recover perhaps a tenth of what you owe, after costs. You are, financially speaking, a bad investment." She smiled again, warmly, as if sharing a confidence between friends. "But a chest bearing the Karvathi seal—that is an investment. That is a rare and beautiful asset. And I am authorized to make you an extraordinary offer."

The word *authorized* landed like a hook through the cheek. Authorized by whom? Beatrix wondered. How far up does this go?

"I'm listening," Beatrix said, and was proud of how steady it came out.

"The entire debt. Forgiven. Today. I have the release papers right here." Merrit patted the folio. "Signed, sealed, witnessed. All of it gone—every lien, every penalty, the mooring arrears, the works. You walk away free, Miss Dunmore. Free boat, free name, free future. All it costs you is the chest."

Silence. The river lapped at the hull. Somewhere downstream a bell buoy clanged its slow idiot rhythm.

"Just the chest," Beatrix said carefully. "Not what's in it."

"What's in it is worthless to you without the key," Merrit said pleasantly, "and the key is worthless without the chest. And the chest"—she spread her hands, apologetic, reasonable, a woman explaining gravity to a child—"is ours. That's the beauty of collateral law, Miss Dunmore. Anything of value you acquire while insolvent technically belongs to your creditors. I could invoke that clause today. I could have my men aboard within the hour with a writ and a winch, and you'd watch them carry it off and still owe me everything." She tilted her head. "But I don't want to do that. I want us to be friends. I want this to be a pleasant memory you look back on. So instead of the writ and the winch, I'm offering you freedom. One chest for one life. It's the best trade anyone will ever offer you. I promise you that."

And the terrible thing—the thing Beatrix would lie awake turning over for nights afterward—was that it *was* a good trade. It was, by any sane arithmetic, an extraordinary offer. Freedom from the debt that had been sitting on her chest like a stone for six years, in exchange for a box of dead country's junk she'd pulled out of the mud nine days ago.

Except.

Except for the way Merrit's eyes had flicked to the boathouse. Except for the fact that the syndicate had sent its *principal*, not its clerks, which meant the chest was worth many times more than the debt, which meant the debt was never the point. Except for the stranger with the gaunt face and the burning eyes who had looked at the seal on that bronze lid and spoken the word *vault* like a prayer, and the things he'd told her about concession permits and expiry clauses and a nation of scattered survivors waiting forty years for their inheritance to come home.

Except for the fact that Merrit Vane was smiling, and Beatrix had learned in the last three weeks that the smiles were the truest warning on the river.

"That's very generous," Beatrix said slowly.

"Isn't it?"

"But I think you've been misinformed." She met the gray eyes and held them. "There is no chest."

* * *

The silence that followed lasted four seconds. Beatrix counted them, because counting kept her from screaming.

Merrit's smile did not falter. That was the horrifying part—it didn't even flicker. It simply stayed where it was, fixed and pleasant, while behind it something recalculated.

"No chest," Merrit repeated.

"No chest."

"Our information says otherwise."

"Then your information is wrong." Beatrix picked up the teapot and refilled Merrit's cup with a hand that did not shake, and the steadiness of that hand surprised her, because inside she was a house fire. "We dredged the shoal after the surge, same as always. Chain, bottles, half a piano. No chest. You can ask my crewman. Dov, was there a chest?"

Dov stood at the rail with the teapot. He was looking at the water. When he answered, his voice came out level, but Beatrix heard the half-second delay before it, the tiny gap where a decision was being made.

"No chest," Dov said.

And Beatrix understood, in that instant, three things at once. She understood that Dov had just lied to the Vane Syndicate's principal enforcer, which was either loyalty or guilt or both. She understood that the half-second delay meant he had considered the alternative. And she understood that whatever conversation Dov had been trying to have with her for three days—the one she'd postponed until after tea—had just become the most important conversation of her life.

But that was later. Right now, Merrit Vane was watching her with those pleasant gray eyes, and Beatrix Dunmore was lying for the first time in her life, and discovering, with a kind of vertigo, that she was *good* at it.

Because here was the secret she was finding out in real time: lying wasn't about the words. Anyone could say *no chest*. Lying was about the architecture around the words. It was about the half a piano. It was about letting irritation show, but only a little, the way an honest person would. It was about the small contemptuous glance at the folio, the sigh of a working woman whose time was being wasted by rich people's rumors.

"River rumors," Beatrix said, settling into it now, appalled and exhilarated at once, like a person discovering a talent for swimming in deep water. "That's what this is, isn't it? Some boy saw us dredging and made up a story about treasure, and it grew legs. You came all the way upriver on a rumor." She shook her head, and let a little real bitterness bleed into it—God help her, the bitterness was easy to find. "You know what I have in that boathouse? A broken winch. Two crates of chain I can't sell. A piano with half its strings. If I had a chest worth forgiving sixty thousand crowns, do you honestly think I'd be eating beans and patching the same sail for three years?"

Something shifted behind Merrit's eyes. Not belief—Beatrix doubted this woman believed anything she hadn't verified twice—but consideration. Recalculation. The dart, perhaps, wobbling in the board.

"There's a simple way to resolve this," Merrit said. "Let me look at your boathouse."

"Absolutely not."

The refusal came out fast and hot, and Beatrix realized halfway through it that it was perfect—that a guilty person would have hesitated, would have said *well, if you must*, and an innocent one would flare. She let the anger stand there in the open air between them, arms crossed, chin up, a debtor's righteous pride.

"My boat is the last thing I own that the syndicate doesn't already have paper on," she said. "I'm not having syndicate eyes walking through it cataloguing my rigging for the next round of penalties. You want to search my property, get a writ. Get the winch-men. Do it legally, in daylight, with witnesses. But you won't, will you? Because if you board me with a writ and find nothing, your office looks like fools, and I imagine—" she let the pause hang, delicate as wire, "—that Merrit Vane does not look like a fool."

The smile again. Wider now. And Beatrix saw, with a swimmer's clarity, that she had guessed right: the offer had been a probe, and the probe had failed, and Merrit could not afford the spectacle of a fruitless search any more than Beatrix could afford a successful one. They were balanced on the same knife-edge, the enforcer and the debtor, each pretending to hold more cards than she held.

"You're a much more interesting woman than your file suggests," Merrit said. She rose, smoothing her suit, and closed the folio without offering the papers. "I'll tell you what I'll do, since I admire spirit. The offer stands for seven days. Seven days, Miss Dunmore. If the chest happens to... turn up... in your possession during that time, my door is open, and so is my pen." She clicked the folio shut. "And if it turns up anywhere else—if it surfaces in a market, or an auction, or another party's hands—well. Then we'll know your crewman talks, won't we? And we'll know what kind of friends you keep."

Her gaze slid, briefly and deliberately, to Dov.

Then she was moving to the rail, light on her feet, pausing at the gangway with one hand on the post, and delivering the parting shot with the tenderness of a grandmother:

"Do give my regards to whoever's been asking questions about Karvath in the river towns. Gaunt fellow, I hear. Reads seals." The gray eyes found Beatrix's and held them, warm as a hearth, cold as a ledger. "Friends like that are expensive, Miss Dunmore. I do hope he's worth it."

* * *

They watched the black boat recede downriver for a long time. Its wake came in last, rocking the *Magpie* gently, like the tail-end of a sentence.

"She knows about Ilya," Dov said finally.

"She knows more than that." Beatrix set down her cup. Her hand was shaking now—now, when it was safe, now that the audience was gone, the tremor arriving punctually like a bill collector. "She knows about the shoal. She knows the chest exists. The offer was theater. She wanted to see my face when she said the word *chest*, and she wanted to see yours when she said *loyal*."

Dov said nothing. He was gripping the rail, and Beatrix watched his knuckles whiten, and the full drawer inside her—three days of half-second delays, of radio static at odd hours, of a man who lingered in doorways unable to speak—creaked open all at once.

"Dov." She kept her voice very quiet. "What were you going to tell me? Before tea."

He turned. And Beatrix saw it in his face before he said a word—the terrible arithmetic of a good man, the sum finally refusing to stay hidden—and she braced herself the way she braced in a storm, knowing the wave was coming and knowing she could not dodge it.

"Bea," he said. "They contacted me. Three days ago."
