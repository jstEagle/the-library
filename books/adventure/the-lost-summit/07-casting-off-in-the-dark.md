## 7. Casting Off in the Dark

The moon did not rise that night, and Ilya Senn understood it as a courtesy.

He had spent eleven years learning to read the sky the way other men read ledgers—cloud cover as collateral, wind as interest accruing—and a moonless night over the Serevan Delta was worth more than any currency he'd ever catalogued. It was darkness with a purpose. Darkness that had been arranged.

"You're smiling," Beatrix said. She stood at the bow of the *Kestrel's Debt*, coiling rope with the economy of someone who had never once owned more than she could carry. "People who smile at night on this river are either drunk or about to do something stupid."

"Both, possibly."

"Which one?"

"I've been sober for six years," Ilya said, "so by elimination."

She laughed—a short, surprised sound, as if laughter were a tool she kept in the bottom of a drawer and rarely remembered to use—and went back to her coils. Behind them, the boathouse loomed black against the blacker water, its doors already shut, its lamp already doused. Inside it, under a false floor Dov had built in four frantic hours, lay a bronze chest stamped with the seal of a country that no longer existed, and Ilya's whole life folded up small enough to fit inside it.

He touched the rail. The wood was damp and cold and real, and he needed it to be real, because everything else tonight felt like a page he'd invented and would be called upon to defend before some tribunal of sober men.

*You are doing this,* he told himself. *You are actually doing this.*

---

They cast off at the turn of the tide, which Dov calculated without instruments, by taste—leaning over the side, touching two fingers to the current, nodding once. The borrowed engine coughed twice, caught, and settled into a rhythm like an old man climbing stairs: reluctant, steady, complaining in a language only Dov spoke.

"No permit," Dov said, not looking at either of them. He said it the way a man reads a weather warning aloud—not to anyone in particular, just so the sky knows he noticed. "Upstream past the Third Bend requires a concession stamp. We don't have one."

"We have something better," Beatrix said.

"We have nothing. That's what we have. That's what we have in writing."

"Dov."

"I'm just saying it out loud so we all agree on what we have." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime in the previous decade. "Nothing. A leaking hull, an engine borrowed from a man who will want it back or want blood, and a passenger nobody invited."

Ilya raised his hand slightly, like a student. "That would be me."

"I know who you are."

"Then you know I'm useful."

Dov finally looked at him, and Ilya held the look. There was a strange arithmetic happening behind Dov's eyes—Ilya had seen it before, in the archive, in the faces of men weighing whether knowledge was worth its price—and whatever sum Dov arrived at, he kept it to himself. He turned back to the engine.

"Sit away from the gunwale," he muttered. "You leak too, probably."

---

The delta opened around them like a held breath let slowly out. Mangrove shadows slid past on both banks, and somewhere in the reeds a bird made a sound like a hinge that wanted oiling, over and over, patient as bureaucracy. The air tasted of silt and rain-that-hadn't-fallen-yet—the whole atmosphere holding its water like a creditor holds a debt, aware of its power, in no hurry to spend it.

Ilya sat amidships with his knees drawn up and watched the shoreline for lights. There were none. That was the gift of the monsoon season: the legitimate traffic had all gone to ground, hauled out, tied down, insured. Only fools and fugitives moved on the water now, and the patrols knew it, which meant the patrols were watching the channels where fools went. Which meant they would go where fools wouldn't.

"The eastern cut," he said quietly.

Beatrix turned from the bow. "The eastern cut is a rumor."

"The eastern cut is a rumor with a channel at low water and a sandbar at high, and right now—" he tilted his hand, feeling the river's mood through the hull, an archivist's trick, reading conditions the way he read marginalia, "—right now it is neither. Right now it is passable, unpatrolled, and unloved. Nobody charts it because there's no money in it."

"There's no *bottom* in it, half the year."

"There's bottom. It's just shy."

Beatrix studied him for a long moment. In the dark her face was mostly suggestion—jaw, brow, the pale slash of a scar along one cheekbone he hadn't yet asked about—but her eyes caught what little light the sky allowed, and they were doing that thing they did when she was deciding whether to trust him, which was to say they were deciding whether trusting him would be interesting.

"You've run this river," she said.

"I've *read* this river. There's a difference, and you're going to find it out around the Second Bend, and I apologize in advance."

Another laugh, shorter this time, bitten off. She turned forward again and called a heading to Dov, and the *Kestrel's Debt* leaned into the dark toward the unloved channel, and Ilya felt something open in his chest that he had no catalogue entry for.

It took him a while to name it. Later, much later, he would call it *velocity*—the specific joy of a man whose life has been stationary for eleven years suddenly acquiring direction.

---

The guilt came at the Second Bend, as promised, though not because of the channel.

It came because of the smell. Old paper. His own hands still carried it—acids and dust and the faint vanilla sweetness of degrading lignin—and he had spent the last three days telling himself he didn't miss the archive, that the archive had never deserved him, that an institution which burned a man's career for asking a question had forfeited its claim on his grief. All true. All beside the point.

He missed it the way you miss a house that expelled you. The way your body misses a room.

"You're doing the thing again," Beatrix said. She had come aft without him hearing her, which should have frightened him more than it did. She lowered herself onto the bench across from him, close enough that their knees nearly touched, and waited.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you go somewhere else. Your eyes go flat and far off, like you're reading something written very small, very long ago." She shrugged. "Dov does it with numbers. You do it with whatever's inside your head. I've been watching both of you for days. It's how I know when people are about to lie to me."

"And am I about to lie to you?"

"Not yet." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "But you owe me something, Senn. Passage isn't free. You said you'd pay in Karvath. So far I've gotten seals and sandbars. I haven't gotten *you*."

The engine droned. The river breathed. Somewhere behind them, downriver, the city of Vanehold glittered with all its lamps lit, every one of them paid for by somebody's debt, and Ilya understood that this was the moment—the quiet moment, the moonless moment—that would decide what kind of man he was going to be on this boat. A useful stranger. Or a crewmate.

He looked at his hands. Paper-stained. Archivist's hands. Hands that had touched the last census roll of Karvath and been forbidden, formally, in writing, from ever touching it again.

"I was expelled," he said.

Beatrix said nothing. She was good at that; he'd noticed. Most people filled silence like it was a hole in a hull. She let silence sit there and do its work.

"Eleven years ago. I was a junior conservator—restoration, binding repair, the work nobody wants and everybody needs. And in the restricted stacks, in a crate marked for incineration—this is true, they had literally scheduled it—I found a folio. Shipping manifests. Karvathi port records, forty years old, pre-annexation. Someone at the Ministry had decided the nation's entire commercial memory was clutter." His voice came out flatter than he intended. He tried to put some spine back into it. "I filed a request to preserve it. Formally. With citations. I was twenty-six and I thought institutions worked the way they said they worked in the charter."

"And they didn't."

"They worked the way they worked. My request went up. It came back down attached to a disciplinary review. Then another. Then a question I still can't answer: *who benefits from the destruction?* Because it wasn't negligence, Beatrix. Negligence is sloppy. This was tidy. The crate had been routed, signed, countersigned. Somebody wanted Karvath not just erased but *unremembered*, and my little preservation request was the first time in forty years anyone had made noise about it." He flexed his fingers. "So they made me quiet. Revoked my credentials. Blacklisted me with every archive in three provinces. Eleven years of hauling crates for shipping brokers, cataloguing cargo I wasn't allowed to care about."

"And the folio?"

"Burned. As far as I know." He met her eyes. "But I read it first. Every page. And that's why I'm here, and that's the part I haven't said out loud until now: I'm not running *toward* your vault, Beatrix. I'm running *away from* the shelf where that folio used to sit. Every mile upstream is a mile further from the smell of smoke. I need you to know that. Whatever noble thing you think I am—I'm a man fleeing a fire that happened eleven years ago, and I may be too late, and I may deserve to be."

The river said what rivers say, which is nothing, endlessly.

Then Beatrix reached over and took his wrist—not gently, exactly, but firmly, the way you take hold of someone leaning too far over a rail—and turned his hand over and looked at the paper stains on his fingertips as if they were a chart.

"My grandmother," she said, "had hands like that."

Ilya stopped breathing.

"She died when I was nine. Ink on her fingers always. Smelled like old books. I used to think she was just messy." Beatrix released his wrist and stood, and her voice went carefully casual, which from her sounded like a man whistling past a graveyard. "Get some sleep, Senn. Second Bend's behind us. Third Bend is where it gets honest."

She went forward. Ilya sat with his heart slamming against his ribs and the word *grandmother* ringing in him like a struck bell, and he did not sleep, and he did not care.

---

The rain found them an hour before dawn.

Not the monsoon—not yet, not the full weight of it—but a scout, a herald, a fine cold needle-rain that hissed on the water and beaded on Ilya's eyelashes and turned the world into charcoal rubbed with a thumb. Dov rigged the tarp without being asked. Beatrix took the tiller. Ilya bailed, because bailing required nothing of him but rhythm, and rhythm was the only prayer he knew.

It was during the bailing that he found the leak.

Not dramatically. His bare foot simply came down on a spot in the bilge where the water rose faster than it should, cold and insistent, welling up between two planks like a secret being told through cupped hands. He crouched. Pressed the seam. Felt it give, minutely, the way old things give.

"Dov," he called. "We're taking water. Portside, midships, below the waterline by the feel of it."

Dov appeared, knelt, pressed the same seam, and made a sound that in a less disciplined man would have been a curse. "She's been weeping since Vanehold. It's the strain of the borrowed engine—she wasn't built for upstream work." He was already moving, pulling tar, a bilge pump, a length of canvas. "Bail. Don't stop bailing. If she ships more than a hand's depth we turn back, and if we turn back we're dead anyway, so bail like turning back isn't an option."

"Cheerful," Beatrix said from the tiller.

"I contain multitudes."

And so they bailed, the three of them, in the grey hour before dawn, passing buckets and the pump handle in a rotation that needed no discussion, and Ilya discovered something he would remember for the rest of his life: that shared labor is a faster intimacy than shared confession. An hour ago he had told Beatrix Dunmore the worst truth of his life and she had answered with a single sentence about her grandmother, and he had thought the distance between them was oceanic. But now her shoulder knocked his in the dark as they traded the pump, and Dov grunted approval at his technique, and the boat groaned beneath them and held, and the distance closed to arm's length, then to nothing.

This, he thought, bucketing river water back into the river. *This is what the archive never gave me.* Purpose, yes. Order, yes. But never this—three strangers becoming a vessel, in both senses, in the rain, with no permit and no future and a chest full of a dead nation sleeping under their feet.

The exhilaration scared him. It was the same exhilaration he'd felt at twenty-six, filing the request that ended his life, and he recognized it now for what it was: the joy of a man who has decided, finally, that some things are worth being ruined for.

He only wished the guilt would keep pace with the joy instead of trailing it. Because downriver, in Vanehold, there would be consequences assembling themselves with bureaucratic patience. A missing archivist. A boathouse watched. A syndicate that smiled. And somewhere in the stacks of his memory, the image he could not shake: the routing slip on that doomed crate, the countersignature at the bottom, neat and cheerful and familiar, belonging to a man who had signed away a nation's memory with the same pen he used for lunch invitations.

Merrit Vane had been a junior clerk then. Ilya had never forgotten the signature. He suspected, with an archivist's cold certainty, that she had never forgotten his request.

"Dov," he said, as the sky went the color of wet slate and the Third Bend opened ahead of them, dark water sliding dark. "How fast does news travel downriver?"

"Faster than boats," Dov said. "Slower than regret. Why?"

"Because I think," Ilya said slowly, watching the bend, watching the water, watching for lights that weren't there yet but would be, "we are about to be regretted."

Around the bend, far off but unmistakable, a lantern blinked twice. Paused. Blinked twice again.

Not a fisherman's signal. Fishermen didn't count.

Beatrix killed the engine, and the *Kestrel's Debt* drifted silent into the dark, and the rain came down harder, as if the sky had been waiting for permission.
