## 8. The Leaking Boat

The squall came down the gorge like something with a grudge.

Beatrix felt it before she saw it—a pressure change in her ears, a hush in the water that had been talking all afternoon, and then the sky simply closed. One moment they were running under a bruised and broken ceiling of cloud; the next, the rain arrived sideways, hard as gravel, and the river stood up in white-capped ridges that slapped the hull like a crowd of angry hands.

"Get the tarp over the cargo!" she shouted, and didn't know if anyone heard her, because the wind took her voice and shredded it.

Dov was already moving, hauling the oilskin over the crates of food and the wrapped bundle of Maren's claim papers, which rode in an iron box now, triple-sealed, because Beatrix had developed a superstition about water getting at paper. Ilya clung to the gunwale amidships, gray-faced, his long fingers white-knuckled on the wood. He'd confessed somewhere in the last hour of light that he couldn't swim. He'd said it the way a man admits to a fear of fire while standing in a burning house—apologetically, as if it were an inconvenience he'd caused everyone else.

The borrowed engine coughed. Coughed again. Then, with a sound like a dying animal's last complaint, it caught, and the boat clawed its way upriver against the current, nose into the waves.

They were three hours out of the diaspora village when the hull gave way.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the thing Beatrix would remember afterward—the sheer ordinariness of disaster. No crack of timber, no geyser of water. Just a soft, wrong sound beneath her feet, a shudder through the planking, and then Dov saying, very quietly, in the tone of a man reading his own death certificate: "We're taking water."

* * *

The leak was under the forward storage bench, a seam that had opened along a line of old rot—the borrowed boat's one dishonesty, hidden under fresh paint. The river came through it in a thin, insistent tongue, then a stream, then something with ambitions.

"Bail," Beatrix said. "Bail like your lives depend on it, because they do."

Ilya found the tin pail lashed to the thwart and began bailing with the grim, mechanical rhythm of a man who had spent his life doing work nobody thanked him for. Dov wedged himself into the bow space, shirtless despite the cold, feeling along the seam with his fingertips, then his palm, then his whole forearm, hunting the wound in the dark.

The rain hammered. The engine screamed. Somewhere behind them, lightning stitched the gorge walls together in brief silver photographs—cliffs, water, the small stubborn shape of their boat—and each photograph showed them how alone they were.

"I can't reach the back of it," Dov called up. "The seam runs under the frame. I need to get the bench out."

"Then get the bench out."

"It's bolted."

"Then unbolt it!"

"With what?"

Beatrix looked around the cockpit as if the answer might be floating in the bilge water, which by now sloshed around their ankles, cold and alive. Her toolkit was under the tarp. She fought her way aft, unzipped the bag one-handed, and threw Dov the wrench, the pry bar, and the roll of tarred canvas and wax they kept for exactly this kind of night, because every storm chaser knew that boats were just slow ways of sinking.

Dov worked. Ilya bailed. Beatrix drove.

That was the arithmetic of the next six hours, and she would think of it later as arithmetic—the division of labor that keeps a boat alive is simple, and merciless, and it does not care who you are or what you owe anyone. Bail. Drive. Patch. Repeat until dawn or until the river wins.

* * *

Around midnight, the squall eased from fury into malice. The rain kept falling but gentler, a steady drumming on the tarp, and the river dropped its whitecaps to a long, rolling chop. The engine settled into a ragged but honest rhythm. In the bilge, the water level held—Dov's patch, a sandwich of tarred canvas, waxed twine, and a wooden shim driven into the seam, was holding. Barely. It wept a little at one corner, and Ilya bailed that corner faithfully, every few minutes, like a priest tending a candle.

They were all soaked. They were all exhausted. And in the strange intimacy of shared survival, something loosened in the boat that hadn't been loose before.

"Eat," Beatrix said, and passed back hard bread and dried fish without looking, the way you feed people you've decided not to lose.

Ilya ate his portion in small, precise bites, hunched over his knees. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped custody.

"What?" Beatrix asked.

"I was thinking." He wiped rain off his face with a sleeve that was already more water than sleeve. "In the archive, when the roof leaked, we used to put out buckets and call it 'the weather's contribution to conservation.' There was a whole filing category. Weather damage, category four." He shook his head. "Forty years of Karvath's records, and half the marginalia is water stains. The river has always been writing on us."

"The river writes on everything," Dov said from the bow. His voice was odd—flat, careful. "It just doesn't sign its name."

Beatrix glanced at him. He was sitting with his back against the patched bench, arms around his knees, staring at nothing. He'd been quiet since the village—quieter than exhaustion explained. There was a particular quality to a man's silence when he's carrying something, and Beatrix had spent enough years reading weather to read men too, or she'd told herself so, right up until her own crew started lying to her face.

She filed it away. Not yet. Not in the dark, not on a leaking boat, not with two days of gorge ahead. But she filed it.

* * *

It was near three in the morning, in one of the squall's false retirements, that she heard the radio.

Not the words—there were no words. A click. A low murmur, pitched below the engine's note, the kind of sound you could spend years explaining away. Dov had the handheld unit cupped in both hands, turned away from her, angled toward the hull, and he was speaking into it in the smallest possible voice, and there was a green glow on his cheek where the screen lit him.

Beatrix's hands went still on the tiller.

The river ran. The rain fell. Ilya bailed, faithful as a clock. And Dov finished whatever he was saying, thumbed the set off, and tucked it back inside his jacket, and sat looking at the water with the expression of a man who has just done something irreversible and is already rehearsing how he'll describe it later.

He felt her looking. She saw it happen—the small stiffening in his shoulders, the half-second delay before he turned around. When he did turn, his face was open, tired, loyal. Forty years of friendship lived in that face. He'd pulled her out of a flood channel when they were nineteen. He'd held her hand at her mother's funeral. He'd eaten at her table more times than either of them could count.

"Dov," she said pleasantly. "Everything all right back there?"

"Just checking the battery," he said. "Storm drains them fast."

"Mm," she said. "Make sure it stays charged. We may need it."

Something passed between them then, quick and cold as the lightning. Neither of them named it. He nodded and turned back to the bow, and she turned back to the channel, and the boat went on upriver through the dark with all three of them pretending, with tremendous discipline, that nothing had happened.

But she filed that too. Filed it deep, in the drawer where she kept the things she wasn't ready to look at: the way he'd gone quiet after the village; the way he'd argued against the summit route and then stopped arguing, which was worse; the way he'd checked the radio's battery at three in the morning during a storm, when no sane man checks anything.

*What are you doing, Dov?* she thought, and the river said nothing, and neither did he.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater, and with it a decision.

The gorge narrowed ahead into a place the charts called the Throat—a slot of water barely wider than the boat, where the current funneled through at speed and the walls rose sheer on either side. Running it in daylight was a gamble. Running it at night was suicide. Running it with a weeping hull was somewhere between.

"We beach here," Beatrix said, pointing to a gravel shelf on the left bank, a narrow apron of stones at the base of a slide scar. "Haul out, dry the patch properly, let the sun do some work. We run the Throat this afternoon, rested."

Ilya looked at the cliffs with naked relief. Dov said nothing, which she noted, and filed.

They grounded the boat on the gravel and stepped out onto land for the first time in eighteen hours, and Beatrix's legs nearly folded under her, the ground seeming to pitch like a deck. The sun broke through in patches, steaming the wet stone. Ilya sat down where he was, in the shallows, fully clothed, and put his face up to the light like a man greeting a relative.

While Dov unloaded gear, Beatrix walked the hull's line, checking the patch. It had held. It would hold better once she re-tarred it in the sun. She crouched at the waterline, working wax into the seam with her thumb, and let herself feel, for one unguarded minute, the strange warmth underneath the exhaustion.

Because that was the truth she kept circling: last night had been awful, and last night had also been the closest she'd felt to another human being in years. Bailing side by side with Ilya Senn, passing the pail back and forth in the dark, watching a gaunt, drowned-looking archivist refuse to stop even when his hands blistered—that had done something to her. He hadn't complained once. He hadn't performed his suffering. He'd just worked, steadily, quietly, the way people work when the work matters more than they do.

"You should sleep," Ilya said, appearing beside her with two mugs of tea made from water boiled on the spirit stove. "You drove all night."

"So did you bail all night."

"Yes," he agreed solemnly, "and my arms have filed a formal grievance. Drink."

She drank. The tea was terrible, over-steeped and faintly smoky from the stove, and it was the best thing she'd ever tasted. They sat on the gravel with their backs against the hull, shoulder to shoulder, watching the Throat's white noise echo up the gorge.

"My grandmother used to say," Ilya offered, after a while, "that Karvathi boats had no name boards. You painted the name inside the hull, where only the owner could see it. So the river would never learn it and take it personally."

"That's not a real proverb."

"No," he admitted. "I invented it just now. But it feels true, doesn't it? Some things you keep facing inward."

Beatrix looked at him. Rain-damp, blistered, ridiculous, brave. She thought about names painted inside hulls. She thought about a compass in her pocket that pointed, apparently, at a country that no longer existed, and a grandmother who had never once mentioned it.

"Dov's hiding something," she said.

It came out before she'd decided to say it, the way confessions do. Ilya didn't startle. He just nodded slowly, watching the water.

"I know."

"You know?"

"He checks the radio at night. He stands where the wind covers his voice." Ilya sipped his tea. "I'm an archivist, Beatrix. Noticing things is the entire profession. Most people think it's about remembering. It isn't. It's about noticing what wants not to be noticed."

"Why didn't you say anything?"

"Because you noticed first." He shrugged, a small, tired motion. "And because I don't know what he's hiding. Only that he is. There's a difference between evidence and verdict. I try not to confuse them." He paused. "Though I'll admit the archive trained me badly for situations like this. In the archive, documents don't lie to you. People are much harder to catalog."

Beatrix almost smiled. Almost. Then the smile died somewhere on the way to her mouth, because across the gravel shelf, Dov had stopped unloading and was standing very still, looking upriver at the Throat, and his hand had gone, absently, unconsciously, to the inside pocket of his jacket where the radio lived.

He caught her watching. This time neither of them pretended.

"Beautiful morning," Dov said.

"Gorgeous," Beatrix said.

The river roared through the Throat, patient as anything that intends to win.
