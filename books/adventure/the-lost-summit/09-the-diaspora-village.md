## 9. The Diaspora Village

The engine died an hour before dusk, and Ilya Senn understood, with the particular clarity that comes to a man who has spent his life reading other people's records, that they were going to have to ask strangers for help.

He said nothing at first. He stood in the stern while Beatrix wrestled the starter cord for the fourth time, her knuckles white, her jaw set in the way he had come to recognize as her answer to every problem the river offered: not surrender, but argument. The water around them had gone the color of steeped tea, thick with silt from the upper tributaries, and the rain had settled into that fine, patient drizzle that did not fall so much as accumulate, beading on his shoulders, his lashes, the cracked spine of the notebook he kept inside his coat.

"We'll row," Dov said from amidships, already shipping the oars.

"Row where?" Beatrix asked. "We don't know this stretch."

Ilya knew it. That was the thing none of them had quite absorbed yet—that he knew it. Not from charts. From a census.

"Two bends ahead," he said quietly. "There's a village on the left bank. Raised houses, stilt foundations, a drying rack shaped like a fish skeleton." He paused, because the next part still felt like handing over something stolen. "It's Karvathi."

Beatrix stopped pulling the cord. She looked up at him through the wet, and he watched her decide—watched the arithmetic move behind her eyes, debt and danger and the bronze chest lashed under the tarpaulin between them—and then she simply nodded and took up an oar.

They rowed into the dark.

* * *

The village announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and river fish and something else beneath, something resinous and sweet that Ilya had not encountered in forty years and recognized anyway, the way you recognize a dead woman's voice on an old recording. *Kessu-wood*, he thought. They burned kessu shavings in the winter houses of Karvath to keep the damp out of children's lungs. He had read that in a monograph. He had never smelled it until now, and the smell went through him like a hook through soft flesh.

The houses rose on blackened stilts above the flood line, connected by walkways of woven bamboo, and lanterns hung from the eaves in strings of three—the old mourning count, three lights for a nation, though he doubted anyone under thirty here remembered why it was three. Dogs announced them. Doors opened along the walkway like eyes opening one after another, and by the time Dov brought the boat against the ladder-platform below the largest house, a dozen faces were looking down at them out of the lamplight.

An old woman came first. She descended the ladder with a slowness that was not frailty but ceremony, each rung taken deliberately, and when she reached the platform she stood before them wrapped in a shawl dyed the deep river-green that Ilya had only ever seen described in textile inventories. Her face was broad and seamed, her eyes nearly lost in it, and she looked at each of them in turn—Dov, Beatrix, himself—and then past them, at the boat.

At the shape under the tarpaulin.

"You're carrying the seal," she said. Her accent was heavy, softened by decades of other languages laid over it like sediment, but the words were Karvathi, and hearing them spoken aloud, casually, in the open air, made Ilya's throat close.

"You know it," Beatrix said.

"I carried it." The old woman touched her own collarbone, where a scar showed pale through the wet fabric. "In 'the year of the erasure,' we were told to burn our papers or bury them. My mother sewed ours into my coat and put me on a barge." She looked at Ilya then, really looked, and something in her expression shifted—not suspicion, but recognition of a different kind. "You're Senn. Archive Senn."

"I—" His voice failed him. He tried again. "My family was from the archive towns. We were clerks. Nobody important."

"Nobody important," the old woman repeated, and there was something in the way she said it that made Dov glance at him sideways. "Come up out of the rain, clerk. All of you. You can tell us on dry ground what a Senn is doing running rivers with a Vane-debt boat and a vault key."

* * *

The house was warm in the way that only houses built against cold water can be warm: a central stove of black iron, kessu shavings crackling, the low ceiling hung with drying herbs and nets and strings of dried peppers the color of embers. Children watched them from doorways with the frank stare of the young. An old man tuned a long-necked instrument in the corner without seeming to listen to it, the notes falling like water off a ledge.

The old woman was called Maren. She fed them first—fish stew, dense with river greens, served in bowls that Ilya noticed, with a lurch, bore the faint glazed crest of the double-summit, worn almost to invisibility by use. They had not stopped using the crest. They had simply let it wear down into the ordinary, into soup and daily bread, which was either grief or defiance or both, and Ilya could not look at his bowl for the first minute.

Then Maren sat across from him, folded her hands, and said, "Now. Tell me how you know my mother's dialect, boy from nowhere-important."

So he told them. It came out of him in pieces at first, then in a flood—he had not spoken any of it aloud in years, perhaps ever, not all together. The archive towns. The expulsion, four decades back, when the maps were redrawn and the concession charters signed and the whole apparatus of a country dissolved into "disputed territory" and "lapsed sovereignty" and other phrases designed by lawyers to make a place stop existing without anyone having to kill it. His grandfather's shelf of Karvathi imprints, confiscated. His own career, the respectable one, the one that ended the day he filed a request to access the sealed Karvathi holdings and was called into a room with two men who explained, kindly, smilingly, the way everyone powerful seemed to smile now, that some archives were closed for reasons of ongoing commercial interest.

"And then?" Maren asked.

"Then I kept asking," Ilya said. "Until they took my credentials. Until I wasn't an archivist anymore, just a man who reads seals."

The old man in the corner stopped tuning his instrument. Somewhere a child laughed and was hushed. And Maren looked at him for a long moment, and then she did something Ilya was entirely unprepared for: she reached across the table and took his wrist, her grip dry and strong, and turned his hand palm-up as if reading it.

"Senn," she said. "Archive Senn. Your people kept the rolls. Births, deaths, land, water rights, the boundary stones, the names of the drowned in the flood years." Her thumb pressed his palm. "When the soldiers burned the provincial hall in Terevan, it was a Senn who carried the master land registry out through the marshes. Everyone knows this story. We thought the line had ended."

"It has," Ilya said. "I'm the last of it, and I'm not even—I'm disgraced. I'm nothing. I read seals on riverbanks for salvage men."

"You're the last of it," Maren corrected, "and you walked into my village tonight carrying the vault key under a tarpaulin, and you think that's nothing." She released his wrist. Around the table, the other elders had gone very still, and Ilya realized with vertigo that they had been listening not as strangers hear a story but as a congregation hears scripture—something known, awaited, arriving late. "Boy. In this house you will eat before you argue with your own name."

* * *

Later, when the stew was gone and the children had been sent to their pallets and the rain had thickened against the roof like a second tide, Maren took them to the back room where the village kept its own small archive: a lacquered chest, half the size of the bronze one in their boat, holding folded papers wrapped in oilcloth, photographs gone amber at the edges, a child's drawing of a mountain with two peaks labeled in careful schoolroom script.

"This is what we saved," Maren said. "What fit in coats and false bottoms. Now you tell me what was in the vault at the summit, since your chest is the key and you must have read the manifests."

Ilya glanced at Beatrix. She gave him a small nod—*yours to tell*—and he understood that too, that she was giving him this, deliberately, the way you hand someone a knife handle-first.

"There is no gold," he said. "If that's what the stories say. There's no treasury, no bullion, no crown jewels. Karvath didn't work that way; the summit vaults were built by the charter leagues, the farming cooperatives, the water councils." He unfolded his notebook to the page he had copied from the concession papers, the inventory schedules buried in annexes no syndicate lawyer had bothered to read past. "Land deeds. Every parcel, every boundary stone survey, every water right on every tributary—including the ones this river runs through. Census rolls. Names. Four hundred years of names, cross-referenced to parcels. Court judgments settling disputes between villages. The whole legal body of the country, written down and locked above the flood line so it would survive even if the country didn't."

The stove ticked. Rain ran off the eaves in ropes.

"The syndicate thinks it's buying a treasure," Beatrix said slowly. "Merrit Vane filed a claim on a treasure."

"The syndicate thinks in bullion," Maren said. "That is their weakness and it has always been their weakness. A deed is worth more than gold, girl, because gold can be stolen twice—a deed cannot be stolen once it is witnessed. Whoever holds those papers holds the proof that this river, these banks, these villages belong to the people who were erased from the maps." She tapped the lacquered chest. "We have fragments. Memories, copies, a grandmother's recitation. Fragments lose arguments. Whole registers win them."

"And the permits expire at monsoon peak," Ilya said. "After that, unclaimed contents revert—to the descendants of the recorded owners. To the people in this room."

"To people in every room on this river," Maren said. "Which is why the syndicate's gunboats will be in the gorge by now." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you report weather, and Ilya saw Beatrix and Dov exchange a look over the stove light.

Then Maren turned back to him, and her voice changed—softened, but not weakened.

"You said you're the last of the Senns. The registry-carriers." She reached into the lacquered chest and drew out a single sheet, brittle and brown, and laid it on the table between them. It was a fragment of a manifest, a corner really, but the header survived: the double summit, and beneath it, in faded clerk's hand, *Registry custody: House of Senn.* "Your family carried those papers out of the fire once already. Do you understand what it means that the key came to *you*? Out of all the boats on this river?"

"I didn't find it," Ilya said. "She did." He nodded at Beatrix. "I just recognized it."

"Recognizing is the work," Maren said. "Anyone can dredge up a box. Knowing what it is—that's the inheritance." She pushed the fragment toward him. "Keep this. It's yours by blood and by trade. And when you sleep tonight, you sleep in the house of people who have waited forty years for a clerk to come up the river."

Ilya held the paper and could not speak. He had spent his adult life among documents, cataloguing other people's belonging, stamping other people's claims, filing other people's families into drawers—and he had believed, somewhere deep in the marrow where belief stops being arguable, that this was all he would ever have of such things: proximity. Secondhand warmth. The archivist's cold seat beside the fire of other lives.

And now an old woman he'd met three hours ago had handed him his own name like a key, and called him home.

* * *

He could not sleep. Near midnight he climbed down to the platform alone, wrapped in a borrowed blanket that smelled of kessu smoke, and sat with his feet hanging over the black water. The rain had gentled to mist. Downriver, far off, a sound rolled along the gorge—engines, maybe, or thunder; impossible to say, and no comfort either way.

Footsteps on the walkway. Beatrix lowered herself onto the platform beside him, a blanket around her shoulders, saying nothing for a while. Below them the boat knocked softly against its mooring lines, the bronze chest sleeping under its tarpaulin like a thing dreaming.

"They fed us," she said finally. "Strangers. Fed us and gave us blankets and didn't ask for collateral." There was something raw under the dryness of it, and he realized she was thinking of smiling men with clipboards, of debts itemized cheerfully, of every kindness she'd ever received that arrived with a price tag attached.

"That's what it's supposed to feel like," Ilya said. "I'd forgotten."

"Did you ever have it?"

"No," he admitted. "But I catalogued it. Thousands of entries. Household inventories, probate records—you can reconstruct a family's whole life from what they owned and who signed for it." He turned the blanket's edge in his fingers. "I used to think that was the closest I'd get. Standing outside the window, taking minutes."

Beatrix was quiet a moment. "Maren called you the last of the registry-carriers."

"She's romanticizing a disgraced clerk."

"She's right, though." Beatrix looked at him sidelong, rain-damp hair stuck to her temple, and there was no bravado in her face now, none of the armor she wore for enforcers and creditors. Just the tired, level gaze of someone deciding something. "I've been dragging that chest around like cargo. Like leverage. Something to trade or sell before someone takes it. But tonight, watching them touch that crest on the soup bowls—" She shook her head. "It's not cargo, is it. It never was."

"No," Ilya said. "It's a door. And doors are heavier than cargo, because you have to decide which side of them to stand on."

Far down the gorge, the sound came again—closer now, unmistakable: engines, more than one, running slow and deliberate, the way patrols run. Neither of them moved. The mist drifted off the water and the lanterns behind them swung in their threes, three lights for a nation, burning all night in the windows of a village the maps had deleted.

"Maren wants to send claim papers with us," Beatrix said. "To the summit registry. Before expiry." She exhaled. "Through gunboats. Through the gorge. With a leaking hull and a crew of three and a debt on my head worth more than this boat."

"Yes," Ilya said.

"I haven't said yes yet."

"You will," he said, and heard how certain he sounded, and was surprised by it. "Because tomorrow morning she's going to show you the school register. Every child in this village, listed by name, with the parcels their grandmothers held. And you're going to realize that Merrit Vane's claim isn't against a chest. It's against every one of those names."

Beatrix stared out at the dark water for a long moment. Then, quietly, almost to herself:

"God help me. I think it already worked."

Downriver, the engines passed on through the gorge, unhurried, patient, counting the bends—and above them, in the raised houses, the Karvathi lamps burned in their threes until morning.
