## 10. Maren's Bargain

The rain came at dusk the way it always did in the high country—not arriving so much as remembering itself, falling in long grey sheets that had been falling somewhere else for a thousand years before they found this village. It drummed on the woven roof of Maren's house and ran off the eaves in silver ropes, and inside, the room smelled of woodsmoke and river clay and something older underneath, something like paper kept dry against all odds.

Beatrix sat cross-legged on a mat that had been laid out for her with a formality that made her throat tight. Across the low table, Maren opened a lacquered box.

The old woman's hands were the first thing Beatrix had noticed about her, back when they'd stumbled up from the boat half-drowned and shivering—hands like knotted rope, broad-palmed, scarred along the thumbs in the way of people who had spent a lifetime hauling nets or lines or, Beatrix now suspected, other things entirely. Those hands moved over the box's lid with a tenderness Beatrix had only ever seen sailors give engines.

Inside: oilcloth. Inside the oilcloth: paper.

Not much of it. A bundle perhaps two fingers thick, wrapped in a second layer of waxed linen, tied with cord gone soft with age. Maren lifted it out as if it weighed as much as an anchor.

"You looked at the seal," Maren said. "On your chest. The night you came up the hill."

It wasn't a question. Beatrix had learned in the last two days that Maren didn't ask questions she didn't already know the answers to; she offered them the way a ferryman offers a plank, and watched to see whether you'd step across.

"I did," Beatrix said. "Ilya told us what it was. What Karvath was." She hesitated. "What it is," she corrected, and saw something flicker behind the old woman's eyes—approval, maybe, or grief wearing approval's coat.

Maren set the bundle on the table between them. Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere down the slope a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Forty years ago there were registries," Maren said. "Land offices. Courts. A census hall in the capital where every family's name was written—every field, every orchard, every grave with its deed and its boundary stones. When the annexation came, the new government burned what it could reach and sealed what it couldn't. But the summit vault—" her hand rested flat on the bundle, "—the summit vault was built by engineers who assumed that one day men with torches would come. It was built to be drowned. Flooded by its own sluices, hidden under the reservoir, reachable only when the water was low or when someone was foolish enough to dive for it. Everything Karvath was, everything it owned, everything it could prove—it went into the dark, and the dark kept it."

"And the syndicate knows," Beatrix said quietly.

"The Vane Syndicate knows what every vulture knows: that dead things are easiest to eat." Maren's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "They filed their claim through the colonial concession office in the spring. Paper stacked on paper, lawyers' names instead of soldiers'. They will go down into that vault with a notary and a salvage permit and they will carry out forty years of a nation's memory in crates, and they will sell it—deed by deed, name by name—to the very families who erased us, so that no one can ever again prove whose land this was."

The fire popped. Ilya, sitting near the wall with his long spine curved around a cup of tea he hadn't touched, made a small sound—not quite a word—and pressed his lips shut.

"And this?" Beatrix nodded at the bundle. "What am I looking at?"

"Counter-claims." Maren untied the cord with fingers that trembled only slightly, and Beatrix understood suddenly that the trembling wasn't age. It was the shaking of a woman holding something she had guarded too long. "Copies. The originals are in the vault—that's the cruelty of it, the law requires the originals—but copies, sworn and witnessed by every surviving elder we could gather, from here to the coast settlements, from the fishing camps to the terraced farms above Kessel. Four hundred and eleven households. Land claims. Blood lines. Testimonies taken down in Karvathi because the language is still alive whether the maps admit it or not." She drew a breath. "If these papers reach the summit registry before the monsoon peak—if they are entered into the record while the concession permits still stand—the syndicate's claim becomes contested. And a contested claim cannot be executed until the dispute is heard. And the dispute cannot be heard after the permits expire, because after expiry the vault reverts to the descendants of the nation it belonged to."

"To us," Ilya said softly. "To the survivors."

"To whoever stands in that vault when the water rises and says *this is ours*," Maren said. "With proof in hand."

* * *

Beatrix looked at the bundle for a long time.

She knew arithmetic. She'd been doing grim arithmetic since she was nineteen years old, since the first engine failure, the first debt, the first smiling man from the Vane Syndicate who had inventoried her winch cable with the cheerful thoroughness of a man pricing a coffin. She knew exactly what her boat was worth, what her gear was worth, what thirty days bought and what it didn't. She knew that the distance between this village and the summit registry was measured not in miles but in gunboats, and that the gorge below the reservoir narrows to a channel where a patrol cutter could pin them against the rock like a moth on a board.

She also knew that Dov had been quiet for three days. Not sulking-quiet. Calculating-quiet. She'd seen him standing at the rail that morning, watching the downstream current with the particular stillness of a man weighing something in each hand.

And she knew—Ilya had told her, in the low voice he used for confessions—that the registry itself might already be compromised, that the syndicate moved faster than weather, that hope with a deadline attached was just another way of saying *grief, scheduled*.

"Maren," she said. "You know what I am. You've seen my boat. You know I owe the Vanes more than this vessel and everything in it is worth. If I take those papers upriver and they catch me, they don't just take the papers. They take the chest, the key, the whole road to the vault—and they'll know, finally and completely, that the diaspora is organized enough to fight them on paper. That's worth more to them than gold. They'll burn this valley looking for everyone who signed those pages."

"We know," Maren said simply.

"Four hundred and eleven households," Beatrix pressed. "Every one of them a target."

"Four hundred and eleven households that have been targets for forty years," Maren answered. "The difference is that now they would be targets *for something*." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the deep seams of her face. "Girl. I have buried a husband under a government that called him a statistic. I have watched my grandchildren learn to sing songs in a language their teachers pretend never existed. I have spent four decades keeping a box dry in a wet world, waiting for someone to carry it uphill. Do you imagine I don't know what I'm asking? I'm asking you to do the one thing I cannot do, which is be young, and be fast, and be foolish on our behalf."

The rain hammered the roof. Down the room, Neshi—Maren's granddaughter, sixteen and sharp-faced and pretending not to listen with the total concentration of the young—had stopped pretending entirely, her mending forgotten in her lap.

"I'm not a hero," Beatrix said. It came out flatter than she intended. "I'm a debtor with a boat. Half my crew wants to sell me to the highest bidder and the other half hasn't decided yet. I chase storms because storms don't ask what you're worth. That's the whole of my qualifications."

"Yes," Maren agreed. "That's why it has to be you."

Beatrix blinked. "Come again?"

"A hero would go to the registry and make speeches and get arrested in the square," Maren said. "A mercenary would take the syndicate's money before she left this room. But a woman who owes more than she owns, who has nothing left to lose but a boat and a bad reputation—" the old woman's mouth bent, not quite a smile, "—that woman understands exactly what these papers weigh. Because she's carried that weight herself. Debt is just a claim someone else holds on your future. These are claims my people hold on their past. I need someone who knows the difference in her bones."

* * *

Later—much later, when the fire had burned down to orange eyes and Maren slept in the inner room and Neshi had finally, grudgingly, gone to her mat—Beatrix stood under the eave and let the rain hit her face.

Ilya found her there. He did it without noise, the way he did everything, materializing at her shoulder like a man who had spent his life being overlooked and had turned it into a skill.

"She asked me first, you know," he said.

Beatrix turned. "What?"

"Years ago. Not specifically—she didn't know me then. But the archive sent inquiries, once, before I was expelled. Formal requests for survivor testimony regarding the summit holdings. The requests were denied from above. I copied the denial stamps." His laugh was thin and self-directed. "That was the crime, really. Not the asking. The copying. An archivist who keeps evidence of the institution's own cowardice is worse than a thief. A thief only takes things."

"They're going to kill you," Beatrix said. It wasn't a question either. "If they catch us. Merrit doesn't waste tea on people she plans to see again."

"Probably." He said it the way he read seals—carefully, without flinching. "But Beatrix, listen to me. I have spent my entire adult life in rooms full of paper about a country that no one was allowed to say aloud. I catalogued its rivers. I filed its trade ledgers. I could recite the grain tariffs of 1931. And I have never once stood on its soil. Never once heard anyone speak the language without lowering their voice." He looked out at the rain, at the black shapes of the hills where Karvath used to be. "Tomorrow we go upriver into a gorge full of gunboats, toward a vault that may already have syndicate divers in it, carrying papers that make us enemies of the most patient criminal enterprise on this coast. And it is the first day of my life I have not been afraid of the wrong thing."

Beatrix laughed—a short, surprised sound, swallowed quickly by the rain.

"That's not comfort, Ilya."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true. I find I prefer true things lately. They're lighter."

* * *

She gave Maren her answer at dawn, standing in the dooryard while the village woke around them—smoke rising from cook-fires, nets being mended, the river below running high and brown with the season's first real anger.

"I'll carry them," Beatrix said. "The papers. To the registry, before peak water. On one condition."

Maren waited, eyebrows raised, a woman who had clearly expected conditions and budgeted for them.

"When this is done—however it's done—the debt I owe the Vanes isn't yours to pay. Don't sell nets for me. Don't empty the community fund. I've seen what happens to villages that bleed themselves white for a stranger's ledger." She held the old woman's gaze. "I'm not doing this to buy my way clear. I want to be clear about that, because I think you'd try, and I think it would hurt you, and I won't carry that on top of everything else."

For a moment Maren said nothing. Then she reached out and took Beatrix's hand in both of hers—those rope-scarred hands, strong as mooring line—and held it the way you hold something you intend to keep.

"You misunderstand what you're carrying," Maren said. "You think it's paper. In four hundred and eleven houses last night, four hundred and eleven families lit a candle and spoke your boat's name into the dark. You are carrying *that*, girl. Paper burns. That doesn't."

Beatrix's throat did something complicated. She looked away, out at the river, and found she couldn't speak for a moment, and hated it, and didn't.

* * *

They loaded at first light.

Neshi appeared at the dock with a canvas satchel over her shoulder and her jaw set at the exact angle her grandmother's did, and announced, in the tone of someone informing rather than asking, that the claim papers would travel better with someone who spoke Karvathi, since the registry clerks upstream were notoriously lazy about languages they pretended not to know.

"That's not happening," Beatrix started.

"Neshi," Maren said, from the top of the bank—and whatever passed between grandmother and granddaughter in the following silence was conducted entirely in frequencies Beatrix couldn't hear, some treaty older than either of them. Neshi's chin lifted. Maren's eyes narrowed. And then, impossibly, the old woman inclined her head a fraction of an inch, and Neshi stepped aboard the *Vespera* like she'd been crew for years, dropping her satchel beside the dredge winch with proprietary confidence.

Dov looked at Beatrix. Beatrix looked at Dov. Something unspoken and unresolved moved between them, a door neither of them opened.

Ilya was already below, wrapping the claim papers in oilcloth and stowing them in the driest corner of the hold, next to the bronze chest with its extinct seal, the key and the claims riding together like two halves of a sentence.

The engine caught on the third pull. The current grabbed the hull. And Beatrix took the wheel and pointed the bow upstream, toward the gorge, toward the gunboats, toward the summit where a drowned country waited to hear whether anyone had come to speak for it.

Behind them, on the bank, the villagers did not wave. They stood in a silent line in the rain—all of them, old and young, the whole settlement—as the *Vespera* climbed the brown water, and Beatrix understood that this was their send-off: not farewell, but witness. They were watching to remember. Whatever happened next, there were people ashore who would testify that she had tried.

She was still holding that thought, warm as a coal, when Dov cleared his throat at her elbow and said, quietly, not looking at her:

"Skipper. There's smoke on the water ahead. Two columns. Moving fast."
