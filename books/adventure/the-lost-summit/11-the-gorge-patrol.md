## 11. The Gorge Patrol

Dov had learned to read water the way other men read faces, and the gorge that morning was lying to them.

It lay in a language of calm. The Karvath River ran green and slow between walls of black stone, so smooth on top that Beatrix cut the engine to half-throttle and let them drift, saving fuel, letting Neshi braid her hair against the wind. Ilya sat at the bow with the chest's papers spread across his knees like a man reading scripture in church, one hand flat on each page as if the wind might steal his inheritance twice. The light came down in long slanted columns through the mist above the cliffs, and everything looked like mercy.

Dov knew better. Calm water in a gorge meant depth, and depth meant somewhere below them was a channel deep enough for anything the syndicate cared to send.

He checked the radio again without meaning to. It had become a tic, the way a man with a bad tooth keeps finding it with his tongue. The set sat under the tarp by the stern, wrapped in oilcloth, its antenna lashed along the gunwale where anyone who looked would think it was spare rigging. He hadn't touched it since the night of the leak. He told himself he hadn't. He told himself a lot of things now, in a voice that sounded less like his own every day.

"Smoke," Neshi said.

She said it quietly, the way her grandmother said things—flat, certain, already true before anyone else agreed. She was standing at the rail with her face into the wind, and when Dov followed her eyes he saw it: a gray thread rising from behind the southern spur of the gorge, too straight and too steady to be a cookfire.

Then came the sound. Not an engine exactly—a pressure, a low abdominal hum that Dov felt in the deck plates before he heard it with his ears, the way you feel a storm two valleys away.

"Beatrix," he called, and hated how level his voice came out. Practiced. That was what betrayal did first, he was learning. It didn't change your face. It changed your voice, made it smooth and useful.

Beatrix was already moving. She killed the engine the rest of the way and the boat swung lazily in the current, and she stood at the helm with her head cocked, listening, doing the arithmetic of distance and echo that had kept her alive through eleven years of chasing weather no sane pilot would fly into.

"Two boats," she said. "Maybe three. Coming downriver fast."

"They'd have to be flying," Neshi said.

"They're not coming downriver." Beatrix turned, and Dov watched her arrive at it a half-second before he did, watched her face do something complicated and then go very still. "They're coming *up*. Nobody comes up the gorge at speed unless they've run it before. Unless they know every rock in it."

Ilya rose from his papers. The wind took one page and he pinned the rest under his elbow with the unbothered precision of a man who had spent his life guarding fragile things from weather. "The registry station at Kell's Narrows," he said. "It has a patrol dock. It's been syndicate-flagged since spring—I found the mooring manifests in the colonial annex." He said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. Ilya never accused. He only laid facts out on the table like a man laying out instruments, and let you pick up whichever one cut you.

"How far?" Beatrix asked.

"Four kilometers. Maybe five. We round the bend at the pillar rock and we're in front of their guns."

* * *

They argued for ninety seconds, which in Beatrix Dunmore's crew constituted a parliament.

Neshi wanted to beach the boat and take the claim papers overland through the goat tracks on the northern wall—the route Maren had described, the route the villagers used when the river was angry. Ilya pointed out, gently, that the papers were the whole mission and the goat tracks were slick with monsoon runoff and a fall there would scatter Maren's village's future across four hundred meters of scree. Beatrix wanted to run for it, engine wide open, hug the shadowed wall where the patrol's sightlines went long, gamble on their borrowed engine being faster than a gunboat's dignity.

And Dov stood at the stern with his hand resting on the tarp over the radio, saying nothing, weighing nothing, being nothing—which was its own kind of weight, heavier than any argument.

Because here was the thing Dov could not say: he knew why the patrol was here. He knew it the way you know a room is colder because a door stands open somewhere. Three nights ago, hull splitting, rain hammering, all hands bailing with buckets and boots and finally cupped palms, he had keyed that radio in the dark of the stern while the others fought for the boat's life, and he had given coordinates to a voice that answered him with terrible kindness. A woman's voice. Merrit Vane's voice, or someone paid to sound like her—warm, unhurried, grateful. *Thank you, Dov. You're doing the right thing. Stay on the river. We'll handle the rest.*

Stay on the river. He had thought, then, that it meant they wouldn't shoot. That his call was a shield he'd thrown over his friends even as he sold them. That was the story he'd built out of it, the little house of good intentions he lived in now, and he had kept the radio wrapped and silent ever since because silence kept the house standing.

But the patrol wasn't passing them. It wasn't sweeping the river. It was waiting at the narrows ahead, exactly where a boat fleeing upriver would have to come. And behind them, if he understood the shape of it—and God help him, he was starting to understand the shape of it—there would be another. Or not even a boat. Just the knowledge, planted in him, that running back meant running toward the debt collectors and Dov's sister's name on their ledger.

There was no direction that wasn't theirs.

"We'll run it," Beatrix decided. "Shadow side, full throttle. Neshi, get low with the papers. Ilya—" she paused, and something in her voice softened by a degree, "—hold onto something that matters."

"I usually do," Ilya said, and gathered his pages to his chest.

Dov went to the engine. His hands were steady. That was the horror of it, he thought as he fed fuel and gripped the starter cord. Not trembling hands, not a guilty conscience screaming at him. Just steadiness. A body that had made its accommodations and gotten on with the work, like a man who'd taken a job he was ashamed of and discovered the shame fit in a coat pocket where nobody could see it, including, most days, himself.

He pulled the cord. The borrowed engine caught on the second try, coughing blue smoke, and Beatrix swung the bow upstream and gave it everything.

* * *

The gorge narrowed the way a throat narrows, and the river remembered it was a mountain's blood and quickened.

Black walls rose on either side, wet stone streaked with white mineral tears, ferns hanging out of cracks fifty feet overhead. The channel coiled around fallen pillars of rock the size of chapels, and Beatrix threaded them with the cold brilliance that had made her name among storm chasers—reading the water's surface the way Ilya read seals, every boil and seam a sentence, every smooth green lane a lie she refused to believe until proven. Spray came over the bow in cold sheets. Neshi crouched in the bilge with the claim papers wrapped in oilcloth inside her jacket, arms locked around her knees, and Dov saw her lips moving—not prayer, he realized, but recitation. She was memorizing the documents. In case the river took the paper, the girl would keep the words.

Ahead, the bend at the pillar rock. Beyond it, according to Ilya's charts, the narrows opened into the long pool below the old registry station, and the pool was wide enough, and calm enough, and perfect enough for gunboats.

Dov's hand found the tarp. Found the radio beneath it.

He didn't decide to do it. That was the part he would carry afterward, the part that woke him at night in the weeks to come: there was no decision, no moment of choice, just his fingers moving with the same awful steadiness as the rest of him, peeling back the oilcloth, keying the set, and speaking four words into the wind-noise at a volume only the microphone could love.

*"Boat inbound. Shadow side."*

A warning. That was how he framed it, even then, even knowing. A warning to whom? To the patrol, obviously—to tell them exactly where to point their guns. But some splintered remnant of himself insisted it was a warning *for* Beatrix, a message sent sideways through enemy wires: *they know you're coming, turn back, turn back.* Both things were true. Both things were the same thing. That was the architecture of treachery; Dov was beginning to understand it was never one building but two, occupying the same foundation, and you lived in whichever one let you sleep.

The voice came back almost instantly, soft through the static, pleased. *"Good boy. Hold your position after the bend. Don't be on the water when we stop them—they'll be looking for a fourth person on board."*

A fourth person. Dov frowned into the spray. There were four of them. Beatrix, Ilya, Neshi, himself. What—

*"You won't be counted,"* the voice said, as if hearing the question form in him. *"Your account is settled, Dov. You're cargo now. Cargo doesn't get stopped."*

The radio clicked off. The engine roared. And Dov stood in the stern of the boat with the river's cold breath on his neck and understood, all at once and completely, what he was.

Not a traitor who had saved them. Not a friend who had failed them. A tool. A hinge. Something the syndicate had oiled and used and would use again, and the worst of it—the part that made his stomach drop through the hull and into the green dark of the river—was the phrase *don't be on the water.* They didn't want the boat stopped at the narrows. They wanted it *through.* They wanted Beatrix past the patrol, committed, deeper into the gorge, closer to the summit, with no way back and no way out except forward—forward into whatever waited at the drowned vault, where the trap had been built all along.

The patrol wasn't a wall. It was a sheepdog.

And he had been the whistle.

* * *

They rounded the pillar rock at speed and the narrows opened before them, and there they were: two gunboats riding at anchor in the long pool, gray-green, syndicate pennants hanging limp in the windless air, a machine-gun mount on the nearer one covered with a tarp that fooled no one. A figure on the foredeck raised a hand—not a weapon, a hand, almost friendly—and Dov's heart slammed once against his ribs like a fist on a door.

Beatrix didn't slow. Of course she didn't. Slowing was dying; everyone aboard knew it. She put the boat's nose at the gap between the anchored patrol craft and the eastern cliff, a lane perhaps thirty meters wide, and drove into it with the engine screaming.

For three seconds nothing happened. The gunboat's tarp stayed on. The figure on the foredeck lowered his hand slowly, almost thoughtfully, and turned away toward the wheelhouse, and Dov watched it happen with the sick clarity of a man watching a play he'd secretly rehearsed: the patrol *turning away.* The wheelhouse door closing. The nearer gunboat's engine, when it finally coughed to life, turning its bow *downstream*—away from them, out of the gorge, as if recalled by some order more important than a leaking river skiff with four fugitives aboard.

The second boat followed. No shots. No siren. No searchlight sweeping the shadowed wall. Within a minute the pool held nothing but their own wake, folding itself against the cliffs, and the silence rushed back in like water filling a hole.

"They're leaving," Neshi said, lifting her head from the bilge. "Why are they leaving?"

"Because they were never there for us," Ilya said quietly.

Everyone looked at him. He stood amidships with his pages clutched to his chest, and the mist moved behind him, and his face had gone the color of the stone walls—gray, ancient, carved by bad news.

"A patrol that wanted to stop us would have stopped us," he said. "We are a leaking boat with a stolen engine and no permit. One shot across our bow and we are finished, legally and literally. They let us pass because letting us pass *is* the operation." He looked at Beatrix, and Dov saw the archivist struggle with how much to say, and lose. "Someone wants us at the summit. Wants us committed. Wants us to lead them to the vault entrance, or open it, or trigger whatever mechanism the concession requires—because a claim filed from a desk means nothing, but a claim made *at* the vault, in person, before expiry..." He stopped. Swallowed. "Merrit Vane doesn't need to chase us. She needs us to arrive."

The engine idled. The river carried them onward anyway, patient as always, toward the place it had been carrying them all along.

Beatrix's jaw worked. Dov watched her take it in—the shape of the cage, bars made of current and canyon—and watched her do what she always did with unbearable information, which was to convert it immediately into heading. "Then we don't arrive on their schedule," she said. "We arrive early. We arrive wrong. We find another way in that they haven't planned for." She turned toward the stern, toward Dov, and her eyes were bright and hard and utterly trusting. "Dov, burn the fuel. I want the narrows behind us before noon."

*Aye,* he should have said. *Aye, Captain.* Instead he heard himself say, "What if there's a third boat?"

"There's always a third boat," Beatrix said, grinning, and pushed the throttle herself, and the boat leapt forward into the narrowing gorge.

Dov stood at the stern with the radio warm under the tarp against his hip, like a coal he couldn't put down, and did the math he'd been refusing to do since the night of the leak. If the syndicate wanted them at the summit, then everything he'd done—every coordinate, every whispered report—hadn't endangered the journey. It had *enabled* it. His betrayal and his loyalty had been steered into the same channel, and he could no longer tell which of his own choices were his. Every instinct he trusted, every plan Beatrix made, every narrow escape—they might all be furniture in someone else's room.

Unless.

Unless the channel ran both ways. If they believed his reports, then his reports were a weapon, and weapons could be loaded with anything. Lies, fed down a wire the enemy had built, delivered by the enemy's own trust. He looked at the back of Beatrix's head, at Ilya's bent shoulders, at Neshi's young hands locked around the future of a dead nation, and felt the two houses inside him collapse into one, and it was not a comfortable house, but it was finally, unmistakably, *his.*

He would have to tell them. Not yet—not here, with the gorge still listening. But soon. Tonight, maybe, in the rain, with the truth in his mouth like a stone he'd swallowed forty years ago.

Behind them, far downstream, faint beneath the engine's roar, Dov heard—or imagined he heard—a third engine, waking.
