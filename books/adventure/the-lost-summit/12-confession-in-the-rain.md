## 12. Confession in the Rain

The rain came down like a verdict.

It had been falling since the gorge patrol turned away—since the gunboats with their grey hulls and their greyer intentions had swung off toward the eastern channel for no reason any of us could name, leaving the river open before us like a door someone had forgotten to lock. We should have celebrated. Instead we sat under the boat's canvas awning and listened to the water hammer the deck, and nobody spoke, because all three of us were doing the same arithmetic in our heads: *gunboats don't turn away. Gunboats are never called off by accident.*

Dov sat at the stern with his hands wrapped around a mug of tea he wasn't drinking. He'd been holding that mug for an hour. The tea had gone cold and he kept lifting it anyway, raising it to his mouth, lowering it, a man rehearsing a toast he couldn't bring himself to give.

I watched him from the wheelhouse window while I pretended to study the chart. Rain streaked the glass and made the river beyond it look like something painted on wet silk—all smears and silver, the far banks dissolved, the world reduced to what we could carry.

I'd known Dov for nine years. I'd pulled him out of the Virel flood when he was twenty-two and too proud to grab the line I threw him, so I'd jumped in after him and we'd both nearly drowned together, which is a faster way of making a friend than most people ever get. He'd crewed for me through lean seasons and leaner ones. He knew my debts better than my own ledger did. He'd once sold his father's watch to keep us fueled through a dry month, and when I found out and tried to pay him back he'd laughed and said, *You'll pay me back when you're rich, Bea, so don't hold your breath.*

That man was sitting at my stern holding a cold cup of tea like a rosary, and he would not look at me.

"Dov," I said.

Nothing. The rain filled the silence for him.

"Dov."

He set the mug down. His hands stayed around it anyway, knuckles pale. "I know," he said. "I know you've been watching me. Since the squall." He swallowed. "Since the radio."

The chart blurred in front of me. Not from the rain on the glass.

"Say it," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "Whatever it is. Say it now, while I'm still the kind of person who can hear it."

* * *

He told it badly, the way honest men always do—haltingly, with detours into justification, circling the thing itself like a dog circles a trap it has already stepped in. The syndicate had come to him six weeks ago. Before Merrit Vane ever boarded us for tea. Before the chest, even—or almost before; they'd known about the dredge operation, known about the shoal, known that the river gives up its dead to whoever bothers to look. A man named Corvel, soft-voiced, patient, had bought Dov dinner in a dockside place with paper lanterns and paid for it in crisp notes, and then laid out the offer the way you lay out a map: not money first, but his sister.

Sera. Who owed the syndicate eleven thousand on a fishing concession she'd signed when she was desperate and nineteen. Who had two children and a boat with a rotten keel and no other way to feed them come winter.

"They didn't want much," Dov said, and heard himself, and flinched. "That's—I know how that sounds. They wanted coordinates. Where we dredged. What we found. And later—" his voice dropped, went rough, "—later, where we were going. That's all. Check-ins. Short transmissions, coded, on the frequency they gave me. I told myself it was nothing. I told myself everyone sells weather reports."

"And the patrol," I said. "The gunboats that turned away."

"I called it in." He finally looked up at me, and his eyes were wrecked, red-rimmed, forty years old overnight. "Corvel said if I gave them our position at the gorge entrance, they'd stand down. He said—they're herding us, Bea. They've been herding us since the shoal. Every time I report, they adjust. They let us run. They *want* us running upstream, toward the summit, because—" 

"Because they can't find the vault themselves," Ilya said quietly.

I'd forgotten he was there. He stood at the edge of the awning's shelter with rain beading on his shoulders, having come up from below without a sound, the way he did everything, and his face had that terrible archival calm he wore when he'd just understood something and wished he hadn't.

"They need the key," Ilya said. "They have half of one—the syndicate's been buying Karvathi relics for years, everything the diaspora was forced to sell or lost. But a vault built by people who expected to be robbed doesn't open for half a key. So they let us do the diving for them. Let us spend our fuel and our nerve finding the entrance. And when we do—" he didn't finish it. He didn't have to.

The rain said it for him. When we find the entrance, they take the chest, the key, the boat, and the three of us go into the river, and the syndicate files its claim on a drowned nation's inheritance with no witnesses left to contest it.

I looked at Dov. Nine years. A flooded river. A sold watch. Sera's kids calling me Aunt Bea every solstice festival, hanging off my arms like I was furniture they loved.

"You put my life in their ledger," I said. "You put *hers*—" I stopped, because Ilya was right there, and some things shouldn't be spent as ammunition. But Dov heard the sentence I didn't say, and it hit him harder than anything I could have shouted.

"I know," he whispered.

"How long." My hands were shaking. I gripped the edge of the chart table until the wood bit back. "How long have you been reporting?"

"Since the night we cast off."

Every mile. Every narrow escape. The leaking hull in the squall—had they known? Had Corvel listened to Dov's frightened voice crackling through the storm and smiled over whatever passed for tea in syndicate offices, noting *subject vessel taking water, morale low, proceed*? Every moment I'd thought we were fugitives outrunning them, we'd been a compass needle they were steering.

I came around the table. I don't remember deciding to. Later, Ilya would tell me I moved the way water moves—no anger in it, just inevitability—and that he'd stepped between us without touching either of us, one hand raised, palm out, the way you'd stop traffic or catch rain.

"Beatrix."

"Move."

"No," he said, gently, absolutely. "Not yet. Not until you've heard the useful part. Because there is a useful part, and if you throw him overboard in this rain you will never hear it, and the syndicate will win, and Sera's children will grow up in debt, and Karvath will stay dead." His dark eyes held mine. Under the calm I saw something else—he was afraid of me, a little, and he stood there anyway. "You can drown him tomorrow. Hear the useful part tonight."

The rain hammered the canvas. Somewhere downstream, thunder rolled along the gorge like a barrel being dragged over stone.

"The useful part," I said, through my teeth.

Ilya turned to Dov. "Your radio. Your channel. It still works?"

Dov blinked. "I—it works. They expect a check-in at dawn."

"Then at dawn," Ilya said, and the ghost of something that was almost a smile crossed his gaunt face, "you are going to tell them exactly what we want them to believe."

* * *

We worked it out through the small hours, the three of us, huddled over the chart table with the lamp turned low and the rain making its argument against the roof.

Here is what I learned about betrayal that night, kneeling over a radio set with the man who'd used it against me: it is a tool, same as a winch or a crowbar. It broke something in me to pick it up. It would break more to use it well. But grace, Maren had told me once in the village, isn't a feeling. It's a decision you make with your hands.

"We don't lie outright," Ilya said, pacing the cramped space, three steps and turn, three steps and turn. "Lies get caught. We tell them truths in the wrong order. We give them a real position—for a boat that won't be there. We complain about damage we don't have. Fear reads as authentic; fear is the best camouflage there is. And above all—" he stopped, and tapped the chart where the summit gorge narrowed to a throat, "—we confirm their herding. We let them think it's working."

Dov sat with the transmitter's handset in his lap like something that might bite. "If they check the positions against the gunboat sightings—"

"They won't. They haven't yet. Why would they start?" Ilya crouched to his eye level, and his voice softened into something I hadn't heard from him before—not the archivist, not the exile. The teacher. "Dov. They trust you because you lied to someone who trusted you. That's the currency you're spending now. Spend it where it does damage."

Dov looked at me across the table. It was the hardest look anyone has ever asked me for. He wasn't asking forgiveness—he knew better than to ask that yet. He was asking permission to keep standing on my boat, to keep breathing my air, to be worth the cost of what he'd done.

"Sera doesn't know," he said. "She doesn't know any of it. She thinks I borrowed money for engine parts."

"I know," I said.

"If this goes wrong—if they trace the disinformation back—"

"I know, Dov."

"Bea." His voice cracked clean through. "Nine years. You jumped in after me. I keep thinking about that. You jumped into a flooded river for a stupid proud kid, and I—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "There isn't a number. There's nothing I can haul or bail or sell that covers it. I just need you to know I know that."

The rain eased, just slightly, the way storms do when they're reloading.

I thought about throwing him off at the next landing. I thought about it honestly, the way you have to think about a thing before forgiving it means anything. I thought about the cold, the current, the syndicate's patience, and how alone we'd be without his hands—and then I made myself think past that, past the arithmetic, to the thing underneath: that the man at my table had chosen his sister's children over me, which was unforgivable, and understandable, and human, and that I had spent my whole life owing cheerful people money and had never once been offered something free, and here was Dov offering me the only thing he had left, which was the truth and the rest of his life to carry it.

"Here's what happens," I said. "You make the dawn call. You make every call after that, and I sit in on every one. You lie to them the way you should have lied to me—which is to say, you don't. And when this is over, if we're alive, you and I are going to sit down with Sera and figure out her debt together, because it's ours now. All of it. That's what crew means. That's the only payment plan I accept."

He stared at me. "Bea—"

"That's the deal. Take it or swim."

His laugh came out broken in the middle, half sob, and he took it.

* * *

Dawn came up grey and trembling, the river running high and brown with the monsoon's first true swell. Dov keyed the transmitter with steady hands—steadier than mine, watching—and gave Corvel's silent office our gift: a position twelve miles south of truth, a hull "shipping water bad," a crew "exhausted, arguing, talking about turning back."

Let them taste our retreat, Ilya had scripted. Let them relax their grip on the gorge. A herd that thinks it's turning walks slower.

The acknowledgment came back in four flat clicks. Dov set the handset down and put his face in his hands, and I put my hand on his shoulder, and neither of us said anything, because there was nothing yet that words could carry that the work hadn't already said.

Ilya stood at the rail watching the brown water rise, and when I joined him he spoke without turning.

"You know what frightens them, don't you? Truly?" The wind pulled at his thin coat. "Not us. Not the chest. *The calendar.* Every day that passes is a day off their permit. They burned the registry because paper terrifies them. They herd us because they cannot afford to let the clock run out. Which means—" he turned, and for the first time since I'd met the man, Ilya Senn smiled like someone who had won an argument forty years overdue, "—all we have to do is survive, and arrive, and *wait*. Time is on the side of the dead, Beatrix. It always was. That's why they erased them."

Ahead, the gorge opened its grey throat, and the monsoon came down the mountains behind it like a nation returning.
