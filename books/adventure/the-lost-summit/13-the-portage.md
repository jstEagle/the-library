## 13. The Portage

The engine died at dawn, on the third morning after the gorge, with a sound like a man clearing his throat before deciding not to speak.

Ilya felt it through the deck before he heard it—a shudder in the planking, a hesitation in the water's push against the hull—and then the silence that followed was worse than any noise they'd endured since casting off. Rain still fell, but softly now, a gray drizzle that turned the river to hammered pewter. The current took them sideways, gently, the way a hand takes a drunk man by the collar.

"No," Dov said, as if the engine were a stubborn animal that might reconsider. He was already at the housing, sleeves rolled, forearms slick with grease and rain. "No, no, we just fixed you. I *bought* you a new gasket. I apologized to you personally."

"Is it the fuel line?" Beatrix called from the tiller, where she stood with one hand on the wheel and the other shielding her eyes, reading the water ahead the way Ilya had seen her read storm clouds—lips slightly parted, whole attention given.

"It's everything." Dov sat back on his heels. "It's the fuel line and the impeller and probably the mounts, and I'd bet my last cigarette the block's cracked. This engine was old when the maps were drawn. It's held together by prayer and rust, and I think the prayer expired."

Ilya crouched beside him and looked into the engine's open gut without pretending to understand it. He had spent his life among things that could be read—seals, marginalia, the faded ink of clerks who had died before he was born. Engines were a language he'd never learned, and standing there, useless, he felt the old shame of the scholar in the physical world, the man who could recite a nation's land registry from memory but couldn't fix a fuel line in the rain.

"We're not sinking," Beatrix said. It wasn't a question. She had a way of stating things that made them true by force of grammar. "We're not sinking, we're not lost, and we're not stopped. We're just—coasting." She looked up at the ridge of dark green that had been sliding past on the eastern bank all morning, rising out of the mist like the back of a sleeping animal. "And there's the shortcut Maren told us about. If we can get the boat over that ridge, we cut two days off the gorge."

"Over the ridge." Dov said it flatly, tasting it. "The boat. Over. The ridge."

"People have portaged heavier boats over worse ridges."

"People have also drowned doing it, which is why we remember them."

Ilya found himself smiling. It startled him, the smile—how easily it came now, how little it cost. Three weeks ago he had not smiled in a month. Three weeks ago he had been a man made entirely of corridors and filing cabinets, a ghost haunting the archive that had expelled him, and now he was sitting in a leaking boat arguing about geography with a debt-ridden storm chaser and a traitor-turned-ally, and he was *smiling*.

He catalogued the feeling, because cataloguing was what he did. It went in the same drawer as the taste of Maren's smoked fish, and the sound of the village children singing the Karvathi counting song—*yesh, dvesh, tressh*—which he had taught them without meaning to, the words rising out of him like water finding an old channel.

* * *

The village that had sheltered them sat a mile back downriver, and it was Neshi who heard the engine die.

She had been following them along the bank, Maren's granddaughter, seventeen years old and built like a river reed—all stem and stubbornness—walking the fisherman's path that traced the eastern shore. When the boat began to drift, she cupped her hands and shouted across the water, and her voice carried with the particular authority of a girl who had been shouting across water her whole life.

"You've died! Your engine has died! Come in to the willow bend and we'll help you, or you'll drift to the falls and then you'll really be dead!"

They rowed—Dov and Beatrix on the oars, Ilya bailing with the dented bucket that had become his particular office—and made the willow bend, where the bank shelved gently down to the water and half the village seemed already to be waiting, as if word of the engine's death had traveled faster than the engine's silence. Perhaps it had. In the villages, Ilya was learning, information moved by a system more reliable than any telegraph: grandmothers.

Maren herself stood at the water's edge, dry under an oilcloth despite the drizzle, her hands folded on her stick. She was small and brown and folded like a walnut, and her eyes had the patience of someone who had outlived a country and therefore outlived impatience.

"So," she said. "The engine has died."

"The engine has died," Ilya confirmed.

"Engines die. Rivers don't. The ridge path goes over to the north channel—my father's generation hauled timber over it when the mill burned. It's a day's work, maybe two, with enough backs." She looked at Beatrix. "You're carrying something that can't be left in a boat unattended, and you're being followed by men in gray boats. So. You'll need more than backs. You'll need people who know the path, and people who owe Karvath a debt they're still paying."

Beatrix opened her mouth—to refuse, Ilya was almost certain. He had learned her refusals by now: the set of her jaw, the quick breath, the pride that stood in front of her like a shield wall. But Maren raised one finger, very slightly, and the refusal died unborn.

"We'll help," Neshi said, before anyone asked her. She had come to stand beside her grandmother, dripping, grinning, a coil of rope already over her shoulder as if she'd been waiting for this her whole life. "I'm coming with you, too. Over the ridge. To the summit."

"Neshi," Maren said.

"You said yourself the papers need carrying, and Beatrix's crew is one man short of a crew and one engine short of an engine. I'm strong. I know the path. And I can swim better than any of you, which matters, because you're all going to drown if you keep going like this." She said it cheerfully, the way the Vane Syndicate's enforcers said cheerful things, but where their cheer had been a knife wrapped in silk, Neshi's was just—cheer. Unarmored. Appalling. Wonderful.

Maren looked at her granddaughter for a long moment. Ilya watched the two of them, and something in his chest turned over. He had no one to look at him that way. He had no one whose refusal or permission had ever mattered to him in that particular, ancient, unpayable currency.

Then Maren turned to Ilya, and her voice softened into the register she saved for the old words. "He was a keeper once, your grandfather. In the old registry. You have his hands."

"I have his eyes," Ilya said. "The hands are my mother's. She copied deeds."

"Then you have the whole family in you." Maren smiled, and it was like watching a locked room open. "Take my granddaughter. And take this—" she pressed something into his palm, small and cold: a brass token, stamped with the seal he knew better than his own heartbeat, the crest of Karvath. "Path token. Show it at the ridge station. There's an old man there, Osten, who kept the timber way-station after everyone stopped needing it. He'll feed you. He'll complain. He was my husband's brother."

* * *

The portage took two days, and Ilya would remember them for the rest of his life, though he could not have said so at the time, because at the time he could not have imagined surviving them.

They built rollers from green logs, and sledges from the boat's spare planking, and they took the *Vesper*—Beatrix had finally told him the boat's name, said it with the reluctance of a woman introducing a child she'd given up for adoption—off the water and onto the mud, and then the mud onto the path, and then the path up, and up, and up.

It took fourteen of them. Villagers mostly, and Neshi at the front with the rope over her shoulder like a yoke, calling the rhythm in a work-song Ilya half-knew from the archive—there had been a transcription of it, a field recording from before the erasure, a woman's voice singing the hauling song of the Karvathi highlands, and now here it was in living throats, in the rain, pulling his boat up a mountain.

*Hoh, and the river runs backward,*
*hoh, and the stone remembers,*
*lift, my brothers, the river runs backward—*

He sang it. Badly. He didn't know he was singing it until Neshi looked back at him with her mouth open, and then everyone was looking at him, and the song stumbled, and Beatrix laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the first he'd heard from her that wasn't a weapon—and the song picked up again, rougher and louder than before, and they hauled.

The path climbed through cloud forest where the drizzle became a green twilight and the trees dripped constant water, where the mud was deep enough to steal boots and the roots grabbed at the sledge runners like hands. Ilya learned to haul. His shoulders blistered and then the blisters broke and then, somewhere in the second day, his hands stopped being a scholar's hands and became whatever they were now—raw, swollen, competent. Dov taught him to set a roller without pinching it, shouting corrections with the patience of a man who had decided, sometime in the night, to stop doing bad math and start doing good work. Beatrix carried the chest herself, on her back, in a frame the villagers lashed together, and refused all help with it, and no one pressed her. Some things a person had to carry alone to know what they were.

And in the evenings, at the ruined way-station where old Osten fed them smoked fish and complaints in equal measure, Ilya found himself teaching.

It began with Neshi. She had the papers—the village's claim documents, wrapped in oilcloth against her chest—and she wanted to know what they said. Not the gist. The words. Every word.

"This clause," she said the first night, tapping the vellum with a finger still rope-burned from the day's hauling. "It says the registry reverts. Reverts to *whom*? It says survivors and descendants. Am I a descendant? My grandmother is a survivor, so am I covered, or do I need my own paper?"

Ilya looked at her, and felt the archive open in him like a wing. "You're covered by derivation, but your own claim is stronger if you file independently, and here's why—" and he was off, drawing on the table with a wet fingertip, citing clause and precedent, and she leaned in with her chin on her fists and her eyes bright, and he realized, with a jolt like the engine's last shudder, that no one had ever listened to him like this. Not once. In the archive he had been tolerated, then suspected, then expelled. Here he was *wanted*. Here, knowing things was not a disease.

By the second evening, half the hauling crew had crowded into the way-station to hear him explain the difference between a concession and a grant, and Osten had brought out a bottle of something that tasted like pine tar and regret, and Neshi was arguing with him—*arguing*, joyfully, about whether a lapsed permit could be refiled or only reasserted—and Beatrix sat by the fire with her boots off, watching him with an expression he couldn't catalogue, and Dov was asleep sitting up with his mouth open, and the rain came down on the roof like applause.

"Where did you learn all this?" Neshi asked, in a lull. "Really."

"The archive. The National Archive of—" he stopped. The name of the country stuck in his throat, the way it always did. It was not his country. He had been born forty years after it was erased. And yet. "Of Karvath," he finished, and the words came out easier than they ever had. "I was the youngest archivist they'd ever certified. I thought it was the greatest thing in the world. I thought paper was the strongest material there is." He turned his cup in his hands. "They expelled me for asking about the sealed collections. The Karvathi holdings. I asked one question too many, and they took my credentials, and I spent six years being a ghost." He looked up. "And then a woman who chases storms for a living dredged a bronze chest out of a shoal, and I read the seal on it, and I followed her onto a boat, and now I'm teaching a girl to file land claims in a timber station on a mountain, and I think—" He stopped. Started again. "I think this is the first time in my life I've been exactly where I'm supposed to be."

Neshi was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "You sing badly."

"I know."

"Grandmother will make you teach the children anyway. She makes everyone teach the children. It's how she keeps the country alive—one song, one clause, one child at a time."

"Then I'll teach them," Ilya said, and heard, in his own voice, an anchor drop.

* * *

They put the boat into the north channel on the morning of the third day, sliding the *Vesper* down the last stretch of rollers into green water that ran fast and cold toward the summit country. The crew of villagers stood on the bank and cheered, and Osten complained that they'd dented his best roller, and Maren's token sat in Ilya's pocket against his hip like a second heartbeat.

Neshi came aboard with her rope coil and her oilcloth bundle and her grandmother's blessing, and she claimed the bow seat as if it had been built for her, and Beatrix looked at the four of them—herself at the tiller, Dov at the dead engine he was already cannibalizing, Ilya amidships, Neshi at the bow—and said something Ilya didn't catch, but Dov laughed, and Neshi laughed, and Ilya found himself laughing too, helplessly, at nothing, at everything, at the sheer improbable fact of being on a boat with people who knew his name and wanted him aboard.

They had a borrowed engine to find, a gorge to run, a registry to reach, and two days of margin that the monsoon was already eating. The gray boats were somewhere behind them, patient as arithmetic.

But as the north channel took them, and the ridge with its singing faded behind the mist, Ilya sat amidships in a boat full of strangers who were not strangers, and let himself think a thought he had not permitted himself in six years:

*We might make it.*

And then, around the next bend, where the channel narrowed and the mist hung in curtains, they heard it—a sound that did not belong to the river, or the rain, or the singing.

Engines. More than one. Coming down the channel toward them.
