## 14. Merrit's Letter

The messenger came at dusk, when the river had gone the color of hammered pewter and the rain had settled into that patient, businesslike drizzle that meant the monsoon was only clearing its throat.

Beatrix heard the outboard first—a clean, expensive sound, nothing like their borrowed engine's asthmatic cough. She stepped out of the boathouse they'd rented for the night, a lean-to of corrugated tin and rotting planks on a mudbank that didn't appear on any chart worth buying, and watched the syndicate skiff slide toward them through the murk. White hull. Chrome rails. A man standing at the bow in an oilskin so new it still creased like paper.

He didn't look like muscle. That was the thing about the Vane Syndicate—they never sent anyone who looked like what they were. The man at the bow had the soft hands and pleasant face of a bank clerk, and he held up a sealed envelope as if it were a gift he'd carried a long way and was tired of holding.

"Miss Dunmore." His voice carried over the water without effort, warm as a lamp. "I have correspondence from Ms. Vane."

Dov appeared at Beatrix's shoulder. She felt him go rigid there, felt the breath stop in him like a fish striking ice. She didn't look at him. She'd been not-looking at him since the leaking boat, since the radio she'd filed away in the back of her skull next to every other debt she hadn't yet paid.

"Set it on the dock," Beatrix said. "You can leave it there."

"I'm instructed to wait for a reply."

"You can be instructed to swim home, too. See how your instructions hold up."

The clerk smiled—the syndicate smile, the one they must practice in mirrors, all teeth and no weather behind it—and set the envelope down on the wet planking with exaggerated care, weighting it with a flat stone as though the river might steal it. Then he stepped back and folded his hands, and waited, and smiled, and waited.

Rain ticked on the tin roof. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered to itself like an old man disagreeing with the news.

Beatrix crossed the dock, picked up the envelope, and turned it over. Heavy stock. Wax seal—actual wax, actual seal, because Merrit Vane believed ceremony was half of ownership. The Karvathi chest sat inside the boathouse ten feet behind her, under a tarp, humming its silent bronze hum, and Beatrix understood with sudden clarity that this letter was addressed less to her than to it.

She broke the seal with her thumb.

* * *

Merrit wrote the way she spoke: pleasantly, precisely, and with the blade hidden until the last sentence, where you'd already swallowed it.

*Dear Miss Dunmore,*

*I hope this finds you well, and dry, though I understand the monsoon has been generous this year. I write to simplify matters between us.*

*You are, I know, a woman who dislikes complication. So let me remove one. The debt you carry with my syndicate is, as of tonight, no longer the issue. I am prepared to forgive it entirely—all of it, principal and interest, thirty days early and without prejudice—if you deliver the chest to our rendezvous at Sallow Point by dawn tomorrow.*

*You will notice I ask only for the chest. Not your boat. Not your gear. Not your crew. I am told the archivist is with you; keep him. I am told the boy Dov is with you; keep him too. I want only the key.*

*Which brings me to the other matter, and I regret its necessity.*

*Dov's sister, Petra, holds a separate obligation to this house. Hers is smaller than yours but older, and older debts gather more interest. If the chest is not at Sallow Point by dawn, her account comes due instead. You know what that means. I won't insult you by spelling it out, and I won't pretend I take any pleasure in it—I assure you I take none whatsoever.*

*One chest, or one sister. It is a simple arithmetic, and I trust you'll do it kindly.*

*With respect and continued good wishes,*
*Merrit Vane*

Beatrix read it twice. The second time wasn't for comprehension—it was for fuel. She read it the way you read a storm cell on radar, looking for the rotation, the hook echo, the thing that tells you where it's going to come down hard.

Then she folded the letter along Merrit's crisp creases and looked up at the clerk on his white skiff, still smiling, still waiting, rain beading on his brand-new oilskin.

"Tell her the reply is no."

"She anticipated that," the clerk said pleasantly. "She said, if the answer is no, tell her that dawn is a fixed point, and rivers run downhill, and everyone arrives somewhere eventually." He tipped two fingers off his brow, a little salute. "Good evening, Miss Dunmore. Mind the gorge. It's been hungry this season."

The skiff swung away into the pewter dark, its running lights shrinking to two red embers, then nothing. The rain closed behind it like a curtain drawn on an act.

* * *

Inside the boathouse, lamplight. Ilya sat cross-legged on the floor beside the chest with Maren's claim papers spread around him in careful rows, cataloguing them by some system only he understood, his gaunt face lit gold from below. Neshi was asleep in the bow of the boat, curled around her grandmother's satchel like it might walk off without her. The rain drummed the tin roof, a thousand small fingers.

Beatrix stood in the doorway with the letter in her fist and found, to her surprise, that she couldn't make her mouth work.

It was Dov who saw her face. He'd been coiling rope, and the coil went slack in his hands, and he said, "Bea? What is it?"

She looked at him—that big, decent, terrible-at-math face of his, the face that had bailed water beside her all night with his sleeves rolled and his jaw set—and she thought about Petra. She'd met Petra once, years ago, at Dov's mother's funeral: a sharp-eyed girl who'd laughed too loud and held Dov's arm the whole time as if anchoring him to the earth. A girl who now existed in Merrit Vane's ledger as a line item, as leverage, as a *kindly arithmetic*.

Rage came up in Beatrix like floodwater. Not the hot flash kind—the other kind, the deep kind, the kind that fills a valley slow and then takes out the bridge.

"Dov," she said, and her voice came out level, which frightened her more than shouting would have. "Sit down."

He sat. Ilya looked up from his papers, and Beatrix handed him the letter without a word, because her hands wanted to do something else—wanted to break something, or build something, and she couldn't yet tell which.

Ilya read it fast, lips moving, and then went very still. "Petra," he said quietly. "Her sister."

"My fault," Dov said. His voice was hollow. "My debts. I took the loans for the boat engine, after Mom—after we buried her, I couldn't—" He stopped. Started again. "I signed things. I sign things. It's what I'm bad at, reading before I sign."

"It's not your arithmetic," Beatrix said. "That's the point. That's the whole rotten point." She began to pace, three steps, turn, three steps, the boards complaining under her boots. "Look at the shape of it. Really look. Merrit forgives my debt—*mine*, the whole reason she's been circling us since the dredge—for the chest alone. She doesn't want me broken. She doesn't even want the boat. She wants the key, delivered, by dawn, to a place she picked."

"So?" Dov said. "So don't do it."

"Oh, we're not doing it. But think about why she wrote it this way." Beatrix stopped pacing. The lamplight caught the sweat on her temples, or maybe it was rain; out here the two stopped being different. "If she just wanted the chest, she'd have taken it off us at the gorge. Her gunboats had us boxed. They *let us go.*"

Ilya's head came up slowly. "You think the letter is the trap."

"I think the whole river is the trap, and the letter is the bait laid on top of it." She crouched by the chest, put her palm flat on the cold bronze, on the extinct crest of a country that no longer existed except in shelves and deeds and names. "Sallow Point by dawn. That's downriver, Ilya. Downriver, away from the summit, away from the registry, away from expiry. Every hour we spend running to her rendezvous is an hour the permits burn down while her claim sits safe and filed. And if we *don't* go—she takes Petra, and she knows I'll tear myself apart choosing, and either way she wins the only race that matters."

"The clock," Ilya said softly. "She isn't chasing us anymore. She's making us chase ourselves."

"And Petra." Dov's voice cracked on the name. "Bea, whatever you decide, I can't—she's my sister. I'll row down there myself, I'll give them the chest, I'll—"

"You'll sit down," Beatrix said, gentler than she'd ever said anything to him, "and you'll listen, because Merrit counted on exactly that. On you going noble. On me going guilty. On all of us scattering along the paths she drew for us." She straightened. Something had shifted in her chest, the rage cooling into a hard bright mineral, something you could cut glass with. "Here's what I know about storms, Dov. You don't outrun them and you don't argue with them. You read them. You find the seam in the wind and you drive *through*."

* * *

They worked by lamplight while the rain thickened outside, and Beatrix laid it out the way she used to lay out chase routes on a chart table, back when the only thing she gambled with was her own skin.

"Maren's papers go to the summit registry before expiry. That hasn't changed. What's changed is that Merrit thinks she owns our choices." She pulled the chart toward her, weighted its corners with four brass fittings pried off the chest's crate—old habit, ballast where you could get it. "So we give her a choice to own. We answer her letter."

"With what?" Ilya asked.

"With the truth, bent just enough to float." Her finger traced the river down from their mudbank, past the gorge, to Sallow Point—a gray tongue of shingle where the current slowed and eddied, perfect for a rendezvous, perfect for an ambush. "At midnight, Dov takes the skiff we salvaged and runs downriver, alone, carrying a crate the right size and weight. He carries a note saying he stole the chest rather than let me trade his sister for it. Which, knowing Dov, is the most believable lie ever written, because it's ninety percent true—he *would*."

Dov stared at her. "And the real chest?"

"Goes up. With us. Tonight, in the dark, while every eye she has is watching the downriver channel." Beatrix looked at him steadily. "But hear the cost before you agree, because I won't spend you cheap. If it goes wrong, you're alone at Sallow Point with her people and a box of rocks, and 'I was deceived' is not a defense that keeps sisters safe. Merrit will know she's been played the moment she opens that crate."

"She'll know before that," Ilya said quietly. Everyone looked at him. He had gone back to his papers, but his eyes were distant, reading something older than any of them. "Weight. A courier who handles cargo knows weight the way a reader knows type. And—" he hesitated, and Beatrix watched him choose honesty over comfort, watched him do it the way he did everything, deliberately, painfully, "—and I should say plainly: I am not brave in the way you two are brave. I run. It's what got me expelled. So weigh my counsel accordingly. But it seems to me that Merrit Vane has made one error, and it is the error every empire makes."

"Which is?"

"She believes everyone can be priced." He tapped the letter, on the word *kindly*. "She has priced your debt, the boy's silence, the sister's fear. She has built her entire trap out of prices. And prices fail against people who have stopped negotiating." He looked up, and there was something in his thin face Beatrix had never seen there before—not hope exactly, but its skeleton. "You stopped negotiating somewhere between the gorge and the village. I watched it happen. That is why the trap cannot close. Not because we're clever. Because she's bidding on goods that are no longer for sale."

The rain roared down on the tin roof, and nobody spoke for a moment, and Beatrix felt the words land in her like ballast.

"Neshi goes with us," she said finally. "Upstream. Dov runs the decoy alone." She raised a hand before Dov could speak. "Not because I don't trust you. Because you're the only one of us Merrit will believe acting alone, and because—" the words came hard, scraped raw, "—because I've spent my whole life owing people who smile. I'm done paying. From tonight, we make *them* owe *us*. Petra included. When this is over, that ledger burns, every page."

Dov looked at her for a long moment. Then he did the thing he always did with feelings too large for his vocabulary: he nodded once, hard, like driving a stake.

"I'll need the note in your handwriting," he said. "She'll check."

"I'll write it tonight." Beatrix rolled the chart closed. Outside, thunder had moved closer, working the ridge above the gorge like a fist working a knot. "And Dov—one more thing. When she asks you why you did it, don't act. Just tell her the truth sideways. Tell her you couldn't watch me sell a dead country to pay a living debt. Let her chew on that."

* * *

Near midnight, the rain eased to a whisper and the river ran black and glossy under a sky bruised purple. They loaded the real chest into the boat in silence, wrapping it in oilcloth, lashing it amidships where the weight rode best. Neshi woke without being called—villagers' children, Beatrix was learning, slept light and woke ready—and took her position at the stern lines without asking what was happening, which was its own kind of heartbreak.

Dov stood on the dock with the decoy crate at his feet and the forged note in his jacket, and for a moment the four of them just looked at each other across the gap of planks, lamplight and river-dark between.

"For Petra," Dov said.

"For Petra," Beatrix said. "And for the rest of it, too."

Ilya pressed something into Dov's hand—a scrap of paper, a page torn from one of his precious catalogs. "The inventory numbers of the vault contents, as far as Maren's elders remember them. If Merrit demands proof the chest is genuine, show her that. She'll believe a list. People like her always believe lists."

Dov tucked it away, then gripped Beatrix's forearm, hard, the old crewman's clasp. "Don't sink," he said.

"Don't get caught," she said. "We'll both disappoint each other."

His skiff slipped out into the black water, running lights doused, and the river took him the way it took everything—without comment. Beatrix watched until the dark ate him, then turned and climbed aboard.

Ilya cast off the lines. Neshi opened the throttle, gentle, gentle, and the borrowed engine coughed itself awake and pushed them upstream, into the throat of the coming storm, toward the drowned summit and the two days left on the world's most important clock.

Behind them, downriver, dawn waited at Sallow Point with a smile on its face—an appointment they intended to stand up.

Ahead, lightning stitched the gorge horizon, once, twice, like a needle drawing thread through cloth.

Beatrix put her hand on the compass in her pocket—her grandmother's compass, quiet and certain—and steered into the rain.
