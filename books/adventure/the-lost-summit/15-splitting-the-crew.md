## 15. Splitting the Crew

Neshi had grown up on stories of the river, but no one had ever told her that a river could sound like an argument. That was what it sounded like now, two days before the monsoon's full arrival — the water below the village landing arguing with itself, brown and swollen, dragging whole trees past the pilings like a man pulling furniture out of a house he'd lost.

She stood at the edge of Maren's porch and watched Beatrix Dunmore pace the length of the boards with her arms folded tight, as if she were trying to hold herself together with her own elbows.

"You're going to wear a groove in my grandmother's floor," Neshi said.

"Your grandmother's floor has survived forty years of monsoons," Beatrix said without stopping. "It can survive me."

Behind them, inside the long room that smelled of woodsmoke and drying paper, Dov sat at the table with his big hands flat on either side of a leather folder, staring at it the way a man stares at a debt. Ilya Senn stood at the window with his back to all of them, reading the sky the way he read seals — slowly, syllable by syllable, as though the clouds were a language someone had tried to erase and he refused to let them.

Neshi had known these people for six days. Six days ago they had been strangers who smelled of engine oil and river rot, and now they were about to ask her to run white water with them into the gorge, and she found — this was the strange part, the part she would turn over for years afterward — that she was not afraid of them. She was afraid *for* them, which was a different animal entirely, one that lived closer to the heart.

Maren came out with tea nobody had asked for, because Maren's answer to every crisis was tea, and set the tray down hard enough to rattle the cups.

"Sit," she said. "All of you. If we are going to divide ourselves like a loaf, we will do it sitting down."

* * *

They sat. The rain began while they sat, a fine gray hiss on the tin roof, the first polite knock of the storm that would come later like a fist.

Beatrix laid it out plainly, because plainness was the only armor she seemed to own. "The registry is burned. That means the only way to contest the permits is in person, at the vault, before expiry. Two days." She turned the compass over in her fingers — the brass thing she'd carried since before any of them knew what it was, its needle swinging now toward the high country instead of north, as if it had been waiting all along for permission. "The syndicate knows we're coming. Merrit wants us coming. She's herded us this far, and she'll keep herding us, because she thinks we're carrying everything."

"So we stop carrying everything," Ilya said quietly.

"We carry nothing," Beatrix said. "We carry *lies*."

Neshi watched Dov's jaw work. He had confessed three nights ago, kneeling in the rain with his secret radio in his hands like something dead he'd dragged out of the water, and Beatrix had not forgiven him so much as *used* him — which, Neshi was beginning to understand, was how Beatrix loved people: she made them useful, and usefulness was her word for trust.

"The syndicate listens to your channel," Beatrix said to Dov. "So tomorrow morning, you tell them where we are. You tell them we're running for the summit with the chest and the claim papers, all of us, together. And then—" she tapped the folder under his hands— "you go downriver."

Dov looked up. "Say that again."

"You take the decoy papers south through the gorge. Loudly. Slowly enough to be caught. Every gunboat Merrit owns follows *you*, because you're the man who sold his friends once and would obviously sell them again."

"That last part's true," Dov said, and there was no self-pity in it, just arithmetic, the same terrible arithmetic Neshi had watched him do every day since the confession. "That's why it works. They believe me because I'm cheap."

"Dov." Beatrix leaned across the table. "You're the only one of us they'll believe. That's not an insult. That's the job."

"And if they catch me?"

"They won't kill you. Dead men don't hand over papers." Beatrix said it steadily, but Neshi saw her thumb press white against the table edge. "They'll take the decoys, they'll gloat, and by the time they open the folder and find sixty pages of Maren's jam recipes translated into bureaucratic Karvathi, we'll be at the vault."

Ilya turned from the window at last. He had a way of moving that always looked slightly delayed, as if his body were waiting for his courage to catch up. "And the real papers?"

Neshi reached into her coat and brought out the oilcloth packet — the village's claim, signed and thumbprinted by every surviving family, wrapped around a sliver of dried fish skin because Maren said important documents traveled better when they looked like lunch. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the boat.

"I carry them," Neshi said.

Three faces turned to her.

"You're sixteen," Dov said.

"I'm the best pilot on this stretch of water, and everyone at this table knows it except you." She kept her voice level, the way Maren had taught her: anger was fuel, never cargo. "Beatrix can't read the gorge marks. Ilya sinks like a sermon. And you—" she nodded at Dov— "have an appointment downriver. So it's me."

* * *

They spent the evening preparing, and Neshi learned that preparation was its own kind of intimacy. She watched Ilya copy the claim papers twice over in his small, precise archivist's hand — one true set, two false sets identical to the eye — and she watched him seal each packet with the same wax and the same extinct crest, and she understood that he was not making forgeries. He was making *equal* things. In his hands, the lie and the truth came out looking exactly alike, because to him every document deserved the same dignity.

"Doesn't it bother you?" she asked, holding a candle for him. "Making fakes of something sacred?"

Ilya considered this with the seriousness he gave everything, as if the question were a seal to be read. "My whole life," he said, "people treated records as sacred and people as disposable. I'd rather do it the other way around." He blew across the cooling wax. "Paper is just paper. What matters is who carries it."

Outside, Beatrix and Dov stood at the landing in the rain, and Neshi — who had grown up in a village where privacy was a rumor — did not pretend not to listen.

"You could refuse," Beatrix said. "Nobody here would think less of you."

"Yes they would. I would." Dov's laugh was short and wet. "You know what I figured out? Downriver, doing their math, I told myself I was protecting you. Keeping you small so they'd stay off you. That was the story I sold myself." He shook his head. "This is different. This time I actually get to protect you."

"How?"

"By being worth following." He held out his hand, and after a moment Beatrix took it, and Neshi saw two people shake hands over everything that had broken between them, which was not forgiveness exactly, but was the ground forgiveness gets built on.

* * *

Dawn came the color of dishwater, and the river had risen another arm's length overnight. The boat sat low at the mooring, impatient, tugging at its lines like a dog that smells weather.

They loaded in silence. Decoy papers into Dov's satchel — the false ones, indistinguishable even from an inch away. Fuel. Food. The real claim, warm against Neshi's ribs where she'd strapped it inside her coat, next to her heart, because Ilya had said *who carries it matters* and she intended to be mattering.

Maren came down to the landing leaning on her stick, and behind her half the village had gathered, quiet in the rain, the way people gather when they know that some departures are also arguments with fate.

Maren took Neshi's face in both hands. Her palms smelled of tea and lamp oil.

"My granddaughter," she said, "is going to give a dead country back its name." Her voice didn't waver, but her thumbs pressed hard against Neshi's cheekbones, once, like a stamp on wax. "Come back with the receipt."

Then she turned to Beatrix, and Neshi saw something pass between the old woman and the storm chaser that had no words in it — or rather, had too many. *You owe us. We chose you anyway.* Both sentences at once.

"Keep her alive," Maren said. "The papers too, if it's convenient."

"It won't be convenient," Beatrix said. "But yes."

* * *

Dov cast off first.

His boat sat heavy in the current, riding low with ballast they'd added to make him look like a man carrying treasure instead of recipes. At the tiller he looked older and calmer than Neshi had ever seen him, a good man finally doing honest math. As he swung out into the channel he raised one hand — not waving, just *raised*, the way you acknowledge a lighthouse — and then the rain took him, first his outline, then his engine note, then the wake, until there was only the river and the argument it was having with itself.

"He'll make it," Ilya said. It wasn't quite a question and wasn't quite a belief. It was the sound a man makes when he decides to spend hope before he's earned it.

"He'd better," Beatrix said, and pushed off.

* * *

The first hour was easy, and Neshi distrusted it completely.

Easy water before the gorge was the river lying to you, her grandfather used to say; the current was just getting your weight settled wrong. She took them up the eastern channel where the floodwater ran fastest but the rocks were ones she'd known since childhood, calling the marks before they appeared — *submerged fencepost, left; gravel bar gone under; eddy behind the shrine rock, use it* — and felt the boat come alive under her hands in a way it never had under anyone else's.

Beatrix stood at the bow bailing when she wasn't needed elsewhere, and somewhere in the second hour she stopped bailing and just stood there, watching Neshi work.

"What?" Neshi said finally.

"Nothing. It's just—" Beatrix gestured vaguely at the whole gray roaring world. "I've chased storms for eleven years. Paid other people to drive while I photographed the sky. First time I've ever been the passenger." She said it lightly, but Neshi heard what was underneath it, the same thing underneath everything Beatrix said lately: *I have spent my whole life watching weather instead of trusting people, and I am learning a new language at gunpoint.*

"Well," Neshi said, "passengers bail."

Beatrix laughed — a real one, startled out of her — and picked up the bucket.

* * *

By noon the monsoon arrived for real, and the world went vertical.

Rain didn't fall anymore; it *arrived*, in sheets, in walls, hammering the river flat and then raising it, and the eastern channel closed its mouth in front of them like a door. Neshi fought them west through water she barely recognized, landmarks drowned or moved, the gorge walls rising on either side dripping green, and the sound — the sound was a cathedral organ played by something with no interest in music.

"We can't stop!" she shouted over it. "If we tie off, the flood takes the boat off the rocks!"

"Then we don't stop!" Beatrix shouted back, and Neshi saw her grip the rail and grin, actually *grin*, spray in her teeth, and thought: *this is what she was for, all along. Not the smiling men with their inventory lists. This.*

Ilya crouched in the stern with the oilcloth packet strapped to his chest now, doubled-protected, one arm locked through the rail, and he was reciting something — Neshi realized with a jolt that it was the claim, the names, all of them, over and over, the way you'd hold a rope. Keeping the village aboard by keeping its names aboard.

A wave came over the bow sideways and took the bail bucket.

Another took Beatrix nearly to the hips, and Neshi's hands did the work while her mind did the terrible quick arithmetic of love: *if she goes over, I turn the boat, and if I turn the boat, we lose the channel, and if we lose the channel—*

Beatrix hauled herself back, spat out half the gorge, and yelled, "IS THAT ALL IT HAS?"

The river, offended, answered.

* * *

They came through the worst of it near dusk, into a long flooded reach where the water spread wide and slow and deceitful among drowned trees, and the three of them sat gasping in the relative quiet like survivors of a wedding.

Neshi's arms shook. She let them. Nobody was watching who hadn't earned the sight.

Then Beatrix, still breathing hard, said: "Dov should be hitting the lower narrows about now."

Silence. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

"If they take him," Ilya said quietly, "they'll find the recipes, and they'll know we split, and they'll know the real papers are upstream with us."

"They'll know," Beatrix agreed. "Tomorrow. Maybe the day after. By then—" she looked upriver, where the drowned summit waited somewhere beyond the darkening water, its vault already breached, its enemy already inside— "by then we'll be standing in front of Merrit Vane with the only documents in the world that can end her."

"And if the river takes us first?" Neshi asked.

Beatrix pulled the compass from her coat. Its needle strained toward the summit, steady now, certain, done wandering. She looked at it a long moment, then at Neshi, then at Ilya, and something in her face settled like sediment clearing.

"Then the river keeps us," she said, "with everything else it keeps. But it won't. I've been running from creditors my whole adult life, and I know exactly how much fight is left in a cornered thing." She snapped the compass shut. "Get us to the registry, pilot. We've got a country to return."

Neshi put the tiller over, and the boat leaned into the current, and ahead of them the gorge narrowed into darkness where lantern light — far off, small, and utterly wrong for empty wilderness — flickered on the water.

Someone was already at the summit. And they were expecting company.
