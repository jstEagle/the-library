## 16. White Water

The sky had been threatening all morning, but the monsoon did not threaten. It simply arrived.

One moment the gorge walls were dripping green and the river ran brown and fast; the next, the world turned to a single vertical sheet of water, as if some enormous hand above them had upended a bucket the size of heaven. The rain hit the deck so hard it bounced. Neshi screamed something that the wind tore apart before it reached Beatrix's ears. Ilya was already moving, lashing down the oilskin bundle that held Maren's claim papers with the methodical desperation of a man who had spent his life protecting things from fire and now had to protect them from water.

"Get her nose into it!" Beatrix shouted, hauling the wheel hard over. The *Kestrel* — Dov's boat, Dov's absence still an open wound in the crew — swung her bow upstream into the current just as the first real wave came down the gorge.

It was not a wave, really. It was the river changing its mind.

The Karvathi gorge in dry season was a corridor of brown glass, deep and slow, walled in by cliffs so sheer that the sun touched the water only at noon. In monsoon it became something else entirely: a beast with too much blood in its body, thrashing against stone. The flood surge came down from the high country in pulses, each one taller than the last, and where the gorge narrowed at the Serrated Teeth — three granite fangs the old charts marked with a warning in four languages, two of them dead — the pulses turned into standing waves that no sane pilot would attempt.

Beatrix had chased storms for eleven years. She had driven a reinforced truck through a hailstorm in the Salt Flats while lightning walked the ground around her like a curious animal. She had stood on a levee in the Vane lowlands and watched a wall of water come for a town she couldn't save. None of it had prepared her for being *inside* the weather instead of beside it, for the moment when the storm stopped being a thing you studied and became a thing that was studying you back.

The *Kestrel* took the first pulse on the bow and climbed it like a hill. For one suspended second they were airborne — engine screaming, hull groaning, the whole river visible below them, churning white between black rocks — and then they came down and the impact went through Beatrix's spine like a struck bell.

"Bilge!" she roared.

Neshi was already there, the hand pump clattering, spitting water over the side in silver arcs. The old leak they'd patched below the Diaspora Village had opened again; Beatrix could hear it hissing under the deck plates, a sound like the river whispering its intentions.

Ilya appeared beside her at the wheel, soaked through, hair plastered to his skull, glasses long since lost somewhere in the first hour of the storm. Without them his face looked younger and more naked, the eyes huge and dark in a face gone gray with cold. He put one hand on the wheel beside hers, not to steer — he didn't know how — but to be there.

"The papers?" she shouted.

"Safe! Lashed under the thwart!" His voice cracked on the word *safe*, or maybe that was just the wind bending it.

The Serrated Teeth were ahead now, maybe four hundred meters, though distance meant nothing in this chaos. The chart said keep left. The chart also assumed a river that obeyed charts. The left channel was a boiling cauldron of reflected current bouncing off the first fang; the right channel was narrower but smoother, except for a submerged shelf the surveyors had noted with the laconic dread of men who'd measured a graveyard: *rock, 1.5 fathoms, wreckage present.*

Beatrix chose right. She chose it because the *Kestrel* was half-full of water and needed speed more than margin, and because every instinct she'd ever honed chasing storms told her that the smooth-looking option in a flood is the one that kills you — but between two ways to die, take the one that lets you keep your head above water longest.

"Hold on," she said, to no one in particular, to everyone, to herself.

They went in.

* * *

Later, Beatrix would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. Memory broke them into shards, each one lit like a lightning flash:

— The bow rising, rising, impossibly, until the sky filled the whole windshield and the rain fell upward past her face.

— Neshi's pump handle snapping, the girl throwing the broken half aside and grabbing the bail bucket without missing a beat, bailing with both arms like a woman fighting a housefire.

— The rock. Not seen — *felt*, a shudder through the keel, a grinding note that went up through the hull and into Beatrix's teeth, and then the horrible lightness of a boat lifting free, spinning, the wheel dead in her hands because the rudder was no longer in the water.

— The thwart cover tearing loose.

She saw it happen as if from very far away: the oilskin bundle, Maren's claim papers, forty years of a nation's hope sewn into waxed canvas, sliding across the tilting deck, hitting the gunwale, and going over.

No hesitation. That was the thing she would remember most, afterward. There was no moment of decision in him at all. Ilya Senn, who weighed nothing, who swam like a sack of books, who had confessed on their first night out that deep water frightened him more than any archive fire ever could — Ilya went over the side after the papers the way other men flinched. Instantly. Completely. As if the alternative had never existed.

"Ilya!"

The river took him before the word finished leaving her mouth.

* * *

What do you do when the person you cannot lose goes into floodwater in a gorge? You do the arithmetic nobody should ever have to do. Beatrix did it in half a heartbeat, standing at the wheel of a spinning, flooding boat with a sixteen-year-old girl bailing behind her and a drowned nation's papers receding downstream inside a man's death-grip.

Turn after him, and lose the boat, and lose Neshi, and lose everything — the vault, the permits, the deadline, the whole desperate architecture of hope they'd built since the Diaspora Village. Let him go, and —

There was no *and*. That was the discovery. She stood at the wheel with her hands frozen and searched for the other branch of the equation and found that it did not exist. She would trade the vault. She would trade the deeds, the census rolls, the names, the whole resurrected country, every scrap of it, without a second thought, without a *first* thought, for one gaunt archivist with no glasses and terrible swimming.

The knowledge arrived not as a realization but as a detonation, silent under the roar of the water, rearranging everything.

Then her hands moved.

"Neshi! Coil line — the stern painter — now!"

The girl moved like she'd been drilled, which, Beatrix realized, she had — by a village that lived on a river and buried its children who hesitated. Neshi got the coil loose and Beatrix knotted one end around her own waist, then around the base of the wheel, a double hitch her father had taught her against exactly this kind of day, though he'd taught it for mooring, not for leaping into a monsoon gorge after a man she'd known for three weeks and would know for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be.

"Keep her pointed downstream if you can! Don't fight the spin — just keep us from the walls!"

And Beatrix Dunmore, debt-ridden, betrayed, half-drowned already by spray, dove into the Karvathi gorge in full flood after a drowning man.

* * *

The water was not cold. That was the horror of it — it was warm, blood-warm, silt-warm, a living thing, and it closed around her like a fist made of muscle. The current did not push her; it *decided* her, picked her up the way a gull picks up a fish, and suddenly she was moving faster than the boat, faster than anything, tumbling, the surface a strobing ceiling of white and gray overhead.

She found him by accident. Or the river, having made its point, relented.

His shoulder struck hers in the churn — a bone-jarring collision — and she grabbed cloth, then wrist, then locked her arm around his chest from behind the way the safety posters in every river town showed and no one ever believed they'd need. He was clutching the oilskin bundle against his body with both arms, wrapped around it like a mother, like a coffin-bearer, and even half-conscious, even blue-lipped, his grip did not loosen.

*I will not let go,* his eyes said, before they rolled back. *Of either.*

The line snapped taut. The *Kestrel* — Neshi, bless her, Neshi holding a spinning boat alone in the teeth of a monsoon — began to drag them both, and the gorge walls blurred past, and Beatrix kicked for the surface with a stranger's weight chained to her chest and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like laughter: *this is what it's like, this is what people mean, this is the thing in the songs.*

Not peace. Not softness. Adrenaline and terror and the absolute refusal to open her arm.

Her head broke the surface. She got one breath — half air, half river — and then a wave folded over them both. The line hauled. Somewhere above, Neshi was screaming words that were probably prayers in Karvathi, a language Beatrix didn't speak and was beginning, anyway, to understand.

Hand over hand along the line. Ten meters that took a year. The hull loomed out of the spray, barnacled and groaning, and Beatrix got one arm over the gunwale and held on and held Ilya and held on, and Neshi's small strong hands closed in her collar, and together — three of them, soaked and sobbing and laughing, actually laughing — they hauled him aboard like a landed fish, like a miracle, like cargo worth more than any vault.

He wasn't breathing.

* * *

Beatrix had never done this on a living person. She'd sat through the coastal safety course hungover, annoyed, twenty-two years old, certain the sea had nothing to teach her. Now her hands remembered what her pride had forgotten: tilt the head, pinch the nose, breathe — *breathe for him, damn you* — press, press, press, the rhythm of it drummed into her by some patient instructor whose name she'd never learned and would now remember forever.

The oilskin bundle lay between them on the deck, streaming water, intact.

On the fourth cycle, Ilya Senn coughed up half the Karvathi river and lived.

He breathed in great ragged whoops, curled on his side, one hand still hooked in the strap of the bundle — even unconscious of everything else, even emptied of air and dignity, he had not let go of the papers. Beatrix pressed her forehead against his wet shoulder and shook, and it took her a moment to understand that the shaking was hers.

"You absolute *idiot*," she said, and her voice broke in the middle of it, and she said it again, softer, which was somehow worse: "You absolute idiot."

"They were going to be lost." His voice was a ruin, scraped raw by river. "Maren's — the village's — everything they are is in there. Everything they *were*. I'm —" he coughed, spat, managed a ghost of his pedantic smile — "I'm replaceable, Beatrix. Archives aren't."

"Wrong." She said it flatly, before she could stop herself, before any part of her brain that calculated costs and odds and thirty-day deadlines could intervene. "You're wrong. Completely wrong. I'd trade the whole vault for you. Every deed, every name, all of it. I'd row away right now and never look back."

The rain hammered down. Neshi, wisely, had found something urgent to do at the bow.

Ilya stared at her. Water ran off his chin. His eyes — enormous without the glasses, unarmored, the eyes of a man who had spent his whole life looking at other people's records because no one had ever written one about him — went wide, then wider, then soft in a way that undid her more than the near-drowning had.

"That's the most reckless thing I've ever heard," he whispered. "And I've read the concession law."

She laughed — a wild, cracked, adrenaline-laugh that turned halfway through into something else, something that had been waiting under her ribs since a boathouse door and a stranger who read seals. She put her hand against his cheek, cold as the river, alive, *alive*, and said the rest of it out loud because the storm had already stripped away every place to hide:

"I love you. There. Said it in a monsoon, in a gorge, over a drowned man's coughing. No romance in the world gets worse conditions than that, so whatever you answer, I'll know it's honest."

He answered by pulling her down and kissing her, tasting of river and wax and forty years of exile ending, and the *Kestrel* wallowed in the flooded gorge with a broken pump and a cracked hull and a dead rudder, and neither of them cared for exactly eleven seconds.

Then Neshi shouted, "The papers — are they *dry?*" — and the spell broke into laughter, and the three of them fell to work, because the river does not grant honeymoons and the monsoon peak was two days out and the summit was still above them, waiting.

* * *

They ran aground at dusk on a gravel bar beneath a cliff overhang, wedged the *Kestrel* against the stones, and took stock. Hull split along two planks below the waterline — patchable, barely, with the canvas and pitch they had left. Rudder sprung. Pump broken. Engine sputtering on fouled fuel. And the papers — Beatrix unwrapped the oilskin with the reverence of a surgeon — damp at the edges, dry at the heart. Maren's signatures legible. The village seal clear. The claim intact.

"We can't run the Teeth again," Neshi said, wrapping herself in the one dry blanket. "Not this hull. Not in this water."

"No," Beatrix agreed. Her mind was already moving, storm-chaser's mind, reading the landscape the way she used to read cloud towers. Above the cliff overhang, a goat track switchbacked up toward a ridgeline she'd marked on the chart days ago. And beyond the ridge — "There's a portage trail. Old survey map showed it. Villagers used it before the eviction. It comes out above the Teeth, at the head of the last reach."

"In this rain?"

"In any rain." She looked at Ilya, gray-lipped under the blanket, alive, watching her with an expression she intended to spend the rest of her life earning. "We haul her over."

He smiled — exhausted, incredulous, certain. "Then we haul."

Far upriver, muffled by rain and distance, a sound rolled down the gorge that none of them could place at first: a low, grinding boom, like a door closing underground. Neshi went still. Ilya sat up.

"Smoke," he said quietly, pointing to the ridge against the bruised sky, where a thin dark thread rose from the summit country and bent in the wind. "Do you smell smoke?"

Beatrix breathed in. Under the rain and the river-mud, faint but unmistakable, the gorge carried the smell of burning paper.

Two days to expiry. And someone, somewhere above them, was feeding a fire.
