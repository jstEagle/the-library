## 17. The Registry Burns

Ilya smelled it before he saw it.

They had been climbing since dawn, the boat abandoned two bends below where the river turned to white rope and the gorge walls closed in like a book deciding whether to shut. Neshi went first, sure-footed on the wet granite, her grandmother's claim papers wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. Beatrix came behind, hauling the chest's iron frame on a travois of salvaged oars, her knuckles split from three days of bailing and portaging. And Ilya brought up the rear, as he always did, because someone had to guard the rear, and because at forty-one years old he had finally learned that his body was better suited to guarding than leading.

The smell reached him on a switchback above the treeline: char and something sweeter under it, the way paper burns when it has been stored long enough to drink its own history. Vellum. Leather spines. The particular ghost of ink heated past its patience.

He stopped walking.

"Ilya." Beatrix's voice, flat with warning.

He was already moving. He climbed past Neshi, past the last stand of cloud-forest pine, his lungs tearing, his boots slipping on scree that had no business being under anyone's feet, and came over the shoulder of the summit ridge into a bowl of thin morning light—

—and there it was.

The Karvath Summit Registry had been built, according to every document he'd ever smuggled out of the colonial archive, as a customs house for an empire that no longer remembered why it had come. Grey stone, quarried local, its lintel carved with the double-crown of Karvath defaced but not destroyed—the chisel marks themselves were eighty years old, and beneath them you could still trace the original lines if you knew how to look, which Ilya did, which was the whole of his life's useless expertise. It had stood empty for four decades. A dead building keeping dead paperwork in a country that officially did not exist.

Now it stood open to the sky.

The roof had gone entirely. The floor was a lake of ash and half-burned ledger boards, still steaming, still breathing thin grey smoke into air so cold it made the smoke look like the building's soul leaving through its own mouth. Along one wall, the shelving—iron, thank God, iron does not burn—stood in rows like ribs, and hanging from those ribs were the blackened corners of things that had once been books. Here and there a spine survived entire, too waterlogged or too dense to catch, and Ilya found himself cataloguing them automatically, the way a drowning man counts the bubbles: *Registry of Concessions, 1911. Land Rolls, Eastern Provinces. Census, Third Enumeration.* Names. All of it names. A nation is not its gold; a nation is its names, written down somewhere safe enough to outlast the people who wrote them.

Someone had made very sure they did not outlast anything.

"They were here," Neshi said. She had come up beside him without sound; she moved like Maren, like all the village women, as though the ground were a courtesy extended to her personally. "How long?"

Ilya crouched and pressed his palm flat against the ash. Warm. Not hot. He looked at the rain-pools already forming in the burned roof-timbers, at the way the ash had begun to clump and darken with moisture.

"Last night," he said. "Maybe twelve hours. They waited for the storm to cover their approach and left before dawn." He picked up a scrap of iron banding, stamped with the Vane Syndicate's smiling-scales mark, and let it fall. "They wanted us to find it exactly like this."

* * *

Beatrix set down the travois with a crack of splitting wood and walked the registry's perimeter the way she read weather—not quickly, not emotionally, reading the structure of the thing. Ilya watched her do it and felt the strange doubled grief he always felt around her: grief for the burning, and underneath it, quieter, the fear that he was watching the only family he had ever assembled begin to understand what it had cost them.

"Tell me the law again," she said finally. She didn't turn around. "All of it. Slowly."

"The Concession Act of 1909," Ilya said. His voice sounded far away to himself, the voice of a man reciting from inside a burned house. "Colonial instrument. It grants extraction rights to any holder of a valid concession permit within the Karvathi summit zone—but the permits carry an expiry clause. Article Fourteen. If the vault and its contents are not formally claimed by a permit-holder before the monsoon peak—" he swallowed "—the contents revert to the recognized heirs of the sovereign territory. Which is to say, to Karvathi survivors. To Maren's people. To no one else, because everyone else's claim dies with the permits."

"And how do you contest a permit? How do you assert the reversion?"

"You file at the registry." He heard his own voice go dry. "In person. At the summit registry. Before expiry."

The silence that followed had weather in it. Above the ridge, the clouds had organized themselves into the long grey shelves that meant the monsoon's true arrival was hours away, maybe less—a front like a held breath, waiting to be released across the whole watershed at once.

"So they didn't just burn records," Beatrix said. "They burned the *door*. There's nowhere left to file. Nowhere legal. The moment we can't contest their claim, their permits are the only ones standing, and when the peak passes—"

"No." Ilya stood. His knees hurt. Everything hurt; it had been hurting for days and he had made a private treaty with the pain, and now the treaty was collapsing. "No, you have it backwards, and it matters that you have it backwards."

She turned then. Rain had started—not the monsoon yet, just the prologue, fat cold drops striking the ash and raising small grey ghosts. Her face was hollowed out with exhaustion, and underneath the exhaustion something was moving that he recognized, because he had carried it for twenty years: the specific despair of a person who has done everything correctly and watched it burn anyway.

"Explain it to me," she said, "like I'm stupid. Please. I need one thing today to be simple."

"It isn't simple. But listen." He crossed the ash to her, his boots printing black on black. "Article Fourteen doesn't say the reversion happens when someone files. It says the reversion happens when the monsoon peak *arrives*, full stop. Automatic. Built into the instrument itself, because the drafters never imagined a world where the heirs would be scattered across nine borders and the registry would be the only door." He held up a hand before she could speak. "Filing is how you *prove* it. Filing is evidence. But the expiry itself—it's a fact of the calendar, Beatrix. It doesn't require a clerk. It requires a date."

Neshi had come closer, the oilcloth bundle hugged to her chest, her eyes going between them. "Then what did they buy by burning this place?"

"Time and doubt," Ilya said. "If there's no registry, there's no witness, no record, no counter-signature. When the syndicate's lawyers arrive after the peak, they will say the reversion never occurred, because nothing was filed, because filing was impossible, because *someone burned the registry*, and who could prove they didn't burn it themselves?" He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "It's elegant. It's the most elegant thing the Syndicate has ever done. They haven't stolen the vault. They've stolen the *proof*."

* * *

They took shelter in what remained of the registry's eastern annex—a stone-vaulted records cellar whose iron door had been locked and then simply abandoned, because fire from above could not reach down into it. Inside, the air was cold and mineral and utterly still, and the shelves held perhaps two hundred documents that the arsonists had either missed or considered beneath contempt: duplicate filings, carbon copies, a clerk's working drafts from the 1950s, the administrative sediment that accumulates under every bureaucracy like silt under a bridge.

Ilya lit their single candle and sat among the sediment and felt, for the first time since the boathouse, absolutely nothing.

It was not despair exactly. Despair he knew intimately; despair was an old colleague, they had shared rooms for years. This was different. This was the silence after the archive expelled him, the silence of the morning he had packed his one suitcase of contraband notes and understood that everything he had spent his life protecting was now, formally, nobody's business. He had thought he had burned that feeling off on the river. Apparently feelings, like documents, kept duplicates.

Footsteps in ash. Beatrix lowered herself onto an overturned drawer beside him, close enough that he could see the salt dried white on her jacket where the sweat had run.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "my mother's boat went down in the Kessel squall. Not lost—sunk at anchor, insured, recoverable. And the surveyor came, and he looked at it, and he said the mooring failure was 'consistent with owner negligence,' which meant the insurance paid nothing, which meant the debt started there. With a word. *Consistent.* One word and twenty years of my life became collateral." She turned her hands over, looked at the blisters. "I've spent my whole life losing arguments to people who write the definitions. So believe me when I tell you I understand exactly what they did up there. They didn't burn paper. They burned the dictionary."

"Yes," Ilya said.

"But you're sitting here like a man at a funeral, and funerals are for finished things." She looked at him directly, and her eyes in the candlelight were the grey-green of river water over slate. "So tell me why you're wrong."

Despite everything, despite the ash and the cold and the twenty years, Ilya felt his mouth twitch. "You're asking me to argue myself out of grieving?"

"I'm asking you to do your job. You're the only archivist left in this entire story. Grieve later. Right now, tell me why a burned registry is not the end."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he got up, because standing helped him think, and began walking the cellar shelves with the candle, trailing his fingers along the document spines the way other men touch railings in storms.

"Because archives are not buildings," he said. "That's the first lesson they teach you and the first lesson they punish you for believing. An archive is a *practice*. It's a chain of custody. The building is just where the chain happens to be anchored on a given day." He pulled a folder free—water-stained, rat-nibbled, glorious. "This is a duplicate filing of the 1953 concession renewal. Worthless on its own. But it references the original 1909 instrument, and it was *countersigned by a colonial magistrate*, which means a copy exists in the magistrate's personal papers, which means—"

"Which means the chain didn't start here," Beatrix said slowly. "It runs backward. Downriver. Into every office that ever touched these papers."

"Into every office, every notary, every magistrate's attic, every missionary's trunk, every diaspora family that carried a land deed out in a false-bottomed suitcase because it was the only piece of Karvath they could hold." His voice was rising and he let it rise. "They burned one room, Beatrix. One room! I have spent twenty years memorizing where the duplicates live. That was my crime, in the end—that I mapped the redundancy of the empire's own paperwork. They expelled me for knowing too much about copies." He turned to face her, and the candle flame bent in the draft from the stairwell, and he heard himself say the thing he had not dared to say even to himself on the river:

"The registry is gone. Fine. The registry was always a fiction of convenience. What we need before the peak is not a filing. We need a *witness with standing*. Someone empowered to observe the expiry and attest to it. Under the Act, that's any licensed notary operating within the concession zone."

Beatrix went very still. "And how many licensed notaries are operating within the concession zone right now, in the middle of a monsoon, in a drowned country?"

"One," Ilya said. "Give or take."

* * *

Neshi found them an hour later. She had been outside, reading the weather the way her people read everything—as correspondence, as letter from a living relative—and she came down the cellar stairs with rain streaming off her and an expression Ilya had learned to dread, because it was Maren's expression, the one that preceded unwelcome arithmetic.

"The front breaks tonight," she said. "Full peak tomorrow, midday, maybe earlier. After that the gorge is impassable for a week and the vault entrance is under six meters of new water." She set down a coil of rope and looked at them both. "And there's a boat coming up the gorge. Two engines. Running dark."

Beatrix was on her feet before the sentence ended. "Syndicate?"

"Syndicate shape. Syndicate speed." Neshi's jaw tightened. "Merrit Vane doesn't send two engines unless Merrit Vane is on board."

Ilya stood in the candlelight among the empire's forgotten carbons and felt the fear arrive on schedule and, beside it, something harder and more stubborn, something that had been growing in him since a woman with a bronze chest had failed to sell it when selling was the sane choice.

"They're coming to be present at the expiry," he said. "Of course they are. They burned the registry, but they know Article Fourteen as well as I do—they wrote half of it. If the reversion is automatic, then the only way to poison it is to be *there*, at the vault, with their own witnesses and their own guns, and construct a record saying the peak passed with no valid contestation." He looked at Beatrix. "They're not chasing us anymore. They're racing us to a signing."

"Then we sign first," Beatrix said.

"With what notary?" Neshi asked. "With what seal? With what—"

"With whatever the river gives us." Beatrix was already moving, gathering rope, checking the oilcloth, doing the thousand small violent efficiencies of a woman who had decided. But she paused at the foot of the stairs, her back to them, rain-light silvering her shoulders, and said quietly, almost to herself:

"My grandmother came out of Karvath with one suitcase and a compass. Everyone always asked her why she kept the compass and sold everything else." She turned, and there was something in her face Ilya had never seen there—not bravado, not the debt-fighter's grin, but certainty, raw and untested, like a blade before its first edge. "Tomorrow we find out what she knew."

Above them, through the registry's ruined roof, the first true sheets of the monsoon came down on the ashes, and somewhere downriver, two engines climbed toward them through the dark.
