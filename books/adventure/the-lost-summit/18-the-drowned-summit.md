## 18. The Drowned Summit

The rain had stopped pretending to be weather. It was simply the world now, a gray vertical country they climbed through, and at the top of it—where the maps said there should be a peak—there was only water.

Beatrix stood in the bow of the battered boat and looked at the drowned summit of Karvath and felt her whole life tilt sideways.

She had grown up believing mountains ended. That was what mountains did: they ended, at a summit, in sky. But the Karvathi peak had been hollowed generations ago, its heart cut out for the vault, and when the engineers of the old regime sealed it and the monsoons came year after year like patient creditors, the mountain had filled from within. Now the summit basin was a lake with no name on any chart, ringed by black granite shoulders, its surface shivering under rain, and somewhere beneath that green-gray skin lay the entrance to everything.

"It's deep," Neshi said beside her. She had the claim papers wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, strapped down like a heartbeat. "Maren said the water rose eleven meters after the last survey. The door was carved to be found by divers, though. The old kings thought of everything."

"The old kings," Ilya murmured from the stern, "thought of everything except staying alive." He was checking their gear with those long archivist's hands, methodical as prayer: lines, lamps, the spare regulator, the bronze chest's key-half lashed inside Beatrix's jacket where it had ridden against her ribs since the shoal. "Which is why we're here instead of them."

Beatrix didn't answer. She was watching the far shore.

There was a boat there. Low, dark-hulled, engines idling under a tarp, and figures moving along the granite shelf with the unhurried economy of people who owned whatever they touched. Even through the rain she knew the posture. She'd spent thirty days being inventoried by that posture.

"They're already here," she said.

* * *

They anchored in the lee of a shattered colonnade—the old processional way, Ilya said, his voice gone strange with reverence, where pilgrims had once walked dry-shod toward the summit registry—and Beatrix suited up in silence while the rain drummed on the water like fingers on a coffin lid.

Dov was two days downriver with decoy papers and a syndicate channel feeding him lies. Every hour she didn't think about him was an hour she failed; every hour she did think about him was worse. So she thought about the water instead. Cold, pressure, air. Things with rules.

"Beatrix." Ilya caught her wrist before she stepped off the gunwale. His face was pale under the hood, rain running off his chin. "If Merrit is already here—"

"Then we're late, and being late has never once stopped me."

"That's not what I was going to say." His grip tightened. "I was going to say: whatever you find down there, whoever you find—you come back up. The papers don't need you to be brave. They need you to be alive at the expiry bell."

For a moment she let herself look at him—gaunt, soaked, expelled from everything he'd ever loved for asking one stubborn question about a dead country—and she felt the thing she'd been refusing to name all the way upriver turn over in her chest like a boat taking water.

"I'll come back up," she said. "Hold my line."

He held her line. She went down.

* * *

The lake swallowed the light in layers. First the green of rain-fed surface, then a bruised teal, then a darkness with texture to it—silt hanging like breath in cold air, the beams of their lamps carving tunnels that closed behind them. Neshi swam at her shoulder, a slim shadow trailing bubbles. Below them the drowned colonnade continued, pillars descending into nothing, each one furred with pale silt-fungus, and Beatrix had the vertiginous sense of swimming down through the throat of a country.

Then the door.

It emerged from the dark the way a whale surfaces: vast before you understand its shape. A wall of fitted stone, forty feet high if it was an inch, carved with the seal of Karvath—the river-and-mountain crest she'd first seen stamped in bronze on a salvaged chest, the crest that had pulled at her like a hook set under the skin. Here it was rendered ten meters tall, wings of water pouring eternally down its face, and at its center, a lock mechanism the size of a wagon wheel. Two keyholes. One of them already occupied.

A steel rod jutted from the lower aperture, bolted in place, syndicate-manufactured, ugly as a splinter in a hymn. Half a key. Someone had gotten here first with half an answer and was waiting for the other half.

Neshi grabbed her arm and pointed.

Lights below. Moving. Not lamp-beams—lantern-glow, warm and steady, spilling up from a ledge cut into the rock beside the great door, an antechamber that must once have housed the door-wardens. Air pocket. Dry ground. And standing on it, visible through the wavering curtain of the exit current, a woman in a dry coat, hands folded, looking directly up at them as if she'd been watching their descent the whole way down.

Merrit Vane was smiling.

* * *

The antechamber was a low vaulted room, half-flooded, its floor a mosaic of the river-and-mountain crest worn nearly smooth by centuries of feet. Beatrix came up the stone steps out of the water with her knife drawn and her lungs burning and found four syndicate men flanking the chamber, rifles slung but hands near, and Merrit Vane seated on a warden's bench like a hostess at her own party, a thermos of tea steaming gently beside her.

"Miss Dunmore." Her voice carried the same terrible cheer it always had, bright as brass in the wet dark. "You look dreadful. Sit down, have some tea. You've had an awful journey—I've had reports the whole way, you know. Dov is a dear man. Terrible liar, but a dear man."

Beatrix kept the knife low and her voice level. "Where are my people?"

"Downriver, presumably, delivering your decoy papers to a registry office that burned down three days ago." Merrit poured a second cup anyway, set it on the bench beside her. Steam curled up and died. "Oh, don't make that face. Nobody's hurt. We don't hurt people, Beatrix—we invoice them. There's a difference, and the difference is that everyone stays smiling."

"You burned the registry."

"We tidied it." Merrit spread her hands. "Permits can only be contested in person, at the vault, before expiry. You knew that. Ilya Senn told you—he's very thorough, our disgraced archivist. So we removed the paperwork and left the contest. In person. Here. Which is where you are. Do you see how generous I'm being? I've saved you the walk."

Neshi surfaced at the pool's edge, gasping, and one of the men moved to intercept her and Merrit clicked her tongue and he stopped. "Let the girl up. She's a Karvathi. This is practically her living room."

"What do you want, Merrit."

"The same thing I wanted at your boathouse, when you lied to me so beautifully over tea—I've thought about that lie since, honestly, it was a masterpiece, you should be proud. I want the chest opened. I want what's in the vault appraised, catalogued, and claimed under concession permit 114-K before the monsoon peak voids it." She checked a pocket watch, sighed happily. "Thirty-one hours, give or take. And for that, Miss Dunmore, I need the other half of the key."

"We don't have it."

"No," Merrit agreed pleasantly. "You have something better. You've had it your whole life, apparently, which is the part I find genuinely delightful." She nodded to one of her men. "Show her."

The man reached into a waterproof case and brought out a document folder, and from the folder a photograph, yellowed, creased, decades old. He handed it to Beatrix because Merrit told him to, and Beatrix took it because her body obeyed before her mind could vote.

A woman stood on the steps of this very antechamber, young, dark-haired, laughing, one hand raised against the camera. Around her neck on a cord hung a compass—a heavy brass thing with a hinged cover and a star-shaped escutcheon.

Beatrix knew the photograph. It had lived in her mother's drawer her whole childhood, unlabeled, unexplained. She knew the compass better. It lived in her pocket now, or it had until the gorge rapids tried to drown her and she'd lashed it inside her jacket next to the chest's key-half, because losing it had never once been an option, not through debt, not through storms, not through any of it. Her grandmother's compass. The one fixed point of her inheritance from a woman who never spoke about where she came from.

"The chest you dredged from the shoal," Merrit said, watching her with bright, patient eyes, "is the left hand of the lock. Your compass is the right. Karvath split its key in two when the vault was sealed—one half to the state, one half to the royal cartographer's line. The cartographer's daughter was evacuated west in the last month of the war. Her name was Dunmore before it was anything else." She let that sit in the dripping silence. "Your grandmother, Beatrix. You've been carrying the key to Karvath around your neck since you were six years old. You just never asked her what it opened. Or maybe she never told you. Either way—" she opened her palms, radiant, reasonable, monstrous— "you're not a debtor. You're a national treasure. Isn't that wonderful?"

The room tilted. Beatrix heard the water moving in the flooded doorway, the eternal pour of it down the great seal, and underneath it her own blood, loud, and underneath that a memory rising like silt stirred from the bottom: her grandmother's hands closing hers over the compass, saying, in the accent the neighbors teased and the family never explained, *this points home, little bird, even when home is underwater.*

She had thought it a figure of speech. Grandmothers were full of them.

Home was underwater. She was standing in it.

"Give me the compass," Merrit said gently, "and the debt dies. All of it. Dov's sister's debt too—it's a package, I'm feeling festive. You walk away rich in the only currency I deal in: nothing owed. And Karvath gets its vault opened by its own blood, which will look beautiful in the filings. Everyone smiles. That's all I've ever wanted, Beatrix. Everyone, smiling."

Beatrix looked at the photograph. At the laughing girl with the compass. At the mosaic under her boots, the crest worn smooth by the feet of people whose names were in the shelves below her, in census rolls and land deeds, in a country that had been erased from the maps but apparently not from her own jacket pocket.

Her whole life she had thought she was running from debt. Storm-chasing, gear hocked, boats borrowed, always one payment behind, always owing someone cheerful. She had thought the compass pointed north and meant nothing.

It had never pointed north. She'd known that, actually—she'd known it for years and called it a flaw, a broken heirloom, a quirk. It swung hard east whenever she crossed the river. She'd blamed the casing.

It wasn't broken. It had been pointing at this door her entire life.

"No," Beatrix said.

Merrit's smile stayed exactly where it was, which was how Beatrix knew it had never been a smile at all, only a shape she wore the way other women wore coats. "No?"

"You want the key? Come take it." She backed toward the water, knife up now, voice steadier than her hands. "But understand what you're filing, Merrit. If the vault opens, it opens for the claim papers too. My grandmother's blood doesn't sign your permit. It contests it."

Something moved behind Merrit's eyes—not anger, worse: recalculation. "Thirty hours," she said softly. "The monsoon peak comes through this gorge like a freight train, and when it does, every unclaimed thing in this mountain reverts to a nation that no longer exists—which means it reverts to whoever's holding its papers. You're standing in the only room in the world where that could be you." She picked up her tea. "I do hope you can hold your breath, Miss Dunmore. Both of you. The inner chambers flood at peak. The old kings designed it that way—anyone inside when the waters rise stays inside."

Behind her, in the great door, the syndicate's steel half-key sat in its lock like a splinter working deeper.

And beneath Beatrix's jacket, against her heart, the compass swung in its case—east, east, east—straining toward the door like a dog at the end of a rope, like a name trying to be remembered, like home calling through forty years of water:

*Open me.*
