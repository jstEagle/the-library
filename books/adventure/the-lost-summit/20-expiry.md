## 20. Expiry

The water came for the vault the way debt comes—slowly at first, then all at once.

Beatrix heard it before she saw it: a low, throat-deep roar rolling down from the summit gorge, the sound of a river remembering its old bed and deciding to take it back. The torchlight in the antechamber guttered sideways. Somewhere above, iron doors groaned on hinges that hadn't moved in forty years, and then the first cold tongue of floodwater slid across the stone floor and found their boots.

"Peak strike," Ilya said quietly. He had his sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink-stained fingers still hovering over an open census roll he'd been reading aloud like scripture. "Early. Or right on time. With Karvath it was always hard to tell the difference."

The syndicate's people were already moving. Two of Merrit's hired hands splashed toward the entrance tunnel, torches held high, and came back shaking their heads. "Sealed," one reported. "The grate's dropped. We're not getting out that way."

Merrit Vane stood in the middle of the drowned light with her coat hem darkening in the rising water, and she did not look afraid. That was the thing about Merrit that had frightened Beatrix since the day two smiling men inventoried her storm rig on her own dock: she never looked afraid, because she had never once needed to be. She turned to the man at her shoulder—a small, damp, precise person in a grey suit who had been introduced, with terrible courtesy, as *our notary*—and said, "Well, Master Quill. It seems we'll conduct our business indoors."

Quill nodded. He had a leather document case held above the waterline like a man carrying a cat through rain. He looked, Beatrix thought, like exactly what he was: a pen for hire, a witness whose witnessing could be purchased by the hour.

The water climbed. Ankle-deep. Then shin-deep. The vault's great chamber opened around them—shelves rising into darkness, tens of thousands of folios, deeds, rolls, registers, the whole paper skeleton of a country that maps had been paid to forget—and the flood lapped at the lowest shelf with something almost like reverence.

Neshi gripped Beatrix's arm. "The papers," the girl said. "If they go under—"

"They won't." Beatrix heard her own voice come out flat and certain, and marveled at it, because inside she was doing arithmetic of her own: the water rising maybe a hand's width every few minutes, the exit sealed, the syndicate holding three guns between them, and herself holding nothing but a dead woman's compass, a stolen half-key, and a clause she'd read so many times on the run upriver she could recite it in her sleep.

*Clause Nine,* she thought. *Come on. Clause Nine.*

Ilya caught her eye across the chamber. He didn't say anything. He just touched the breast of his coat, where Maren's claim papers rode dry against his heart, and then—he did the smallest thing. He looked at the notary. Then at Beatrix. Then at the notary again.

Oh, she thought. *Oh.*

"Merrit," Beatrix said loudly, over the water-sound. "You brought a lawyer into my grandmother's house."

Something flickered across Merrit Vane's smile—the first hairline crack Beatrix had ever seen in it. "Our claim is properly filed. Quill is here to certify possession."

"Possession." Beatrix waded forward, deliberately slow, deliberately loud, because everything now depended on being heard. "Filed under concession permits. Colonial-era. Issued by a government that hasn't existed in forty years, against a nation your own syndicate helped erase from the maps." She stopped an arm's length away, water swirling around both their knees. "Do you know what happens to those permits when the monsoon peaks? Because I do. I've read them until the words wore grooves in me."

"You're a debtor," Merrit said pleasantly. "You're a thief standing in a flooded hole with no way out. Whatever you think you're about to say, no one is going to record it."

"No?" Beatrix turned to the notary. "Master Quill. You're a sworn officer of registry, aren't you? Commissioned under the river codes?"

Quill's eyes darted to Merrit and back. "I—I am retained by the Syndicate—"

"The river codes don't retain you," Beatrix said. "They *bind* you. I grew up on boats, Master Quill. My mother ran cargo up these gorges before Karvath drowned, and she taught me the law the way other mothers teach lullabies. A commissioned notary present at the moment of a permit event is required to witness and record it. Not for whoever pays him. For the *river*. Refuse, and your commission drowns with you down here, and everyone upstairs knows why."

The water was at their thighs now. Cold. Insistent. Somewhere deep in the shelves, a folio slumped wetly off its ledge, and Ilya made a small wounded sound and caught it, and held it, and did not put it down.

Merrit laughed. It was a good laugh; it always had been. "This is charming. Beatrix. Truly. Hand over the chest and the key, and I'll have Quill witness whatever little ceremony you like on the way out. You can even tell yourself it meant something."

"It's already begun," Beatrix said. "The peak struck when the water crossed the threshold. That's the trigger condition. *Upon the seasonal high water entering the vault proper.* Your own filed claim cites it, Merrit—it's in your paperwork, because your lawyers are thorough, and thorough people read Clause Nine." She let that land. She watched Merrit's smile stop reaching her eyes. "You knew. You've known all along. That's why the gunboats herded us instead of sinking us. That's why you burned the registry—you couldn't contest the clause, so you tried to burn the only place it could be invoked. And it didn't matter, because here it is. Here *we* are. Trapped in the exact room, at the exact hour, with the exact witness the law requires."

The chamber had gone very quiet except for the water. Even Merrit's hired men had stopped moving. Torchlight shivered on the black surface stretching between them all, and in it Beatrix could see reflections: shelves, flames, faces, the whole impossible congregation of the moment.

"Quill," Merrit said, without turning her head. "Witness nothing."

And Beatrix Dunmore—who had spent thirty days being smiled at by cheerful men cataloguing her life for collateral, who had lied beautifully for the first time on her own deck with tea going cold between her and this same woman, who had watched Dov's betrayal and Ilya's near-drowning and Neshi's white-knuckled courage and had swallowed every ounce of rage and fear and called it fuel—felt the last of the swallowing finish. What was left underneath was calm. River-calm. The kind that looks like stillness and is actually enormous pressure moving toward the sea.

She reached into Ilya's coat—not asking, just taking, because there was no time left for asking—and drew out Maren's claim papers, wrapped in oilcloth, dry as a prayer. Then she pulled her grandmother's compass from her own pocket and set it on top, its needle swinging once, twice, and settling. Pointing, as it always had, home.

"Then I'll read it anyway," she said, "and the water will hear it, and the shelves will hear it, and forty years of names will hear it, and if you want to stop me you'll have to do it in front of your own notary, in writing, forever."

She unfolded the oilcloth. Her hands were steady. She would remember that later with disbelief—her hands, steady.

"*Concession Grant Karvath-Summit, Renewal Four,*" she read, and her voice carried in the stone the way a bell carries. "*All rights of extraction, salvage, and possession revert absolutely upon the seasonal high water entering the vault proper, there being no lawful government remaining to renew the grant, and all contents reverting thenceforth to the people of Karvath, their heirs, assigns, and survivors, wheresoever dispersed.*"

She looked up. "Clause Nine. Witnessed by no one, because no one ever thought the drowned would get a hearing."

"Beatrix—" Merrit started, and for the first time in all the weeks of smiles and tea and polite knives, her voice was not pleasant.

"*The date of reversion,*" Beatrix went on, louder, "*shall be certified by any commissioned notary present at the event, whose duty to record shall supersede all private retainer.*"

She turned to Quill. Water to his waist now, document case hugged to his chest like a shield, grey suit gone the color of the river itself.

"Master Quill," she said, gentler now, because she could see it—the war behind his eyes, the whole cheap architecture of a bought man discovering the one wall money doesn't buy. "You can walk out of here owned by the Vane Syndicate. Or you can walk out of here as the notary who certified the return of a nation. One of those things gets you a bonus. The other gets you a page in the history of a country that everyone said would never come back." She held out the compass and the papers. "Your commission. Your call. That's what the law says. *Your call.*"

The water surged. Knee-high became waist-high became a shove, and someone shouted, and a torch died with a hiss. In the chaos Quill did not move toward Merrit. He stood in the flood with his eyes closed for one long second—Beatrix would swear forever it was exactly one second, a single heartbeat in which forty years hung in the balance—and then he opened his case, took out his book, and began, with the small precise movements of a man doing the only honest work of his life, to write.

"*Present at the vault of Karvath-Summit upon the entry of seasonal high water,*" he recited as he wrote, voice thin but clear, "*the undersigned commissioned notary does witness and certify the operation of Clause Nine, Concession Grant Karvath-Summit, Renewal Four. All concessions lapse. All contents revert to the people of Karvath.*"

"You're finished," Merrit said to him. Quietly. Which was worse than shouting.

"Yes," Quill agreed, still writing. "Since about an hour ago, I suspect."

Merrit's men looked at her. Merrit looked at the sealed entrance, the rising water, the shelves, the small damp man with the pen—and Beatrix watched the arithmetic complete itself behind those clever eyes. There was nothing left to steal. There was no claim left to file. There was only a flooded room full of worthless paper that had just become the most valuable paper on the river, and a woman standing in front of it who would not move.

"You'll drown down here with your ancestors' receipts," Merrit said to Beatrix.

"Probably," Beatrix said. "But they'll be *ours*. Get out if you can find a way. The diaspora will be along to inventory what's left of you."

For a moment—one raw, unguarded moment—Merrit Vane's face did something human. Something almost like grief, or envy, or the memory of a time before smiling had been her whole job. Then it closed, and she turned, and she and her men waded into the dark of the upper galleries, hunting some maintenance shaft, some ladder, some crack in the mountain, and the flood swallowed the sound of them.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and Beatrix did most of it on instinct.

"Pass the books!" she shouted, and the chamber became a bucket line. Ilya at the shelves calling titles—*deeds, west district, deeds!*—Neshi hauling armloads to the high ledges above the waterline, Beatrix wading chest-deep to catch whatever fell, Quill clinging to his certification with one hand and passing folios with the other like a man who had decided, mid-drowning, which side of history got his good arm. The water rose past their waists, their ribs. The cold found bone. Ilya slipped once on submerged shelving and Beatrix had him by the collar before she'd consciously decided to reach, hauling him up gasping, a census roll clutched to his chest like a child.

"Not the roll," he coughed.

"You absolute idiot," she said, and kissed the wet crown of his head, and neither of them mentioned it afterward, because there wasn't time, because there was never going to be enough time, because the river was taking the room one shelf at a time.

It was Neshi who found the airway—an old ventilation flue near the ceiling of the eastern gallery, breathing cold storm-smelling wind, barely wide enough for a determined girl and a bag of claim papers. They boosted her up with the last dry documents strapped to her back, and she went, crying and cursing and going anyway, up into the dark toward daylight and the village and Maren.

Then the water took the lower shelves entirely, and the four of them—Beatrix, Ilya, Quill, and ten thousand drowning names—clung to the highest gallery and waited for the peak to spend itself.

It took hours. They huddled together for warmth like strangers on a wreck, which was what they were. At some point Quill asked, in a small voice, whether the Karvathi would prosecute him. Ilya considered it seriously.

"They'll probably make you read your certification aloud every year," he said. "At the homecoming festival. In front of everyone. Forever."

"That sounds awful," Quill said.

"It is," Ilya said. "That's what belonging costs."

When the water finally began to fall—imperceptibly at first, then unmistakably, a wet line retreating down the stone like a tide changing its mind—they were all three of them laughing, or sobbing, or some amphibious thing between the two, and Beatrix sat on the highest shelf with her grandmother's compass open in her palm, needle steady, pointing at nothing and everything, at a country that had just walked out of the water and picked up its own name.

Below, gleaming in the returning torchlight, the receding flood left the vault floor scattered with silt and leaves and one abandoned syndicate document case, floating gently, going nowhere.

And from far above—from the flue, faint but unmistakable—came voices. Many voices. Descending. Singing, in a language the maps had been paid to forget, a song about a river giving back what it borrowed.

"They're coming down," Ilya whispered. "Beatrix. They're coming *down*."

She stood up on the shelf, soaked to the soul, and went to meet them.
