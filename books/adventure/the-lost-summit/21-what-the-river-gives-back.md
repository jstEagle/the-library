## 21. What the River Gives Back

The river took nine days to give back what it had taken.

Neshi counted them from the high porch of the summit registry, watching the brown water drop finger-width by finger-width down the stone steps of the drowned approach, leaving silt lines like the rings inside a tree. Each morning the gorge smelled different—less of churned mud and drowned pine, more of wet stone and, faintly, impossibly, of the old terraced gardens below, their roots still alive after forty years underwater, still sending up shoots into the light.

She had never seen Karvath. Her grandmother had been carried out at age six on a fish cart, wrapped in a quilt, and had spent the rest of her life describing a country the way you describe a dream you're afraid to wake from. Neshi had grown up on those descriptions: the smell of the cedar smoke, the blue door lintels, the way the bells in the registry tower used to ring the hour even during the occupation because the bell-ringer, an old man named Piotr Vass, refused to stop and they never quite dared to hang him. She knew all of it secondhand. She knew none of it firsthand.

And now she stood inside the registry tower itself, with its scorched stairwell and its water-stained shelves, and she found that the descriptions had been wrong in every particular and right in every way that mattered.

* * *

They came upriver on the fourth day after the flood peak, when the water had dropped enough for flat-bottomed boats to make the crossing over what had once been the market square.

Neshi heard them before she saw them—the sound carrying strangely off the gorge walls, a low murmur like weather approaching. Then the first boat rounded the shoulder of the cliff, and then another behind it, and another, until there were eleven boats strung out along the flooded street like beads on a wire, and every one of them was full.

Old people. That was what struck her first, before anything else. Old people in their good clothes, the clothes they had kept folded in trunks for four decades against exactly this day, which some of them must have stopped believing in and others never had. A woman in a coat embroidered so densely at the cuffs that her hands disappeared into pattern. A man wearing a hat that had gone soft with age and handling, brim held between his fingers like something living. Children, too—children who had been born in exile camps and fishing settlements and borrowed cities, children who had never seen this place except as stories, standing at the prows with the same expression the old people wore, which was not joy, not yet, but the terrible careful blankness of people refusing to believe something until it is safely true.

Maren stood at the front of the first boat. She had insisted on coming despite her granddaughter's protests, despite the state of the roads, despite everything, and now she stood gripping the prow post with both hands, and Neshi saw that her grandmother was crying without any movement of her face at all, tears simply falling from her chin into the river, one after another, as if the grief of forty years were being paid out through her body drop by drop into the water that had taken everything.

The boats grounded on the steps of the registry. And then nobody moved.

That was the thing Neshi would remember afterward, the thing she told the story of for years: that for a long moment—long enough that the water lapped twice against the hulls, long enough for a gull to cross the whole width of the gorge crying—nobody stepped ashore. Eleven boats full of people who had waited their entire lives for this moment, and they stood in them, silent, as if stepping onto Karvathi soil were a thing too large for any single pair of legs.

It was the oldest man in the third boat who broke it. He was past ninety, and he had to be handed down like cargo, and when his feet touched the submerged step he went down on his knees in the floodwater, right there, robes and all, and put his palm flat against the stone beneath the surface, and said, in Karvathi, in a voice like dry leaves:

"We are here."

And then everyone came ashore at once, and the silence broke into weeping and shouting and the calling of names across forty years—*Sela? Sela Voss? You're alive—* —and Neshi stood on the registry steps holding the claim papers against her chest and let herself be swept aside by a country coming home.

* * *

Beatrix Dunmore worked through all of it.

Neshi watched her, half the day, with a kind of puzzled admiration. While the old people knelt in the water and the children ran shouting through the drowned lanes and someone began, inevitably, to sing—badly, brokenly, a harvest song that kept collapsing and restarting—Beatrix hauled shelving out of the flooded lower rooms of the registry and set it upright in the sun to dry. She carried armloads of sodden ledgers up the tower stairs two at a time. She spread census rolls across every flat surface on the summit, weighting their corners with stones, and stood over them shooing away gulls like a woman defending a catch.

"You could rest," Neshi said, finding her on the tower's third landing, wringing riverwater out of a bundle of land deeds with the tenderness of someone bathing a child. "Nobody's asking you to work today."

"I know." Beatrix didn't look up. "That's rather the point."

Neshi leaned against the doorframe. Below, the singing had found its feet; the harvest song rose whole now, ragged but unbroken, echoing off the gorge walls until it sounded like fifty voices instead of eleven boats' worth.

"My grandmother says you saved more than papers," Neshi said. "She says you saved the proof. The deeds, the rolls, the names. Everything the maps needed." She paused. "Everything the world needs, if anyone's ever going to write us back onto it."

Beatrix finally looked up. There was riverwater on her cheek and exhaustion carved under her eyes, and she smiled the crooked smile Neshi had come to know on the river—that smile that always looked slightly surprised at itself, as if it had been checked for contraband at the border and found, to its own astonishment, permitted.

"Tell your grandmother," Beatrix said, "that I've spent my whole life chasing storms. It turns out I just hadn't met the right one yet."

* * *

Dov came back on the sixth day.

Neshi was mending nets on the lower terrace when she saw the boat—a stolen syndicate skiff, flying no colors, riding low and wallowing—and Dov in it, alone, soaked to the ribs, with a week's beard and the hollow-eyed look of a man who has been running decoy downriver through hostile water on a promise and a prayer.

He grounded the skiff badly, stumbled up the steps, and stood there dripping, looking at the crowd of Karvathi survivors as if he weren't sure any of it would still be real if he blinked.

Then Beatrix came down the terrace fast, and Neshi braced for the storm—for the fury that had nearly ended the journey in the rain, the fury Dov had earned with his radio and his bad arithmetic and his weeks of quiet betrayal.

But Beatrix crossed the gravel, took one look at him, and hit him. Once. Open-handed, across the shoulder, hard enough to stagger him.

"That," she said, "is for the radio."

Then she grabbed two fistfuls of his soaked shirt and pulled him into a hug that lifted him half off his feet, and said into his ear, loudly enough that Neshi heard every word:

"And that is for coming back."

Dov made a sound that was mostly laugh and mostly sob. "You're forgiven, then?"

"Forgiven?" Beatrix released him and stepped back, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious at her own tears. "You absolute idiot. You steered a syndicate skiff through a patrol line on forged coordinates and a dead man's engine. You're not forgiven. You're *promoted.*"

* * *

On the seventh day, Maren climbed the registry tower.

She climbed slowly, resting on each landing, and Neshi went with her—not helping, because Maren had made it clear over seventy years that help offered before it was asked for was an insult, but walking one step behind the way she had walked behind her grandmother through every camp and settlement of the diaspora, keeper of the pace, witness to the climb.

At the top, in the round chamber where the bells had hung, where the archive now lay spread across trestle tables in the sun, Maren stopped and looked for a long time.

Ilya Senn was there, of course. He had barely left the tower since the flood receded—Neshi suspected he slept in it, curled on a bench among the census rolls like a cat claiming a warm hearth. He was cataloguing. Of course he was cataloguing. He had rigged a lamp, though it was midday, and he was moving along the tables with a pencil behind his ear and an expression of such concentrated tenderness that it hurt to watch, the way it hurts to watch someone pray.

"Maren Osta-Vell," he said, without turning around. "Census roll 114, household 9. You were twelve years old when the occupation began. Your father was a bridge-warden. You had a brother, Tomas, listed as relocated in '41, status unknown." He turned at last, and his gaunt face was wet. "Status unknown is the worst entry in the whole archive. I've read ten thousand of them. I'm going to resolve every single one."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, in Karvathi, "Who taught you our language, archivist?"

"A dead man's notes," Ilya said. "In a basement, in a city that expelled me for reading them. I learned your country from its paperwork, Maren Osta-Vell. It's the only home I ever had. Until—" he gestured, helplessly, at the tower, the tables, the sunlit names, "—until it turned out to have a location."

Maren nodded slowly. Then she turned to where Beatrix stood in the doorway, compass in hand—the compass that had turned out to be half a key, the compass that had opened a vault, the compass that had, Neshi thought, finally stopped pointing anywhere but here.

"The concession permits are void," Maren said. "The syndicate's claim died with the monsoon peak, and their gunboats are downstream licking their wounds. The survivors are reclaiming the valley. But an archive is not paper, Beatrix Dunmore. An archive is a *keeping*. Someone must hold these records against the next flood, the next fire, the next smiling man with a contract. Someone who has already bled for them." She looked from Beatrix to Ilya and back. "I name you keepers of the archive of Karvath. Both of you. If you'll have a country that has nothing else to offer."

Beatrix was quiet for a moment. Neshi watched her turn the compass over in her fingers, once, twice.

"It offers quite a lot, actually," Beatrix said at last. Her voice wasn't entirely steady. "It offers a family. Apparently I collect them now." She looked at Ilya, who was trying very hard to look composed and failing beautifully. "Well, archivist? Shall we share a homeland?"

"I should warn you," Ilya said, blinking rapidly, "that I am extremely difficult to live with. I alphabetize things that cannot be alphabetized."

"Perfect," said Beatrix. "So do I."

* * *

On the ninth day, the river gave back the last thing.

Neshi was on the lower steps at dawn, when the light comes up the gorge gold and sideways, and the water had dropped far enough now that the tops of the old market stalls showed black and dripping above the surface. She had come down to look at them, because her grandmother had described the market a thousand times and Neshi wanted to see the reality of it while it was still strange enough to feel like a story.

That was when she saw the chest.

It sat on a shelf of exposed rock below the waterline, wedged where the flood had left it, bronze gone green-black with forty years of river, its lid thrown open and empty. The seal of Karvath was worn almost smooth—but not quite. Neshi crouched and touched it, and the old double-eagle crest came up under her fingers like a scar coming back to life.

The chest that had started everything. Fished out of a shoal by a drowning woman and her crew, sold and stolen and fought over, half-key and whole key, the object every faction in the gorge had bled for. And here it was at the end of it all, empty, ordinary, worth nothing to anyone anymore—except that it had carried a nation's inheritance out of the dark and delivered it home.

The river keeps one secret even from her, her grandmother liked to say. Neshi looked at the empty chest, and then upstream, toward the vault entrance, still sealed, still flooded, still holding whatever it held beneath the waterline where no permit or deed or keeper's oath could reach.

She reached into the chest and lifted out the small thing lying in the bottom of it, which the flood had left there like a gift or a message or a joke only the river understood.

A single brass surveyor's marker, stamped with coordinates.

Coordinates that matched nowhere on any map Neshi had ever seen.

She stood there a long time, the dawn coming up the gorge, the water dropping another finger-width, the country breathing itself back into existence around her—and turned the marker over in her hand, and wondered what the river was trying to tell them now.
