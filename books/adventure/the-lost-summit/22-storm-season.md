## 22. Storm Season

The monsoon left the way a guest leaves who has stayed too long: gradually, then all at once, with an apology nobody believed.

For three days after the peak, the river ran brown and swollen, carrying fences, roofs, a chapel bell, half a bridge from somewhere upstream that would miss it. Then the water began to drop, and the valley emerged like something surfacing from sleep—terraces glistening, the drowned road to the summit registry reappearing inch by inch, and along the banks of every tributary, the small bright wreckage of a season's violence drying in a sun that seemed almost embarrassed by its own warmth.

Beatrix Dunmore stood on the deck of her boat and watched the waterline fall.

The boat had a new name now. She'd painted it herself, kneeling on the dock at the diaspora village with a brush too big for the letters, so that *KARVATH'S LEDGER* curved slightly across the stern like a smile it hadn't earned yet. Dov had laughed at it. Neshi had corrected her spelling twice. Ilya had said nothing at all, but he'd stood behind her the whole time she painted, close enough that she could feel him reading each letter as it dried, and when she finished he touched the wet paint with one fingertip and then pressed the fingerprint gently against the back of her hand, like a man stamping a document.

That had been eleven days ago.

Now the morning was gold and cool, and the river was lowering itself back into its old channels, and Beatrix was counting planks.

Not money. Planks. The villagers had settled her debt—not by handing her coin, which would have been a transaction, but by emptying their own stores: Maren's cousin the shipwright donating seasoned teak; three brothers who fished the lower gorge contributing caulking and pitch; an old woman whose name Beatrix still couldn't pronounce pressing a canvas tarp into her arms and refusing, with tremendous dignity, to take no for an answer. The Vane Syndicate's enforcers had come downriver one last time in their spotless launch, and Merrit Vane herself had stood on the dock with her hands folded and looked at the boat, at the archive crates stacked under oilcloth, at Ilya cataloguing in the doorway, at Neshi sharpening a fish knife with the unhurried menace of a seventeen-year-old, and had smiled her terrible smile and said, "Well. This is more expensive than I planned," and signed the release papers without being asked twice.

"Your debt is forgiven," she'd told Beatrix, folding the paper into quarters. "Consider it a donation to a lost cause."

"It's not lost," Beatrix had said. "It's just upstream of you."

Merrit's smile had flickered—one frame, maybe two—and then she'd stepped back onto her launch and been carried away downriver, smiling at the water, smiling at the birds, smiling at everything the way a knife smiles at a whetstone.

Beatrix thought about that smile sometimes, usually at night, and found that it frightened her less each time. That was what winning did, apparently. It didn't make the enemy smaller. It made your own fear smaller, and the enemy stayed exactly the same size, and you learned to walk past it carrying lumber.

* * *

The refit took the whole of the dry season's first month.

They gutted the aft cabin and built shelving—Ilya designed it, of course, with the obsessive precision of a man who had spent his exile imagining perfect shelves the way other men imagined lovers. Acid-free boxes. Sealed against humidity. A map drawer deep enough for the Karvathi survey charts, which Maren's people had begun bringing out of hiding one or two at a time, wrapped in oilcloth, sewn into mattress seams, buried in clay jars beneath kitchen floors. Forty years of a nation folded up small and hidden in the homes of people who had refused, quietly and stubbornly, to stop being that nation.

Every crate that came aboard felt less like cargo than like a pulse.

Dov did the heavy work and pretended not to care and cared enormously. He'd come back upriver nine days after the flood peak, soaked to the ribs, having run Merrit's decoy chase two hundred kilometers downriver and back again through country that wanted him dead, and he'd walked up the dock holding his sister's hand—he'd collected her from the syndicate's own office, of all places, walking in with the decoy papers and the truth and a face like a closed door until they gave her back—and Beatrix had hit him once, hard, in the shoulder, and then hugged him hard enough to crack a rib, probably, and neither of them had ever mentioned either event again. That was how they forgave each other. In weather. In work. In the wordless grammar of people who had bailed the same leaking hull at three in the morning and knew exactly what the other's hands were worth.

Neshi appointed herself navigator, quartermaster, and morale officer, in descending order of competence. She was sixteen going on forty, Maren's granddaughter, sharp-tongued and riverwise, and she had decided somewhere during the portage over the ridge that Beatrix's boat was now partially hers, and Beatrix had discovered she didn't mind. It was strange, this thing that kept happening: people attaching themselves. She'd spent thirty years keeping crews at arm's length because arm's length was where you could count on them from, and now here was a girl who slept curled in the chart room like a cat and called her "Captain" only when she wanted something, and here was an archivist who read aloud at dinner from recovered census rolls—the names of villages, the names of children born in 1961, the names of everyone—as if reciting a litany, and here was Dov humming while he caulked, off-key, unashamed.

Belonging, Beatrix thought, watching them work in the gold light. Nobody tells you it's a chore. Nobody tells you it's mostly chores.

* * *

On the last day of the refit, Maren came down to the dock.

She was old the way river stones are old—not worn smooth so much as condensed, all her softness pressed into density. She walked the length of the boat slowly, touching nothing, looking at everything, and stopped at the bow where the compass sat in its brass housing, lid open, needle steady.

"You never told us where you got it," she said.

"My grandmother." Beatrix leaned on the rail beside her. "I never met her. She died before I was born. My mother kept the compass and never used it, and when my mother died it came to me, and I used it for storms, because it doesn't drift. Everyone always said it doesn't drift."

"And now?"

"Now I know why." Beatrix turned the housing so Maren could see the underside of the case, where the Karvathi crest was stamped small and fine, twin to the seal on the chest they'd dredged from the shoal a lifetime ago. "She was from the summit country. The vault key was cut in two halves. One went in the chest. One—" she tapped the compass glass, "—went in a family that walked out before the maps were burned. My family. I've been carrying half a nation's front door in my pocket for thirty years and using it to find weather."

Maren was quiet a moment. The river moved under the dock pilings, patient, brown-gold, going about its business.

"The river keeps secrets," she said at last. "Even from itself, sometimes. It gave you the chest. It gave you the boy who reads seals. It gave you the storm that brought you to our dock." She looked up at Beatrix, and her eyes were the exact gray of rain coming. "Or maybe it didn't give you anything. Maybe you just finally asked."

"I don't think I asked," Beatrix said. "I think I got robbed, chased, nearly drowned, and lied to by everyone I loved."

"Yes," Maren agreed comfortably. "That's also how it happens."

* * *

They launched on a morning so clear the mountains looked cut from paper.

Half the village came down to see them off, which embarrassed Beatrix profoundly and pleased her even more profoundly, though she'd have bitten her tongue off before admitting it. Maren gave Ilya a packet of letters—survivors' testimonies, sealed, to be filed in the vault registry when the restoration was ready—and gave Neshi a kiss on both cheeks and instructions that were clearly a list of grievances pre-emptively filed. Dov got a sack of smoked fish and a warning about his cholesterol that he accepted with the gravity of a knighthood.

And to Beatrix, Maren handed a single sheet of paper, folded once.

It was a deed. Land, in the summit country—a hillside above the gorge, terraced, long abandoned, its legal description written in the old script that Ilya had taught Beatrix to read in stolen evenings through the winter. Her name was on it. Not leased. Not loaned. Deeded.

"Maren, I can't—"

"You can," Maren said, "and you will, and if you argue with me on a public dock I will say things about your caulking that cannot be unsaid." She closed Beatrix's fingers over the paper with both her old hands. "A keeper of the archive should have ground under her feet. Even if she's rarely on it. Especially then."

Beatrix looked at the deed for a long time. Somewhere behind her, Dov had gone suspiciously quiet, and Neshi was pretending very loudly to check a mooring line.

"Thank you," she said finally, which was inadequate, and Maren nodded, which meant she knew, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

* * *

The first season of the traveling annex looked like this:

They went where the records needed to go and where the people were who needed to see them. Up tributaries that weren't on any current map, following sketches drawn from memory by old men in riverside kitchens. They set up the archive's reading table on docks, in schoolhouses, under a tarpaulin in a market square while rain hammered the canvas and a crowd of three hundred stood shoulder to shoulder reading the census rolls by lamplight, finding their grandparents' names, weeping without noise, passing the pages hand to hand like communion.

They chased storms, still. That was the joke Beatrix had made when she first proposed it to Maren—that she was a storm chaser, that was the one skill she had, and if the syndicate's lawyers or the government surveyors or anyone else came asking inconvenient questions, well, the annex was simply a meteorological vessel conducting research, and the archive crates were simply ballast. But it stopped being a joke somewhere in the second month, standing on the foredeck with Neshi beside her and the sky going green-black over the hills, realizing that she was reading the storm differently now. Before, a storm had been a thing to survive, or a thing to sell—data, footage, a living scraped out of other people's disasters. Now it was a thing to be *ahead of*. To warn the villages. To move the elders up the hill before the surge came, to get the boats hauled, to stand in the rain with a bullhorn shouting the names of the low houses until every one of them emptied.

The river took things anyway. Rivers do. But it took less, those seasons, and the difference had faces attached, and Beatrix counted the faces instead of the losses, because that was the arithmetic she'd chosen.

Ilya changed most of all. He filled out—not in body, he'd always be a bundle of sticks tied with string, but in manner. He argued now. Loudly, joyfully, with village headmen about filing conventions and with Neshi about whether the survey charts should be organized by watershed or by year (watershed, obviously, he was wrong, it was watershed) and once, memorably, with a syndicate survey team that anchored too close to a reading session and got treated to forty minutes of archival law delivered at increasing volume until they weighed anchor purely to escape. Beatrix watched him do it from the dock and felt something in her chest that she eventually, privately, with great difficulty, identified as happiness.

At night, when the boat was tied up and the others slept, the two of them would sit in the cockpit with the lamp turned low and go through the day's acquisitions. A photograph album rescued from a flooded house. A marriage register. A child's exercise book full of the old alphabet, practiced in pencil, dated the last spring before the erasure. Ilya handled each item like a sleeping animal.

"Do you ever miss it?" Beatrix asked him once. "The archive. The real one. The marble and the climate control and the dignity."

He considered this with the seriousness he gave everything.

"I spent fifteen years there," he said, "and no one ever handed me anything and said *this matters, carry it carefully, it's yours.*" He turned a page. A wedding party stared up out of the photograph, frozen mid-laugh, forty years dead and forty years alive. "Here, they hand me things every day. So no. I don't miss it. I miss who I thought I was supposed to be there. But I like who I turned out to be out here better." A pause. "She has better company."

Beatrix looked out at the dark water and said nothing, because some things you keep the way the river keeps things—down deep, in the cold, where they stay true.

* * *

The compass hung at the bow, and it pointed, as it always had, north-northwest, toward the summit country, toward the vault, toward the hillside with her name on the deed.

But that wasn't what Beatrix meant anymore when she thought about where it pointed.

She meant the mornings. She meant coming up on deck at first light and finding Neshi already at the wheel, steering by landmarks Ilya had taught her from charts older than the girl's grandmother, calling corrections in a voice that thought it was casual. She meant Dov frying fish in the galley and burning half of it and serving all of it. She meant the reading tables, the lamplight, the crowds, the old woman in the market square at Tel Vora who had kissed the census page with her grandfather's name on it and then kissed Beatrix's hands and said, in broken trade-tongue, *you brought them home.*

Her grandmother's compass had never drifted. Thirty years, every storm, every bearing—it had held true to a heading she hadn't chosen, toward a country she hadn't known was hers, and she'd called that reliability, and it had been, and it had also been a kind of captivity: a life steered by an inheritance she'd never opened.

Now the needle pointed the same place it always had. But Beatrix chose the heading every morning. Chose it with the tide tables and the request letters and the list of villages waiting, chose it with Dov and Neshi and Ilya and a boat full of a dead nation's living memory, chose it the way you choose anything that matters: again, and again, and on purpose.

* * *

They were two days out of the summit country, running light up the gorge with the dry-season water low and glassy, when Neshi came forward from the chart room with a piece of paper in her hand and an expression Beatrix had learned to distrust.

"Radio traffic," Neshi said. "Fishing boats downstream are passing it around. There's a survey vessel working the delta. Syndicate charter, but flying academic colors—some university society, supposedly. Mapping the old Karvathi coastline." She paused. "They're asking questions in the villages. About records. About who's collecting them. About *where they're being kept.*"

Beatrix took the paper. Read it twice. Felt the old cold start to rise in her chest—the debt-cold, the dock-cold, the Merrit-smile cold—and then felt it meet something else, something that had grown in her all winter among the shelves and the lamplight and the names, and the two of them canceled each other out and left, in their place, a flat and durable calm.

"They can ask," she said.

"That's it? That's the plan?"

"Neshi." Beatrix folded the paper and handed it back. "We have the deeds. We have the rolls. We have the vault, the law, the expiry clause, the notary's signature, and forty years of grandmothers who hid a nation in their mattresses. Let them map the coastline." She turned back to the wheel, toward the north-northwest, toward the hills where her name waited on a piece of ground. "We're the ones holding the country."

Ahead, the gorge opened its green jaws, and the river ran on, keeping what it keeps, giving back what it gives, and the *Karvath's Ledger* went upriver into the morning with the compass steady at the bow and the whole crew awake, and the storm season coming, and none of them afraid of the weather anymore.
