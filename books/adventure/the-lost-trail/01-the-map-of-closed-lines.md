## 1. The Map of Closed Lines

The trestle had been dying for thirty years, and Coral Crane intended to argue with every one of them.

He stood at the base of the middle bent, morning fog still clinging to the timber in gray ropes, and looked up at the work the way a man looks at an old wound he's decided to finally clean out. The Kettle River trestle. Ninety-two feet of creosoted fir and rusted iron, built in 1889 by men whose names were carved into the cap beams and then forgotten by everyone except him. The line it carried hadn't seen a train since 1931. The rails up top were gone—stripped for the war effort, most likely—but the trestle itself still stood over the gorge like a skeleton that refused to lie down.

Coral liked that about it.

"Timber's sound on six of eight bents," Okonkwo called down from the deck, his voice carrying through the fog with the flat certainty of a man reading scripture. "Seven's got rot you could push your thumb through. Eight's holding on out of spite."

"Spite holds better than creosote," Coral said. "Mark seven for replacement."

He heard Dess laugh somewhere above him, quick and low, and Pell's younger voice asking what spite was made of, and Dess telling him to shut up and hand over the chisel. The sounds of his crew moved through the morning air like weather. Four men and a boy on a dead railroad in the hills, fixing something no one would ever use, because that was the whole of what they did now, and because nobody else would do it at all.

Coral climbed.

He climbed the way he always did, without thinking about it, hands finding the old spikes and bolt-holes by memory even though he'd never climbed this particular trestle before. That was the thing people didn't understand about closed lines—they were all the same line, really. Same gauge, same timber, same greed and optimism frozen into the landscape. He'd walked forty-one of them in eleven years. He kept count the way other men counted money or sins.

At the top, the deck stretched away into the fog, ties rotted soft as bread, and beyond it the grade ran straight into the hillside cut where ferns grew waist-high through the ballast. Somewhere under all that green was a roadbed built to last a century, used for forty years, abandoned in a single afternoon when the copper played out and the company folded its tents. Coral had read about it in a county archive two winters ago: *Kettle River & Northern, twelve miles, completed 1891, abandoned 1931.* One line of ink for forty years of men's lives.

He took off his coat, hung it on a spike, and got to work.

* * *

The map lived in his breast pocket, where a man might carry a photograph of his family.

It wasn't a map anyone else would have wanted. It was drawn on the back of a survey sheet so old the paper had gone the color of weak tea, and it covered the mountain country in a hundred-mile radius with nothing but lines—dozens of them, penciled, re-inked, crossed out and redrawn. Railroads that had died. Some were marked with dates: *Abandoned '29. Torn up '34.* Some just had a small X where the last spike had been pulled. In the lower corner, in Coral's careful draftsman's hand, was a legend with only one symbol left unused—a short, open line, waiting for something living to be written there.

Eleven years, and he'd never once filled it in.

Dess had asked him about it once, three years back, sitting across a fire in the Wallowas with his boots steaming. *You keep a map of graveyards, Coral? What for?* And Coral had said, because it was mostly true and entirely safe: *So I know where the work is.* Dess had nodded and let it go, because Dess understood that some answers are doors you don't open unless you're invited, and Coral had never invited anybody anywhere. That was the arrangement. They followed him from dead line to dead line, and he paid them out of the repair contracts and the salvage rights and the occasional county stipend for hazard abatement, and none of them asked why a man with his talent—his real talent, the kind that got a twenty-six-year-old made chief engineer on a mainline survey—spent his life shoring up railroads the world had already buried.

They didn't ask. But Coral knew they wondered. He could feel the wondering sometimes at night, around the fire, a warmth in the circle that stopped exactly at the edge of his silence.

He hammered bolts until noon. The fog burned off and the sun came down hard on the deck boards, and by early afternoon Bent Seven lay in pieces on the bank below, and Pell was down in the shallows with a peavey, wrestling a new fir log into position while Okonkwo directed him with the patience of a man who had once taught school in a country Coral had never asked him about either. There was a lot nobody asked anybody. It was how the crew worked. A family built out of people who'd each left somewhere else.

Coral stood on the deck at midafternoon, wiping sweat off his forearms, and did what he did every day around this hour whether he meant to or not. He took out the map.

Unfolded it against his thigh. Let his eyes travel the dead lines the way a man runs his tongue over a missing tooth.

There. Eleven miles east-southeast, up over the divide, tucked into a fold of mountains he could almost see from here if the air were cleaner—the Kestrel Spur. He didn't need to look at the map to find it. His finger went there anyway, the way it always did, drawn along the pencil line like water finding a channel it had cut long ago. The spur climbed fourteen hundred feet in six miles through a granite canyon, switchbacking twice, crossing the river three times, and then it ran into the mountain itself, into the tunnel.

Two miles of tunnel. The longest bore on any branch line in the state, drilled through a fault zone the original engineers had sworn at for three years. And at the mouth of it, or near enough, was a place Coral had not stood in ten years and thought about nearly every day of them.

He folded the map before his hand could shake. It always shook, a little, at this part. Nobody had ever noticed. He made sure of that.

"You look at that thing like it owes you money," Dess said.

Coral turned. Dess had come up the ladder without a sound, the way he did, a lean gray man of fifty-odd with a face like a closed ledger. He leaned on the guardrail beside Coral and looked out at the cut, the ferns, the fog remnants burning off the far ridge.

"It does," Coral said.

"What's it cost you so far?"

"Everything I don't think about."

Dess grunted. He was the only one of them who ever pushed, and he pushed gently, and never far, and Coral had learned to read the exact depth of each push the way he read timber—sound or rotten, one knock with the knuckle. This one was shallow. Dess just wanted to stand next to him for a minute, which was its own kind of question, and Coral could bear those better than the others.

"Log's set," Dess said after a while. "Pell wants to know if he can drive the first bolt on Seven."

"He'll split it."

"He wants to anyway."

"Then let him want it till tomorrow. We'll show him how." Coral refolded the map and put it away, and felt, as he always felt, the strange double weight of it going back into his pocket—heavier than paper should be, lighter than what it actually held. "Bent Eight tomorrow. Then we're done here."

"Done here," Dess repeated. He said it the way he always said it, with a small flat note underneath, and Coral knew what the note meant: *and then where.* It was the crew's oldest joke and oldest complaint, that their boss was a man who fixed things in order to leave them. That they'd spent eleven years following a man running from a place he'd never named.

Below them, Pell drove the peavey into the gravel and shouted something triumphant about the log, and Okonkwo told him the log had done nothing to deserve shouting at, and the afternoon went on being ordinary, which was the most valuable thing Coral owned.

* * *

The telegram came at dusk.

Coral heard the rider before he saw him—a single set of hooves on the county road above the camp, moving at the unhurried trot of a man doing a job he'd done too many times. Coral was at the fire, mending a chain fall with Dess, and he watched the horse come down the switchback trail with the flat orange light behind it and felt something in his chest tighten before he knew why. Telegrams did not come looking for crews like his. Telegraph offices did not know where he was; he made sure of that, the way he made sure of everything. If a wire found him, it was because someone had spent money and effort finding him.

That was its own message, before a word was read.

The rider was a kid, sixteen maybe, bored and saddle-sore. "Crane? Cyrus Crane?" — using the name Coral had half-abandoned, the name from the old life, the chief engineer's name — "Wire for you. Sign here."

Coral signed. The kid turned his horse and was gone up the trail, and Coral stood there holding the envelope while the fire cracked and spat and Dess watched him with that closed-ledger face, saying nothing, which was how Dess said the most important things.

Coral opened it by the firelight.

The paper was cheap and the hand was a telegrapher's, all capitals, but he knew the signature at the bottom before he finished the second line. He'd have known it blind. He'd have known it in the dark, underwater, ten years gone.

**CRANE STOP UNDERSTAND YOU ARE WORKING KETTLE RIVER LINE STOP HAVE JOB FOR YOU AND CREW STOP KESTREL SPUR REOPENING BEFORE MONSOON SEASON STOP TRIPLE YOUR GOING RATE PLUS BONUS FOR ON-TIME DELIVERY STOP CAN DISCUSS TERMS AT YOUR CONVENIENCE STOP SILAS VANE.**

He read it twice. The fire popped. Somewhere up the slope, Pell was laughing at something Okonkwo had said, the bright unguarded laugh of a nineteen-year-old who had not yet learned what Coral had learned at twenty-six, which was that some names arrive in your life like weather fronts, and you feel them coming hours before the sky changes.

Silas Vane.

Ten years since Coral had heard that voice in person, and it had gotten inside a telegram anyway, somehow—the charm of it, the needle in it. *Understand you are working Kettle River Line.* Which meant Vane had made inquiries. Which meant Vane had wanted him located, specifically, by name, and had paid to do it. Triple rate. Kestrel Spur. Before monsoon.

Nobody reopened the Kestrel Spur. The spur was a corpse with a tunnel through a fault zone, and every hydrologist in three counties had said the spring floods through the canyon made any rebuild a gamble against the river itself, and the company that owned the right-of-way had let it sit for a decade precisely because sane men wouldn't touch it.

Which meant Silas Vane wasn't offering a job. Silas Vane was offering something else wearing a job's clothes, and Coral had spent ten years learning to recognize that particular tailoring.

"Coral." Dess's voice, quiet. "Bad news?"

"No news." Coral became aware that he had crushed the telegram slightly in his fist, and made himself smooth it out, fold it, put it in his breast pocket—against the map, paper against paper, the past touching the past. "An offer. From somebody I used to know."

"Good somebody?"

"There's no such animal."

Dess almost smiled. "Somebody you owe, or somebody who owes you?"

Coral looked at the fire. The coals had settled into that deep orange glow that reminded him, as it always did, of the lamp inside the tunnel mouth, the one that swung on its wire when the air moved, though nothing should have been moving at all.

"Neither," he said. "Worse. Somebody who knows me."

Dess studied him for a long moment. Then he picked up the chain fall again and went back to work, and let it be, because that was the arrangement, and Coral loved him for it and hated himself a little for needing the arrangement at all.

* * *

He didn't sleep.

He sat up after the camp went quiet, with the map spread on his bedroll and the telegram beside it, and the moon came over the ridge and laid itself across the dead lines in silver, and Coral did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since dusk.

Triple rate. Winter debts—Pell's mother's doctor bills down in the valley, Okonkwo's letters from home that he read alone and answered alone, Dess's quiet pride that would never ask for anything but deserved everything. A season's wages that would set four men up for years instead of months. All of it hanging on one word in one wire: *Kestrel.*

His finger found the line on the map again. East-southeast, over the divide. Six miles of canyon. Three river crossings. Two miles of tunnel.

And at the end of it, a name he had not spoken aloud in ten years, that the telegram had not needed to print because Vane knew—Vane had always known—that Coral would hear *Kestrel Spur* and see the tunnel mouth and feel the rope in his hands and the water rising and Marrow—

He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes until the dark went red.

The map was a refuge. That was the truth of it, the truth he'd built eleven years on: as long as he stayed among the dead lines, repairing what nobody cared about, the world couldn't ask him anything. No mainline office would ever request his credentials. No board of inquiry would ever pull a file with his name in it. Out here among the ghosts, Cyrus Crane—the youngest chief engineer the company had ever certified, the man who signed the Kestrel safety report, the man who walked out of the canyon while they zipped his partner into a bag—could become Coral, just Coral, a handyman for graveyards, and stay hidden in plain sight forever.

But a refuge, he thought, looking at the silvered paper, is only a prison you've agreed to.

And now Silas Vane had come to the door of it, cheerful as a landlord, holding a key.

Coral folded the map. Folded the telegram around it, so the two papers lay together in his breast pocket, and lay awake listening to the Kettle River run in the dark below the trestle they'd spent all day saving—a river that went somewhere, whether it wanted to or not.

In the morning, he'd burn the wire. Or he wouldn't. He honestly didn't know which, and that frightened him more than the name at the bottom of it.

Because triple pay was a hook, and Kestrel Spur was the line, and somewhere south of the divide, Silas Vane was already smiling—and Coral Crane, who had outrun the truth for ten years on the flattest, deadest rails he could find, could feel the ground beginning, very slightly, to tilt toward the mountains.
