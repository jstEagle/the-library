## 2. The Crew That Stays Together

The river camp sat in the elbow of the Alder, where the current slowed and spread itself thin over gravel bars, and by the time Coral came down from the trestle with the last of his tools slung across his back, the smoke from Okonkwo's cook fire was already standing straight up in the windless evening air. That was a good sign. Straight smoke meant no storm coming. Meant the sky intended to let them alone for one more night.

He crossed the plank walkway they'd built in their first week here—Dess's design, Pell's hammering, Okonkwo's insistence that they set the pilings deep enough to survive a hundred-year flood—and felt the particular ease that came with walking toward a fire where people expected him. It was an ease he'd spent ten years learning not to trust.

The telegram rode in his breast pocket like a coal that wouldn't cool.

* * *

Dess had claimed the flat rock nearest the water as her territory, and she sat on it now with her boots off, her feet in the shallows, mending a net she had no practical use for. She fished because it steadied her hands, she said, though her hands were the steadiest Coral had ever seen on a wrench or a rifle or a man's broken arm. The net was ritual, not necessity. He'd stopped asking about it years ago.

"You look like a man who lost an argument with himself," she said without turning around.

"I won," Coral said.

"Worse." She pulled a strand tight with her teeth. "Supper's on. Pell caught two trout and is telling everyone about it."

Pell was indeed telling everyone about it. He stood at the fire's edge with the fish strung over his shoulder like a bandolier, narrating to Okonkwo the epic struggle of the second trout—the one that had nearly taken the pole into the current—and Okonkwo listened with the grave attention he gave to everything, nodding at intervals, which only encouraged the boy. Pell was nineteen and all elbows and appetite, and he had come to them three winters back off a freight car with frostbitten ears and nothing in his pockets but a railroad spike he claimed was lucky. He'd never said why he'd left wherever he'd been before. None of them ever did.

"Engineer!" Pell called, spotting him. "Tell Okonkwo the drag on a line like the Kestrel would run what—a four percent grade? I've been saying three and a half and he says I'm full of it."

Coral stopped dead for half a step, so small a hitch that nobody but Dess would have caught it, and Dess caught it. The name had come out of nowhere, out of the boy's idle mouth, out of the same country the telegram had come from.

"Where'd you hear about the Kestrel?" Coral asked.

Pell shrugged, pleased to have been asked anything. "Fella at the feed store in Millbrook was going on about it. Says some company's buying up the old spur rights. Says there's money in reopening the mountain lines now, timber prices being what they are." He frowned, reading something in Coral's face. "Bad news?"

"No news," Coral said. "Grade's closer to five where it climbs past the switchbacks. You'd know if you'd ever ridden it."

"Have you?"

The question hung there a moment longer than it should have. Coral walked past him to the fire and held his palms out to the coals, and behind him he heard Okonkwo say, quietly, in the voice he used when he wanted a thing dropped: "Fish'll burn, boy."

* * *

They ate trout and cornbread and beans cooked with a ham hock Okonkwo had been saving for an occasion, though he never named his occasions, just produced them when the mood struck him, the way other men produced pocketknives. They ate sitting on logs around the fire, and the talk went the way camp talk went—Pell arguing for a new winch cable, Dess saying the old one had another season in it, Okonkwo defending his position on both counts with the patience of a man who had been right more often than anyone deserved to be.

Coral let it wash over him. This was the part of his life he could not account for, the part that made no entry in any ledger he kept: that these three had gathered around him and stayed. He'd found Dess first, seven years ago, working a wrecking crew outside Corvane where the foremen drank through lunch and blamed the Irish for every derailment. She'd been the only one who could read a bridge's fatigue by looking at it, the way a doctor reads a face, and when the crew was disbanded she'd shown up at whatever line Coral was repairing next with her tools wrapped in oilcloth and no explanation offered. He hadn't asked for one. Asking questions was how you learned things you couldn't put down again.

Okonkwo had come later, a big quiet man who'd worked the coastal lines and spoke six languages and used none of them more than necessary. He'd appeared one morning at the edge of Coral's camp with a bedroll and a carpenter's adze and said, "I hear you fix what other men abandon." And Coral had said, "Who told you that?" And Okonkwo had said, "A man in a bar. He said you were either a saint or hiding from something, and I wanted to see which." Seven years on, Coral suspected Okonkwo still hadn't decided.

And Pell—Pell had simply arrived, the way young things arrive at any warmth, and stayed because nobody told him to go.

None of them knew about Marrow. None of them knew about the tunnel, or the report, or the night ten years ago that Coral carried folded up inside him like the map in his pack, worn soft at the creases from handling. They knew he didn't drink whiskey, and accepted this without comment. They knew he never took contracts east of the divide, and accepted that too. They knew he woke sometimes shouting a name into the dark, and had the grace to never once ask whose name it was.

That was the bargain none of them had spoken aloud: they gave him their loyalty, and he gave them work and protection and the fiction of a man who was exactly what he appeared to be. Everyone got something. Everyone paid something. It was, Coral thought, watching Pell laugh at his own story for the third time, the most honest arrangement he'd ever been part of, and the most dishonest, both at once.

The telegram pressed against his chest when he leaned forward. He could feel its edges through the shirt.

* * *

After supper, Dess found him at the waterline, where he'd gone under the pretense of checking the boat tie-off. The boat was fine. The tie-off was fine. Dess looked at both anyway, then at him.

"Vane," she said.

It wasn't a guess delivered cold. She'd seen him flinch at the Kestrel name; she'd watched him touch his pocket twice during supper like a man checking a wound. Dess assembled evidence the way she assembled engines—one part at a time, in silence, until the whole machine stood revealed.

"You opened it," Coral said.

"I read the outside of it. Your face did the rest." She crouched, ran river water over her wrists, an old habit of hers after handling metal. "Silas Vane doesn't send telegrams to men who don't matter. So either you matter to him, or you owe him, or he owes you. Which is it?"

"None."

"Coral." She said his name the way she'd say *that bearing's shot*, flat and certain. "I've followed you up and down these hills for seven years. I've earned the right to be lied to properly. At least make it a good lie."

He almost did it. Almost told her—the shape of it, if not the substance. Vane. The Kestrel Spur. A tunnel with a number instead of a name, because naming it would mean naming the man inside it. But the words stacked up behind his teeth and wouldn't come, because underneath them lay the truth he'd never said even to himself in full sentences: that whatever Vane wanted, it wasn't the spur reopened. Men like Silas Vane didn't spend money on old rail. They spent money on leverage. And somewhere in that flooded valley lay the only leverage in the world that could ruin Coral Crane completely.

"It's a job offer," Coral said. "Reopening work. Mountain line, monsoon deadline. Triple pay."

"And you're going to turn down triple pay because—"

"Because I don't like the man offering it."

Dess studied him for a long moment. The river moved against the gravel with its endless patient sound, filling the silence the way it filled everything, eventually.

"All right," she said finally, and stood, and he understood with a sinking feeling that her *all right* was not belief. It was a door left open. She'd wait for him to walk through it on his own, or she'd watch him drown behind it. "But when you're ready to tell me the real reason, don't dress it up first. I'll know."

She walked back toward the fire. Coral stayed at the waterline until the stars came out hard and white above the ridge, and then he took out the telegram at last and read it again by moonlight, though he already knew it by heart.

*CRANE. HAVE ACQUIRED KESTREL SPUR RIGHTS. REQUIRE YOUR CREW FOR REOPENING BEFORE MONSOON. TRIPLE STANDARD RATE. YOU OF ALL MEN KNOW THE GROUND. — S.V.*

*You of all men know the ground.*

It was not an invitation. It was a hook set gently in the water, baited with the one name Vane knew Coral could not ignore. Somewhere south of here, in a valley the maps had started forgetting, a tunnel mouth waited under ten years of floodwater, and behind it waited a truth Coral had buried deeper than any rail bed.

He fed the telegram to the cook fire's embers and watched the paper curl and blacken and go to ash, and told himself that was the end of it.

Behind him, Pell turned in his sleep and murmured something cheerful. Okonkwo's breathing was slow and even. Dess, he noticed, was not asleep at all. Her eyes were open in the firelight, fixed on him across the dying flames, patient as water.

Three days later, Silas Vane stepped off the morning steamer at Millbrook dock wearing a linen suit too clean for the mountains, and smiled at Coral like a man greeting an old debt.
