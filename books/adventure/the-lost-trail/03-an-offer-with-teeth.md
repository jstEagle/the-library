## 3. An Offer With Teeth

The telegram had sat in Coral's breast pocket for two days, folded into a square small enough to swallow, and he could feel it against his sternum like a coal that wouldn't cool. He'd taken to touching it the way other men touched worry beads—thumb finding the crease, pressing down, letting go. Dess had noticed. Dess noticed everything, which was one of the hundred reasons Coral both trusted and feared him.

On the third morning, Silas Vane came up the river road in a white motorcar that had no business existing within fifty miles of a rail grade this dead.

Coral heard it before he saw it—the wrong kind of engine note for these hills, too smooth, too city—and he straightened from the joint he was spiking and looked down the overgrown right-of-way toward the switchback where the old Kestrel branch met the county road. The car came around the bend in a rooster tail of dust, gleaming like a lie told at a funeral.

"Company's coming," Pell called from the water barrel, where he'd been soaking his blistered hands.

"I see it."

"You expecting anybody?"

"No." And that was true enough. He hadn't expected Vane to come in person. A man who sent telegrams wanted an answer on paper, something with a date and a signature. A man who came himself wanted something else entirely.

* * *

The motorcar stopped at the foot of the trestle they'd spent three weeks nursing back to something like honesty—new stringers where rot had hollowed them, fresh planking, the whole structure creaking but sound under a hand-car's weight. Vane stepped out of it the way he did everything: like the world had been arranged in advance for his convenience.

He was smaller than Coral remembered, or maybe Coral had grown. Ten years ago, in the company offices at Harrow Junction, Vane had seemed to fill a room—not with bulk but with attention, the sense that everything you said was being weighed against something you couldn't see. Now he stood in his pale linen suit at the edge of the wilderness, dust already dulling his shoes, and he looked around at the trestle, the cut brush, the crew's tarped camp beyond, with the expression of a man appraising a horse he intended to buy cheap.

"Coral Crane," he said, spreading his arms. "Still chasing ghosts along the dead lines, I see."

"Vane." Coral set down his spike maul with care. It wasn't a threat. It just felt better in his hand than at his feet. "You're a long way from anywhere with air conditioning."

"I'm a long way from a great many comfortable things." Vane climbed the near abutment with surprising ease, boots finding the old stone steps as if he'd walked them recently—which, Coral thought, maybe he had. "That's rather the point of me being here."

"We're working."

"So I see. Good work, too. This is the Alder Creek trestle, isn't it? Closed in '09 after the washout. Nobody's run anything heavier than a velocipede over it since." He ran a gloved hand along a new stringer, reading the grain. "And here you've got it carrying timber again. Remarkable. You always did have a gift for raising the dead."

"That's the job."

"That was never the job, Coral. That was the penance." Vane smiled when he said it, easy and warm, the smile of a man offering you a drink. "May we walk?"

"No."

Vane laughed—a real laugh, Coral hated to admit, genuine delight flickering through the charm. "Still the same. All right, then we'll stand. I'll be brief, because I know how you feel about me taking up your daylight." He reached into his jacket and produced a leather folder, thick as a Bible, and held it out. Coral didn't take it. After a moment Vane let his arm drop, unhurried, unoffended. "Triple scale. Triple, plus completion bonus, plus hazard pay your men have never seen the like of. One job. Reopen the Kestrel Spur from the valley junction to the mine head. Grading, drainage, tunnel clearance. Sixty days, start to finish, and monsoon season be damned—or rather, monsoon season be beaten."

Coral said nothing. Above them, a hawk turned circles in the hot blue, riding the thermals off the rock cuts.

"You've heard of the spur, of course," Vane went on, conversational now, strolling a few steps along the rail as though inspecting it. "Everyone has heard of it and no one will touch it. Flooded twice since closure. The valley road's gone, the mine's sealed, the whole watershed's been quietly eating itself alive for a decade. The state wants the line reopened before the rains to move flood-mitigation equipment up-valley. There's money in it—state money, insurance money, more money than a crew of four will spend in ten years of patching trestles nobody remembers." He turned, and his eyes found Coral's and held them, and the warmth in them didn't change at all, which was exactly what made it terrible. "And there is no one else. I've asked. Every contractor in three counties took one look at the survey maps and developed sudden religious convictions about their schedules. But you—" he gestured at the trestle, the camp, the whole ragged enterprise of Coral's life— "you don't look at maps. You look at rails. And you go where the rails are dead."

"The answer's no."

"I haven't finished making my offer."

"You finished it a minute ago. Everything since has been decoration."

Vane studied him for a long moment. Somewhere below, Okonkwo's hammer rang against iron, steady as a heartbeat, and Pell's laughter carried up from the creek where he was washing out cook pots. The sounds of the only family Coral had left, going about its morning.

"Your crew," Vane said softly. "Dessard, who walked out of a prison work camp and into your keeping. Okonkwo, who lost his certification in the Meridian wreck and would still be blacklisted if you hadn't put your name under his. The boy, Pell—sixteen? seventeen?—who follows you around like a dog that's decided you're the last good thing in the world." He ticked them off on gloved fingers, gently, like a man counting coins he meant to give away. "Winter's coming, Coral. I know what their debts look like. I made it my business to know. Triple scale would clear every one of those ledgers by Christmas, with enough left over that none of them ever has to sleep in a tent again unless he wants to."

"You leave my crew out of this."

"They're the entire reason this offer exists. You think I'd hire you alone? You're not worth triple scale by yourself, Crane. You're worth triple scale because of what you can hold together." Vane let that sit, then sighed, theatrical, regretful, and looked out at the valley. "I had hoped it wouldn't come to names."

The word arrived before Coral understood he should brace for it.

"Marrow," Vane said.

The hawk cried once, high and thin, and was gone over the ridge.

Coral's hands were very still at his sides. He had learned stillness the way other men learned prayer, and it served him now: face blank, breath even, pulse hammering somewhere far away behind his ribs like a train trying to get through a blocked tunnel. "What about him?"

"He was your partner. On the Kestrel survey. Ten years ago this autumn." Vane's voice stayed pleasant, almost gentle, and that gentleness was the cruelest thing about it. "He died in the number two tunnel. Rockfall, according to the report. Tragic accident, according to the report. Closed casket, according to the report—you remember why, don't you? Of course you do. You signed the report."

"I signed what the engineers wrote."

"You wrote half of it yourself." Vane took a step closer. Dust hung in the light between them, turning slowly. "Here's what I know, Coral, and I want you to hear all of it before you decide how much you hate me. I know the Kestrel Spur is reopening whether you say yes or no. I know the state will send its own investigators up that line, because the flood money comes with strings, and the strings have auditors attached. And I know that when those investigators pull the original survey file—and they will pull the original survey file, because tunnels that kill people get pulled files—they are going to find a safety report with your signature on it certifying ground conditions that any first-year man could see were rotten." He paused. "They're also going to find that the man who died in that tunnel was buried without an inquiry, on the strength of that report, and that the engineer who signed it has spent ten years hiding in the dead ends of the railroad map where no one asks questions."

The river sounded very loud suddenly. Or maybe it was just Coral's blood.

"What do you want, Vane?"

"To hire you. Genuinely. That's the joke of it—that's the beautiful, ugly joke of it. I want the best line man in the territory on this job, and the best line man in the territory is standing in front of me refusing to shake my hand." Vane opened the folder at last and held it out again, and this time Coral looked at it: contracts, schedules, survey sheets, and beneath them, visible at the folder's edge, a corner of older paper, yellowed, typed, bearing a header Coral knew better than his own handwriting. "Take the job. Lead your crew up that valley, reopen that spur, and do it well. Because if you do it, you'll be there—on site, in charge, indispensable—when the questions come. And a man on the inside can shape the answers. A man on the outside gets shaped by them." He smiled again, and for one unguarded instant the charm dropped away and something older and wearier looked out of his face, something that might even have been pity. "Or refuse, and spend the rest of your life waiting for a knock on whatever door you're sleeping behind. You're good at waiting, Coral. I've watched you wait for ten years. But waiting isn't the same as being safe. Nothing up that valley is safe. It never was."

Coral took the folder.

He told himself afterward that he took it only to end the conversation—to get the weight of it out of Vane's hands and into his own—but his fingers closed on the leather and did not let go, and they both knew what it meant, and neither of them said it.

"I'll read it," Coral said. "I'm not promising anything."

"Of course not." Vane buttoned his jacket, satisfied, a man closing a deal he considered already closed. "Read it tonight. Discuss it with your people—they'll want to discuss it; they're honest men, which is why they follow you. I'll send my foremen ahead to the valley junction regardless. Equipment doesn't wait on sentiment." He descended the abutment steps, paused at the bottom, and looked back up, and the light caught his face so that for a moment Coral couldn't read it at all. "For what it's worth—and I know it isn't worth much—I liked Marrow. Everyone liked Marrow. He deserved better than a rockfall and a rubber stamp." A beat. "So did you, probably. Different reasons."

The motorcar pulled away in its rooster tail of dust, and the hills swallowed it, and the quiet came back the way floodwater comes back into a channel—slowly, then all at once.

* * *

Coral sat on the stringer at the top of the trestle until the shadows went long, the folder unopened across his knees.

Below, the crew built the evening fire. He could hear Pell arguing with Okonkwo about something—the boy's voice cracking with laughter, the big man's rumble answering—and Dess's silence underneath it all, steady as ballast. Ten years of nights like this. Ten years of moving along the dead lines, fixing what the world had thrown away, asking nothing of the past except that it stay behind him.

He thought about the yellowed paper at the edge of the folder. He didn't need to open it. He knew that document the way a man knows a scar—by touch, by weather, by the ache that came before rain. His own signature at the bottom of it, certifying the number two tunnel sound. Certifying it while the water seeped through the crown in threads fine as hair, while the timbers groaned in a register he'd told himself was normal, while Marrow had grinned at him that last morning and said, *We'll be drinking beer in the valley by Friday, Coral, and you can buy.*

The report had been a lie. Not his lie—he had signed it, but the numbers had come down from above, from men whose names weren't on anything, and the choice had been presented to him in a room in Harrow Junction with a lawyer present and the crew's wages hanging in the balance: sign, and the men keep their jobs and the widow keeps her settlement; refuse, and the company finds another way, and the crew finds the door. He had signed. He had told himself for ten years that he'd signed to protect them, and some mornings he even believed it, and other mornings he woke with Marrow's grin behind his eyes and knew that protection had been the cheaper of the two things available to him, and the other thing—the truth—had cost a dead man his name.

Now Vane wanted to lead him back up that valley with the truth in his pocket like a knife held handle-first, ready to hand to whoever came asking.

Or ready to use.

Because that was the thing sitting cold in Coral's gut, colder than the river fog coming up the banks: Silas Vane did not spend motorcar fuel and triple-scale money on charity. If Vane wanted the spur reopened, it was because reopening it served Vane. And if Vane wanted Coral on that line, it was because Coral on that line served Vane better than Coral anywhere else. A man didn't dangle a dead partner's name unless he intended to pull the rope tied to it.

"Boss?" Dess stood at the foot of the trestle stairs, firelight behind him, his face unreadable in the way it always was when he'd been watching Coral a long time without saying so. "Supper's on. You coming down, or you planning to sleep up there with the ties?"

Coral looked at the folder. Then he tucked it inside his coat, against the same pocket where the telegram had burned for three days, and stood.

"Coming down," he said. "There's something we need to talk about."

Dess's eyes went to the shape of the folder under the wool, and something moved behind them—recognition, or dread, or both—and he turned without a word and led the way to the fire.
