## 4. Sworn Off

Dess Marlowe had learned to read Coral Crane the way other men read weather. You watched the sky of him—the set of his shoulders, the angle of his jaw when he chewed the end of a pencil—and you knew what was coming before it came. Rain, or silence. Work, or a night spent staring into the fire like a man counting debts he couldn't name.

So when Coral turned down three times their going rate without so much as blinking, Dess knew the weather had turned bad.

He found him at the edge of camp an hour after Vane's boat had chugged away downriver, leaving a wake that slapped against the pilings like slow applause. Coral stood with his back to the water, working a whetstone along the edge of a spike puller that didn't need sharpening. The stone hissed against steel in long, even strokes. It was the sound of a man trying not to think.

"Triple pay," Dess said.

"I heard."

"Triple." Dess let the word sit there between them, fat and obvious. "You know what that means? New boiler for the winch. Boots for Pell, who's been walking around with his toes saying hello to the morning. A winter where we don't eat beans because beans is all we can afford."

Coral kept working the stone. "I know what it means."

"Do you? Because from where I'm standing, it looks like you just threw a year of our lives off a bridge." Dess crossed his arms. He wasn't a big man—Okonkwo had the size in this crew—but he'd spent twenty years arguing with foremen and railroad barons and one memorable mule, and he knew how to make himself heavy. "Silas Vane. The Silas Vane. Comes upriver in a boat worth more than everything we own combined, offers us a fortune, and you tell him no. Not 'let me ask my crew.' Not 'we'll consider it.' Just no. Like swatting a fly."

"He's not a man you want to owe anything to."

"Nobody's asking to owe him. We're asking to be *paid* by him. There's a difference."

"There isn't." Coral set down the whetstone. His hands were steady—that was the thing about Coral, his hands were always steady, even when his eyes weren't. "Not with men like Vane. The money's the hook, Dess. The line comes after."

"What line?"

"The kind you can't see until it's through you."

Dess blew out a breath and looked out over the river. Evening was coming down gold and slow, the way it did in the weeks before monsoon, when the whole valley seemed to hold its breath. Their camp sat on a shelf of gravel above the water: two tents, the cook fire, the winch barge moored to dead pilings from some line that had died before any of them arrived. It wasn't much. It was theirs.

"Coral," Dess said, quieter now. "Ten years I've followed you up and down these dead lines. Ten years. I've never once asked you why we do what we do—why a man with your head for engineering wastes himself patching track nobody rides, in valleys nobody remembers. I figured a man earns his privacy. Everybody's got a drawer they keep shut."

Coral said nothing. The whetstone lay on the gravel between them like a dropped word.

"But this," Dess went on, "this ain't a drawer anymore. This is you standing in front of good money with your arms out, and I've got three men behind me eating through my savings, and I need to know what's in that drawer. So I'm asking. Plain. Why won't you take the Kestrel Spur job?"

For a long moment Coral just looked at him. And Dess saw something move behind his eyes—not anger, not annoyance, but a kind of tired grief, old and worn smooth, like a rail joint polished by a million wheels that would never roll again.

"Bad stretch of country," Coral said finally. "The Kestrel valley floods early and hard. Ground's unstable. Tunnel work in ground like that, before monsoon, with Vane rushing the schedule—he'll push past every safety margin there is. Somebody gets hurt. That's not a guess, Dess. That's arithmetic."

"That's a speech," Dess said. "It's a fine speech. I might almost believe it, except for one thing." He held up a finger. "You didn't say any of that to Vane. Standing right in front of him, triple pay on the table, you had every reason to give him the arithmetic. Instead you just said no and looked at him like—" Dess searched for it. "Like you'd seen a ghost wearing his face."

Coral picked up the whetstone again. Set it down. Picked up a stick instead and poked at the cold fire ring, though there'd been no fire there since morning.

"Vane and I have history," he said.

"Everybody has history. History doesn't scare you. I've watched you walk into collapsed tunnels on rotten legs of timber humming hymns."

"This history does."

"Why?"

"Because it's mine."

* * *

That was the answer, then. The drawer, shut again.

Dess should have let it go. Any sensible man would have. But Dess had grown up in company towns where sensible men starved quiet, and he'd made a religion out of not being one of them.

They ate supper around the cook fire—beans, because beans was what they could afford, which Dess noted aloud with a pointed look at Coral that Coral absorbed like ballast. Okonkwo told a story about a bridge inspector he'd known in his riverboat days who was afraid of bridges, and Pell laughed so hard he choked on his coffee, and for an hour the camp was warm and ordinary and Dess almost believed the day had passed clean.

Almost.

Because Coral barely ate. And twice, when the talk swung near the Kestrel valley—Pell asked if they'd ever work that far north, since he'd heard the fishing was good—Coral went still in a way that wasn't natural stillness. It was the stillness of a man holding a door closed from the inside.

Dess had seen that stillness exactly once before. Ten years ago. One night he had never discussed either.

* * *

It had been raining then too—not monsoon rain, but the mean cold drizzle of late autumn, the kind that got under canvas and into bones. They'd been working a branch line in the western hills, Dess newly hired, barely knowing Coral beyond his reputation: the engineer who fixed what the world gave up on. There'd been a third man on that job. Dess remembered him only in pieces now, the way you remember anything you weren't supposed to notice—a laugh that carried across a cut, a hat with a feather in the band, the name *Marrow* spoken once or twice and never again.

He remembered the night itself in worse pieces. Waking in his tent to voices raised low, which is its own strange thing—anger compressed to a whisper is louder than shouting. Coral and another voice he didn't know, a smooth voice, a voice used to being obeyed. Words coming through the canvas in fragments: *the report*, *your signature*, *what you saw*, and once, terrible and clear, *the tunnel*. Then boots on wet gravel, an engine turning over, taillights drowning in the rain.

And then Coral standing alone in the yard, soaked through, staring at the black mouth of a tunnel half a mile up the line as if it were staring back. Dess had come out, meaning to ask, and Coral had looked at him—looked *through* him—and said, in a voice Dess had never heard from him before or since:

"Some things get buried, Dess. Best thing a man can do is shovel honest."

Then he'd walked into his tent, and in the morning he was Coral again, steady-handed, humming over the spike mauls, and the man with the feathered hat was gone from the crew roster as if he'd never been, and the job wrapped up two weeks early, and nobody ever said the name Marrow again.

Until today. Until Silas Vane stood on their dock with his gold watch chain and his easy smile, and said a word that hit Coral like a sledge hits a bent rail.

Dess hadn't imagined it. He'd been watching. When Vane said *Marrow*, Coral's hand had moved—just slightly, just a twitch toward his coat pocket, where he kept the folded map of every dead line he'd ever worked. As if the name lived in there with the map. As if it had always lived there.

* * *

Later, when Okonkwo and Pell had turned in, Dess found Coral at the water's edge, skipping nothing, throwing nothing, just standing with his hands in his pockets looking north up the dark river, in the direction Vane's boat had gone. Toward the Kestrel valley, though you couldn't see it from here. Toward whatever was in that drawer.

"You knew him," Dess said. No preamble. He was done with preambles. "Marrow. He wasn't just a name Vane pulled out of a hat to rattle you. You knew him."

Coral didn't turn. "Go to bed, Dess."

"He was on a crew with us. Ten years back. Western hills. I remember the hat. I remember the night somebody left in the rain, and I remember you standing in that yard looking at that tunnel like it owed you money or blood—I couldn't tell which. And I remember you telling me to shovel honest." Dess stepped closer. "I've shoveled honest for ten years, Coral. I've earned the truth."

Now Coral turned, and in the low light of the banked coals his face was hard to read, which was unusual, because Coral's face was usually the easiest thing in the world to read—open as a schedule board. This was a different face. This was a face built for the occasion.

"I knew him," Coral admitted. "A long time ago. He died. It was an accident, and it was handled, and it broke something in me that I've been walking on ever since. That's the whole of it, Dess. That's the drawer."

"Is it?"

"Yes."

"And Vane was there. That night. That was his voice through my tent wall."

Coral was quiet long enough that the river filled the silence, talking to itself in the dark.

"Vane was there," he said at last.

"And the Kestrel Spur runs through—"

"Don't." The word came out sharp, and Coral caught it, reeled it back, and when he spoke again his voice was level and warm and utterly, perfectly controlled. "Dess. I appreciate what you're doing. I do. But there are things a man carries so the men beside him don't have to. That's not secrecy. That's the job. I lead. You follow. That arrangement has kept everyone breathing for ten years, and I'd take it as a kindness if you'd trust it a while longer."

And there it was. The lie.

Not a lie in the words—every word was probably true, in its way. It was a lie in the shape of them, in the smoothness, in the way Coral's voice went warm like a stove you could lean against. Dess had heard that warmth before, from company men and insurance adjusters and one lawyer who'd promised a widow her settlement by Christmas. It was the sound of a door closing politely.

Dess looked at his friend—at the steadiness of those famous hands, at the careful calm of that rebuilt face—and understood, suddenly and completely, that Coral Crane was going to lie to him until the day one of them died, unless something cracked him open first. And nothing Dess could say would crack him. Nothing but the thing itself, whatever it was, waiting up that drowned valley like a load waiting on a washed-out trestle.

So Dess did the only thing left to do. He lied back.

"All right," he said. He nodded slowly, letting his shoulders drop, letting the fight go out of him like air from a valve. "All right, Coral. Bad country, bad memories, bad man offering the money. I believe you. We won't take the job."

Relief moved through Coral's frame—quick, quickly hidden, but Dess had been watching for it the way you watch a rail gauge for hairline fractures. Relief. That was the tell. A man with nothing to hide doesn't feel relief when you stop asking.

"Glad," Coral said. "Get some sleep. Big day tomorrow—winch cable needs re-laying before the season turns."

"Sure," Dess said. He clapped Coral on the shoulder, felt the muscle underneath flinch almost imperceptibly, and walked back toward the tents.

At the flap of his tent he paused and looked back. Coral hadn't moved. He stood at the water's edge with his hands in his pockets, a silhouette carved out of the last coal-light, facing north.

Dess crawled onto his cot and lay in the dark listening to the river, and thought about drawers, and shovels, and honesty, and how the deepest holes are the ones a man digs for himself and calls it protection.

In the morning, he decided, he'd talk to Okonkwo. Not to go behind Coral's back—never that—but because if the crew voted on the Kestrel Spur, the way crews vote when winter's close and the money's triple, then Coral wouldn't have to refuse for them. Wouldn't have to carry it alone. Wouldn't have to lie quite so hard.

And maybe, Dess thought as sleep took him, maybe whatever was buried up that valley needed digging up anyway. Shovel honest. Coral's own words, ten years old, rusted now but true.

Some things get buried. Dess rolled over. But water has a way of unearthing what a man plants.

Out on the dark river, far upstream, thunder muttered—too early in the season, too far north, too close to the Kestrel valley—and the first fat drops of a rain that meant to become a monsoon began ticking against the canvas like fingers counting.
