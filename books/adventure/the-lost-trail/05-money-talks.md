## 5. Money Talks

Pell had never seen a man refuse three thousand dollars before. He'd seen men refuse two hundred, once—a mule skinner up in the Coyle country who wouldn't haul for a mine owner he'd sworn off—and the whole camp had talked about it for a week like they'd witnessed a bear walk into church. But Coral Crane turning down Silas Vane on the flat rock by the river, turning him down slow and polite as a man declining a second helping he didn't trust—that was something else entirely. That was a thing Pell turned over in his mind all that night while the fire burned down to orange eyes and Okonkwo's snores kept time with the water.

Three thousand dollars. Split four ways. Pell did the arithmetic the way he always did, twice, then a third time hoping the number would shrink out of shame. Seven hundred fifty dollars apiece. His father's debt to the Harrow Mercantile was six hundred and forty, counting the interest old Man Harrow tacked on every quarter like a barnacle. Seven hundred fifty would clear it with a hundred left over—enough for a new coat, enough for seed, enough to walk into that store with his chin level instead of ducking past the window like a thief.

And Coral had said no.

* * *

Morning came gray and cold off the river, mist standing on the water in columns like the ghosts of bridge pilings. Pell woke first, as usual, and got the coffee going, because that was his job and he guarded it jealously—the one thing on this crew nobody could take from him. Dess emerged from her bedroll already scowling at the sky, which meant she'd been thinking about Vane too. Okonkwo came last, unhurried, rolling his bedding with the precision of a man folding a flag.

Coral was already down at the boat, checking the caulking, which was nonsense because he'd checked it twice yesterday. Pell watched him run his thumb along the seam where the planks met and thought: *he's not looking at the boat. He's looking through it.* At something upstream. At something ten years back.

They ate breakfast in the particular silence of people who all want to say the same thing and are waiting to see who breaks first. It was Dess, naturally.

"So," she said, tearing her biscuit in half with more force than the biscuit deserved. "We're just going to pretend that didn't happen."

"Nothing happened," Coral said. "A man made an offer. I declined it."

"A man made an offer," Dess repeated. "Triple rate. Winter work. Kestrel Spur." She said the name of the spur the way you'd set a card face-up on a table. "You didn't even let him finish talking."

"I heard enough."

"You heard the number is what you heard." Dess leaned forward. Her hair was coming loose from its braid and she didn't bother with it, which among Dess's signals ranked somewhere between a warning shot and a declaration of war. "Coral. We've eaten beans since September. My boots have more patch than leather. Pell's been sending every cent home to—" She caught herself, glanced at Pell, softened by a degree. "We're all carrying weight. You know we are."

"I know it."

"Then say why."

Pell held his breath. This was the moment—he felt it in his chest like a held note—the moment when Coral would either open some door in himself or weld it shut, and Pell had learned over two years on this crew that you could count on Coral for anything except that door.

Coral looked at the river. When he spoke, his voice was even and reasonable and completely closed. "Silas Vane doesn't hire men. He spends them. Whatever's on that spur, he wants it done fast and cheap and he wants witnesses who'll swear to whatever story he tells afterward. I've worked for men like him. So has everyone in this camp, if they've worked long enough. The money's real. What comes attached to it isn't worth the paper the contract's printed on."

"That's not a reason," Dess said. "That's a speech."

"It's the reason I've got."

Okonkwo spoke for the first time, quiet, without looking up from his cup. "The man's afraid of something."

The word landed on the fire like a wet branch. Smoke went up wrong. Coral's jaw tightened—not much, but Pell had spent two years learning to read that face the way other men read gauges, and he saw the needle jump.

"I'm careful," Coral said. "There's a difference."

"Sure," Okonkwo said, and drank his coffee, and let the difference sit there unexplained between them like a stone in a boot.

* * *

Pell found Coral at midday mending the winch cable, and worked up his nerve the way you work up nerve to touch a stove you've already been burned by.

"My dad owes six hundred forty dollars," Pell said. "To Harrow's. In Corben Falls."

Coral's hands didn't stop moving on the cable, but they slowed. "I figured it was something like that."

"He had a bad year. Then a worse one. The orchard froze twice running and he borrowed against next year, and then next year froze too, and now the interest eats faster than he can pay. He's sixty-one years old, Mr. Crane. He stands behind that counter twelve hours a day so Harrow won't call the note, and Harrow calls it anyway, every quarter, just to watch him flinch." Pell heard his own voice going tight and hated it, but couldn't stop. "Seven hundred fifty dollars. That's what my share would be. I've done the figuring eleven times."

"You should do it a twelfth," Coral said quietly. "Numbers don't change, but it helps."

Pell almost laughed. Almost. "Why won't you take the job? And don't give me the speech. I've heard the speech. I'm asking for the true thing underneath it."

For a long moment Coral said nothing. He coiled the repaired cable in slow, exact loops, each one the same diameter, and Pell understood that the looping was how the man thought—hands busy so the mind could go somewhere it didn't like going.

"When I was your age," Coral said finally, "I took a job because the money was good and the man offering it seemed fine. Better than fine. Charming." The loops stopped. "Some jobs cost more than they pay. Some costs don't show up on any ledger. That's all I can tell you, Pell. I wish I could tell you more."

"Why can't you?"

"Because some doors," Coral said, "you only get to open once, and I opened mine already." He slung the coil over his shoulder and stood, and for a second—just a second—Pell saw something move behind his eyes like water rising in a well shaft. Then it was gone, capped, and Coral was Coral again: steady, competent, unreachable. "Talk to the others. If the crew votes to take it, that's a crew decision. I won't stand in front of a vote."

"But you'd stand in front of Vane?"

Coral paused at the edge of the trees. "That's different," he said, and walked into the shade, and Pell sat alone with the winch and the river and the feeling—cold despite the sun—that he'd just watched a man promise to walk into a fire rather than let anyone else touch the matches.

* * *

They voted that evening, properly, around the fire, because Dess insisted on proper form. She'd grown up in a mining town where her father had been a union steward, and she believed in votes the way Coral believed in caulked seams: as the thin membrane between order and drowning.

"Those for taking Vane's offer," Dess said. "Kestrel Spur, reopening, triple rate, completion before monsoon season. Speak plain."

Okonkwo went first. He was a long time answering, staring into the flames, and when he spoke it was slow, weighing each word like ballast. "I have a sister in Meridian with three children and a husband who lost his hand to a stamping mill. The company paid him off with forty dollars and a pat on the head. I send her what I can. Triple rate means I send double what I can." He looked up. "I don't like the smell of this job. But I've smelled worse for less. I'm for it."

"Dess?"

Dess folded her arms. "I'm for it, and I want it noted that I think Coral knows something he isn't saying, and I want it further noted that I intend to find out what." She said it looking straight at him, giving him the courtesy of the threat to his face. "But loyalty runs both directions in this crew, and right now the crew needs feeding. For it."

"Pell?"

Every eye turned to him. Pell's mouth had gone dry. He thought of his father behind the counter, stooped a little more each winter, flinching at the quarterly letter. He thought of Coral's face when Okonkwo had said *afraid*, and the well-shaft look, and the words *some costs don't show up on any ledger*. He thought about how Coral had said *I wish I could tell you more* and meant it, meant it the way a man means it when he's standing on a trapdoor and calling it a floor.

"I'm for it," Pell said. "Against my better judgment, which I'd like noted too."

"Noted," Dess said dryly. "Coral. You heard the vote. Three to nothing against your recommendation. Crew rules say you lead us or step aside."

The silence stretched. The fire popped. Somewhere across the river an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

Pell watched Coral's hands. They were still, flat on his knees, and Pell knew—knew with the certainty of two years of reading this man—that inside, nothing was still at all. He watched Coral weigh it, whatever *it* was, the secret thing, the door, the trapdoor. Watched him arrive somewhere.

"I'll lead you," Coral said.

Relief went around the fire like a passed flask. But Coral wasn't finished. He leaned forward, and the firelight carved his face into planes and shadows, and his voice came out low and absolutely level, the voice he used on bad cable and bad weather.

"On conditions. Mine. Non-negotiable." He raised one finger. "First: we do the job by the book. Every inspection, every test, every measurement written down and signed. If Vane's foremen push us to cut corners, we cut nothing, and if they insist, we walk, pay or no pay. I'd rather owe Harrow than bury any of you."

"Second:" another finger. "Nobody works alone. Not in the tunnel, not on the line, nowhere. Pairs, always. If your partner says come out, you come out, and arguments happen after."

"Third." He paused, and Pell saw the hesitation, small as a hairline crack. "If I tell you to run, you run. You don't ask why. You don't come back for me. Is that understood?"

The fire cracked. Nobody answered fast enough.

"Coral," Dess started, "that third one sounds like—"

"Is it understood?" The level voice, but under it, faint as a rail hum, something else. Something urgent.

"Understood," Okonkwo said slowly.

"Understood," Dess said, unhappy.

Pell swallowed. "Understood," he said, and hated himself a little for how easily the word came, and hated the vote more, and hated most of all the look on Coral's face as he nodded once and settled back—not relief, not gratitude, but something grimly settled, like a man signing papers he'd read all the way through and signed anyway.

Guilt dressed up as duty, Pell thought later, lying awake. Or duty dressed up as guilt. He couldn't tell which way the garment hung, only that Coral wore it like it fit, and that frightened him more than Vane's smile had.

* * *

They broke camp two days later. The boat rode low with gear; the river ran high with early melt. As they pushed off from the bank, Pell took the steering oar and Coral the bow, and the current took them the way currents do—gently at first, then with the indifference of something much larger than you.

It was near dusk, the hills going purple, when Pell saw it: Coral standing in the bow with his worn map unfolded, the famous map of dead lines, holding it toward the last light. From the stern Pell couldn't see the markings. He only saw Coral's thumb, moving along some route only he could read, pressing hard on one place, over and over, like a man testing a bruise to see if it still hurt.

It still hurt. Pell could tell from where he sat.

Ahead, the valley narrowed. Ahead, somewhere up that drowned green corridor, the Kestrel Spur waited with its flooded grades and its sealed tunnel and whatever truth Coral had welded shut ten years ago. The water carried them toward it at the river's own pace, which was to say: faster than any of them wanted, slower than none of them could stop.

"Mr. Crane," Pell called forward, because silence was worse. "What's the first job on a reopened line? Once we get there?"

Coral folded the map. When he answered, he didn't turn around, and the wind off the water thinned his voice to something almost like an echo.

"The tunnel," he said. "Vane wants the tunnel surveyed first."

The oar creaked in Pell's grip. Behind them the camp smoke faded; ahead the hills closed like a book.

He rowed on anyway.
