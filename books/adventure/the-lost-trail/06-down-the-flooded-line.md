## 6. Down the Flooded Line

The valley had been a valley once. Now it was a lake with opinions.

Coral stood at the tiller of the *Maggie B*—a flat-bottomed workboat older than Pell and about as trustworthy—and watched the water slide past the gunwale in long brown ribbons. The Kestrel valley had flooded eleven years ago when the dam company went bust mid-construction, leaving an unfinished spillway and a reservoir nobody maintained. The river had done the rest. It had swallowed orchards, fence lines, a church steeple that still broke the surface on the north shore like a drowning man's last knuckle. And it had swallowed the rail line.

Most of it.

Here and there the rails rose out of the water like the spines of some enormous sleeping animal—a quarter mile of track on a stone causeway, then a gap where the flood had won, then track again. The crews who'd built the Kestrel Spur had engineered for a hundred-year flood and gotten a permanent one. Even drowned, the line refused to lie down flat.

"Look at that," Dess said from the bow. She had her boot up on a crate of survey stakes and her hat tipped back, reading the water the way other people read newspapers. "Causeway's holding. Somebody built that to outlive God."

"Somebody built it to outlive an inspector," Coral said. "Same thing, most days."

Okonkwo grunted from the stern bench where he sat coiling rope with the patience of a man who believed every knot deserved its own hour. Pell was amidships, elbows on the gunwale, watching the flooded treetops slide past—oaks standing to their shoulders in the lake, dead and silver, their branches full of cormorants.

"How deep?" Pell asked.

"Over the old grade? Fifteen, twenty feet in the low spots." Coral eased the tiller over, threading between a fence post and the ghost of a barn roof just under the surface—their wake hissed across it. "The spur climbs east toward Kestrel Pass. Where we're headed, the line comes back above waterline. That's why Vane wants boats instead of mules."

"That's why Vane wants *us*," Dess said. "Nobody else would run this stretch without a priest aboard."

Coral didn't answer. He was looking at the third trestle.

He'd told himself he wouldn't recognize anything. Ten years was a long time; water rearranges a country; memory lies about distances. But there it was, rising out of the lake exactly where his traitor mind said it would be: the pilings of the old Kestrel trestle number three, black with rot above the waterline, marching off toward the eastern shore in a crooked row. A heron stood on the fourth piling, motionless, like a sentry that had forgotten which war it was guarding.

He had walked that trestle. He could still feel the give of the planks under his boots, the way the whole structure breathed when a loaded ore car crossed it—a slow, living sway, like standing on the ribs of something alive. He could feel the morning air of the day they'd surveyed it, cold enough to see your breath, Marrow beside him with his notebook open against his chest, complaining cheerfully about the coffee.

Coral's hands tightened on the tiller until the wood creaked.

"You all right back there?" Okonkwo asked quietly. Okonkwo asked everything quietly. It was his way of giving you room to lie or not.

"Fine," Coral said. "Current's tricky past the pilings. Watch your gear."

It wasn't entirely a lie. The current *was* tricky. The whole valley drained toward the dam breach on the south end, and where the old channel ran, the water moved with a long muscular pull you could feel through the hull, patient as a hand on your ankle.

They passed the trestle at a distance of forty yards. Nobody spoke. The heron lifted off the piling as they went by, unhurried, flapping away east—the direction they were headed, the direction of Kestrel Spur, the tunnel, and everything Coral had spent ten years not thinking about at a volume that kept the whole crew awake some nights without any of them knowing why.

* * *

They made camp that first night on a gravel bar that had been, according to the map burned into Coral's skull, the town of Lowry's Crossing. Population four hundred, once. A depot, a hotel, a sawmill. Now: gravel, willow scrub, and the corner of a brick chimney leaning out of the shallows like a headstone.

Pell built a fire while Dess and Okonkwo got the tarps rigged. Coral checked the boat.

That was the thing eating at him, more than the chimney, more than the trestle. The *Maggie B* leaked. Not dangerously—not yet—but she leaked, a slow seep along a seam on the port side that he'd found with his palm on the first morning and hadn't mentioned to anyone. He'd stuffed it with oakum and tallow that afternoon while the others set up camp, working by firelight, and told himself it would hold. Boats always leak. You just have to stay ahead of them.

But they were three days from the Spur, monsoon season was coming early—he could smell it in the air, that green metallic weight that meant the sky was loading itself—and if that seam opened up in open water with the wind on the lake...

He bailed the bilge by lantern light, measured the seep with his eye. An inch in two hours. Manageable. Barely.

"You're doing that thing," Dess said behind him.

He hadn't heard her come down the bank. She stood with her arms folded, firelight catching the gray in her hair. Twenty years running crews together, and she could read him like a gauge.

"What thing."

"The thing where you fix something twice and tell nobody." She nodded at the oakum poking from the seam. "How bad?"

"Bad enough that I fixed it. Good enough that I fixed it right."

"Coral."

"It'll hold to the Spur," he said. "After that we're on dry grade, and I'll re-caulk her proper before we ever take her out again."

Dess looked at him a moment longer, the way she did when she was deciding whether to spend a fight she couldn't afford. Then she crouched, took a handful of the oakum, rubbed it between her fingers, and grunted—the sound of a professional approving another professional's panic.

"Vane's paying triple," she said. "You know what I keep coming back to? Triple pay means Vane expects triple trouble. Men like him don't pay extra for nothing."

"He pays extra so nobody asks questions about the rest of the bill."

"And what's the rest of the bill?"

Coral fed a stick of oakum back into the seam and pressed it home with his thumb. "Ask him. He loves talking."

Dess snorted. But she stayed crouched there, watching the water lap the hull, and after a while she said, not looking at him: "Ten years ago. Before I signed on with you. There was a job up in the Kestrels everybody talked about and nobody talked about. Survey crew. Man died."

The fire crackled up the bank. Somewhere out on the dark water, a loon called, that wild wavering note that sounds like laughter and grief arguing.

"Lot of men die around railroads," Coral said.

"This one was different. This one, the company paid out quick and quiet and the report came back saying ground shift, act of nature, nobody's fault. My cousin worked the relief crew that carried him out." She finally looked at him. "Name was Marrow."

For a moment the night tilted. Coral felt the water rise—not the lake, the other water, the one inside—and then he put it down the way he'd learned to, the way you ease a heavy crate onto a dolly without letting it slam.

"Old news," he said.

"Yeah," Dess agreed softly. "Old news." She stood, stretched, and started back up the bank. At the top she paused. "You'd tell us. If a job was something worse than a job."

"I'd tell you."

She held his eyes a second longer than was comfortable, then went to her bedroll. Coral sat alone with the boat and the bilge and the loon crying out over the drowned town, and he did not sleep much.

* * *

The second day the valley narrowed and the water deepened, and the drowned railroad became a scripture they traveled by.

That was how Okonkwo put it, anyway, and Coral let him. They ran the line east all morning: causeway, gap, causeway, each stretch of surviving track a verse, each gap a silence you had to interpret. Coral read the water for them aloud because he couldn't help it—the knowledge was in him deeper than the map, deeper than the scar. *Milepost 41, cut through shale, watch for submerged rock on the left. Milepost 44, the creek crossing—the culvert's still open, you can hear it pulling.* Pell hung on every word like it was adventure serial stuff. Dess watched Coral say things like *the culvert's still open* about a place he claimed to barely remember, and said nothing, and filed it wherever she filed things.

Around noon they came to the water tower.

It stood where the line left the lake and began to climb—a latticed timber tower, eighty feet of blackened wood, listing maybe ten degrees but standing, absurdly, impossibly, its tank hooped with iron bands gone to rust-lace. The flood had reached only its ankles here. Behind it the grade rose out of the water for good, dry gravel and weed-grown ties climbing toward a wall of green mountainside, and above it all the mountains themselves, the Kestrels, blue-black under a sky going the color of wet slate.

"There she is," Pell breathed. "The Spur."

"Part of her," Coral said. His voice came out strange and he cleared it. "Line runs nine miles up the valley wall to the pass. Three tunnels. The big one's tunnel two—half a mile through the shoulder of Kestrel Peak."

"Where the rockfall was," Pell said. "Right? That's the story. The whole reason the spur closed."

"That's the story," Coral agreed.

Okonkwo looked at him. Just looked, for a beat too long, then went forward to help Dess warp the boat alongside the old loading platform that jutted from the base of the water tower, planks silvered and sprung but solid underfoot.

They beached the *Maggie B* in the lee of the platform and got her lines fast to a mooring ring somebody had bolted into the timbers fifty years ago. Coral stepped onto the platform and his boots found the boards and the boards found his memory, and for one unguarded second he was thirty years old again, stepping onto this same platform with a clipboard and a thermos, Marrow laughing about something, the whole valley dry and loud with birdsong—

He gripped the mooring post until the feeling passed.

The grade climbed ahead of them through the trees, and it was wrong. That was the thing he noticed first, before anything else, the thing that turned his stomach slow and cold. The grade was *cleared*. Brush cut back. Ties replaced in stretches—new creosote, dark and oily, gleaming among the gray old ones. Someone had been working this line already. Weeks of someone. Maybe months.

"Vane said the spur was untouched since closure," Dess said slowly. She'd seen it too. Everyone had.

"Vane says a lot of things."

Pell crouched by a tie, touched the creosote, sniffed it. "Fresh. Month old, maybe less." He looked up the grade, where the line vanished into green shadow. "Somebody's been getting ready for us."

"For the job," Coral corrected. "Not for us."

But even as he said it, he was counting things a preparation crew leaves behind, and the count was wrong. No tool cache. No supply dump. No camp ash. Crews that work a line live on it, and living leaves litter—tin cans, rope ends, burn piles. This grade had been groomed, not lived on. Worked by people who arrived, did their piece, and left no trace of themselves. Who worked like that?

Men who didn't intend to stay. Men whose employer wanted no evidence they'd ever been there at all.

A wind came down the valley, and with it the smell he'd been dreading since the lake—that green iron weight, stronger now, rolling off the peaks like smoke off a forge. The monsoon, loading. Early. Days early, maybe.

"We make the tunnel by tomorrow evening if the weather holds," Coral said. "If it doesn't hold, we make camp and hold tight. Nobody works ahead of me on this line. Nobody goes anywhere alone. Understood?"

Three nods. Then Pell, squinting up-valley: "Boss. Smoke."

Everyone turned. Far up the grade, where the trees thinned near the first tunnel portal, a thread of woodsmoke stood straight up in the still air. A cook fire. Somebody was up there now, tonight, waiting on the line Vane swore was empty.

Coral looked at the smoke a long time. Ten years ago he had stood at that same portal with rain coming and a partner's voice in his ear, and he had made a choice in ninety seconds that had cost a man everything and bought a crew their lives, and he had carried it ever since like ballast in his chest. Now the valley was carrying him back up toward it, one tie at a time, and the water was rising everywhere—in the lake, in the sky, in the seams of the boat, in the seams of himself.

"Get the gear squared away," he said. "Double-lash everything. We're taking the *Maggie B* up the last stretch at first light, and I want her dry when we do."

Okonkwo paused, rope over his shoulder. "And the smoke?"

Coral looked up the grade, at the thin gray thread standing against the bruised sky, at the black mouth of the mountain beyond it where his whole buried life waited with its hand out.

"The smoke," he said, "we go meet."
