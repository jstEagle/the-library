## 7. The Stranger at the Dock

The dock at Harrow's Landing was the kind of place Okonkwo trusted on sight, which is to say he didn't trust it at all. It leaned. Every plank had a different opinion about how much weight it could carry, and the pilings wore a collar of green scum that rose and fell with the river like something breathing. A tin-roofed supply shed squatted at the landward end, its walls papered with faded bills for tonics and mining stock and a circus that had come through—according to the date—eleven years ago and never, apparently, left in spirit.

"Keep your weight forward," Okonkwo said, throwing the bow line to Pell as they came alongside. "This dock will lie to you."

"Everything lies," Pell said cheerfully, catching it. "It's what docks do."

Okonkwo grunted. He liked the boy well enough, but there were days when Pell's cheerfulness felt like weather from some other country—a climate Okonkwo had no use for. He stepped onto the planks himself, testing each one before committing his full weight, an old habit from bridge crews where a man who trusted wood died of it.

They had been two days on the water since leaving the river camp, poling and paddling the leaky flatboat up the drowned valley of the Kestrel, and Okonkwo's shoulders carried the ache of it. The valley itself was beautiful in the way drowned things are beautiful—the rails of the old spur appearing and vanishing beneath tea-colored water, fence posts standing to their chins, a barn roof tilted like a hat knocked askew. Coral had spent most of those two days silent at the tiller, reading the waterline against the hillsides, and whatever he was reading made his face do something Okonkwo had never seen it do before.

Coral Crane was not a man with many faces. He had the working face, the tired face, and once in a great while, around Dess, something almost young. But these last two days he'd worn a face Okonkwo could only call *remembering*, and it sat wrong on him, like a coat cut for another man.

Harrow's Landing existed for one reason: it was the last dry ground before the spur climbed into the mountains, and Vane's operation had contracted with the dock's owner to stage supplies there. Crates were stacked under canvas tarps—cement, blasting powder, lamp oil, canned goods enough to feed thirty men through a season. Two of Vane's foremen lounged by the shed, and they watched the boat come in with the particular stillness of men counting heads.

"Four," one of them murmured to the other, just loud enough. Okonkwo heard it. He was built for hearing things—had grown up in a house where hearing things wrong got people hurt—and he filed it away without letting his stride change.

Dess went to negotiate with the dock man about freight, because Dess could haggle the shine off a coin, and Pell went with her, already talking about whether the crates might hold peaches. That left Okonkwo standing guard over the boat and Coral standing at the end of the dock, looking up-valley at the water and the drowned rails and whatever else he alone could see.

That was when the stranger came down the path.

* * *

She came out of the trees on the trail from the east, and Okonkwo marked three things before she'd taken ten steps.

First: she walked like someone used to heavier country than this. Her boots were mountain boots, resoled twice, and she placed her feet the way climbers do, heel first, testing, even though the trail here was soft and easy.

Second: she carried a leather case slung across her back, long and flat, strapped like a rifle but too light for one. Instruments, then. Survey gear.

Third—and this was the thing that put the cold in Okonkwo's stomach—she never once looked at the supplies. Not at the tarps, not at the crates, not at the foremen by the shed. In a place like Harrow's Landing, everything worth looking at was stacked right there under canvas, and every soul who came down that trail looked at it. She looked past all of it, straight along the dock, to where Coral stood at the end with his back half-turned.

Then she adjusted. It was small, the adjustment—her eyes flicking to the water, to the boat, to the middle distance, arranging herself into the posture of a traveler taking in a view—but Okonkwo had seen it done before, and he knew what it meant. She had been caught looking, and she had covered it inside a second and a half.

He moved himself casually between her line and Coral, the way you'd shift your weight on a bad trestle.

"Afternoon," she said. Her voice was low and even, an accent underneath it that had been sanded down by years of speaking English to people who didn't care where she was from. "Is this the Vane survey party?"

"It's a boat," Okonkwo said.

A pause. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—not wide, but real, the smile of someone who appreciated a door held shut in her face more than most people would. "Fair enough. Ilsa Reyes." She dug in her coat and produced a folded paper, holding it out. "Hydrologist. Contracted through the Vane company office. I'm to measure flow rates and gauge the flood crest on the upper Kestrel before the monsoon sets in."

Okonkwo took the paper without touching her fingers. It was a contract, properly drawn, stamped with the company seal, signed by a name he recognized from the freight manifests—Vane's chief clerk. All of it in order. All of it exactly what a document should look like when a company wanted a hydrologist on a job.

That was the problem. Everything about Ilsa Reyes was exactly what it should look like.

"You'll want to talk to Dess Marlowe," he said. "She handles the crew book." He turned and bellowed toward the shed, "DESS. Company man—or woman—for you."

* * *

Dess took the contract, read it twice, asked four questions in the time most men would ask one, and ended by hiring Ilsa Reyes on the spot, because the numbers were good and a hydrologist would tell them when the river meant to kill them, which was information Okonkwo admitted—even in his suspicion—was worth having.

"I've worked flooded lines before," Ilsa said, while they loaded her case into the boat. "The Verde diversion, up north. And a year on the Sorrow Fork after the slide." She stowed her gear with quick, economical movements, tying everything down against chop. "Your hull takes water."

"She weeps a little," Pell said. "We encourage her hourly."

"Bailing schedule?"

"Every forty minutes if we're lucky, twenty if we're not."

Ilsa nodded and said nothing else, and Okonkwo watched her say nothing else. A talkative liar was a nuisance; you could tire them out, trip them up, let them hang themselves with their own rope. A quiet liar was a different animal entirely. A quiet liar chose every word in advance.

They cast off within the hour, the flatboat riding low with freight, and the valley closed around them again—green water, gray stone, the rails surfacing like the spines of sleeping animals. Ilsa settled herself amidships near the instruments, opened her case, and began assembling a current meter with hands that knew the work cold.

And she watched Coral.

Not constantly. That was the artistry of it. She'd watch the water for a while, take a reading, write in a small notebook with a pencil stub. Then, in the natural course of glancing around—checking the sky, checking the banks, checking anything a careful person checks—her eyes would pass over Coral at the tiller and *stay*, a half-second longer than passing warranted. Sometimes two seconds. Once, when Coral leaned forward to read something in the water ahead, she watched him so steadily that Okonkwo, watching her watch, nearly missed the rock shelf themselves.

"Shoal," Okonkwo said, and Coral hauled the tiller over, and the boat slid past the brown teeth of the ledge close enough to touch.

"Thank you," Ilsa said, to Okonkwo.

"For what?"

"The shoal." She smiled her small real smile again. "You see everything first. It's a gift. My father had it—he could walk into a room and know which chair was broken before anyone sat down."

"Useful in a hydrologist," Okonkwo said.

"Useful everywhere." She went back to her meter.

Okonkwo turned his face to the water and thought about chairs.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar below the ruins of a station whose name had washed off its sign years ago. The fire was small—they were conserving fuel until the supply cache—and supper was beans and salt pork and hard bread softened in the pot, and it was, as always, the best hour of the day. This was the thing about the crew that outsiders never understood, the thing Vane's money couldn't buy: the food was poor and the work was brutal and the future was a question mark, but between the last light and the first sleep, with the fire talking and the river talking, they were rich in the only currency Okonkwo had ever believed in. Each other.

Pell told a story about a mule he'd known in his first railroad year, a mule named Deacon who could open any gate on the line and considered this a sacred duty. Dess corrected three details and improved two others. Even Coral laughed—one short bark, surprised out of him—and for a moment the remembering-face was gone and he was just their engineer, tired and human, passing the bread.

Okonkwo let himself enjoy it for exactly as long as it lasted. Then he noticed Ilsa Reyes sitting slightly outside the firelight, notebook on her knee, and noticed that she wasn't writing.

She was watching Coral laugh.

There was nothing casual in it now. The camp was dark beyond the fire ring; there was no one else to glance at, no cover story of general vigilance. She simply looked at Coral Crane laughing over hard bread, and her expression was the strangest thing Okonkwo had seen in a long time, because it wasn't suspicion and it wasn't calculation. It was grief. Old, practiced, carefully rationed grief—the kind a person wears in private, caught wearing it in public by accident.

Then she saw Okonkwo seeing her, and it shut off like a lamp, and she bent to her notebook and wrote something brief, and the moment was gone as if it had never existed.

But Okonkwo had grown up learning to read faces in a house where misreading them was dangerous, and he had not misread that.

* * *

Later, when the others had rolled into their blankets, Okonkwo found Coral awake at the edge of the gravel bar, feeding twigs to the last coals. Okonkwo sat beside him without asking. That was their arrangement; it had been since a night in the third year, when Coral had found Okonkwo standing watch over nothing at 3 a.m. and had simply sat down next to him and said nothing either, and both of them had understood that this was a language they shared.

"Our new hydrologist," Okonkwo said.

"What about her?"

"She doesn't watch the water."

Coral's twig hand paused, resumed. "She took readings all afternoon. I saw the book."

"She takes readings. She watches you." Okonkwo kept his voice level, conversational, pitched under the river noise. "At the dock, before she introduced herself, she looked straight at you and then pretended she hadn't. On the boat, all day, in little pieces—enough to count if you're counting. Tonight, at the fire, when you laughed—" He stopped. How to describe it? "She looked at you like a woman looking at a photograph of somebody she lost."

The silence went on long enough that the coals ate through two more twigs.

"She's company-hired," Coral said finally. "Vane's office. Dess checked the contract."

"Yes. And everything on that contract is exactly correct. Which is what bothers me." Okonkwo turned to look at his friend directly. In the coal-light, Coral's face was the remembering-face again, and older than it had been on the water. "Ten years I've worked with you, Crane. You taught me to read a bent rail by sound. So hear me now the way I'd hear you. Something about this job knows you. Not the pay, not the spur, not the monsoon. *You.* And that woman came here to be near whatever it knows."

Coral stared into the fire. For a moment Okonkwo thought he would speak—that the whole buried thing, whatever it was, the thing Dess circled and Pell sensed and Okonkwo had always chosen not to press, might finally come up out of the ground like a rail surfacing from the flood.

Instead Coral said, quietly: "Maybe she's exactly what she says. Maybe the company wants its flood data."

"And maybe the river's made of whiskey. I've drunk worse theories." Okonkwo stood, brushed gravel from his knees. "I'm not telling you to turn her out. She may save our lives with that meter before this is done. I'm telling you I'll be watching. And Crane—" he waited until Coral looked up, "—whatever's waiting for us up this valley, you don't stand in front of it alone. That's not a request. That's the crew book."

Coral held his eyes for a long moment. "Go sleep, Okonkwo."

"I will. One of us should be rested." He walked back toward the blankets, and behind him he heard the twigs stop feeding, heard Coral sit alone with the coals and the dark water, and did not envy him it.

* * *

Okonkwo woke once in the black hours, as he always did, some internal watchman making its rounds. The fire was ash. The river ran loud. And across the dead fire pit, Ilsa Reyes was awake too, sitting wrapped in her coat, her notebook open on her knees.

She wasn't writing. In the faint starlight she was looking at the page—at what Okonkwo now understood must be something written long before tonight, something she'd brought with her up this drowned valley—and her thumb moved slowly across the paper in a small repeated motion, back and forth, the way a hand moves over a face in a photograph.

Then she closed the book, tied it shut, and looked up, and found Okonkwo watching her across the ashes.

Neither of them moved. Neither of them spoke. The river filled the silence between them, running down out of the mountains, out of the dark, out of the direction of a tunnel neither of them mentioned, carrying everything it carried toward the sea.

In the morning, Dess announced they'd reach the tunnel mouth by dusk.

Okonkwo saw Ilsa's hands go still on her pack straps—just for a breath, just long enough—and he began, quietly, to count.
