## 8. Tunnel Mouth

The river narrowed all morning, and by noon the valley had closed around us like a fist.

I stood at the bow of the lead boat with a pole in my hands and the water running brown and fast beneath us, carrying branches, fence posts, half a barn door painted the color of old blood. Ten years since I'd come up this way, and the valley remembered me better than I wanted it to. There was the shelf of gray rock where we'd camped the first night of the survey — Marrow had cooked trout in a coffee can over green willow smoke and sworn it was the finest meal in the territory. There was the bend where the rails went under the flood line for good, steel disappearing into the current like something being swallowed whole. And there, dead ahead, rising out of the mist the way it rose out of my sleep, was the shoulder of Kestrel Mountain.

The tunnel mouth sat two hundred feet above the waterline, a black slot in the rock face, sealed behind a timber bulkhead that somebody had built badly and abandoned quickly. Even from the boat I could see the rot in it. Even from the boat I could feel it looking back.

"You're doing that thing," Pell said beside me.

"What thing."

"Where you go somewhere else." He was poling the stern, seventeen years old and all elbows, his hat soaked through because he refused to wear the oilskins properly. "Your eyes go flat like a pond in winter."

"Watch the channel," I said. "There's a submerged crib wall off the left bank. It'll take the bottom out of this boat if we clip it."

He watched the channel. But I felt him watching me too, the way young men do when they've decided someone is worth studying, and I kept my face level and my hands easy on the pole, and neither of those things were true of what was happening inside me.

Because here is what nobody tells you about the past: it doesn't wait for you underground like a body in a coffin. It climbs. It comes up through the water table of your life, slow and patient, until one day you're standing in a field you thought was safe and your boots are soaking in it.

* * *

We made the landing below the tunnel at midafternoon — a gravel fan where the old construction spur had once run switchbacks up the mountainside, now a beach of slate and driftwood. Okonkwo brought the second boat in clean and Dess brought the third in crooked, swearing at the current in three languages, which meant she was in a fine mood. We hauled the boats above the flood line, stacked the crates, and I set Pell and Okonkwo to pitching canvas while Dess walked the shoreline with her hands jammed in her pockets, reading the ground the way she read everything: skeptically.

"There's tracks here," she called after ten minutes. "Fresh ones. Boot prints, mule sign, wheel ruts. Somebody's been running traffic on this beach."

I came down and looked. She was right. The gravel was churned in a wide swath leading up toward the old switchback grade, and the prints were days old at most, pressed sharp into the damp stone before the last rain.

"Vane said his advance crew wouldn't be up for another week," Okonkwo said, coming to stand over the tracks. He didn't say anything else. He didn't have to. In the four years he'd worked for me, Okonkwo had developed a whole vocabulary of things he declined to say, and every one of them landed harder than shouting.

"Maybe they moved fast," Pell offered. "Triple pay, right? Men move fast for triple pay."

"Men move fast for triple pay," Dess agreed. "They don't move fast with mule-drawn freight wagons and a portable forge." She crouched and touched a black smudge of coal dust ground into the gravel. "This is a working camp already. Has been for a while."

I looked up the switchbacks, into the trees, at the place where the grade disappeared against the rock, and my stomach did something slow and cold that I recognized from bad crossings and collapsing trestles — the body knowing before the mind admits it.

"We'll go up," I said. "All of us. Nobody wanders."

"Coral," Dess said quietly, just for me. "You knew about this."

"I didn't."

"You suspected."

I didn't answer that, and she let me not answer, which was its own kind of accusation, and we climbed.

* * *

The old grade was still sound under the brush — Vane's crews had cleared it recently, cut stumps pale and fresh along both edges, laid corduroy over the soft sections. Whoever had done the work was good. That bothered me more than anything else so far. Sloppy men make sloppy camps; you can read their carelessness from a distance and know what you're walking into. Careful men are the ones who frighten me.

We came around the last elbow of the mountain with the afternoon sun slanting gold across the rock face, and there it was.

Kestrel Tunnel.

The bulkhead across the mouth had been rebuilt. New timber, new bolts, a fresh plank walkway running up to it, lanterns hung on iron hooks either side of the entrance — unlit now, but hung, waiting. A derrick stood off to the left with a steam winch under a tarpaulin. Two freight tents, a cook shack with a proper iron stovepipe, cordwood stacked in cords, a powder magazine set back against the rock with a lightning rod on top. Mules picketed in a corral of new-cut poles. Everything squared, everything staked, everything oiled and greased and ready.

And men. Eight or nine of them, moving through the camp with the unhurried economy of people who have been there long enough to stop being careful about strangers, because they don't expect any.

Dess stopped beside me. "Coral," she said. "How long has this camp been here?"

I did the arithmetic off the woodpile, the cut of the stumps, the settled look of the tent ropes. "Six weeks," I said. "Maybe eight."

"Vane hired us eleven days ago."

"I know."

"So the job was never waiting on us." Her voice stayed level, but I heard the flint in it. "The job was finished waiting on us. We're not the crew, Coral. We're the last piece."

Pell had gone quiet in the way he did when he understood more than he could name. Okonkwo stood with his arms folded, surveying the camp the way a man surveys a poker table, counting chips.

A man detached himself from the cook shack and came toward us. Big, gray-bearded, wearing a foreman's coat too good for the mud he walked through, and I knew him before he got close enough to smile, because I'd known him for fifteen years, back when we both worked for the company and neither of us had anything buried.

"Hollis Brandt," I said.

"Coral Crane." His smile was wide and warm and wrong, the way a stove door is warm. "Hell of a thing, seeing you up here. Heard you'd gone soft, poking at dead lines. Guess rumors travel slower than money."

"What are you doing on this mountain, Hollis?"

"Same as you. Working for Silas Vane." He spread his hands at the camp, proud as a father. "Been opening her up since spring. Dewatering pumps ran all summer down at the adit. We've got her drained to the thousand-foot mark, timbers replaced through the first three hundred feet, track going back in next week." He said it easily, conversationally, and every word of it was a hammer swinging at something inside my chest. Drained. Replaced. *Track going back in.* They weren't reopening the spur. They were reopening the wound.

"The bulkhead was sealed by court order," I said. "After the accident. Company petitioned to close the line permanently."

"Company changed its mind." Brandt shrugged. "Companies do. New management, new freight contracts, monsoon shipping schedules. You know how it is. Paper burns, paper gets written." He smiled again. "You'd know all about paper, Coral."

Behind him, two of his men had drifted closer without appearing to move — big men, watchful, standing the way men stand when they're not there to work. I marked them and marked Brandt marking me mark them.

"Why the hurry?" Dess asked, stepping up beside me, all business, all daylight. "Vane told us monsoon was six weeks out. Why hire an outside crew at triple rates if you're this far along?"

Brandt looked at her with something close to respect. "Because the deep section's tricky. Past the thousand-foot mark the old survey maps go to hell — the originals burned in the company fire, if you can believe our luck, and the replacements are copies of copies. Vane wants men who can read a tunnel by feel. Men who've worked dead lines." His eyes came back to me. "Men with a history on this mountain. Call it sentiment."

"I'd call it something else."

"I expect you would." He clapped his hands once, brisk, a man closing a transaction. "Well. Your tents are down by the water, that's fine, keep your own camp, most crews do. You start tomorrow at first light. Deep section, day one." He turned to go, then paused, and when he looked back at me his smile had thinned down to something honest, which was somehow worse than the other one. "It's good work ahead, Coral. Clean work. Nothing down there but rock and water and old rail." A beat. "Nothing at all."

He walked away. The two big men peeled off after him like gulls after a boat.

* * *

We didn't talk until we were back down at our own stretch of gravel, with the canvas up and a small fire going and the mountain between us and that camp. Even then we talked low, the four of us crouched around the flames while the dusk came down the valley and the river talked its endless brown talk below us.

"Eight weeks," Dess said. "They've been here eight weeks and Vane hires us eleven days ago and tells us we're racing the weather."

"He wanted us here before we could hear anything," Okonkwo said. "Before the story could get ahead of us. Now we arrive and the camp is fact. Established. Hard to argue with a thing that's already built."

"But why us?" Pell said. "If they're this far along, why pay triple for—"

"Because of me," I said.

They all looked at me. The fire cracked. Somewhere up the slope a mule complained and was quieted.

"Vane didn't hire a crew," I said. "He hired a name. He hired the man who was here ten years ago, whose signature is on the original survey, whose report is in whatever files didn't burn. When this line opens and the trains run, there's going to be questions someday about what happened in that tunnel. And it'll matter enormously to somebody whether those questions land on the company or on—" I stopped. My jaw had gone tight enough to ache.

"On you," Dess said softly.

"On the engineer of record."

Nobody spoke. Pell stared into the fire like it might explain itself. Okonkwo turned his hat slowly in his big hands, around and around.

"Coral," Dess said at last, carefully, the way you step onto ice. "Ten years ago. Whatever happened up there. You told us it was a collapse. You told us you lost a partner and you signed papers and you left. That's all you've ever told us."

"That's all there is."

"No," she said. "That's all you've ever *said*. Those aren't the same thing, and you know it better than anyone, because saying things true is your trade." She held my eyes across the fire, and hers weren't angry. They were worse. They were sad. "I've run lines with you nine years. I've seen you walk onto trestles that were groaning like dying animals because there was a chance somebody was trapped under them. I've never once seen your hands shake on iron." She glanced down, briefly, at my hands where they lay on my knees. "But they shook up there today. At that tunnel. In front of Brandt. He saw it too. Everybody saw it."

I looked at my own hands. Steady now. Liars, both of them.

"So here's what I'm asking," Dess said. "Not tonight. Not even tomorrow. But before we go into that deep section — you tell us what's down there. What really happened. Because I won't take these men—" she tipped her head at Pell, at Okonkwo, at herself, "—into a hole in a mountain blind, on a job that was rigged before we ever saw the telegram. Loyalty's carried us a long way, Coral. But loyalty runs on truth same as a locomotive runs on coal, and we're getting low."

The fire popped. Downriver, far off, thunder muttered — the first word of a conversation the sky intended to have with this valley whether we liked it or not, weeks early or not.

"All right," I said.

Dess blinked. "All right?"

"When we're ready. Before the deep section. I'll tell you." I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Not because you've earned it. You have. But because Vane thinks it's a weapon he's holding. And I'd rather it be a tool in the hands of people I trust."

Okonkwo put his hat back on, which from him was a benediction. Pell just nodded, young and solemn, keeping some question of his own behind his teeth.

I sat back and looked up the darkening slope, to where the lanterns at the tunnel mouth had been lit at some point in the evening — two small yellow eyes burning in the rock face, patient, unblinking, waiting for morning the way they'd waited ten years.

Tomorrow at first light, Brandt had said. Deep section, day one.

Thunder rolled again, closer, and the first fat drops began ticking against the canvas, and I thought: the monsoon isn't six weeks out. Maybe it isn't even six days.

Whatever was going to happen in that tunnel, the water was coming to hurry it along.
