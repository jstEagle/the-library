## 9. Ilsa's Questions

The rain had held off all day, which Ilsa Reyes had learned—in three weeks on this drowned spur of the world—was not a blessing but a held breath. The valley kept its water the way a debtor keeps his ledger: out of sight, accumulating interest. She could feel it in the air, that thick green pressure sitting on the ridgelines like something patient.

She watched Coral Crane through the steam of her tin cup.

He sat apart from the others, as he always did, on an upturned crate near the fire pit, mending a bootlace with the concentration of a man defusing something. The firelight did him no favors. It found every seam in his face and deepened it—the creases around eyes that had spent too many years squinting into weather, the scar along his jaw she'd catalogued the first night and still couldn't account for. Forty-six, maybe forty-eight. Hands of a working engineer, knuckles gone square with old breaks. He moved like a man who had made a discipline of not being noticed, and had gotten so good at it that he'd fooled even himself.

*You knew my father,* she thought, and the thought had teeth now, after eleven years of carrying it. *You knew him better than anyone alive.*

"Reyes." Okonkwo's voice, low, from behind her. "You're staring."

"I'm observing."

"You're staring," he repeated, settling onto the log beside her with the deliberate weight of a big man conserving energy. He turned his cup slowly in his hands. "In my experience there's a difference, and it matters."

"And what does your experience say about me?"

Okonkwo smiled without much humor. "It says you're the best hydrologist I ever met who won't look at the river. Three days you've been up here and you've taken one gauge reading. You spend the rest of your time watching us." He sipped. "Especially him."

The fire popped. Across the camp, Pell was arguing cheerfully with Dess about whether a man could taste the difference between river trout and lake trout, and Dess was letting him win, because Dess let Pell win at everything that didn't involve rope or money. It was, Ilsa admitted, a good crew. That was the complication she hadn't planned for. In eleven years of reconstructing her father's last months—letters, ledgers, the company's shredded paper trail—she had imagined Vane's men as villains and everyone else as furniture. She had not imagined she'd come to like the furniture.

"Coral hired you on my recommendation," Okonkwo said quietly. "That means if you're a problem, you're my problem. So I'll ask once, plain. What are you doing here?"

Ilsa turned to face him fully. Lying to Okonkwo would be work; the man had a face like a customs officer's. But the truth was a door she wasn't ready to open, so she gave him the truest thing short of it.

"My father worked these lines," she said. "Kestrel Spur was his last survey. I grew up on his stories about it. When I saw the posting for hydrological assessment on the reopening—" she shrugged, and the shrug cost her more than Okonkwo could know— "I wanted to see it. That's all."

"That's all," Okonkwo echoed. He looked at her for a long moment, then nodded slowly, the way a man nods when he's decided to lose an argument on purpose. "All right. But I'm going to tell you something for free, since we're being honest with each other." He leaned in slightly. "Whatever you think you're going to find in Crane—whatever story you've built about him—I'd hold it loose. Men who carry what he carries aren't usually what people build them into."

"What does he carry?"

Okonkwo stood, brushing off his trousers. "Ask him," he said. "Seems like you're planning to anyway."

* * *

She waited two nights, because patience was the only inheritance from her father she'd ever been grateful for. Elias Marrow had been a patient man—patient with instruments, with green surveyors, with a daughter who followed him around the yard asking why the rails sang before a train came. *Because everything under strain tells you what it is, little bird, if you're quiet long enough.* She'd been nine when he left for Kestrel Spur. Ten when the company's letter came, four paragraphs of condolence wrapped around a single sentence: *the accident occurred during routine tunnel inspection amid unforeseen geological conditions.*

Unforeseen. She'd hated that word for eleven years. Her father foresaw things for a living. It was the whole of his profession—to see what the mountain intended before the mountain did it.

On the third night the rain finally came, a soft steady drizzle that drove everyone under the tarps but didn't yet have the monsoon's violence in it. The crew turned in early. And Coral, as he always did on wet nights, stayed up last, feeding the fire down to a red, thoughtful core, drinking coffee he never seemed to finish.

Ilsa crossed the camp with two fresh cups.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she said, holding one out.

He looked at the cup, then at her, and something flickered behind his eyes—the wariness of a man deciding whether a kindness was a kindness. "Thank you," he said finally, and took it.

She sat on the crate opposite him, close enough for conversation, far enough for retreat. The drizzle ticked against the tarp overhead. Down the valley, somewhere in the dark, the river muttered to itself over its drowned rails.

"Can I ask you something professional?" she said.

"Professional." He almost smiled. "Nobody comes out here at midnight for professional questions."

"The tunnel. Kestrel One. You surveyed it before, didn't you? Before the closure."

The firelight held still on his face, but something behind it moved—a shutter drawn, quick and quiet. "Company records say that."

"Company records are wrong about a lot of things." She blew across her cup. "I read the closure report. All of it. The geology annexes, the drainage assessments, the incident documentation. It's a strange document. Half of it is very thorough, and half of it is—"

"Gaps," Coral said.

She looked up. He'd said it flatly, without inflection, the way a man names a thing he's stepped on before.

"Gaps," she agreed. "Eleven pages missing from the incident file. The safety report is signed by an inspector whose name doesn't appear anywhere else in the company archive. And the geological survey cited in the closure order doesn't match the survey referenced in the original construction correspondence. Someone rebuilt the paper trail." She paused. "You were there. Weren't you?"

For a long moment the only sound was the drizzle and the river. Then Coral set his cup down on his knee with great care, as though it might go off.

"Why does a hydrologist care about a ten-year-old incident file?"

"Because water doesn't forget." She heard her own voice sharpen and didn't soften it. "Your tunnel didn't flood because of unforeseen conditions, Mr. Crane. I've walked the approach cut twice. The seepage pattern in the limestone is old—it predates the closure by decades. The aquifer above Kestrel One was compromised during construction. Anyone who read the rock honestly would have known that tunnel was living on borrowed time. Which means someone either didn't read the rock, or wasn't allowed to say what they read."

Coral's gaze had gone past her, into the dark, to some coordinates only he could see. When he spoke again his voice was lower, rougher, like a mechanism engaging after long disuse.

"Elias Marrow read the rock," he said.

Her heart stopped and restarted, hard. She had rehearsed this moment a hundred ways—had imagined pressing him, cornering him, watching him break—and instead he had simply handed her the name like a man laying a card face-up.

"You knew him," she managed.

"Everyone on the survey knew Marrow." He took a slow drink of coffee. "Best field geologist the company ever wasted money on. He used to say the mountain would tell you anything you wanted, so long as you asked it in limestone." A ghost of something crossed his face—not quite a smile, not quite grief. Something older than both. "He had a daughter. Little bird, he called her. Followed him around asking why the rails sang."

The cup trembled in Ilsa's hands. She steadied it against her knee and made herself breathe. *Not yet. Not yet. Let him talk.*

"What happened to him?" she asked. "In the tunnel. The report says a roof collapse during routine inspection."

"The report," Coral said, "says a lot of things."

"Then tell me what it doesn't say."

His eyes came back to hers, and for the first time since she'd boarded their boat at the dock, he looked at her—really looked, the full weight of that exhausted attention—and she understood that Okonkwo had been right, that she had been staring, that she had given herself away in a hundred small ways she'd never catalogued. Coral Crane had spent ten years surviving men who lied to him. He knew what a loaded question sounded like.

"Who sent you?" he asked quietly.

"No one."

"People don't walk into the mountains alone with a head full of other people's incident files."

"I walked into these mountains," Ilsa said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated herself for it, "because my father died in a hole in the ground and the company that killed him sent my mother a check for four hundred dollars and a form letter. Because I was ten years old and they told me it was unforeseen, and I believed them until I was old enough to read the rock myself. Because—" She stopped. The drizzle filled the silence.

Coral had gone very still. Not wary now. Something else. Something that looked, absurdly, like recognition—as if he were hearing a voice he knew coming out of a stranger's mouth.

"Little bird," he said again, barely audible.

"Don't call me that."

"I'm sorry." He set his cup down. His hands, she noticed, were not quite steady. "Elias talked about you. Every letter home, Dess said—he wrote about his girl who wanted to know why the rails sang. We used to tease him about it. He'd just smile and say, wait till she's grown. She'll ask better questions than any of you."

Tears, then, hot and unwelcome, and Ilsa let exactly two of them fall before she wiped her face with the heel of her hand like a child angry at weather. Eleven years. Eleven years of imagining this conversation in courtrooms and offices, in front of lawyers and boards, and it was happening on an upturned crate in the rain over burnt coffee, and it was worse and better than all of them.

"Then answer me," she said. "One question. Just one, tonight. Was it unforeseen?"

Coral looked into the fire for a long time. The coals had settled into that deep arterial red, and his face above them seemed carved out of the same material—something hot buried under gray ash.

"No," he said.

The word landed between them like a dropped tool.

"It was foreseen," he went on, each word dragged up from somewhere deep, "documented, reported, and buried. By men whose names you already know, because you've read the same paper trail I have. And by me." His jaw tightened. "What I did and didn't do in that tunnel, Miss Reyes—that's not a midnight question. It deserves daylight, and sobriety, and more courage than I've got in a tin cup. But I'll tell you this much now, because you've earned it and because lying to Elias Marrow's daughter is a debt I'm not willing to add to the pile."

He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the firelight caught the scar on his jaw.

"There were reports. Your father filed them himself—three of them, formal, in writing, flagging the seepage and the crown deformation in the north bore. I never saw them. I signed off on a safety certification the week before the collapse, based on data I was given, and the data I was given was clean, and I was too tired and too trusting to ask why it was cleaner than the rock was. Whether that makes me a fool or an accomplice is a question I've been answering for ten years, and I still don't like my answer." He straightened. "Silas Vane was the one who handed me the folder. Remember that, whatever else you learn."

Ilsa's pulse was a drumline. *Three reports. Formal. In writing.* Her father's reports—she'd searched every archive, every company office, every county records hall, and found nothing. If they existed, they existed where Vane had buried them. Possibly where her father had hidden them. Possibly—

"In the tunnel," she said. "When it collapsed. Where was he?"

Coral closed his eyes. "At the face. North bore. Taking readings he shouldn't have needed to take, because the company wouldn't send anyone else in after his reports went unanswered. He went back in alone rather than let the numbers stay unverified." A breath. "I was forty meters behind him. I heard it start. I reached the collapse point ninety seconds later." His voice didn't break, but it cost him visibly, the way a beam costs a wall when it takes a load it was never rated for. "Ninety seconds, Miss Reyes. I've done the arithmetic every night for ten years. Ninety seconds faster, and it would have been me under there, and your father would be the one carrying it."

"Or neither of you would be carrying anything," Ilsa said softly, "because the tunnel should never have been open."

Something moved through Coral's face then—relief, or its cousin, the terrible comfort of being seen accurately for the first time in a decade.

"No," he said. "It shouldn't."

The drizzle thickened around them. Down-valley, the river's muttering climbed half a tone, and both of them heard it, and both of them pretended not to.

Ilsa stood, legs stiff, and looked down at this man who had been the villain of her story for eleven years and was, tonight, refusing the part.

"I have his letters," she said. "From the survey. I brought them with me. There are things in them about the tunnel—and about you—that I need you to see. Tomorrow. Daylight, like you said."

Coral looked up at her, and in the failing light of the fire his expression was unreadable and utterly open at once, a man standing in a doorway he'd bricked shut ten years ago and finding the bricks gone.

"Daylight," he agreed.

She was three steps toward her tent when his voice stopped her.

"Miss Reyes." She turned. He was still looking into the coals. "The night before he died, your father told me he'd found something in the north bore he couldn't explain. Something the water was doing that didn't fit any map he had. He laughed about it. Said the mountain was keeping secrets even from him." Coral's hands curled slowly around his cold cup. "I never asked him what it was. It's the only question I have left, and there's no one left to ask it."

Ilsa stood in the rain, her heart hammering against eleven years of careful patience, and heard herself say:

"He did explain it. To me. In the last letter he ever wrote."

Coral's head came up sharply. "What did he say?"

Ilsa looked down the black throat of the valley, toward the tunnel mouth waiting in the dark like a held breath, and said, "He said the water was running backward."
