## 10. What the Water Keeps

The first sign was the birds.

Coral noticed them at midmorning, a ragged exodus lifting off the valley floor all at once—herons, crows, something small and brown he couldn't name—streaming up the canyon walls like smoke pulled by a draft. He stood on the staging platform with a surveyor's rod in his hand and watched them go, and the rod felt suddenly heavy, the way a tool feels when it stops being a tool and becomes a thing you're holding while the world changes around you.

"Okonkwo," he called. "How's the barometer?"

Okonkwo came out of the tool shed wiping his hands, squinting at the sky the way he squinted at a bad weld—patient, professional, unimpressed. "Been falling since dawn. Thought it was the altitude playing tricks."

"It's not the altitude." Coral set the rod down. The clouds had come over the western ridge an hour ago, a bruise-colored wall of them, and he'd told himself it was a squall, a passing thing, a summer tantrum. But the birds didn't argue with. "That's monsoon weather. That's *early* monsoon weather."

"Vane said three weeks."

"Vane says a lot of things."

He felt it before he heard it—a pressure change, a hush, the valley holding its breath. Then the wind came down the canyon in a single long exhale, flattening the canvas over the supply stacks, and the first rain hit the platform like gravel thrown by an angry hand.

* * *

By noon it wasn't rain anymore. It was a wall of water falling out of the sky with the patience of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use every minute of it. The river below the camp—two days ago a green thread meandering through the drowned valley—had begun to swell, browning, quickening, carrying branches and whole sections of fencing downstream like a man emptying his pockets.

Coral stood under the awning of the mess tent and watched the water rise and felt the old familiar arithmetic begin in his chest. Every inch the river climbed was an inch of escape gone. The boats were sound—or sound enough—but the river was turning into something that didn't care about sound boats. Above them, the Kestrel tunnel mouth gaped in the rain-blurred cliff face, half-hidden by scaffolding, and Coral caught himself staring at it the way you stare at a scar you've decided not to look at anymore.

Dess ducked under the awning, shaking water off her oilskin. "Vane's foremen are battening everything down like they expected this."

"They did expect this. Just not for three weeks." Coral turned. "Get Pell off the lower track. Anything below the forty-foot mark, pull the men off it. I don't care if Vane's schedule says otherwise."

"Vane's schedule says we're two hundred yards from clearing the approach."

"Vane's schedule is going to be underwater by morning."

She looked at him a moment longer than she needed to, and he recognized the look—the one from the river camp, the one that said *you're lying to me about something, and I'm letting you, and we both know I am.* Then she pulled her hood up and went back out into the deluge.

The rain came down harder. Coral stayed under the awning and did the arithmetic again, and the answer was the same: they were stranded. The return route down the valley was a day's run in good weather, and this wasn't good weather and wouldn't be for days. The supply dock downstream was cut off. The radio in Vane's office shack crackled with static and nothing else. They were thirty men, a camp, a half-cleared rail approach, a tunnel full of standing water, and a storm that had arrived like an uninvited guest who'd brought luggage.

And somewhere in that camp, Coral thought, was whatever Silas Vane had actually brought them here to find.

* * *

He went looking for it that evening, and he told himself it was about the supplies.

That was the story he gave himself: the rain had flooded the lower magazine, the powder stores needed moving, and Vane's foremen were too busy arguing about their own tents to do it. Coral had spent twenty years reading rail yards the way other men read faces, and a camp told you its truths in its layout—who slept where, what was guarded, what was left out in the open because it had nothing to hide. Vane's camp had a layout too. And one shack, set apart on the high bench above the flood line, had a padlock on it and a lantern burning behind its shuttered window at nine o'clock at night while every other man on the mountain was fighting the rain.

The magazine didn't need a lantern. The magazine didn't need a padlock. The magazine didn't need to sit on the high ground.

Coral told himself all of this on the walk up, boots sucking in the mud, rain hammering his hood, and he knew he was lying to himself the same way he'd been lying to himself for ten years, and he went anyway. That was the thing about lies. They got you moving. That was practically all they were good for.

The shack sat on cut stone, dry above the mud. The lantern light leaked through the shutter slats in thin gold lines. Coral circled it once, slow, and heard voices inside—Vane's, unmistakable even through the rain, that warm baritone he used on men he was about to use up—and a second voice he didn't know. He couldn't make out words. He didn't need to. What he needed was a reason to open that door, and the reason arrived on its own: the door wasn't fully latched. The wind had sprung it, just a finger's width, and through that finger's width came the smell of lamp oil and paper.

Paper. In a magazine full of powder, you stored powder. You didn't store paper where a stray spark could end the whole valley's argument at once.

Coral put his eye to the gap.

* * *

They were at a table, Vane and the stranger—a lean man in a company suit, dry despite the storm, with the neat, bloodless hands of a man who had never held a tool. Papers were spread across the table in ordered rows, weighted at the corners with brass fittings, and Vane stood over them with a glass of something amber in his hand, and he was smiling. Coral knew that smile. He'd spent ten years trying to forget it. It was the smile Vane wore the day the survey crew came down out of the hills and the company men asked their questions and Vane had said, *Coral's report speaks for itself,* and put his hand on Coral's shoulder like a man steadying a son.

"—the beauty of it," Vane was saying, "is that it's all true. Every word. That's what they never understand, the ones who lie. You don't lie. You *arrange.*" He touched one of the papers, turned it so the lamplight caught it. "The original survey report. Coral Pemberton's own signature. The safety addendum, dated the ninth of March. His initials in the margin, here, here, and here. And this—" he lifted a single page with two fingers, delicate as a man lifting a moth, "—this is the draft. The one he worked from. Note the corrections in his own hand. Note how the water readings change between the draft and the filed copy."

The stranger's voice was dry as the paper. "And the tunnel crew will corroborate?"

"The tunnel crew will corroborate what the documents say, because the documents are what they'll be shown first. Memory follows paper, Mr. Hale. It always has. You show a man a signature and he remembers signing it." Vane set the page down and drank. "Ten years ago I needed the line open and the incident closed, and Coral needed his crew paid and his name clean, and we made a report that did both. It was a kindness, really. A mercy. Marrow was dead either way—the ground took him before anyone could have reached him, whatever the readings said. The only question was who the company would grieve for and who it would bury. I chose to bury the readings." He smiled again at the papers. "And now, with the line reopening, the readings come back to life. Filed correctly. Discovered honestly. A tragic falsification, uncovered at last—engineer Pemberton altered the hydrology data to rush the survey, concealed the instability, and his partner Elias Marrow paid the price for it. Coral takes the fall. The company takes a sad lesson about the pressures of the frontier. And I—" he spread his hands, "—I take a clean line, a cleared name, and a very large contract."

Hale was quiet a moment. Rain filled the silence. "And if he won't sign the confession?"

"He'll sign." Vane said it the way you say *the sun will rise.* "I've known Coral Pemberton for twenty years. He's a man who carries other people's weight. You put enough weight on a man like that, he doesn't break—he *bends*, and bending men sign things. Besides." He gathered the papers into a leather folio, squared its edges, and set it on the table with a small, final tap. "He's already signed once. Men who've signed once will always sign twice. It's the first one that costs. The rest are just postage."

Coral stepped back from the door.

He did it slowly, one boot at a time, the way you back away from a sleeping animal, and the rain took him and the dark took him and he walked down off the bench with the folio's contents printed behind his eyes like a brand. The draft report. The changed readings. His own signature, or something close enough to it—Vane had always been good with paper, good with pens, good with the small forgeries of arrangement. Ten years of believing he had buried the truth himself, alone, his sin and his secret—and the whole time it had been *filed*. Kept. Curated. Sitting in a company strongbox like a loaded gun pointed at the back of his head, waiting for the day Vane needed it fired.

He had thought he was the keeper of the secret. He had been the *specimen*.

The river had climbed another foot while he stood at the window. He could hear it now even over the rain, a deep muscular sound, the sound of the valley filling up like a cup. Down at the camp, lanterns swung in the wind, and men moved between the tents in oilskins, small and stooped, doing what men do in weather—battening, bailing, holding things down. Dess was out there. Pell. Okonkwo. Men who had signed on to reopen a line because the pay was triple and the boss was Coral, and who were, at this moment, standing in the rain on top of a buried report that said their boss had killed a man.

*No,* he thought. *The report says I falsified data. The report doesn't say what actually happened.*

And there it was—the old reflex, rising in him like water in a shaft. *What actually happened.* Ten years he'd carried the true version, the private version: the readings that had come in wrong, the warning he'd raised, Vane's hand on his shoulder and Vane's voice saying *the company needs this line, Coral, and your crew needs this winter's pay, and dead men don't need anything at all.* Ten years of telling himself he'd signed to protect his men, that the falsification was Vane's sin executed by Coral's hand, that the difference mattered.

Standing in the rain with the river rising, Coral understood, finally and completely, that the difference had never mattered to Elias Marrow. Marrow was under the rock either way. Marrow's daughter—if the woman at the dock was what Coral was beginning to fear she was—had grown up without a father either way. And Vane had kept the proof of it all along, not as blackmail exactly, not as insurance exactly, but as *seed corn*. A thing to plant in season. And the season, Coral thought, watching the brown water eat the valley, was now.

He stood at the edge of the camp light a long time before he went in. He watched his crew move through the storm, and he felt the strangest sensation, like a man watching his own house from outside: he loved them, and he had lied to them, and the lie had a signature on it, and the signature was in a leather folio on a dry bench above the flood, and in the morning Vane would begin, gently, patiently, in that warm baritone, to arrange things.

Unless.

Coral's eyes went up the cliff face, to where the scaffolding clung to the rock like a ladder of bones, and above it the tunnel mouth, black in the rain. Marrow had kept field notes. Coral knew that the way he knew rail gauge—Marrow had kept notes on everything, had kept them in a tin box, had kept them in the tunnel office, had been the kind of man who wrote things down because he believed paper was the only witness that couldn't be promoted or paid off. If the tin box was still in there—under the rock, under the water, under ten years of collapse—

Then there were two sets of paper on this mountain. And only one of them told the truth.

The rain redoubled. The river made its deep, patient sound. Coral pulled his hood down and walked into the camp, into the lantern light, into the faces of the men who trusted him, and he smiled at Dess as she came to meet him, and the smile cost him more than anything he had ever forged.

"Storm's settled in," she said. "Vane's men pulled off the lower track an hour after you did. They're not worried about the schedule anymore."

"No," Coral said. "They're not."

He looked past her, up the valley, to where the tunnel mouth waited in the dark with everything it kept—and everything the water was about to give back.

"Get some sleep," he said. "Tomorrow, I'm going into the tunnel."
