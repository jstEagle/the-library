## 11. The Leaking Boat

Pell had never been afraid of water until the valley taught him what water could do.

He'd grown up dry, in the scrub country east of the divide, where creeks ran thin as wire and a boy could cross them without wetting his bootlaces. Water was a thing you drank, a thing you bathed in on Sundays. It was not a thing with weight and temper, a thing that could pick up a forty-ton locomotive and fold it like a newspaper. But that was before he'd seen the drowned valley of the Kestrel Spur, where the river backed up behind a slide ten years gone and turned a working railroad into a brown glass mirror, with telegraph poles standing to their armpits in it and the roofs of two section houses just breaking the surface like the backs of sleeping animals.

Now Pell knew what water was. Water was patient. Water remembered where things used to be and wanted them back.

"You're steering too wide," Ilsa Reyes said from the stern.

"I'm steering fine."

"You're steering wide because you don't trust the channel." She sat with her hands loose in her lap, watching the water the way other people watched a card game—reading it, counting it. "The current wants you left. You keep fighting it right. That's how boats get killed out here."

Pell gripped the tiller and said nothing, which was his way of saying she was probably right. The boat was one of Vane's foremen's, a flat-bottomed work skiff with an outboard that coughed like a consumptive, loaded nose-heavy with crates of bolts, drums of fuel, sacks of flour and beans and salt pork for the camp at the tunnel mouth. Dess had picked him for the run because he was light and quick and because Okonkwo's shoulder was still bad from the trestle work. And because, Pell suspected, Dess wanted him away from Coral for a day. Everyone at camp had noticed how Coral got when the fog came down the valley walls and the water went still and gray—the way he'd stand at the shore with his hands in his pockets, looking upriver toward the black seam in the mountain, like a man visiting a grave he wasn't sure he had the right to mourn at.

Ilsa had volunteered for the run before anyone asked her. That was another thing people had noticed. She'd been at the dock three days now, quiet as a heron, doing hydrology readings nobody had requested, and she watched Coral the way Pell watched a poker hand he'd folded but couldn't stop thinking about.

"Left," Ilsa said again. "Now."

Pell swung the tiller. The skiff wallowed, corrected, slid into the deeper channel between two drowned fence posts. Below them, faint as a rumor, he could see the rails running along the old grade under six feet of green-brown water, shining where the silt hadn't covered them yet. It gave him a crawly feeling, riding above them. Like walking on somebody's back.

"How do you know this water?" he asked, mostly to make conversation, mostly not.

"I've been reading rivers since I was nine," she said. "My father took me out on survey boats. He said a river is just a map that moves." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under the surface, there and gone. "He liked maps too."

"Everybody here likes maps," Pell said. "Coral's got one older than sin. Dead railroads, all of them. He carries it around like—" He stopped himself. He didn't know how to finish the sentence, and Ilsa was looking at him with that still attention of hers, the attention that made you want to say more than you meant to. "Like it matters," he finished, lamely.

"Does it?"

"Ask him." Pell nodded upriver, where the fog was peeling off the water in rags. "Though I wouldn't count on an answer. He's got more locked doors than a bank."

* * *

They made good time for the first hour. The channel widened where the valley opened, and the sun came through hard enough to warm the back of Pell's neck, and for a while it was almost pleasant—the motor's ragged rhythm, the crates riding steady, the mountains sliding by with their skirts of pine. Ilsa even talked a little. Not about herself; he noticed she never did that. About the water. Where the slide had come down in '09, how the river had ponded behind it, how the engineers who surveyed the blockage had argued about whether to blast it or build around it, and how the company had done neither, because doing neither was cheaper.

"They knew the spur was dead," she said. "They wrote it off. But they kept the file open."

"Why would they do that?"

"Because files are cheaper than funerals," Ilsa said, "and sometimes more useful."

Pell laughed, then stopped laughing, because she hadn't.

It was maybe twenty minutes past the halfway mark, where the valley pinched down and the current quickened over the buried grade, that he heard the sound. A soft, wrong sound, like a knuckle cracking somewhere inside the hull. Then another. Then a long tearing whisper, forward and to the left, low near the waterline.

"What was that?" Ilsa said.

Pell didn't answer. He was already looking down between his boots, at the dark seam that had opened in the planking like a mouth deciding to speak. Water came through it not in a jet but in a smooth, muscular tongue, the way water comes through a dam that has decided to fail.

"We're taking water," he said, and heard how young his voice sounded.

"Plug. Anything. Now."

He dropped the tiller and threw himself at the crates, hauling at the nearest one, dragging it aft, slamming it down over the split. The crate slowed the water but didn't stop it; it found its way around the edges, patient, muscular. The bilge, which had been carrying two inches of gray water since the dock, was carrying four, then five. The skiff settled by the head and the current, feeling the change, began to take an interest.

"The pump," Ilsa said. She was already moving, calm in a way that scared Pell more than panic would have. "Where's the pump?"

"There isn't one. There was supposed to be—Dess said—"

"Then bail. Bail now."

She threw him a tin pail lashed under the stern seat, and Pell bailed like a man possessed, scooping and flinging, scooping and flinging, while the river came in faster than he could send it home. The motor coughed, caught, coughed again, and died. The silence after it was enormous. They were drifting sideways in the current now, broadside to the flow, and the water was pushing them—not toward the shore, which was fifty yards off through standing timber, but toward the pinch downstream, where the drowned grade rose under the surface and the river broke white over it in long ragged lines.

"Pell." Ilsa's voice was level. "Look at me. We are going to hit that grade. When we do, the boat will come apart. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

"So take off your boots."

"What?"

"Boots. Off. Now. You can swim or you can drown wearing them, those are your choices."

He tore at the laces with fingers that didn't feel like his own. The white water was louder now, a long continuous roar ahead, and through it he could see the grade itself—a ridge of ballast and rail just under the surface, sharp as a spine. The skiff swung. The current had them. There was no more steering, only arithmetic: where they were, where the river wanted them, and the short ugly distance between.

"Hold onto something," Ilsa said, "and when it goes, push off hard. Away from the wreck. Don't grab at the boat. The boat will try to kill you."

"That doesn't make any sense—"

"It makes perfect sense. Boats sink. Sinking boats pull. Let go of everything that floats down instead of up."

The grade hit them like a train.

* * *

Later, Pell would remember it in pieces, the way you remember anything that nearly kills you: not a story but a handful of photographs.

The hull splitting along its whole length with a sound like a door kicked in. The world tilting, going green, going cold in a way that closed on his chest like a fist. The crates bursting their ropes and rising around him, flour sacks blooming pale clouds in the brown water. His hand finding a rope—he couldn't have said why, except that drowning men grab—and the rope being attached to the skiff's forward thwart, and the thwart being attached to the boat, and the boat being dragged down and downstream over the grade with him tied to it like bait.

He got one breath. Just one, torn off sideways, half water. Then the current rolled him under and the rope hauled him along the bottom, over the ballast stones, past something huge and angular that might have been a derailed flatcar, and his lungs were a red alarm ringing in his ears, and the surface was a shimmering ceiling he couldn't reach, and he thought, with a strange clarity: *this is how it happens, this is how it happened to someone, this is exactly how it happens.*

Then hands on his collar. Hands strong as cable winches. A knife blade flashed silver in the murk—close, so close to his throat he felt the cold of it—and the rope parted, and he was free, and the free-ness was its own kind of terror because now nothing held him and nothing guided him and the river had him entire.

But the hands didn't let go. They turned him, pushed him, angled him upward, and the ceiling of light came down to meet him, and he broke through into air and noise and daylight and coughed up half the Kestrel River.

"Swim," Ilsa said, beside him, her hair plastered flat, her voice not even winded. "Angled. Across the current. Not against it. Swim."

He swam. Or his body swam, some animal part of him that had no interest in his opinions. The far shore came at them in slow motion, willows first, then mud, then gravel, and then his knees hit bottom and he crawled, actually crawled, on hands and knees up the bank until the water was only holding his ankles, and then he collapsed face-down in the gravel and shook.

Ilsa stood over him, dripping, breathing hard for the first time. She wrung out her sleeve with two efficient twists and looked down at him with an expression he couldn't read at all.

"You grabbed the boat," she said.

"I grabbed the boat," Pell agreed, into the gravel.

"Don't grab the boat."

"I won't grab the boat."

He started laughing then, or crying, or both—it was hard to tell the difference and he didn't fight it—lying in the shallows with the river roaring past and his whole body ringing like a struck bell. After a moment Ilsa sat down next to him in the mud, which surprised him, and did not say anything, which surprised him more. Most people filled silence like that. She just let it sit there, wide and calm as the valley.

After a while he managed: "You cut the rope. With a knife. While we were underwater."

"Yes."

"That's the bravest thing I ever saw anybody do."

"It's the second bravest," she said. "The first was your crew coming up this valley at all. Your boss knows what's in that tunnel, Pell. Whatever it is, it scares him worse than this river does. And he came anyway." She looked out at the water, at the wreckage of their supplies already strung out downstream, a flour sack snagged white in the willows like a ghost giving up. "That's either courage or guilt. Usually it's both."

Pell propped himself on his elbows. His teeth were chattering. "You talk about him like you know him."

"I'm getting to know him."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

They salvaged what the river gave back. Half the fuel drums had washed into a backwater eddy below the grade, dented but sealed; two sacks of beans, swollen and ruined; one crate of bolts, which weighed the same wet as dry and mattered more than everything else combined. Ilsa worked with a methodical economy that Pell found himself studying between loads—the way she tested each footing before trusting it, the way she read the eddy's rotation before wading in, the way she carried twice what he carried without comment. By the time they'd ferried the salvage along the bank to the old wagon road above the floodline, the sun was slanting long and orange through the pines, and Pell had stopped being afraid of her and started being grateful, which felt sturdier.

They were loading the last drum onto the road cart when he heard the whistle—two short, one long, Dess's signal—and saw figures coming down the track bed at a trot: Dess first, lantern already lit against the dusk, then Okonkwo moving easy on his bad side, and behind them Coral, running flat-out, hatless, his coat flying, with a look on his face that Pell had never seen on him and would spend years afterward trying to describe. It wasn't anger. It was the look of a man arriving at a graveside expecting one body and finding none.

Coral reached them and stopped, and for a second nobody spoke. His eyes went over Pell—quick, thorough, counting limbs the way you'd count a crew after a wreck—and then to Ilsa, dripping, mud to the waist, the knife still sheathed at her hip.

"The boat split at the grade," Pell said. "We lost most of it. Ilsa—" His voice cracked and he hated it and pushed through anyway. "Ilsa pulled me off the wreck. Underwater. Cut me loose. I was done, Coral. I was finished, and she—"

"I know what she did." Coral's voice was rough. He looked at Ilsa a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes, some ledger being reopened and re-examined. "Why'd you volunteer for this run?"

"Someone had to read the water," Ilsa said. "Your boy steers like he's apologizing to the river."

A sound came out of Coral that in a man with a lighter heart would have been a laugh. He put his hand on Pell's shoulder—brief, heavy, real—and left it there a beat longer than he needed to.

"Get him to the fire," he said to Dess. "Dry clothes. Hot food. And somebody find out what else we lost, because if we're short on bolts, we're short on time." He turned to go, then stopped. "Reyes."

"Coral."

There was a pause, the length of a held breath. The river filled it, talking to itself in the dark.

"Thank you," he said, and it cost him visibly, the way it costs some men to bleed. "For my crew."

"He's your crew?" Ilsa said. "Or your penance?"

Coral didn't answer. He walked back up the track bed into the dusk, and Pell watched him go, and watched Ilsa watch him go, and understood suddenly, the way you understand a card you should have seen three tricks ago, that whatever she'd come up this river to find, it wasn't hydrology.

That night, wrapped in blankets by the fire with a tin cup of Okonkwo's terrible coffee in his hands, Pell heard Dess and Okonkwo talking low on the far side of the flames.

"She saved his life," Dess said. "You don't fake that."

"People fake harder things," Okonkwo said. "But no. I don't think she faked it. I think she meant it. Question is what she means by the rest."

"And what's the rest?"

Okonkwo stirred the fire. Sparks went up into the black like a slow constellation.

"The rest," he said, "is whoever she is, she didn't come here for the water. She came here for us. For him. And tomorrow, one way or another, we find out why."

Across the fire, Ilsa sat awake, sharpening the knife that had cut Pell free, and watching the dark shape of the tunnel mouth up the valley, where the mountain held its one long held breath.
