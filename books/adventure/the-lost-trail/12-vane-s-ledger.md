## 12. Vane's Ledger

Dess had never trusted a man who kept his books too clean.

She'd learned that from her father, who ran a dry goods store in Harlow Bend for thirty years and said the ledger was the truest confession a man ever made. Ink didn't lie. It could be made to lie, sure—her father had caught two suppliers fudging freight weights in his lifetime—but a careful reader could always find the seam where the truth had been stitched over. You just had to know where to look.

So when the rain drove everyone under canvas and the work on the Kestrel Spur ground to a sullen halt, Dess did what she always did in bad weather. She found something to read.

Vane's field office sat at the high end of camp, a prefabricated box of corrugated steel that had been flown in pieces and bolted together by men who clearly expected to be gone before anyone thought to ask why they'd come so well supplied. The foremen—Brack and the one everyone called Tally because he counted everything, nails, hours, cigarettes—kept irregular hours, and tonight they'd gone down to the dock to argue with the boat pilot about mooring lines while the river climbed its banks like something impatient to get inside.

Dess told herself she was only looking for the pay schedule. That was the story she'd carry if anyone asked, and it was even mostly true. Winter debts hung off the crew like wet canvas—Pell owed the clinic in Marrow Junction for his mother's treatments, Okonkwo had co-signed a loan against his brother's shop, and Dess herself had been sending half her wages east to a sister whose husband kept breaking the same promises. Triple pay was the reason they were all standing in a drowned valley waiting for a storm to finish arriving. She wanted to see the numbers in ink. She wanted to believe them.

The door wasn't locked. That was the first wrong thing. Men with honest business lock their doors; men with secrets leave them open because an open door looks like nothing to hide.

Inside, the office smelled of kerosene and damp paper. A map of the spur covered one wall, marked up in red grease pencil—survey points, water gauges, the tunnel mouth circled twice. A cot in the corner, military-tight. A desk with a lamp turned down to a coal of light, and on the desk, stacked square and neat as bricks, the ledgers.

Dess stood in the doorway a long moment, listening to the rain hammer the roof, listening harder for footsteps in the mud outside. Nothing but weather. She went in and closed the door behind her, which was itself a kind of confession, and told herself she'd only stay a minute.

The top ledger was what she'd expected: materials, wages, freight. Vane's handwriting was small and exact, every column footing perfectly, and that perfection was the second wrong thing. No man running a rush job through monsoon country kept books this clean. There should have been smudges, corrections, pages torn out and recopied. This was a ledger written after the fact, or written for someone else to read.

The third ledger down was different. Thinner. Bound in gray cloth with no label, and when Dess opened it, the handwriting changed—not Vane's careful script but a faster, harder hand, pressed deep into the page by a man writing angry.

She recognized the name before she understood the entry.

*Marrow, E. — surveyor, Kestrel extension. Final report attached. See correspondence file, March.*

Below it, in Vane's hand again:

*Original incident report unserviceable. Replacement drafted. C.C. signature obtained under standard conditions. Full attribution to engineer error upon reopening. Company record clear.*

Dess read it three times. The rain filled the silences between readings.

*Full attribution to engineer error.* Engineer. Coral Crane was the engineer. Coral Crane was the only engineer who had ever mattered to that sentence.

She turned pages with fingers that didn't feel like hers. There was a draft of a report—the replacement, the *serviceable* one—and it was a masterpiece of small violence. Every date nudged. Every measurement softened until the tunnel's instability read as routine seepage. And threaded through it, in language so reasonable it took a moment to feel the knife in it, the whole weight of the collapse gathered and placed on one set of shoulders. *Engineer Crane approved the alignment despite documented concerns. Engineer Crane signed the safety certification. Engineer Crane's decision to proceed resulted in the fatality.*

*Fatality.* Not a name. Not Elias Marrow, who used to sing hymns off-key on the morning survey walk and once carried Dess four miles down a scree slope when she broke her ankle at nineteen, back when she was nobody's crew and Coral was nobody's legend. *Fatality*—a word shaped like a stone you could hide a man under.

And beneath the draft, clipped together with a brass fastener, the correspondence file. Letters in Marrow's hand—she knew that hard, angry script now—warning about the fault line, warning about the water table, asking, then demanding, then begging for the survey to be pulled. Each letter stamped RECEIVED and filed and answered with nothing. Buried alive on paper years before the mountain buried him any other way.

Dess sat down on Vane's cot without meaning to. Her legs had decided things on their own.

Because here was the shape of it, plain as a rail profile: Vane hadn't hired Coral to reopen a line. He'd hired him to reopen a *verdict*. Once the spur ran and the company's name needed clearing, these papers would surface—leaked, discovered, produced at some inquiry—and the world would learn that Coral Crane had falsified the original safety report, that he'd signed off on a tunnel he knew was rotten, that his partner's death was his doing. Vane would stand before the inquiry with clean cuffs and a clean ledger and grieve, professionally, for a good man undone by ambition. And Coral—

Coral would carry it. Coral, who already carried something ten years old and heavy enough that he flinched when people said certain names. Coral, who had taken this job against his own judgment because Pell's mother needed medicine and Okonkwo's brother needed breathing room and Dess needed her sister fed. Vane hadn't bought Coral's labor. He'd bought the crew, knowing Coral would follow them into the trap rather than let them walk in alone.

That was the part that made Dess's hands go cold. She'd voted for this job. In the circle of lamplight at the river camp, with winter's debts laid out honest between them, she'd voted yes, and Coral had looked at each of them one by one and said, *Then I'll lead you,* and she'd thought it was duty. Now she understood it had been surrender. He'd known—or suspected—and he'd come anyway, because the alternative was letting them come without him.

*Standard conditions.* Whatever those were, whatever Vane had done ten years ago to put Coral's signature on a false report, it had worked once. Dess thought about the night Coral never discussed, the night that lived behind his eyes like weather. She thought about how smoothly he lied when she'd asked him why he refused Vane's money, and how she'd pretended to believe him, and how pretending had felt like a kindness at the time and felt now like complicity.

Thunder rolled down the valley, long and low, the sound of a sky clearing its throat.

Dess heard boots in the mud outside—two sets, heavy, unhurried. Brack and Tally coming back from the dock. She had perhaps twenty seconds. The ledgers wouldn't sit right if she moved them; she'd noted their positions the way her father taught her, spine alignments, corner distances, the brass fastener's angle. She rebuilt the stack in the dark, killed the lamp, and let herself out the back where the canvas flap over the supply window hung loose. She was forty feet away, crouched behind a stack of creosoted ties with the rain running off her hood, when the office door opened and lamplight spilled yellow across the mud.

Brack went in. Tally stood outside a moment, scanning the camp, and Dess held her breath and became a shadow among shadows, the way you learn to when your life has depended on not being noticed.

Tally went in. The door closed. The light stayed on.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She lay in her bunk in the crew tent listening to the river rise—a sound she'd never heard before this job and hoped never to hear again, a vast slow swallowing, the valley drinking itself—and she turned the choice over until it wore grooves in her.

Warn Coral, and maybe lose everything. Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: if Coral confronted Vane, if he tore up the deal or walked off the job or did anything that stopped the spur from reopening, the pay died with it. Triple pay, monsoon bonus, completion fee—all of it contingent, all of it inked in that clean ledger upstairs. Pell's mother would go another winter on charity medicine. Okonkwo's brother would lose the shop. Her sister would—Dess couldn't finish that thought, so she didn't. And beyond the money there was the other danger, the plainer one: a man like Vane, cornered, with desperate foremen and a flooded valley and no witnesses worth fearing, was a man who might decide loose ends were cheaper than lawsuits. Warn Coral badly, and you might just tell Vane it was time to stop being subtle.

Say nothing, and watch them hang ten years of murder on a man who'd carried you through every hard season of your working life. Watch Ilsa Reyes—that quiet stranger who watched Coral like she was reading a document—learn the worst version of the truth and believe it. Watch the world bury Elias Marrow a second time, deeper, under Coral's name this time.

She got up in the black hour before dawn and stood at the tent flap watching the rain come down silver in the work lights. Across the compound, Coral's lamp still burned. He slept little on this job; she'd seen the light moving at strange hours, seen him standing at the edge of the floodwater looking at the tunnel mouth like a man visiting a grave he wasn't sure he had the right to kneel at.

Okonkwo stirred behind her. He was a light sleeper; ten years of river camps had made all of them that way.

"You've been standing there an hour," he said quietly. "That's Dess-for-trouble posture."

"Go back to sleep."

"I'm a poor sleeper and a worse liar." He came to stand beside her, big and warm in the cold air, arms folded. "So. What did you find in Vane's office?"

Her heart stumbled. "I don't know what you—"

"You came back soaked an hour after the foremen went in. You haven't slept. You're holding your left wrist, which you do when you're deciding whether to say something." He looked down at her, patient as stone. "I won't ask twice. But I'll say this much free of charge: whatever it is, it doesn't get smaller by keeping it."

Dess watched the rain. She thought about loyalty, real loyalty, the kind that isn't obedience. Her father had a third saying about ledgers, the one he saved for the suppliers he caught cheating: *A book balanced with lies isn't balanced. It's just closed.* The crew's pay was a book. Coral's name was a book. Only one of them could stay open honestly.

"If I tell you," she said, "you're in it. All the way. No middle ground with a thing like this."

Okonkwo nodded slowly, and in the work-light she saw that he'd already decided, had probably decided the moment he saw her standing guard at the flap. "Been in it since the telegram," he said. "We all have. We just didn't know the price yet."

So she told him. Low, fast, complete—the gray ledger, the replacement report, the letters buried in the file, the sentence that moved a dead man's death onto a living man's shoulders. Okonkwo listened without interrupting, which was his way, and when she finished he was silent for a long time, watching the water climb the tie stacks.

"They mean to break him," he said finally. "After. When the line's open and we're paid and scattered to the winds. By then it won't matter what we say. Who believes three laborers over a company's paperwork?"

"Pell can't lose this pay." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "You know what's waiting for him at home. And my sister—" She stopped, jaw tight. "That's the trap, Okonkwo. It's not just built around Coral. It's built around us. Our need is the walls of it."

"Then we do what Coral did at the vote." Okonkwo turned to her, and there was something in his face she'd rarely seen—anger, but banked, aimed, useful. "We don't let anyone walk into it alone. Wake him."

"Now? It's nearly four—"

"He's awake. His lamp's been burning." Okonkwo was already pulling on his coat. "And Dess—if Vane's men are watching him the way they watch everything else, better we go to him than he comes looking for us and finds us mid-whisper."

They crossed the compound through the rain, two shadows among many, and Dess's pulse hammered with every step—not fear of the storm, not quite fear of Vane, but the terrible vertigo of a woman about to burn a bridge she'd spent a week convincing herself was solid. The pay. The beautiful, impossible, triple pay. She could almost hear it dissolving already, running off with the floodwater.

Coral opened the door before she knocked. He looked hollowed out, days of bad sleep carved into his face, and when he saw their expressions his own went carefully blank—the look of a man bracing for news he'd been expecting for ten years.

"Coral," Dess said, and found her voice steadier than she deserved. "We need to talk. Inside, away from the windows. It's about Marrow."

Something moved behind his eyes—fear, and under the fear, a flicker of something that looked almost like relief, like a man hearing at last the name of the thing that had been hunting him.

"It always was," Coral said, and stepped aside to let them in.
