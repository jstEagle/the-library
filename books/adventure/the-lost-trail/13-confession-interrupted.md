## 13. Confession Interrupted

The rain had settled into a rhythm by the third day of the monsoon—a long gray drumming on the tarps, on the tin roof of the cook shack, on the flooded rails below camp—so that silence had become a thing you noticed only when it stopped. It stopped that evening, briefly, when Ilsa Reyes set down her cup across the fire barrel from me and said, "Tell me about my father."

I had been waiting for that sentence for eleven days. I'd rehearsed it in a hundred small hours: on watch, in the boat, staring at the tunnel mouth through binoculars while the water climbed the portal's arch like something patient climbing stairs. I had rehearsed it so many times that when it finally came, plain and unadorned, with rain hissing behind it, every rehearsal fell out of my head at once and left me holding nothing but the truth.

Which was, perhaps, the point.

"He liked his coffee too sweet," I said, because it was the first true thing I could find. "He'd put four spoons in a cup that size. Okonkwo used to say Marrow didn't drink coffee, he drank dessert."

Something moved behind her eyes—not a smile, not yet. A door opening onto a dark room where a smile might be.

"His letters say he learned to take it black," she said. "Out here. He wrote that the mountains ruin you for sweetness."

"They do." I looked at the fire barrel, at the coals shifting under their gray skin. "They ruined me for a lot of things."

"Coral." She said my name the way you'd set a heavy box down on a table between us. "I've been on this crew nine days. I've watched you read water the way other men read faces. I've watched you walk the line every morning before anyone else is up, checking trestles nobody asked you to check. And I've watched you avoid the Kestrel tunnel like it has teeth. My father died in that tunnel. The company told my mother it was a rockfall, sudden, unforeseeable, an act of God. His last letter to me—he wrote me letters, did you know that? He wrote me every week, even from out here—the last one says, *If anything happens, ask Coral Pemberton. Trust him over any man in a suit.*"

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere down the valley, the river made a sound like a throat clearing.

"I'm asking," she said. "And I'm trusting."

* * *

Here is what I should have done. I should have told her all of it, right there, with the fire between us and the rain sealing us off from the rest of the camp: that the rockfall was not sudden; that the survey reports had flagged the east shear zone three weeks before; that Silas Vane had ordered the crew through anyway because the company's bond payment hinged on hitting a date; that when the ceiling came down and took Elias Marrow with it, Vane stood in the mud with rain running off his hat brim and gave me a choice framed as a kindness—write the report clean, or every man on that crew carries the blame of having worked a condemned heading, and no railroad in the territory will hire them again.

That I chose them. That I have spent ten years telling myself it was a choice at all, and not just the shape cowardice takes when it wears loyalty's coat.

Instead, what came out was: "It wasn't a rockfall."

Her breath caught. The fire popped.

"It wasn't—" She leaned forward. "Then what was it?"

"The east wall was bad. Your father knew. He filed reports—I never saw them, Ilsa, I swear that, I found out later—but Vane saw them. Vane buried them. We went through on schedule because the schedule was money, and the mountain—" My hands were shaking. I watched them shake, detached, as if they belonged to some other man being interrogated. "The mountain did what mountains do. But it had been warned about. By him. In writing."

"And the report afterward? The official finding?"

I opened my mouth.

And that is when the world decided I would tell this story some other night, or never.

* * *

Later—and I have had ten years of practice reconstructing the exact sequence of disasters, so believe me when I say I know how they unfold—I would trace it back to the winch. The old hoist winch on the loading platform above the camp, the one we'd been using to swing pallets of cement down the cut bank to the tunnel works. Its brake band had been wearing for days. Pell had flagged it. One of Vane's foremen, a broad silent man named Crake, had said he'd see to it.

Crake had seen to it.

But standing there at the fire barrel, with Ilsa's eyes on me and the confession half-born in my throat, all I heard was a sound from up the slope—a metallic groan, brief, almost conversational, like a large animal shifting its weight—and then the crack of the anchor pin letting go.

I have been around machinery my whole life. Machinery fails loudly and then quietly, and in between there is always a moment where the failure announces itself, where a man with quick feet can get clear. This time there was no announcement. The cable simply came down the slope like the arm of God, thirty feet of swinging steel with two hundred pounds of counterweight still shackled to its hook, and it took the corner of the cook shack off at shoulder height and swept the fire barrel sideways in a gout of sparks, and if I had been sitting where I'd been sitting four seconds earlier—if Ilsa had not leaned forward and I had not leaned back to meet her—it would have taken me through the ribs and pinned me into the mud like a specimen.

As it was, the hook's shackle clipped my shoulder as I dove, spun me, and slammed me into the side of the supply shed. The world went white, then red at the edges, then very quiet except for the rain, which suddenly seemed enormously loud.

"Coral!" Ilsa was on her knees beside me. Her hands were on my collar, my jaw, checking me with quick professional movements. "Coral, look at me. Look at me."

"I'm looking," I said, and discovered my voice worked, which felt like a miracle on loan.

Above us, men were shouting. Lights swung down the slope. And cutting through all of it, calm as a man stepping onto a platform to catch a train he knew the timetable of, came the voice of Silas Vane.

"Somebody get that winch secured! Crake—" a pause, precisely measured, "—didn't I tell you that brake band was worn? Get it replaced tonight. Tonight, gentlemen, or we'll be burying more than schedules."

* * *

They got me to the mess tent and Dess cut my shirt off my shoulder with the tenderness of a woman who has stitched worse in worse places. The shackle had laid a stripe across my collarbone and upper arm, deep enough to bruise purple-black by morning, shallow enough to bleed impressively without threatening anything vital. Okonkwo held a lantern. Pell hovered. Ilsa stayed at the edge of the lamplight, arms crossed, watching me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't dare try to.

"Worn brake band," Dess said, not looking up from the needle. Her voice was flat in the way I had learned, over years, meant danger. "Funny thing about that. Pell flagged it Tuesday."

"I remember," I said.

"Funny thing," she said again, drawing the thread through, "how things Vane's people are supposed to fix stay broken until they break over somebody."

"Dess."

"I'm just remarking on the weather, Coral."

Vane himself appeared at the tent flap while she was tying off. He had a way of arriving that made it seem like he'd been present all along and merely become visible. Rain dripped off the brim of his hat. His face wore concern the way some men wear cologne—generously, and over something else entirely.

"Pemberton." He stepped in, ducking under the ridgepole, and crouched by my cot with genuine-seeming warmth. "You gave us all a fright. Another inch and—" He shook his head, clicked his tongue. "Old equipment. Monsoon rot. I told the company this spur needed capital before it needed courage."

"You told them a lot of things," I said.

"I did." He smiled. Up close, in lantern light, his eyes were the color of creek water over limestone—clear and cold and moving constantly over something solid underneath. "How's the shoulder?"

"Attached."

"Good. Good. Because I need your head clear tomorrow. The east approach survey can't wait on weather, and frankly—" he patted my knee twice, twice, like a doctor concluding an examination, "—a man your age shouldn't be standing under hoist lines anyway. Leave the platform work to my crews. You stick to the books."

He rose, touched his hat brim to the room—Dess, Okonkwo, Pell, Ilsa, each getting exactly the same courteous nod—and stepped back out into the rain.

Nobody spoke until his footsteps faded. Then Pell said, very quietly, "He called it old equipment before anybody even checked it."

"I noticed that," Okonkwo said.

"So did I," said Ilsa Reyes, from the edge of the light.

* * *

They left me alone near midnight, against my objections, on the theory that a concussed man needs sleep whether or not he agrees. The lamp was turned low. Rain worked the canvas overhead. Through the gap in the tent flap I could see the loading platform up the slope, where Crake's crew worked by floodlight, re-rigging the winch, and I lay there watching them and doing arithmetic.

A worn brake band doesn't fail without warning. It groans. It chatters. Any man who'd run a hoist longer than a week hears it. Crake had heard it. Which meant either Crake was incompetent—which the way he handled that cable told me he was not—or Crake had been told to let it wear.

And the timing. The timing. I had been sitting at that fire barrel with Vane's daughter, opening my mouth to hand her the one thing Vane could not afford for her to hold. Four seconds. If she hadn't leaned forward—

Or if she had. The thought arrived cold, the way the worst thoughts do, dressed in reason. She'd asked the question. She'd drawn the confession up out of me like a splinter. And then the cable came down. Coincidence is a luxury of men who haven't met Silas Vane. But Ilsa had nearly been under that cable herself—she'd been close enough that the shackle missed her by the same margin it missed me. Would Vane spend his own leverage that freely?

Unless he didn't know she was at the fire. Unless he'd counted on one body, mine, and gotten lucky twice.

Or unless none of it was luck at all, and the message was meant for both of us: *this is what happens to people who talk.*

I lay in the dark and tested the thought every way I knew, and every way led back to the same place, which was that I could not know, and that not-knowing was itself the weapon. Vane had built a career on plausible deniability the way other men build on brick. A worn brake band. Monsoon rot. An act of God. The same words they'd carved onto Marrow's death certificate, polished and reused, and why not? They'd worked once.

Sleep would not come, so I got up instead, careful of the shoulder, and stood at the tent flap watching the rain come down the valley in sheets, watching the river eat another foot of the lower track, watching the floodlight on the platform throw Crake's shadow huge and wavering against the cut bank like a man hanging.

Dess found me there. She didn't ask how I was. She handed me a mug of coffee, black, and stood beside me looking at the same rain.

"You were going to tell her," she said. Not a question.

"Yeah."

"And now?"

"Now I know what it costs to start." I wrapped my hands around the mug. The heat bit the bruise where the shackle had caught me, and I was grateful for it—pain you can verify, pain with an honest source. "Vane just showed me the price tag. Question is whether it was a warning or a down payment."

Dess was quiet a long moment. Down the slope, the floodlight flickered, steadied.

"There's something else," she said finally, and her voice had dropped into the register she saved for bad news delivered in front of witnesses. "While they were carrying you in—Okonkwo went through the supply shed. Checking inventory, making sure nothing got crushed." She paused. "Coral, the manifest crate from Vane's office barge. The one with the company seals on it. It's gone."

"Gone?"

"Gone. And Crake's crew spent twenty minutes 'searching' for it in plain view of everybody, real theatrical, and then reported it lost in the flood." She turned and looked at me straight on, and in the half-dark her eyes were hard and frightened at the same time, which on Dess was the most honest expression I'd ever seen her wear. "Whatever Vane's keeping in that crate, he wanted it out of reach tonight. Same night somebody drops a winch cable on you."

Outside, the rain kept falling, patient as arithmetic. Somewhere up the valley, deep under all that water, the Kestrel tunnel sat full of dark and held its dead, and I understood—with the terrible clarity that comes after a near miss, when your own heartbeat sounds like someone else's—that whatever happened next would happen fast, and that if I was going to give Ilsa Reyes her father's truth, I had maybe one night left in which to do it alive.
