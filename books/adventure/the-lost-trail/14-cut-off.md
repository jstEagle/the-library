## 14. Cut Off

Okonkwo had spent enough years on the water to know the sound a river made when it stopped being a river and became something else. It wasn't louder, exactly. It was *fuller*—a low continuous roar beneath everything, like the valley itself had begun to breathe through its teeth.

He stood at the edge of camp in the gray half-light of the fourth morning of rain and listened to it, and what he heard made his stomach tighten.

The Kestrel valley had flooded before. That was old knowledge, written into the way the dead rails climbed the canyon walls on stone embankments built higher than any sensible engineer would bother with. The men who laid this line had known what the water did here every spring. They'd built for it. But they hadn't built for whatever was coming down out of the high country now, and neither, Okonkwo suspected, had Silas Vane's schedule accounted for it. The monsoon was supposed to be two weeks out. It had arrived angry and early, and it had arrived with them still inside it.

He turned up his collar and walked the camp's perimeter the way he did every morning—not because anyone asked, but because someone had to count what they had left, and Coral couldn't be the one to do it anymore. Coral had gone strange since the night he'd come back from Vane's railcar with his face the color of wet ash. He worked. He worked harder than any of them. But there was a distance in him now, like a man standing on one side of glass while the rest of them lived on the other.

Dess was already at the cook fire under the tarp, coaxing heat from damp wood. Pell sat wrapped in a blanket beside her, still moving stiffly from the near-drowning three days past. Ilsa Reyes crouched at the water's edge with her instruments, reading the gauge she'd rigged from a surveyor's rod, writing numbers in her waterproof notebook with the calm of a woman who had already done her grieving about the weather and moved on to arithmetic.

"Tell me," Okonkwo said.

She didn't look up. "River's up another meter since midnight. If it climbs like this through tomorrow, we lose the lower embankment. The one we came in on."

"The road out."

"There is no road out." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "There hasn't been since Tuesday. I thought you knew."

He had known. He had walked down to the switchback below camp on Wednesday and found forty meters of track bed simply *gone*, dissolved into brown water, the rails hanging in the air like threads off the edge of a torn coat. He had known then that the boat run Pell and Ilsa had barely survived was the last supply run anyone would make. He had known it and said nothing, because saying it aloud would make it true in front of everyone, and there were some truths you carried until you absolutely could not.

But the food situation had stopped being a thing a man could carry alone.

* * *

They gathered under the big tarp at midday, all six of them, while the rain hammered the canvas like a fist demanding entry. Okonkwo had counted the stores himself: eleven days of food if they ate thin, five if they ate like working men. The diesel for the pump was nearly gone. The radio—the good set Vane's company had installed at the tunnel portal, the one that was supposed to check in twice daily with the mainline office—had gone silent the night before.

"I tried it again this morning," Pell said. He was twenty-three and tried hard not to look scared, which only made it more visible. "Dead air. Not static even. Just nothing. Like somebody pulled the plug on the whole world."

"Antenna might've gone down in the wind," Dess said.

"The antenna's fine. I checked it twice." Pell turned the radio over in his hands as if it might confess. "And the backup set in the foreman's car? Same thing. Both of them, same night."

Okonkwo watched Coral across the fire. Coral had gone very still, the way he went still when he was doing fast, ugly math.

"Both sets don't fail by accident," Coral said quietly.

"No," Okonkwo agreed. "They don't."

The silence stretched. Rain filled it. Somewhere up-valley, a section of hillside let go with a long grinding groan, and everyone flinched except Ilsa, who wrote something down.

"So we're cut off," Dess said flatly. She was the oldest of them after Okonkwo, a small wiry woman with gray coming in at her temples and hands that had rebuilt more bridges than most men had ever crossed. She didn't waste words on fear. "No radio. No road. Eleven days of beans. And Vane's got eight men up here who answer to him and nobody else."

"Seven," Pell said. "One of them took the pump barge downriver day before yesterday. Haven't seen him since."

"That's my point exactly," Dess said. "Where'd he go, Pell? You can't take a barge down a washed-out river."

Nobody answered. The question just sat there among them, growing.

Okonkwo looked around the circle—at Dess's set jaw, at Pell's white knuckles around the radio, at Ilsa's careful blankness, at Coral staring into the fire like it was showing him something he'd paid to see—and felt the shape of their situation settle over him with a weight he recognized from bad places in his past. He had been in camps like this before, in another country, in another life, where the men in charge decided that the people who knew too much were worth less than the trouble they caused. He knew how it started. It never started with violence. It started with arithmetic.

"We are not workers anymore," he said. Everyone looked at him. "I want to say it plainly so nobody can pretend later they didn't hear it. A worker is somebody the boss needs. We dug, we pumped, we surveyed, and Vane needed us, so we were safe. The line is as open as it's going to get. The water has ended the work. From today, we are not workers. We are witnesses. And witnesses on a closed site with no radio and no road—" He let it hang.

"Witnesses to what?" Pell asked. His voice cracked on the second word.

"To whatever Silas Vane doesn't want read out loud," Ilsa said.

Everyone turned to her. She met their eyes without apology, and Okonkwo saw her decide something, saw her weigh it and set it down, the same way she weighed everything.

"My father worked this spur," she said. "Ten years ago. He died in the tunnel. The official report called it an accident. I have reason to believe it wasn't, and I have reason to believe the man who wrote the safety report that kept the crews going in—that man is sitting right here at this fire."

Her eyes went to Coral. Coral didn't look up. The rain hammered. Okonkwo felt the ground shift under all of them, the social ground now as surely as the physical.

"You knew?" Dess said to Coral. Not shouting. Worse than shouting—quiet, precise, wounded.

"I knew there was a death," Coral said. "I knew the reports were wrong. I signed things I shouldn't have signed, ten years ago, to keep the company from burying the rest of the crew alongside him. That's the short version. There's a longer version and you'll get it, all of it, I swear it—but not tonight, because tonight I need you listening to me about something else." He finally raised his head, and Okonkwo saw that behind the exhaustion there was still steel, the thing they'd all followed up dead lines and onto rotten trestles, the thing that made men trust him against their own interest. "Vane brought us up here to reopen this spur and put his name on it. But the flood changed his arithmetic too. We know things now. Dess found his ledger. Ilsa knows who she is. And Vane cannot let a single one of us ride down out of this valley carrying what we carry. Not while the world outside thinks we're just a crew stuck in the rain."

"He wouldn't," Pell started.

"He buried my father's reports," Ilsa said. "He let men believe a lie for ten years. What do you think he does with seven loose ends and a flooded valley?"

* * *

Okonkwo took first watch, because he always did, because sleep came hard to him and useless watch-standing came easy. He walked the perimeter with the rain running off his hood and counted the things that mattered: the boats, two, both damaged, one badly; the food cache, moved that afternoon to high ground inside the old powder magazine above camp, dry stone walls a century thick; the arms—one shotgun, Dess's, four shells; and the positions of Vane's men, which he checked last and longest.

Their camp sat two hundred meters downstream, under the lee of the tunnel portal's masonry. Seven men. Cook smoke all morning, then none after noon—which meant either they'd finished eating or they'd doused the fire to move unseen. Their boats were gone from the bank. All of them.

At dusk he saw the lantern signal. Three flashes from the direction of the washout, answered by two flashes from Vane's camp. Then nothing. He stood in the dark and the downpour and understood that the men downstream were talking to somebody upstream, and that the geography of the trap had just gotten smaller.

He found Coral at the magazine, checking the food cache by lamplight, and told him in a low voice. Coral listened with his eyes closed, the lamplight jumping on his face.

"How many ways in and out of this valley?" Okonkwo asked.

"One road, gone. One river, unnavigable. The tunnel." Coral opened his eyes. "The old Kestrel bore runs clean through the ridge to the next drainage. Ten kilometers. Half of it flooded, but the crown of the arch stays above water for the first three—I walked it once, years ago. On the far side there's a forest service track, if it still exists, if it isn't washed out, if a man can walk ten kilometers of drowned tunnel in rising water."

"That's a lot of ifs."

"It's also the only door left." Coral closed the magazine's iron door and shot the bolt. "And Vane knows about the tunnel. Which means if he decides to close that door too—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Okonkwo had already done the arithmetic: seven desperate men, explosives in the powder magazine that Vane's company had stocked for the reopening, and a tunnel full of witnesses' only exit. A little charge at the right place, and the valley would keep its secrets indefinitely. Accidents happened in floods. Dead men told no ledgers.

"We should move tonight," Okonkwo said. "While they're still deciding."

"And go where? Into that?" Coral gestured at the black roaring dark where the river ran. "We'd drown honest instead of dying convenient. No. We hold the magazine—it's stone, it's defensible, it's above the waterline. We ration to nine days. And we watch them every hour. If they start moving toward the tunnel mouth with anything heavier than a lantern, we'll know what they've decided, and we'll meet them there."

"With a shotgun and four shells."

"With the truth," Coral said, and for a moment Okonkwo heard something underneath it—not confidence, exactly. Reckoning. As if Coral had already chosen the price he was willing to pay and was only waiting to find out whether he'd have to pay it alone.

* * *

Past midnight, the rain eased for the first time in four days—not stopping, just dropping to a steady whisper—and in that relative quiet Okonkwo heard the other sound. A scraping. Rhythmic. Coming from the talus slope above the camp.

He woke Dess with a hand on her shoulder and a finger to his lips, and the two of them went up the slope together without lamps, moving on the balls of their feet the way he'd learned in a war he rarely talked about. The scraping resolved into a figure crouched among the rocks, prying at something wedged in the scree.

It was one of Vane's men—young, hollow-cheeked, the one they called Tully. He had a pry bar and a canvas sack, and he froze when Dess's shotgun clicked back its hammer an arm's length from his ear.

"Easy," Okonkwo said. "Slow hands."

"I wasn't—" The boy's voice shook. Up close, in the faint moonlight through torn cloud, Okonkwo could see he was soaked through, shivering, and terrified in a way that had nothing to do with the gun. "I had to get clear. I had to warn somebody."

"Warn us of what?"

Tully's eyes darted between them. When he spoke, the words came out in a rush, tumbling. "Vane's got a case of gelignite from the magazine stock—he told us it was for blasting a logjam, but he sent two men up to the tunnel mouth an hour ago with it, and he's got the last boat hidden at the falls, and he told us nobody rides out of this valley till the company man comes, and the company man isn't coming because there is no company man, there's just him and the ledger and—" He swallowed. "He said the flood does what the flood does. He said it twice. Like he was practicing saying it."

Above them, up-valley, a dull concussive thump rolled down the canyon, felt more than heard, and the dogs of echo chased each other along the ridgelines. The rock under their feet shivered.

Dess looked at Okonkwo. Okonkwo looked toward the tunnel, invisible in the dark, where the only door out of the valley had just, perhaps, been sealed with ten sticks of dynamite and a lie about a logjam.

"Coral," Dess breathed. "Get Coral. Now."

The rain came back all at once, hard and total, erasing the world down to arm's length—and somewhere beyond it, at the mouth of the tunnel where a dead man's name was carved into the masonry, the water began to rise.
