## 15. Marrow's Notes

The rain had found a rhythm by the third day of the flood—a long, patient drumming on the tarps and tin roofs of the stranded camp, like something enormous waiting just outside the light. Ilsa Reyes sat in the lee of the supply tent with her father's letters spread across her knees inside an oilcloth sleeve, and for the hundredth time she asked herself the question that had carried her up this drowned valley: *How do you hand a man his own guilt when he's been carrying someone else's?*

She had watched Coral Crane for two weeks now. She knew how he moved through a camp—quietly, always last to eat, first to check a rope or a bilge line. She knew the way his eyes went to the tunnel mouth every time the clouds parted, as if it were a wound he couldn't stop touching. And she knew, with the certainty of a woman who had spent nine years reading other people's grief, that whatever he was carrying was heavier than cowardice. Cowardice looked different. She'd seen it on Vane's foremen, who laughed too loudly and checked their pay ledgers too often.

Cowardice didn't look like Coral Crane standing alone at the water's edge at dawn, saying nothing to anyone.

* * *

He came to her tent that evening, which surprised her. She had expected to have to go to him again—to corner him over coffee the way she had twice before, laying out her little probes like snares and watching him step around every one of them without ever quite springing them. But tonight the rain drove everyone under canvas, and the camp had contracted to its smallest shape: three crew tents, Vane's foremen huddled apart with their rifles wrapped in oilskin, and the black seam of the tunnel mouth across the water, half-drowned, patient.

Coral stood at the entrance of her tent with rain running off his slicker, and he did not come in.

"You asked me about the report," he said. "The safety survey. The one before the collapse."

"I did."

"There wasn't one." His voice was flat, careful, like a man walking on ice he didn't trust. "Whatever you've heard, there was no formal survey filed. I wrote what I could from memory afterward, and the company took it, and that was the end of it. If someone told you different—"

"Mr. Crane." She said it quietly enough that he stopped. "Sit down."

He didn't sit. He never sat in her presence, as though standing were some penance he'd assigned himself. So she reached into the oilcloth sleeve instead, and drew out the letters, and held them where the lantern light could find them.

Three bundles, tied with waxed cord gone soft with age. The topmost envelope was water-stained but legible, addressed in a small, precise hand she knew better than her own heartbeat: *E. Marrow, Field Engineer, Kestrel Spur Survey.*

Coral went very still. Not the stillness of surprise—the stillness of a man hearing a sound he has spent ten years pretending doesn't exist.

"Where," he said, "did you get those."

"They came to my mother in 1919. Four years after the tunnel." Ilsa kept her voice level. It had taken her years to be able to say the date aloud; she would not let it shake now, not here, not in front of the man whose face was doing something complicated and terrible in the lantern light. "My father mailed them the week before he died. He told her if anything happened to him, she should keep them somewhere safe and tell no one—not the company, not the railroad board, no one—until she felt the time was right. My mother never felt the time was right. She died feeling that way."

"Elias Marrow didn't have—" Coral's voice caught and restarted. "He didn't have family. That's what I was told."

"He had a wife in Ashford and a daughter he sent money to under a false name so the company wouldn't dock his wages for dependents." She heard the bitterness come through and didn't try to leash it. "He was saving to bring us out west in the spring. Spring of 1916. He had a drawing of a house he wanted to build. I still have it. It had a porch facing east because he liked to watch the sun come up over the rails."

The rain filled the silence between them, drumming, indifferent.

"He wrote to us every week," Ilsa said. "Every week, Mr. Crane, for eight months. And then the letters changed. And then they stopped." She untied the cord on the first bundle with fingers that were steadier than she deserved. "I've read these maybe four hundred times. I know them the way you know scripture. But there are things in them I didn't understand until I came up this valley and saw the rock myself. Things I think only another engineer would understand. Which is why I need you to read them. Not me. You."

"Why me?"

"Because your name is in them."

That landed. She watched it land—watched his shoulders take the weight of it like a man taking a blow he'd been bracing for all his life and finding it heavier anyway.

* * *

He sat down then. Finally. On her spare crate, slicker dripping, hands open on his knees like a man surrendering to something.

Ilsa passed him the first letter and turned away to tend the lantern, giving him the mercy of not being watched while he read. But she listened. She had learned to listen to men reading her father's words—the catch of breath at certain lines, the way a page might be held a moment longer than the eyes needed.

The first letter was ordinary. Weather, wages, a joke about the cook's beans. She let Coral finish it.

"The second one," she said.

This one was dated March 2nd, 1916—eleven days before the collapse. She had it by heart, but she watched Coral's lips move slightly as he read, the way readers do when they've stopped skimming and started drowning.

*"There is water in the rock where there should not be water. I flagged it Tuesday and again today. The seep line runs diagonal across the north heading at chainage 440, and it is widening. I have measured it three times this week and it gains a finger's width each time. This is not seasonal. This is the mountain telling us what it intends."*

Coral's breathing changed.

*"I have written to the division office requesting we halt advance work on the north heading until drainage can be cut. I do not expect an answer I like. Vane wants the spur driven through before the monsoon closes the passes, and Vane does not hear things he does not want to hear. But I am writing it down. Every measurement, every flag, every date. If the mountain wins, the record will show who was warned and who chose not to listen."*

"Stop," Coral said.

Ilsa stopped.

Coral was holding the page the way a man holds a bird—too carefully, afraid of crushing it, afraid of it flying away. "This isn't possible," he said. "There was no record. Afterward, when the company inquiry came through, they said there'd been no warnings filed. No flags. They said the collapse was unforeseeable. Act of God, unstable strata, nobody's fault—or—" and here his voice did something she had never heard it do in two weeks of watching him: it broke, briefly, and he mastered it, "—or somebody's fault. Mine. Because I signed the reconstruction of the heading schedule. Because I kept the crews driving when Elias wanted to stop. They put the paper in front of me and I signed it and eleven days later the mountain came down, and I have spent ten years believing that I killed him because I didn't listen hard enough."

"You didn't see his reports."

"No." The word came out cracked. "I never saw any reports. I asked—I asked after, whether Eli had raised concerns, and Vane himself told me, man to man, hand on my shoulder, that Eli hadn't flagged anything, that the poor devil never knew what hit him, that no one could have known. And I believed him because wanting to believe him was easier than the alternative." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and aimed, for once, not inward. "Vane told me there were no reports."

"And there were." Ilsa passed him the third letter. "Dated March 9th. Five days before."

Coral read it in silence, and the silence stretched so long she thought he might not speak again. When he did, his voice had gone quiet in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have.

*"Crane is pushing the crews hard and I cannot blame him—he answers to Vane same as I do, and Vane has made it plain that delays will cost men their winter wages. But I have now formally reported the seepage three times, in writing, copies retained. If the north heading goes, it will not be because the warning was not given. It will be because the warning was buried. I have taken the liberty of mailing duplicates to my home address. A man ought to keep his conscience somewhere the company can't reach it."*

"Duplicates," Coral whispered. "He mailed duplicates."

"To my mother," Ilsa said. "All of them. Every report, every measurement log, every letter to the division office marked *received* and never answered. Nine documents, Mr. Crane. Nine times my father told the company that tunnel was going to kill someone. Nine times the paper went into a drawer in Silas Vane's office and stayed there." She leaned forward. "And then the mountain came down, and Vane needed the record to show nothing existed to bury. So the record showed nothing. And the only man left alive who'd been inside the north heading that week was you."

Coral set the letters down on the crate between them, aligned their edges with a precision that looked almost compulsive, and put his face in his hands.

For a long time the only sounds were the rain and, far off, the river worrying at its new banks.

"I signed the schedule," he said finally, into his palms. "That part's true. I did that. Whatever Vane buried, I still kept thirty men driving steel toward a heading I had a bad feeling about, because I trusted the man above me to have read the reports I never saw. I can't put that down. I won't. Ten years I've carried it and I'm not going to set it on your father's grave and call it lighter than it is."

"I'm not asking you to."

He looked up.

"I'm asking you to carry the right weight," Ilsa said. "You've been carrying guilt for killing him. That was never yours. What's yours is smaller and worse—you trusted a man who fed his own engineer to a mountain to protect a schedule, and then he fed you the lie that kept you quiet for ten years. You weren't the killer, Mr. Crane. You were the alibi. There's a difference, and it matters, and I need you to know it before—" She stopped herself.

"Before what?"

Before Vane finishes what he started, she thought. Before the staged accident becomes a real one and the ledger pins everything on a dead man's name and a broken survivor. But she said none of it. Some knowledge was still hers to spend wisely.

"Before you decide what to do with the truth," she said instead. "Because it changes things. Guilt makes men hide. But this—" she touched the letters, "—this isn't guilt anymore. This is evidence. And evidence obligates."

* * *

He stayed an hour. Longer. She gave him the letters one at a time and watched him read every word, and somewhere in the middle of the fifth document—the measurement logs, columns of figures in her father's neat hand, seep widths and dates and chainages—Coral began, haltingly, to talk.

He talked about the night of the collapse. For the first time in her hearing, and she suspected for the first time in anyone's, he talked about it whole: the groan the rock made before it let go, the headlamps swinging, the dust that turned their lights to brown stars. He talked about digging. Three days of digging with his bare hands bleeding, pulling out seven men alive and then, on the third night, finding Elias Marrow under a slab of granite with his field notebook still buttoned inside his coat, its pages soaked through, its ink run to gray ghosts.

"The notebook," Ilsa said sharply. "What happened to it?"

"The company took it. 'For the inquiry.'" Coral's mouth twisted. "It never appeared in any inquiry. I asked. Vane told me it had been lost in processing." He shook his head slowly. "Lost in processing. Like the reports. Like the truth. That man buries paper the way other men bury bodies."

"That's why he needs you dead or discredited," Ilsa said. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. But the letters had burned the caution out of her. "Mr. Crane—if my father's reports surface, Vane's entire account collapses. The company can't claim unforeseeable. The widows' suits reopen. The Kestrel settlement gets overturned. Everything he built on top of that tunnel comes down. And the only versions of events he can't control are yours and mine. Yours he's trying to buy. Mine—" she smiled, and it wasn't a warm smile, "—mine he thinks is just a hydrologist who asks too many questions."

Coral stared at her. Then, slowly, he said: "You're not a hydrologist."

"I have a hydrologist's training. It was useful for reading the valley." She gathered the letters back into their sleeve, tying the cord with deliberate care. "But no. I'm Elias Marrow's daughter, and I came up this river to find out what happened to my father, and I have found out. Now the question is what happens next—and to whom."

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere down the dark slope, a shout went up from the foremen's tents—short, sharp, wrong—and was swallowed again by the water. Neither of them moved.

Then Okonkwo's silhouette filled the gap in the tent flap, rain streaming off him, and his voice came low and urgent:

"Coral. Dess just came back from checking the boat lines. Vane's foremen are loading crates onto the barge in the dark—and Coral, one of them dropped it. Dess saw the papers inside." A pause, the rain roaring. "They're your signature on the old survey. Originals. And Vane's men are taking them to the tunnel."
