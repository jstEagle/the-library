## 16. The Choice at the Culvert

The rain had found a rhythm by evening, steady as a metronome, and Coral stood under the tarp strung between two supply crates and watched the water climb the culvert's throat.

It was an old stone culvert where the Kestrel Spur crossed a side channel of the valley river, built in the same year as the tunnel, and it was doing what all the old work did—holding, barely, grudgingly, like a man who'd been asked to carry too much for too long. The water came through it in a brown rope as thick as his waist now. That morning it had been knee-thick. He'd been measuring it every hour since noon, not because anyone had asked him to, but because measuring things was what he knew how to do when he couldn't fix them.

Behind him, the camp had gone quiet in the way camps go quiet when everyone is pretending to sleep. Dess had banked the cook fire down to coals under a sheet of tin. Okonkwo sat up on the flatcar they'd hauled to high ground, rifle across his knees, watching the dark. Pell was asleep for real—Pell could sleep through anything, which Coral envied more than he could say—and Ilsa Reyes was somewhere with her father's letters, reading them again by candlelight, or maybe just holding them.

And Silas Vane was coming down the slope from the foremen's tents, alone, without a lantern, moving through the rain like a man who had never once been inconvenienced by weather.

Coral heard him before he saw him—the particular unhurried crunch of Vane's boots on wet gravel—and felt his whole body tighten the way it used to tighten, years ago, before inspections, before hearings, before any moment when a man in a good coat might decide the fate of men in bad ones.

"Mr. Crane." Vane stepped under the edge of the tarp, shaking rain off his shoulders with a small efficient motion, as if even water obeyed him. "You're up late."

"So are you."

"I don't sleep well in weather." Vane looked out at the culvert, at the brown rope of water, and made a low appreciative sound. "She's rising faster than my engineers predicted. Faster than yours would have, if you were still allowed to have opinions about this line." He turned. In the coal-light his face was half gold, half shadow, and both halves were smiling. "I've been thinking about you, Coral. About us. About how this ends."

"It ends when the water goes down and we walk out."

"That's one ending." Vane produced a cigar but didn't light it; he rolled it slowly between his fingers, a habit Coral remembered from ten years ago, from hearing rooms and hotel lobbies, from the last time Vane had smiled at him across a table while deciding something terrible. "There's another one. Better. Cleaner. I thought you should hear it tonight, while there's still time to choose it."

Coral said nothing. He had learned that silence pulled words out of Silas Vane the way a siphon pulls fuel.

Vane obliged. "The boat comes tomorrow night. Not the leaky tub your boy nearly drowned in—a proper craft, covered, engine, enough fuel to run the flooded line back past the Narrows before the peak hits the gorge. Two seats. You and whoever you name." He let that sit. Rain drummed on the tarp above them like fingers tapping out a question. "Dess. The boy. Okonkwo. All three, if you want. I'll allow it."

"You'll *allow* it," Coral repeated.

"I'm being generous. Don't make me regret the word choice." Vane finally lit the cigar, cupping the match against the wind that wasn't there, purely out of ceremony. The smell of it hit Coral like a hand on the back of the neck—tobacco and sulfur, the smell of boardrooms, of the hearing where a man named Aldous Marrow had become a statistic. "All it costs is a signature."

"There it is."

"There it is," Vane agreed pleasantly. "One page. A confession, in your own hand, dated and witnessed. You falsified the safety report on the Kestrel Spur survey. You suppressed the instability findings. You ordered the crew into the tunnel against protocol. Marrow's death was the result of your negligence and your forgery, full stop. The company's record comes out clean. My name comes out clean. And you—" he exhaled smoke, watched it curl away into the rain, "—you get to live. Get your people out alive. Start over somewhere the map doesn't know you. Isn't that what you're always doing? Starting over? Here's a chance to do it properly. Funded, even. There'd be money in it. Enough that you'd never have to patch another dead man's railroad as long as you live."

Coral looked at the culvert. The water in its throat had climbed another inch since he'd started listening. He thought: *an hour, maybe less, before it overtops the low ground behind camp.* He thought: *the boat is real. Vane doesn't bluff with hardware. If he says there's a boat, there's a boat.*

He thought: *one signature, and Pell never has to run another river in a splitting hull. One signature, and Dess gets paid, gets warm, gets home.*

That was the thing about Silas Vane. He never offered you a lie. He offered you the truth, bent into the shape of a mercy.

"And Ilsa?" Coral said.

"What about her?"

"She reads those notes of her father's. She knows the reports existed. She knows the company buried them. Your clean ledger doesn't survive her."

Vane's smile didn't move, but something behind it did—a shutter closing, a lamp carried to another room. "Miss Reyes is a grieving woman who has gotten some ideas. Grieving women with ideas are a temporary problem. Whereas you—" he tapped ash into the rain, "—are a permanent one, unless we come to terms. I'd rather we came to terms, Coral. Truly. I've always liked you. You were the best engineer I ever ruined."

"You didn't ruin me. I ruined me. You just held the door."

"Semantics." Vane spread his hands, magnanimous, rain streaming off his cuffs. "The point is this. Tomorrow night, either you sign and ride out dry, or you don't sign and stay here with the rest of us, and we all find out together what kind of flood this turns out to be. And whether the witnesses to certain documents happen to survive it." He paused, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse than anything else he'd said. "You've spent ten years carrying this alone so those men wouldn't have to. I'm offering you the same bargain you already made. Just this time, you get paid."

Coral's hands had gone cold inside his gloves. That was the seduction of it—he could feel it working on him even as he hated it, the way floodwater works on a foundation, patient, finding every crack. A clean ending. One page. His whole long penance compressed into a single stroke of the pen, and everyone he loved carried out ahead of the water.

It was exactly the lie he'd been telling himself for ten years—that a sin signed off and buried was the same as a sin survived.

"One night," Coral said.

Vane raised an eyebrow.

"I want one night to decide. You get your answer at dawn."

"Coral—"

"You want my signature, you'll wait twelve hours for it. Or take it off my corpse, which seems to be plan B anyway." He kept his voice level, kept his eyes on Vane's, and was distantly proud that neither shook. "One night."

For a long moment the only sound was the rain and the culvert swallowing. Then Vane laughed softly, put the cigar between his teeth, and nodded. "One night. Dawn, then. I'll be at the foremen's tent." He stepped back out into the rain, then stopped, half-turned, and added, almost as an afterthought: "Do think about the boy. Pell, isn't it? Young men float poorly in cold water. It's a fact of hydrology. Ask Miss Reyes."

He walked away up the slope without a lantern, because of course he didn't need one. Men like Vane never needed light to find their way through the dark. They'd built it.

* * *

Coral didn't go to his bedroll. He walked the camp's perimeter instead, the way he did on bad nights, checking lines and anchors he'd already checked, and it was on the third pass that he found Dess awake, sitting on the flatcar steps with a blanket around her shoulders and a look on her face that meant she'd heard every word.

"How much?" she said.

"Enough."

"He offered you a boat."

"He offered me a boat."

Dess looked out at the culvert. The water had come up over the lowest rail tie now; he could see it glinting. "Ten years I've worked for you," she said. "Ten years, and I've never once asked you what happened at Kestrel. Never asked why we only ever work dead lines. Never asked why you flinch when someone says the word *tunnel*." She pulled the blanket tighter. "I figured a man earns his silence. But Coral—if whatever's eating you can be traded for Pell's life, then it's not worth what it costs. Whatever it is. Sell it."

"That's what he's counting on."

"Then maybe he's counting right." She stood, kissed his cheek the way a sister does, brisk and fierce, and went to her bedroll, leaving him alone with the rain and the arithmetic.

* * *

Near midnight, Coral took the lantern and walked out along the spur to the culvert itself, boots in standing water, and crouched at the mouth of the stone throat with the light held low.

The water ran hard and brown and full of the valley—pine needles, silt, a broken fence post turning end over end. And snagged against the culvert's grate, half-submerged, turning slowly in the current, was a sheet of heavy paper, sodden but legible. Vane's stationery. Part of a ledger page, torn free, ink bleeding but not gone. Numbers. Dates. A column of figures in a neat clerk's hand, and beside them, in Vane's own writing, annotations Coral had learned to read ten years ago in a hearing room: *adjusted*, *reconciled*, *per C.C.*

Per Coral Crane.

Vane hadn't lost this page. Vane had left it. Placed it, maybe—let the flood deliver it to Coral's door like a calling card, a sample of the goods, a promise of what the confession would bury forever. *Sign, and no one ever sees this. Refuse, and every scrap of it surfaces, and it will hang you, and I will hold the rope.*

Coral fished the page out of the grate with numb fingers and held it in the lantern light until the ink ran and the dates dissolved and it was only paper again, only pulp, only nothing.

But he'd seen the dates. And one of them was wrong—one entry dated three days *after* the report Coral had supposedly forged, three days after Marrow died, which meant the ledger wasn't just leverage. It was sloppy. It was *false*. Vane had built his trap too fast, in a flood season, and the seams were showing.

Coral stood in the rain at the mouth of the culvert and felt something shift in his chest—not hope, exactly. Something harder and older than hope. The feeling of a man testing a tie plate and finding, beneath the rust, sound iron.

Behind him, up the slope, a candle flickered in Ilsa Reyes's tent. She was awake too. Of course she was.

Dawn was four hours off, and Vane wanted his answer at first light.

Coral turned toward her tent, the ruined page dripping in his fist, and started walking.
