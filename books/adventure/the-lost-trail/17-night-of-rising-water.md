## 17. Night of Rising Water

### Dess

The river spoke first.

Dess heard it before she was fully awake—a low grinding under the rain, like millstones turning somewhere in the dark. She'd grown up on the Kettle River, and she knew that sound. It was boulders moving. It was the river rearranging its own bones.

She rolled out of her bunk into six inches of cold water.

"Up!" she shouted, already moving. "Everybody up—the camp's going under!"

The lanterns had drowned. She found Pell by his breathing, hauled him off his cot into water that sloshed at their knees, and shoved him toward the supply tent where Okonkwo's voice was cutting through the rain like a knife through canvas. Outside, the world had come apart. The Kestrel valley, which all day had been a slow brown ribbon between black hills, was now a lake with a current in it. The dock was gone—she could see only the tops of its pilings breaking the surface downstream. Vane's foremen were spilling out of their tents with lamps held high, shouting at one another, and above it all came the sound Dess would remember for the rest of her life: the deep, patient roar of water finding every low place at once.

"Coral!" Pell screamed into the storm. "Where's Coral?"

Nobody answered. Nobody knew.

* * *

### Okonkwo

Okonkwo had the strongbox out of the supply tent before the second wave hit. It wasn't much to look at—an ammunition case with a rope handle—but inside it were Marrow's field notes, wrapped in oilcloth, and Ilsa's copies of the letters, and the page of Vane's ledger that Dess had pulled from the foreman's tent three nights ago. Everything they had. Everything Vane wanted.

He cinched the strap across his chest and waded toward the high shelf of rock at the camp's eastern edge, where the tunnel mouth gaped like a wound in the hillside. That was the plan, if there'd ever been a plan: get to high ground, get to the tunnel, wait out the peak. But halfway there a lamp flared in front of him and Bracken—one of Vane's men, big through the shoulders, rain streaming off his jaw—stepped into his path with a crowbar held low.

"That box," Bracken said. "Vane wants it."

"Vane can swim for it," Okonkwo said.

He saw the swing coming—he always saw swings coming; you didn't grow up docking freight in Port Mercy without learning—and he dropped his shoulder and let the crowbar pass over him, close enough to part the rain. Then he drove both hands into Bracken's chest and pushed, and the man went down into the flood with a splash that swallowed his curse. The current took him ten feet before he caught a tent guy-line and hauled himself up, coughing, and by then Okonkwo was gone, chest-deep now, the box riding high against his collarbone, the tunnel mouth a black rectangle ahead of him lit from within by someone's surviving lamp.

Ilsa stood in the entrance, hauling on a rope tied off to an old survey spike. Behind her, deeper in, the water ran shin-deep and rising.

"In!" she screamed. "Get them all in!"

* * *

### Pell

Pell couldn't swim. He'd never told anyone that—not Dess, not Okonkwo, not even Coral, though Coral had once watched him refuse a turn at the swimming hole outside Meridian Falls and said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy. So when the flood took the camp, Pell did what he'd done his whole life: he grabbed hold of something fixed and refused to let go.

The something was the cook wagon's axle. He clung to it as the water climbed his ribs, then his shoulders, the wagon groaning around him like something being eaten. Rain hammered his face. Somewhere upstream a section of rail line gave way with a sound like the world cracking open, and the river jumped another foot in the space of a breath.

Then hands closed on his collar.

He thrashed—instinct, blind animal panic—and heard Ilsa Reyes shout directly into his ear: *"Stop fighting me or we both die!"*

She'd come out along the collapsed dock pilings, hand over hand, a rope around her waist paid out from the tunnel behind her. She got her arm across his chest, locked her grip, and kicked, and together they swung out into the current with the axle spinning away beneath them. The river pulled. Pell's feet left the bottom of the world. He stopped thinking entirely and simply held on to this stranger who had saved his life twice now—once from drowning, once, maybe, from himself—and let her haul him hand over hand up the rope while the flood roared past their legs like a freight train with no track and no destination.

They came up the tunnel mouth gasping. Dess pulled Pell inside by the arm hard enough to bruise. Ilsa knelt on the wet stone, dripping, and looked back out at the drowned camp, at the lamps of Vane's men scattered across the water like dying stars.

"They're still coming," she said. "Look."

* * *

### Dess

They came in waist-deep water, four of them, shoulder to shoulder, because Silas Vane had told them the box mattered more than anything else in the valley—including, apparently, their lives. Dess watched them advance through the tunnel entrance, lambs held in their teeth, and felt something cold settle in her that had nothing to do with the flood.

Bracken led them. His nose was bleeding from where he'd gone under, and he wiped it with the back of his hand and kept coming.

"You're stealing company property," he called over the water. "That's theft. That's prison."

"It's a dead man's words," Dess called back, "and you're standing in the river that killed him. There's a sermon in that if you've got the sense to hear it."

Bracken lunged.

What happened next happened fast and half-blind, in lamplight and spray. Okonkwo met the first man with a length of broken oar and turned him aside like a gate swinging shut. Dess went into the water deliberately—she was the strongest swimmer among them, and she knew the advantage belonged to whoever chose the depth—and came up behind the second foreman and locked her arm across his throat and rode him down until he thrashed loose and retreated, gagging on the river. Pell, pressed back against the tunnel wall with nowhere to run and no strength worth naming, did the only brave thing available to him: he picked up the lantern and threw it. Glass burst on the rocks. Burning oil spread across the shallows in a blue sheet, and two of Vane's men broke off at once, flinching from the flames as they skated on the current.

That left Bracken and Okonkwo, chest-deep, circling each other in the tunnel mouth with the whole roaring night beyond them.

"I know what you're carrying," Bracken panted. "I know what it proves. You think Vane lets any of you walk out of this valley?"

"He doesn't have to let us," Okonkwo said. "The river decides who walks out. Same as it decided for Marrow."

And then the river decided again.

It came without warning—a wall of it, born of some collapse far upstream, some final surrender of the hills—and it hit the tunnel mouth broadside. Dess saw it coming as a blackness blacker than the night, heard it as a change in pitch, a rising note like a train whistle blown backwards. She screamed for everyone to get behind the rock spur. The water took Bracken mid-swing, tore him off his feet, and folded him into the dark. Okonkwo caught the tie-down rope with one fist and the strongbox strap with the other and became, for three endless seconds, a bridge between the flood and everything they'd fought for. Dess got a handful of his coat. Pell got Dess. Ilsa got Pell. Four people and a dead man's notes, chained together against the biggest thing any of them had ever seen.

The surge passed. The water level dropped as suddenly as it had risen, leaving them sprawled on the wet stone, coughing, alive.

Bracken was gone. No one said so, but everyone counted.

* * *

### Ilsa

Later—Ilsa would never be able to say how much later, minutes or hours—they regrouped in the dry pocket forty yards into the tunnel, where the old maintenance alcove still held its rusted tool rack and a shelf above the waterline. They had one lamp, one strongbox, five soaked and shaking people, and no Coral.

"We can't stay here," Dess said. Her voice was scraped raw. "The tunnel floods top to bottom when the peak crests. My father worked these lines—he told me. There's a ventilation shaft somewhere past the third bend, but it's a crawl, and it comes out on the ridge."

"Then we go now," Okonkwo said, "before the water makes the choice for us."

But Ilsa was looking at the entrance, at the rain-silvered dark beyond it, and thinking about a different choice entirely. "Coral went upriver," she said quietly. "Before the peak. Pell—you saw him go. Toward the culvert."

"To meet Vane," Pell said. "To take the deal or refuse it. He wouldn't say which."

Ilsa unwrapped Marrow's notes from the oilcloth, checked them, rewrapped them, and handed the bundle to Dess. "My father trusted this man with his life, and it killed him. I've spent ten years deciding whether that made Coral a coward or a prisoner." She stood, and took the rope, and coiled it over her shoulder. "Tonight I'm done deciding from a distance."

"You'll drown," Dess said flatly. "The crossing alone will kill you."

"Maybe." Ilsa met her eyes. "But my father died in this tunnel waiting for someone to come back for him. I'm not going to stand in a dry hole and wait for the same thing."

Okonkwo grunted—the closest thing to applause he ever gave—and started tying the rope off to the survey spike. "Somebody has to carry the box up the shaft," he said. "That's me. Dess, you're the best climber. Pell—" he paused, and something almost gentle moved through his exhausted face, "—you keep the lamp lit and count our heads. All of them. However many come back."

Pell nodded, throat working.

Ilsa stepped back into the flood.

The water took her to her waist in the first three strides, then her chest, and the current leaned on her like a crowd trying to push past. She locked both fists on the rope and hauled herself forward one arm-length at a time, out of the tunnel mouth, into the hammering rain, toward the distant shape of the culvert where a lantern still burned—or where she believed it burned, or where she needed to believe it, which on a night like this amounted to the same thing.

Behind her, faint through the roar, she heard Dess shout something that might have been *come back* or might have been a prayer.

Ahead of her, at the edge of the lamplight, a figure rose out of the water against the culvert stones—broad-shouldered, drenched, one arm raised against the storm—and even before she could make out his face, Ilsa knew him, the way you know a face from a photograph you've carried too long.

Coral Crane was standing at the water's edge with Vane's signed confession crumpled unread in his fist, and he was shouting her father's name into the teeth of the flood.
