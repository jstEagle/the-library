## 18. Into the Tunnel

The rain had spent itself down to a thin gray whisper by the time we stood at the tunnel mouth, and somehow that was worse. A storm you can brace against. A storm that pauses is only gathering its breath.

The Kestrel tunnel opened in the hillside like a wound that had never learned to close. Half its arch was above water still—black stone weeping runoff, the old timber framing gone soft as bread—but the lower third had drowned years ago, and the water inside was not the brown of the river outside. It was a deeper color, a held color, the color of something the mountain had swallowed and kept.

I stood on the collapsed loading platform with the rope coiled over my shoulder and let myself look at it. Ten years I'd made a discipline of not looking. Now I looked.

"It's not what you built it up to be," Ilsa said beside me. She had her father's letters wrapped in oilcloth inside her coat, and she kept one hand pressed over them the way a person touches a bruise without meaning to. "Tunnels are just tunnels. It's the stories we pour into them that drown people."

"Marrow would have argued with you," I said. "He used to say the mountain keeps its own minutes. Every collapse, every seep, every crack—written down in stone whether we read it or not."

"Then go read them." She handed me the lamp. Her fingers were steady. Mine were not, and she saw it, and she was kind enough to say nothing about it. "The notes are where he said he'd leave them. Third crosscut, forty paces in, in the drainage niche above the old sump line. He wrote it to my mother like a recipe. Like he expected someone to come bake the bread."

"He expected the company to come burn the bread," I said. "That's why he buried them instead of sending them out."

"Then let's not keep the company waiting."

* * *

We rigged the rope off the stub of a signal mast that still stood at the platform's edge, iron-rusted but honest, and I tested it three times with my full weight and then Dess's, because Dess weighed more than any two of us and if it held Dess it would hold God. Okonkwo had wanted to go in himself. I'd refused, and he'd taken the refusal the way he took most things—silently, with a look that filed it away for a later reckoning I hoped never to face. Pell had wanted to come worst of all, and I'd told him flatly no, and the boy had gone off to the high camp with his jaw set like a man carrying a grudge in each hand.

So it was Ilsa on the rope, and me on the rope, and the mountain in between.

I went down the flooded apron first, into water that came to my chest before I was ten feet inside the arch. The cold went through the oilskins like they were woven from rumor. My lamp threw a small trembling circle on the wet stone ahead, and the rest of the tunnel went up into dark the way a throat goes up into a body.

"Coral." Ilsa's voice came from the entrance, flattened and strange against the stone. "Rope's free. Say something every twenty feet. If you stop talking, I start pulling."

"And if I'm just catching my breath?"

"Then you'll be catching it at a higher altitude."

Almost, I laughed. Almost, I told her the other thing I'd been carrying since the letters—*your father knew, he wrote it down, he begged them in ink and they buried his ink*—but the words wouldn't fit through the cold in my throat. Later. I'd told myself *later* for ten years. But this later had a shape to it now, a door at the end of it, and I waded on.

The water deepened by degrees, then all at once. At thirty paces the floor dropped where a collapse had torn it out, and I was swimming, or something near it—lamp held high, boots dragging, the rope hissing through Ilsa's hands behind me. The tunnel narrowed. The old timber framing closed in overhead, black with a decade of standing water, and where my free hand brushed it the wood came away in soft wet ribbons under my fingers like the skin of something long dead.

"Twenty feet," I said. My voice came back at me doubled, tripletled, a congregation of Corals all saying the same frightened thing.

"Counted," Ilsa said. "Go."

The third crosscut opened on my left, a black slot in the black wall, and I knew it before my lamp found it. Not because I remembered it—I had spent ten years making sure I didn't—but because my body remembered it. My shoulders turned into it the way a shoulder turns into a familiar doorway at home. The water in the crosscut was shallower, waist-deep, then knee-deep, the grade rising, and I slogged up it with the rope dragging behind me like a lifeline to a world that still had weather in it.

The drainage niche was where he'd said it would be.

I'd imagined this moment a hundred ways and in none of them had it been this quiet. I'd imagined the mountain groaning, water rising, some cinematic struggle between me and the dark. Instead there was just a man standing in knee-deep water in a dead tunnel, holding a lamp up to a niche in the rock, and in the niche—wrapped in tar paper, tied with wire, sealed with what was left of a man's stubbornness—was a bundle the size of a hymnal.

Marrow's field notes.

My hands shook so badly I could barely work the wire. I had to set the lamp on a shelf of stone and use both hands, and even then the wire bit my fingers twice before it gave. The tar paper had held. Inside, the notebook was swollen and gray at the edges but whole, the pages fused in places, and I opened it with the care you'd open a letter from the dead, because that is exactly what it was.

His hand. I knew his hand the way you know a voice. *Kestrel, day 12—seepage at chain 340 increasing, water carrying fines, this is not surface runoff, this is the mountain moving its own weight. Recommend immediate shoring or abandonment of the bore. Have reported to Vane. No response. Reported again. No response.*

*Day 19. They've told the crews the readings are instrument error. The instruments are not in error. I am not in error. If anything happens here, it was not an accident.*

I read that line three times in the lamp light with water rising around my knees, and something in my chest that had been clenched for ten years—some fist I'd mistaken for my own heart—loosened by one knuckle. *It was not an accident.* He'd written his own epitaph in advance and they'd buried it with him. They'd let the whole valley believe the engineer had been careless, and they'd let me carry the other half of the lie, and all the while the truth had sat forty paces inside a drowned tunnel, patient as water, waiting for someone to come read it.

"Coral." Ilsa's voice, tighter now. "The river's coming back up. I can see it in the entrance—six inches in the last ten minutes. Get moving."

"Got it." I wrapped the notebook back in the tar paper, then in the oilcloth she'd made me carry for exactly this, and lashed it flat against my chest under my coat. "Coming out."

* * *

The crosscut tried to keep me. That's the honest way to say it. The water that had been knee-deep on the way in was thigh-deep on the way out, and the current had found the tunnel the way current always finds a throat, and by the time I reached the main bore it was shoving at me sideways with real intent. My lamp guttered, drowned, and died, and for four or five seconds I was in the absolute dark, the total dark, the dark I had spent ten years telling myself I had made peace with and never had.

I didn't panic. I want that written down somewhere, even if only here: I didn't panic. I put my hand on the rope and I pulled myself along it hand over hand, and I talked, because I'd promised I would.

"Twenty feet," I said to the dark. "Still here. Still talking."

"Counted," Ilsa's voice came down the tunnel, thin and steady, an anchor dropped from another world. "Keep pulling. The water's rising faster. Don't stop for anything."

"I've got it, Ilsa. I've got his notes."

There was a silence, and then her voice again, and it had changed—roughened at the edges, the way a voice does when a person has been holding something for a long time and finally sets one corner of it down.

"Then bring him out," she said.

I came up the flooded apron into gray daylight with the river already lapping at the platform's foot, and Ilsa's hands were on my collar before my boots found stone, hauling me up the last few feet, and then we were both on the platform, both breathing hard, the rope slack between us and the notebook a hard small weight against my sternum like a second heart.

She didn't ask to see it. She put her palm flat against my chest, against the oilcloth, the way you'd check whether something was still breathing, and left it there for a long moment.

"Third crosscut," she said quietly. "He was thirty-one years old and he wrote instructions to his own grave like a recipe."

"He didn't expect to be in it," I said. "He expected the truth to outlive the company's version of it. He just needed someone to come dig."

"Ten years," she said. "It took ten years for someone to come dig."

"It took ten years for someone to hire the digger," I said, and we both understood what that meant, and neither of us said Vane's name, because the name had gone rotten in all our mouths.

* * *

We were halfway back along the high bank trail when we heard the shot.

One crack, flat and hard, bouncing off the wet hillsides until you couldn't tell its direction—which was the point, I understood later. Ilsa and I both froze, and the notebook seemed to go cold against my chest, and down in the valley the river answered the shot with its own voice, a long rolling growl of water on the move.

Then Dess came crashing up the trail toward us, hatless, soaked to the ribs, moving with the particular controlled urgency of a man carrying news he's already decided how to deliver.

"They took the camp," he said. "Vane's foremen—six of them, maybe seven. Okonkwo and Pell got out the back with the letters and the ledger pages, they're at the culvert, but Coral—" He stopped, got his breath, made himself finish. "They've got the boats. All of them. And Vane's asking for you. By name. He says come down to the water's edge and bring what you found in the tunnel, and everybody walks away."

Ilsa's hand found the oilcloth over my heart again, and this time it wasn't a blessing. It was a grip.

"He knows," she said.

"He's always known," I said. "He just didn't know we'd find it."

Below us, at the gray edge of the rising river, lanterns were being lit in a line, one after another, like a man counting out his winnings. And the water was still coming up.
