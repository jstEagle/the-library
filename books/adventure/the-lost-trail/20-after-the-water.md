## 20. After the Water

Dess woke with mud in her teeth and the taste of iron at the back of her throat, and for a long moment she didn't know where she was—only that she was cold in a way that had gone past feeling and into something like memory. Then the light came. Not sunlight, not yet, but that gray, washed-out promise of it, seeping through the shredded canvas of what had been the mess tent, and she remembered: the river, the night, Okonkwo's arm locked around hers in water that wanted them both, the sound of Vane's foremen shouting over the roar as the camp came apart around them.

She was lying on a stack of dunnage timbers lashed to two barrels, half on and half off, one boot still on her foot and the other somewhere downstream. Her hands were ruined—split knuckles, a nail torn back to the quick on her right ring finger, every finger swollen like sausage casing. She'd hit one of Vane's men with those hands. She remembered that too, with a clarity that surprised her. The man's jaw under her fist. The way the whole fight had happened in silence, more or less, because the river was louder than anything human beings could do to each other.

She sat up. The world tilted, then held.

The camp was gone. That was the first true thing. The mess tent was a rag draped over a broken frame. The cook shack had folded into itself like wet paper. The supply dock they'd built in the first week—a good dock, honest work, Pell had been proud of it—was a scatter of planks caught in the willows forty yards from where it should have been. The river itself had dropped maybe three feet since midnight, but it still ran brown and fast and full of the valley's belongings: fence rails, a door, somebody's trunk with the lid open and empty, turning slowly in an eddy like a man deciding whether to come up.

And there were people standing in the wreckage of it all, looking at each other the way people do after a storm, which is to say not quite believing the others are real.

Okonkwo was at the fire ring, coaxing flame out of soaked kindling with the patience of a man who had done harder things this week than start a fire, which he had. Pell lay wrapped in two blankets near the cook shack ruins, asleep or pretending to be, his face the color of candle wax and his breathing visible—the small hitching rhythm of lungs that had taken in river and hadn't entirely forgiven him for it. Ilsa Reyes sat beside him, upright, her hair drying in salt-stiff ropes, holding a tin cup of something hot that she wasn't drinking. She was watching the tree line.

Dess followed her gaze and understood why.

Down at the mouth of the valley, where the old haul road met the floodplain, there were boats. Three of them, flat-bottomed, riding low, and men in oilskins poling them through the drowned willows with the unhurried competence of people who had been sent rather than volunteers. A lantern still burned on the lead boat, yellow and absurd in the growing daylight.

"Rescuers," Dess said, or meant to say. Her voice came out as gravel.

"Or something," Ilsa said.

* * *

By the time Dess got herself down off the timber pile—one boot, hopping, dignity abandoned somewhere around the second hop—the boats had grounded on the remains of the haul road and the men were wading ashore. There were eight of them. Six wore the canvas coats and brass-buttoned caps of the Mountain Division rescue service, and Dess knew two of them by sight from the supply docks downriver, men who'd hauled their freight all summer and never once asked questions. The other two wore plain dark clothes and carried themselves differently, and one of them carried a leather document case strapped across his chest like a bandolier.

"Which of you is the crew chief?" the tall one called out. His voice carried the flat authority of a man who asked questions for a living and resented having to raise it.

"That depends who's asking," Dess said.

He came closer, boots sucking in the mud, and looked her over—the split hands, the missing boot, the blanket she'd tied around her shoulders like a shawl because her coat was under four feet of new lake somewhere. Whatever he saw seemed to satisfy him. "Inspector Aldous Crane, Territorial Railway Commission." He said it without pride, the way a man says a thing he's said so many times it's worn smooth. "We've been trying to reach this site for six days. The wire went down Tuesday. When we got the last transmission out of your radio operator—" he paused, and something human flickered behind the official mask— "the one about armed men and a flooded tunnel, frankly, we didn't expect anyone to be alive."

"We nearly weren't," Dess said. "Several times. In several orders."

Behind her, Okonkwo had come up quiet as he always did, and Pell had struggled upright inside his blankets, gray-faced and stubborn, and Ilsa stood with her arms crossed and her eyes never leaving the inspector's face. Dess watched her watch him and thought: *she knows why they're really here.* Or guessed it. Or had made it happen. Dess honestly no longer cared which. Six days ago suspicion had been the air she breathed; now it just felt heavy.

"Where is Silas Vane?" Crane asked.

Dess turned and pointed.

Vane was sitting on an overturned freight dolly at the edge of the flooded cutting, thirty yards from anyone, exactly where he'd been sitting when the sky finally went black and the fighting stopped. He'd put his coat back on. He'd even found a comb somewhere, or kept one, and his silver hair was combed flat and neat, and he sat with his ankles crossed and his hands folded in his lap like a man waiting for a train that was late. Two of his foremen sat nearby, farther away than they needed to be, both of them looking anywhere but at him. The third foreman—the big one, the one whose jaw Dess's fist had found in the dark—lay under a tarp by the tool shed, groaning softly, alive, which was more than Dess had been sure of at the time.

"He hasn't run?" Crane said, and there was genuine puzzlement in it.

"Run where?" Dess said. "Look around you, Inspector. Every road out of this valley is at the bottom of the lake. Besides." She heard her own voice go flat and hard, and let it. "Men like Silas Vane don't run. They wait to be found. It looks better in the papers."

* * *

They took him at mid-morning, when the sun finally broke through.

It came suddenly, the way mountain sun does after rain—one moment the clouds were a low gray ceiling bleeding mist, and the next there was a hole in them and light poured through like something spilled, catching the brown water and turning it briefly to hammered bronze. Everyone in the wrecked camp stopped what they were doing and looked up. Even Vane looked up. Dess caught his face in that light and read it plainly for the first time in six days: not defiance, not charm, none of the silken needle-pointed talk he'd used on Coral back at the river camp, none of the *triple pay, boys, triple pay* and the smile like a knife laid flat on a table. What she saw was tiredness. Deep, structural tiredness, the kind that lives in the beams of a house. He looked like a bridge that had finally been asked to carry one ton more than it was ever rated for.

The inspectors crossed the mud toward him. Vane stood up. He straightened his coat. And then—and Dess would remember this until she died—he bowed. Actually bowed, a small precise inclination of the head, to Inspector Crane, as though being arrested were a social engagement he'd arrived early for.

"Silas Vane," Crane said, "you're detained pending inquiry into the death of Tomas Marrow, falsification of safety documentation, conspiracy, and the events of the past six days, which I am given to understand include assault, sabotage, and attempted murder. Do you wish to say anything?"

Vane's eyes came up and traveled across the camp—across the ruined tents, the drowned dock, the crew standing in a loose line among the wreckage like fence posts—and they rested, just for a heartbeat, on Dess. And Dess, who had spent ten years reading men's faces across card tables and freight ledgers and campfires, read what was in his and was almost disappointed by how simple it was. He was measuring her. Adding her up. Deciding, even now, even here, what she might be worth and to whom.

"No statement," Vane said. His voice was level. Almost gentle. "You'll find everything you need in my ledger."

"In your ledger," Crane repeated.

"It's in the mud," Vane said, and smiled—the smile, the real one, thin and white as a scar. "Near the north culvert. Where I dropped it during the flooding. You'll want to dry the pages carefully."

Dess felt the ground shift under her, and not from the water.

Because she knew that ledger. She'd seen it two nights ago, wedged in the false bottom of Vane's document case while he slept—or pretended to sleep—in the survey office, when she'd gone in meaning only to count the payroll money and make sure it was still there. She'd seen the columns in his neat, ruthless hand. She'd seen the entries dated eleven years back, the ones about Kestrel Spur, the ones with Marrow's name in them. And she'd seen, folded between the pages, the original safety report in Coral's own handwriting—the report Coral had always claimed was lost—with words on it that had made her sit down hard on the floor of that office and put her hand over her mouth.

She also knew, with the same terrible clarity, that the ledger Vane had carried was not the only version. She'd seen the second book too, or the ghost of it—the way the ink of certain entries sat slightly wrong, slightly fresh, the way a page can be written yesterday and dated a decade ago if a man has steady hands and no soul. Vane had forged the ledger. Of course he had. The real record was whatever Vane said it was; that had always been the whole architecture of the man.

But now he was telling the inspectors where to find it. Voluntarily. Cheerfully.

*Why?*

The answer arrived in her chest like a dropped stone: *because a ledger in the mud is evidence he can afford. Because whatever's in those pages, he believes it hurts Coral more than him. Because even arrested, even drowned-out, even finished, Silas Vane is still playing his last card.*

"Miss," Crane was saying, and Dess realized he'd been talking to her. "You're the crew chief?"

"I'm the quartermaster," Dess said. "Crew chief's Coral Crane. He's—" She looked around, and felt the small cold drop of it again, the same drop she'd felt at some point in the black hours of the night when the fighting ended and she'd counted heads and come up one short. "He was at the tunnel mouth when the second surge came through. We haven't—" Her voice did something she didn't authorize. She stopped, swallowed, started again. "Ilsa Reyes was on rope with him. Ask her."

Every face turned to Ilsa.

Ilsa set down her untouched cup with great care, as though it were made of glass instead of dented tin, and stood. Mud had dried on her in layers, geological strata of the night. She looked ten years older than she had at the dock, and steadier too, the way things get steadier once they've already broken.

"Coral Crane is alive," she said. "He pulled himself onto the culvert apron before the surge crested. I saw him do it. He's sheltering in the old watchman's hut above the cutting with a busted left arm and a gash on his head, and he refused to come down until the water dropped, because—" her voice caught, just barely, and she mastered it— "because he said someone should stay with the tunnel. With Dad. Until it's over."

Nobody spoke. The river filled the silence, running lower now, running honest.

Pell started crying. He tried to hide it inside his blankets, seventeen years old and half-drowned and tougher than any of them had ever given him credit for, and he failed completely, and Okonkwo put one enormous hand on the back of the boy's neck and left it there, and that was the whole conversation.

* * *

They found the ledger at noon.

Crane insisted on going himself, with two constables and Dess along as witness, because Dess had told him—flatly, in front of everyone—that Vane's books had a habit of improving themselves between finding and filing, and that if anyone was going to stand over that mud and watch pages get counted, it ought to be someone Vane couldn't buy. Crane had looked at her for a long moment. "You have reason to doubt the integrity of Mr. Vane's records," he'd said, not quite a question.

"I have eleven years of reason," Dess said. "Ask me again when we're dry."

The north culvert was a half-mile's walk through wreckage. The flood had left its signature everywhere—mud laid down in smooth terraces like the rings of a tree, willows bent flat and pointing downstream, a section of rail torn from the spur and wrapped around a boulder in a curve that hurt to look at, metal persuaded into a shape metal should never take. Dess walked it in one boot and a borrowed rubber overshoe one of the constables had produced from a boat locker, and she catalogued the damage the way she catalogued everything, automatically, in ledgers of her own: *trestle four, gone. Culvert wing wall, cracked through. Spur bed, scoured to ballast for two hundred yards.* Numbers were how she prayed. She'd never told anyone that.

The ledger was where Vane said it would be, jammed in the grate of the culvert, wrapped in oilcloth because of course it was—dropped, rescued, protected, staged. Crane knelt in the mud and unwrapped it with gloved hands while Dess stood over him and the constables stood over both of them, and the sun came and went behind the clouds like a man checking in.

The pages were warped but legible. Crane turned them slowly, and Dess watched his face change as he read—not the dramatic changes, nothing a painter could use, but the small ones, the tightening at the eyes, the way his thumb began pressing harder on each page as if to hold it still. Near the end he stopped altogether.

"This entry," he said quietly. "Dated the ninth of March, eleven years ago. It states that engineer C. Crane altered the tunnel inspection figures and filed a passing safety report over the objection of foreman T. Marrow, and that Marrow's subsequent death resulted from conditions Crane knowingly certified as sound."

"Yes," Dess said.

"And you're telling me this is false."

"I'm telling you that entry was written in ink that isn't eleven years old," Dess said. "I'm telling you I've seen Vane's actual hand at bookkeeping, and this page is it, and the dates are theater. And I'm telling you that the man who actually filed the original report is lying in a hut above this cutting with a broken arm, and that Tomas Marrow's own field notes—his real reports, the ones saying the tunnel was unstable, the ones Vane buried so the schedule wouldn't slip—are in that same hut right now, in a waterproof roll, and have been for eleven years." She heard her voice shaking and discovered she didn't care. "Your ledger says my friend killed a man. The truth is that your commission's own schedules killed him, and Silas Vane signed the order to keep them, and then spent a decade building a story with my friend's name at the bottom of it. Read the field notes. Read them against this. Paper doesn't lie, Inspector. Men do. That's the entire trade."

Crane closed the ledger. He looked at it for a while, this small mud-stained object that had cost so much, and then he looked up at Dess, and whatever was in his face, it wasn't the commission anymore. It was just a tired man in a beautiful, terrible morning.

"You'll swear to all of that," he said. "In writing. Today."

"I'll write it twice," Dess said. "Once for you and once for me. I've learned to keep copies."

* * *

They went up to the watchman's hut in the afternoon, all of them, because nobody could stand not to.

The path was a stream bed now, and they climbed it in single file, Pell leaning on Okonkwo, Dess hopping her bad-footed way over stones, Ilsa ahead of everyone, climbing like the hill owed her something. The hut sat on its shelf of rock above the cutting, small and square-roofed, and Coral Crane was sitting outside it on an upturned crate with his left arm bound in a sling made from a shirt sleeve, his head wrapped, his face bruised green and yellow along one side, watching the water with the expression of a man who has been having a very long conversation with it.

He heard them coming—of course he did; he'd have heard them from the moon—and he turned, and when he saw them all, the whole ragged line of them, something went out of his shoulders that had been in them for eleven years. Dess saw it leave. It was like watching a man set down a load so heavy he'd forgotten he was carrying it.

"Well," Coral said. His voice was rough as a rasp. "You look terrible."

"Says the man wearing his own shirt as a bandage," Dess said, and then her throat closed and she couldn't say anything else, so she didn't try. She just walked over and sat down on the mud beside his crate, in the wet, in the one-booted undignified state she'd long since stopped minding, and leaned her shoulder against his knee, and he put his good hand on her head, the way he'd done the first week she'd joined his crew, ten years and a lifetime ago, when she'd been a runaway bookkeeper with a stolen abacus and nowhere else to go.

"They took Vane," she said.

"I know. I watched the boats come in."

"The ledger's recovered. The forged one. But Crane has your word against it now, and Marrow's notes, and Ilsa's testimony, and mine." She looked up at him. His eyes were the same as ever—gray, patient, worn smooth as river stone—but there was something behind them that hadn't been there before, something that had a name, and the name was *relief*, and it looked almost like pain on a man who'd had no practice with it.

"Dess," Coral said quietly. "There's something I have to tell you. All of you. The whole of it, tonight, by the fire, every part I've never said. I'm done keeping the books closed."

Dess nodded. She found she wasn't afraid of it, whatever it was. Eleven years of wondering had been heavier than any truth could be.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"You tell it plain. No editing yourself down to protect us. We're not glass, Coral. We never were."

Something moved across his face—gratitude, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes. Below them the river ran on, three feet lower than last night and falling, uncovering the valley inch by inch, the way truth uncovers everything eventually, given enough pressure and enough time.

Down at the waterline, Ilsa Reyes stood alone at the edge of the new lake, holding a bundle of oilcloth-wrapped letters against her chest, looking out at the place where the tunnel mouth had been. And Dess, watching her, saw her suddenly go still—completely still, the stillness of a person who has spotted something in the water that should not be there—and raise one hand, and point.

"What is it?" Dess called. "What do you see?"

Ilsa didn't answer. She was already wading in.
