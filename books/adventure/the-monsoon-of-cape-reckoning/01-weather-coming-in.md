## 1. Weather Coming In

The barometer at Cape Reckoning had been falling for three days, which everyone in town knew meant one of two things: either the monsoon was finally arriving to make good on its threats, or old Petter Lindqvist's knee was acting up again and the weather was simply following his example.

Freya Hartwell chose to believe it was the monsoon. She'd learned a long time ago that the sky over the Cape didn't do anything out of politeness to anyone's joints.

She was loading the Cessna before seven in the morning, working by the yellow glow of the hangar's single floodlight because dawn hadn't bothered to show up yet. The light came through the cloud deck the color of weak tea, and the air smelled the way it always did this time of year—salt, diesel, wet cedar, and the green rot-smell of the muskeg inland that said *rain, rain, rain* in a language older than any of the towns along the coast.

"Insulin goes on top," Tomas called from under the wing, where he was doing something to the landing gear with a wrench and an expression of deep personal grievance. "Mrs. Okonkwo will know if you put the insulin under the mail again."

"Mrs. Okonkwo always knows," Freya said. "That woman has radar in her dentures."

"I'm serious. Last time she made me stand in her kitchen for forty minutes while she explained thermal stratification to me. I don't even know what that is."

"It's why your beer gets warm at the bottom of the cooler, Tomás."

He emerged from under the wing looking betrayed by physics itself. "See, now I have to think about that all day."

Tomas Reyes had been her mechanic for six years and her friend for longer than either of them could precisely date, mostly because their friendship had begun with him telling her—correctly—that her left magneto was about to quit, and her telling him—incorrectly—that he didn't know her plane better than she did. The magneto had quit over Blackfish Narrows. She'd limped home on one cylinder, landed, walked straight into his shop, and said, "What else do you know?" He'd been answering ever since.

Now he wiped his hands on a rag that had achieved a state of oiliness beyond what seemed chemically possible and looked up at the sky with the squint of a man reading bad news.

"You're going inland today?"

"One run. Kettle River, Moose Crossing, maybe as far as Tla'amin if the ceiling holds." She patted the fuselage of the Cessna, whose name—*Wren*, painted on the nose in her grandfather's handwriting, repainted twice since—was flaking again. "Medicine, mail, a crate of batteries for the school, and whatever gossip I can carry back."

"Gossip flies free," Tomas said. "It's the only cargo that doesn't count against your weight limit."

"That's because everybody ships it."

The airstrip at Cape Reckoning was a thousand meters of gravel laid across a shelf of land between the town and the sea, and it was, in Freya's professional opinion, the finest airstrip on the entire coast, which was a bit like being the tallest building in a village of cabins. It had been cut into the headland by her grandfather's generation, back when the government still remembered the Cape existed, and every year the salal crept a little closer to the edges like it was trying to take the strip back. Every year Freya flew low over the approaches and dropped seed bombs of nothing in particular just to keep the brush honest.

The town itself sat below the strip in a crescent around the harbor: two hundred and some souls, a cannery that hadn't canned anything in twenty years but still employed nine people fixing boats, a general store, a school, a clinic with one doctor who doubled as the dentist on Thursdays, and a church that doubled as everything else. There was no road in or out. There had never been a road in or out. The ocean took care of the heavy freight in summer, and Freya took care of everything else, twelve months a year, in a plane older than she was.

People sometimes asked her—it happened maybe once a season, usually a tourist off one of the few passing yachts—whether it wasn't lonely, living somewhere the map barely bothered to name. Freya always said no. What she never managed to explain was that the Cape wasn't isolated so much as *deliberate*. The town sat where it sat, small and quiet and easy to fly past, and there were families on this coast who had chosen that invisibility the way other people chose a house: carefully, and intending to stay.

Her grandfather had been one of them. But that was a thought for another day, and today the sky was already grumbling about it.

* * *

Kettle River was ninety minutes inland, a clearing beside a river the color of steeped spruce, where eleven people lived at the end of a trapline and the nurse—a wiry, cheerful Métis woman named Dot—kept the closest thing the interior had to a medical station, which was a converted fishing cabin with a satellite phone and a very firm opinion about proper dosages.

Dot met the plane on the gravel bar in rubber boots and a rain jacket that had given up its waterproofing sometime during the previous administration.

"Tell me you brought the insulin," she said, before hello.

"On top. Under the mail would be malpractice."

"Under the mail would be Tuesday." Dot hauled the crate out and checked it anyway, because trust was one thing and refrigeration was another. "How's the barometer doing down at the Cape?"

"Falling like my grandfather's opinion of the ferry service."

"So it's coming." Dot looked north, where the mountains had spent the morning pretending to be elsewhere. The cloud had come down to meet them, low and soft and gray, pressing on the treetops like a lid. "Third week of October. Right on schedule. You flying back tonight?"

"Depends on the ceiling. Depends on the gossip."

"Oh, I've got gossip." Dot lowered her voice with the ceremony of a woman opening a vault. "The government people. The survey crew."

"What about them?"

"They went quiet."

Freya zipped the med crate closed. "Quiet how? They're surveyors. Quiet is the job description. They go sit in the bush and measure things nobody needs measured, and then they come out and file reports nobody reads."

"This is different." Dot handed her a thermos of coffee that smelled like it could strip paint, which was Dot's love language. "Ranger station hasn't heard from them in nine days. Nine days, Freya. Those people check in like clockwork. My cousin's husband does their resupply runs out of Port Hardy, and he says the office went strange about it. Not worried-strange. *Careful*-strange."

"Careful-strange," Freya repeated. "What does careful-strange look like?"

"Like they stopped asking people to help look and started asking people not to talk about it." Dot shrugged, but her eyes stayed on the treeline a beat too long. "Could be nothing. Government's got a hundred reasons to be quiet that aren't bodies in the muskeg."

"Sure."

"But you'll keep your ears open down at the Cape?"

"Tomás says gossip flies free."

"And lands everywhere," Dot said. "Go on, get out of here before the ceiling drops. And tell Mrs. Okonkwo if she doesn't take the full course this time, I'm prescribing her a lecture."

"She'll love that. She collects lectures."

* * *

Moose Crossing was a fuel stop and a wave. Tla'amin was a mudfield, a schoolhouse, thirty pounds of batteries, and a class of children who treated Freya's arrival as the day's main entertainment, which she encouraged shamelessly by doing a wing-waggle on approach that would have gotten her a talking-to from any examiner alive.

By the time she turned south for home, the ceiling had come down to eight hundred feet and the wind had found its voice out of the southeast, moaning through the passes with that long, hollow note that pilots on this coast called the monsoon tuning up. Freya flew the river valleys home, low and slow, the way her grandfather had taught her mother and her mother had taught her: *the water knows the way, girl. When the sky forgets, follow the water.*

The Wren bucked and swayed in the gusts but held her line, faithful as a dog. Freya talked to her the way she always did—"I know, I know, we're almost there"—and let the rhythm of the work settle something in her chest that had been restless since Dot's thermos.

Restless. That was the word. She'd felt it for days without naming it, a fine vibration under the competent calm, like a wire humming before you can hear the note. The season did that to her sometimes—the closing-in, the darkening, the sense of the whole coast pulling its collar up against the winter. But this felt different. This felt less like weather and more like a held breath.

She was still holding it when she walked into the Rusty Anchor at six o'clock, shaking the rain out of her hair.

* * *

The Anchor was the closest thing Cape Reckoning had to a parliament. It was warm, dim, smelled of fryer oil and spruce tips, and on a Monday night in late October it held perhaps fifteen people, all of whom turned to look when Freya came in, because a pilot walking into a pub is news the way a letter is news: you want to know if it's for you.

"Pint of the dark," she told Maggie Osei behind the bar, and then, louder, to the room: "Batteries delivered. Insulin delivered. Mrs. Okonkwo sends greetings and a complaint about the price of ginger."

A ripple of comfortable noise went around the room. This was the ritual. This was the pulse of the place—Freya as the circulating blood of a body too spread out to have veins. Somebody asked after the Kettle River kids; somebody asked whether the Tla'amin road—there was no road, but the question was traditional—had washed out again; somebody asked if she'd seen whales in the Narrows, and she said three, and someone else said they'd seen five, and the whale-counting dispute resumed where it had apparently left off, which was years ago.

Freya took her pint to the corner table by the window, where the glass ran with condensation and the harbor lights smeared sideways in the rain, and let herself just sit for a moment. The Wren was tied down and double-roped. The barometer was still falling. Tomorrow would be a weather day, probably, and the day after, and the town would close in around its stoves and wait out the monsoon the way it had waited out a hundred of them.

She was halfway through the pint when the conversation at the next table shifted key.

There were four men there, loggers mostly, playing cards, and they'd been talking about hockey. Then one of them—Silas Brandt, who worked the barge dock and knew everything that floated past the Cape, which was everything—dropped his voice, and dropped it badly, because Silas had never in his life dropped his voice below the volume of a marine radio.

"—that's what I'm telling you. Survey crew. Six of them, out of Victoria. Been in the bush since August, mapping the coastline up past the point."

"Mapping what?" said one of the others. "There's nothing up there but rock and trees and my ex-wife's family."

"Government doesn't pay six salaries to map trees." Silas leaned in. The whole table leaned with him, and so, quietly, did Freya—turned slightly toward the window, pint raised, ears wide open. "Ranger station says they missed their last two check-ins. Two. And here's the part." He paused, savoring it, a man who understood that information was like fish: best served fresh, and best when everyone could see you had it. "There's a boat coming. A company boat. Salvage outfit, out of the south. Due in the next week or two, weather permitting. Asking questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"About the survey team." Silas dealt the cards with great significance. "And about flights. Old flights. Out of this strip."

The table went quiet in the way tables do when a conversation has grown teeth. Freya kept her eyes on the rain running down the window and drank her pint and did not turn around, and the effort of not turning around was enormous.

Old flights. Out of this strip.

There was exactly one family name attached to old flights out of this strip, and she was sitting in it.

"Hartwell," said one of the card players, and Freya's heart performed a maneuver she'd have failed her on in any checkride. "They mean old man Hartwell. Freya's grandfather."

"Maybe," said Silas, noncommittal in the way of a man who was not noncommittal at all. "Maybe they mean lots of people. Lots of people flew out of here, back in the day."

Nobody laughed, because it wasn't true and everyone knew it wasn't true. Back in the day, most of the coast couldn't afford wings. Her grandfather had been the one who had them.

Freya finished her pint, set money on the table, and stood. The card game glanced up at her with the guilty synchrony of men who had just been talking about her family, and she gave them her best easy grin—the one that said *competent, calm, nothing rattles me*—and said, "Silas, if a salvage boat comes asking about my grandfather, you send them to me first. I charge for stories."

"Double rates," Silas said, grinning back, relieved. "It's the only cargo that counts against the weight limit."

She laughed with them, and walked out into the rain, and the laugh fell off her face somewhere between the door and the first streetlamp.

* * *

The walk home took her past the harbor, where the fishing boats knocked gently against their moorings like horses shifting in a stable, and past the old Hartwell house on the rise above the water—her house now, though she still thought of it as his, with its sagging porch and its view of the whole gray sweep of the strait. The wind had come around harder while she'd been in the pub, and the rain came at her sideways, needling, insistent.

She stood on the porch a long moment, dripping, looking out at the black water.

Nine days quiet. A salvage boat asking about old flights. Careful-strange at the ranger station. And underneath it all, that hum in her chest, that wire waiting for its note.

Her grandfather had died eight years ago, in this house, in the big chair by the window, with the tide going out. He'd left her the plane, the strip, the house, and forty years of logbooks in a cedar chest—flights recorded in a hand so neat it looked typeset, right up until the places where it wasn't. Where pages were missing. Where coordinates had been smudged, deliberately, with a thumb, by a man who flew precision approaches in fog you wouldn't drive in.

She'd never asked why. He'd been gone by the time she'd thought to ask, and the silence had seemed like part of the inheritance, the way the sagging porch was. You didn't interrogate a gift.

But gifts, she thought now, watching lightning walk silently along the far horizon over the open water, could be heavier than they looked.

The monsoon arrived properly around midnight, arriving the way it always did—not gradually but all at once, as though it had been waiting offshore for permission. Rain hammered the roof. Wind found every loose shingle and played them. Freya lay awake in the dark listening to it, and beneath the weather she could hear the deeper sound of the sea working against the point, the tide turning, patient as arithmetic.

Six salaries. Two missed check-ins. A boat full of questions heading for a town that had survived a hundred years by being easy to overlook.

She got up, crossed the cold floorboards to the cedar chest in the corner, and rested her hand flat on its lid. She didn't open it. Not yet. But she left her hand there a while, the way you'd rest a hand on the shoulder of someone sleeping, someone you had questions for.

Outside, the rain kept falling, and the tide kept counting.
