## 2. The Contract

The rain came sideways that morning, the way it always did in October at Cape Reckoning, as if the weather had somewhere better to be and resented the detour. Freya Hartwell stood under the corrugated awning of her hangar with a mug of coffee going cold in one hand and a wrench she had no immediate use for in the other, watching the water sheet off the roof and thinking about carburetors.

The Cessna—*Kestrel*, because her grandfather had named every plane he ever flew after a bird that could hover in bad wind, and some traditions were load-bearing—sat inside with her panels half-open like a patient mid-examination. Tomas Reyes was elbow-deep in her engine cowling, which was technically his job but which he performed, as always, with the theatrical suffering of a man asked to perform surgery on a relative.

"She's telling you something," Tomas said without looking up.

"She's telling *you* something. I just fly her."

"No, no. Listen." He tilted his head toward the engine with the gravity of a priest interpreting omens. "Number three cylinder's got an opinion about the fuel mixture. A *strong* opinion."

"Everyone on this coast has strong opinions about fuel mixture."

"Yes, but hers are expensive." He emerged from the cowling wiping his hands on a rag that had been gray since sometime last year, and gave her the look—the one that meant he'd been building up to saying something and was now going to say it whether or not she wanted to hear it. "Also. You heard about the survey team?"

Freya took a sip of cold coffee to buy time. "Ruthie mentioned it at the co-op. Something about them missing their check-in."

"Not missing. *Quiet.* There's a difference. Missing is when somebody doesn't come home. Quiet is when somebody stops answering and nobody official wants to say why." He tossed the rag onto the workbench. "Ruthie says the liaison flying in tomorrow. Government man. Wants to talk to you specifically."

"To me specifically," Freya repeated. "That's either very flattering or very ominous."

"With government men it's usually both."

She laughed, but the laugh didn't sit right in her chest, the way a seatbelt sometimes didn't sit right until you'd flown through it a few times. The survey team had been in the interior for three weeks—four people, hydrology and heritage assessment, whatever that meant, camped somewhere up past the Kettle Lakes doing whatever such people did. She'd flown over their grid twice by accident, seen the orange tents stitched into the green like punctuation marks, thought nothing of it. Now the punctuation marks had stopped appearing on schedule, and the whole coast had gone slightly stiff about it, the way a room goes stiff when someone mentions money.

The rain kept coming sideways. Somewhere out over the strait, the monsoon was assembling itself like a slow argument.

* * *

The government man arrived the next afternoon on the mail flight, which meant he arrived wet, grumpy, and holding his hat, in that order. He was younger than Freya expected—mid-thirties, city shoes already ruined, a laminated folder clutched against his chest like a life vest. He introduced himself as Mr. Okafor, Regional Liaison Office, and shook her hand with the firmness of someone who'd read that firm handshakes built trust.

"Ms. Hartwell. Thank you for seeing me."

"You caught me between oil changes. Sit down before the awning leaks on you." She kicked a crate toward him. Her office was the corner of the hangar with a desk, two chairs, a wall calendar featuring lighthouses, and a coffee urn that predated her by decades and possibly by aviation itself.

Okafor sat, surveyed the hangar—the tools, the fuel drums, the Kestrel with her cowlings open—and visibly recalibrated whatever expectations he'd flown in with. "You're the only charter operator on this stretch of coast."

"I'm the only anything on this stretch of coast. Doctor visits, mail, engine parts, the occasional wedding cake. If it fits in a Cessna, I've carried it. Once I carried a goat. Don't ask."

"A goat."

"He had opinions about the altimeter. What can I do for you, Mr. Okafor?"

He opened the laminated folder. Inside were maps, and Freya's eyes went to them the way they went to all maps—hungrily, automatically, cataloguing coastlines before she could stop herself. "The survey team at Kettle Lakes missed three scheduled check-ins. We're mounting a search. Aerial first, ground teams later if weather permits." He slid a chart across the desk. "We want to contract you to fly a grid pattern. Systematic. Here—" his finger traced a series of parallel lines east of the lakes, "—through here, here, and here. Daily flights, weather permitting, for as long as it takes. Double your standard charter rate."

Double. Freya kept her face pleasant and did arithmetic behind it. Double rates through monsoon season would cover the Kestrel's overhaul, the hangar roof, and roughly a year of not lying awake wondering how the books would balance. It was, objectively, easy money.

"And the catch?" she said.

Okafor blinked. "There's no catch."

"There's always a catch. When there's no catch, that's the catch." She tapped the map. "This grid. Why these lines? If I were hiding four people in that country, I wouldn't hide them in neat rows."

"The grid is based on their planned survey routes."

"Mm." She looked at the map properly now, and something at the edge of it snagged her attention—a hatched band along the western margin, running down to the coast below the point. Restricted airspace overlay, the kind that showed up on charts for military zones and watershed closures. Except she'd been flying this coast since she was nineteen, and she knew every closure on it the way she knew the freckles on her own hands, and this one wasn't real. Or rather: it hadn't existed until recently. "And this?"

"Temporary restriction. Survey equipment in the area. Sensitive instruments." He said it smoothly, but not smoothly enough; there was a half-second hitch, like a needle skipping a groove. "You'd want to stay well clear of it anyway. Weather off the point is treacherous this time of year."

"Mr. Okafor." She leaned back in her chair. "I have landed on that beach in weather that made grown fishermen pray to gods they didn't believe in. I know exactly what the weather off the point does. What I don't know is why a hydrology team's missing check-ins require a restricted zone the size of a parish."

For a moment—just a moment—he looked tired. Genuinely tired, the way people look when they've been given a script and told to read it and haven't quite convinced themselves. "Ms. Hartwell, I'll be honest with you within the limits of what I'm authorized to be honest about. There are aspects of this situation that are above my pay grade. The search area is the search area. Fly the grid, log what you see, report to me daily. You'll be paid double rates and thanked in writing." He stood, offered the handshake again. "Can I count on you?"

Freya looked at the map. At the false restriction hugging the coastline below the point—at, though she couldn't have said why yet, the exact stretch of shore her grandfather's old photographs had loved best. At the space where four people might or might not be waiting for someone to come find them.

"Give me until morning," she said.

* * *

Tomas was waiting for her at the Rusty Anchor that evening, occupying his usual stool at the end of the bar with the posture of a man who had been rehearsing an argument and intended to win it. The Anchor was half-full—fishermen mostly, playing cards, arguing about the storm front, pretending not to listen. Ruthie Okonkwo was behind the bar, and she poured Freya's cider before she'd fully sat down, which in Ruthie's language meant *you look like you need this and also I already know everything.*

"So," Tomas said.

"So."

"The government man. Double rates."

"Word travels fast."

"Ruthie told me. Ruthie tells everyone. It's basically infrastructure." He turned on his stool to face her fully, and Freya recognized the expression: it was the one he wore when a customer insisted a part wasn't needed and he knew, with the certainty of scripture, that it was. "Don't take it."

"Tomas—"

"Hear me out. One: double rates means they think it's dangerous, and things that are dangerous pay double because most people with sense refuse. Two: that restricted zone isn't real. I checked the NOTAMs. Nothing filed. Which means whoever drew it on that map wants you looking at the middle of nowhere while staying away from somewhere specific. Three—" he held up a third finger, then seemed to lose track of where he was going with it, "—three, I don't like it."

"That's not a reason, that's a mood."

"My moods have an excellent service record. Ask anyone." He signaled Ruthie for another round. "Freya. Four people are missing out there. Actual people, with families checking phones that aren't ringing. And the government's response is to hire one bush pilot, draw fake boundaries around the answer, and hope nobody asks questions? That's not a search. That's a performance of a search."

Freya wrapped both hands around her cider. Through the Anchor's salt-fogged window she could see the harbor lights smearing in the rain, and beyond them, invisible in the dark, the point, and beyond the point, the interior going green and silent toward the Kettle Lakes. Four orange tents. Three missed check-ins.

"If I refuse," she said slowly, "they'll fly someone in from Port Callum. Some contract outfit that doesn't know the country, that'll burn fuel circling the sanctioned grid and call it diligence. And if those four are alive out there—if they're hurt, or lost, or hiding from something—" She stopped. Took a breath. "I can't be the reason nobody came."

"Or," Tomas said, "you take the contract, fly exactly where they tell you, see exactly what they let you see, and spend the rest of the season wondering what was behind the line you agreed not to cross." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "I know you, Freya Hartwell. You're already planning how to 'accidentally' drift west of the grid."

"I am not."

"You're doing it right now. Your left eyebrow does a thing."

Freya touched her eyebrow, betrayed by her own face. Ruthie, passing with a tray, snorted audibly.

"Look," Tomas said, quieter now. "Your grandfather flew this coast for forty years. You ever wonder why he never talked about half of it? Some places out there, people keep quiet for reasons. Good ones and bad ones, but reasons. This job smells like one of the bad ones wearing a good one's coat."

Freya looked into her cider. The truth—which she would not say aloud, not here, not yet—was that the money mattered less than the itch. The itch that had started the moment Okafor's finger traced that false boundary along the coast below the point. Because her grandfather's logbooks were still in the footlocker under her bed, and she had read them so many times she could recite chunks, and there was a flight in there—unfiled, undated properly, coordinates smudged by a thumb that had pressed too hard—that ran along that same stretch of shoreline. Coincidence, probably. Coastlines repeated themselves. Geography was full of echoes.

Probably.

"I'll take the contract," she said.

Tomas closed his eyes briefly, the way he did when a customer insisted on the cheap part. "Of course you will."

"But I'll fly the grid. Exactly the grid. By the book, logged clean, nothing clever." She met his eyes. "And if I see something outside the lines—something that looks like people, or smoke, or a signal mirror—I'm reporting it. To whoever needs hearing it. Contract or no contract."

"That's all I ask." He raised his glass. "To boring flights."

"To boring flights," she echoed, and they drank, and outside the rain kept falling on Cape Reckoning, steady and patient, the way it fell on everything the wilderness had decided to keep.

It was only walking home afterward, hood up, boots splashing through the flooded ruts of Harbor Road, that she admitted to herself what the itch really was. Not the money. Not even the missing team, though God, she hoped they were alive.

It was the smudged coordinates. Her grandfather's thumb, pressing too hard, pressing deliberately, erasing something he'd decided no one else should ever see.

Tomorrow she'd sign the papers. Tomorrow she'd fly the lines they drew for her.

But tonight, before bed, she was going to open the footlocker.
