## 3. First Grid

The Cessna lifted off the strip at Cape Reckoning at 6:14 in the morning, which by Freya Hartwell's reckoning was a full hour later than she'd wanted and exactly on time for the government.

She'd spent the extra hour arguing with Tomas about the fuel load.

"You're carrying too much," he said, standing under the wing with a coffee thermos like it was a weapon he hadn't decided to use yet. "Weight is range. Range is margin. You're flying a search grid over drowned country in monsoon season, and you want to lug around an extra forty gallons of *just in case*."

"That's what just-in-case is for."

"Just-in-case is for people who don't check their weather twice." He tapped the fuselage. "You checked it twice. It's ugly but it's honest. Fly light, fly home."

"Tomas." She looked at him over the doorframe, one foot already on the strut. "The last time I flew light over the Saddle, I came back on fumes and prayer, and you lectured me for twenty minutes about how prayer is not a certified engine component."

"And I stand by that. Prayer has terrible maintenance records." He handed her the thermos anyway, because that was the shape of their friendship—arguing while giving her everything she needed. "Grid starts at the river mouth?"

"River mouth, north leg first, eight-mile spacing. In and out of the pattern every ninety minutes to report."

"Report to whom? The liaison gave you a radio frequency that doesn't exist on any chart I've ever seen."

"He said it exists."

"He also said double rates, which is the sound money makes when it wants something from you." Tomas stepped back and raised his hand in a lazy salute. "Fly the grid. Come home when the sky tells you to, not when the frequency does."

The sky told her things almost immediately.

The monsoon wasn't due for another week—every almanac, every old-timer, every ache in Maren Oduya's knees agreed on that—but the weather had started rehearsing early. Cloud sat on the interior in long gray shelves, stacked up against the mountains like furniture pushed against a door. Between the shelves were corridors of clean air, and flying a search grid meant threading them, climbing when they closed, dropping when they opened, reading the country the way her grandfather had taught her: not as terrain, but as text.

*Water lies,* he used to say, tapping the window of the old de Havilland. *Rain lies. But the land only ever tells the truth, and it tells it slowly.*

She flew the north leg first, as briefed.

The grid was simple enough on paper: parallel runs at eight-mile spacing, eyes on the forest canopy and the riverbraids, looking for anything man-made that shouldn't be there—a tarp, a signal fire, a helipad hacked into a clearing, a flare. The survey team had been quiet for eleven days. Eleven days in that country was either nothing or everything. People went quiet all the time out here; radios failed, plans changed, pride kept men from admitting they were lost until they weren't lost anymore, they were just dead. But eleven days, with a government contract behind them and satellite check-ins scheduled—and then silence, total, like a held breath.

Freya flew the line and looked down at the green and saw nothing.

That was the first wrong thing. Not that she found no sign of them—that she found no sign of *anything*. No deer trails broken open, no birds mobbing a clearing, no fisherman's skiff pulled up on a bank where no skiff had business being. The interior had gone polite. Curated. Like a room someone had tidied before guests arrived.

She keyed the radio at the end of the second leg and reported, as instructed, on the frequency that existed according to exactly one person.

"Grid One, north legs complete. Negative contact. Weather building along the western wall, visibility holding at six miles, deteriorating."

"Copy, Grid One," said the voice, flat and patient, a voice like a filing cabinet. "Continue south legs. Note any anomalous activity."

*Anomalous activity.* She'd turned the phrase over all night. Anomalous activity could mean a lot of things. It could mean the survey team. It could mean poachers. It could mean, though she'd tried not to let the thought take shape, an old Hartwell flight path that somebody somewhere still remembered.

"Define anomalous," she'd asked the liaison, Delacroix, in his rented office that smelled of paper and air freshener fighting a losing war against mildew.

"Anything outside normal patterns," he'd said, smiling the smile of a man who had never once been surprised and intended to keep his record intact.

"The whole coast is outside normal patterns. That's why you hired me."

"Which is why we pay you double," he'd said, and slid the folder across the desk, and the conversation had ended the way conversations with him always ended—with her holding more paper and less certainty than before.

So she flew the south legs, and the rain found her there.

It came in off the sea in a gray curtain, soft at first, the kind of rain that seemed apologetic, and then it stopped apologizing. Visibility dropped to three miles, then two. The riverbraids below her turned to pewter and vanished. She dropped to eight hundred feet, then six, flying by memory and the ghost-logic of a coastline she'd been reading since she was small enough to need a booster cushion made from a life vest and stubbornness.

And through all of it—the rain, the rhythm, the drone of the engine doing its honest work—she kept noticing the edges of the grid.

That was the thing nobody told you about flying a search pattern: the lines on the map became walls in your head. You flew your lane, you scanned your lane, and the space beyond the lane softened into irrelevance, the way a hallway disappears once you're in the room you were heading toward. She'd caught herself doing it twice already, easing her scan back from the boundary like a dog respecting an invisible fence.

On the fourth south leg, at the eastern edge of the sanctioned grid, where the map said STOP and her instructions said REPORT ANYTHING BEYOND THIS LINE IMMEDIATELY, she saw the glint.

It lasted maybe two seconds.

A flash of light in the drowned country east of the Saddle, where the rivers braided into a hundred silver threads and lost themselves in tidal marsh. Not sunlight on water—she knew that signature the way she knew her own heartbeat; water glitters in sheets, in long shivering bands. This was a single point. Hard. Brief. Metallic. There, then swallowed by cloud-shadow, then there again as she banked—only slightly, only a few degrees, just enough to bring it into the corner of her windshield—and then gone entirely as the rain thickened between her and it.

Her hand was already moving on the yoke before her mind finished the argument.

She leveled out. Sat in the noise of the engine and the drum of rain on the cowling and did arithmetic.

East of the line. Two, maybe three miles east. Inside the drowned country. Nowhere near anything the survey team should have been—Delacroix's own briefing had put their last known position northwest, upriver, a full day's hike from where that glint lived.

She keyed the radio.

For a long moment she didn't say anything. The frequency hissed, patient, empty. And Freya Hartwell, who had never in her life filed a false report, who had landed in a lightning storm rather than tell a dispatcher she couldn't make it in, who considered the truth a tool you maintained better than your engine—Freya heard herself deciding not to speak.

Not lying. Not yet. Just... waiting. Holding the observation in her cupped hands like water from a stream, looking at it before drinking.

"Grid One," she said finally. "South legs complete. Negative contact. Weather's closing the western corridor—I'm coming home."

"Copy, Grid One," said the filing-cabinet voice. "Resume tomorrow, zero-six-hundred. Note any anomalous activity."

"Nothing anomalous," she said, and heard how easily it came out, and hated how easily it came out, and flew home through the rain with the glint burning in her memory brighter than any beacon.

* * *

Tomas met her on the strip with the wind sock snapping overhead and the first real gusts of the front pushing at his back. He took the tie-down ropes without being asked, which was his way of saying welcome home, and squinted up at her as she climbed down.

"You look like a woman who saw something."

"I look like a woman who got rained on."

"You've been rained on since birth. This is different. This is the face you made when you were nine and broke the church window and decided, en route to confession, that God would understand." He looped the rope around the post. "What did you see?"

She should have told him. That was the whole architecture of their friendship—he saw the planes' insides, she saw the country's outsides, and between them they kept each other honest. Instead she heard herself say:

"A bird catching the sun."

"At altitude? In rain? Facing away from the sun?" He straightened up slowly. "Freya."

"It was probably a bird."

"It was probably not a bird." He wiped his hands on a rag that had achieved a texture somewhere between leather and regret. "You logged it privately. Don't bother denying it—I watched you write in the kneeboard book instead of the flight log, and you have the guilt posture of a Lutheran."

"I don't know what I saw. If I log a contact east of the grid, Delacroix sends people east of the grid. Armed people, maybe. Or worse, careful people, the kind who file paperwork." She pulled the kneeboard book from her jacket—waterproof, oilcloth-bound, older than her career—and held it without opening it. "If I log nothing, and there are people alive out there..."

"Then you go look yourself," Tomas said. "Tomorrow. Early. Before the grid, before the frequency, before the filing cabinet gets his morning voice. You and me and the good binoculars, and we find out if it's a bird."

She looked at him. Rain ran off the wing and dripped between them, ticking on the gravel like a slow clock.

"When did we decide this?"

"We didn't decide anything. I'm telling you what you already know so you can pretend I talked you into it. It's kinder to both of us." He shouldered the fuel hose. "Go dry off. And Freya—whatever your grandfather's old routes are whispering to you right now, whatever the shape of that coastline reminds you of—write it down somewhere only you can find. Some maps get confiscated."

She stood a moment longer in the rain, the oilcloth book heavy in her pocket, heavier than its weight.

Because that was the crack, and she felt it now, clean and cold as a fault line underfoot: she had flown for the government today, taken their double rates, spoken on their frequency, reported *nothing anomalous* in a steady voice. And all the while, three miles east of their neat little fence, something had flashed at her from the drowned country—something that fit, with horrible precision, inside the smudged coordinates of a dead man's logbook she hadn't opened in years.

Loyalty, she thought, taxiing nowhere in particular in her head, is easy when everyone points the same direction.

Tomorrow she'd point east.

* * *

That night the storm arrived properly, as storms do when they've finished rehearsing, and Freya sat in the hangar office with the lamp turned low and the rain hammering the tin roof like applause from an enormous, indifferent audience.

The kneeboard book lay open in front of her. Her handwriting stared back, neat and damning:

*Day 1, south leg 4, ~2 mi E of grid boundary. Single hard glint, metallic, intermittent w/ cloud shadow. NOT water. Position fixed off Saddle ridge + old channel marker (fallen). Est. 3 mi ESE of marker.*

Below it, after a long pause, she'd added a second line, smaller:

*Why does the drowned country sit inside Grandpa's smudge?*

She hadn't opened the logbooks yet. They were upstairs in the cedar chest, wrapped in oilcloth beside her grandmother's quilts, and she knew—knew the way you know a tooth is going to hurt before you touch it—that once she opened them, the silence her family had kept would stop being abstract. It would have coordinates. Dates. His handwriting, explaining nothing, apologizing for nothing, because he had never explained himself to anyone living.

Outside, lightning walked the horizon out over the water, silent and slow, counting something.

Six days, the liaison had said, before the salvage fleet's advance ship reached Cape Reckoning waters. Six days of grid flights, of filing-cabinet voices, of reporting *nothing anomalous* while the truth sat three miles east of the fence line, flashing at her whenever the clouds parted.

Freya clicked the pen closed and set it across the page like a bookmark holding her place in a story she was suddenly afraid to finish.

Tomorrow, she and Tomas would go and see whether it was a bird.

She was already certain it wasn't.
