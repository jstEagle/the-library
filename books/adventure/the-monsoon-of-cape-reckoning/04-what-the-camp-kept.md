## 4. What the Camp Kept

The glint site sat eleven miles up the Kestrel River valley, which meant eleven miles of no road, no trail, and — according to Tomas, who had said it three times since dawn — eleven miles of reasons to have stayed in bed.

"You know what lives out here?" he asked, slapping the back of his neck for the fourth time in a hundred yards. "Mosquitoes the size of your fist. Leeches with ambitions. And that's just the wildlife with a work ethic."

"Keep talking," Freya said, pushing through a curtain of devil's club. "It keeps the bears away."

"That was one time, Freya. One time I sang show tunes on a hike and you've never let it die."

"It wasn't the singing, Tomas. It was that you knew all the words."

He grumbled something she chose not to hear, and they climbed on.

She had told him everything the night before — the glint outside the grid, the private log entry, the coordinates she'd written on her palm like a schoolgirl passing notes. She'd expected an argument. What she'd gotten instead was Tomas standing in her hangar at six a.m. with two packs already packed, a thermos of coffee strong enough to strip varnish, and an expression that said he'd been awake all night deciding whether to talk her out of it or come along, and coming along won because he didn't trust her not to do something stupid alone.

"This is off the books," he'd said. "You understand that. If the liaison asks why we hiked into unsanctioned territory, we were birdwatching."

"We hate birds."

"We love birds. We're passionate about birds. Nobody suspects a birder of anything."

Now, three hours in, the valley narrowed around them. The rainforest closed overhead in a green cathedral, dripping. The monsoon hadn't fully arrived yet — this was only its opening argument, a fine persistent drizzle that found every seam in a jacket — but the air had that pressed, waiting quality, like the whole sky was holding its breath between sentences.

Freya navigated by the river and by memory of the terrain she'd seen from above. That was the thing about flying this country for fifteen years: from the air, the land made sense. Rivers wrote clean sentences across it. On foot, the same land became a paragraph with no punctuation, all bramble and blowdown and ground that pretended to be solid until you committed your weight.

"There." Tomas stopped, pointing uphill through the trees.

She saw it a moment later: a rectangle of faded orange, tarp or tent fabric, maybe two hundred yards up a bench of flat ground above the river.

They approached slowly, which felt foolish — nobody had been here in weeks, probably — but neither of them could help it. Some silences you walk into gently.

The camp resolved out of the green all at once, the way things did in deep forest: suddenly it was simply there, as if it had grown.

Two dome tents, one collapsed under a fallen limb, one still standing. A cook tarp strung between alders. A folding table with a camp stove on it. Gear — real gear, government-issue gear, the kind with inventory numbers stenciled on everything — arranged with the tidy desperation of people who expected to come back.

Nobody had come back.

"Tomas," Freya said quietly.

"I see it."

The stove sat on the table with a kettle beside it. Not packed away. *Set down*, the way you set something down when you mean to pick it up again in ten minutes. A mug stood next to the kettle, half full of rainwater and old leaves.

Tomas crouched at the edge of the clearing, studying the ground the way he studied engines — hands hovering, not touching yet, reading. "No tracks leading out," he said after a while. "Look. There's trails all around camp, worn paths to the latrine, to the water. People lived here. But there's no exit trail. No path leaving north, south, toward the river, anywhere. It's like they—" He stopped.

"Went up," Freya finished.

He looked at her.

"They didn't leave, Tomas. They went *up*. Or along the ridge. Or—" She shook her head. "But there's nothing up there. I've flown this valley my whole life. There's nothing up there but rock and more rock."

"Maybe they got picked up."

"By who? There's no landing zone within forty miles that isn't mine or the river. And if a boat came, there'd be a track to the water. There's nothing." She turned in a slow circle, and the wrongness of the place settled over her like the drizzle — weightless, everywhere, impossible to shake off. "Everything's here, Tomas. Their gear is *here*. You don't abandon satellite phones and cold-weather kit in monsoon country unless you left in a hurry or you never intended—"

She stopped herself. Neither ending of that sentence was one she wanted to say out loud.

Tomas had moved to the standing tent. He unzipped the fly with the exaggerated care of a man defusing something. Inside: sleeping bags rolled, a crate of ration packs — untouched, seals intact — a first aid station, and pinned to the tent wall with four brass map pins, a chart.

Not a government chart. Government charts came laminated, stamped, annotated in bureaucratic blue. This was a nautical tide table, commercially printed, the kind sold in every chandlery on the coast — but someone had worked it over hard. Six dates circled in grease pencil, marching forward day by day. Beside each date, a time and a height, written in a small, precise hand. And beneath the dates, drawn directly onto the chart in the same grease pencil, freehand but confident:

A coastline.

Freya stared at it. Her whole life she'd looked at this coast from above — every bay, every bar, every river mouth from Cape Reckoning to the border. She knew the shape of it the way she knew her own signature.

This wasn't it.

It was *close*. That was the unsettling part. The pen strokes had the rhythm of the real coast — the same deep indentations, the same island chains trailing offshore like beads off a broken necklace. But the proportions were wrong. Stretched. Shifted. As if someone had taken the coastline she knew and pulled it sideways, or as if they'd drawn a coast that existed somewhere else entirely — or nowhere at all.

In the corner of the chart, small, almost apologetic, someone had written: *sixth tide.*

"Tomas," she said. Her voice came out strange. Flat. "Come look at this."

He crowded in beside her, dripping. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere above them, a raven said something rude and flapped off.

"That's not any chart I've ever seen," Tomas said finally.

"It's not any *coast* I've ever seen." She reached out, then made herself stop, hand hovering. "Six dates. Count them. Six high tides, starting—" she checked her watch against the first circled date, doing the arithmetic twice because she didn't trust the answer the first time gave her, "—starting nine days ago."

"So the fifth one is..."

"Day after tomorrow." Something cold walked down her spine, unhurried, taking its time. "And the sixth one is four days out."

"The survey team goes missing," Tomas said slowly, thinking aloud, "nine days ago. Same day somebody circles the first tide on a chart and draws a coastline that doesn't exist. And writes *sixth tide* in the corner like it's an appointment."

"Like it's a deadline."

They stood there in the doorway of the tent, two people who between them could fix any engine and name every mountain on the horizon, and for once neither of them had anything useful to say. The drizzle ticked against the tent fabric. Down in the trees, water dripped from leaf to leaf with the patience of something counting.

Freya took photographs. Every angle of the camp, the chart, the kettle, the untouched rations, the ground without tracks. Her hands were steadier than she expected them to be. It occurred to her, distantly, that she was documenting a crime scene, except she couldn't have said what the crime was. Disappearance wasn't a crime. Drawing a coastline that didn't exist wasn't a crime. Circling tides wasn't a crime.

So why did the whole clearing feel like a held breath?

"We should take the chart," Tomas said.

"No."

"No? Freya, it's evidence. It's the only—"

"If we take it and anyone finds out we were here, we're not birdwatchers anymore, we're suspects. If we leave it, we can always come back." She zipped the tent flap closed, restoring it exactly as they'd found it. "Besides," she added, and heard the obsession creep into her own voice and didn't much care, "I don't need to take it. I memorized it."

Tomas looked at her for a long moment. "You memorized it."

"Every line. Every number."

"Of course you did." He sighed, but she caught the ghost of a smile under the beard. It was the smile he wore when she did something reckless and impressive and he was furious about both. "You know what my grandmother used to say about people who memorize things they shouldn't?"

"What?"

"'That's how haunts get started.'"

* * *

They made better time going down, gravity and unease both pulling. By the time they reached the river flats the light had gone pewter-colored, and the drizzle had thickened into something with intentions. The monsoon's opening argument was becoming its thesis statement.

At the boat cache where they'd left the skiff, Tomas paused with his hand on the painter and looked back up the valley, where the camp had already dissolved into gray-green nothing.

"Say the team walked out," he said. "Say they're alive, holed up somewhere, waiting out weather. Why not stay at camp? Camp had food. Camp had shelter. You don't leave a good camp unless something scared you out of it."

"Or unless the camp wasn't the point," Freya said. "The chart was the point. They weren't camping, Tomas. They were *counting*. Counting tides, watching for something that only shows up on certain days." She thought of the glint she'd seen from the air, the flash of light outside the sanctioned grid, and said nothing about it, because she'd already decided not to say anything about it until she understood it herself.

"And when the sixth tide comes," Tomas said, "four days from now—"

"Something happens. Or something surfaces. Or somebody arrives." She pushed the skiff into the current and jumped in beside him. "Either way, we've got four days to figure out what a coastline that doesn't exist is doing on a government surveyor's wall."

The river carried them down through the darkening trees. Tomas ran the outboard low and quiet, and Freya sat in the bow with the rain running off her hood, tracing the phantom coastline in her mind, stroke by stroke, the way you'd trace a face from a dream.

And that was when it happened — not as a thought but as a physical jolt, low in the chest, like turbulence with no cloud to blame.

The proportions were wrong. Stretched. Shifted. Pulled sideways.

She'd seen that before. Seen a coastline dragged out of true, redrawn by a heavy hand, smudged at the edges by someone trying to erase it.

Not on a chart.

On paper. Old paper. In a box under her bed, in a house on the point, in a pilot's logbook belonging to a man fourteen years dead who had never once, in all his long stories about the early days, mentioned whatever those unfiled flights were for.

"Tomas," she said.

"Yeah?"

"How fast can this thing go?"

He looked up from the outboard, read her face, and opened the throttle without asking another question. The skiff rose onto plane and the river blurred, and Freya Hartwell stared north toward Cape Reckoning and the house where her grandfather's silence sat waiting in a box, patient as a tide, and understood with total certainty that she would not sleep tonight.
