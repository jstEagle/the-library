## 5. Grandfather's Logbook

The rain started around nine, the way it always did in the first week of monsoon—politely at first, like a guest testing whether it was welcome, and then all at once, as if it had never been anything but rude.

Freya stood in her kitchen with a mug of coffee she'd already forgotten about and watched it hammer the tin roof of her hangar across the yard. The sound was familiar enough to be almost comforting, the way a toothache is familiar. Somewhere under that noise, the whole coast of Cape Reckoning was settling in for four months of being weathered on, and everyone would complain about it constantly and be secretly glad, because the rain was the reason the salvage boats stayed away and the government men filed their surveys from desks in the capital instead of tramping through the muskeg.

Invisibility by precipitation. Her grandfather used to call it that. *Best fence ever built, Frey-girl,* he'd say, nodding at a sky the color of wet slate. *Nobody crosses a fence they can't see the top of.*

She had thought about him all the way back from the survey camp. Not the comfortable thoughts, either—the ones where you remember a person so clearly you can hear their laugh, and then remember that the person had a whole interior life you were never invited into, and possibly never knew about. Sixty years of flying together, or near enough, and she had never once heard him mention a coastline that matched nothing on any chart.

The tide chart was folded into her jacket pocket. She hadn't taken it out since the camp. She was aware of it the way you're aware of a stone in your boot.

"Right," she said to the empty kitchen, because saying things out loud made them less enormous. "Logbooks."

* * *

Her grandfather's papers lived in the cedar chest at the foot of her bed, which was itself an inheritance—the chest, not the papers, though now she supposed both. It had crossed the country twice and an ocean once, and its hinges complained in a language all their own. She knelt on the cold floorboards and lifted the lid, breathing in the smell that lived inside it: old canvas, engine oil, pipe smoke that had soaked into everything decades ago and refused to leave, like a tenant with no notice period.

On top lay the flight jackets, the photographs, the brass compass with the dented case that had once, according to family legend, saved his life by stopping a bullet, and according to Tomas's more plausible version, had simply been dropped on a rock. Beneath those, wrapped in oilcloth, were the logbooks.

Forty-one of them. She counted every time, and it was forty-one every time, and every time she felt the same small vertigo. Forty-one books covering fifty-two years of flying, because he'd started before he was legal and lied about his age on the first page in handwriting so confident no examiner had ever questioned it.

She carried them to the kitchen table in three armloads and stacked them by year, oldest at the left, and sat down with the lamp pulled close, and began.

Reading his logs was like hearing his voice. He'd kept them properly—dates, aircraft, route, times, remarks—but the remarks column was where he lived. *Winds spiteful. Landed long, cow on strip, cow unbothered.* *Mrs. Okonjo's insulin + 2 doz eggs (eggs hers, not mine). Fog ate the valley again, told it off.* *Frey-girl's first solo. She waved at me on downwind, which is against every rule, and I have never been prouder of anyone in my life.*

Freya stopped on that one longer than she meant to. She'd been seventeen. She could still feel the stick in her hand, still feel him standing at the edge of the apron pretending not to watch.

She made herself keep going.

Here was what she was looking for, or rather here was what she wasn't looking for, which amounted to the same thing: flights that didn't add up. A pilot's logbook is an honest document in a way few documents are, because fuel is honest, and time is honest, and a man who writes *departs Cape Reckoning 0940, arrives Port Merrow 1140* has committed himself to two hours over a stretch of coast that takes fifty minutes. You can't fudge it without someone noticing, and her grandfather had known that better than anyone.

So he hadn't fudged it. He'd done something cleverer.

It took her until the second pot of coffee and well past midnight to see the pattern, and when she saw it she sat back so fast her chair creaked in protest.

Gaps. Whole days missing—not torn out, nothing so obvious, the man would have sooner torn out his own teeth—but simply absent. A run of pages in the spring of 1963 where the entries jumped from the fourth of April to the eleventh, no note, no explanation, as if the week between had never happened. Another gap in '68. Two in '71, close together, like a stutter. And in the earliest book, the one with the boyish handwriting and the lied-about age, a gap of nearly a month that even young Hartwell's usual irrepressible commentary couldn't paper over, because there was simply nothing written there at all.

And interleaved with the gaps—this was the part that raised the hair on her arms—were smudges.

She'd noticed them earlier and dismissed them as water damage, oil stains, the ordinary decay of old paper. But now, holding the lamp low and raking the light sideways across the page the way Tomas had taught her to check a crankcase for hairline cracks, she saw them for what they were. The ink hadn't faded. It had been *worked*. Dragged. Someone had taken a thumb or a cloth or the edge of a sleeve to wet ink and pushed it sideways into a grey smear, deliberately, carefully, in a way that destroyed the numbers while leaving the surrounding text untouched.

Coordinates. Every single smudge was a coordinate.

"Oh, you old fox," she said softly, to a man twenty years dead, and heard how much of it was love and how much was something with an edge on it.

* * *

She worked through the night the way she'd once worked through engine troubleshooting—methodically, stubbornly, refusing to be beaten by a machine or a mystery just because it was bigger than her patience. By two in the morning she had a system: red thread for the gaps, blue for the smudges, laid out across the table until the kitchen looked like a conspiracy board, which, she admitted, was exactly what it was.

The gaps clustered. That was the thing. They weren't scattered random across fifty years; they bunched into three seasons—all of them in the weeks after the spring tides, all of them in years she could now, with the survey chart unfolded beside the stack, match against the six dates penciled in a dead surveyor's hand.

He'd flown out after big tides. He'd come back and blacked out where he'd gone.

And one more thing, one small terrible thing, which she found at half past three in the morning in the 1971 book, in the entry for a day that otherwise read entirely normally—*mail run, winds fair, Doc Reyes airsick again (told him to fly less, drink more)*—except that below the remarks, in the margin, in handwriting smaller and tighter than his usual scrawl, someone had drawn a line.

A coastline.

Freya went very still.

It was a sketch, quick but sure, the kind of line a pilot draws from memory because memory is the only instrument that holds it. A headland shouldering out to sea. A bay behind it, hooked like a question mark. A point beyond that, running north, with hatching along its seaward face—cliffs, or rocks, or both.

She put the survey chart next to it. The hand-drawn coastline the missing team had pinned inside their tent, the one that matched nowhere official.

Line for line. Bay for bay. The hooked question mark of the harbor. The hatched cliffs on the northern point.

It wasn't that the drawings matched. It was that they were the *same drawing*, decades apart, made by two different hands that had both stood—or flown—in the same impossible place and seen the same impossible coast.

Her grandfather had been there. Before the survey team, before the tide chart, before any of it—he had stood over that water and drawn it in the margin of a mail run, and then he had spent the rest of his life making sure no one else ever found it.

Freya got up and paced. Three lengths of the kitchen, four, five, hands laced behind her head, the way she did when a preflight inspection turned up something she didn't want to find. The rain kept up its drumming on the roof, patient, indifferent, keeping all its old promises.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape, and she was good at arithmetic, it was most of her job: her grandfather had not merely found something. Finding things was nothing—you found crashed planes and lost hunters and odd glints of metal every season out here. Finding was weather. What he had done was *hide* it, actively, deliberately, across five decades, smudging his own records with his own thumb, leaving gaps in his own history like a man pulling boards up behind him on a bridge.

And he had never told her. Never told Maren, as far as she knew. Never told anyone. Whatever was behind that headland, whatever the tide chart marked and the manifest—if there was a manifest—recorded, Elias Hartwell had looked at it and decided that the correct response was silence, and had maintained that silence so completely that his own granddaughter, who shared his blood and his hangar and half his vocabulary, had grown up thinking the only family secret was that Great-Aunt Bettina hadn't technically been married to the man called Uncle Bettina's father.

She laughed at that, alone, at four in the morning, and the laugh came out wrong.

There was a knock at the door.

She froze. Nobody knocked at four in the morning in monsoon season unless something was on fire, and if something were on fire she'd have smelled it. She crossed to the window and eased the curtain back an inch.

Tomas, on her step, soaked to the elbows, holding a lantern, wearing the expression of a man who has been arguing with himself for an hour and has lost.

She opened the door. "If the plane's on fire, I want it noted that I did nothing wrong."

"The plane's fine." He came in dripping, looked at the table—the logbooks, the threads, the charts, the margins full of secret coastlines—and his eyebrows climbed slowly toward his hairline. "Huh," he said. "So it's like that."

"You've been drinking."

"I've been *thinking*, which is worse." He set the lantern down and lowered himself into a chair, wincing, a big man folding himself into a small space. "Varga's advance ship docked tonight. Came in ahead of the weather, riding the last of the ebb. Big gray hull, no name painted on the bow that I could see, which is a choice." He rubbed water out of his hair. "They're buying rounds at the Anchor already. Cash for stories. And Frey—" he hesitated, and she braced herself, because Tomas hesitating was rarer and worse than Tomas shouting, "—the mate doing the asking, he wasn't asking about the survey team. Not mostly. He was asking about *old Hartwell flights*. Where they went. What year. Whether anyone kept the logs."

The rain filled the silence for a while.

"They can't have the logs," Freya said.

"No," Tomas agreed. "But here's my problem. Danny Kettle's already told them about the '63 gap. I watched him do it. He doesn't know it's a gap, he thinks it's a fun story—old Elias flying off the map for a week, ha ha—and they wrote it down and bought him another round, and by tomorrow morning it'll be all over town, and by the day after it'll be in Varga's file, and she'll know exactly what we know." He looked up at her, and in the lamplight his face was older than it usually let itself be. "Whatever your grandfather hid, the hiding's coming apart. Rain's lifting in two days, maybe three. So whatever you're going to decide, Frey-girl, decide it soon."

*Frey-girl.* Her grandfather's name for her, worn smooth now by Tomas's use of it, passed along like a tool.

She looked at the table. Forty-one books. Fifty-two years. One coastline drawn twice by two hands, and a silence deep enough to land a plane in.

"I'm going to need more coffee," she said. "And you're going to tell me everything that mate asked, word for word, starting from when the ship came in."

Tomas sighed the sigh of a man who had known, walking up the path, that this was exactly how the night would go. "Get the pot," he said. "I'll start at the anchor chain."

Outside, the rain went on falling on the roof of the world, keeping its old promises, and for the first time in her life Freya wondered who else, besides her grandfather, had ever counted on them.
