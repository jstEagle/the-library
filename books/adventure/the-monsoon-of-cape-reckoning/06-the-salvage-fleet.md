## 6. The Salvage Fleet

Tomas Reyes had a rule about strangers who arrived by sea: the bigger the boat, the smaller the truth. The *Meridian Grace* broke that rule only in scale. She was a hundred and forty feet of grey steel and white superstructure, riding into Cape Reckoning's harbor on a Tuesday morning like she owned the tide, and every soul in town came down to look at her.

Tomas watched from the open doors of Hartwell Aviation, a rag over his shoulder and Freya's Cessna 180 half-gutted behind him, her engine hoisted on a chain like something being interrogated.

"That," he said to the engine, "is trouble with a helipad."

The ship anchored with a competence that made his teeth itch. No wasted motion, no fumbling with lines. Within an hour a tender was shuttling ashore, and men in company jackets were walking the dock as if they'd been born there, shaking hands, handing out business cards thick enough to stop a bullet.

By noon, three things were known across Cape Reckoning, which is to say they'd been said at Dora's counter over coffee strong enough to strip varnish:

One: the salvage company was called Meridian Recovery Group, out of Port Calloway, and they were legitimate — licensed, bonded, insured, the whole liturgy.

Two: they were here about the missing government survey team, contracted to assist the search once the weather window opened, and their advance team was buying fuel, provisions, and goodwill.

Three: they were paying cash for both.

"Three dollars a gallon over list for avgas." Old Pete Kowalski said it the way other men said amen. "Three dollars over, Tomas. I haven't seen money like that since the fishery year."

"And all they want in return," Dora said, wiping the counter that didn't need wiping, "is conversation."

"There it is," Tomas said.

Dora looked at him over her spectacles. "You've got a suspicious mind, Reyes."

"I've got a functioning one. There's a difference."

* * *

He met Ilse Varga on the third day, and understood within four minutes why she ran that ship.

She came to the hangar unannounced, which already put her ahead of most visitors, who usually announced themselves at length. She was maybe fifty, silver at the temples cut short and practical, wearing deck shoes that had actually seen decks. She stood in the doorway of his workshop, looked at the engine on its chain, and said:

"Continental W670. You don't see those running anymore. Who does your overhauls?"

"I do."

"You do them well." She stepped inside without waiting to be invited, which Tomas noted, and stopped in front of the workbench, where Freya's grandfather's old tools hung in their shadowboard — every wrench traced in white paint so a missing one showed as a ghost. "My father had a board like that. He said a man who knows where his tools are knows where he is."

"He sounds like he'd have gotten along with the woman who owned these."

"Hartwell," Varga said. "Elias Hartwell. That was before your time flying with her, though. You're newer."

"Four years."

"Long enough." She turned and offered her hand, and her grip was dry and honest and exactly as strong as it needed to be. "Ilse Varga. I'd have come sooner, but I've been doing the rounds. Your town is very good at talking."

"We practice."

"So I've heard." She smiled, and it reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "I'll be direct, Mr. Reyes, because I'm told that's the local currency. We're under contract to support the search for the survey team. Six people, twenty-two days silent. My ships are better equipped for offshore search than anything the government can scramble up this season, and my divers are idle until the monsoon passes anyway. It's good work finding lost people. I like good work."

"And?"

"And I pay well for local knowledge, because local knowledge is the difference between searching a grid and searching a guess." She produced a card from her jacket — heavy stock, embossed, absurd — and set it on his bench next to a jar of cotter pins. "I'm interviewing everyone who flies, sails, or walks the interior. Standard background for the search planning. Flight corridors, fuel stops, known hazards. If you or Ms. Hartwell have logs going back any distance, they'd be genuinely useful. Old flight paths tell you where people thought it was worth looking."

Tomas picked up a cotter pin and turned it over. "Freya handles her own paperwork."

"Of course." Varga's eyes went past him, briefly, to the shadowboard, to the ghosts of missing tools — two, currently, because Freya had borrowed a spanner last week and never returned it, a crime Tomas intended to prosecute. "Her grandfather flew this coast for forty years, I understand. There can't be many men who knew it better."

"No," Tomas said. "There can't."

Varga held his gaze for a moment longer than comfort, then nodded as if something had been filed away in a cabinet he couldn't see. "Thank you for your time. The offer stands — for you, for her, for anyone. Three dollars over list, and a finder's fee structure I'll be posting at Dora's, since apparently that's the newspaper here."

"It's the only institution we trust," Tomas said.

"A sensible town." At the door she paused. "Mr. Reyes. The survey team has families. Whatever else anyone in this town might be protecting, those six people are somewhere out there, and every day matters. I'd hate for pride, or privacy, or — sentiment — to cost someone their life."

Then she was gone, her footsteps even on the gravel, and Tomas stood holding a cotter pin and disliking how much sense she made.

* * *

That evening he walked down to the dock, because the dock was where the town went to be itself, and what he saw there sat in his stomach like bad fuel.

Varga's advance men had set up a folding table outside the chandlery with a cash box and a ledger, and there was a line. Not a long line — Cape Reckoning didn't do long lines, it did clusters — but a line. Pete Kowalski was counting bills with the reverence of a man handling scripture. The Ferreira boys were answering questions about the river bars, gesturing wide, getting paid. Maren Okonkwo's grandson was telling some story about a moose and a floatplane that Tomas was fairly sure he'd invented on the spot, and getting twenty dollars for it.

And in the middle of it, holding court near the gangway with a coffee she hadn't bought, sat Ruth Ellery — schoolteacher, church treasurer, the closest thing Cape Reckoning had to a conscience — laughing at something one of the company men said.

Tomas drifted close enough to hear.

"—so the captain says to me, we're not treasure hunters, Mrs. Ellery, we're historians with a budget—"

Ruth laughed again. "Historians with a budget. Well. Half our history got here on boats, I suppose it's fitting it leaves on one."

"Ma'am?"

"Nothing, dear." Ruth patted the young man's arm. "You tell your captain the survey team flew out of here on the ninth. Everyone saw them. Nice young people. Too many clipboards between them."

Tomas moved on before his face could say anything his mouth would regret.

At the end of the dock, sitting on a bollard with her boots dangling over black water, was Amara Osei — the ferry operator's daughter, nineteen, home for the season, sharp as a gutting knife. She watched him come and scooted sideways to make room without being asked.

"You look like a man doing arithmetic," she said.

"Always am."

"Let me save you some." She nodded at the table, the cash box, the line. "Pete took two hundred dollars today. For stories he tells free at Dora's. The Ferreira boys took a hundred and fifty for river bars that are on every chart ever printed. And Ruth—" Amara shook her head. "Ruth told them everything she knew about the survey team, and then apologized for not knowing more."

"People are allowed to earn a living."

"People are allowed to be bought," Amara said. "It just usually takes more than three dollars a gallon." She looked at him sidelong. "They asked about her, you know. About Freya. Not just the survey team. They asked when she flies, where she goes, whether her grandfather kept logs. Pete told them Elias Hartwell could find a lake in fog by smell. He thought it was a compliment."

Tomas said nothing.

"They asked if the logbooks still exist," Amara said quietly. "Pete said he didn't know. But he said it like a man who'd wondered himself."

The tide was coming in, filling the harbor with that slow patient sound it made, like the coast clearing its throat before saying something important. Out past the point, the *Meridian Grace* rode her anchor light, steady as a fact.

"Tomas." Amara hopped off the bollard. "Whatever Freya's sitting on — and everyone with eyes knows she's sitting on something, she's been flying grids off-book for a week and lying badly about it — whatever it is, decide fast. Because that woman isn't buying fuel. She's buying the town. And this town has never been able to afford itself before."

She walked off up the dock, and Tomas stayed, watching the anchor light, doing the arithmetic she'd tried to save him.

* * *

Freya came back the next afternoon, mud to the knees, tired in the way that meant she'd found nothing and hated it. He heard the 180 before he saw her — that particular off-key note in the left magneto he'd been threatening to fix — and had the coffee on by the time she rolled the plane into the hangar.

"Grids nine through twelve," she said. "Nothing. Water, trees, water pretending to be trees." She dropped onto the work stool and accepted the mug. "Town's crawling with suits, I hear."

"Two of them interviewed Dora for an hour. She charged them for pie and gave them gossip for free, which is backwards, but Dora operates on her own economy." He wiped his hands, slowly, giving himself time. "Their boss came by here yesterday. Varga."

Freya's mug paused halfway to her mouth. "What did she want?"

"Local knowledge. Flight corridors. Hazards." He met her eyes. "Old flight paths. Yours specifically. Her grandfather's specifically. Whether the logbooks still exist."

The pause lasted two seconds. To anyone else it would have looked like fatigue. Tomas had four years of reading this woman's face, and it read like a door being locked from the inside.

"Everybody's grandfather flew something," Freya said.

"Not everybody's grandfather smudged his own coordinates."

"Tomas—"

"I'm not asking." He raised both hands. "I'm not. You'll tell me when you tell me, or you won't, and either way I'll keep fixing your engines, because somebody has to keep you alive and clearly you've decided it's me. I'm just telling you what's happening out there." He nodded toward the harbor. "Cash on the dock. Questions at Dora's. Ruth Ellery is calling them historians with a budget and meaning it kindly. Pete told a stranger you could find a lake by smell, and then told him he didn't know if the logbooks survived — which means Pete has been wondering, which means everyone will be wondering by Friday."

Freya looked into her coffee. "How much have they spent?"

"On the town? Pete swears the advance man's cash box started with ten thousand in it. Could be bluffing. Probably isn't."

"And on us?"

"She hasn't made you an offer yet. Which means she's building one." Tomas crouched by the engine, mostly to have something to do with his hands. "Freya. A woman like that doesn't spend ten thousand dollars learning what the whole town already knows. She's spending it learning what it doesn't. And right now the only thing this coast keeps off the charts is yours."

For a long moment the only sound was the harbor breathing through the open hangar doors, and the tick of the cooling engine, and somewhere far off the gulls arguing about something eternal.

"The survey team," Freya said finally, and her voice had changed — quieter, steadier, the voice she used when she'd stopped deciding and decided. "Six people, twenty-two days. Varga keeps saying every day matters, and she's right. So tomorrow I fly grid thirteen. And fourteen. And however many it takes."

"And the rest of it?"

She stood, drained the mug, and set it on the bench beside Varga's embossed card, which Tomas had left there deliberately, like bait, like a warning.

"The rest of it," she said, "is mine. For now."

She walked out toward the house, and Tomas stood alone in the hangar with the engine on its chain and the card on the bench, and after a while he picked the card up and read the back of it, where in small neat type was printed a single line:

*Meridian Recovery Group — Everything Lost Is Somewhere.*

"Creepy," he told the engine, and put it in the drawer with the cotter pins.

But it was later, locking up under a sky going the color of a bruise, that he noticed the thing that took the ease out of his whole evening: the shadowboard, and the two white ghosts where tools should be. One was Freya's borrowed spanner, fine, that was a standing crime.

The other was the small brass-handled magnifier — the one Elias Hartwell had used, by all accounts, to read his charts — and Tomas was absolutely certain it had been hanging there yesterday, and absolutely certain neither he nor Freya had touched it.

Outside, down at the harbor, the *Meridian Grace*'s anchor light burned steady and patient, and the tide came in over the flats like a question nobody wanted answered out loud.
