## 7. Six Tides

The rain had a rhythm to it that night, the kind of steady, patient drumming that made the whole coast feel like it was holding its breath between verses. Freya Hartwell sat at her kitchen table with the tide chart pinned flat under four coffee mugs, two nautical almanacs open like sleeping birds, and a pencil she'd chewed down to something resembling a beaver's opinion.

Tomas had gone home three hours ago, but not before leaving a plate of his mother's cassava cake wrapped in foil and a note that read: *Eat something or I'll know.* She'd eaten something. She'd also eaten most of the rest of it, which was the trouble with Tomas's mother—her cooking came with an implied contract you couldn't get out of.

The chart itself was a beautiful, maddening thing. Six dates written in a surveyor's careful hand, each one circled twice, and beside them a hand-drawn stretch of coastline that matched no map Freya had ever flown over—and she had flown over every map this side of the Reckoning River, some of them by accident, one of them on purpose during a dare from a man who now sold bait in Port Anselm and refused to make eye contact with her.

The coastline drawing was wrong in a very specific way. It showed the point at Cape Reckoning extending further out than it did—a hooked finger of land reaching into the water where, on every official chart, there was nothing but open channel and a sandbar that only made appearances when it felt like it.

Or when the tide let it.

"That's it," she murmured to the kitchen, to the rain, to nobody. "That's the whole trick of it."

She worked backward through the almanacs, cross-referencing lunar tables against the six dates. The survey team hadn't picked random days. They'd picked *springs*—the big tides, the ones that came when moon and sun pulled together like men hauling a rope. And not just any springs. The sixth date fell on the lowest low tide of the season, the kind of extreme drawdown that happened maybe twice a year, when the sea pulled back so far it seemed to change its mind about the whole arrangement.

At that tide, the sandbar off the point wouldn't just show. It would stay exposed for hours. Long enough to walk on. Long enough to dig.

Long enough for a wreck to break the surface—or come close enough to reach.

Freya did the arithmetic twice, then a third time because the first two answers disagreed with her stomach. Four days out. The sixth high tide would fall on Thursday morning, just past dawn, and the window would last until roughly noon before the ocean came back to collect whatever it had lent.

Four days. The salvage fleet was already anchored somewhere beyond the headlands—she'd seen the lights through the rain the night before, a string of them too orderly to be fishing boats, too patient to be anything good.

She sat back and looked at her grandfather's logbook, open on the corner of the table where it had lived for the past two days like a cat that had decided the spot was hers now. The smudged coordinates. The flights never filed. The deliberate, loving silence of a man who had apparently spent decades flying circles around a secret.

"Grandpa," she said, "you could have left me a boat. Or money. Literally anything other than a mystery."

The logbook said nothing, which was very much in character.

* * *

Morning came gray and rinsed-clean, the kind of light that made everything look honest whether it was or not. Freya walked down to the harbor to buy diesel and gossip, in that order, though everyone in Cape Reckoning knew the real order was reversed.

The gossip had teeth that week. The advance ship—the *Corvina*, white-hulled and too clean for these waters—sat at the end of the pier with her cranes folded like a mantis at prayer. Her crew had been ashore for two days now, buying rounds at the Anchor Line, paying cash for supplies at triple the going rate, and asking questions with the smoothness of men who'd been trained to make curiosity sound like friendliness.

"They bought Denny Okafor's whole crab quota," said Marta at the fuel dock, handing over the hose. "Paid him for a month he hasn't even fished yet. Denny's walking around like a man who's been touched by God."

"Denny would walk like that if someone overpaid him for a sandwich."

"True." Marta leaned in, lowering her voice even though the entire harbor already knew everything. "But here's the thing. The tall one—the one with the nice watch—he was asking Ruthie about old flights. Flights out of here, twenty, thirty years back. Who flew them, where they went."

Freya kept her face doing nothing at all, which took effort. "Ruthie tell him?"

"Ruthie told him what Ruthie tells everybody, which is whatever gets her the best tip." Marta shrugged. "So, everything."

Wonderful. Freya paid for her diesel, accepted a cinnamon bun Marta pressed on her with the fierce generosity of a woman who considered refusal a personal insult, and walked back up the pier with her mind running calculations that had nothing to do with fuel.

That was when Ilse Varga found her.

She came down the *Corvina*'s gangway like weather—not hurrying, just arriving, inevitable. She was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, wearing a foul-weather jacket that probably cost more than Freya's annual maintenance budget, and she moved with the particular confidence of someone who had never once had to wait for a table.

"Pilot Hartwell." She extended a hand. Her grip was dry and firm, a handshake that had clearly been calibrated. "Ilse Varga. I've been hoping to meet you."

"You could have come to the hangar. I'm easy to find. I'm the one covered in hydraulic fluid."

"I wanted to see how you carried yourself first." Varga smiled, and it was a good smile—warm at the edges, practiced at the center. "You walk like your grandfather did. I met him once, you know. Years ago, in Port Anselm. He refueled one of our survey boats' floatplane. Quiet man. Very precise."

Something cold slid down Freya's spine and settled in behind her ribs, taking up residence. "Small coast," she said. "Everybody's met everybody."

"Isn't it, though." Varga gestured toward the bench at the end of the pier, and without waiting for agreement, sat. Freya, having no graceful alternative, sat too. Below them the water slapped the pilings in a lazy, tidal rhythm, counting down to something.

"I'll be direct, because I suspect you prefer it," Varga said. "We're here on a recovery operation. Maritime heritage salvage, fully licensed, fully insured, all the paperwork a person could want. We're also short a pilot who knows this coast in monsoon conditions, and every name I hear mentioned is yours."

"There are other pilots."

"There are other pilots the way there are other coffees. Technically true, practically irrelevant." Varga turned to look at her directly, and her eyes were pale gray and entirely unfooled. "I'll pay you triple your government rate. Your own schedule. Fuel, maintenance, insurance—all ours. And when we finish, a finder's fee that would buy you a new plane and a new hangar and a very long vacation somewhere it doesn't rain."

"That's generous for a ferry job."

"It's generous because I want more than a ferry job." Varga paused, letting the waves fill the silence. "I want someone who knows where things are. Old things. Things that don't appear on charts."

And there it was, laid out on the wet planks between them like a fish on ice. Freya looked at this woman—competent, courteous, dangerous in the specific way that competence becomes when it stops caring who it steps over—and felt the offer settle around her shoulders like a coat cut exactly to her measurements. Triple rate. A new engine for the Otter, which needed one the way Tomas needed sleep. Security for the airstrip, for the town, for the medicine runs that kept half the interior alive through the wet season.

It would be so easy. That was the thing about temptation nobody warned you about—it didn't arrive looking like a devil. It arrived looking like a solution.

"I'll think about it," Freya said.

Varga's smile deepened by a single degree, the smile of a woman who had heard *I'll think about it* many times and knew precisely what it meant coming from a woman who'd already done the math. "Of course. But do think quickly. The tides won't wait, and neither will I." She stood, brushed a fleck of spray from her sleeve, and added, lightly, as though it were an afterthought: "Your grandfather was offered the same terms once, you know. By my predecessor's company. He declined. I've always wondered why."

She walked back up the gangway, and Freya sat on the bench for a long time, watching the water count.

* * *

Tomas was under the Otter when she got back to the hangar, legs sticking out from beneath the fuselage like a mechanic-shaped offering to the aviation gods. She could hear him talking to the aircraft, which he always denied doing, and which everyone within earshot had witnessed roughly nine hundred times.

"The number two cylinder is not 'fine,' Consuela, it is *tired*, and pretending otherwise is how people end up as a headline—"

"Tomas."

A clunk, a curse, and then he rolled out on the creeper, blinking up at her with a smear of oil across one cheekbone like war paint applied by a toddler. "You're back early. Did the harbor explode?"

"Worse. Varga made me an offer."

He sat up slowly. The humor drained out of his face the way water drains out of a lock, leaving everything below the waterline visible. "How much?"

"Triple rate. Plus a finder's fee that would fix every problem this airstrip has ever had."

"And?"

"And she knew Grandpa. Or claims she did. And she said—" Freya stopped, hearing the words again in Varga's calm, expensive voice. "She said he was offered the same deal once. And she's always wondered why he said no."

Tomas was quiet for a moment. He wiped his hands on a rag that had stopped being a rag sometime in the previous decade and had become, through sheer persistence, a philosophical object.

"So she knows there's something to find," he said. "She doesn't know what. And she thinks you might."

"She suspects. There's a difference."

"Is there? From where I'm sitting, both of those end with strangers digging up our coastline." He stood, wincing slightly, forty-two years old in his knees and nineteen in his opinions. "Frey. Listen to me. Whatever your grandfather hid, he hid it for a reason, and the reason wasn't greed, because that man once drove forty miles to return a two-dollar overpayment to the co-op. He hid it because hiding it was *kind*. I'd bet Consuela's cylinders on it."

Freya looked at the logbook, sitting in its canvas flight bag in the corner where she'd taken to keeping it, close enough to touch, far enough that it didn't feel like it was watching her.

"I keep thinking about what he gave up," she said quietly. "All those years. Every flight logged wrong on purpose. Living his whole life one lie wide. What does a man carry like that, Tomas? And why would anyone choose it?"

"Maybe ask someone who was there." Tomas tossed the rag onto the workbench. "Maren's still up at the Point. She flew with him longer than anyone. If anybody knows what your grandfather was carrying, it's the old woman who rode shotgun while he carried it."

Freya had been avoiding that thought the way you avoid a loose tooth—aware of it, prodding it anyway. Maren Okonkwo-Voss was eighty-one years old, sharp as a propeller edge, and had not forgiven Freya for grounding her garden tractor in a dispute over noise ordinances that Maren had, by every reasonable account, started.

"She'll yell at me."

"Probably."

"She'll make me prune things."

"Definitely."

"She'll tell me the truth while doing both, and it'll hurt."

Tomas smiled, tired and fond. "Yeah. That's how you know it'll be worth hearing."

Outside, the rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist, and beyond the headlands, invisible but perfectly audible to anyone who knew how to listen, the salvage fleet sat at anchor, waiting out the same water, counting down the same tides.

Six high tides until Thursday. Five, after tonight's.

Freya zipped the logbook into its bag, slung it over her shoulder, and went to find the oldest pilot on the coast before the sea gave up its secret—and took the choice of what to do about it right along with it.
