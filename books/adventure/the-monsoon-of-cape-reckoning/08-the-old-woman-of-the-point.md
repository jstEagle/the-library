## 8. The Old Woman of the Point

Maren Okonkwo lived at the end of the point, in a house that looked like it had been assembled by the wind out of spite and driftwood. Freya had been flying over it her whole life—the crooked chimney, the blue door that hadn't been repainted since before she was born, the garden where rose bushes and fishing netting had long ago agreed to a truce. But she couldn't remember the last time she'd actually landed and walked up to it.

That was the thing about Maren. Everyone in Cape Reckoning knew she was there, the way you knew about the lighthouse or the tide itself, and everyone gave her exactly the same amount of trouble: none.

Freya parked the truck at the top of the shingle path and sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel. The monsoon light was doing its late-afternoon trick, turning the water the color of hammered pewter, and somewhere behind the hills the first thunder of the season grumbled like an old man being asked to move his car.

She'd rehearsed this on the flight back from the hangar. *Morning, Maren. Mind if I ask you something strange?* Except nothing about this was going to be polite or morning-shaped, because it was nearly five o'clock and what she wanted to ask was: *Did my grandfather find a shipwreck and spend fifty years pretending he didn't?*

There was no gentle way to ask that. There was only asking it.

The blue door opened before she knocked.

"I could hear you thinking from the kitchen," Maren said. She was small and round and fierce, wrapped in a cardigan the color of storm clouds, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair that defied gravity on principle. "You've got your grandfather's walk. All shoulders and hurry. Get inside before the rain figures out where you are."

* * *

Inside smelled of woodsmoke, dried kelp, and forty years of strong tea. The front room was a museum of a life lived outdoors: charts yellowed in frames, a harpoon head on the mantel that nobody ever explained, photographs of aircraft so old they were practically fossils. Freya's eyes went straight to one of them—a floatplane on the old Cape Reckoning dock, two young people standing on the floats, laughing at whatever the camera had just said.

Her grandfather. And a girl with a braid down to her waist.

"That's you," Freya said.

"That's me," Maren agreed, pouring tea without asking whether tea was wanted. It was wanted. It was always wanted in Cape Reckoning; refusing Maren's tea was considered a medical event. "Nineteen years old and convinced I knew everything. Your grandfather was twenty-two and knew slightly less than me, which made him insufferable." She handed over a cup painted with faded puffins. "Sit. You didn't come all this way to stand."

Freya sat. The rain arrived, right on schedule, drumming on the tin roof like applause from an audience that had seen the show before.

"Maren." She turned the cup in her hands. "I need to ask you something, and I need you not to decide I'm crazy until at least the second question."

"Those are my favorite kinds of questions."

Freya took the folded page from her jacket—the tide chart from the survey camp, the hand-drawn coastline with its six dates marching along the margin—and laid it on the table between them.

Maren put her glasses on. She looked at the drawing for a long time. Long enough that the rain filled the silence, long enough that Freya's heartbeat got loud enough to embarrass her.

Then Maren took off her glasses, folded them, and set them down with the deliberate care of someone putting down something heavy.

"Where did you get this?"

"A government survey team drew it. Then they vanished off the face of the earth."

"And you flew their search grid and found nothing, because the grid wasn't where the truth was." It wasn't a question. "Yes," Maren said quietly. "I know this coastline. I've known it since before your mother was born. That's the north shore past the point. And that notch there—" her finger hovered above the paper without quite touching it, as if the ink might still be wet after all these years— "that's the cove under the sea caves. Your grandfather called it the Pocket."

Freya's mouth had gone dry despite the tea. "Called it?"

"He named things he intended to keep secret. Said if you gave a place a name, you'd remember it, and if you remembered it, you'd have to decide what to do about remembering." Maren looked up, and her eyes—still sharp, still quick, aged like everything else but not dimmed—held Freya's. "He found a wreck, Freya. Out there under the point. A big one. Colonial timbers, older than the town, older than anything with a flag on it around here."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a plane does when it finds an air pocket.

"You knew." Freya heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "You knew my whole life. Every Christmas. Every funeral. You sat across from me eating my aunt's terrible trifle and you knew."

"Yes."

"For fifty years."

"Forty-six," said Maren, who had never once let precision slide out of respect for anyone's feelings. "And yes, I knew, and yes, I kept it, and no, I'm not sorry, which you can be angry about later, after you've asked me the questions you actually came to ask. You've got three. I can see them lined up behind your eyes like planes waiting to take off."

Freya laughed—a short, cracked sound that surprised her. Trust Maren to run a tower control on her grief.

"All right." She set down the cup. "Question one. What is it? The wreck."

"A ship called the *Reckoning*. Which your grandfather thought was the funniest coincidence in maritime history, given the name of the cape, and used to say God had a sense of humor and poor taste." Maren settled back into her chair. "It went down in the seventeen-hundreds carrying cargo seized out of the trade networks here—goods taken from communities who'd been trading these waters for ten thousand years, loaded onto a colonial hull by men with paperwork they wrote themselves. The kind of paperwork that says *mine* because the person writing it has a gun."

"The survey team's notebook mentioned a manifest."

"Then the survey team knew more than your grandfather ever did. He found the hull. He never found proof of what was in it—just enough to know it mattered, and enough to know that other people would kill, or worse, *file lawsuits*, to get it." Maren's mouth twisted. "He flew over it every year for decades. Logged the flights as mail runs, fish spotting, weather checks. Smudged the coordinates himself with his thumb, wet from his tea, sitting right where you're sitting, probably, worrying about the same thing you're worrying about."

* * *

The rain thickened. Maren got up, moved a lamp closer, and added wood to the stove with the economy of a woman who had done ten thousand chores in weather worse than this. When she sat again, she looked older—not frail, never frail, but like the weight of the story had finally been allowed to rest on her.

"Question two?" she prompted.

"Why me?" Freya said. "Why now? He died six years ago. If this was going to land in my lap, why did it wait for a missing survey team and a salvage fleet to carry it here?"

"Because he trusted you to find it yourself." Maren said it simply, the way you'd report the wind direction. "He told me once—I must have been seventy—he said, 'If Freya's half the pilot I think she is, she'll never be able to fly over that coastline without looking twice.' And you did look twice. Didn't you. Outside the grid."

Freya nodded, throat tight.

"There it is, then. He didn't leave you a map because a map is an order. He left you a mystery, because a mystery is a choice." Maren reached over and tapped the tide chart. "Six dates. Whoever drew this understood the tides out there better than the almanac does. At the sixth high tide, the sandbar shifts and the water drops just enough. That's when the wreck becomes reachable. That's also when every greedy soul within two hundred miles will be able to reach it, if they know where to look. Which, judging by the fact that a salvage fleet is sitting in your harbor buying drinks for dockhands—they know."

"How do you know about the fleet?"

"Mrs. Adeyemi told me. Mrs. Adeyemi tells me everything. Half the town's intelligence network runs through my kitchen and the other half through her bakery, and neither of us has left this point in years." Maren smiled briefly, then it faded. "Which brings us to question three. Ask it."

Freya looked at her hands. When she spoke, her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"Was he a good man? Because I built my whole life on him being a good man, Maren. I learned to fly because of him. I learned that you tell the truth even when it costs you, because that's what he did. And now I find out he spent fifty years lying—to the government, to the townsfolk, to *me*—and I don't—" Her voice caught. She pressed on. "I don't know which grandfather is real."

The stove ticked. Rain ran down the windows in silver ropes.

"Both," Maren said. "That's the answer nobody wants. Both of them, at the same time, forever." She leaned forward. "You think he hid that wreck out of greed? Freya. I flew with that man for thirty years and he once turned back a paying charter because the passenger had a cold and he didn't want to risk her ears. He hid the wreck because of what was *around* it. This coast—our town, the villages upriver, the families who've fished these waters since before maps—all of it sits on land claims that go back generations, claims that hold because nobody official has ever had a reason to look too closely. You drag a colonial treasure ship out of those waters, and the lawyers arrive before the archaeologists do. Boundaries get redrawn. Titles get 'clarified.' Communities that have been invisible on purpose for a hundred years suddenly become *very* visible, and visibility, sweetheart, has never once been good for people like us."

"So he buried it to protect them."

"He buried it so no one would ever have to choose what you're choosing." Maren's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "He carried it alone so nobody else would have to carry it. That's not a lie, child. That's a load-bearing wall. And now you've gone and pulled it out to see what's holding up the house."

Freya stared at the fire. Something in her chest was cracking in a way that hurt and helped at the same time—grief arriving late for a man she'd lost six years ago, because only now was she losing the version of him she'd actually loved. Or rather: only now was she meeting the whole of him, and discovering the whole was larger and stranger and lonelier than the piece she'd been given.

"He should have told me," she said finally. "Even if I'd have chosen the same thing. He should have let me choose."

"Yes," Maren said. "He should have. And he knew that, and it kept him up nights, and he did it anyway, because love makes cowards of the best of us in exactly the places we're bravest everywhere else." She refilled Freya's cup, unasked. "Don't waste your anger on a dead man's decisions. Spend it on the living ones. Varga's fleet has four days. What are you going to do?"

* * *

Freya stood at Maren's window afterward, watching the rain march in off the sea in gray curtains. Behind her, the old woman hummed something tuneless and washed the teacups, having apparently decided the conversation was complete, which with Maren it always was, abruptly, like a radio station signing off.

Four days. Six tides. A fleet in the harbor and a truth in her jacket pocket and a grandfather's smudged coordinates burning a hole in her memory.

"Maren," she said. "One more thing. Not a question. A favor."

"Those cost extra."

"If this goes wrong—if the fleet gets there first, if the lawyers come, if the whole coast ends up redrawn on somebody's map—I need to know you'll forgive me for whatever I decide. Even if it's the wrong thing. Especially if it's the wrong thing."

Maren came to stand beside her at the window, small and solid, smelling of woodsmoke and tea. For a moment Freya felt seven years old again, being lifted onto a floatplane wing by two laughing people who thought the sky belonged to them.

"Your grandfather asked me the same thing, the night before his last flight," Maren said. "I told him what I'll tell you. I don't forgive wrong things in advance. Nobody should. That's how wrong things get comfortable." She patted Freya's arm once, briskly. "But I'll tell you this for free: he hid the truth because he believed silence was the only shelter this coast had left. You look at that fleet out there and ask yourself whether the shelter's still standing. Because if it isn't—then keeping his secret isn't loyalty anymore, Freya. It's just a longer way of losing."

Freya drove home through the rain with the wipers beating and the tide chart on the seat beside her, and by the time the lights of the harbor came through the wet dark, she had stopped grieving the grandfather she'd imagined and started dreading the choice the real one had left her.

Because Tomas was waiting under the hangar lights, and his face told her the fleet had already made its next move.

Someone had broken into her hangar.
