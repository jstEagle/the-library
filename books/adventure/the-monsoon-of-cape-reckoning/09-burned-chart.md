## 9. Burned Chart

Tomas Reyes had a system for mornings, and the system did not include finding his best friend's hangar door hanging by one hinge like a broken jaw.

The system went: coffee at the Galley before six, argue with Dot about whether her coffee qualified as coffee or as punishment, walk the long way along the dock so he could check on the *Pelican* — Freya's Cessna, his life's work, his second-most-complicated relationship after his mother — and then open the hangar at first light to start whatever the plane needed that day.

He got as far as the coffee and the argument. He never made it to the dock.

The hangar's side door was supposed to be locked. It was instead lying in the gravel at an angle that suggested it had been removed by someone in a hurry and without feelings for hinges. Beyond it, the hangar interior looked like a weather event had happened indoors. Freya's tool chest stood open, drawers pulled out and upended. Her spare oil drums were on their sides. The workbench where Tomas kept his torque wrenches — torque wrenches he had owned longer than he'd owned most of his opinions — had been swept clean, everything scattered across the concrete floor like confetti at a very bad wedding.

And pinned under a fallen shelf, half-buried under a tarp that normally covered the winter tires, was the chart.

Or what was left of it.

Tomas crouched. The paper was torn into pieces, deliberate pieces, and someone had tried to burn what wouldn't tear. The edges were blackened and curled. He could still make out fragments — a coastline drawn in Freya's careful hand, the one she'd copied from her grandfather's logbook two nights ago while he sat across the table pretending not to watch. A smudge of blue ink. A curve of shore that matched nothing official.

They hadn't just stolen from her. They'd destroyed the copy and left the wreckage where she'd find it.

"Okay," Tomas said out loud, to nobody, because saying things out loud helped him think and also because the hangar felt like it wanted a witness. "Okay."

He did what any sensible mechanic would do with a crime scene: he checked on the airplane first.

The *Pelican* sat in her corner, nose toward the doors, looking — at a glance — untouched. But Tomas had rebuilt this plane panel by panel over eleven years. He knew her the way some men knew their trucks and better than most men knew their wives. He walked around her twice, running his hand along the fuselage, and on the third pass he stopped at the aft baggage compartment.

The access panel was ajar. Not forced. Closed badly, by someone who didn't know that Tomas tightened every screw on that plane to the same quarter-turn past snug, and that one screw now sat loose as a missing tooth.

He opened it.

Inside, wedged behind the emergency kit where Freya thought she was being clever — and where Tomas had known about it since Tuesday, because there was nowhere on that airplane he didn't know about — lay her grandfather's logbook. The original. Water-stained, spine cracked, smelling of cedar and decades. Untouched.

Tomas let out a breath he felt all the way down in his knees.

Whoever had done this had searched the hangar top to bottom and missed the thing that mattered, because they'd been looking at the plane as cargo, not as a machine. They'd seen a Cessna. Tomas saw forty years of Hartwell stubbornness riveted together, and apparently so, in some small way, did the universe.

He tucked the logbook inside his jacket, zipped it, and went to find out who had done this.

* * *

By seven-thirty, half of Cape Reckoning had walked through or peered into the hangar, which told you everything about the town's relationship with privacy, which was that they believed in it strongly in theory.

Dot arrived first, still in her apron, carrying a thermos of coffee that Tomas accepted mostly as a gesture. She surveyed the wreckage, sniffed once, and said, "Amateurs. Real professionals would've taken the good wrenches too."

"That's your read?"

"I raised three boys and ran a diner for thirty years. I know a tantrum when I see one." She nudged a scorched scrap of chart with her shoe. "This is somebody angry and somebody scared, both at once. Angry people smash. Scared people burn." She looked up at him. "You want me to say it, or you want to say it?"

Tomas said nothing.

"Varga's people docked four days ago," Dot said. "Cash flowing around the harbor like it's spring salmon season. And everybody suddenly asking questions about old Hartwell flights like it's a hobby." She poured herself a cup, drank, grimaced at her own coffee — which meant it was genuinely terrible — and left.

The constable arrived next, a round, unhurried man named Okafor who treated police work less like law enforcement and more like gardening: things grew if you paid attention, and most problems could be pruned. He took photographs with a phone older than the hangar, asked gentle questions, and wrote down answers Tomas wasn't sure were true.

"When did you last see the hangar secure?" Okafor asked.

"Last night, around nine. I closed up after Freya flew the evening mail run to Kettle Bay. Everything locked."

"No signs of forced entry on the main doors?"

"The side door was off its hinges."

Okafor studied the door, lying in the gravel. "Lifted off," he agreed. "That takes time and a crowbar and a certain lack of fear about being seen." He made a note. "You see anyone around lately? Strangers?"

Tomas hesitated. And here was the thing about hesitation: Okafor noticed it the way Tomas noticed a loose screw.

"There's been more foot traffic down at the docks," Tomas said carefully. "Since Varga's ship came in."

"Mm." Okafor wrote something that may or may not have been related. "I'll ask around. You do the same. Quietly."

When he'd gone, Tomas stood in the middle of the ruined hangar with a logbook against his ribs and a decision forming in him like a storm cell.

Because there was one detail he hadn't given the constable. One detail he was saving, the way you save the last screw for the panel that matters.

Last night, closing up, he'd seen Danny Quist on the dock road. Danny Quist, dockhand, twenty-six years old, sweet as overripe fruit and about as reliable. Danny had been walking away from the hangar end of town at nine o'clock at night, in the rain, carrying nothing, going nowhere a person goes. When Tomas had called out a hello, Danny had flinched like he'd been shot and said he was "checking a line" — a line that, as far as Tomas knew, existed nowhere but in Danny's mouth.

And this morning, Danny was the only dockhand not down at the water. Called in sick, according to the harbormaster. Danny Quist had not been sick a day in his life except the day after the festival, and that had been earned honestly.

Tomas zipped the jacket tighter over the logbook and headed for the docks.

* * *

Cape Reckoning's harbor in the rain was a gray-on-gray painting with boats in it. The advance ship — the *Meridian Grace*, all white superstructure and new money — sat at the deep-water berth like a wedding cake at a fish fry. Her crew moved around the deck with the crisp efficiency of people who were paid well enough to be crisp.

Varga's operation had been buying goodwill for four days now, and it was working, and Tomas hated how well it was working. Cash for stories. Retainers for "local knowledge." Two of the fishing crews had already signed on as guides. The harbormaster had a new radio. Even Dot had taken money — though Dot, to her credit, had charged them forty dollars for a pot of coffee and told them it was a heritage rate.

Tomas found Danny Quist exactly where he expected: not at the docks, but at the back table of the Galley, nursing a cocoa at ten in the morning, looking like a man rehearsing a confession in front of a mirror.

Danny was big the way a mastiff is big — all of it softness and apology. He'd grown up three houses down from Tomas, had once cried at the funeral of a crab, and currently held the record for most consecutive years voted Cape Reckoning's worst liar, an honor awarded annually at the fall fair and defended fiercely.

Tomas slid into the seat across from him. Danny jumped hard enough to slosh cocoa.

"Tomas! Hey. Hi. I'm sick."

"You look great."

"I'm very sick. Inside-sick."

"Danny." Tomas kept his voice low and even, the voice he used on engines that had started making a sound they shouldn't be making. "Somebody went through Freya's hangar last night. Tore it apart. Burned her grandfather's chart."

Danny's face did something complicated — guilt and fear fighting for the wheel, fear winning on points. His eyes went to the window, toward the harbor, toward the white ship.

"Did you see anything?" Tomas pressed gently. "Last night? On the dock road?"

"I was checking a line."

"Danny."

"A line," Danny repeated, with the conviction of a man reading words off a card handed to him by someone scarier.

Tomas leaned back. He could push. He could probably get the whole story out of Danny in four minutes; he'd gotten worse confessions out of him faster. But pushing Danny would get Danny hurt, and whoever had put Danny up to this — because somebody had, because Danny couldn't plan a picnic, let alone a break-in — would still be standing on that white ship, unbothered, holding the information that mattered.

So instead Tomas did the hardest thing he'd done all week, harder even than seeing the burned chart: he softened.

"Hey," he said. "Whatever it is. Whoever talked to you. I'm not the constable, and I'm not them. I'm just the guy who fixes planes. And right now my best friend's family history is in pieces on her floor, and I'd like to know who's afraid enough of a dead man's handwriting to burn it."

Danny stared into his cocoa for a long moment. Then, barely audible:

"They knew things, Tomas."

"Who did?"

"The woman. Varga's fixer — the one with the accent, Rask. She knew about the flights. Old ones. She knew dates." Danny's voice dropped further. "She knew about your dad's boat, Tomas. The *Rosa*. She knew it sank in '89 and she knew where." His eyes were wet now. "You don't tell people things like that unless you already know everything else too. She said if I didn't help, they'd find out things about other people. People I—" He stopped. Swallowed. "People I care about."

Tomas felt the floor tilt slightly under his certainty. Rask. He'd seen her on the dock — tall, gray-eyed, always writing in a little book. He'd assumed clerk. Apparently she was the kind of clerk who collected a town's secrets like specimens.

"What did she have you do?" Tomas asked quietly.

"Just unlock the side door and leave it. That's all, I swear. Somebody else went in after. I don't know who. She gave me five hundred dollars and I haven't spent it, I was going to give it back, I was going to—"

"Danny." Tomas reached across and put a hand over the big man's wrist. "Listen to me. You're going to keep that money. You're going to stay sick for a few days. And you're going to tell Constable Okafor everything you just told me, today, before Rask decides you're a loose thread instead of a useful one. Can you do that?"

Danny nodded, miserable and relieved in equal measure.

"And Danny? If anyone asks — and someone will — you never talked to me."

"I never talk to anybody," Danny said, with the first honest pride Tomas had seen in him all morning.

* * *

Freya landed at noon, in weather that was trying to decide whether to become the monsoon early. Tomas watched the *Pelican* come in through the low cloud, wheels touching the strip with the softness he'd taught her tires to expect, and felt the strange double weight of what he carried: the logbook against his chest, and the knowledge of what had happened to her copy.

She climbed out, saw his face, and stopped halfway to the hangar.

"Tomas."

"Somebody hit the hangar last night."

She didn't run. That was the thing about Freya that people mistook for coldness — she went very still and very quiet, and the stillness was louder than shouting. She walked the rest of the way, stepped through the broken doorway, and stood in the middle of the ruin with her hands in her jacket pockets, turning slowly, taking inventory.

"The chart," she said finally.

"Burned. Torn. Both." He watched her face. "The original's safe. It was in the plane." He drew the logbook out of his jacket and held it out. "Behind the emergency kit. Which, for the record, is a terrible hiding place, and I've known about it since Tuesday."

For a moment something flickered across her face that might have been a laugh, strangled at birth. She took the logbook and held it against her sternum the way he'd been carrying it, and Tomas understood that she was holding more than paper. She was holding her grandfather, and forty years of silence, and the growing suspicion that the silence had been worth killing for.

"Varga?" she said.

"Her fixer, Rask, set it up. Used Danny Quist to get the door open." He told her the rest — the threats, the secrets Rask had already harvested, the five hundred dollars sitting unspent in Danny's pocket like a stone. Freya listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of terrifying.

"So they know about the flights," she said at last. "They know the logbook exists. They didn't find it." She looked down at the cracked leather cover. "But they will look again. Harder. And next time they'll bring someone who knows airplanes."

"Next time they'll bring someone who has to go through me," Tomas said.

"Tomas—"

"Don't 'Tomas' me. Eleven years I've kept that plane flying. Nobody touches her. Nobody touches you." He said it plainly, without drama, because drama was Freya's department and he was strictly maintenance. "Besides," he added, because the moment had gotten heavy and heavy moments made him itch, "they tore apart my tool wall. My torque wrenches are on the floor like common screwdrivers. There are lines, Freya."

Something loosened in her shoulders — a millimeter, maybe two. "We should report it."

"Already did. Okafor's asking around. Danny's giving his statement this afternoon." He paused. "There's more. Rask knew about my father's boat. Where it sank. Things nobody living talks about." He watched that land. "She's been collecting us, Freya. The whole town. Every secret we ever buried, filed away in a little book. We spent fifty years being too small and too wet for anyone to bother with, and now—"

"Now we're worth knowing," Freya finished quietly.

"Yeah." He looked out through the broken doorway at the gray harbor, at the white ship riding at anchor, patient as a spider. "Invisibility's officially over."

Freya opened the logbook right there, standing in the wreckage of her hangar, rain ticking on the tin roof. She turned pages until she found the smudged coordinates, the flight that never officially happened, and Tomas watched her lips move as she worked through something — a calculation, or a memory, or both.

Then she went still in that particular way of hers, and said:

"Tomas. Get the tide tables."

"Why? What is it?"

She looked up, and her eyes had the light in them that preceded either brilliance or a hospital visit. "The survey team's chart marked six dates. Six tides. And my grandfather's smudged coordinates line up with a stretch of coast that only opens up at extreme low water." She tapped the page. "Someone's been counting down to something for forty years, and I think we just figured out what they're counting toward."

Outside, the rain thickened, and far out past the point, almost lost in the gray, the lights of the salvage fleet's next ship came up over the horizon — two days early.
