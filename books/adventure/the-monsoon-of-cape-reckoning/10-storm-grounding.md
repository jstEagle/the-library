## 10. Storm Grounding

The monsoon arrived the way Tomas had promised it would—without apology and without a schedule anyone could argue with.

Freya woke to the sound of rain hitting the hangar roof like a fist demanding entry, and by the time she'd pulled on her boots and crossed the yard to the office, the wind sock at Cape Reckoning's airstrip was standing straight out horizontal, quivering like a dog that wanted very badly to be somewhere else.

"No," she said to it.

The sock said nothing, because socks are wiser than pilots.

She checked the barometer anyway, out of habit, then the coastal forecast, then the window, as if the sky might reconsider. It wouldn't. This was the big one—the first true monsoon front of the season, the kind that sat down over the interior for a day or two and turned every strip from here to the mountains into a mud bath with delusions of being a lake. Nobody was flying. Nobody sane, anyway.

Which meant Varga's fleet was grounded too.

That thought should have comforted her. Instead it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, because a grounded fleet wasn't a stopped fleet—it was a fleet with nothing to do but think, plan, and buy drinks for dockhands.

Tomas was already in the office when she came in, hunched over the radio with a mug of coffee that had clearly been poured an hour ago and forgotten. He looked up when she entered, and his face did something complicated.

"Morning," he said. "Bad news, worse news."

"Give me the bad news first."

"That *is* the bad news. The worse news is you're going to want coffee before I tell you."

"Tomas."

"Varga's ship moved in the night." He tapped the radio. "Coast station logged them repositioning at three this morning—in *that*." He jerked his thumb at the window, where the rain was coming down hard enough to make the world look like a watercolor left out in a storm. "They shifted anchorage around the point. Closer to the lee side. Somebody over there knows exactly what they're doing, and they're not wasting a grounding day sitting on their hands."

Freya stood at the window and watched the rain hammer the strip. Somewhere under all that gray water, four days out by her count now—three, if you counted the way sailors counted—the tide chart's sixth date waited like a held breath.

"They're getting ready," she said.

"Everybody's getting ready." Tomas pushed the cold coffee toward her. "Question is whether we're getting ready faster."

* * *

The day refused to pass. That was the thing about storm groundings nobody warned you about—you didn't get a rest, you got a waiting room. Freya reorganized the medicine locker twice, inventoried the spare parts, read the same page of a paperback novel four times without retaining a single word, and finally gave up and walked down to the harbor through rain so thick it felt less like weather and more like an opinion.

The town had gone strange. Storm days usually brought Cape Reckoning together—board games in the hotel lobby, someone's cousin playing guitar badly, the whole population compressed into a few warm rooms while the sky tried to wash the coast off the map. But today there was a new center of gravity. Varga's advance ship rode at anchor in the lee of the point, lights burning through the murk, and half the town seemed oriented toward it like iron filings toward a magnet.

In the hotel dining room, Freya found the source: Varga herself, holding court at the corner table, buying the room's goodwill one round at a time. She'd shed the salvage-company polish she wore on the docks—no clipboard, no crew, no entourage. Just a woman in a rolled-sleeve shirt, laughing at something old Petter the harbormaster had said, looking for all the world like a guest rather than an occupying force.

Varga saw her across the room. Of course she did. The woman missed nothing; Freya had learned that much already.

"Hartwell." Varga raised her glass an inch. "Come sit. Your town tells better stories when they've had two drinks, and I'm building quite the collection."

"I'll bet you are."

"Sit," Varga said again, and there was nothing commanding in it—it was almost gentle—but Freya found herself pulling out the chair anyway, which annoyed her more than an order would have.

Dinner at the hotel was fish stew and bread that Maren's grandniece baked, and it was good, and Freya ate it in stubborn silence while Varga talked easily about everything and nothing: shipping lanes, the price of aviation fuel, the particular treachery of the reef off the point. It took Freya ten minutes to realize she was being handled—not interrogated, just *warmed*, the way you'd warm an engine before asking it to do something difficult.

"You moved your ship last night," Freya said finally, setting down her spoon. "In the teeth of this front."

"We did."

"To the lee of the point."

"Where else would a sensible captain put a ship?" Varga smiled. "You've been watching us."

"Somebody has to."

"Mm." Varga turned her glass slowly on the tablecloth, a full rotation, thoughtful. "You know what I did before salvage? Eleven years running supply flights out of the northern archipelagos. Weather like this grounded me half the season. I used to hate it—sitting ashore while the work piled up." Her eyes came up. "Then I learned what the smart ones learn. A grounding isn't lost time. It's the only time everyone's in the same room."

"And what work piles up in your room, Captain?"

"The honest kind." Varga leaned back. "So let's be honest. You flew a search grid for my missing survey team. You found things outside the grid—I know you did, because my people watch the water and the water watches back. And now you're counting tides. Don't look surprised; harbormasters talk, and yours talks even more after two drinks."

Freya kept her face still, which took effort. "If you already know everything, why buy me dinner?"

"Because knowing and understanding aren't the same animal." Varga set the glass down. "I grew up in a place like this. Smaller. Poorer. The sea gave and the government took, and everybody survived by keeping their heads down and their business invisible. I got out by being useful to people with money, and I told myself that was different from selling out because I sent money home." She paused. "It took me twenty years to admit those were the same sentence."

The rain filled the pause, drumming on the roof like applause from a distant crowd.

"Why are you telling me this?" Freya asked quietly.

"Because tomorrow I go back to being your problem, and tonight I'd rather be honest with you once." Varga's voice stayed level, easy, but something underneath it had gone taut as wire. "There's a wreck off this coast. My company believes it's significant. There are documents involved—old ones—and my employers care about documents more than gold, which surprises people. And there is a family on this coast whose name keeps surfacing around that wreck like a body that won't stay down. Hartwell."

The name landed between them on the tablecloth.

"I loved my grandfather," Freya said. "Whatever you think you know about him—"

"I don't think anything. That's the point." Varga spread her hands. "I'm offering you the chance to tell me. Because here's my experience, Hartwell: secrets rot. They don't protect anybody. They just decide who pays later. I paid late and I paid double, and I have the scar tissue to prove it."

"And if I told you nothing was there? No wreck, no documents, no secret?"

Varga studied her for a long moment, and Freya watched her do the arithmetic behind her eyes—the same arithmetic Freya had been doing for days, weighing what a person knew against what a person could afford to believe.

"Then I'd say you're a better liar than I am," Varga said, "and I'd still respect you. But I'd also keep digging, because I've never once found nothing where everybody worked so hard to find nothing." She signaled the waitress for another round. "Eat your stew. It's good, and tomorrow we're adversaries again."

Freya ate her stew. It was good.

* * *

She walked home through the rain afterward, hood up, boots squelching, and found Tomas waiting in the hangar with a lantern and the expression of a man who had rehearsed a lecture and intended to deliver it.

"Well?" he said.

"She bought me dinner."

"Freya."

"And she told me her life story, and she asked me straight out about Grandfather, and I told her nothing, and she knew I was telling her nothing, and—" Freya stopped, dripping on the concrete, and heard herself finish, quieter, "—and I liked her, Tomas. That's the worst part. She's not a monster. She's *me*, thirty years on, if I'd taken the money instead of the sky."

Tomas was quiet for a moment. Rain hammered the roof. The lantern threw their shadows huge against the Cessna's flank, where the original logbook rode hidden beneath the floor paneling, closer to Freya right now than her own heartbeat.

"That's how they get you," Tomas said at last. "Not with threats. With recognition." He handed her a dry towel from the workbench. "My mother always said the devil doesn't knock. He sits down at your table and orders what you order."

"She said something else, too." Freya scrubbed the towel through her hair. "She said secrets rot. That they don't protect anybody—they just decide who pays later." She looked at the plane, at the floor panel, at the shape of the silence her grandfather had maintained for fifty years. "And the horrible thing is I couldn't argue with her. I've been carrying his silence for a week and it's already heavier than any cargo I've ever flown."

"So what do we do?"

Freya didn't answer right away. Outside, through the hangar's open door, the storm raged on over the black harbor, and beyond it, invisible, Varga's fleet rode at anchor in the lee of the point—repositioned, patient, three tides from the answer.

"Tomorrow," she said finally, "when this front lifts, I'm flying east. There's a route in the survey team's field notes I haven't run yet—a line heading inland, away from the wreck, away from everything. If they ran from something instead of toward it, that flight path will show it." She met Tomas's eyes. "And if I'm right, then whatever my grandfather hid, and whatever Varga wants, there are still two people out there who might be alive. Everything else can wait until I know that."

Tomas nodded slowly. "Everything else," he repeated, in the tone of a man who fully expected everything else to refuse to wait.

As if the coast had been listening—as if it usually was—the radio on the workbench crackled to life through the static. Coast station, broadcasting to all vessels: a report, garbled by weather, of flares sighted overnight far upriver, inland, in country where no boat could go.

Freya and Tomas looked at each other across the lantern light.

"Inland," she breathed. "Nobody goes inland."

The rain came down harder, and somewhere upriver, in the dark, somebody was still alive.
