## 11. The Survey Team's Last Flight

The rain had let up to a fine gray drizzle by the time Freya spread the maps across her kitchen table, and she took that as a personal favor from the sky. Small mercies. The monsoon had been generous with everything else—rain, wind, and a low-grade headache that lived behind her eyes like an unpaid tenant.

She weighted the corners with her coffee mug, a tin of Tomas's biscuits, and the brass lamp her grandfather had used for exactly this kind of work. The tide chart from the survey camp lay in the center, its hand-drawn coastline still bothering her the way a splinter bothers a thumb. She'd traced it three times now against every chart the government published for the coast between Cape Reckoning and the Serpent Islands. It matched none of them.

But it matched his logbook. Page forty-one, the smudged coordinates, the flight he'd never filed. Her grandfather had drawn that coastline—or someone who'd seen what he'd seen had drawn it—and either way, the survey team had been looking at the same ghost she was.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the kitchen, to the maps, to nobody. "Let's pretend you're competent."

She started with what was known. The survey team—four people, government contract, heritage assessment—had flown into Cape Reckoning on the ninth. She remembered it because she'd been on the strip when their chartered floatplane came in, and one of them, a tall woman with sunburned cheekbones and a laugh like a door hinge, had asked her where to get decent coffee. Freya had told her the truth: nowhere. The woman had bought the bad coffee anyway and drunk it without complaint, which Freya had privately rated as excellent field discipline.

Amara Osei. She'd read the name off the manifest of supplies they'd loaded. Geologist, or something adjacent to geologist. The kind of person who looked at a landscape and saw paperwork in it.

From Cape Reckoning, the team's filed itinerary said they were working a grid along the coast north of the point. That was the official story, and official stories, in Freya's experience, were usually true in the way that a fence is true—it tells you exactly where somebody didn't want you to go.

So she ignored it.

Instead she worked backward from the camp. She and Tomas had hiked out to the glint site two days ago, and she'd spent those two days not sleeping and doing arithmetic instead, which was its own form of not sleeping. The camp sat eleven kilometers inland from the coast, up a shallow river valley that no sane surveyor would choose for coastal work. Too far from the water to sample tides efficiently. Too sheltered to get good barometric readings. Wrong in every way except one: it was hidden. From the air, tucked under the ridge line, you could stand in that clearing and be functionally invisible unless you already knew where to look.

Freya knew about invisible places. Her whole childhood had been built inside one.

She laid a sheet of tracing paper over the regional chart and began plotting. Not the route the team had *filed*—the route their boots had actually taken, reconstructed from the small honest evidence people leave behind whether they mean to or not.

Boot prints in the mud at the creek crossing, heading upstream, not down. Cigarette ends—two brands, so at least two smokers—clustered on the east bank, facing inland. A ration wrapper wedged in alder roots at the second bend, weathered just enough to say *days, not weeks*. And the thing that had been nagging at her since the hike: the camp itself. Four tents, but only three bedrolls showed real use. The fourth tent had been storage, and the storage had been organized—rope coiled, crates stacked, the kind of order you impose when you expect to come back for things.

People who vanish don't pack neatly for a return trip. People who are running do.

"They left," she said slowly, testing the shape of it. "They weren't taken. They *left*."

And they'd left in a hurry—that part was written in the camp too, once you knew how to read it. Food untouched in the cool box, because food is heavy and fear makes you travel light. The radio left behind, which had seemed insane until she thought about it for ten straight minutes at three in the morning: a radio transmits. A radio can be triangulated. If you're running from someone who has better equipment than you do, you leave the radio, and you walk, and you hope the rain scrubs your trail faster than their dogs—or their drones, or whatever else they had—could follow it.

Running from whom? The government had sent her to find them. But governments were big, clumsy animals with many mouths, and one mouth could send a search pilot while another sent something else entirely. She thought of the liaison, Mr. Okonkwo, with his careful smile and his warning to stay clear of certain coordinates. *Certain coordinates.* He'd handed her a search grid with a hole in it and expected her to fly the hole like a good little instrument.

She'd logged the glint outside the grid instead. And now here she was, four days later, drinking cold coffee and drawing lines toward the exact place she'd been told not to look, feeling less like a rebel and more like the family resemblance finally kicking in.

Her grandfather would have approved. That was the uncomfortable part. Two weeks ago she'd have found that thought warm. Now it came with a chill attached, because she was starting to understand that his silences hadn't been accidents. They'd been architecture.

She bent over the tracing paper and drew the line.

Upstream from the camp, following the creek to where it steepened, then cutting southwest over the saddle—the low shoulder of the ridge, the only crossing that wouldn't leave a party of four exhausted and exposed. From the saddle, the terrain funneled you, whether you liked it or not, down toward the coast at the base of the point. Toward the sea caves country. Toward the smudged coordinates on page forty-one.

Toward the wreck that shouldn't exist.

The line on her paper ended at the same stretch of coastline as the hand-drawn chart in the surveyors' tent. Eleven days of walking, maybe twelve with injured members, in monsoon conditions. It fit. God help her, it fit like a key.

Which raised the question she'd been circling all morning: if the team had walked *to* the wreck, then they hadn't stumbled onto it. They'd gone looking. A heritage assessment team, carrying tide charts marked with six dates, marching inland toward a colonial-era shipwreck that appeared on no register anywhere. Somebody had given them that chart. Somebody had told them where to walk.

Or they'd found something in the archives—a fragment, a rumor, a smudged logbook entry in some other family's attic—and come out here to chase it themselves.

Freya sat back and rubbed her eyes. Outside, the drizzle ticked against the window like a patient finger. Somewhere down at the harbor, Varga's advance ship sat at anchor, buying drinks and goodwill, asking her neighbors gentle questions about old Hartwell flights. Six tides. The number kept ticking in her head alongside the rain.

*Focus,* she told herself. One problem at a time. Right now the problem was four missing people, and one of them had laughed like a door hinge and drunk terrible coffee without complaint.

* * *

Tomas arrived at noon, letting himself in with the ease of twenty years of friendship, dripping on her floor and immediately eating the biscuits she'd been using as a map weight.

"You look terrible," he said fondly.

"I've been reconstructing a flight path."

"With maps? On a table?" He peered at the tracing paper, chewing. "That's not flying, that's homework."

"It's how my grandfather did half his best work. The flying was just the commute." She pulled the paper free and smoothed it flat. "Look. Tell me what you see."

Tomas studied it the way he studied engines—with suspicion first, understanding second. His finger traced the line from the camp, up the creek, over the saddle, down to the coast. He frowned.

"That's not their survey area."

"No."

"That's the—" He stopped. His jaw did the thing it did when he was deciding whether to say the sensible thing or the true thing. With Tomas, the sensible thing usually lost. "That's where your grandfather's smudged coordinates point."

"Yes."

"And the survey team walked there. On purpose." He set down the biscuit. That, more than anything, told Freya how seriously he was taking this. Tomas did not abandon biscuits lightly. "Freya. People don't walk eleven klicks into monsoon country for a shipwreck. Shipwrecks are worth money, sure, but not *boots-on-the-ground-in-that-weather* money. Not leave-your-radio-behind money."

"Not unless what's on the wreck isn't money," Freya said.

Tomas looked at her. Rain dripped off his collar onto the map, right across the saddle of the ridge, blurring her neat pencil line—which felt, she thought, about right. Nothing about this stayed neat for long.

"What else is there?"

"Paper," she said. "Cargo manifests. Bills of lading. Ownership documents." She'd been turning it over since Maren's visit, since the old woman's sharp voice had said *he hid it so no one would have to choose*. "A wreck full of gold is a treasure. A wreck full of *documents* is a weapon. You can steal treasure from anyone. But documents—" she tapped the coastline, the smudged coordinates, the six dates marching toward the sixth tide— "documents tell you who owns what. And who never owned it at all."

Tomas was quiet for a long moment. Then: "You want to go up there. Follow their line."

"I want to follow their line *from the air* first. The saddle crossing should be visible if the cloud lifts this afternoon. Boot trail, cut brush, anything. If they went over it, I'll see it." She stood and started gathering gear out of habit—flight bag, thermos, the battered binoculars her grandfather had carried for thirty years. "And if I see it, then tomorrow we walk it."

"*We*," Tomas repeated, but he was already checking the zipper on his own jacket, because there had never really been a version of this conversation where she went alone and he stayed home. Twenty years of friendship ran on rails like that. "If the cloud lifts. If. The barometer's been falling since dawn like it owes someone money."

"Then we'll be fast."

"We're always fast. That's the problem. Fast is how planes end up in trees." But he was smiling, sort of, the way he smiled when he was scared for her and had decided to convert it into sarcasm rather than say the actual words. He'd been converting it for twenty years. She loved him for the efficiency of it.

* * *

The cloud did lift—not much, and not for long, but enough. By three o'clock there was a ragged ceiling at eight hundred feet over the ridges, and Freya had the Cessna off the strip and into the gray, Tomas beside her navigating with a paper map and a pencil stub because he distrusted GPS on principle, the way other men distrusted politicians.

The valley was running brown and loud beneath them, the creek swollen with rain, and she flew the line she'd drawn on her kitchen table as though the pencil mark were painted on the earth. Upstream past the camp clearing—empty, dark tents sagging with water, a flag of mud-stained tarp flapping from one pole. Past the creek crossing where the boot prints had been; the prints were gone now, drowned, but the alders still held their scars. Up the steepening gradient, the trees closing in, the ceiling pressing down until she was flying between the ridges like a bird in a corridor.

"There." Tomas pointed. "Saddle. Ten o'clock."

She saw it. Even through the streaming windshield she saw it, and her heart did something complicated in her chest.

The saddle crossing was a shallow green notch in the ridge line, and slashed across its near slope was a scar of broken brush—fresh, pale wood showing wet through the rain, a wound in the hillside wide enough for four people moving in single file, dragging something heavy. Cut branches lay in a rough line downslope, deliberately dropped. Deliberately. Nobody drops cut brush in a tidy line by accident. That was trail-marking. That was *we came this way, follow us*—or *we came this way, please find us*, and Freya couldn't tell which, and both possibilities made her throat tight.

"They went over," she said. Her voice sounded strange in the headset, too loud. "Tomas, they went over the saddle. On foot. In this."

"Or were driven over," Tomas said quietly. But he was leaning forward against his harness, squinting, and his hand had drifted to the binoculars between the seats.

She brought the plane around for a second pass, lower, watching her altimeter and the treetops with the divided attention of every bush pilot who ever lived. On the far side of the saddle the slope fell away toward the coast, and the brush scar continued—fainter now, interrupted by slides and deadfall, but there, stitching downhill like a loose thread someone had tried to hide and failed.

Halfway down, the thread stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. At a shelf of rock above a ravine, the scar simply ended, and below it the forest was unbroken green all the way to the gray line of the sea.

"They stopped there," Tomas said. "Why would they stop there?"

"Because they didn't stop," Freya said slowly. "Because they went to ground. Caves." She thought of the coastline below the point, the limestone country, the sea caves Maren had mentioned once, decades ago, in the offhand way old pilots mentioned things they'd sworn not to mention. "There's karst through that whole headland. Holes in the rock everywhere. You could hide a regiment in there and the rain would erase every track within an hour."

"So they're alive." Tomas said it carefully, the way you handle something fragile, testing whether it holds weight. "Four people, hiding in sea caves, eleven days after vanishing from a camp with their food still in the cooler."

"Three bedrolls," Freya reminded him. "Maybe fewer than four. Maybe hurt." She banked away from the ridge before the cloud could close the corridor behind her, and the plane slid out over the valley, rain hammering the wings again, the world going soft and gray. "Eleven days, Tomas. Cold, wet, probably injured, hiding from whoever scared them out of their own camp. That's not survival odds. That's a countdown."

"Then we'd better be quick." He was quiet a moment, and then, in the tone of a man handing over something he'd been holding onto: "I checked the hangar this morning before I came up. Everything's where you left it. Logbook included."

"Good."

"Varga's people have been asking around about old Hartwell flights. Denny at the fuel dock says the captain herself asked about your grandfather. By name." He turned and looked at her, rain-light flickering across his face. "Whatever your family buried out here, Freya, it's not buried anymore. It's got a salvage fleet and a government contract digging at it. The only question left is who gets there first, and what they do when they arrive."

Freya flew on through the rain, the saddle falling away behind them, and let that settle. Her grandfather's silence, cracked open. A survey team running blind toward the same secret. A woman named Varga buying her town drink by drink. And somewhere under the point, in the dark and the salt and the cold, people who had chosen the caves over the radio—people who might still be breathing, waiting for the tide chart's sixth date, waiting for anyone.

Waiting, maybe, for a bush pilot with a family talent for finding invisible places.

Back at the hangar, while Tomas fueled and she dried the plane, she went through the survey supply manifest one more time—the one she'd helped load on the ninth, the one she'd read without reading. And there it was, in item fourteen, so ordinary she'd skimmed past it a dozen times: *field notebooks, waterproof, qty 6.*

Six notebooks. Government property, serial-numbered, itemized. If the team had scattered—if they'd run in ones and twos, dropping weight as they went—then somewhere along that brush-scarred line, in the mud or the creeks or the rocks above the ravine, there might be pages. Pages describing what they'd found. What had frightened them. What *the hold* contained, and why a geologist with a door-hinge laugh had walked away from her own camp and left her radio behind.

Tomorrow, she and Tomas would walk the line. Every meter of it, eyes down, the way her grandfather must have walked a hundred shorelines looking for what the sea gave back.

She just hoped they'd find the notebook before the sixth tide found the wreck—and before Varga's fleet found everything else.

Outside the hangar, the rain thickened, and the barometer needle leaned into the red like a runner into a final stretch.
