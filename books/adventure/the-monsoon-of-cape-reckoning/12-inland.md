## 12. Inland

The river had a name on the maps—Kestrel Water—and another name among the people who actually lived beside it, which was simply "the river," because when something is the only one of its kind for eighty miles, it doesn't need to compete.

Freya stood at its edge at first light and watched the water run brown and fast with last night's rain. Somewhere up that current, if Amara Okafor's field notebook was right, two people were walking toward the coast caves. Two people who had been running for days from men with radios and rifles and a salvage company's blessing.

"Tell me again why we're doing this on foot," Tomas said behind her. He had his pack straps cinched so tight he looked like a man being slowly compressed into a smaller, angrier version of himself. "I have a plane. You have a plane. Planes are famous for going places quickly."

"The river bends east past the falls. The notebook says they cut straight north along the ridge." Freya tapped the photocopied page she'd wedged into a plastic sleeve in her breast pocket. "If we follow the water we lose half a day. If we take the ridge we gain it back."

"And if we take the ridge in this weather, we fall off it."

"That's why I packed the rope."

"You packed *rope*?"

"I also packed the good coffee. Drink some before you argue with me further."

Tomas grumbled, but he drank the coffee, and by the time the sun cleared the treeline they were climbing, single file, through forest so wet it seemed less like trees than like a green idea of trees, everything dripping, everything breathing mist. The monsoon hadn't fully arrived yet—it was still out over the shelf sea, gathering itself like a fist—but its outriders came through every afternoon now: squalls that arrived without appointment, dumped their load, and left the air feeling wrung out and guilty.

They made good time until midmorning, which was when the rain found them.

* * *

It came sideways off the ridge, warm and relentless, the kind of rain that didn't fall so much as relocate. Within twenty minutes Freya couldn't tell where her sweat ended and the sky began. She kept her eyes on Tomas's orange pack ahead of her and counted her steps the way her grandfather had taught her—*hundred steps, look up; hundred steps, look up*—because the wilderness forgave nothing, but it did respect arithmetic.

At the top of the second switchback Tomas stopped and held up a hand.

"What?" Freya came alongside him, breath ragged.

He pointed down through the trees. Below them, half-hidden under a leaning slab of granite, was a structure. Not a structure exactly—a suggestion of one. A lean-to of driftwood poles and torn tarpaulin, lashed with what looked like parachute cord, tucked into the hillside like a secret someone had whispered to the mountain.

The notebook had described a shelter. *Built lean-to above the second stream crossing. Cold nights. R. says keep moving.*

"Days cold," Freya said quietly. She'd read that line a dozen times since dawn. *Cold nights.* Written in a hand that started neat and got progressively more cramped as the pages went on, as though the writer were fighting both the cold and the fading light.

They went down to it carefully. Inside, the lean-to was dry-ish and smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool. A ring of stones held the ashes of a fire, three days dead at least. There was a tin cup. A strip of dried fish, gone hard as shingle. And pinned to the center pole with a fishhook, so deliberately placed it could only be a message, was a page torn from a survey ledger.

Tomas unhooked it with hands that weren't quite steady. He read it once, then handed it to Freya without a word.

The writing was Amara's. It said:

*If anyone from Cape Reckoning finds this — we're alive. Both of us. R.'s ankle is bad but he walks. We're making for the caves below the point. Don't trust the liaison. Don't trust the fleet. Trust the tide tables. — A.O.*

Freya read it twice. Then a third time, because the third reading was the one where her eyes stopped snagging on the words and let the meaning all the way in.

"They're alive," she said. It came out strange, half laugh, half prayer. "Tomas. They're *alive*, and they're moving, and they're—" She stopped, because the rest of it landed then, the way heavy things land. "They're heading for the caves. The ones under the point. The ones I've flown over my whole life without ever once wondering what was underneath them."

"Your grandfather flew over them too," Tomas said. Gently, but he said it.

"Yes," Freya said. "He did."

She stood there a moment longer, rain drumming the tarpaulin, looking at the cold fire ring. Somebody had sat here three nights ago, maybe four, hurt and hunted and hundreds of miles from anywhere that loved them, and they had built a fire anyway. Had hung a message on a fishhook anyway. Had believed, against every reasonable expectation, that somebody from Cape Reckoning might come looking.

"Well," Freya said, and stuffed the page into her pocket next to the notebook copy. "We should probably hurry."

* * *

They didn't hurry, not really. Nobody hurries on a rain-slicked ridge with forty pounds of gear and a storm coming in behind them like a debt collector. But they moved faster than comfort allowed, and by early afternoon the terrain began to change around them in ways that told Freya they were winning the race against the weather even if they were losing the race against their own legs.

The forest thinned. The trees gave way to wind-scoured scrub, and the scrub gave way to bare granite streaked white with old bird droppings, and beyond it, far below, the coast opened up—the long gray sweep of it, the point thrusting out into the sea like a jaw, and beneath the point, invisible from this height but perfectly known to her now, the caves.

Tomas caught up to her at the overlook, wheezing, and followed her gaze.

"There," he said.

"I see it."

"No, I mean *there*. Look at the beach. At the base of the point."

Freya looked. At first she saw only what she always saw: sand, kelp lines, the tidewrack the sea leaves behind like receipts. Then she saw what Tomas had seen.

Footprints.

Even from the overlook they were visible—a wandering, staggering line of prints in the wet sand above the high-tide mark, heading south along the shore toward the dark mouths of the caves. Fresh. The edges hadn't slumped yet, hadn't been erased by wind or spray. Hours old, not days.

"Two sets," Tomas said. He had binoculars up. "One favoring the left leg. They stopped there—" he pointed to a huddle of rocks "—sat down a while. Then kept going."

Freya took the binoculars. Through the lenses the footprints leapt close, heartbreakingly human, each one a small flooded valley. At the place where they'd rested there was a pressed rectangle in the sand where someone had lain down, and beside it, tiny and absurd, a shape she recognized.

A tin cup. The mate to the one in the lean-to. Left behind on purpose, sitting upright, catching rainwater.

"It's a trail," Freya said. "They're leaving us a trail. Cup at the camp, cup here—they knew anyone hunting them would be watching for signs of flight, so they made signs instead. Signs for friends." Her throat tightened. "Amara Okafor has been thinking about this for days. Walking wounded, starving probably, hunted—and she's been *thinking about us*."

"She's been thinking about the tide tables," Tomas corrected. "That's what scares me. 'Trust the tide tables.' Freya, the sixth tide is day after tomorrow. Whatever they know about those caves, they know it involves the water coming in."

Freya lowered the binoculars. Out past the point, the sea had gone an ugly green-gray, and the horizon had developed that soft, smudged look that meant the monsoon's main body was finally making landfall. Behind them, inland, the air had the bruised stillness of a held breath.

"We can make the caves before dark if we descend now," she said. "Take the gully down to the beach and walk in at low water."

"And if the tide turns while we're inside?"

"Then we'll have something in common with everyone else in this story."

Tomas gave her a look that contained, in roughly equal measure, exasperation, loyalty, and the particular despair of a mechanic whose pilot keeps treating physics as a suggestion. Then he shrugged the pack higher on his shoulders and started down toward the gully.

"You know," he called back over the wind, "when you hired me to fix your engine, nobody mentioned cave diving."

"I didn't hire you. You just showed up."

"And I've regretted it daily for eleven years!"

But he was smiling when he said it, or would have been if the rain hadn't been in his teeth, and Freya followed him down into the gully with the notebook page against her chest and her heart going like a twin-prop at full throttle.

Because here was the thing she couldn't have explained to anyone, least of all herself: an hour ago she had been exhausted in the ordinary way, the way of blistered heels and wet socks and a body that wanted very much to lie down. Now she was exhausted in a completely different way—the way of a person who has been carrying a question for weeks and has just felt it begin, at long last, to answer.

Someone was alive. Someone was leaving cups in the sand like breadcrumbs, trusting the tide tables, trusting *her*, without knowing her name, without knowing anything except that Cape Reckoning was a town where people still came looking.

The rain thickened. The light went bronze and strange. Far below, the caves waited in the cliff face with their dark mouths open, and between Freya and those mouths lay four hundred feet of slick granite descent, a rising sea, and whatever truth had been waiting under the point since before either of their grandfathers was born.

"Freya," Tomas said suddenly, stopping dead on the slope.

"What?"

He was staring back the way they'd come, up the ridge, into the wall of weather. For a moment she thought he'd seen the storm front—and then she saw it too, threaded through the gray: a flicker of orange. High up. Moving.

"Not rescue orange," Tomas said quietly. "Fleet orange. That's Varga's survey gear."

Freya's stomach dropped through the wet granite and kept going. The salvage crews weren't waiting offshore anymore. They were on the ridge, behind them, between them and home—and whoever wore that orange had almost certainly found the lean-to too.

Had almost certainly found the message on the fishhook.

And the message said exactly where the survivors were going.

"Down," Freya said. "Now. We walk in the waterline so the tide takes our tracks, and we pray to whatever god looks after stubborn people that low water holds another six hours."

They went down fast after that, faster than was wise, hands and boots and occasionally seats, the rain hammering, the sea booming against the point like something enormous turning over in its sleep. And as Freya dropped the last few feet onto the wet sand and turned toward the black mouths of the caves, she saw it—there at the nearest entrance, half-erased already by the licking foam—

Fresh footprints. Going in.

And beside the entrance, propped upright against the stone where no wave could miss it, a third tin cup, glinting in the dying light like a small, defiant bell.

Calling them home.
