## 13. The Caves at Low Tide

The tide went out like a held breath finally released.

Freya had timed it from the almanac and then from her own eyes, standing on the shingle below the point with Tomas beside her, watching the sea peel back from black rock in long silver sheets. By the almanac's reckoning they had four hours of exposed caves. By her own reckoning—watching the way the water kept retreating past where it had any business retreating—they might have five.

"Remind me," Tomas said, shifting his pack on his shoulders, "why we're walking into a hole in the ground that the ocean fills up twice a day."

"Because someone walked out of it recently," Freya said. "And because whoever they are, they've been hiding down there for eleven days, which means they know things we don't."

"That's your whole plan? They know things?"

"It's worked so far." She started down toward the waterline. "You came anyway."

"I always come anyway. It's my defining flaw." He caught up to her, boots crunching on wet stone. "That and I can't let you die alone; you'd never hear the end of it from Maren."

The footprints they'd found at the lean-to were two days old at most, and they'd led them here along the base of the point, to a place Freya had flown over a hundred times without ever once thinking about what lay beneath it. From the air, Cape Reckoning's eastern point was just rock and gull colonies and the white scribble of surf. From sea level, with the tide out, it was something else entirely: a low arch of stone, maybe chest-high, half-hidden behind a curtain of kelp, breathing cold air outward.

She crouched at its mouth and felt that breath on her face. It smelled of salt and stone and, faintly, unmistakably, of woodsmoke.

"Tomas."

"I smell it." He was already unclipping his headlamp. "Somebody's been keeping warm."

They went in bent nearly double, the ceiling scraping close, and then the passage opened like a throat and they could stand. The light swung across walls streaked with mineral green, across tide pools holding their own small skies, across—

"Look at this," Tomas said softly.

Marks on the wall. Not graffiti. A map, drawn in charcoal, careful and repeated, gone over so many times the lines had grooved into the stone. A coastline. And beside it, in smaller strokes, a shape Freya knew in her bones before her brain finished naming it: a hull, broken-backed, ribs open to the sea.

The coastline matched the one in her grandfather's logbook. Matched the one pinned inside the survey team's dead camp. Matched nowhere official anywhere on any chart she'd ever filed.

"They knew," she whispered. "Before they ever got here, they knew what they were looking for."

"Then why hide in a cave?" Tomas asked. "If you find the thing you're surveying for, you radio home and take credit."

"Unless what you found isn't what you were sent to find." Freya ran her fingers near the charcoal, not touching it. "Unless what you found is people who don't want to be found by the same government that sent you."

* * *

The cave system opened inward and upward in ways no chart would ever show, chambers branching off the main passage like rooms in a house built by something patient and enormous. They followed the smoke smell. Twice the floor dropped into tide pools deep enough that they had to edge along the walls, packs held high, and once the passage narrowed so severely that Freya had to exhale everything and turn sideways to squeeze through while Tomas fed her pack through after her like a midwife delivering luggage.

"You know," he said through the gap, voice echoing, "in town they think you're the brave one."

"In town they think *you're* the sensible one. We're both living lies."

The third chamber was different. Warmer. There was a driftwood fire ring, ash still holding heat when Freya dared touch near it, and supplies laid out with the particular neatness of people who have decided order is the last thing they control: tins stacked, a tarp folded into a precise square, socks drying on a line made of fishing leader.

And propped against the wall, a man sat up as their lights hit him, one arm cradled against his chest in a sling improvised from a shirt, a length of pipe held in his good hand like he fully intended to use it.

"Don't," he said. His voice was wrecked, sandpaper over gravel. "Whatever Varga promised you, whatever she's paying—I'm telling you now, I will swing this pipe until the water comes back, and then you'll have drowned for nothing."

Freya raised both hands slowly, lamp beam tilted at the ceiling. "We're not Varga's."

"Everyone says that." But the pipe wavered. He was squinting against the light, and she watched him try to make sense of them—mud-caked, rain-soaked, a big mechanic with a gentle face and a woman who looked, if she was honest with herself, like she hadn't slept properly in a week.

"We're from Cape Reckoning," she said. "I fly out of the strip there. Hartwell Air. My name is Freya Hartwell."

The pipe lowered an inch. Then another inch. The man stared at her with an expression she couldn't read at all, and behind him, from the deeper shadow at the chamber's back, a woman's voice said, quietly, "Let them come closer, Dev. Look at her face. She's got his jaw."

Freya went very still. "I'm sorry—who are—"

"Amara Osei," the woman said, coming forward into the light. She moved carefully, favoring one leg, wrapped above the ankle in dressings that had been changed recently and competently. She was younger than Freya had imagined from the notebook—late thirties, close-cropped hair going grey at the temples, eyes that had done a great deal of watching lately. "Heritage survey team, Ministry designation 4-C. Which officially does not exist anymore, because as far as our department knows, we vanished on the ninth." She looked Freya up and down. "You're Aldous Hartwell's granddaughter."

The name landed in the cave like a dropped stone. "You knew my grandfather?"

"I corresponded with him. Thirty years ago, when I was a graduate student. About a coastline." Amara nodded at the charcoal map on the wall of the first chamber. "He wrote me three letters, and then he stopped writing, and I spent most of my career wondering why. When our team pulled the old colonial shipping records and found a vessel listed as lost off this exact point—the *Reckoning*, 1841, cargo manifest sealed by court order—I wrote to the address in my files, half hoping. An old woman wrote back. Maren. She said Aldous was gone but his granddaughter flew the coast now, and that if I came asking questions I should be prepared for answers nobody up here wanted found."

Tomas made a small sound beside Freya. "Maren sent you."

"Maren pointed us here like a lighthouse," Amara said. "And then—" Her composure flickered, just once, a crack showing the eleven days underneath. "And then we found the wreck. And then we found out who else was looking for it. And then we ran."

* * *

They gave the survivors what they'd carried in—water, the dense seed bars Tomas's mother made that could survive anything short of direct artillery, a proper first-aid kit that made Dev close his eyes with something like prayer. In exchange, sitting around a fire rebuilt small and shielded, they got the story.

"There were six of us," Amara began. "Dev Ranganathan, our geologist. Priya, our diver. Marcus, Jonas, Elena, and me. Standard heritage assessment—coastal erosion zones, sites at risk from the salvage leases. That's what we were told we were assessing." She turned a tin cup slowly in her hands. "Except the lease maps didn't match the heritage registry. Sites marked protected in our files were marked clear in the ones the salvage company was working from. Same coordinates. Different histories."

"Falsified surveys," Freya said.

"Worse than falsified. *Erased.* Whole stretches of coastline where the registry showed generations of seasonal camps, fish weirs, burial grounds—and the salvage company's documents showed nothing at all. Empty coast. Nobody to consult, nobody owed anything." Amara's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "We flagged it internally. Our own ministry liaison told us to stand down and complete the erosion assessment only. So we completed it. And we also, quietly, did our real job. We found the *Reckoning*."

"And the wreck matters because—"

"Because of what's in her hold." Dev spoke up from against the wall, arm cradled, face hollowed but eyes burning. "I'm a geologist, not a historian, but even I could read it once Priya got into the stern section. Trade goods. Copper, blankets, dentalia—seized stock, taken from the trade networks all along this coast under licenses that were never issued. And paperwork. Ship's papers, court filings, correspondence. The colonial administration seized that cargo, and then the claim was contested, and the case files say—" he paused, savoring it even now, even exhausted, "—the case files say the cargo was *never lawfully transferred*. Title never settled. Legally, that hold has been in dispute since 1841, and every party to the original dispute is a nation or community that still exists."

"The descendant communities," Freya breathed. "It's not treasure. It's evidence."

"It's a land claim with a ship attached," Amara said. "Every artifact in that hold ties a community to this coastline in law, in record, in ink. And every falsified survey that erased those communities from the registry becomes worthless the moment the manifest is read aloud. You can't claim empty coast if the coast's own history is sitting in a hold with receipts."

"So Varga's fleet isn't here for salvage value," Tomas said slowly. "Or not mainly. They're here to make sure that hold never gets read. Or to get legal title themselves before anyone else can."

"Now you understand," Amara said, "why we didn't radio home."

Freya felt it settle over her, cold and clarifying, like the fog that rolled off the glacier fields. "Your liaison. The one who told you to stand down."

"Varga's advance ship arrived the morning after we flagged the discrepancies," Amara said simply. "Coincidence takes a lot of believing after that. And then Marcus went down to the beach to check our supply cache and came back white as bone, saying men in company jackets had been through it. Had photographed everything. Including our field notes." She looked into the fire. "Priya wanted to stay and document the hold properly. Elena argued for the ridge and the satellite phone. We argued too long. The storm came in early, the way it does here, and we tried to move inland fast and—" her hand tightened on the cup— "the slope went. Below the falls. Marcus and Elena and Jonas were swept. Priya broke her leg trying to reach them. We carried her two days to the caves because Dev remembered the charts showed sea caves under the point, and we thought—" 

She stopped. Started again, flatter. "We thought anywhere the ocean owned twice a day, the company wouldn't look. Priya's leg went bad on the fourth day. Fever. We did what we could." Her voice didn't break, but it went somewhere very quiet. "On the sixth morning she wasn't feverish anymore. She was gone. We buried her above the tideline, under stones, with her dive slate for a marker. And then it was the two of us, and the tide chart, and waiting to see whether the next people through that arch came to help us or finish the job."

Freya looked at the fire, at the neat stacks, at the charcoal map on the far wall drawn and redrawn by hands that needed something to hold onto. Eleven days. Two people, injured, hiding in a hole the sea filled twice daily, guarding knowledge worth more than anyone in the room could carry.

"You said the tide chart," she said. "Six dates. We found it in your camp."

"Dev's work. The *Reckoning* sits in a scour channel under the point. Most tides she's buried and the approach is suicide. But the spring series—the sixth high tide of this sequence—pulls enough water off the bar that the stern section exposes. Priya figured it. She dove her at the third tide of the last sequence." Amara met Freya's eyes. "Two tides from now, the wreck surfaces. And Varga's fleet will be anchored offshore waiting for exactly that, because they have the same charts we do. The only question left is who stands on that bar when the water goes out."

* * *

Later, while Tomas re-dressed Dev's arm with the steady hands of a man who'd patched everything from float pontoons to fishermen, Freya walked back alone to the first chamber and stood in front of the charcoal map.

Her grandfather's coastline. Drawn here, in a cave, by strangers—but drawn first, decades ago, in a smudged logbook by a man who had found this same truth and buried it deeper than the sea buried the ship.

"He hid it," she said to the dark. "My grandfather found the *Reckoning*. I think he found the manifest too. And he hid it. Flew false routes, smudged his own logs, never told a soul except maybe Maren. Forty years of silence."

Amara had followed her, moving carefully on her bandaged ankle. She stood beside Freya, studying the wall. "Do you know why?"

"Maren says love. Says he did it so no one would have to choose what I'm choosing." Freya laughed, without much humor in it. "For thirty years I thought he was just a bush pilot who liked his privacy. Turns out privacy was the whole architecture. If the wreck stayed lost, the old claims stayed dormant, the companies stayed bored, and the coast stayed invisible. Safe. Poor, maybe. Ignored. But safe."

"And now?"

"Now the surveys are forged, the fleet is anchored, and invisible stopped being safe about eleven days ago." Freya touched the wall beside the charcoal hull, feeling the grooves of the lines. "He chose silence because silence was protection. But it isn't anymore. It's just silence. And silence is exactly what Varga needs."

Amara was quiet a moment. "In my letters, Aldous wrote something I've never forgotten. I was young and furious about a destroyed site down south, and I wrote him something grandiose about truth and justice. He wrote back: *'Truth's heavy cargo, girl. Make sure you're strong enough to carry it before you load it.'*" She smiled faintly. "I thought it was an old man being condescending. Now I think he was telling me the weight of it. What it costs to bring something like this into the light."

"I found his confession," Freya said. "In the logbook. I haven't finished reading it. I keep—" she exhaled— "I keep stopping, because I know how it ends. He chose the lie. And I'm about to choose against him, and I don't know yet if that makes me his heir or his apology."

"Maybe both," Amara said. "Maybe that's what inheritance is."

From the inner chamber, Tomas's voice floated out, warm and absurdly domestic in the dripping dark: "Freya! Your new best friend Dev wants to know if you can fly a man with a busted arm out of here, and I told him you once landed a Beaver on a gravel bar in crosswinds that made grown fishermen cry, so no pressure, but the legend precedes you."

Freya laughed despite everything—and then stopped, because Amara had gone rigid, head cocked, one hand raised.

Above them, muffled through forty feet of rock and the breathing arch of the cave mouth, came a sound that did not belong to wind or gulls or the sea.

An engine. Low, circling, patient.

A spotter plane, flying slow along the point, flying the kind of pattern Freya herself flew when she was hunting something hidden.

"They're sweeping the coast early," Amara whispered. "That's not a sixth-tide search. That's a *now* search."

Freya killed her headlamp and stood in the dark with her grandfather's coastline glowing faintly grey on the stone beside her, and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding: the tide chart said two tides. The plane overhead said less.

Whatever she was going to choose, she was going to have to choose it faster than she'd planned.
