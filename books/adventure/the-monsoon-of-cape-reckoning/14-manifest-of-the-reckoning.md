## 14. Manifest of the Reckoning

The fire was small enough to hide behind a bent knee, and Amara Okonkwo had spent three days learning exactly how small a fire could be and still keep two broken people alive.

She crouched over it now, feeding it driftwood so dry it burned almost smokeless, and watched the pilot woman—Hartwell, the granddaughter of *that* Hartwell—turn the brass instrument over in her hands like it might bite.

"That's a theodolite," Amara said. "Or half of one. The rest is at the bottom of a ravine with most of our dignity."

"Your friend's leg—" Freya started.

"Dev will live. He complains too much to die." Amara nodded toward the back of the cave, where a long shape under a survey tarp groaned on cue, theatrical as ever. "See? He heard me. He always hears me when I insult him."

Tomas, the mechanic, laughed—a short, startled bark, like he'd forgotten caves could hold laughter. It echoed off the wet stone and came back smaller. Amara liked that about this place. Everything that went into it came out diminished. Sound. Light. Hope, if you weren't careful.

But not truth. She'd learned that much in six weeks. Truth got bigger down here.

"You said you'd tell us everything," Freya said. "Once we were inside."

"I did." Amara settled onto her heels, palms open toward the little fire. Outside, far above, the tide was turning; she could feel it in the way the water sighed against the cave mouth, patient as a creditor. "And I will. But I want you to understand something first. Everyone who comes near this story wants a piece of it. The government wanted data. Varga wants cargo. You—" she pointed the stick at Freya, gently, "—you want your grandfather. So before I give you any of it, I need to know what you'll do with it."

Freya didn't flinch. Amara had noticed that about her on the walk in from the lean-to: the woman had a stillness that wasn't calm, exactly. More like a held breath that had been held for years.

"My grandfather hid something," Freya said. "I think I already know what. I just don't know why."

"Then we can start there." Amara fed another piece of wood to the fire. "Because the answer is the same for all of us. He hid it because knowing is expensive. And nobody on this coast has ever been able to afford the price except by paying it together."

* * *

Dev insisted on being propped up for the telling, which cost him twenty minutes of sweating and one truly impressive string of curses in three languages, but there was no talking him out of it. He was the historian. Amara was the surveyor. Between them they'd spent eleven years mapping heritage sites along this coast for the Crown Lands Office, and Dev had a horror of imprecision even while feverish.

"Right." He sat against the cave wall with his splinted leg straight out before him like a ship's prow, wrapped in every blanket they owned. "The wreck. Her name was the *Reckoning*. Colonial packet ship, 1841, outbound heavy. And when I say heavy, I mean criminal."

He let that sit. Dev loved a pause. In the field, Amara used to throw pebbles at him during his pauses.

"She'd been working the coast for two seasons," he went on. "Officially she was a trade vessel—cloth, tools, salt. Unofficially, she was a collector. Wherever the trade networks ran—the river marts, the winter gatherings, the island markets—she arrived just behind them, buying up what couldn't be bought. Or taking what wouldn't sell."

"Took what?" Tomas asked. He'd gone quiet somewhere around *criminal*, and hadn't come back.

"Everything," Amara said, because Dev would take an hour and she could do it in a word. "Copper work. Woven robes. Carved boxes. Canoe paddles painted for ceremony. Regalia. Medicine bundles." She looked at the fire. "Grave goods. That's the part people don't say out loud. There were burial sites along the outer islands that were opened. Not looted in a night—opened properly, methodically, catalogued, like it was science. Like it was legal."

"It was made legal," Dev said bitterly. "That's the whole trick of it. There are papers aboard that ship—manifests, receipts, certificates of purchase signed by men whose authority extended exactly as far as their muskets. Every item listed. Every theft documented beautifully. The Empire was very good at writing down what it took. It's how we know so much about what was lost."

Amara reached into the survey pack at her side—the one thing she'd carried out of the camp when everything went wrong—and drew out the oilcloth bundle she'd guarded through rivers, nights, and one genuinely terrifying encounter with a wolverine.

"This is why we're alive," she said. "And why other people aren't."

Inside the oilcloth was a notebook, water-swollen and re-dried, its cover stamped with the Crown Lands crest. She passed it to Freya, who opened it with hands that were steadier than her face.

"Our last month of work," Amara said. "We were running the heritage survey for the point—you know this, you found our camp. What you don't know is what we found *before* the camp. Three weeks ago, low tide, after a storm. Dev and I were walking the sandbar below the point, checking erosion on a midden site, and the sea gave us a gift."

"The mast," Freya said quietly.

"The mast." Amara smiled despite everything. "You should have seen Dev. He sat down in six inches of seawater and didn't move for an hour. Then we came back with gear, and then we came back with more gear, and then we found the hull under the sand, and then we found—" she tapped the notebook, "—the hold. Sealed. Intact. Cold, dark, dry as a church. Two hundred years of cargo sitting in the dark like it was waiting for someone to ask it questions."

"And the documents?"

"In the hold. A strongbox, lead-lined. Ship's papers, manifests, correspondence." Dev shifted, wincing. "Including letters between the ship's supercargo and the colonial governor discussing—quite openly—how to structure the 'acquisitions' so no Indigenous nation could ever file a claim. How to record gifts as sales. How to record seizures as salvage. It's a confession written in ledger form, Ms. Hartwell. Two hundred pages of it."

The fire popped. Somewhere deeper in the cave system, water dripped with the regularity of a clock.

"So the paperwork proves the cargo was stolen," Tomas said slowly, "and never lawfully claimed. Which means..."

"Which means every artifact in that hold belongs—to the extent the law recognizes such things—to the descendant communities of the nations it was taken from," Amara finished. "Not to the Crown. Not to a museum in the capital. Not to a salvage company. To the people whose great-grandparents made those things and buried their dead with them."

"And Varga?" Freya asked.

Amara felt the cold come back, the one that had nothing to do with the cave.

"We didn't know who she was at first," she said. "A salvage advance ship docking at Cape Reckoning, asking friendly questions—we thought she was chasing rumors of the wreck, same as anyone might. But then our liaison from the Lands Office started making strange requests. Delay the survey results. Restrict site access. Redraw the heritage boundaries on our maps so the point fell outside protected waters." She looked at Dev. "Tell them the rest."

Dev's jaw worked. This part cost him; Amara could see it costing him.

"The falsified surveys," he said. "Varga's company had gotten to people inside the Office. We found copies in our own field files—surveys under our names, Ms. Hartwell, *our names*, certifying that the point held no significant heritage value. Signed with our credentials. Dated with our field dates. If those documents stand, the wreck site is legally empty water. Free for salvage. And if anyone later claims otherwise, the record shows two Indigenous surveyors certified it clean themselves." He laughed, short and awful. "They weren't just going to steal the cargo. They were going to steal us into the bargain. Make us the signature on our own dispossession."

"When we refused to play along," Amara said, "when we told our liaison we'd file the true report, our extraction flight stopped coming. Radio calls went unanswered. And then men from Varga's fleet started walking the coast road past our camp, twice a day, taking notes." She shrugged, and the shrug hurt, and she let it show. "So we walked inland. Figured whoever came looking would search the coast, not the bush. We were mostly right. Mostly is how Dev broke his leg."

Silence, then. The good kind and the bad kind mixed together, the way silence usually is.

Freya was staring at the notebook in her lap, and Amara watched her turn to a page near the end—the page where Amara had sketched the coastline from the old tide chart they'd found pinned in the survey tent, the hand-drawn coast that matched nowhere official. Watched her touch the ink.

"This matches my grandfather's logbook," Freya said. "A flight he never filed. Coordinates he smudged out himself."

"Then your grandfather found her too," Dev said softly. "Decades ago. And he looked at that hold, and he looked at the world outside it, and he decided the world wasn't ready to be trusted with what was inside."

"He hid it," Freya said. "All these years. Why? If the manifest proves the cargo belongs to the communities—if revealing it would have *helped* them—why bury it?"

It was Amara's turn to be quiet. She poked the fire, buying a moment, because this was the part she'd turned over a hundred times in the dark, watching Dev sleep, watching the tide charts.

"Because of what the manifest also proves," she said finally. "Think about it. Those papers don't just list stolen cargo. They name the networks it came from. The families. The trade routes. The winter gathering places. In 1841, that information was worth killing for—it's how the seizures were planned. And your grandfather, flying this coast in whatever decade it was—he'd have known what happens when governments get a map of who holds what. Land claims get challenged. Fishing rights get 'reviewed.' Communities that stayed invisible for a hundred years suddenly become lines on somebody's ledger." She met Freya's eyes. "He didn't hide treasure, Ms. Hartwell. He hid his people. The wreck was the safest place for all of it—proof and inheritance both—locked in the dark, under the sea, where no one could use it against the living."

Freya looked down at the notebook for a long time.

"He kept us invisible," she said at last, and her voice had a crack in it that she papered over fast. "The whole town. Off the maps. Out of the ledgers. I grew up thinking he was just... careful. Old-fashioned. A man who liked his privacy."

"He was a man who loved something enough to lie to it," Amara said. "Those are the hardest lies. They're the ones that outlive the liar."

* * *

Later—after Dev had been dosed with the last of their fever tablets and had fallen asleep mid-sentence, which he'd deny; after Tomas had gone out to check the plane tied down above the tideline and come back soaked and grinning about it ("She'll fly. Battered, ugly, but she'll fly"—"Like her owner," Freya said, and Tomas said "I was going to say that"); after the fire had been fed down to embers—Amara sat beside Freya at the cave mouth and showed her the tide chart.

Six dates, marked in the dead surveyor Okafor's neat hand. Five crossed off in Amara's own pencil, one left bare.

"Spring tides," she said. "Every few months the moon and the sun line up and pull harder, and the water drops lower than it has any business dropping. On the sixth date, the bar below the point drains almost dry. The hull will be reachable on foot. Salvageable with hand tools, if you're quick. That's why the chart exists—that's what Okafor calculated before he died. He knew the window. He died keeping it."

"How long?"

"Two days. Tide four is tomorrow's evening low—too high to reach the hull, but the fleet will be able to see the bar clearly for the first time. Tide five, the day after, the mast breaks surface. Tide six—" she tapped the bare mark, "—the door opens. And closes. After that, the weather systems stack up and the point won't see a low tide like it for a season."

Freya was quiet a moment. Beyond the cave mouth, the Pacific breathed in and out, enormous and indifferent.

"Varga's fleet repositions tomorrow," she said. "Tomas saw the tender lights moving last night. She'll have spotters up at first light."

"Then we have a problem," Amara said. "Because the moment anyone on that fleet sees the bar at tide four, they'll know the window too. And they have divers, cranes, lawyers, and guns. We have a broken historian, a borrowed rifle, and a plane."

"Not quite." Something had changed in Freya's voice—some decision settling into it like sediment. She pulled the grandfather's logbook from her jacket, and in the firelight Amara saw the smudged coordinates, the gaps, the deliberate silences of a man who'd spent a lifetime choosing what the sky would and wouldn't know. "My grandfather kept this coast invisible for forty years because he believed the truth would be used as a weapon against it. Varga thinks the same thing. That's her whole strategy—control the truth, own the truth, or drown it." She closed the logbook. "But she's wrong about one thing. Truth's only a weapon if you're the only one holding it."

Amara studied her. The pilot woman had salt in her hair and exhaustion in her shoulders and something lit behind her eyes that Amara recognized from mirrors—people at the exact moment they stop calculating and start committing.

"What are you planning?" she asked.

Freya looked out at the dark water, where—far offshore, invisible but perfectly imaginable—the lights of a salvage fleet rode at anchor.

"Tide four," she said, "I'm going to teach that fleet to look east."
