## 15. Tide Four

The fleet came in on the morning tide like a herd of patient whales.

Freya saw them first from the air, because of course she did—three hulls sitting fat and grey off the point, riding at anchor with the smugness of money that had nowhere to be until the water told it otherwise. Varga's advance ship had been joined overnight by two more, one of them flying a salvage pennant that flapped in the wind like it was trying to get away.

She banked the Beaver wide, keeping the point between herself and their bridge windows, and counted masts out of habit. Three ships. Two spotter planes folded on the aft deck of the largest, tarped against the spray. By tomorrow they'd be airborne, circling the coast like gulls that had learned to read charts.

"Two tides," she said aloud, to no one, to the plane, to her grandfather's ghost riding shotgun the way he always did over open water. "Two tides, and then everything."

The radio crackled. "Cape Reckoning traffic, Beaver Four-Seven-Bravo, inbound from the north, full stop, taxi to the hangar." Her own voice, steady as a tide table. That was the trick of it, she'd learned. The voice could lie even when the hands were shaking.

She landed, rolled out past the fuel bowser, and shut down in front of the hangar where Tomas stood waiting with his arms crossed and his face arranged into an expression she'd seen on him exactly twice before: once when she'd proposed flying into the Kestrel Pass in a rising gale, and once when she'd proposed it a second time.

"No," he said.

"I haven't asked you anything yet."

"You were going to. You've got your planning face on. It's the one where you look like you're about to apologize for something you haven't done yet."

Freya climbed down onto the tarmac. Her knees announced their opinion of the last three days, and she let them, because Tomas could read her posture better than any instrument panel and there was no point pretending to be fresh. "Amara and Dev can't stay in the caves," she said quietly. "The tide chart says the caves flood to the roof on the fifth high tide. And Varga's people have been walking the point all morning with that dog of theirs. It's over for hiding. It's move them or lose them."

Tomas looked at the fleet for a long moment. Three grey shapes, patient as arithmetic. "And your plan is?"

"I fly east."

He blinked. "East. Where there is nothing."

"Where there is nothing," Freya agreed, "which is exactly why they'll follow me. A plane flying a tight grid over empty coastline at first light? That's not a patrol, that's a search. Their spotters will see me working a pattern and they'll think I've found something. Varga will send her planes east to see what I've seen, and while they're out there counting gulls and arguing about shadows on the water, you take the truck up the logging spur and move Amara and Dev inland to Ruth Ellery's place."

"Ruth Ellery's." Tomas said the name the way a man tests ice with one boot. Ruth Ellery had been the town's nurse for forty years and had opinions about the salvage fleet that she expressed the way weather expresses itself—broadly, and without apology. "Ruth is going to ask questions."

"Ruth is going to ask questions and then she's going to lock her door, and nobody on this coast locks a door for anything short of a bear."

"And if they follow you east and you're wrong? If there's nothing there and Varga figures out it was theater?" He shook his head. "Freya. They're paying you. This is the part I keep coming back to. The government is paying you to search, and you're about to use their own search pattern to misdirect their own contractors. That's not a gray area. That's a chalk outline of a gray area."

Freya leaned against the Beaver's strut and looked down the strip, at the town below it—Mrs. Okonkwo's store with its new coat of paint bought with Varga's goodwill money, the dock where the dockhand who'd ransacked her hangar was now conspicuously absent, the school where the salvage company had donated a new weather radio last week and gotten its name stenciled on the side in letters the size of a child. The town had been bought in increments so small nobody had felt the change of weight, the way you don't feel a tide coming in until your boots are wet.

"Here's what I know," she said. "I know that Amara Okafor and Dev Ramanathan are government surveyors who found out their own contractors were falsifying heritage surveys to steal from every family on this coast. I know Varga's crew left them to drown in a cave. And I know that if I do the honest thing—the paid thing, the follow-your-instructions thing—those two end up in the water or in a room with Varga's lawyers, and the manifest ends up in a hold somewhere, and this town's land claims end up as a footnote in somebody's salvage report." She shrugged. "So yes. I'm going to lie to the law. I've been sitting with that. It sits worse than I expected."

Tomas was quiet for a while. Out past the point, the fleet's anchor lights blinked on in the grey morning, three patient eyes.

"You know what my grandmother used to say," he said finally. "She said, the law is like a river. Mostly it goes where it should, but you don't drown yourself in it to prove a point." He held up a hand before Freya could speak. "I'm not talking you out of it. I'm telling you I've decided. If you're going to lie to powerful people on behalf of this town, then this town should be lying right beside you. That's all. That's the whole speech."

Freya felt something in her chest loosen that had been wound tight since the caves. "That was a good speech."

"It was a short speech. I'm saving the good one for your funeral." He was already moving toward the truck, pulling the tarp off the back where he'd loaded a stretcher frame, blankets, a thermos of the soup Mrs. Okonkwo had sent up without being asked and without explanation, which was its own kind of signature. "First light tomorrow?"

"First light. I'll run the decoy grid until they commit their spotters east. Once both planes are up and pointed away, you move. I'll stay east until you radio me the word 'herring.'"

"Herring."

"You'll think of something better on the drive. You always do."

"I once thought of 'herring' under pressure," Tomas said. "It was not my best work."

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing the thing that frightened her more than the flying: nothing.

There was nothing to do but wait, and waiting turned her hangar into a room full of clocks. She serviced the Beaver anyway, though she'd serviced it two days ago—drained the water from the fuel sump, checked the aileron cables, ran her hands along the fabric of the fuselage looking for the soft spots that meant trouble. The old girl had taken spray on the sandbar landing at the point and she wanted to know her plane the way you want to know a horse before a long ride.

Her grandfather's logbook sat in the plane's map case, wrapped in oilcloth. She'd taken to keeping it there, which Tomas called superstition and which Freya privately acknowledged was exactly that. But the plane had been her grandfather's first, and the book had flown a thousand hours in that same map case before her, and there was a stubbornness in the paper that she wanted on her side. Last night she'd read the final pages again, the confession, the careful hand gone shaky:

*I did not hide treasure. I hid a truth that would have cost the coast its land, and I have told myself for forty years that this was love. Tomorrow I will tell myself again. The wind does not ask permission.*

The wind does not ask permission. She'd fallen asleep with that sentence turning over like a stone in a creek bed. Her grandfather had chosen silence because silence was the only tool he had. She had a radio that reached every kitchen on the coast, and a town that had, in the last two days, started asking her questions with their eyes—why was she flying east, why was the truck going up the logging spur at odd hours, why did Mrs. Okonkwo's soup keep finding its way into Tomas's truck bed. The town wasn't stupid. The town was deciding, the way a crew decides in bad weather, whether to trust the person at the controls.

She just had to make sure she deserved it before they finished deciding.

Near dusk, a boat came across the harbor—Varga's tender, white and quick, with the salvage captain herself standing in the bow like a figurehead who'd fought her way to the position. She climbed the ladder at the dock, walked up to the hangar with the unhurried stride of a woman who owned the ground she stood on, or at least held the mortgage.

"Hartwell." Varga looked up at the Beaver, at the open cowl, at Freya's grease-stained hands, and something almost like approval crossed her face. "Still turning your own wrenches. My chief engineer would weep with envy or horror, I've never determined which."

"Captain." Freya hopped down from the wing, wiping her hands on a rag that was past saving. "Come to see the sights?"

"Come to confirm tomorrow's weather window. Your forecast office is a man with a barometer and a squint, so I'm asking the pilot instead." Varga's eyes went east, down the coast, and stayed there a moment too long. "You've been flying the eastern shore. Unusual. There's nothing out there but rock and regret."

"Search grids don't get to choose their scenery."

"No. But pilots do get to choose their patterns, and yours has been... interesting." Varga turned back to her, and her voice stayed pleasant the way a calm sea is pleasant, which is to say with all its weight hidden underneath. "I'll say this once, Hartwell, because I think you and I understand each other. Whatever you think you found—and I'm not saying you found anything—I've seen what happens to small towns that get between a salvage claim and its cargo. The town doesn't lose. The town just stops being the town. It becomes the place where it happened."

Freya held her gaze. "And I'll say this once, Captain. The town was here before your claim and it'll be here after. That's the whole business model of a town."

Varga smiled then, genuinely, and it was somehow worse than the threat. "First light tomorrow, then. I'm launching my spotters at dawn regardless. Weather window closes on the fifth tide, and I intend to be standing on that sandbar when the water gives up what it's holding." She turned to go, then paused. "Your grandfather flew this coast for thirty years and no one ever caught him at anything. I've always wondered what that kind of discipline costs a person."

"Ask me in two days," Freya said. "I'm pricing it up now."

* * *

Dawn came the color of pewter, with a wind out of the southeast that smelled like the whole monsoon arriving at once. Freya stood in the hangar doorway and watched the fleet's spotter planes unfold from their tarps—two little amphibians, bright with new paint, climbing into the grey like insects off a log.

Tomas's truck idled at the end of the strip, headlights off, loaded and waiting. He'd shaved. She noticed that. He'd shaved and put on a clean shirt, which on Tomas was the equivalent of full ceremonial dress, and it made her throat tight in a way she didn't have time for.

She ran up the engine, checked her heading, and lifted off into the wet air with the logbook in its map case and the whole coast holding its breath below her.

East. She turned east.

Behind her, the two spotter planes wheeled, hesitated, and then—like gulls following a fishing boat—began to climb and turn east as well. She could almost see Varga on the bridge of the lead ship, watching her pattern, doing the arithmetic. Hartwell's working a grid. Hartwell doesn't waste fuel. Therefore Hartwell's found something.

Freya flew the pattern beautifully. Tight legs, careful spacing, the occasional deliberate orbit over a stretch of empty rock that she made look meaningful by the simple act of looking at it hard. Twice the spotter planes dropped low near her, close enough that she could see the observers' faces, and she gave them exactly what they wanted: a pilot absorbed in her work, oblivious, certain.

Below her, the eastern coast unrolled—black rock, white water, the wreck of an old fish cannery that every child in Cape Reckoning was told was haunted and every child in Cape Reckoning had investigated. She was so far east now that the fleet was a smudge behind her, and the point where the caves were had slipped below the horizon's curve, and somewhere in that hidden water Tomas's truck was grinding up the logging spur with two survivors and a thermos of soup, moving at the speed of trust.

The radio crackled. Varga's voice, relayed through her own ship, addressed to all aircraft: *All spotter assets, concentrate eastern grid. Report any anomaly. Pilot Four-Seven-Bravo, maintain your pattern, over.*

Freya keyed her mic and made her voice bored. "Four-Seven-Bravo, maintaining, over." And then, because the wind does not ask permission, she added, "Got some shadowing on the water here. Could be kelp. Could be interesting."

She watched the spotter planes tighten their pattern around her like a fist closing on nothing.

Twenty minutes later the radio crackled again—low power, close range, a voice she knew. Tomas, from somewhere under the trees, on the truck's set, one word:

"Herring."

Freya laughed out loud, alone in the cockpit, with the rain starting to streak the windscreen and the spotter planes still circling her empty grid like moths around a lamp that had never been lit.

"Copy herring," she said. "Coming home."

She turned the Beaver west, and behind her, east of everything, the two spotter planes kept searching the blank water—while ahead of her, out past the point, the fleet's anchors came up one by one, and the lead ship began, quietly and ahead of schedule, to move.
