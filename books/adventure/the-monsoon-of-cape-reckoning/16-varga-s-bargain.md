## 16. Varga's Bargain

Ilse Varga had learned long ago that the sea gave up its secrets only to people who were willing to get wet, and that people gave up theirs even faster if you brought coffee. So she stood on the aft deck of the *Meridian Grace* at six in the morning, holding two cups of the terrible instant stuff the galley produced, watching the floatplane hangar across the harbor for signs of life.

The storm had moved off overnight, dragging its rain curtains east like a reluctant guest. The harbor water lay flat and pewter-colored, and the mountains behind Cape Reckoning stood up out of the mist with the smug clarity of scenery that knows it's being admired. Varga did not admire it. She catalogued it. Twenty-two years in salvage had trained her to see coastlines as balance sheets: that headland was a navigational hazard, that bay was a sheltered anchorage, that river mouth was a supply route. Beauty was what other people saw when they didn't know what they were looking at.

She knew what she was looking at. Somewhere under that pewter water, or under the point, or in the caves her divers had been refused permission to enter, was a ship worth more than the rest of her fleet combined. And somewhere in that sagging hangar with the peeling green door was a bush pilot who knew more than she was saying, and a logbook older than Varga's career.

Freya Hartwell came out of the hangar at half past six, carrying a toolbox and looking like a woman who had slept in her clothes and won the argument with herself about whether to change them. Varga raised one of the coffee cups in salute.

"I was told you don't take no for an answer," Freya called across the water, not slowing her walk.

"I was told you don't take yes for one either. Come aboard. The coffee's awful but it's hot, and I'd rather do this dry."

Freya looked at the gangway the way she probably looked at weather she intended to fly through anyway. Then she came up the ramp, took the offered cup, and drank it without flinching, which Varga noted with professional approval. A woman who could drink that coffee without flinching could be negotiated with.

"I'll be blunt," Varga said. "It saves us both the part where we pretend to be dumber than we are."

"That would be a first for this harbor."

"Your survey team didn't vanish. Two of them are alive. My people found their lean-to upriver before your friend Tomas found it, and we've been watching the caves at the point since Tuesday." She watched Freya's face do something complicated and settle into stillness. "Don't bother being outraged. If I'd wanted them harmed they'd have been harmed. I wanted them *found*, because frightened people talk, and I wanted to know what they'd found before I committed the fleet. They told my diver enough. The *Reckoning*. Colonial registry. A hold full of seized trade goods and a manifest that says the cargo was never lawfully claimed."

The harbor lapped against the hull. Somewhere behind them a crewman dropped a wrench and swore at it with real feeling.

"You've been busy," Freya said.

"I've been *expensive*," Varga corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is that expensive things work. Now. Here's my problem, and I'm going to trust you with it, because trust is cheaper than lawyers and lasts longer." She set her cup down on the rail. "A manifest like that is a bomb. It doesn't just say who owned the cargo. It says who *stole* it, and it says the theft was documented, which means every land claim and heritage survey built on the official record for the last hundred and forty years is built on sand. My company's surveys are clean. Some of my subcontractors' are not. If that manifest comes up in public, I lose my permits, my contracts, and probably my ship. If it comes up in private—" she spread her hands— "it comes up in private."

"And if it never comes up at all?"

"Then everyone keeps their illusions, the town keeps its quiet, and a great many people who have done nothing wrong keep their livelihoods. Including you." Varga let that sit. "I know about the logbook, Hartwell."

Something moved behind Freya's eyes—Varga had seen it before in divers who'd just felt the bottom shift under them. "Do you."

"1974 through 1979, your grandfather filed flight plans for maybe sixty percent of the hours his engine was running. I've spent eleven days in this town buying drinks and listening, and old men remember a Hartwell who'd come back with his tanks dry and his mouth shut. There's a gap in the man's records exactly the width of the coastline on that tide chart your surveyors were carrying. You found the chart. I know you found the chart, because my dockhand is a coward and a thief, but he's *my* coward and thief, and he described what he stole before he stole it." She picked her cup back up. "I'm not angry about the copying. Honestly, I'd have been disappointed if you hadn't."

Freya drank her coffee. Varga gave her the time. This was the part of the negotiation Varga actually liked—not the numbers, but the watching. The pilot was young, maybe thirty, with the sun-cracked hands and the particular patience of someone who spent her working life alone with a machine that would kill her if she got bored. She wasn't frightened. That was rare. Frightened people took bad deals; unafraid people took *long* ones.

"Say your offer," Freya said finally.

"Partnership. Fifty-fifty on the salvage value—yes, I know how that sounds, but the cargo's trade goods and coin, and half of a colonial hold is still more money than this town has seen in its history. The manifest comes up under seal, through arbitration, quietly. The artifacts go to a trust—my lawyers will draft it so it looks generous and behaves like a vault. Your grandfather's flights stay unexplained. The Hartwell name stays what it's always been out here: respectable, unremarkable, and nobody's business." Varga leaned against the rail. "And you get something I suspect you want more than the money. You get your town left alone. No more fleets. No more interviews at the Dockside. No more strangers asking old Maren questions she shouldn't have to answer twice. Cape Reckoning goes back to being invisible, and invisible, out here, means *safe*. You know that better than anyone. Your whole family built a life on knowing it."

There it was. Varga watched the words land exactly where she'd aimed them, and felt no satisfaction about it, only the cold competence of a shot placed well. She had grown up in a town like this one—smaller, colder, on the other side of the map—and she had spent her first twenty years learning that the outside world only arrived in a place like that to take something. Her whole career had been a long argument with that lesson. Sometimes she suspected the lesson was winning.

"You've done your homework," Freya said.

"I've done my *homework* the way you've done yours. We're more alike than you'd like, I think." Varga smiled, and meant it, which was the dangerous part. "You fly alone into weather other people won't drive in. I sail alone into disputes other people won't litigate. We both make our living being the only one willing to go. The difference is that I'm offering to split the going."

"And the surveyors? Amara and the one with the broken wrist?"

"Safe passage, medical care, and a settlement that buys their silence, same as everyone's. They were hired by a government office that knew its subcontractors were dirty and sent them out anyway. They're not innocent in this, Hartwell. They're just better at looking like it."

"They were running inland for two days with no food," Freya said quietly. "One of them set his own wrist. That's not what guilty looks like. That's what *frightened* looks like."

"Guilty and frightened wear the same jacket out here. I've never once been able to tell them apart, and I've stopped trying." Varga finished her coffee and set the cup down. "You don't have to answer now. The fleet anchors at the point tonight. The sixth tide is tomorrow's evening high water. I need your answer by the fifth tide, and I need you to understand that if the answer is no, I'll do this without you, with lawyers, and the town will learn what a discovery hearing is. I'd genuinely rather not. I like it here. The people are stubborn in a way I find restful."

Freya looked out at the harbor. Varga watched her look—at the hangar, at the strip beyond it, at the hills where the town's houses sat with their woodsmoke standing up straight in the calm air. Watching her, Varga understood the shape of the thing she was actually offering, and understood, with the particular honesty that came to her only in the early morning, that it was the shape of a bribe dressed as a kindness. *Stay small. Stay hidden. I'll pay you to stay the way you are.* It was the offer she herself would have spat at at twenty-five. It was the offer she was making at fifty-three because she had learned that most people, given the choice between the truth and the town, would take the town, and she could not afford to be wrong about this one.

"You said your lawyers would draft the trust," Freya said. "Drafted by the company that needs the manifest buried. You see the problem."

"I see that every trust in the world is drafted by someone with an interest. The question is only whose."

"And the artifacts? The ones that were taken from the trade networks here? Those go back to the descendant communities—actually back, with the land claims the manifest supports—or there's no deal, and no amount of fifty-fifty changes it."

Varga had prepared for this. She had a counter ready, a partial concession, a face-saving structure with a trustee and a board and a decade of appeals built into it like bulkheads. She opened her mouth to offer it.

What came out instead, because she was tired and the morning was too clean and this pilot reminded her uncomfortably of herself, was: "That's the part I can't give you. If the manifest supports their claims, my company doesn't survive it, and neither do the hundred and forty people who work for me. I'm not asking you to help me steal from them. I'm asking you to help me not drown. There's a difference. It's a small difference. But I've built a life on small differences."

Freya set her empty cup on the rail with a small precise click, and Varga saw that she had made a decision—not about the offer, but about Varga herself, some filing of her into a category, and Varga found she badly wanted to know which one.

"My grandfather flew those gaps for years," Freya said. "And he told no one. Not my grandmother. Not Maren, hardly. He carried it alone his whole life and died with it, and everyone in this town thought he was just a man who liked flying at night." She looked at Varga with an expression that was not anger and not pity but something adjacent to both. "You're offering me his life. Money and silence and a town that never has to know him. I appreciate that you think it's a kindness. I need to go think about whether it is."

She went down the gangway without waiting for a reply, toolbox swinging, and Varga stood at the rail and watched her cross the harbor in the flat morning light, a small figure with a straight back, and thought: *she's going to say no, and she's going to say it in the worst possible way, and I am going to let her get to the water first.*

Because that was the other thing Varga had learned in twenty-two years. The sea gave up its secrets only to the patient. But the tide chart didn't care about patience. The sixth tide was coming whether anyone was ready or not, and tomorrow evening, when the water dropped off the sandbar below the point, either the *Reckoning* would rise into the hands of the woman who had kept it secret for fifty years—

—or into the light, and take everything down with it.

Varga picked up both cups, looked at the awful coffee, and poured it over the side.

"Signal the fleet," she told her first officer. "All anchors at the point by nightfall. And get me the weather on the sixth. I want to know exactly how much water is coming in, and exactly how fast."

Behind her, across the harbor, the green door of the hangar closed. Somewhere inside it, she was certain, a fifty-year-old logbook was open on a workbench, and a woman who flew into weather other people wouldn't drive in was reading the last pages her grandfather had ever written.

Varga found she was almost hoping they said what she feared they said. It would make the ending cleaner. It would make the ending *hers*.

The *Meridian Grace* weighed anchor at noon.
