## 17. What Grandfather Chose

The rain came down like it had a grudge.

Freya sat in the hangar with the lamp turned low and her grandfather's logbook open on the workbench in front of her, and for a long time she didn't touch it. She just looked at it. The leather cover was cracked like old paint, soft at the corners where sixty years of hands had worried it, and the brass clasp had been broken since before she was born. It smelled of kerosene and cedar and something older underneath—something like the inside of a boat that had been closed up too long.

Outside, the monsoon worked at the roof. Inside, the Cessna's shadow lay across the bench like a sleeping animal, its belly still patched from the sandbar run, its fuel tanks full, its radio tuned to every frequency Varga's fleet might be listening to. Tomorrow at low tide, the mast of the *Reckoning* would break the surface for the first time in two hundred years, and everything would happen very fast.

Tonight was for this.

She'd been putting off the last pages. She knew she had. She'd read the logbook front to back twice since Maren's visit—the careful entries, the smudged coordinates, the flights logged as "mail run" that no mail run ever took—and each time she'd stopped at the final section, where the handwriting changed. Where it got smaller. Where the neat surveyor's letters of a young pilot gave way to something cramped and hurried, like a man writing against a closing door.

Tomas had gone home an hour ago, over her objections. "You're going to sit there all night staring at it," he'd said, zipping his jacket. "At least stare at it with soup in you."

"I'm fine."

"You're fine the way a propeller is fine when it's got a crack in it. Looks fine. Spins fine." He'd tapped the workbench twice, his version of a blessing. "Read it, Frey. Whatever he wrote, he wrote it for you. For whoever came after. Men like your grandfather don't write things down by accident."

Now she poured herself the last of the coffee, cold, and drank it anyway, and turned the page.

* * *

The first entry was dated forty-one years ago, three years before Freya was born.

*Found her today. Or found enough of her. The point gives up her secrets at the lowest tides of the year and today she gave up timber — black oak, grown in England, not here. Nobody grows oak like that on this coast.*

*I dove the pool behind the second arch. Cold enough to stop your heart but I got down twice. She's whole. Whole as the day she went down, which wasn't a storm the way they'll tell you. Her hull is stove in from the inside out. They scuttled her, and they did it in a hurry, and they did it here because nobody comes here.*

Freya's hand had gone still around the mug.

*Manifest is sealed in the captain's strongbox, aft. I could not open it without destroying it and I did not try. But the papers in the chart room were loose and I read them by torchlight in a wet cave like some kind of grave robber, and God forgive me, I understood immediately why they sank her instead of sailing her home.*

She read the next paragraph three times. Then she set the mug down carefully, the way you set down something when your hands aren't steady, and read it a fourth time aloud to the empty hangar, because saying it made it more real than seeing it.

*The Reckoning was carrying what they took. Not gold — or not only gold. Trade goods seized from the villages along this whole coast. Canoes. Copper shields. Regalia. And the ledgers — the ledgers are the worst of it, because the ledgers prove none of it was ever bought. Taken under color of law that didn't exist. And the ship's own papers name the officials who ordered the seizures, the same families whose names are on the land grants, the same grants this whole coast's title rests on.*

*If that ship comes up, those ledgers come up with her. And if those ledgers come up, every land claim from Cape Reckoning to the Sound gets dragged into court and torn apart. The companies will win. They always win. They have the lawyers and we have the rain.*

*So I made a choice tonight and I don't know yet if it was the right one. I moved the channel markers. I put the reef a mile east on my charts and burned the true ones. I logged a false bearing in this book and smudged the real one with my thumb while the ink was wet, so even I couldn't be sure later. If anyone ever asks me, and someone will, I can say honestly: I never marked it. I never told. I flew over it two hundred times and saw nothing but water.*

Freya sat back. The lamp hummed. Rain hammered the tin roof in long rolling sheets, and somewhere out in the dark the sea was already beginning its slow rehearsal for tomorrow's tide.

*They'll call it a lie,* the old man had written. *Maybe it is. But I've watched what happens when the government and the companies come asking questions with clipboards. Questions are how they take things. A wreck is a question that never stops being asked. So I'm going to make sure nobody asks it.*

*The people here — the Oyelk families, the Lindqvists, old Sam Toma at the cannery, all of them — their grandfathers fished these waters before there was a chart. Their right to be here doesn't come from paper. But paper is what protects them now, and the paper stands until somebody finds a reason to knock it over. I won't be the reason.*

There was a blot on the page, a drop of something — rain, or seawater, or something else — that had bloomed the ink into a small gray flower. Freya touched it with one fingertip, very lightly, the way you'd touch a scar.

*Am I a coward? Maren says yes. Maren says the truth is heavy but it floats and lies sink eventually and drown everyone holding them. Maybe. But Maren doesn't have children. I do. I have a son who fishes these waters and a granddaughter who isn't born yet who will, God willing, grow up thinking this coast has always been hers, because it has, and because nobody ever found the reason to take it away.*

*If she ever reads this — and someday she will, because I am hiding this book badly on purpose, in the one place a Hartwell would look — then hear me, girl:*

*I did not hide treasure. There is nothing down there worth a tenth of what people will imagine. I hid a truth that would have cost the people I love everything they have, to give strangers something they have no right to. That's not silence out of shame. That's silence out of love. I hope you can forgive the difference. I hope you never have to learn it.*

Freya read that last line until the words blurred.

Then she laughed — one short, cracked sound that startled her in the empty hangar — because of course. Of course he'd hidden it badly on purpose. The smudged coordinates, the false bearings, the book left in the flight bag in the plane where any Hartwell poking through inherited junk would find it. He hadn't buried the secret. He'd *entrusted* it. He'd left it where only family would find it, wrapped in enough ambiguity that even discovery could be denied, and he'd written his reasons down in ink that couldn't be unsaid.

He'd given her the choice. That was the thing that undid her. He could have burned the book. He was a man who burned charts; burning one logbook would have been nothing. Instead he'd kept it, and kept it findable, and written *I hope you never have to learn it* across forty years like a letter posted to the future.

Well, Granddad. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, annoyed at herself. Weather's come in. I learned it.

* * *

She got up and walked the length of the hangar, because sitting still had become impossible. The rain followed her along the roof. At the big doors she unlatched the viewing panel and looked out at the strip — the runway lights drowned in gray, the town beyond them a scatter of yellow windows, the sea invisible but audible, working the shore like a man kneading dough.

Varga's fleet sat out there somewhere past the point, four hulls riding at anchor with their lights doused, waiting for the tide the way wolves wait at a treeline. And tomorrow morning Freya was going to fly a decoy run east with her transponder squawking and her lights blazing, drawing their spotter planes away while Tomas moved Amara and Devi inland to safety, and then she was going to land on a sandbar that existed for about ninety minutes and take her grandfather's confession out of a strongbox two hundred years old.

And after that—

After that was the part she'd been avoiding. Because there were two versions of tomorrow, and she'd been letting them blur together the way the smudged coordinates blurred, keeping her options soft and undecided.

Version one: recover the manifest case quietly. Fly it out. Bury it the way he'd buried the wreck — a false bearing, a smudged thumbprint, another generation of Hartwell silence. Keep the coast safe the way he'd kept it safe. Keep the town invisible. Keep herself invisible. It would work. It had worked for forty years. It was the inheritance, handed to her in her grandfather's cramped and loving hand: *this is how you protect people, you lie to the world on their behalf.*

Version two was the radio.

She thought about the broadcast she'd already half-planned, the one where she read the manifest out loud on every frequency, and she tried to feel what her grandfather would have felt hearing it, and she couldn't. That was the trouble. She kept trying to consult a dead man about a decision he'd made under different skies, and he kept refusing to answer, because dead men keep their own counsel and leave you the weather.

But he hadn't refused entirely. He'd left instructions of a kind. Not in the confession — in the way he'd hidden the book. Badly. On purpose. Findable.

If he'd wanted the silence carried forward forever, he'd have made the book unfindable. If he'd wanted the truth shouted from the mountaintops, he'd have shouted it himself in 1983 and taken whatever came. Instead he'd done the harder, more human thing: he'd passed the weight along and trusted the next pair of shoulders to decide what the times required.

His times required silence. The companies owned the courts, the newspapers, the survey offices. One man in a tail-dragger against all of it wasn't courage, it was suicide, and worse — it was suicide that would take the town down with him.

Her times were different. Varga's fleet was running on forged heritage surveys and borrowed legal cover, and legal cover evaporates in daylight. The descendant communities had lawyers now — real ones, Amara had said, funded and furious and waiting for exactly this. The difference between 1983 and now wasn't that the truth had gotten lighter. It was that for the first time, the truth had somewhere to land.

Love doesn't require lies anymore. That was the sentence that arrived in her, fully formed, the way sentences sometimes do — not reasoned into but simply *there*, like the moment you realize the fog has lifted while you weren't watching. He lied because love had no other tool. She had others. Radio frequencies. Testimony. Two living witnesses from the survey team. A salvage captain whose entire operation rested on paperwork that was about to become evidence.

Keeping his silence now wouldn't be honoring him. It would be embalming him.

"Okay, Granddad," she said to the empty hangar. Her voice came out rough. "Here's what I've got."

She went back to the bench, took out a fresh page from her own flight log, and began to write — not coordinates, not bearings, but a plan, numbered, plain, the way he'd taught her to brief a flight. *One: decoy run east at first light, Tomas moves the survivors upriver to the Lindqvist place. Two: land the bar at low tide, 0940, window ninety minutes. Three: manifest case secured aboard. Four:* — and here her pen paused, and she pressed it down hard enough to dent the page — *four: broadcast. Everything. Manifest, ledgers, survey frauds, all of it, open frequency, before the water comes back. Let the whole coast hear it while it's happening. No deniability. No smudged ink.*

She looked at what she'd written and felt the strange double weight of it — grief and relief in the same breath, the way the air feels after a storm breaks. Somewhere in the last hour she'd stopped being the keeper of her grandfather's secret and started being the carrier of his truth, and those sounded like the same job and were not the same job at all.

There was one more page. She'd been saving it the way you save the last of anything. She turned it.

It was a single line, dated the week before his last flight, in a hand gone shaky but legible:

*If the day ever comes when hiding it costs more than telling it — tell it. And tell them I'm sorry it took so long.*

Freya closed the book and held it against her chest for a moment, the way you hold a person.

"Yeah," she said. "Me too."

* * *

She was still at the bench when headlights swept the hangar doors — Tomas's truck, bouncing up the strip through the rain at an hour when sensible mechanics were asleep. She latched the logbook back into its hiding place behind the avionics panel and opened the door to let him in, and knew from his face before he said a word that sleep was officially cancelled.

"Tomas?"

"Radio traffic." He was soaked through, breathing hard, holding a clipboard like a shield. "Coast guard band, encrypted relay off Varga's lead ship. Our friend with the shortwave picked up fragments." He swallowed. "Frey — they're not waiting for the sixth tide. They're moving the salvage barges into position tonight. First light, they go for the wreck."

Freya looked out at the dark, at the rain, at the invisible sea where four ships waited with their lights doused.

"They've got their own tide tables," she said quietly. "They know the fifth tide exposes the bar."

"And they know you know." Tomas set the clipboard down on the workbench, right on top of her plan, and his voice dropped. "So. Whatever you decided tonight, whatever the old man told you—" he tapped the page once, "—you'd better decide fast. Sunrise is in five hours."

Freya looked at the numbers she'd written, at the word *broadcast* standing in the lamplight like a lit fuse, and reached for her flight bag.

"Then we'd better get moving."
