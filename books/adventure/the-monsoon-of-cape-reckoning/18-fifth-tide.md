## 18. Fifth Tide

**FREYA**

The tide went out like a held breath finally released.

Freya stood on the point at 4:40 in the morning, thermos of Tomas's terrible coffee cooling in her hands, and watched the ocean peel itself back from the coast in long silver sheets. The chart had promised this—the fifth tide, the lowest of the series, a minus tide so deep it would drag the waterline half a mile past where any living soul had seen it go. The almanac called it an extreme low. The old survey notebook, its pages soft as moth wings in her jacket pocket, called it something else: *the sea opens its hand.*

She'd thought that was survey-team poetry. It wasn't. It was a description.

Because there, in the gray pre-dawn light, breaking through the wet sand like a rib through skin, was the mast.

It rose maybe twenty feet, black with three centuries of salt, canted at an angle that made her stomach drop to look at—like a man drowning who had frozen mid-reach. Torn rigging hung from it in ropes of stone. Around its base, the retreating water had carved channels through the sandbar, and the sand itself was wrong, dark and glassy in places, littered with things her eyes kept trying to file as driftwood and failing. A curve of timber. A shape like a door. The shoulder of something enormous lying just beneath the surface film, waiting.

*The Reckoning.*

Her grandfather had flown over this exact patch of ocean for forty years and never told a soul what slept under it. She felt him beside her now, or the idea of him, weathered and quiet, watching her watch his secret come up for air.

"You picked a hell of a thing to keep," she said to the wind.

Behind her, up at the plane, Tomas was running the preflight by flashlight, moving fast, not talking. They'd agreed on no talk until they were airborne. Talk invited doubt, and doubt at this hour was a luxury with teeth.

But the radio didn't care about their agreement. At 4:52 it crackled to life in the truck, and Freya's chest went cold.

"—all vessels, all vessels, this is *Meridian Star*, salvage fleet command. Fleet is moving to station. Repeat, fleet is moving to station. Survey aircraft inbound from the east. Anyone on the water inside the exclusion line will be considered—" a pause, the kind of pause a lawyer charges for, "—non-compliant."

Non-compliant. Out here, in the dark, on open water. Freya turned the word over and found Varga's fingerprints all over it.

She jogged back to the strip. Tomas had the Cessna's doors open, engine cowling still warm from the run-up, and he looked at her with the particular expression he saved for decisions he'd already lost arguments about.

"They're coming," she said.

"I heard." He handed her the headset. "Sandbar's got maybe ninety minutes of exposure before the turn. After that the water comes back like it's owed money."

"Then we're not late."

"We're not early either." He swung up into the right seat, which was against every rule he had—he was the mechanic, she was the pilot, and the seating chart between them was older than their friendship—but today he buckled in beside her anyway. "Decoy first," he said. "You're sure about the decoy?"

"The spotter planes are already east looking for us. We give them something to find—a flight path, a beacon ping, ten minutes of me being very visible over the wrong bay—and then we go dark and come in low from the north while they're patrolling empty water." She keyed the starter. "Varga thinks in grids, Tomas. So we'll be a bad coordinate."

The engine caught. The propeller blurred into a disc, then into nothing, and the runway lights of Cape Reckoning slid away beneath them into the dark.

* * *

**TOMAS**

Tomas had fixed engines for nineteen years, and in all that time he had developed exactly one theory about flying: the sky is fine, it's the ground that kills you. Which meant that today, when the plan required his best friend to land a damaged bush plane on a wet sandbar next to a shipwreck, with a salvage crew storming ashore and the ocean scheduled to return on a schedule, he found himself thinking less about the sky than ever.

The decoy worked better than it deserved to.

Freya flew them loud and bright down the eastern channel, transponder squawking, lights strobing, doing lazy wide circles over Gullwater Bay like a woman searching for something she'd lost. Within six minutes two spotter planes broke off from the fleet and came east after them, and Tomas could hear the chatter on the scanner—*visual contact, single Cessna, matching the description*—and he had to admire it, the sheer theatrical nerve of her. She even waggled her wings at one of them. Friendly. Harmless. A bush pilot out at dawn because that's what bush pilots do.

Then, over a ridge, she cut everything.

Transponder off. Lights off. Radio silent. The world dropped away to engine noise and starlight, and the Cessna banked hard north into the river valley, hugging terrain so close that Tomas could smell the spruce.

"They'll figure it out in fifteen minutes," he said.

"Twelve, if they're smart." Her face was lit faintly green by the instruments, calm as a lake. "Varga's smart. That's why we have twelve."

They came out of the valley at two hundred feet, over black water, and there it was ahead of them—the point, the bar, the mast standing up out of the sand like a flag planted by the drowned. And around it, coming out of the western dark, running lights in a ragged line: the fleet. Three hulls holding position beyond the shallows, and two launches already in the water, bows cutting white lines toward the exposed sand.

"They're early," Tomas said.

"They were always going to be early." Freya's hands were steady on the yoke. "Everyone's early when it's the last chance."

He ran the numbers without wanting to. The launches: four minutes out, maybe five. The landing: Freya would need to set it down on a strip of wet sand roughly the length of a runway built by someone who hated runways, crosswind off the point, surface soft at the edges, firm only along the old channel scours. One pass. There would not be a second.

"Flaps," she said, more to herself than to him, and the plane slowed, sank, became a thing listening to the ground.

The sandbar rushed up at them, pale and ridged in the dawn light, and Tomas did the only useful thing left to him, which was to believe in her completely and say nothing at all.

The wheels touched. Once—a bounce, a lurch, his heart somewhere near his tonsils—and then again, and then they were rolling, spraying sheets of wet sand, the wreck sliding past the window close enough to count the barnacles on the fallen spar, and Freya stood her on the tail wheel and stopped them thirty feet from the mast with the ease of a woman parking outside a grocery store.

"Go," she said. "Case is aft of the mast, in the scoured channel. I saw it glinting on approach."

He was out the door before the prop stopped.

* * *

**FREYA**

Freya killed the engine but left the mags hot, because leaving in a hurry was the entire shape of this morning. Through the windshield she watched Tomas sprint across the sand, small and fast, following the channel scour toward the base of the mast, and she let herself have exactly three seconds of terror on his behalf before she made herself look west instead.

The launches were closer. Close enough now to see faces. Eight, ten men, oilskins, one of them carrying something long and metal that was not a tool for archaeology.

And behind them, at the fleet's anchorage, a third boat was lowering—fast, low, black-hulled. Varga's own tender. Of course. Ilse Varga had never once in her life sent other people to do the thing she wanted done.

Freya keyed the radio and kept her voice level, because the whole coast might be listening and she needed them to hear a professional, not a frightened woman. "Cape Reckoning traffic, Cessna niner-seven, on the bar at the point. Two boats approaching my position. I am not armed and I am not leaving."

Static. Then, smooth as poured cream: *"Freya."* Varga herself, on the frequency, which meant she'd been waiting for exactly this transmission. *"You're standing on my claim."*

"It's not your claim. It's not anyone's claim. That's rather the point of the case I'm about to pick up."

A pause, and in it Freya could hear the launches' engines, closer, and Tomas's figure bending at the base of the mast, digging.

*"Whatever you think you've found,"* Varga said, *"documents rot. Wrecks don't. Come fly for me and you'll never worry about a hangar roof again."*

Freya looked at the mast—at the black wood, the stone-stiff rigging, the great dark shoulder of the hull emerging from the sand as the last film of water drained away—and thought about her grandfather's smudged coordinates, forty years of them, silence stacked on silence like ballast.

"Not this morning," she said.

Tomas straightened. Even at this distance she could see it—the way he tucked something against his chest and turned and ran, sand exploding behind his boots, and the lead launch swinging in toward the bar's edge, men already standing up in the bow.

He reached the plane at a dead sprint, wrenched the door, fell half into his seat with the case hugged to him like a child. It was iron-banded, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, smaller than a suitcase. Three hundred years underwater and the seal had held. Somebody aboard the *Reckoning* had wanted this opened eventually.

"Got it," Tomas gasped. "Go, go, *go*—"

Freya was already feeding throttle. The engine roared, the prop bit sand, and the Cessna lurched forward across the bar just as the first boot hit dry ground behind them. In the mirror she caught a flash of Varga's black tender grounding hard at the bar's edge, and a tall figure stepping out into the ankle-deep wash, coat snapping in the wind, not running—walking, unhurried, the way a person walks when they believe the tide works for them.

It didn't. Not anymore. But it was coming back, and fast.

The end of the bar rushed up. Freya pulled, and the wheels left the sand, and the wreck fell away beneath them—the mast, the ribs, the whole drowned century of it laid bare in the morning light for anyone with eyes to see.

"Tomas," she said, banking north, climbing, the case locked between his feet. "Open it."

His hands were shaking. So were hers, on the yoke, where nobody could tell.

Behind them, on the sandbar, the sea had begun to return.

* * *

**TOMAS**

The wax cracked like old ice.

Inside the case, wrapped in tarred canvas gone stiff as bark, lay papers. Real paper, browned at the edges but whole, and ink that had faded to the color of weak tea but had not gone anywhere. Tomas, who had never in his life cared about history unless it was the history of carburetors, found his breath going strange.

There was a manifest. Columns of it. Cargo listed item by item—not spice, not silk, but things with names that made his skin prickle, because he knew those names. Carved panels. Burial goods. Trade goods seized, the word *seized* written plainly, in a clerk's careful hand, alongside figures and dates and the names of places that still existed, that his neighbors still lived beside.

And beneath the manifest, bound separately, a bundle of letters. Official ones. He couldn't read them all, not bouncing in a climbing plane with salt spray on the windows, but he read enough. Enough to understand that the cargo had never been lawfully claimed. Enough to understand that some clerk three hundred years ago had put the truth in a box and sealed it, the same way Amos Hartwell had put the truth in a logbook and smudged it.

People kept choosing the truth, Tomas thought, and then burying it. Maybe because the truth was heavy. Maybe because carrying it cost something every single day.

"Freya." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "This is it. This is everything. The surveys, the claims—all of it. It proves the whole thing."

She glanced down, one second, and he watched her face do something complicated—grief and relief and fury all trying to fit through the same door.

"Radio," she said. "Every frequency. Get ready to read."

"Read? Now?"

"Now, while the whole coast can hear it, and while Varga's lawyers are still asleep." Her jaw was set, but her eyes, when they flicked to his, were bright and scared and certain, all at once. "Truth doesn't work if you deliver it quietly, Tomas. Believe me. My family tried quiet for forty years."

He unfolded the manifest against his knees, keyed the mic, and began to read the names of the dead out loud into the morning air, over the sound of the engine, while below them the tide came home to the *Reckoning* like a debt collector, patient and absolute, and somewhere behind them a black-hulled tender sat grounded on a vanishing bar, its captain watching her claim dissolve into seawater.

The first voice crackled back within a minute—an old fisherman on channel sixteen, asking them to say again, say again, the whole town was waking up and turning on its radios.

Tomas read on.
