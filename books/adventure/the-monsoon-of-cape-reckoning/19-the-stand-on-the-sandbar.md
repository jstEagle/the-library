## 19. The Stand on the Sandbar

The sandbar was the color of old bone and it stretched from the drowned point to the wreck like a road someone had built for exactly this moment, four generations too early.

Freya stood beside her plane with the manifest case under one arm and the wind trying to take her hair apart strand by strand. Behind her, the Reckoning's ribs broke the surface in a long black arc—masts gone, hull open to the sky like a whale that had died mid-song. Ahead of her, coming across the wet sand at a pace that pretended to be a walk, came Ilse Varga.

Varga had traded her captain's coat for a salvage jacket, but she still moved like a woman who had never once been told no by the ocean. Four of her crew fanned out behind her, and Freya noted—with the part of her brain that was always noting things, the pilot's part, the part that kept her alive—they were carrying crowbars and cutting tools and not one of them looked comfortable about it.

"Tomas," Freya said into her headset, low.

"I'm here." His voice crackled from the Cessna's radio, where he sat in the right seat with the engine ticking over. "I'm also hiding behind the chart bag, if you're wondering."

"I wasn't."

"You were a little."

* * *

It had gone wrong quickly, the way things go wrong on water. The fifth tide had pulled back faster than the almanac promised, and the bar had surfaced gleaming and treacherous, and for eleven minutes it had belonged to nobody. Freya had put the plane down on the firm sand near the point with a landing she'd later describe as "textbook" and Tomas would describe as "a controlled crash with delusions of grandeur." She'd waded the last thirty meters through ankle-deep surf to the wreck's exposed hold, pried loose the sealed case wedged beneath a fallen beam—her grandfather's coordinates, his smudged logbook entries, sixty years of silence all pointing at this one box—and she'd been turning back toward the plane when Varga's launch had rounded the point.

Now here they all were.

"Dr. Hartwell," Varga said, stopping six feet away. She said it pleasantly. She said everything pleasantly; that was the thing people misunderstood about her. "You're standing on my salvage claim."

"Your claim's based on heritage surveys signed off by your own consultants," Freya said. "Two of whom are currently sitting in a cave with broken ankles telling a very different story."

"Allegedly."

"Amara Okafor. Daniel Reyes. You can look them up. Or I can introduce you. They're chatty."

Something flickered behind Varga's eyes—not guilt, Freya thought, nothing so useful as guilt. Recalculation. "The surveys will be re-verified."

"They'll be re-verified by the same firm that falsified them, yes. That's generally how these things stay tidy." Freya shifted the case against her hip. It was heavier than it looked. Everything true was heavier than it looked; her grandfather could have told her that, if he hadn't spent fifty years telling her nothing instead.

Varga glanced past her at the wreck, and for just a moment the pleasantness dropped and Freya saw what was underneath: hunger, real and enormous, the kind that eats its owner slowly from the inside. "Freya. Listen to me. Whatever's in that case—the cargo manifests, the claims documents—it's paper. Paper rots. What's in that hull is worth more than every town on this coast combined, and there is a version of this where you and I split it and your airstrip gets a paved runway and your mechanic gets dental."

"He has dental."

"Better dental."

"That's the most honest sentence you've said all week."

Behind Varga, one of the crew—a young man with a crowbar he was holding like it might bite him—shifted his weight and looked out at the water instead of at the two women. Freya catalogued that too.

"Here's what happens now," Varga said, stepping closer, voice dropping into something almost gentle. "My crew takes the case. My lawyers file an injunction before the sixth tide. By the time anyone reads those documents properly, the artifacts are in a bonded warehouse in three jurisdictions and the story is whatever we say it is. You fly home. Nobody hurts anybody. Your grandfather kept this coast invisible for sixty years, Freya—I'm offering to keep it that way, only richer. Isn't that what you people want? To be left alone?"

And there it was. The oldest wound, found on the first probe, because Varga was very good at her work.

Freya thought about her grandfather's last pages. *I hid it not because the truth would cost me something, but because I couldn't bear what it would cost them.* He'd chosen silence out of love. She understood that now the way you understand a current—by being caught in it.

But love didn't require lies anymore. That was the whole revelation, the whole terrible gift of the last two weeks: the silence had worked when the coast was small and the world was far away. The world wasn't far away anymore. It was anchored offshore in four ships with spotter planes, and it had already bought the dockhands and half the goodwill in Cape Reckoning, and the only thing silence protected now was Ilse Varga's profit margin.

"No," Freya said.

Varga sighed, genuinely regretful, which was somehow worse. "Take the case, boys."

Nobody moved.

Freya had already turned, already keyed her headset, already done the arithmetic—thirty meters to the plane, Varga's crew ten meters behind her, the tide table in her head counting down. But the crew hadn't moved, and that changed the arithmetic entirely.

She stopped. Turned back around. Looked at the young man with the crowbar.

"You're from Kettle Harbour," she said. It wasn't a guess. She'd flown medevac out of Kettle Harbour twice that season; she knew the accent the way she knew weather. "Marta's boy? You used to ride along on the mail runs when you were twelve and throw up over the side of the inlet and I told everyone it was the fish oil."

The young man went red. "That was—" He looked at Varga. Looked at the sea. "Ma'am, I signed on for salvage. Not for... whatever this is."

"It's a lawful recovery operation," Varga said sharply.

"It's a woman on a beach with a box," he said, "and I've got a crowbar, and my mum listens to channel sixteen."

*Channel sixteen.* The words landed in Freya's chest like a key finding its lock.

Of course. Of course. The whole coast lived on channel sixteen the way other coasts lived on gossip and church—every fisherman, every floatplane, every kitchen radio left on while someone fried something. Her grandfather had known it. Had counted on it, maybe, in the end. A secret kept on a coast like this was never really a secret; it was just a broadcast waiting for the courage to transmit.

"Freya," Tomas said in her ear. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"You know what I'm thinking."

"I do, and I want it noted that I hate it, and also that the handheld's in the door pocket and the battery's full because I charged it this morning because unlike some people I plan for disasters."

"I love you, Tomas."

"Everyone does. Go."

* * *

She walked to the plane. Nobody stopped her. That was the strange part, the part she'd replay for weeks afterward—that Varga's crew simply watched her go, five professionals on a beach, undone not by force but by a mother listening on channel sixteen.

She pulled the handheld from the door pocket, checked the battery out of pure habit, and climbed onto the wing root so she could see the whole bar: the wreck, the water, the fleet lying grey and patient offshore, Varga standing alone now in the wind with her jacket snapping like a flag of a country that had just stopped existing.

"Varga," Freya called. "Last chance to be a footnote instead of the headline."

"You'll destroy yourself," Varga said. "Your family's name. Sixty years of quiet. Is that worth a box of paper?"

"It was never mine to bury." Freya thumbed the transmit switch. "This is Foxtrot Bravo, Freya Hartwell, broadcasting open on marine band, all channels, monitoring sixteen. If you can hear me, you're hearing this live from the sandbar at the Point, and I need about four minutes of your time."

She opened the case.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had held for a century, lay the manifest of the *Reckoning*—and beneath it, the thing that made her hands shake: the survey documents Amara had described, copies smuggled out of Varga's own offices, bearing the signatures of consultants who had certified Indigenous trade sites as "no significant cultural material present" on dates when those same consultants had never left port. And beneath that, brittle and brown, the colonial claim papers themselves—the ones proving the cargo had been seized, never lawfully purchased, from trading networks whose descendant communities still held recognized title to the coast.

She read. She read names, dates, tonnages. She read the survey fraud aloud, line by line, firm name and signature and date. Her voice carried flat and clear across the water, and the wind tried to take it and failed.

Somewhere offshore, a spotter plane banked. Somewhere in a galley in Kettle Harbour, Marta's boy's mother turned her radio up. Somewhere in Cape Reckoning, she knew, the café had gone silent, and Old Pete was probably standing on a chair.

"—and item forty-one," Freya read, "one carved trade chest, provenance: Salishan coastal network, seized without compensation, claimant signature illegible, which is convenient for everyone except the claimants—"

"Turn it off." Varga had started toward her across the sand, and for the first time since the advance ship had docked, she was running. "Hartwell, turn it *off*—"

Freya kept reading. Her voice didn't rise; that was the trick of it, the thing her grandfather's logbooks had taught her without meaning to. You didn't shout the truth. You just said it plainly, at altitude, and let gravity do the rest.

"—certification of site 7-C, dated March, signed R. Ellery, who per harbour records was moored at Port Anselm on the date of inspection, a distance of two hundred kilometers, which makes this either the fastest survey in maritime history or—"

Varga reached the plane and grabbed the strut. "You're finished on this coast. Every contract, every charter—"

"Probably," Freya agreed, and kept reading.

Because that was the cost, and she'd counted it in the dark of her hangar two nights ago, and she paid it out loud now, mid-broadcast, letting the whole coast hear the price tag attached to the truth: her contracts, her name, the careful invisibility her grandfather had built and she had inherited like a debt. She read the manifest and she read her own obituary as a bush pilot, and the strange thing, the liberating thing, was how light it felt. Sixty years of silence lifted off her shoulders and left her standing in the wind with nothing to hide and nowhere to hide it, and it felt like the first clear-air flight after a monsoon.

"—and finally," she said, "the descendant communities named in these claims are listed on page nine, and their title was never extinguished, and as of approximately ninety seconds ago, everybody knows it."

She lowered the handset.

The sandbar was very quiet. The young man from Kettle Harbour had set his crowbar down in the wet sand, deliberately, the way you surrender a weapon. Two others had drifted back toward the waterline. Varga stood gripping the strut, and Freya watched the exact moment the legal cover evaporated—watched it happen in the set of her jaw, the recalculation running out of numbers. Four ships offshore. Not one of them could afford to be within a hundred miles of this beach now, and every radio on the coast knew it.

"You could have been rich," Varga said quietly.

"I was already rich," Freya said. "I just did the inventory late."

Varga looked at her for a long moment, and underneath the fury Freya saw it again—the hunger, and beneath the hunger, briefly, horribly, recognition. Two women who had both learned to survive by controlling what the coast was allowed to know. One of them had just chosen differently, on a beach, in front of everyone.

Varga let go of the strut. "This isn't over."

"It's over on this sandbar," Freya said. "That's all anything ever is."

Then she heard it—or rather she felt it, in her boots, in the soles of her feet, a vibration too low for ears. She turned toward the sea.

The water at the edge of the bar had stopped going out.

It stood still for a moment, trembling, gathering itself, the way the whole coast gathered itself before the monsoon broke. Then the first swell came over the lip of the bar, thin and white and fast, and raced up the sand toward the wreck, toward the plane, toward them.

"The sixth tide," Tomas said in her ear, very calm, the way he got when he was terrified. "Freya. The almanac was wrong. It's early. Get in the plane."

The water took the young man's abandoned crowbar first, plucked it up and carried it away like it was settling accounts.
