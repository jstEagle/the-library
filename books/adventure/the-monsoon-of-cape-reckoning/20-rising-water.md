## 20. Rising Water

The sea did not knock before entering.

Freya felt it first as a change in the sound under her boots—a wet, greedy hiss from the seaward edge of the sandbar, where the sixth tide had begun its run. She had stood on enough beaches in her life to know the difference between a tide that arrived and a tide that *collected*. This one was collecting.

"Tomas!" she shouted over the wind. "Water's turning!"

He was thirty meters away at the plane's tail, hauling the tie-down rope through his fists like a man arguing with it. The Cessna sat on the exposed bar where she'd put her down twenty minutes ago—wheels half-buried in wet sand, left strut bent from the landing, one tire looking soft and philosophical about its future. Behind them, the Reckoning's black ribs rose out of the retreating water like the skeleton of some enormous drowned animal, and around those ribs, Varga's crew was still scrambling, still shouting, still trying to salvage a theft that had just been read aloud to the entire coast on every frequency Freya's radio could reach.

The manifest case was lashed into the back seat of the plane. It had cost her everything to get it there. It was not going to cost her the return trip.

"Strut's worse than you think!" Tomas yelled back, arriving at a jog, spray already speckling his jacket. "She'll fly, but she'll fly angry."

"Everything flies angry if you insult it enough," Freya said, and yanked open the pilot's door.

* * *

Tomas had fixed planes for nineteen years, and in that time he had developed a private taxonomy of aircraft. There were planes that were healthy, planes that were sick, and planes that were lying. This one was lying. It looked airworthy the way a man with a broken rib looks fine until he laughs.

He climbed into the right seat and pulled the door shut against a fistful of wind, then looked down the length of the sandbar and did the arithmetic he did not want to do.

The bar ran maybe four hundred meters, northwest to southeast, a pale tongue of sand that only the lowest tides ever uncovered. At the north end: open channel, deep water, the fleet anchored beyond it like patient vultures. At the south end: rocks, white water, and the point where Maren's cottage still kept its stubborn watch. The wind was coming off the ocean from the southwest at what he guessed was twenty-five knots, gusting higher, which meant a crosswind for either direction of takeoff, which meant there was no good direction. There was only less bad.

And the tide was coming in faster than forecast. He'd checked the almanac himself, twice, with Freya, over coffee, arguing happily about minutes. The almanac said they had until mid-afternoon. The almanac, Tomas now understood, had never met this particular monsoon.

"Almanac says we had an hour," he said.

"The almanac," Freya said, running her preflight checks with hands that moved fast but didn't fumble, "is a book. Books don't watch the sky." She glanced up through the windshield at the cloud deck—low, torn, moving like it had somewhere urgent to be. "That does."

"Freya." He made himself say it plainly, because between the two of them plainness was the currency and they'd never once gone broke. "If the engine coughs on the roll, we're in the drink with a case full of paper and no raft."

"If the engine coughs on the roll," Freya said, "we swim, and I will be extremely annoyed with you forever."

"That's your comfort?"

"It's the best I've got." And she grinned at him—that grin, the one that had gotten him into every bad decision of his adult life, most of which he'd have chosen again—and Tomas felt the fear rearrange itself into something he could carry. Not smaller. Just carried together.

Behind them, down the bar, a shout went up from Varga's crew. He twisted in his seat. Two of them were wading toward the wreck's ribs, and the water around their knees was already moving the wrong way—sliding landward, quickening, the sea reaching up the sand like a hand closing.

"They're not going to make it off clean," he said.

"Not our problem."

"You sure?"

"I'm sure the case is our problem," Freya said. "I'm sure the survivors are our problem. I'm sure Amara is sitting in a boat behind that point praying we come back, and she is our problem. Those men made their choices on dry land, in daylight, with lawyers." She set the brakes, tested them, frowned at whatever the brakes told her. "The wilderness doesn't hold grudges, Tomas. It just keeps accounts. They opened theirs."

* * *

Freya had landed on worse strips. That was the honest truth of it, and she held onto it like a rail. She'd landed on lake ice with a crack singing down its middle; she'd landed on a gravel bar in '09 with a bear standing at the far end considering the situation. A sandbar was just a runway the ocean lent out and took back, and the whole trick of borrowing anything was knowing when to give it back.

She gave the engine throttle in increments, watching the manifold pressure, listening. The Lycoming caught and held, rough at idle, smoothing as the revolutions climbed. The bent strut transmitted a shudder through the airframe that she felt in her teeth. The soft tire would pull left. The crosswind would push left too, coming quartering across the bar from the sea side. Left, left. So she'd trim right and feed right rudder and pray to whichever saint handled crosswind landings, though in her experience that job fell to no saint at all, just to the next three seconds of your own attention.

"Flaps ten," she said, mostly so Tomas would know she was flying and not merely panicking with instruments.

"Flaps ten," he confirmed, and set them, and then, because he was Tomas: "You know my mother asked me last week why I keep flying with you."

"What'd you tell her?"

"Told her it's because somebody has to keep count of your sins." He braced a hand on the panel. "Starting to feel like a full-time position."

"Good benefits?"

"Terrible benefits. The pay is gossip and near-death."

"Near-death is the retirement plan," Freya said, and pushed the throttle to the stop.

* * *

The plane lurched forward and Tomas's stomach stayed where it was for a full second, which is how he knew it was serious.

Sand dragged at the wheels. The soft tire pulled hard left exactly as advertised, and Freya fed in right rudder with small, vicious corrections, and the Cessna slewed down the bar gathering speed in a way that felt less like acceleration and more like being thrown. Spray came off the seaward edge in long gray sheets and broke across the windshield, and for a moment Tomas was flying blind inside a drum of white noise, trusting a woman and a machine and four hundred meters of borrowed ground.

Then the windshield cleared and he saw the end of the bar and it was closer than he wanted and the water on it was not lapping anymore. It was running. Sheets of it, sliding diagonally across the sand ahead of them, silver and fast, the tide arriving all at once the way water does when it finally finds the low place—the whole ocean leaning in through a gap somewhere offshore, impatient after six days of waiting.

"Water on the strip," he said, calm, because someone had to narrate.

"I see it."

"There's quite a lot of it."

"I see it, Tomas."

The airspeed needle climbed. Sixty. The tail came light. The bent strut began to shake the whole aircraft like a dog with a rag, and the left wheel hit a sheet of running water and hydroplaned sideways a foot, and Freya held it—held it—with rudder and nerve and something older than either, and the bar's end came up at them, and past it the channel, gray-green and deep and absolutely certain of itself.

Seventy knots. She hauled back.

For one suspended heartbeat nothing happened, which is the ocean's favorite trick, letting you believe for just a moment that it has decided to be kind. Then the wheels left the sand with a sound like a gasp, and the Cessna staggered into the air with perhaps eight feet to spare, and the rising tide reached the spot where they'd been parked and closed over it without comment.

* * *

They did not climb so much as argue their way upward.

The crosswind shoved them toward the point, and Freya crabbed into it, wings rocking, the damaged strut groaning its objection to existing. Below and behind them the sandbar was already a smear, then a suggestion, then nothing at all—just white lines of surf stitching shut over the place where the Reckoning's ribs still stood, and would stand, until the archaeologists came with permits instead of guns.

Tomas watched it go and found he couldn't speak for a moment. Not from fear. From the strange grief of watching a place erase itself, taking with it the footprints of the worst and best morning of their lives.

"Varga's boats are pulling out," he managed finally. The fleet was weighing anchor in a hurry, three hulls swinging north into the channel, running for deeper water before the tide and the law caught up with them equally.

"Let them run," Freya said. Her voice was steady but her hands, he noticed, were doing that thing they did after—the fine tremor she always hid by gripping the yoke harder, as if the airplane might notice and lose confidence. "Radio check. Call the point."

He keyed the set. "Cape Reckoning traffic, Cessna niner-four-seven, airborne off the bar, manifest secure, inbound to the point. Anyone hearing, the tide's in and the bar's gone."

Static. Then Amara's voice, thin with distance and relief: *"Copy, nine-four-seven. We see you. God, we see you."*

"We're coming around for the cove," Freya said. "Tell the boat to stand off the rocks. My landing's going to be ugly."

*"How ugly?"*

"Ugly enough that nobody should film it."

There was a laugh on the other end, cracked and wet, and Tomas felt his own chest unclench by a degree. Then the engine hiccuped.

* * *

It was not a big hiccup. It was a single missed beat, a stumble in the rhythm, the kind of thing a mechanic hears and files away and dreams about later. But at two hundred feet, over open water, downwind of a rock point, in a gusting monsoon crosswind, with a bent strut and a fuel line that had taken salt spray on the roll—it was a sentence with a very clear ending.

"Tell me that was the wind," Tomas said.

Freya didn't answer. She was already scanning gauges, already easing the nose down to trade altitude for margin, already doing the arithmetic pilots do in silence: distance to the cove, height above the swell, weight of the aircraft, weight of the case, weight of everything they'd fought six tides to protect.

"Fuel pressure's flickering," she said. "Probably spray in the vents. Probably."

"And probably isn't a word you like."

"Probably is the only word I've got." She banked them around the point, and the cove opened below—gray water, steep sides, the survey team's boat bobbing where Amara and the others waited, and beyond it, tiny and impossibly dear, the thread of the airstrip road climbing toward home. "I can make the cove. I can make the beach past it. After that the engine and I are negotiating."

"Land on the beach?" Tomas looked at it. Narrow, littered with storm wrack, backed by trees, the surf coming ashore in long uneven sets. "That's not a beach, that's a rumor of one."

"Every runway's a rumor," Freya said. "Some are just better documented." She dropped the flaps another notch and the plane sank, wallowed, caught itself. "Get the case ready. If we go in the water short, you get it out and you swim for the boat and you don't look back."

"I'm not leaving you in—"

"*Tomas.*" Not sharp. Worse than sharp—quiet, the way she got when the truth had stopped being negotiable. "It's paper. It's the whole coast's paper. You swim, you deliver, you let me deal with the water. The water and I have an understanding."

"That's what worries me," he said, and unlatched the case's straps anyway, and held it in his lap like the child they'd never admit this whole mad family had been raising for six days.

Ahead, the beach grew. The engine stumbled again, twice, caught, stumbled. The swell marched in below them, tall and unhurried, keeping its ancient books, forgiving nothing, forgetting nothing, willing—as always—to wait.

Freya lined up on the sand and said, mostly to herself, mostly to her grandfather, mostly to the sky:

"Hold together. Just once more. Hold together."

The engine answered with a cough, a catch, and then—barely, grudgingly, like an old man agreeing to one more dance—held.

But the beach was shorter than it had looked, and the tide was already licking at its northern end, and Freya Hartwell had exactly one attempt to turn a rumor into a runway.
