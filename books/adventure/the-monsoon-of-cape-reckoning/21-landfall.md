## 21. Landfall

The strip at Cape Reckoning had never looked so beautiful or so short.

Freya saw it all in the half-second the nose came around into the wind: the gravel running out toward the treeline like a gray ribbon someone had dropped and forgotten, the hangar with its new dent, the crowd spilling off the porch of the Kettle & Crane because of course they'd heard her on the radio—every soul on the coast had heard her on the radio—and Tomas standing at the windsock with both arms up, which meant *land*, and then one arm chopping sideways, which meant *now*.

"Come on, old girl," she told the plane. The Cessna shuddered under her hands, one wheel shimmying where Varga's crewman's boot had caught the strut on the sandbar, the airframe groaning like a ship itself. Spray still beaded the windshield. Salt streaked back off the wing. Behind her, in the second seat, the manifest case sat strapped in where a passenger would ride, dry as a sermon.

The tide was coming for the bar behind her. She could see it in the mirror of her mind even now—the water folding over the sand in long silver sheets, swallowing the black ribs of the *Reckoning*, closing over the whole argument like a book. Let it. She'd gotten what mattered off that bar. Everything else down there belonged to somebody else now. That was rather the point.

The wheels touched.

It was not, strictly speaking, a landing. It was a controlled disagreement between an airplane and the earth, resolved in favor of the earth. The left tire, soft from a scrape Freya hadn't yet inventoried, grabbed the wet gravel and hauled the tail around, and the world became a smear of gray and green and one very surprised seagull, and then the wingtip kissed the ground the way you'd kiss a relative you tolerated, and then everything stopped moving except Freya's heart, which intended to keep going for some time.

Silence. Rain on the roof of the plane. The tick of cooling metal.

She checked the case first. Habit, she supposed—or maybe not habit at all. Maybe it was the newest thing she knew about herself: check the truth before you check your own bruises.

Then she put her forehead against the yoke and laughed, one ragged, disbelieving note, the kind of laugh that lives next door to crying and borrows its sugar.

The door handle stuck. Of course it did. She got it open on the third try and swung out onto the gravel on legs that had opinions of their own, and the crowd came across the strip like water finding a channel.

Tomas reached her first. He didn't say anything—he just took her by the shoulders and looked her over the way he looked over an engine after a hard run, top to bottom, cataloguing damage, and whatever he found seemed to satisfy him, because he pulled her into a hug that cracked something loose in her chest.

"You landed," he said into her hair.

"Barely."

"I watched barely." He held her out at arm's length. "That was barely with style points. The judges gave it a six."

"The judges can kiss my propeller."

Behind him the town arrived: Ruthie Okonkwo from the clinic with her medical bag already open; old Pieter Van Damm leaning on his cane and pretending he hadn't run the last fifty yards; the Delgado twins, nine years old and vibrating with the specific electricity of children who have just watched history land in their town and are furious they weren't allowed closer. And at the edge of them, two figures wrapped in blankets who had no business being upright—Amara Osei and Dev Chandra, the survey team's survivors, flown in ahead of the storm window by a floatplane pilot who owed Freya a favor and would now owe her considerably more.

Amara reached her first. She was thin as a winter branch and moved like one, but her grip was strong.

"The case," she said.

"Intact. Sealed. Slightly damp on one corner, like the rest of us."

Something went out of Amara's shoulders—a tension she'd been carrying, Freya realized, since the caves, maybe since the day she'd walked inland away from her own camp. "Then it's done," she said. "Whatever happens next, it's done."

"It's not done," said a voice from the crowd's edge, "until I say what happens next."

Inspector Halloran of the Regional Enforcement Service pushed through, rain jacket buttoned to the chin, expression arranged into official gravity. He'd come in on the same floatplane as the survivors, and he'd spent the flight, according to Tomas, reading documents and looking increasingly like a man watching his paperwork catch fire.

"Hartwell," he said. "You're aware you broadcast classified enforcement material over open frequencies."

"I'm aware I broadcast *lies* over open frequencies," Freya said. "Yours, specifically. The fraudulent heritage surveys your office signed off on. The permits Varga's fleet was operating under. The part where the cargo of the *Reckoning* was seized from trade networks that never sold it, documented in the ship's own manifest, which is currently strapped into my passenger seat getting rained on." She tilted her head. "Classified how, exactly? Classified like the coordinates you warned me off? Classified like the search grid that conveniently pointed east?"

A murmur went through the crowd. Halloran's jaw worked.

"That matter," he said, "is under review."

"Everything's under review," Freya said. "That's the whole trouble with this coast. Under review since 1897." She nodded at the case. "You want it? Fine. Take it. But you take it in front of witnesses, with a chain-of-custody form filled out here on the hood of Ruthie's truck, and you take it knowing that copies exist in four places I'm not going to tell you about, and that Amara Osei has read every page twice and has a memory like a tide table. This isn't evidence disappearing into an evidence locker, Inspector. This is evidence going home. Eventually. After you've done your job."

Halloran looked at her for a long moment. Then, to Freya's genuine surprise, something in his face shifted—not softening exactly, but settling, like a man putting down a weight he'd been carrying wrong.

"Varga's officers are on the *Meridian Star* right now," he said. "My people boarded twenty minutes ago. Her captain's license is suspended pending investigation. The fleet's permits are void." He paused. "For what it's worth, Hartwell—I signed those surveys because the assessments came through channels I trusted. That trust was misplaced. That's on me, and it'll be in my report."

"Well," said Pieter Van Damm loudly, from the front row of the entire town, "that's more honesty than we've had off a government boat since the ferry stopped running."

Laughter rippled through the crowd, and the knot in Freya's chest loosened another turn.

* * *

They opened the case right there on the strip, because Amara insisted and because, Freya suspected, everyone present wanted to see it with their own eyes before any institution got its hands on it. Halloran spread a tarp over the Cessna's wing. Ruthie contributed a clean sheet from her medical stores. The rain thinned to a drizzle, as if the sky were also curious.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against a century and a half of seawater, lay the papers: the *Reckoning*'s cargo manifest, ink brown but legible, listing bales and boxes and crates—and beside each entry, in a clerk's careful hand, the words *seized, no bill of sale* over and over like a heartbeat. Beneath the manifest, letters. A colonial administrator's correspondence admitting the cargo had been taken from trading families whose descendants still lived along this coast. A complaint from a ship's officer who thought the seizures disgraceful and had written it down, bless him, because there is always one. And a claim register showing the wreck had been reported in 1901 and quietly struck from the record by an official whose signature matched, Amara noted with terrible calm, the family name of a company that still operated in the region today.

"Every artifact in that hold," Amara said, her voice carrying across the silent strip, "has a living community it belongs to. Not belongs to a museum. Belongs to *people*. The manifest proves the taking was theft, and the cover-up proves they knew it." She closed the case gently, like tucking in a child. "Custody should pass to the descendant communities. All of them. Jointly."

"There will be hearings," Halloran said. "Process. It won't be fast."

"It hasn't been fast for a hundred and forty years," Amara said. "We can wait a little longer. We've gotten good at it." Then, softer, almost to herself: "But not invisible anymore."

Freya felt those last words go through her like a splinter finding its depth.

* * *

The Kettle & Crane that evening was fuller than Freya had ever seen it, and quieter than she'd ever heard it. Word had traveled, as word does. Not just about the wreck, the arrest, the manifest—about the *other* thing. The thing the radio broadcast had dragged up alongside everything else.

Because halfway through her transmission from the sandbar, voice shaking, spray freezing on her lips, Freya Hartwell had said: *"My grandfather found this wreck sixty years ago. He logged it and then he hid the log. He hid it so the government wouldn't come, so the salvage men wouldn't come, so this coast could stay ours and left alone. He chose silence. I'm choosing differently. Judge us both if you need to."*

Now the whole town had judged, apparently, and the verdict was soup.

Ruthie had pressed a bowl into her hands without a word. Marta at the Kettle had refused her money three times and threatened a fourth. Old Pieter had raised his glass and said only, "To the ones who kept us safe when we didn't know we needed keeping," and the room had drunk, and Freya had had to look very hard at the ceiling beam for a moment.

But there were other looks too. Not hostile—nothing so simple. Curious looks. Recalculating looks. The look of people discovering that the map of their town had an extra room in it all along. A fisherman named Cole, whom Freya had known her whole life, caught her eye across the room and held it a beat longer than comfortable, and she couldn't read what was in his face, and that unreadability was the cost, she understood. Not exile. Just—knowledge. The town would never again be the place where the Hartwells were simply the Hartwells, bush pilots and fixers and gossips. They were now the family who had known. Who had decided. For sixty years.

Tomas slid onto the bench beside her with two cups of something amber and medicinal.

"You okay?" he asked.

"I crashed an airplane, arrested a salvage empire with a radio, and found out my grandfather was either a saint or a liar depending on the decade." She turned the cup in her hands. "And half the town is being kind to me and the other half is being *careful* with me, and I honestly don't know which is worse."

"They're the same thing," Tomas said. "Careful is just kind with questions in it." He clinked his cup against hers. "Your grandfather kept a secret to protect people. You broke it to protect more people. Anybody in this room who can't do that arithmetic wasn't going to do it anyway."

From across the room, Amara raised her cup to Freya—slowly, deliberately, the way you salute across a distance that matters.

"So," Tomas said. "What now? The hearings, the custody fight, the archaeologists descending like gulls on a fish boat. You going to stick around for all that?"

Freya looked out the fogged window, past her own reflection, toward the dark strip where her battered Cessna sat under tarps, one wheel flat, wingtip dented, engine full of salt air and grievance. Toward the point beyond it, where the sixth tide was even now rolling high over a place that had kept its secret for a century and a half and didn't need to anymore.

"Tomas," she said, "somebody has to fly the first survey team out to that site when the weather clears. Somebody local, who knows the winds off the point, who won't charge the communities an arm and a leg for the privilege." She drank. "Besides, I hear the archive in Port Meridian takes donations of old logbooks. And I happen to know where there's one that's been missing a few pages of honesty."

Tomas grinned. "Sixty years of silence, ending in one week."

"Better late than filed," Freya said.

Outside, far off over the water, thunder rolled—soft now, retreating, the monsoon spending its last temper somewhere out past the islands. The storm was breaking. In the morning, for the first time in weeks, the sky over Cape Reckoning would be clear all the way to the horizon, and Freya Hartwell intended to be airborne in it, carrying nothing but passengers, fuel, and the truth—finally, finally light enough to lift.
