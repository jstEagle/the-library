## 22. Clear Air After Rain

The archive at Cape Reckoning occupied the back half of the old customs house, which meant it smelled of salt, old paper, and the particular despair of a dehumidifier that had been fighting the monsoon since before Freya was born. The archivist, a round and cheerful woman named Odile who had once been the schoolteacher and had never quite stopped treating everyone like a promising student, looked up from her desk when the bell over the door rang.

"Freya Hartwell," she said. "Twice in one month. People will talk."

"People have been talking about me all season. I figured I'd give them something accurate for once."

She set the logbook on the counter between them. It was smaller than she remembered it being as a child, when she'd found it in the bottom of his flight bag and been told—gently, firmly, in that voice that ended conversations—that it was pilot business and none of hers. Forty years of entries in a hand that started careful and got looser as the years went on, the way hands do. And in the middle of it, the gaps. The smudged coordinates. The flights that never appeared in any filed plan, logged only here, in ink he'd tried to blur with his own thumb and hadn't quite managed.

Odile put on her glasses. She didn't touch it at first. Archivists, Freya had learned, had a kind of reverence that pilots reserved for engines.

"This is Elias Hartwell's?"

"It is."

"The one from the hearings? The one they kept asking about?"

"The very one." Freya slid it across. "Full provenance. My grandfather's, my inheritance, and as of this morning, nobody's secret."

Odile opened the cover. Her eyebrows went up slowly, like a tide coming in. "Oh," she said softly. "Oh, Freya. Do you know what's in here?"

"I've read every page. Twice." She had done most of that reading at three in the morning, sitting at her kitchen table with tea going cold, learning her grandfather one confession at a time. He had not hidden the Reckoning out of greed. He had not even hidden it, mostly, out of fear. He had hidden it because in 1961, a revealed wreck carrying seized cargo and fraudulent colonial claims would have given the government exactly the excuse it wanted to void the land titles of every community from Cape Reckoning to the upper river. *If the coast is built on stolen things,* he had written, *they will say the coast itself is stolen, and take it.* So he had flown his unfiled flights, smudged his coordinates, and carried the silence home like ballast for forty years.

He had been wrong, probably. But he had been wrong the way you're wrong when you're trying to keep everyone you love inside the circle of your arms, and Freya had decided she could live with loving him for it while refusing to inherit it.

"There are pages here that contradict the official survey record," Odile said, already reaching for cotton gloves. "Pages that support Amara Okafor's testimony. This is—" She stopped herself, the way teachers do, and looked at Freya over her glasses. "How do you want it catalogued?"

"Open access. No embargo. If a fourteen-year-old kid doing a school project wants to read what my grandfather hid, I want her reading it by Thursday."

Odile smiled. "You know what this makes you, don't you?"

"Tired?"

"A donor. With a plaque."

"I'll allow a plaque if it doesn't use the word 'heroic.' My grandfather would rise from the grave purely to be embarrassed."

They filled out the forms together. Freya signed her name in the accession register, right under the date, and felt something shift in her chest that she couldn't immediately name—not lightness exactly, because the book still weighed what it weighed—but something like setting down a load you'd been carrying so long you'd forgotten it wasn't part of your body.

Outside, the air did the thing it had been doing for two weeks now: it was clean. The monsoon had broken on a Tuesday with a thunderclap that rattled every window in town, and after that the sky simply gave up its grudge. The light came in low and gold off the water. The airstrip grass was growing back green where the flooding had flattened it, and Tomas had already complained twice about having to mow around the tie-downs, which was how Freya knew he was happy. Tomas complained when he was happy the way other men whistled.

She walked back along the harbor road, past the customs house and the chandlery and the fish plant running full shift again, and thought: *this is what it cost.* Not the crash landing, though her shoulder still ached on cold mornings and the Cessna was on her third inspection of the rebuilt wing spar. Not the hearings, though those had gone on long enough that she'd learned to nap sitting up. The cost was this: everyone knew now. Knew about the wreck, about the manifest, about Varga's forged heritage surveys, and yes—about the Hartwells. About sixty years of a family keeping the whole coast invisible to keep it safe. Old Mrs. Calloway had said something about it at the market, not unkindly, in the way of people who have decided to forgive you but want credit for the effort. *"Your grandfather must have loved this place an awful lot, to lie to it that long."*

That was the sentence Freya kept turning over. Not accusation. Almost tenderness. The town had taken the truth the way it took weather—grumbling, adjusting, carrying on—and somehow that was harder and better than either forgiveness or fury.

At the hangar, Tomas was under the Cessna with a grease rag and an expression of profound grievance.

"You're mowing again," she said.

"I'm *contemplating* mowing. There's a difference. Contemplation is free." He rolled out, stood, and cracked his back in three distinct stages. "Well? Did the dragon lady accept your offering?"

"Odile accepted it. And she's not a dragon, she's just thorough."

"Thorough people are dragons with filing systems." He tossed her the rag to wipe her hands, an old ritual. "So it's done. The book's public."

"It's done."

"Huh." He looked at her for a moment, and whatever he saw made him nod once, slow. "Elias would've hated it for about a week. Then he'd have snuck down there himself to read it, pretending he was checking on something."

"That's exactly what he'd have done."

"And you?" Tomas asked, gentler than usual. "You okay with it? Really?"

Freya considered lying, considered the truth, and picked something in between, which was their native language. "I keep waiting to feel like I betrayed him. Instead I feel like I finally met him. All of him. Turns out I like the whole man better than the statue."

Tomas nodded again. "Good. Now come look at your left magneto, because it's making a sound I don't like, and if you're taking Okafor up today like you promised, I want it fixed first."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to ask with your whole face. Sit down and let me work."

* * *

Amara Okafor arrived at the strip at four in the afternoon, walking from the taxi like someone still learning that she was allowed to walk places without looking over her shoulder. Six weeks out of the caves and the cast was off her wrist; the limp was nearly gone. She wore the survey team jacket still, patched at the elbow, and carried a canvas bag that clinked.

"You brought tools?" Freya said, helping her into the passenger seat.

"Samples. And a camera. And—" she patted the bag, "—a thermos of my mother's ginger tea, because you look like a woman who has been signing paperwork all day."

"I have been signing paperwork all day. You may be a witch."

"Archaeologist. Close enough." Amara settled the headset on, moving carefully around her healing ribs, and looked out the windshield at the runway with an expression Freya recognized from mirrors: the hunger of a person about to go somewhere the ground can't follow. "I haven't flown since the survey plane dropped us. That was—" she paused, recalibrating, "—I used to love it."

"You'll love it again. First minute's the worst. After that it's just the best room in the world."

Tomas gave them the wave-off from the hangar, arms crossed, wearing the face of a man who trusted no magneto anywhere. Freya ran up the checks, felt the engine settle into its honest drone, and took the runway into the evening light.

The climb out over the harbor was the thing she never got tired of—the town shrinking into its own outline, the fish plant smoke standing straight up in the clean air, the point unspooling ahead of them with its lighthouse blinking early against the blue. Amara had gone quiet in the way people go quiet when they're holding themselves very still so nothing breaks the spell.

"Left rudder a touch," Freya said, "and look down."

The Reckoning site lay just off the sandbar, marked now by four yellow buoys and a permanent exclusion float with a flag on it. At low tide, on clear days, you could see the dark ribs of the hull through six feet of water, the great curved bones of her, and the mound of the hold where the artifacts still lay in their layers of silt and history, waiting for the descendant communities' recovery teams to bring them up at their own pace, on their own terms. The first dive barge was due next month. Amara's team—*her* team now, reconstituted, properly funded, with her name on the permit—would lead it.

"There," Freya said, banking gently so the sun came through the water. "That's her."

Amara leaned toward the glass. For a long moment she said nothing, and Freya flew the circle and let her look.

"She's smaller than I imagined," Amara said finally. "From underneath, in the dark, she felt enormous. Like a country."

"Everything feels enormous in the dark. That's why we write things down." Freya leveled out, heading south along the coast, giving her the long view. "Speaking of which. How's the report?"

"Finished. Two hundred pages. Varga's surveys weren't just sloppy—they were manufactured. Same handwriting on supposedly independent assessments, dates altered in three archives. The tribunal accepted it last week. Her licenses are suspended pending criminal review." Amara's voice was flat and precise, the voice of someone who had earned every fact the hard way. "She'll probably surface again somewhere. People like that always do. But not here."

"Not here," Freya agreed.

They flew the coastline in the gold light, past the river mouth where she and Tomas had hiked through the rain chasing footprints, past the ridge where the survey team's lean-to still stood, left as evidence, slowly becoming a landmark. Amara pointed out features like a tour guide of her own ordeal, and gradually the flat precision warmed into something else—wonder, maybe, or the beginning of a story she'd tell for the rest of her life.

Near the end of the hour, as Freya turned for home, Amara reached into the canvas bag and pulled out something wrapped in cloth. She passed it over.

"What's this?"

"Open it at altitude. Tradition. I just invented it."

Inside the cloth was a small brass fitting, green with age—a ship's nameplate fragment, salvaged under permit from the debris field outside the protected hull. Stamped into it, worn but legible: *RECKONING.*

Freya held it in her palm and let the propeller's vibration hum through it. "I can't take this. It belongs to—"

"It belongs to everyone," Amara said. "That's the entire point of everything we did. But the recovery teams voted, and this piece came up in the first survey tranche, and they said the pilot who broadcast the manifest to the whole coast should have the first thing that ever came off that ship legally." She shrugged, suddenly shy. "It was unanimous. I lost the vote to fly it out here myself, but then I got the ride anyway, so really I won."

Freya laughed, and it came out rougher than she intended. She wrapped the plate back in its cloth and stowed it in the map pocket beside her knee, where her grandfather's charts used to live.

Below, Cape Reckoning came back into view, lit up for the evening, every window a small defiance of the coming dark. The airstrip grass glowed green. On the apron, a small figure was mowing around the tie-downs after all, muttering visibly even from eight hundred feet.

"He's mowing," Amara observed.

"He's happy. Don't tell him I said so."

She lined up final, called her position on the frequency out of pure habit even though the whole coast knew her voice by now, and set the wheels down soft in the long gold light. As they rolled out, past the customs house where a certain logbook sat in its new drawer under cotton gloves and a plaque pending, Freya felt the ache in her shoulder and the weight in her map pocket and the clean air coming through the vents, and thought that inheritance, it turned out, was not what you were given.

It was what you carried forward, and who you carried it to.

She taxied in. Tomas waved them down with both arms, then pointed sternly at the engine cowling, then at his watch, then, relenting, at the thermos in Amara's bag, because he could smell ginger tea through aluminum at fifty yards and had never once pretended otherwise.

"Come on," Freya said, unbuckling. "Tea first. Then I'll show you what the dragon archivist sent home with me."

Amara raised an eyebrow. "Sent home with you?"

"A form." Freya grinned, hopping down onto the warm tarmac. "For the next donation. Turns out I've got my grandmother's survey notebooks too, and apparently—" she glanced back at the plane, at the sky still holding the last of the light, "—the archive thinks the family silence might have been a two-generation habit. Somebody's got to read them."

She said it lightly. But her heart was already climbing.
