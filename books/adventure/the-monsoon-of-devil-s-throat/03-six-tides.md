## 3. Six Tides

The harbor at Port Calloway smelled of diesel and brine and the particular rot of fish left too long in sun, and Elodie Winterbourne stood on the quay with a brass strongbox under her arm like a woman carrying an unexploded shell.

She had not slept. The tide had chased her out of the cave at half past two in the morning, water rising around her ankles in the dark while she clutched documents she hadn't yet read, and she had rowed back to her survey skiff with her heart hammering out a rhythm she refused to translate into anything so soft as fear. Now it was nine. The fishing fleet had gone out hours ago. Gulls argued over the gutting tables. Everything was ordinary, and nothing was.

The harbormaster's office had a spare room — a records room, really, smelling of mildew and old ink, where charts of the coast went back to 1890. She'd rented it before, during earlier surveys. The clerk, a stooped man named Ferro who had never once asked her a question she didn't answer first, took her money and handed her the key without looking up from his newspaper.

"Storm coming," he said. "Three days, maybe four."

"I know."

"Everyone knows. Barometer's been falling since Tuesday." He turned a page. "You surveying through it?"

"No." She hesitated at the door of the records room, the strongbox heavy against her ribs. "Ferro. Do you keep tide tables for Devil's Throat? Long series?"

"Back to the forties. Why?"

"How many tides between now and Saturday's high water?"

He looked at her then, properly, over the top of his spectacles, and something in her face must have told him not to pursue it, because he only shrugged and said, "Six. Six tides, give or take the storm's surge. Why do you care about Saturday?"

*Because someone else is counting them too,* she almost said. *Because I watched a ship's boat lower a sounding line off the Throat at dawn and I know what a hydrographic survey looks like when it isn't one.* But she said only, "Engineering curiosity," and closed the door.

The lock clicked. She set the strongbox on the scarred oak table beneath the single green-shaded lamp, and for a long moment she simply looked at it.

It was smaller than she remembered from the cave — everything shrank in daylight, she supposed, even wonders. Thirty centimeters by twenty, brass gone green-black with eighty years of salt, the hinges fused except where she'd forced them with a pry bar from her own kit, which still shamed her a little; you didn't open other people's graves with your tools. Stamped into the lid, worn nearly smooth but unmistakable to anyone who had grown up tracing it on letterheads and silver and the flyleaf of every book in her grandfather's library: the Winterbourne crest. A compass rose over crossed hammers. *We Measure True*, said the motto underneath, and she had always thought it quaint.

Her hands were steady. That was the strange part. Seven years of measuring grief in precise units had taught her body this trick: when the thing inside got too large, the hands went perfectly, terribly still.

She opened it fully and laid the contents out on the table in a row, the way she'd lay out instruments before a survey. Method first. Feeling later. If ever.

A ledger book, water-swollen, its pages fused into gray felt at the edges but the center leaves legible. A oilskin packet, tied with tarred twine. And a document — a single sheet, folded twice, the paper stiff as bark.

She unfolded that one first because it was the lightest and lightness frightened her least.

The header read: **APPLICATION FOR EXCLUSIVE SALVAGE RIGHTS — WRECK OF THE S.S. MERIDIAN STAR**.

Her grandfather's signature sat at the bottom. Elias Winterbourne, in the confident downstroke she knew from a hundred engineering drawings, from the margins of her childhood textbooks, from the last letter he ever wrote her. Below the signature, the date.

Elodie read the date four times.

Then she took out her notebook — the same leather-bound book she used for every survey, columns ruled in her own hand — and wrote the wreck's date beside it. October 14th, 1911. The night the Meridian Star went down off Devil's Throat with all hands, thirty-one souls, the disaster that had made her family's fortune and its legend: Elias Winterbourne, the man who had braved the cave and claimed the gold, the hero of the story her father told at every dinner table until the telling wore grooves in it.

The salvage application was dated October 11th.

Three days before the ship sank.

The lamp hummed. Somewhere outside, a winch clanked. Elodie stood very still and let the arithmetic do what arithmetic does, which is destroy things quietly, without raising its voice.

Ships did not apply for salvage rights before they were wrecks. Men did not file claims on disasters that had not happened — unless they knew. Unless the disaster was not weather, not reef, not fate, but *arrangement*. Unless the thirty-one names on the memorial plaque in the Port Calloway churchyard were not mourned by the man who signed this page but *spent* by him, converted into a fortune with a signature three days in advance, the way you'd convert currency at a favorable rate.

*We Measure True.*

She sat down slowly on the clerk's stool. Her hands were still steady. She hated them for it.

* * *

The ledger took longer, because grief is patient but water is not, and she had to steam the fused pages apart over the room's little kettle, leaf by leaf, drying each on the lamp's warm housing like a woman pressing flowers. What emerged was her grandfather's hand throughout — survey notes, soundings, tide calculations in his beautiful drafting script — and threaded through them, in a different ink, entries that were not surveys at all.

Cargo manifests. Insurance valuations. A list of names with amounts beside them, and the word *settled* repeated down the margin like a rosary.

And on the last legible page, dated the day before the sinking, a single line that stopped her breath:

*Throat will take her at the ebb. Cave holds what the sea gives. Tell no one until papers are filed.*

Tell no one until papers are filed. He had known the cave would hold the wreck — had *counted on* it — because he had measured those waters himself, years before, as a young engineer mapping the coastline for the railway spur. He had known exactly where the ship would go down, exactly where the current would drag her, exactly which chamber of the sea cave would cradle her hull beyond the reach of any casual recovery. He had engineered his own legend the way he engineered bridges: to spec, on schedule, with tolerances calculated to the millimeter.

And the crew — the manifest in the oilskin packet confirmed it, thirty-one names in a clerk's careful hand — the crew had simply been the ballast cost of the operation. Men who trusted their captain, their company, their sea. Men who had no idea that their route had been sold, their deaths pre-filed, their salvage value assessed before they weighed anchor.

Elodie closed the ledger. Sat with her palms flat on the cover as if she could hold the dead in place through sheer pressure.

Seven years she had carried her grandfather's memory like a lantern. It had lit every dark stretch of her life since the funeral — since the accident she still could not name, the one she outran mile by mile along dead rail lines, measuring distance because distance obeyed rules and loss did not. The lantern had been the best thing about her family. And now she had opened it and found the fuel was made of people.

She thought of the crest on the lid. *We Measure True.* He had measured true. That was the horror of it — not that he lied, but that he hadn't needed to. The numbers were honest. Only the men were not.

* * *

She was repacking the oilskin packet when the light changed.

Not the lamp — the window. A shadow had slid across the harbor glass, slow and deliberate, and Elodie looked up from the table with the instinct of a woman who has spent seven years reading water for a living.

A vessel was coming into the outer anchorage. Not a fishing boat. Not a coaster. A white-hulled survey ship, maybe forty meters, with a crane amidships and a helicopter deck aft and a hull shape that said *money* the way her grandfather's signature said confidence. On her bow, in letters she could read even at this distance through the salt-filmed glass: **MERIDIAN SALVAGE & RECOVERY LTD.**

She laughed once — a short, ugly sound with nothing in it — because of course. Of course the company would name itself that. Predators always wore the skin of the thing they ate.

She moved to the window. The ship was anchoring in the outer roads, well inside territorial water, well positioned for the Throat. As she watched, a launch dropped from her side and began a slow circuit of the harbor mouth, and standing in the bow of the launch was a man with binoculars who swept them across the quay, across the records office windows, across her.

She stepped back from the glass before the lenses found her face. Old habit. Surveyors learn early that being seen changes what gets measured.

On the desk, her notebook lay open to the page where she'd written the dates. Three days' difference. She stared at it, and then at the strongbox, and then at the ship settling on its anchors with the calm of something that had already won.

She understood the machinery now, all of it, the whole elegant mechanism. Salvage law was tide law: finders' rights flowed to whoever filed first on a wreck not under claim, and somewhere in a registry office there was a filing cabinet, and somewhere in that cabinet there was either a forged claim or a genuine one, and either way the clock was running. Six tides. Ferro had counted them for her without knowing why. By Saturday's high water, everything in that cave — the hull, the manifests, the truth itself — would belong to Meridian Salvage & Recovery Ltd., legally, cleanly, irreversibly. They wouldn't even need to steal it. They'd just need to be faster than grief.

Unless someone filed first. Unless someone had documents older than theirs, truer than theirs, and the nerve to use them.

Elodie looked down at her own hands again, and this time she understood their stillness. It wasn't shock. It was calibration. All those years of measuring distances instead of feeling things, all those closed lines and dead rails and precise, meaningless numbers — they had been training for this. For the moment when the only instrument left that worked was her.

She opened the notebook to a fresh page and drew a single line down the middle. On the left she wrote: *Tide 1 — tonight, 21:40.* On the right, after a moment, she wrote: *File counter-claim. Find the descendants. Read everything.*

Below that, in smaller letters, she added the only sentence she allowed herself:

*He spent thirty-one lives. I have six tides to spend proving it.*

Outside, the survey launch completed its circuit and turned back toward its mother ship, and the first clouds of the monsoon came up over the headland like smoke from a fire below the horizon.
