## 4. The Fleet at Anchor

Marcus Vane had spent thirty-one years learning to read water the way other men read faces, and the water off Devil's Throat was lying to him.

It lay flat and green as bottle glass in the late afternoon light, which was wrong for this coast in this season. The pilot charts called the anchorage untenable above Force 4; the swell model on his tablet agreed. Yet here it lay, smooth as poured pewter, holding his three vessels as gently as a hand holds eggs — the *Corvus*, his crane barge; the *Tern*, the survey launch; and the old supply tender *Magpie* riding low on her waterline with enough gear crammed below decks to strip a cathedral.

The sea was being polite. Vane did not trust polite seas.

He stood at the rail of the *Corvus* with a mug of coffee gone cold in his fist and watched the cliffs come apart into shadow. Devil's Throat got its name honestly: a mile of basalt where the land had been torn open like something swallowed whole, black walls dropping sheer into forty fathoms, riddled with sea caves that the old charts marked not with soundings but with warnings. *Local knowledge required.* *Do not attempt in swell.* One chart from 1911 — he'd bought the reproduction in Port Calloway that morning, along with fresh batteries and a newspaper he hadn't read — carried a marginal note in faded copperplate: *Cave mouths numerous. The natives will not dive them.*

Vane didn't believe in curses. He believed in tonnage, tide tables, and paperwork. And the paperwork, folded in a waterproof case inside his cabin, was beautiful.

Three sheets, letterhead of the Maritime Salvage Authority, embossed seal, signatures in blue-black ink. Exclusive salvage rights to the wreck of the *Meridian Star*, lost with all hands off Devil's Throat, 14 October 1911. Rights granted to Halcyon Deep Recovery Ltd., effective upon filing, enforceable for ninety days. Six tides of quiet before anyone official would think to look twice, and by then the site would be a hole in the seabed and the *Meridian Star* would exist only as inventory on a barge manifest.

The signatures were forged. Vane knew because he'd paid for them, and he'd paid a man in Singapore who charged what good work cost. The seal was real — lifted from a decommissioned office during an archive renovation, which was the kind of detail amateurs missed and auditors never forgave. The paper stock was correct to the year. He'd checked it against three authenticated documents under raking light in a hotel room while rain hammered the shutters, and when he'd finished he'd sat for a long moment with the case closed on his knees, feeling nothing in particular, which was its own kind of professional satisfaction.

Thirty-one years in salvage and every legitimate claim he'd ever worked had been picked over by someone faster, richer, or better connected. The honest wrecks were stripped before you could file. What remained were the wrecks nobody wanted — too deep, too dangerous, too tangled in litigation — and the wrecks nobody knew about. This one, he suspected, was both.

His retirement account said otherwise. Eighteen months, maybe twenty if the market sulked, and then a house above a cold harbor somewhere, a boat he'd actually sail, and no more mornings listening to young divers breathe through their regulators like frightened men. One haul. That was the arithmetic. It always came down to arithmetic, and Marcus Vane had never once flinched at a sum.

"Skipper." Dez Okonkwo, his survey chief, came up the ladder with a tablet under one arm and salt drying white on his forearms. Twenty-six years old, brilliant with sonar, constitutionally incapable of knocking. "You're going to want to see the side-scan."

"I've seen the side-scan."

"You've seen yesterday's side-scan." He held out the tablet. "I ran the cave mouth again after lunch. Tide dropped further than the almanac predicted — freak low, maybe a meter under datum. Enough to dry the threshold."

Vane took the tablet. The image resolved in grays: the cliff face as a wall of hard echo, the cave mouth as a black notch, and within the notch —

"There," Dez said, tapping.

Vane looked. At first it read as noise, a smear of return cluttering the floor of the entrance gallery. Then his eye did what thirty-one years had trained it to do, and the noise organized itself into geometry. A hull. Ribbed frames showing through collapsed planking, laid over at perhaps fifteen degrees, half-buried in sediment. A ship's skeleton, curled in the throat of the cave like something that had crawled in there to die.

"The *Meridian Star*," Dez said. "Or what's left of her. She's inside, skipper. Not on the reef outside like the histories say. Inside."

Vane zoomed. The wreck filled the screen, and the screen went quiet around him — the deck sounds, the generator thrum, the gulls arguing over the stern — all of it receding behind the pulse in his ears. A sealed wreck. Unsalvaged since 1911. Cargo manifest unknown, but a coastal packet steamer of that class running that route in that season would have carried bullion for the mining houses, or specie, or both. His mind began, helplessly, to multiply.

Then Dez tapped the screen again, lower down, near the cave threshold where the rock shelved into the waterline.

"And there's this."

A line. Thin, bright, cutting across the sediment fan at the entrance like a scratch across glass. Vane enlarged it until the pixels broke apart, but the shape held: a groove in the silt, parallel striations, the unmistakable signature of a rope hauled taut across stone.

Fresh rope marks.

"How fresh?" he asked, though he already knew the answer lived in the question itself. Sediment in a sealed cave resettles in hours. Anything cutting a clean line through it had happened recently. Days at most.

"Tide's been covering that shelf since yesterday evening," Dez said. "So — inside the last day and a half. Somebody went down there between the lows." He paused, and Vane heard him choose his next words with unusual care. "Somebody who knew the tide was going to drop. That wasn't luck, skipper. That almanac error isn't published anywhere. You'd have to be surveying this coast yourself to catch it."

Vane handed back the tablet and looked out at the water, which still lay flat and false and courteous, and felt the particular cold that had visited him only a handful of times in his career — the cold of arriving second.

Someone had found the wreck first. Someone had gone down alone, on a freak tide, with rope and nerve, and had stood inside the greatest find of Vane's working life before his anchors were even down.

"Who surveys this coast?" he asked.

Dez shrugged. "Rail people, maybe. There's a decommissioned spur along the clifftop — I saw the old survey stakes. Or fishermen. Or—" He stopped.

"Or whoever filed against us," Vane said quietly. "Check the registry again. Every claim, every objection, every inquiry touching this grid square in the last five years. Names, vessels, home ports. Go."

Dez went. Vane stayed at the rail until the light failed and the cliffs became a single black mass with the cave mouth invisible somewhere in its throat, and he thought about the rope marks the way a chess player thinks about an opponent's last move — not as a threat, but as information. Whoever it was had gone down once. Once meant discovery, not recovery. Nobody strips a wreck solo on a single low tide. They'd have taken what they could carry and left the rest, and they'd be back, because finds like this made liars of patient people.

Fine. Let them come back. Vane had six tides of legal cover, three vessels, a dive team that owed him their certifications, and a crane that could lift a locomotive. If a rival wanted to contest the site, they'd have to do it in court, and by the time any court convened, the *Meridian Star* would be ballast and memory.

Still. He found himself looking at the flat green water and thinking: *polite seas lie.*

He went below, unlocked the waterproof case, and read the forged papers once more — not for reassurance but for rhythm, the way a musician reads a score before a performance. Then he opened his logbook and wrote, in the small blocky hand that thirty-one years of salt spray had worn down to essentials:

*Site confirmed. Wreck intact, interior. Anomaly: recent human activity at entrance. Assume rival interest. Accelerate.*

Underneath, after a moment, he added: *Retirement is a country with no charts. Sail carefully.*

* * *

Seven miles north, in the harbor master's office at Port Calloway, Elodie Winterbourne spread the contents of the brass strongbox across a scarred table under a swinging lamp, and the lamplight made the old ink look wet.

Her grandfather's name looked back at her from the top sheet. *Salvage claim, provisional.* Dated 11 October 1911. Three days before the *Meridian Star* ever touched the reef.

She had spent the afternoon telling herself there was an innocent explanation — clerical error, backdated filing, some bureaucratic quirk of a century-dead registrar. She was good at telling herself things. Seven years of practice. But engineers don't get to live in explanations; they get to live in load paths, in what actually carries weight, and the weight of that date bore down through the paper like a cracked girder.

He had known. Her grandfather — the legend, the rescuer, the man whose portrait hung in the maritime museum two streets away — had filed a salvage claim on a ship three days before she sank.

Elodie pressed her palms flat against the table until the wood bit back, and made herself breathe, and reached for the next document.

Outside the window, far down the coast, the lights of an anchored fleet pricked the dark off Devil's Throat — three vessels riding at station where no honest operator would anchor, in water that was lying flat the way water does when it's waiting.

She counted the lights twice. Three ships, six tides, one confession.

Whatever came next, it would come fast.

* * *

On the *Corvus*, Marcus Vane slept badly for the first time in years, and woke before dawn to Dez shaking his shoulder, tablet in hand, face gray in the emergency lighting.

"Skipper. The registry came back." He swallowed. "There's one inquiry on our grid square. Filed four days ago. Single applicant." He turned the tablet around.

Vane read the name, and the cold came back, and this time it stayed.

"Winterbourne," he said.
