## 6. The Descendants' List

The journal named them the way a coroner names them — coldly, completely, without mercy for anyone still breathing.

*Meridian Star, lost with all hands, 14 October 1911. Crew manifest as filed:*

Eleven names. Elodie copied them onto the back of a tide table in her own careful surveyor's hand, because her hand was the steadiest thing she owned and she needed something steady. *Aldous Merrick, boatswain. Peter Reyes, deckhand. Samuel Okafor, fireman.* Eight more beneath them, each one a life her grandfather had sold for the price of an insurance settlement and a salvage claim dated three days before the drowning.

She had not slept. The lamp in her grandfather's study had burned down to a stub, and the room smelled of dust and old varnish and, now, of something worse — the particular staleness of a secret kept too long in a closed house. She sat among his field journals like a woman sitting in wreckage of her own making.

Seven years she had measured grief in miles. Now she understood that the miles had always led here, to this desk, this list, this arithmetic of the dead. Every closed line she'd surveyed, every decommissioned spur she'd walked alone, was a track laid toward this room by a girl who couldn't name what she was running from and hadn't known she was running *toward* it.

Peter Reyes. Deckhand, aged twenty-three according to the marginal note in her grandfather's cramped script — a note that wasn't about the man at all but about the *problem* of him. *"R. asked too many questions at the hiring hall. Struck twice last spring. Will be on the boat regardless — no one will look for him."*

No one would look for him. Because no one was meant to find him.

Elodie folded the tide table along its crease until the eleven names were hidden inside it, then unfolded it again, unable to bear concealing them even from herself. Somewhere out there, if the sea had been kind across four generations, someone carried one of these names forward. Someone had lost whatever the Reyes family lost when the Meridian Star went down with blacklisted strikers aboard and a salvage claim already signed, sealed, and waiting like a spider in its web.

She found the first thread in the port archive, three days later, sitting under a flickering fluorescent tube with the patience of a woman used to waiting out weather. The scandal after the wreck had been brief and vicious — an inquiry, a rumor of scuttling that no one could prove, a shipping company quietly dissolved, and a fishing family named Reyes who had once held half the harbor leases and afterward held nothing. The newspaper clippings were thin, but the property records were not. The Reyes leases lapsed in 1912. The family boat, *Estrella*, was sold at auction to cover "debts of association."

Debts of association. A phrase invented to ruin people without accusing them of anything.

The trail thinned through the decades — a cousin in the cannery towns, a marriage into a family upcoast — until it narrowed to a single living branch: Tomas Reyes, thirty-one, licensed fisherman, operating out of a mooring at the far end of the harbor where the water went shallow and the tourists didn't bother walking.

*

His boat was called *Marisol II*, which told her there had been a *Marisol I*, and the paint on her hull was the gray of a thing worked hard and loved anyway. Tomas Reyes was mending net on the dock when she approached, and he looked up with the flat assessment of a man who had learned that strangers arriving at his mooring were never bringing good news.

"You're the Winterbourne girl." Not a question. The name traveled ahead of her like weather.

"Elodie."

"I know who you are." He set down his netting needle. His hands were scarred in the way of working hands, knuckles nicked white. "Grandfather used to point at your family's house on the hill. Told us what your name cost us. So either you're very brave or you don't know why I'm looking at you like this."

"I know why." She held his gaze. It took effort. "I'm not here to defend him. I'm here because I found proof of what he did."

Something shifted behind his eyes — not softening, exactly. Recalculation. "Proof," he said, tasting the word. "Of what, exactly?"

"That the Meridian Star was sunk deliberately. That my grandfather sold the route to pirates. That the crew —" her voice caught on the seam she'd been avoiding for days, and she let it catch rather than smooth it over; he deserved to hear that it cost her something — "the crew included men he helped blacklist. Your great-grandfather was one of them. Peter Reyes. Twenty-three years old."

The harbor sounds filled the silence between them — halyards ticking against masts, a gull arguing with the wind, the far diesel mutter of a boat heading out. Tomas stood very still.

"My grandmother had a photograph," he said finally. "A young man in a borrowed coat. Nobody ever told us straight what happened to him. Just that the wreck broke us, and the inquiry broke us again, and we stopped asking." He looked at her the way a man looks at a chart of waters he's sailed all his life and been shown a reef he never knew was there. "And now you walk down my dock saying you have proof. Why should I believe you? Your family's word drowned my family's livelihood. Why is your word worth more now?"

"It isn't." She reached into her satchel. "My word isn't worth anything. That's why I brought this instead."

The strongbox came out heavy and green with a century of salt, and when she set it on the dock planks between them it landed with the sound of something final. She opened it. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle as autumn leaf, lay the salvage claim — her grandfather's signature, the date three days before the sinking — and beneath it the draft of a confession, unfinished, the ink fading mid-sentence as though the hand writing it had finally failed its author.

Tomas read. She watched him read. She watched the color leave his face and come back wrong, and she watched his jaw work through things he didn't say, and when he finished he closed the box with exaggerated gentleness, the way you close a coffin.

"He dated it before," Tomas said. "He knew."

"He knew."

"And the company paid him, and the town looked the other way, and my people spent a hundred years being the ones who 'associated' badly." He laughed, and there was no humor in it at all. "You know what my father said when I got my license? He said, *good, now the Reyses work the water again, like we were always meant to.* Like it was some accident we ever stopped."

"I'm sorry." The words felt inadequate and true, both at once.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." He crouched beside the strongbox, studying the crest stamped into the brass — her family's crest, worn nearly smooth. "What do you want, Elodie Winterbourne? Because nobody digs up a hundred-year-old crime out of pure conscience. You've been carrying this for days. I can see it. What do you actually want?"

The question went through her defenses like water through a cracked hull. What did she want? She had come down the cliff into the cave chasing wonder, and come out of it holding a curse, and every hour since she had told herself she wanted justice, and that was true, but underneath it —

"I want it to stop being mine," she said quietly. "The secret. The name. I've spent seven years measuring everything in distances because distances don't ask you who you are. And now I know who I am. I'm the heir to this." She touched the box. "I want to give it back. The gold, the truth, all of it. To the families. To the record."

"The gold?" His head came up sharply. "There's gold?"

"The Meridian carried a payroll consignment. My grandfather's claim covered it. It's still in the wreck — I've seen the hull, Tomas. She's intact inside the cave. Sealed since 1911."

For a long moment he just stared at her, and then at the water, and she could see him doing the arithmetic any fishing man does instinctively — tides, weather, risk — applied now to something no one in his family had been permitted to calculate for a century.

"And the fleet," he said. "Everyone in the harbor's heard about the salvage outfit anchoring off the Throat. Vane's people. They've got papers."

"Papers forged or bought. Either way, in six tides everything in that cave becomes legally theirs, and the truth goes down with it forever. That's why I'm here today and not next month. That's why I need help." She made herself say the rest plainly, because he had earned plainness. "I can't dive the lower gallery alone, and I can't hold off a salvage fleet alone, and frankly I don't trust myself alone anymore. I've been alone seven years and it turns out alone was how he lived too."

Tomas looked at her for a long time. Then he did something unexpected — he smiled, briefly, tiredly, and it changed his whole face.

"You know what my grandmother used to say? *The sea keeps honest books.* Everything gets entered eventually." He stood, wiped his palms on his thighs, and offered his hand — scarred, steady, extended across a hundred years of grievance. "All right, Winterbourne. Here are my terms, and they're not negotiable. This doesn't stay quiet. No private settlement, no discreet transfer, no lawyers in a back room dividing gold while the town keeps believing the lie it's believed since 1911. The manifest, the confession, the claim — all of it goes public. Coast guard, newspapers, every descendant family we can find. My great-grandfather drowned twice — once in the water and once in the scandal. We dig him out of both."

"Agreed. Completely."

"And the gold goes to the families. All of it. Eleven shares, plus interest the sea owes them."

"All of it. I keep only the map." She almost smiled. "It's a good map. I made part of it myself."

He shook her hand, and his grip was firm and warm, and Elodie realized with a small internal shock that this was the first handshake she'd initiated or accepted in longer than she could precisely measure — and that for once she didn't reach for the number.

They spread the tide tables across the top of a bait barrel, two heads bent together over the same water, and began planning a dive neither of them might survive against a deadline neither of them controlled.

It was near dusk when Tomas went still, his finger pressed flat against the tide column, his eyes on the horizon where the salvage fleet's lights were beginning to show.

"Elodie," he said. "That front coming down the coast. That's not six tides away. That's three."
