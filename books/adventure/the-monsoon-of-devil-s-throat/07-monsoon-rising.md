## 7. Monsoon Rising

The barometer on the *Corvus Maris* had been falling all night, and Marcus Vane had watched it fall the way other men watched a lover pack a suitcase — knowing exactly what was leaving and unable to say a word that would stop it.

Twenty-nine point four inches at midnight. Twenty-nine point one by dawn. The glass was sweating, the brass casing fogged from the inside, as if the instrument itself were dreading what it had to report.

He stood in the wheelhouse with a mug of coffee gone cold in his fist and studied the horizon off Devil's Throat. The sky out there had the wrong color to it — not storm-gray but something older, a bruised violet pressed down against the sea like a thumb testing fruit for ripeness. The swell had come around to the southeast overnight, long and low and patient, six feet between crests where yesterday there'd been two. The water was telling him before the weather service did. Water always told you first, if you were willing to listen.

"Front's accelerated." Priya Nair, the fleet's meteorologist, came into the wheelhouse with her tablet hugged to her chest and rain already spotting her shoulders from the squall working through the anchorage. "It was supposed to make landfall Thursday night. It's running eighteen hours ahead of the model. We'll have sustained forty-knot winds here by tomorrow midday, gusting sixty in the squalls. Seas building to five meters inside the Throat approaches."

"And after?"

"After, it sits on top of us for three days. Maybe four. Then the back side drags another trough behind it." She didn't soften it; he'd hired her because she didn't. "Marcus, realistically, we're looking at a workable window that closes tomorrow afternoon."

Vane drank the cold coffee anyway. It tasted like pennies and bad decisions.

*Six tides.* That was what the lawyer in Port Calloway had promised them when the papers cleared — six full tidal cycles of uncontested salvage rights before the descendants' trust could file an injunction, before any court could so much as sneeze in their direction. Six tides to strip a wreck that had waited a hundred and fourteen years for someone with the equipment and the nerve to take it.

They'd used two.

"Get Hollis on the line," Vane said. "And get me Delacroix. If we're moving up the schedule, I want the crane barge repositioned before the next slack water, and I want every diver briefed that this is now a two-dive-per-man day."

Priya hesitated at the door. "Marcus. Five-meter seas inside a cave approach. You've read the hydrology reports on Devil's Throat. When that surge funnels through the narrows—"

"I wrote half the hydrology appendix on compressed-air salvage, Priya. I know what the Throat does." He set the mug down on the chart table, deliberately, so his hand wouldn't betray anything. "What I also know is that there are eleven families in this country who've spent a century believing their people drowned as scabs and cowards, and a salvage trust that will own everything in that cave if we wait out this storm like sensible men. Sensible men retire poor. Move the operation up. Full mobilization by 0800."

She went. Through the wheelhouse windows he watched the fleet wake: deck lights snapping on along the *Corvus Maris*, the crane barge *Iron Meridian* getting up steam, the dive tender churning gray water as it swung on its anchor against the building swell. Forty years in the trade had taught him that a salvage fleet under way sounded like nothing else on earth — hawsers groaning, compressors coughing to life, men shouting numbers at each other across wet steel. It was the sound of taking things back from the sea. He'd loved it since he was nineteen, and he loved it still, even now, even with retirement sitting on the far side of this one haul like a lit doorway.

One more job. That was the promise he'd made himself, and made to Elena, who'd stopped asking when he was coming home and started asking whether he ever would. The Meridian Star was the door. Gold bullion and registered specie, insured manifests, a wreck sealed in cold anoxic silt since 1911 — the kind of site salvors wrote books about and never got to work. Six tides of legal cover, forged so cleanly that even Vane had to admire the penmanship, and then a lifetime ashore with salt money and no alarms.

If the weather would only hold its tongue for thirty-six more hours.

* * *

Delacroix came up from the dive deck at noon with his helmet under his arm and his face doing something Vane had never seen it do.

Rudy Delacroix had twenty-two years underwater and a reputation for calm that bordered on pathology. He'd once finished a cutting operation through a hull breach while a tiger shark cruised the work light. He did not look rattled. But he looked *wrong*, standing there dripping on the wheelhouse deck, and Vane felt the day tilt a degree before the man said a word.

"Talk."

"We ran the interior survey on the aft hold like scheduled." Delacroix set the helmet down carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. "Visibility's improved since the front pushed the thermocline around — fifteen, sixteen feet in the lower gallery. Good conditions. Better than good." He wiped his mouth. "Marcus, the strongbox is gone."

The word landed in Vane's chest and sat there. "Gone."

"Gone. Not moved, not shifted by settling — the cradle it sat in is still there, silt scour marks intact, no disturbance pattern consistent with collapse or current. Somebody took it. Recently. There were drag impressions in the silt leading toward the seaward gallery, fresh enough that the edges hadn't slumped. And—" Delacroix paused, and Vane understood he was saving the worst for last, which meant the worst was very bad indeed. "There's rope. New polypropylene anchor line, spliced eye, tied off to a dead coral head near the original entrance throat. That line hasn't been in the water more than a few days. Salt bloom hadn't even established on the fibers."

Vane turned to the chart table and spread the site plan flat, weighting the corners with coffee mugs and a brass diviner's compass he kept for luck and never admitted to. The Meridian Star lay folded into the sea cave beneath the Devil's Throat cliffs like a knife in a drawer — accessible only through a submerged entrance passage that breathed twice a day with the tide, dangerous enough that the old salvors' charts marked it with a skull and the words *NO ANCHORAGE, NO HARBOR, NO PRAYERS.*

Nobody dove that cave by accident. Nobody found it by accident. And nobody — nobody — had known it was open until the freak low tide three days ago drained the entrance throat below the sill for the first time in living memory.

Which meant somebody else had been watching the tide tables too.

"Who?" Delacroix asked. "Local sport divers wouldn't have the gas or the nerve. Treasure hunters don't work this coast — the insurance registries burned in the Calloway fire, nobody even knew the Star's cargo manifest survived."

"Somebody knew." Vane traced the coastline with one finger, past the fleet's anchorage, past the rail spur ruins, up to the abandoned survey station on the cliff shoulder where the decommissioned railway had once run instruments down to the waterline. "Somebody with survey-grade knowledge of these approaches, equipment, and the sense to keep quiet about it. This wasn't opportunism, Rudy. This was a rival who got here first and didn't file a claim."

"You think it's another company?"

Vane thought about the rope. Polypropylene, cheap, practical — working line, not trophy-hunter gear. A splice done fast and competently. Someone who dove alone or nearly alone, who entered and exited without leaving a surface marker, who took one box and left the rest of a fortune untouched.

One box. Not the gold bars racked in the hold — those had all still been there, Delacroix confirmed, furred with a century of concretion, fat and patient as sleeping pigs. Just the strongbox. The small one. The one thing in that cave that mattered more than money.

"The strongbox was the ship's purser's safe," Vane said slowly, working it aloud because saying things made their shape visible. "Manifests, registers, personal papers. Cargo documents. If our rival took the box and left the bullion, they're not after salvage value. They're after *proof*." He looked up. "Documents. Something in that box that's worth more to them than gold."

Delacroix frowned. "Like what?"

"Like a reason the whole haul might be legally poisoned." Vane straightened. The forged claim was airtight — he'd paid enough to make sure of it — but paper was only as strong as the absence of better paper. Somewhere in that drowned purser's safe might sit a document that could unravel six tides of legal cover into six minutes of criminal charges. "Somebody out there knows what we're carrying and what we're carrying it against. Get me the lawyer in Calloway. And Rudy — I want your best man on the entrance throat at slack water tonight. Not diving. Watching."

* * *

The lawyer called back at dusk, when the first outer bands of the front were dragging rain across the anchorage in long silver curtains, and the news was worse than the weather.

"The injunction risk just changed," Amara Osei said, her voice thin over the satellite link, competing with wind noise on her end too. "A clerk in the admiralty registry flagged a query against our claim this morning. Anonymous inquiry — someone pulled the chain of title on the Meridian Star's insurance settlement. That's pre-litigation behavior, Marcus. Someone is building a case."

"Then we're out of time entirely." Vane stood at the stern rail of the *Corvus Maris* and watched lightning walk the horizon over Devil's Throat, silent and continuous, heat lightning gone wrong, the storm showing itself off before it arrived to do real work. "We lift tomorrow. First safe window, whatever it looks like. If the front closes on us mid-operation, we cut and run with whatever's on the hook."

"That's reckless even for you."

"It's arithmetic, Amara. A wreck we lose to weather can be re-surveyed after the season. A claim we lose to an injunction is gone forever, and so is the money you and I and four hundred shareholders are counting on." He gripped the rail and felt the ship roll under him, patient, enormous, obedient. "Besides. Our rival has to come back for the rest eventually, or come back to finish whatever they started. Caves only have one mouth. I intend to be sitting in front of it."

He spent the evening making it so. Two of his men ashore before dark, tucked into the cliff ruins above the old rail spur with night glasses and a radio, covering the landward approach — because a rival clever enough to find that cave might be clever enough to reach it from above, the way the old railwaymen once had. The dive tender anchored cross-current to the entrance throat with sonar painting the passage mouth every ninety seconds. And in the wheelhouse, Vane laid out the trap the way he'd laid out a hundred cutting operations: quietly, completely, with every contingency priced in.

If the rival came by sea, the tender would see them. If they came by land, his shore team would call it. If they were already inside — already down there in the dark with his wreck, breathing his air, carrying away his future piece by piece — then the rising tide and the coming storm would do his work for him, sealing the throat, holding them under until he chose to let them up.

At which point, Vane reflected, a conversation could be had. He was not, whatever the trade gossip said, a violent man. But he was a thorough one, and thorough men did not leave loose ends floating in six-tide windows.

By midnight the barometer read 28.9 and falling faster. Rain hit the wheelhouse windows sideways. Out at the Throat, the cliffs stood black against the lightning, and somewhere beneath them, in a cave that the old charts said devoured trespassers, the Meridian Star waited with her ribs full of gold and her secrets half-stolen.

Vane poured the last of the coffee, cold again, and drank it standing up.

"Come on, then," he said to the storm, to the rival, to the dark water generally. "Admission's free. But everything in there belongs to me."

At 0340, the shore team's radio crackled: movement on the cliff path above the cave. One figure, maybe two, heading down toward the water with the tide just beginning to turn.

Vane smiled without any pleasure in it at all, and reached for the intercom.

"Wake the tender. Our guest has arrived."
