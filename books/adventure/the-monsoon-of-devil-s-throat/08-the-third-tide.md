## 8. The Third Tide

The barometer on Elodie's survey bench had fallen eleven millibars in six hours, and she had watched every one of them go down like a man watching a train leave without him.

"Front's moving faster than the forecast," Tomas said from the doorway of the boathouse, rain already beading in his hair. He'd brought the boat around at dawn, a twenty-six-foot fishing hull named *Consuelo* after a grandmother he would not yet talk about. "Harbormaster says it'll make landfall by tomorrow night. Maybe tomorrow morning if it keeps eating water like this."

"Then we dive today." Elodie zipped the dry bag shut over the manifest's coordinates — not that they needed coordinates anymore. She could walk the cave blindfolded now. She had, in her head, forty times since the strongbox. "Third tide. Slack water at 14:40. We have maybe ninety minutes of workable visibility before the surge turns the Throat into a washing machine."

Tomas didn't move. He stood with his arms crossed, looking at the gray wall of cloud stacking up over the headland, and Elodie recognized the posture because she'd worn it herself for seven years: a man deciding how much fear to admit to.

"You've done this before," he said. Not quite a question.

"I've surveyed flooded tunnels in three countries. I've never dived one during a monsoon onset."

"That's supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make you accurate." She slung the bag over her shoulder and met his eyes. "You can stay on the boat. Run the line, watch the surface, keep the engine warm. If we're not up in fifty minutes, you call the coast guard and you tell them exactly where we are and exactly what we went in for. The manifest matters more than either of us. That's not bravery, Tomas. That's arithmetic."

He looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face shifted — not softening exactly, but settling, the way ballast settles.

"My grandmother used to say the Reyes family was cursed by liars," he said. "I thought she meant the company men who blacklisted my great-grandfather. Now I think she might have meant your grandfather too."

"Yes," Elodie said. There was no other honest answer.

"Good. At least you don't flinch." He picked up the second tank rig. "Let's go get the truth before the storm does."

* * *

The Devil's Throat lived up to its name in weather.

The swell came out of the southeast in long, green-gray hills, and the cliffs took each one and threw it back as spray that fell upward in the wind. Tomas brought the *Consuelo* in on the back of a lull, engines reversed hard, and Elodie went over the side into water the color of steeped tea, the cave mouth opening below her like a held breath.

Down. Down past the kelp that whipped like signal flags. Down to where the surge noise faded into a deep, rhythmic boom — the ocean knocking on the mountain's door.

Inside, the cavern was dimmer than on her first descent; the storm clouds were stealing the light even here. Her lamp beam found the Meridian Star's ribs, black and patient, furred now with a season's new growth since the freak tide had first bared them. The ship had been dead for over a century and still looked like it was waiting for orders.

"The purser's cabin," she signaled, and Tomas nodded, bubbles streaming.

They worked fast. The cabin bulkhead had rotted to lace, and behind it, wedged in an iron document case bolted to what had once been the purser's desk, the manifest waited — oilcloth-wrapped, lead-sealed, the shipping line's own obsessive bureaucracy preserving the names of eleven men the world had been allowed to forget. Elodie worked the bolts with a wrench from her kit, her hands steady in the way hands get when the body understands that shaking is a luxury.

*Tomas Reyes, fireman.* She'd seen the name in her grandfather's journal and hadn't told Tomas it was there. Some things a person should read in their ancestor's own hand.

She'd just cinched the case into the lift bag when the sound changed.

It was small at first — a pressure shift in her ears, a flutter of disturbed silt from the tunnel behind them. Then the boom of the surf against the outer rocks arrived a half-beat early, and again, and again, shortening, quickening, and Elodie felt the cold hand close around her heart before her brain caught up.

The tide wasn't slack anymore. The surge was coming in early — pulled ahead of schedule by the storm's own low pressure, the sea rising to meet the falling sky.

And then she saw the light change at the tunnel mouth. Not the pale flicker of open water. A hard, mechanical white.

A steel mesh gate was descending across the cave entrance, lowered on cables from above, and beyond it, silhouetted against the surface glare, two divers in commercial gear hung in the water like sentries. One of them raised a hand — not a wave. A salute. Or a taunt.

Vane.

Her mind did what it always did under load: it went cold, and quiet, and precise. *Gate across the throat. Cable-operated, so there's a winch on the cliff top — probably mounted on their survey vessel or a shore station. They sealed us in to keep us from leaving with anything, or to keep anyone else out while they work. Either way, they don't know this cave. They bought a chart, not a survey.*

*But I made a survey.*

Tomas grabbed her arm, pointing frantically at the gate, at the divers, at the lift bag. His eyes through the mask were wide with a question that had no words.

Elodie pointed deeper into the cave — away from the wreck, toward the back wall where the water funneled into a fissure the charts called nothing at all. Then she tapped her temple, twice: *trust me.*

Because her instruments had found it three years ago, on a day she'd logged as a curiosity and filed under anomalies: a barometric pulse in the back gallery, synchronized with the swell outside. Air moving. A chimney. On her master map of the spur line — the map she'd drawn for a railroad that would never be built — she'd penciled a single note in the margin: *blowhole, est. 4–6 m bore, vents upper cliff bench, likely karst connection, unverified.*

Unverified. Well. Today it got verified.

They swam into the fissure. It narrowed, widened, narrowed again, the ceiling pressing down until Elodie's tank scraped stone and her lamp showed only a writhing corridor of rock and bubble. Behind them, muffled by a hundred meters of mountain, she heard the surge arrive properly — a long, subterranean roar, the ocean claiming the lower gallery room by room. The water around them shuddered. Somewhere back in the dark, the Meridian Star groaned, a century-old hull flexing under a fist of green water.

*Faster.*

The passage tilted upward. Her depth gauge said nine meters, then seven, then five, and the roar behind them grew until it wasn't background anymore, it was the whole world. The water began to move — not gently, but with intent, a river running uphill, dragging them toward the ceiling. Elodie kicked hard, Tomas beside her, both of them swimming against a current that wanted to help them and might kill them doing it, because a blowhole in surge conditions didn't vent air. It fired it.

The shaft opened. Above her, in the lamplight, she saw it: a column of churning foam, and beyond the foam, a coin of daylight.

"Now!" she screamed into her regulator, knowing he couldn't hear, knowing he'd see it in her body — she pointed up, kicked, and let the mountain throw them.

The blowhole exhaled.

It was less like surfacing than like being shot. The water lifted them the last four meters in a single convulsion and hurled them out onto a shelf of wet rock in a burst of spray and thunder, and Elodie hit the stone on her forearms and knees and slid, clawing, until friction won. She ripped her mask off and dragged air — real air, storm-flavored, salt and ozone — into her lungs in great tearing gulps.

Beside her, Tomas lay on his back, chest heaving, laughing — actually laughing, a wild, disbelieving sound torn out of him. "You *knew,*" he gasped. "Your map. You put a note on a map three years ago and it just — it *saved our lives—*"

"It saved our lives because I'm incapable of throwing anything away," Elodie said, and then, to her own astonishment, she laughed too. It came out rusty, cracked from disuse, and it kept coming anyway. Seven years of measured distances and controlled breathing, and here she was on a cliff ledge in a monsoon, soaked and bleeding at the elbows and laughing like a woman who had forgotten she knew how.

The manifest case sat between them on the rock, intact, the lift bag deflated around it like shed skin.

Below and behind them, muffled by stone, the lower gallery finished flooding. The Meridian Star went under the surge entirely — but not into darkness, not into forgetting. Not this time.

* * *

They climbed the goat track off the bench in the first horizontal rain, and by the time they reached the clifftop road, the storm had arrived in earnest, the sky gone the color of wet slate, the pines along the ridge bent and hissing. The *Consuelo* rode her mooring in the cove below, pitching hard but holding.

From the road's high shoulder, Elodie could see the Throat itself — and she stopped.

Out in the churning water, floodlit against the storm-dark, sat Vane's crane barge, riding at anchor it had no business keeping in these seas. Its crane arm swung slowly over the swell, and beneath it, divers' lights moved in the white water like fireflies in a gale. They weren't stopping. The lawyer's window was closing and Vane was spending his six tides all at once, gambling the whole fleet against the storm.

"They're going to try to raise her tonight," Tomas shouted over the wind. "In *this?*"

"He doesn't have a choice anymore." Elodie pulled the manifest case tighter against her chest, feeling the lead seal dig through the oilcloth. "We took his timeline away. Now he takes his chances with the weather." She looked at the barge, at the lights, at the eleven names pressed against her ribs, and the cold clarity settled over her again — but warmer now, shared, carried by two people instead of one.

"Tomas. How fast can we get to a radio?"

His grin came back through the rain, fierce and ready. "Depends whose radio you mean."

"All of them," Elodie said. "Every coast guard frequency, every newsroom, every descendant on that list. By midnight, I want the whole coastline to know what's coming up out of the Throat — and exactly whose gold it is."

Lightning walked the horizon, and the monsoon came down like a verdict.
