## 9. Devil's Throat

The storm arrived like a debt collector.

Elodie felt it first in the cliff itself — a tremor through her boots as she crouched at the survey station above Devil's Throat, wind tearing at the tarp over her equipment. Below, the sea had gone the color of wet slate, and the swell was running thirty feet, maybe more, detonating against the cave mouth in white columns that climbed the black rock and vanished. Somewhere under all that violence lay the Meridian Star, and somewhere on that wreck, if Tomas's last dive had told them true, the manifest still sat in its lead-lined case in the purser's cabin, waiting to be either testimony or loot.

She keyed the transmitter for the fourth time in an hour. Static. Then, faintly, the coast guard station at Port Merrow crackled back.

*"Say again, Winterbourne. You're claiming an illegal salvage operation at Devil's Throat? We have no record of any permit—"*

"That's because there isn't one." She had to shout over the wind. "Vane Salvage Group, crane barge *Iron Meridian*, anchored half a mile off the Throat. Their papers are forged — I can prove it. And I have proof of something older. A murder dressed up as an accident from 1911. I'm broadcasting the evidence now, wideband, unencrypted. Get it on record."

A pause. *"Winterbourne, you cannot just—"* But she was already switching channels, opening the frequency to anyone with a receiver: the coast guard, the harbor office, the maritime press wire out of Port Merrow, the little community band the fishing fleet used. Her voice would go out raw and wind-shredded, but it would go out.

Tomas stood beside her under the tarp, holding the strongbox like it might bolt. Eleven families' worth of names were inside it, along with her grandfather's confession in his own cramped hand. The descendant families were already gathering on the clifftop road behind them — Reyes cousins, Okafor grandchildren, a woman named Halloran who'd driven four hours with her father's photograph in a Ziploc bag. They huddled against the rain, watching the barge's lights swing at anchor through the murk.

"Read it," Tomas said. "All of it. Don't summarize. Let them hear the names."

Elodie unfolded the journal page, weighted its corners with stones, and began to read into the storm.

* * *

Marcus Vane heard the broadcast at 14:40, twenty minutes before his dive window closed.

It came through the *Iron Meridian*'s radio room while he stood over the chart table with a coffee he hadn't touched, and for three full seconds he simply listened — to a woman's voice, hoarse and precise, reading names. *Silas Reyes. Amara Okafor. Daniel Halloran.* Crew of the *Meridian Star*. Then the turn that made his jaw tighten: *My grandfather, Edmund Winterbourne, sold their route to pirates for the insurance money and filed his salvage claim three days before they drowned.*

"Kill that frequency," he said.

His comms officer shrugged helplessly. "It's open band, boss. Anyone can transmit. We can jam it locally but it's already out — press wire picked it up, coast guard's acknowledging."

Vane set down the coffee. Forty-one years in salvage, and this was how it ended: not with the haul of a lifetime settling into his hold, but with a dead man's granddaughter reading a confession into a monsoon. He thought about the villa in Cozumel. The boat. Retirement, six weeks away, one clean lift between him and the rest of his life.

The lawyer, Pell, appeared in the doorway, gray-faced, clutching a satellite phone. "Coast guard cutter's underway from Port Merrow. Two hours out, maybe less in this sea. Marcus — we need to cut and run."

"We need to *lift*," Vane said. "Wreck's hooked. Cable's tensioned. Twenty minutes to break her free of the seabed and we're legitimate possessors — finders-keepers holds until a court says otherwise, and courts take years." He was surprised by how steady his own voice sounded. "Pell, get on the radio to our people ashore. If those papers are questioned, I want the originals 'discovered' in a notary's office by morning."

"And the woman?"

Vane looked at the sonar display. The *Meridian Star* glowed in green contour forty fathoms down, and near her stern, moving, two small returns — divers. His divers. Except his diver, Okonkwo, was aboard recovering from the morning shift, which meant the returns belonged to someone else.

Someone was on his wreck. On *his* wreck, in *his* storm, with *his* retirement hanging in the balance.

"Bring the crane to full tension," he said. "And get me the cave mouth on the floodlight."

* * *

Forty fathoms down, the world was a shaking green cathedral.

Elodie had made the descent herself — Tomas had argued, lost, and come down beside her, because neither of them trusted the other alone with the truth anymore. The beacon was a steel-cored cylinder the size of a thermos, transmitting the manifest scans and the journal photographs on a loop to every frequency she could program. All she had to do was fix it to the wreck's highest point, where no court on earth could claim the evidence had been planted after seizure.

Her headlamp swept the *Meridian Star*'s hull — the same ribbed iron she'd found seven years of grief ago in a drained cave, now shrouded in storm-stirred silt. The crane cable hung above them like a harpoon line, hooked into the old ship's spine with a choker chain some rigger had slung without a care for what he was desecrating.

"Beacon's set," she said into her mask comm, bolting the clamp to the foremast stump. "Signal's live. Whatever happens now, it's on the record."

"Then we go," Tomas said. "Now. Elodie — the cable. Look at the cable."

She looked. The great steel line had begun to sing — a high, thin vibration she felt in her teeth more than heard, the sound of metal loaded past its design tolerance. Above them, invisible through forty fathoms of boiling water, a crane was trying to tear a century-old wreck off the seabed in a storm.

And then the wreck moved.

Not much. A shudder, a groan transmitted through the water as pressure, a soft avalanche of silt sliding off the hull. The *Meridian Star* shifted on her grave like a sleeper disturbed, and Elodie understood with cold, total clarity what came next: either the cable held and the ship broke, or the cable broke and everything above them — the hook, the choker, whatever tore loose — came down through the water column like the fist of God.

"Tomas. Up. *Up.*"

They kicked for the surface along the cable itself, using it as a guide rope through the brownout, and they were maybe ten fathoms down when the cable parted.

* * *

Vane never gave the order to cut it. That was the thing he would tell the inquiry, and the thing that would be true: he didn't have to. The cable snapped on its own at 15:12, at ninety-three percent of rated load, and the stored energy of a crane barge fighting a monsoon released all at once.

The recoiling line whipped across the deck and took out the rail stanchions aft. The sudden loss of load threw the *Iron Meridian* hard to starboard; she rolled twenty degrees, then kept going, because the sea that hit her beam at that exact moment was a thirty-foot face of black water with the whole Pacific behind it.

Vane went across the chart table. Coffee, laptop, the forged permit folder — all of it went airborne. He caught the doorframe with both hands and held on while the barge lay over far enough that the deck became a wall and the floodlights swung their beams out across the water like lighthouse fingers, and in one of those beams, for half a second, he saw them.

Two heads. Divers surfacing fifty yards off the port quarter, right in the path of the next set.

"No," he said, to no one. To himself. To the arithmetic of it — two swimmers, one breaking wave, and his barge wallowing beam-on with no steerage. There was no decision to make, which was the only mercy in it. "Hard aport! Get her bow into it! Searchlight on the water — *now!*"

* * *

The wave was a moving cliff, and Elodie saw it coming in the barge's own light.

They'd surfaced into chaos — the severed cable's broken end thrashing the sea astern of them, the *Iron Meridian* rolling drunk, floodlights sweeping like search parties. Then the light found them and held, and beyond it, rising, the wave.

There was no time to swim anywhere. There was time only to do the thing she had trained her whole careful, measured life to do: read the water. The wave would break left to right along its face; the trough ahead of it would suck them toward the cliff base of Devil's Throat itself; the only survivable water was the shoulder, where the wave spent itself early against the shoaling bank.

"Dive down and left!" she screamed. "Under the shoulder — *now!*"

They dove. The world above them turned to thunder. Elodie felt the wave pass overhead not as impact but as pressure, a vast hand pressing down through the water, and then the turbulence took her — a washing-machine violence of bubbles and silt that spun her end over end until she no longer knew which way was up. Her regulator bucked. Something struck her shoulder, hard enough to white out her vision.

She stopped fighting. That was the trick, the one the sea taught everyone eventually or killed them for not learning: stop fighting, feel for the pull. The bubbles around her streamed downward, so up was the other way. She followed her own exhaust toward the light.

Her head broke surface into rain. The cliffs were a black wall to her left, terrifyingly close. The barge was a wall to her right, closer than that.

"Tomás!"

"Here!" Ten feet away, coughing, one arm raised. Behind them, the *Iron Meridian* was fighting her way bow-on into the seas, searchlights raking, and a line — an actual line, a heaving line with a float — arced out of the spray and landed six feet from Tomas's head.

"They're throwing us a rope," Tomas said, disbelief cracking his voice.

"Take it anyway," Elodie said. "Justice doesn't require drowning."

* * *

They hauled the two of them aboard the *Iron Meridian* at 15:31, and Marcus Vane met them at the rail with a blanket and the expression of a man watching his life's ledger close itself.

"You're Winterbourne," he said.

"I'm Winterbourne."

"The beacon. It's transmitting."

"It's been transmitting since 14:55. Every word of the manifest. Every word of his confession. Your name is attached to it now too, Mr. Vane — you salvaged a crime scene with forged papers during a storm, and the whole coast heard it happen."

Vane looked out at the Throat, where the floodlights lit the cave mouth one last time before the seas swallowed even that. He thought about arguing. About the lawyers, the appeals, the years. Then he looked at the woman wrapped in his blanket, salt bleeding from a cut above her eye, holding nothing — no gold, no claim, no map even — and looking at him with something worse than hatred. Pity, almost. Recognition.

"How long have you known," she asked quietly, "that the papers were forged?"

"Since I signed them," Vane said.

The cutter *Resolute* came around the headland at 16:05, riding the storm better than any commercial hull could, and put an armed boarding party across the *Iron Meridian*'s deck in the driving rain. Vane did not resist. He watched them confiscate his logbook, his charts, the strongbox manifest copies Elodie surrendered into official custody with both hands, formally, like handing over a child.

Through the wheelhouse glass he watched her stand at the rail afterward, watching the wreck site, and he understood finally what he'd been racing for six tides. Not gold. A funeral, a hundred and twelve years late, for eleven men the sea had been asked to keep quiet.

He hoped, in the flat way of a ruined man, that she got the weather for it.

* * *

On the cliffs, the rain had thinned to silver threads by dusk, and the descendant families were still there — nobody had suggested leaving, so nobody left. When the cutter's lights appeared escorting the barge in, a sound went up from the clifftop road that Elodie felt in her chest before she heard it with her ears: not cheering exactly, though there was that, but something older. The sound of a debt being acknowledged after a century of silence.

Tomas Reyes stood at the edge, soaked through, holding the strongbox against his ribs.

"They'll want statements," Elodie said. "Days of them. Lawyers. Cameras."

"Let them come." He looked at her — really looked, the way you look at someone you've decided to keep. "You know what my grandmother used to say? That the sea keeps accounts. She just takes her time reading them out."

Below them, the tide was turning, and the cave at Devil's Throat was filling with dark water, closing over the *Meridian Star* like a hand closing over a finished letter. Elodie checked her watch out of pure habit — 18:42 — and then, for the first time in seven years, did not check it again.

But the storm wasn't done with them yet. Down the coast, the harbor master's flag was climbing to a warning she'd only seen once before in her surveys, and the radio at her elbow crackled with a message that made her blood run colder than the sea ever had:

*"Winterbourne — be advised. The strongbox you recovered. We've opened the inner compartment. There's a second document inside, sealed in oilskin. It's addressed to you. By name."*
