## 10. Years, Not Miles

The trust papers were signed on a Tuesday, in a lawyer's office that smelled of lemon polish and old rain. Elodie read every line twice before she signed — not because she distrusted the words, but because she had spent seven years learning that documents were where families hid their sins, and she wanted to be certain this one held none.

*The Meridian Star Descendants' Trust*, it was called. Eleven names at its head, eleven men who had gone into the Devil's Throat in November of 1911 and been left there by a man whose portrait still hung, she had learned only weeks ago, in three separate halls of maritime history.

She signed with a pen that trembled only once.

Tomas Reyes sat beside her, his hands flat on the table as though steadying something. When the lawyer slid the final page toward him, he didn't sign immediately. He looked at the names first — all eleven of them, including his great-grandfather, Mateo Reyes, boatswain, age thirty-four — and he touched each one lightly with his forefinger, the way a man touches a rail before trusting it with his weight.

"They'll get letters," Tomas said. "Real ones. Not lawyers' letters."

"Real ones," Elodie agreed. "From both of us."

Outside the office window, the harbor lay flat and bright under a washed sky, the monsoon long spent, the water holding that post-storm clarity that made even the pilings look honest. Somewhere out past the headland, the Devil's Throat was quiet. The cave mouth had closed again with the tides, keeping its admission fee to itself.

* * *

Marcus Vane's trial began the following month, and Elodie attended exactly one day of it.

She told herself it was for the record — that her testimony about the forged survey permits, the doctored tide charts, the crane barge's illegal anchorage inside a protected wreck site, needed a living witness behind it. That was true. But the deeper truth was simpler: she wanted to watch a man be held to account in daylight, slowly, by procedure, instead of by drowning or storm or any of the sea's faster mercies.

Vane had cut the cable himself. Everyone knew it now; the barge's own engineer had testified to it, voice shaking, describing how the load swung wild when the wire parted, how the barge had rolled forty degrees with Elodie Winterbourne in the water off its starboard beam. Vane's lawyer argued stress, weather, exigent circumstance. The prosecution played the recording of the manifest broadcast — Elodie's voice, steady over the coast guard channel, reading eleven names into the wind while the cliffs filled with descendants holding candles against the rain.

She watched Vane across the courtroom. He looked smaller than he had on the deck of his barge, grayer, but his eyes were the same: a salvage master's eyes, always calculating what could still be recovered from a loss. When the verdict came down — charges sustained, the forgery proven, the barge seized, the man himself facing years rather than tides — he did not look at her. He looked at his own hands, turning them over as if checking them for rust.

On the courthouse steps afterward, a reporter asked Elodie if she felt justice had been done.

"Justice is a slow tide," she said. "It comes back for everything eventually. You just have to leave the door open."

She did not tell the reporter that the hardest part wasn't Vane at all. It was the archive room of the historical society, where a curator with white cotton gloves had peeled back the velvet covering a bronze plaque: *AUGUST WINTERBOURNE, SALVOR AND BENEFACTOR, 1849–1912.* The plaque was coming down. A new one would go up — plainer, truer, naming the eleven dead before it named him, and naming what he had done. The record was being rewritten, line by line, and Elodie had written half of it herself.

Her grandfather's name would survive. But it would survive honestly now, which was a harder thing than surviving beautifully.

* * *

The map went to the maritime museum on an ordinary Thursday morning.

Elodie carried it herself, in a flat archival case, up the stairs past the model ships and the harpoon guns and the wall of framed figureheads with their painted eyes staring at nothing. The curator, a small woman named Dr. Okafor who had testified as an expert witness during the trial, met her in the conservation room, where the light was raked low and everything smelled of acid-free paper.

"You're certain," Dr. Okafor said. It wasn't quite a question. "This is the original survey. Your grandfather's hand."

"My grandfather's hand." Elodie laid the case open. The map spread beneath the conservation lamp — the Devil's Throat coastline drawn in iron-gall ink, the soundings marked in his tight, confident numerals, and there, in the lower margin, the annotation she had stared at through three sleepless nights after finding it: *Cave admits at extreme low water. Wreck within. Claim staked 3 days prior.*

Three days before the Meridian Star ever struck the rocks.

"He drew the truth into the margin," Elodie said, "and then he built a legend on top of it. I used to think the legend was the inheritance. It wasn't. This was." She tapped the air above the margin, not touching the paper. "He couldn't destroy the evidence and he couldn't confess, so he hid the confession inside the thing he loved most — the survey work itself. He left it for whoever measured carefully enough to find it."

"And you kept measuring."

"I didn't know I was." Elodie smiled, and it cost her something, and she let it. "I thought I was just running from grief along closed rail lines. Turns out I was retracing his route. Every mile I surveyed, I was walking in his footsteps toward this."

Dr. Okafor studied her for a moment. "You're donating everything? The journals too?"

"The journals, the strongbox, the manifest copy. Everything except one page."

"The last one."

Elodie took it from her coat then — a single leaf, soft-edged with handling, its ink faded to the brown of dried kelp. Her grandfather's final entry, unfinished, the sentence breaking off mid-word as though the pen had been set down and never lifted again:

*I have counted the years since the Throat took them, and every year I have taken something from the sea to balance the ledger, and the ledger will never—*

It ended there. No date. No signature. Just the broken arithmetic of a guilty man's life.

"I'm going to finish it," Elodie said. "In my own hand. Below his."

Dr. Okafor raised her eyebrows. "You'd alter a primary document?"

"I'd add to it. Dated, initialed, in different ink — museum standard, I checked. A confession shouldn't end mid-sentence forever. Someone has to close the ledger." She looked at the page a long moment before handing it over. "He measured his whole life in miles and salvage tons and years of guilt. I want the last measurement on this page to be mine."

* * *

That evening she wrote the line.

She sat at the kitchen table of her rented cottage above the harbor, the journal page squared before her on blotting paper, a fountain pen filled with archival black ink — deliberately, visibly different from the faded iron-gall brown. Tomas had offered to stay. She'd asked him to come at sunset instead. Some sentences you have to write alone first.

She thought about what to say for a long time. An hour. Two. The tide turned below the point without her noticing, which would have been unthinkable a year ago.

Then she wrote, in a hand steadier than she expected:

*The ledger is closed. Eleven names restored, eleven families paid, the gold returned to those it was taken from. I am my grandfather's granddaughter, and I am not his heir — I am his correction. Counted and signed: E. Winterbourne, this day, in fair weather.*

She blotted it, dated it properly, and sat looking at the two hands on one page — the criminal's and the corrector's, seventy years apart, bound by blood and amended by choice.

When Tomas knocked, she was ready.

* * *

They walked down to the point below the cottage, where the old rail spur she'd originally come to survey ended in a stub of rusted track jutting from the grass like a rib. The monsoon season was fully over; the evening was cool and enormous, the sky doing that late-autumn thing where every color got saved up and spent at once — copper, ash, a bruised violet at the seaward edge.

Below them, the tide was coming in. She could hear it filling the gullies under the cliff, patient as bookkeeping.

"Do you know what I did yesterday?" Tomas said. He had his hands in his pockets, shoulders easy, a fisherman off duty in the last light. "I went through my grandmother's trunk. Looking for anything about Mateo. And I found a letter he sent the week before he sailed. Last one. He wrote—" Tomas stopped, cleared his throat. "He wrote, *Tell Rosa I'll measure our new house in footsteps when I come home, so she can walk it with me in the dark.*"

Elodie said nothing. The waves said enough.

"Footsteps," Tomas repeated softly. "Not miles. Not money. Footsteps, so she could walk beside him." He glanced at her. "Your grandfather counted everything, didn't he. Miles, tons, years of guilt. And it bought him nothing but a cave full of silence."

"It bought him a margin note," Elodie said. "And the margin note bought the truth. So maybe counting isn't the sin. Maybe it's what you count."

They stood together while the light went out of the water, leaving it pewter and pearl. Elodie became aware, gradually, of a habit loosening in her chest — the small constant gesture of seven years, the wrist-turn, the glance downward, the conversion of every moment into units of distance from something. She had measured her grief in miles of dead track. She had measured her courage in fathoms. She had measured her grandfather in decades of lies.

She reached for her watch now, purely from habit, to time the tide.

And stopped.

Her hand hung in the air a moment, then dropped back to her side. The tide would take as long as it took. The water didn't need her to count it. It had been coming in and going out over the Devil's Throat since before her family had a name, and it would go on long after the plaque was recast and the trust paid its first dividends and the last person who remembered the Meridian Star as scandal remembered it instead as eleven men who came home, at last, in ink.

"Tomas," she said. "What would you say to surveying the coastal lines with me? The decommissioned spurs, the old sea routes. Properly this time. For the record, not the escape."

He smiled, and the last light caught it. "I'd say the tide's coming in, and we've got nowhere else to be."

She laughed — really laughed, the first unmeasured sound she'd made in seven years — and they turned back up the path as the first stars found the water. Behind them the sea kept its own time, and ahead of them the cottage lamp burned in the window like a fixed point on a chart that no longer needed correcting.

At the door, she paused. On the table inside, visible through the glass, lay her grandfather's final journal page, two hands upon it now, the ledger closed.

Tomorrow, she would carry it to the museum. Tonight, she simply locked the door on it gently, the way you lock a door on someone sleeping — and walked home through the dark without once asking how far it was.
