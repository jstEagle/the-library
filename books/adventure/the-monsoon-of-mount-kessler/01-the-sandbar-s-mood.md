## 1. The Sandbar's Mood

The Kessler River had a mood, and that morning it was sulking.

Jonah Sterling could read it the way other men read weather or women or the fine print on salvage contracts—by the way the water lay against the pilings, by the color of the foam, by the particular sullen gray the current wore when it had somewhere better to be. Today the river was low and slow and full of opinions, which meant the *Pelican's Rest* would scrape her belly over the sandbar at the harbor mouth unless Jonah threaded her through with the kind of care most men reserved for defusing ordnance.

"Easy, old girl," he murmured to the wheel, giving it a quarter-turn. "Nobody's watching."

Nobody was. That was the trouble with Port Calloway these days. Forty years ago it had been a boomtown built on river freight and sea salvage, all brass fittings and big talk. Now half the storefronts on Front Street stood empty behind salt-fogged glass, and the other half sold bait, beer, or boat parts, usually two out of three. The cannery had closed before Jonah was old enough to remember it open. The church steeple leaned like a drunk leaning on a friend. Even the gulls had gotten lazy, wheeling overhead without conviction, as if they'd given up on the town ever producing anything worth diving for.

Jonah had not given up on anything in his life, mostly because he never stayed anywhere long enough for hope to calcify into disappointment.

That was the philosophy, if you wanted to call it one: *never stop, never owe.* He'd learned it young, the hard way, the way boys learn things when their mothers are disgraced salvagers who drown in shallow water and leave their sons a name nobody says above a whisper. A man who kept moving owed nothing to any place. A man who stopped started belonging, and belonging, in Jonah's experience, was just another word for being held down while the tide came in.

He brought the *Pelican's Rest* alongside the municipal dock with a kiss so soft the fenders barely sighed, cut the engine, and stood a moment in the sudden quiet listening to the hull tick as it cooled.

"There she is," said Dockmaster Pruett, shuffling down the planks with his clipboard held like a shield. "Thought maybe you'd finally sunk her."

"She'll outlive us both, Harlan. She's too mean to drown."

"That's what they said about your mother."

The words landed the way such words always landed—casually thrown, heavily felt. Pruett didn't mean anything by it. In Port Calloway, nobody meant anything by anything anymore; meaning required energy, and the town had run dry of it years ago. Jonah tipped two fingers off his cap brim and let it pass.

"Just need water and diesel," he said. "And whatever's edible at the mercantile that isn't older than me."

"The pickled eggs are fresh-ish."

"I weep with gratitude."

Pruett almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth the dock had produced since the last warm front, and Jonah took it as payment in full. He signed the ledger—*J. Sterling, passing through*, same as always—and went up the street to see about supplies.

* * *

The mercantile smelled of rope, coffee, and the particular sweetness of a building slowly surrendering to salt air. Old Mrs. Adeyemi ran it now, third generation, and she was the only person in Port Calloway who still asked questions she expected answers to.

"You look thin," she said, ringing up diesel cans he hadn't finished loading yet.

"I always look thin. It's the wind."

"It's the running." She slid a paper sack across the counter—bread, cheese, a jar of something amber and suspicious. "You've been through here four times in six years. Never twice the same season. A man runs from something, Jonah, or he runs toward something, and either way he ought to stop once and figure out which."

"And if it's both?"

"Then he ought to stop twice." She pushed the jar an inch closer. "On the house. You've got your mother's face when you're hungry. Did anyone ever tell you that?"

"Everyone tells me that," Jonah said, "and no one ever tells me anything useful afterward."

Mrs. Adeyemi looked at him a long moment over her spectacles, and for a second he thought she might break the pattern. But the bell over the door jangled, a customer came in wanting minnows, and the moment drowned quietly, like most moments did in Port Calloway.

Back at the boat, there was mail waiting—or rather, there was a single envelope wedged under the wheelhouse door, rain-spotted and soft-cornered, as though it had been traveling a long time and had nearly given up. No stamp. No return address. Just his name in ink gone brownish with age:

*Jonah Sterling.*

His mother's handwriting.

He knew it instantly, the way you know a voice heard once across a crowded room forty years ago. The tall ascenders. The little hook she put on the J, like the letter was trying to climb out of its own word. Jonah stood on the deck holding the envelope without opening it for longer than any reasonable man would have, because the reasonable thing and the possible thing were separated by a wide channel, and he wasn't sure he could swim it.

She had been dead eleven years. Drowned off Greywater Shoals in four feet of water, which everyone agreed was the strangest part—the great Ilsa Sterling, who could hold her breath longer than any diver on the coast, taken by four feet of water on a calm day. They called it an accident. They called her worse than that. *Thief,* mostly. Because eleven years before she drowned, the cargo of the *Meridian Star*—a salvage prize worth more than the whole of Port Calloway—had vanished from her custody, and Edmund Voss, her own business partner, had sworn before the insurance men that she'd taken it. She never found it. She never cleared her name. She died owing the world an explanation, and the world, being the world, collected the debt from her son instead.

Jonah sat down on the wheelhouse bench. Opened the envelope with his thumbnail, careful, like it might startle.

Inside were two things.

The first was a letter, one page, written in that climbing hand. It began without greeting, because apparently even from beyond the grave his mother saw no reason to waste words:

*If you're reading this, then someone finally got it to you, and I'm sorry it took so long. I paid a boy to deliver it after—if you understand me. I couldn't send it while I lived. You know why.*

*I never took the Star's cargo. I want that written down somewhere besides my own head, where it's been rattling around keeping me up nights. I know who did. I could prove it, once. Then I couldn't anymore, and saying so out loud became a good way to stop breathing.*

*But I found the wreck. Not where they say she sank—where she actually sank. The charts will show you. The cave only opens on a freak tide, the kind that comes maybe once in a generation, and the next one is coming. I could feel it in the tables. I was going in after the proof when I drowned. Make of that what you will.*

*Don't come looking, Jonah. That's the mother in me talking. The salvager in me knows you won't listen, so I've drawn everything you'll need. Tide tables, depths, the passage. If you go—and you'll go—remember: the cave floods complete in six tides. Count them. Count them twice.*

*You were the best thing I ever pulled out of the water. Forgive me the rest.*

*—I.S.*

Jonah read it three times. The third time his eyes blurred, which he attributed to the sea spray, though the morning was dry and the *Pelican's Rest* was moored a quarter mile from any spray worth mentioning.

The second item was the chart.

It was a nautical chart of the Kessler coast, hand-annotated in colored pencil—his mother's colors, blues and greens faded now to the memory of themselves. Depths marked in her cramped figures. Current arrows. And there, on a stretch of coastline south of Greywater Shoals that Jonah had passed a hundred times without a second glance, a small circle drawn in red, and beside it coordinates he had never seen, precise to the second, and beneath those, in letters pressed so hard into the paper they'd nearly torn it:

*THE MOUTH OPENS ONLY WHEN THE SEA GOES OUT TOO FAR. WATCH FOR THE LOW THAT SHOULDN'T HAPPEN.*

Jonah sat with the chart spread across his knees while the light went long and gold over the dying town, and he felt the strange double weight of it—grief and purpose arriving together, conjoined twins, impossible to tell where one ended and the other began. She had died trying to reach this. Four feet of water, they said. A calm day. And here was the truth of it drawn in her own hand: she'd been diving a passage no one else knew existed, chasing the proof that would have cleared her name, and the sea had closed over her the way the sea closes over everything eventually.

Everyone had called her a thief. His whole life, he'd carried that word like ballast he couldn't jettison. And now his inheritance, eleven years late, was not gold, not cargo, not even an apology—just a chart, a warning, and a question with teeth:

*What was she going to find?*

He should burn it. That was the smart play. The safe play. Take on his diesel, point the *Pelican's Rest* upriver, and keep moving the way he'd always moved, owing nothing, stopping nowhere, letting dead women and dead ships and dead towns lie where the water had put them.

Jonah looked at the red circle on the chart. South of Greywater Shoals. Two days' run, maybe less with the ebb behind him.

Then he looked up, and his breath caught—not at the chart, but past it, out through the wheelhouse glass, at the horizon south of the harbor mouth.

The sea out there had gone strange. He'd seen it only once before, as a boy—a low so deep and wrong that the water seemed to be holding its breath, drawing back from the land like a fist pulling out of a glove. The sandbars were showing dark and wet where no sandbar had a right to be. Somewhere out past the point, in water that should have been thirty feet deep, the sea was peeling itself away from the coast.

A freak low. The kind that came once in a generation.

The kind his mother had counted on.

Jonah Sterling stood up so fast the chart slid to the deck, and for the first time in eleven years, every restless mile in his blood pointed the same direction at once.

He fired the engine. The *Pelican's Rest* grumbled awake, old and mean and ready.

"Hold on, Mama," he said to nobody, to everybody, to the water. "I'm coming to count your tides."
