## 2. Six Tides

The tide went out that morning like a man pulling a tablecloth from under a dinner party.

Jonah Sterling had seen low tides before—seen them sulk and slump and leave the harbor smelling of mud and broken promises—but he had never seen the sea *leave*. It went out past the channel markers, past the kelp beds, past the sandbars that had wrecked better boats than his, until the Kessler coast stood exposed like a woman caught mid-dress, all ribs and secrets and no place left to hide.

He was standing on the deck of the *Magpie*, his riverboat and the only inheritance he'd ever wanted, drinking coffee that tasted like it had been boiled twice out of spite, when he noticed the birds.

Gulls were wheeling over a point of black rock two miles north, screaming in a way that gulls usually reserved for a fish spill or a funeral. Jonah had learned long ago that when birds agreed on something, it paid to listen. Birds had no politics. Birds never testified against anybody's mother.

"Delilah," he said to the engine, because he talked to the engine and not to himself, since one of those was a habit and the other was a warning sign, "I think the ocean just showed us its ankles."

He got the *Magpie* underway with the particular tenderness of a man starting a cold engine he could not afford to replace. She was sixty feet of patched riverboat, built for the delta channels, not the open coast, and she grumbled about the salt water the way old women grumble about weather. Jonah let her grumble. Grumbling meant she was still arguing with him, and the day she stopped arguing was the day he'd have to bury her.

The letter was folded in his shirt pocket, over his heart, where he'd been carrying it for eleven days like a stone he couldn't decide to skip or keep.

*The cave on the north point opens six tides a year,* his mother had written, in the cramped handwriting of a woman who'd learned to write on rocking decks. *But once in a great while the sea forgets itself. When it does, go look. And Jonah—go alone.*

Go alone. That was Sable Sterling all over. Even dead, even drowned, even disgraced, she was still issuing instructions from beyond, still convinced that the world was full of people who'd sell you out for a share of salvage. She'd been right about that, as it happened. She'd just been wrong about which people.

The *Magpie* rounded the point at half past seven, and Jonah stopped breathing.

* * *

The cave mouth was a hundred feet of black archway in the cliff face, exposed from the waterline down to a floor of glistening rock, dripping with forty years of held breath. Barnacles crusted the arch in great white constellations. Water hung in the entrance like a curtain of glass, thin as a soap bubble in places, and through it—through it—

Jonah killed the engine and let the boat drift, because some things you do not approach under power. Some things you approach the way you approach a sleeping animal.

The *Meridian Star* lay inside on her beam, half-buried in sand and stone, her hull furred with a generation of coral, her masts snapped like winter branches. But there was no mistaking her. Jonah had grown up on the story of the Meridian Star the way other children grew up on lullabies—the three-masted freighter that went missing with a full cargo in '61, the wreck that never surfaced, the salvage rights that had made and unmade half the families on this coast. He'd seen her portrait a hundred times in the harbor office, and in the taverns, and once—unforgettably—in the margins of his mother's tide charts, drawn in pencil by a hand that had trembled.

She was real. She was *here*. And the sea had peeled itself back for one morning to let him look at her, the way a magician lets you glimpse the card before the deck closes.

Jonah stood at the rail with his coffee going cold and felt something in his chest that had no business being there. It wasn't greed. He'd spent forty-one years making sure greed never got a hook in him—never stop, never owe, that was the Sterling creed, and it had kept him alive and unattached through more ports than he could name. No, this was something older and worse. This was the feeling of a locked room in his childhood suddenly standing open, and his mother's voice coming out of it, saying *I told you I wasn't lying.*

Because that was the thing. That was the whole rotten heart of it. Sable Sterling had claimed for twenty years that she knew where the Meridian Star lay, and the coast had called her a liar, and then a thief, and then—after the diving accident that took her—a cautionary tale. *Drowned chasing what she stole,* the harbor men said, spitting into the water. Jonah had left Kessler at nineteen and had not once come back to stay, because a town that buried your mother under a lie is not a town, it's a wound with streets.

And now the sea itself had testified on her behalf.

"Look at her, Ma," he said quietly. "Look what you found."

The gulls screamed. Somewhere inside the cave, water dripped in the dark, patient as a clock.

* * *

It was the shadow on the water that ruined the moment, because there is always a shadow, and Jonah had learned that the beautiful things in this world come with an invoice attached.

He saw the masts first—three of them, then five, rounding the southern headland in a line like knives being laid out on a table. Diesel smoke. Crane arms. The flat gray hulls of working boats, and at their center a salvage tender with a helipad and a name painted on the bow in letters you could read from a mile off:

*VOSS MARINE SALVAGE — WE FIND WHAT THE SEA KEEPS.*

Jonah's hands went still on the rail.

Edmund Voss.

There were men whose names you learned the way you learn the names of diseases. Edmund Voss had been the fleet's navigator forty years ago, the man whose charts placed the Meridian Star's last position, the man who took the stand in the salvage hearings and said, in a voice like a hymn, that Sable Sterling had been seen near the wreck site with salvage equipment she could not account for. The testimony had been neat. The testimony had been *navigated*, Jonah's mother always said, with a bitterness she wore like a second coat. *That man can chart a lie as straight as he can chart a channel.*

And now his fleet was coming around the headland into the bay, into the same freak tide, on the same morning, as if the sea had sent out invitations and only told half the guests.

Jonah did some arithmetic he didn't like. Voss had sonar, divers, a crane, and lawyers. Jonah had a riverboat, a dead woman's tide chart, and a coffee he hadn't finished. The tide would hold the cave open maybe six hours, maybe less—his mother's notes said the water would come back through the entrance like a fist closing, and after that the cave wouldn't open again for months, if the charts could be trusted, and his mother's charts could always be trusted, which was the one thing the coast had never believed about her.

He looked at the cave. He looked at the fleet. He did the arithmetic again and got the same answer, which was the worst thing about arithmetic: it never took pity on you.

"Well, Delilah," he said, and started the engine. She coughed, caught, and settled into her grumble, and Jonah turned her nose toward the cave mouth with the particular calm of a man who has decided to do something stupid and would prefer to do it competently.

* * *

The entrance was shallower than it looked, which was to say it was a graveyard with good lighting. Jonah took the *Magpie* in on the last of the tide, easing her over the sill of the arch with six inches to spare and a prayer he didn't believe in, and the moment her keel cleared the bar the cave swallowed them whole.

The sound changed. That was the first thing. The sea's noise fell away and was replaced by something older—dripping, echoing, the soft percussion of water finding its way through stone. The air smelled of salt and iodine and something mineral and cold, like the inside of the earth holding its breath.

And the Meridian Star rose up out of the gloom.

Photographs had not prepared him. Photographs never did. She was *big*—a cathedral of a wreck, her stern rearing up against the cave wall, her rail lines furred white, her name still legible on the transom beneath a century of barnacles: *MERIDIAN STAR, PORT KESSLER*. Forty years down there in the dark, and the sea had kept her name polished.

Jonah brought the boat alongside a shelf of rock that might once have been a loading dock, tied off, and just stood for a while, hat in hand, the way you do at a graveside. Because that's what this was. A grave with a cargo hold. A grave his mother had charted in secret and died trying to open.

"Six tides a year," he said aloud, and the cave repeated it back to him in a voice like a drowned man's. *Six tides. Six tides.* "You could have picked a better hiding spot."

He was reaching for his mother's chart, spread on the chart table under a lantern, when he heard it—engines, out in the bay, and not the distant growl of a fleet standing off. Close engines. A launch, running light, coming around the point with the confidence of somebody who knew the water.

Jonah doused the lantern and stood in the dark of the cave, listening to the tide lap against forty years of silence, and did the arithmetic one more time.

Six hours of open cave. One fleet coming in. One dead woman's chart. And somewhere out there on that lead boat, if the name on the transom meant what it meant, a navigator who had grown up in Edmund Voss's house—which meant a navigator who had grown up on the same lie Jonah had.

The launch rounded the point. Through the cave mouth, shrinking now as the sea began its slow return, Jonah watched it come on—and saw, standing at its bow with a pair of binoculars and a face he couldn't read at this distance, a young woman who was not looking at the cave at all.

She was looking at the *Magpie*.

Jonah exhaled slowly, folded his mother's chart, and tucked it into his shirt pocket, over his heart, where the letter lived.

"Never stop, never owe," he told the dark. The dark, being a cave, said nothing. But somewhere in its flooded depths, the Meridian Star waited with her hold full of answers, and the water was already coming back to keep her.
