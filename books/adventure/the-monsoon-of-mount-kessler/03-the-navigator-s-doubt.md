## 3. The Navigator's Doubt

Mara Voss had learned navigation the way other children learned prayers: at her father's knee, before dawn, with salt stinging her eyes and his voice steady as a compass needle. *Numbers don't lie, little star,* he'd say, tapping the chart with one blunt finger. *People lie. Weather lies. The sea itself lies half the time. But numbers—numbers are the honest bones of the world.*

She was thirty-one years old now, and she had begun to suspect that her father had never met a number he couldn't bend.

The fleet's flagship, *Iron Meridian* — Edmund had named it for the wreck he swore he'd find someday, though Mara privately thought it was less a tribute than an obsession wearing a ship's paint — sat at anchor in Kessler Bay with her running lights burning against the thickening dusk. Behind her, the rest of the fleet rode their moorings like a string of dark beads: four vessels, forty crew, and enough salvage equipment to raise a cathedral. They had come because of a rumor. A freak tide. A cave mouth bared on the Kessler coast like a secret told in sleep.

And inside that cave, if the rumor held true, the *Meridian Star*.

Mara stood at the chart table in the aft cabin and did what she had done eleven times since the fleet dropped anchor: she laid her father's original survey of the sinking beside the coordinates from the new sighting, and she let them argue.

They argued beautifully.

"Say it out loud," she murmured to the paper. "Go on."

The numbers said it plainly enough. Forty years ago, when the *Meridian Star* went down in the storm of '84, Edmund Voss — then a young mate with a reputation for being lucky — had filed the position report himself. He'd placed the wreck two miles south-southeast of the Kessler headland, in open water, forty fathoms down. It was the position every chart since had carried. It was the position three separate salvage expeditions had dragged their grappling hooks across, finding nothing but sand and old disappointment.

It was also, according to the sighting that had brought the fleet here, wrong by nearly two miles and eighty feet of depth.

Wrecks drift, Mara reminded herself. Hulls break apart; currents carry pieces. She'd written that explanation in her own logbook twice, in her neatest hand, like a girl copying lines on a slate.

But a hull doesn't drift two miles *inland*, through solid rock, into a sea cave.

She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye until colors bloomed. There were other explanations, kinder ones. Her father had been concussed during the sinking — everyone knew the story of how he'd washed ashore clinging to a hatch cover, half-drowned, heroic. Maybe his bearings had been scrambled. Maybe he'd reported what he believed rather than what he'd seen, and pride had kept him from correcting it later, and then decades of pride had built up around the error like coral around a spar, until pulling it free would wound everything it touched.

That was the version she'd carried all day. It fit inside her chest without cutting anything.

Then there was the other thing. The small thing. The thing she kept turning over like a stone in her pocket.

Her father's logbook from the *Star*'s last voyage — the one he kept locked in his cabin, the one he'd shown her exactly once, years ago, to prove some point about seamanship — recorded the cargo manifest as timber and machine parts. Ordinary freight. But the insurance settlement, which Mara had helped organize for the archive last winter, listed the *Meridian Star*'s insured value at more than six times what timber and machine parts could possibly be worth.

Six times. She'd flagged the discrepancy in a memo. Her father had taken the memo from her hand, read it, smiled at her — that warm, crinkling smile that had gotten her through every storm of her childhood — and said, "Insurance men round up, little star. Always have." And he'd burned the memo in the galley stove while she watched, still smiling, as if it were a small joke between them.

Numbers don't lie, little star.

Mara rolled the charts closed and went out onto the deck, because the cabin had started to feel like a held breath.

* * *

The bay at dusk was the color of hammered pewter. Out past the headland, the sea lay unnaturally flat under the first bruised bands of cloud pushing down from the north — the monsoon was coming early this year, the fishermen said, and they said it the way people say things they're hoping will turn out to be superstition.

Mara walked the quarterdeck, checking lights, checking lines, doing the small honest work that kept her hands busy while her mind gnawed its bone. That was when she saw the boat.

It came creeping along the shallows inside the headland, a riverboat gone to seed — shallow draft, paddle-wheel housing rusted to lace, riding low and unlit. Nobody ran a riverboat into Kessler Bay after dark unless they were lost, desperate, or poaching. She raised her glass and found the figure at the wheel: broad-shouldered, weathered, moving with the particular economy of a man who had spent his whole life alone on water.

Jonah Sterling.

She knew him by silhouette the way you know a story you were told as a child. Son of Lena Sterling, the salvager. The thief. The woman who, according to the tale every deckhand on the coast could recite, had tried to loot the *Meridian Star* twenty years ago and drowned in disgrace off the Kessler shore. Edmund Voss had been the one who identified her charts, who gave testimony about her intentions, who had spoken at the inquiry with grief in his voice about how the sea forgives nothing.

Mara lowered the glass slowly.

What, precisely, was Jonah Sterling doing skimming the shallows beneath the headland — directly between the fleet's anchorage and the cave mouth?

She should sound the alarm. That was the arithmetic of loyalty, simple and clean: stranger in forbidden waters, daughter of the fleet's master, bell rung, threat removed. Her father would praise her. The fleet would grumble approvingly. The world would stay in the shape it had always been.

Instead she found herself going down the accommodation ladder, untying the gig, and rowing out alone into the pewter dusk, telling herself it was reconnaissance. Telling herself a great many things, apparently, lately.

* * *

The riverboat sat aground on a spit of sand below the headland, listing gently, looking embarrassed about it. Its owner was in the shallows to his knees, hauling something out of the water — not salvage, Mara realized as she brought the gig alongside. Driftwood? No. He was pulling up lengths of old line, tangled and weed-hung, examining each one and letting most of them fall back.

"You're aground," she called.

He didn't startle, which annoyed her slightly. He turned, waist-deep now, water sheeting off his arms, and looked at her with eyes the color of wet slate. "So I am," Jonah Sterling said. "You must be the navigator."

"And you must be either lost or stupid. This bay's posted. Salvage rights are claimed."

"Claimed by whom?" He waded toward her, unhurried. "Your father hasn't filed yet. I checked. Paperwork takes three days. Until then, the bay belongs to whoever's standing in it." He gestured at the water around his legs. "Present company included."

"That's not how rights work."

"It's exactly how rights work. I've been reading tide tables and maritime law in equal measure for twenty years, and I can tell you the law is just tide tables with better handwriting." He reached the gig and rested one arm on its gunwale, close enough now that she could see the salt dried white on his shoulders, the rope scar across one forearm. Up close he didn't look like a poacher or a thief's son. He looked tired, in the way of a man who had been carrying something heavy for a long distance.

"What are you pulling out of the water?" she asked.

"Old mooring line." He held up a length, weed-green and rotten. "Somebody anchored off this spit once. Long time ago. Heavy tackle, deep-set, professional work." His eyes came up to hers, and there it was — the question underneath the question. "Your father's fleet uses this kind of line."

Something cold moved through Mara that had nothing to do with the evening air.

"My father's never anchored here," she said. "His surveys put the wreck two miles out."

"Do they," Sterling said. Not a question. Just a low, careful note, like a man tapping a wall and listening for the hollow place behind it.

"The numbers are wrong," Mara heard herself say, and was astonished at her own mouth. "Two miles wrong. Eighty feet wrong. I've checked them eleven times."

Sterling went very still. Water dripped off his elbow into the gig. "Eleven times," he repeated.

"Don't read anything into it. I check everything eleven times. It's a habit."

"Of course it is." Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. "My mother used to say the truth doesn't mind being counted twice. It's the lies that get nervous."

The name landed between them like a dropped tool. *Mother.* Mara watched him watch her take it in, and understood that he'd said it deliberately, the way you show a card to see what the other player holds.

"Lena Sterling's charts were wrong too," Mara said quietly. "That's what my father always said. She plotted the wreck in the caves. Everyone knew she was—"

"Everyone knew she was what?" His voice stayed level, but something underneath it had shifted, tectonic. "Finish the sentence. You've heard it your whole life. Say it."

"A thief," Mara said. "I'm sorry. I don't—" She stopped, because she suddenly wasn't sure what she didn't. Didn't believe? Hadn't wondered? The stone in her pocket turned over again, showing a new face. "My father testified at her inquiry. He said her charts proved intent."

"He said a lot of things at that inquiry." Sterling looked out at the darkening water, at the cave mouth hidden somewhere beyond the headland, holding its dead like a fist. When he spoke again his voice was quieter. "You want to know why your father's numbers are wrong, Navigator? Ask him why he'd remember a wreck position wrong by two miles when he claims he watched it go down. Ask him why the insurance valuation runs six times the manifest. Ask him—" He stopped himself, jaw working.

"Ask him what?" Mara said. "Ask him what?"

But Sterling shook his head and stepped back, water streaming off him, and she realized with a strange lurch that he wasn't withholding the answer to punish her. He was withholding it because he liked her — or wanted to — and didn't yet trust her with it. Which was somehow worse.

"I'll trade you," she said quickly.

He paused.

"Information for information. You tell me what your mother's charts showed. I tell you..." She cast about, and found, to her horror, that the truest thing she had was also the most dangerous. "I tell you that I've been checking those numbers for eleven days, and that tonight I decided I'm not going to stop."

For a long moment the only sounds were the lap of water and, far off, the fleet's anchor bells ringing soft and patient in the dusk.

"That's not much of a trade," Sterling said at last. "That's just you, talking."

"It's the only thing I have that's actually mine."

Something changed in his face then — a door opening a crack in a wall she hadn't known was load-bearing. He fished inside his shirt and drew out a folded square of oilcloth, unwrapping it with the care of a man handling a relic. Inside was a chart, old and salt-stained, covered in a fine precise hand and annotated in a code of tiny symbols — moons, wave-marks, numerals in circles.

"Lena Sterling's tide chart," he said. "For this coast. For this cave." He held it where she could see but not touch. "I can't read half of it. Yet. But I know someone who reads charts the way priests read scripture, and she's standing waist-deep in front of me pretending to be angry."

"I *am* angry," Mara said automatically, and then, honestly: "I don't know what I am."

"Welcome to the family," Jonah Sterling said grimly. "We're mostly confused."

Behind them, out in the bay, the *Iron Meridian*'s lanterns burned in a neat, obedient row — her whole life, lit up and anchored, waiting for her to come back aboard and be her father's daughter. Ahead, in the dark beyond the headland, the cave waited with whatever it waited with.

Mara looked at the chart in Sterling's hands, at the small confident symbols drawn by a dead woman the world had called a liar, and heard her father's voice saying *numbers don't lie, little star* — and thought, with the first real vertigo of her life:

*No. But somebody taught them to.*

"I'll need the chart till morning," she said. "And a lamp. And you," she added, before she could stop herself, "to explain what these moon symbols mean, because if my father finds out I've—"

She stopped. Sterling waited.

"If he finds out," Mara finished quietly, "there won't be a second conversation. There'll just be consequences."

"Then we'd best make sure he doesn't," said Jonah Sterling, and handed her his mother's chart.

Far out past the headland, the first wind of the monsoon came off the sea and stirred the flat water, and somewhere in the deep, patient dark, the third tide began its long slow climb.
