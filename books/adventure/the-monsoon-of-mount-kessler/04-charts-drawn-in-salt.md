## 4. Charts Drawn in Salt

The tide chart had been folded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth, and Jonah Sterling held it under the lamp in his cabin like a man reading a letter from the dead—which, in every way that mattered, he was.

His mother's handwriting covered it. Not the careful schoolroom script she'd used on grocery lists and birthday cards, but the quick, sure shorthand of a working salvager: numbers stacked in columns, arrows curling around the margins, little annotations in pencil that had faded to ghosts. *Slack +40. Watch the second sill. Don't trust the ebb after rain.* He could almost hear her voice saying them, that dry Kessler-coast drawl she'd never lost even after twenty years upriver.

He'd spread the chart across the chart table in the *Pelican's Grace*, his boat, his whole floating life, and for three hours he had been turning it this way and that, drinking cold coffee, feeling like a man trying to read a letter through fogged glass. The coordinates from the cave matched a small penciled cross near the bottom of the sheet. Around it, his mother had drawn six arcs, one after another, each labeled with a date.

Six tides.

He counted again, because counting was what you did when you didn't understand something. Six arcs, six dates, spaced roughly twelve hours apart—no, not quite twelve. The intervals shortened as they went, the way tides do when a spring tide is building toward its peak. And beside the sixth arc, in letters pressed so hard into the paper they'd nearly torn it:

*FULL FLOOD — CAVE CLOSED.*

Jonah sat back. The lamp swung gently with the boat's motion, throwing shadows across the chart like slow hands.

The cave flooded completely in six tides. Whatever window existed—the freak low tide that had bared the cave mouth, the one he'd stood in two days ago with his heart hammering against his ribs—that window wasn't just closing. It was closing on a schedule, and his mother had mapped that schedule forty years ago to the hour.

She'd known. She'd stood where he was standing, or somewhere very like it, and worked out the same arithmetic of water and stone, and she'd written it down and folded it away—and then she'd gone out to Mount Kessler and drowned, and everyone in Port Calloway had said *thief* over her grave, and the chart had sat in a lawyer's drawer for four decades waiting for a son who never stopped moving long enough to open an envelope.

"Damn it, Mama," he said quietly, to no one. The coffee was terrible. He drank it anyway.

He bent back over the chart. There was more. Between the third and fourth arcs, his mother had drawn a small shape—a narrow channel snaking inland from the cave mouth, marked with hatching like a hazard on any honest chart. Beside it: *SUBMERGED PASSAGE. THIRD TIDE ONLY. Depth at slack: 4 fathoms, maybe 5. Air pocket past the sill — CONFIRMED 3×.*

Confirmed three times. Which meant she'd been through it. Three times at least, swimming or diving through a flooded throat of rock into the dark belly of a mountain, on a tide window measured in minutes.

And then, beneath that, smaller, in pencil so faint he had to tilt the page to catch the lamplight:

*Hold is reachable. Cargo intact. E.V. knows. Be careful of E.V.*

E.V.

Edmund Voss.

The name landed in Jonah's chest like an anchor dropped from height. He set the chart down carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and walked to the galley window and looked out at the black water of the harbor, where the lights of the Voss salvage fleet rode at anchor like a string of carnival bulbs strung across the dark.

*Be careful of E.V.*

Forty years ago, Edmund Voss had sat in a hearing room in Port Calloway and testified, calm and sorrowful, that Sera Sterling had been seen taking cargo from the wreck of the Meridian Star before the insurance survey. That testimony had ended her career, emptied her accounts, and written *thief* on her name so deeply that even drowning hadn't washed it out. Jonah had grown up in the shadow of that word. He'd built his whole philosophy on top of it—never stop, never owe, never let anyone close enough to hand you a word like that.

And here was his mother's own hand, telling him the man who'd buried her had known about the hold all along.

* * *

Sleep didn't come, so he did what he always did when sleep wouldn't come: he worked. He cleared the chart table, weighted the corners of his mother's sheet with the brass ashtray, the biscuit tin, and the ship's cat—who objected, was bribed with sardines, and settled into a purring paperweight—and began to copy everything out clean.

That was the ritual. His mother had taught it to him when he was nine years old, sitting cross-legged on the deck of her old salvage skiff while she copied charts by lantern light. *"A chart in your head is an opinion, Jonah-bird. A chart on paper is a promise. Copy it clean, check it twice, and then you can bet your life on it."* He'd thought it was just something salvagers said. Now, copying her arcs and annotations line by line onto fresh paper, he understood she'd been teaching him how to inherit her.

By the fourth hour of the night, the picture assembled itself in his head with the slow, inevitable click of a lock turning.

The freak low tide that had exposed the cave mouth was the first of six. Each following tide would be higher inside the cave than the last, the water climbing the rock in steps, until the sixth tide filled the chamber entirely and sealed it—maybe for another forty years, maybe forever. The wreck of the Meridian Star sat on the floor of that chamber, and for the first tide or two, a man might walk to her across wet stone, wading, hauling gear. Easy. Generous, even.

But the cargo hold was another matter. His mother's hatched channel—the submerged passage—ran beneath the wreck's stern section, a flooded crawl of rock that only gave access when the water level sat in a narrow band: too low, and there wasn't depth to swim the passage; too high, and the pressure and current made it a meat grinder. Third tide. Third tide only. A window of perhaps ninety minutes at slack water, four or five fathoms down, through a passage his mother had swum three times and come back from three times.

And on the fourth attempt—the one that must have fallen between the third and fourth arcs on her chart, the gap in the record, the blank space where the handwriting stopped—

On the fourth attempt, she hadn't come back.

Jonah put both hands flat on the table and breathed until the shaking stopped. It took a while.

He had spent thirty years telling himself his mother's death was an accident, because the alternative was a grief with a name on it, and a named grief demands things. Demands answers. Demands confrontation. Demands that a man stop running long enough to face what he's running from. So he'd run. River after river, contract after contract, never moored longer than a season, never owed anyone anything, never stayed anywhere long enough for anyone to say *thief's son* to his face and mean it.

Now the grief had a name, and it also had a schedule, and a depth, and a slack-water window, and his mother's neat, patient handwriting all over it.

*You left me the map,* he thought. *Not the gold. You knew I'd come looking for gold, and you left me the map instead.*

Because that was the thing, the thing that kept catching in his chest like a fishhook: the chart wasn't a treasure map. There were no X marks, no depth-of-gold, no valuation notes. Salvagers wrote valuations; it was the first thing any of them did. His mother had written depths and times and hazards and warnings about Edmund Voss, and not one word about what the cargo was worth. As if the cargo had never been the point. As if what she'd wanted recovered from the Meridian Star wasn't silver or silk or whatever the insurers had claimed, but proof—the captain's records, the manifest, whatever would clear her name. She'd died trying to reach a piece of paper.

Or she'd died trying to reach the truth, which amounted to the same thing and weighed considerably more.

Outside, the sky over the harbor had begun to bruise. Monsoon season came early some years, and the barometer on the bulkhead had been falling all evening with the steady, unhurried honesty of a witness who has nothing to gain by lying. Jonah checked it, wrote the reading in the logbook out of habit, and caught himself writing it in his mother's shorthand without meaning to.

*Watch the second sill. Don't trust the ebb after rain.*

"No," he said aloud, to the cat, to the chart, to the woman who'd drawn it. "I don't suppose I will."

* * *

Dawn came up gray and restless, wind out of the southeast, and Jonah went up to the wheelhouse with the copied chart rolled in a waterproof sleeve under his arm and a plan assembling itself behind his eyes with the same inevitability as the tide.

Third tide fell tomorrow, late morning. That gave him today to sound the approach channel, rig dive lines, and lay in air—one good tank, one spare, and the old hookah rig in the aft locker that he'd swear was reliable if nobody asked him directly. Ninety minutes at slack. Four fathoms. A passage his mother had swum three times.

He could do it alone. He'd done harder things alone, or told himself he had.

But the fleet was still anchored in the bay, and Edmund Voss hadn't kept a salvage operation idling in a dying harbor for two days out of sentiment, which meant Jonah had maybe one tide of quiet before the race stopped being a race and became a collision. And there was the other matter—the matter of Mara Voss, standing in the shallows two days ago with salt drying in her hair, looking at her father's wreck-position figures like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the answer to, and trading him information instead of reporting him. He hadn't stopped thinking about that. He suspected he wasn't going to.

He was weighing all of this, one hand on the wheel, the *Grace* idling out past the breakwater into a sea the color of hammered pewter, when he saw the dinghy.

It came around the end of the breakwater fast, engine laboring, riding sloppy in the chop—the kind of handling that meant either a drunk or someone in a hurry who didn't care who saw them. As it closed, Jonah made out the figure at the tiller: lean, dark-haired, wearing a fleet oilskin with the collar turned up against the spray.

Mara Voss brought the dinghy alongside with more courage than finesse, hooked a fender over his rail, and looked up at him with the expression of a person who has already burned a bridge and is now checking whether the far bank was worth it.

"You need to see something," she called over the wind. "From my father's cabin."

Jonah looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached down, killed the engine, and threw her a line.

"Aboard," he said. "And mind the cat. He bites negotiators."
