## 5. What Edmund Knew

The *Corvus* smelled like her childhood: pipe tobacco, chart ink, and the particular staleness of a man who believed fresh air was for other people. Mara Voss stood in the doorway of her father's cabin and felt eleven years old again, waiting to be told she'd done something wrong.

She hadn't been invited in. That was the point.

Edmund Voss kept his cabin locked when the fleet was at anchor—not against thieves, he'd said once, laughing, but against curiosity, which was worse. The lock was old brass, older than Mara, and it had surrendered to her hairpin in under a minute, which told her something about how seriously her father had ever expected to be robbed by his own daughter.

"Sorry, Papa," she murmured to the empty room, not meaning it even slightly.

* * *

She went first to the chart drawer, because that was where navigators lied best. Her father's charts were famous—Mara had grown up tracing his coastlines like scripture, learning that the sea rewarded precision and punished sentiment. But she'd spent three nights now with the wreck's position burning in her head, and his numbers didn't fit. The Meridian Star, according to Edmund's own report, should have gone down four miles north of where Jonah Sterling had found her. Four miles. On a chart, four miles was a continent.

So she wasn't looking for navigation anymore. She was looking for motive.

It took her an hour. The cabin was organized the way her father organized everything: ruthlessly on the surface, chaos underneath, like a man who'd tidied his whole life over one bad week forty years ago and never looked beneath the floorboards since. She found the insurance file wedged behind a false back in the drawer where he kept his winter socks—a hiding place so banal it was almost touching.

*MERIDIAN STAR — CARGO MANIFEST & CLAIM*, stamped in faded red. Dated six weeks after the sinking.

She sat on his bunk—the bunk where she'd slept through storms as a girl, listening to him read lighthouse inventories aloud until she calmed—and she read.

The manifest listed machine parts, cotton bales, tin ingots. Respectable cargo. The claim was filed for the respectable cargo, at the respectable value, and the insurer had paid out promptly because Edmund Voss's paperwork was, as always, immaculate.

But paperclipped to the back, in a hand that wasn't her father's—the captain's, maybe, or an adjuster's—was a second inventory. A short one. And Mara, who could read a tide table faster than a menu, felt the room tilt.

Opium, pressed into tins marked TIN INGOTS. Unmarked crates of rifles. And a line item that made her laugh out loud, once, harshly, alone in a locked room: *12 cases "perfume," contents unverified, owner declines inspection.*

Her father hadn't just known about the contraband. The shipping orders were initialed *E.V.* He'd arranged it. He'd loaded the Meridian Star with poison and guns, insured the honest half, and then—when the storm came, when the ship broke on the Kessler coast—he'd written a wreck report so clean, so boring, so precisely wrong about the location that no one would ever go looking for what actually lay in that hold.

And when a salvager named Iris Sterling started asking questions—when she got close enough to find the cave, close enough to see the tins—

Mara put the file down because her hands were shaking and she didn't want to smudge the evidence. That was the navigator in her. Protect the instruments.

There was a deposition copy clipped behind the claim. Her father's testimony, given when Mara was four years old. *I observed Iris Sterling removing cargo from the wreck site prior to official salvage adjudication.* Twenty-two words. Twenty-two words that had turned a woman into a thief, a boy into an orphan of reputation, and a port town into a place that still spat when the Sterling name came up.

He'd testified against a woman who knew too much. And he'd used his daughter's future inheritance—the fleet, the name, the immaculate charts—to pay for everyone's silence ever since.

Mara sat very still for a long time. Outside, the gulls argued over something dead, and the fleet's rigging sang its low evening chord, and none of it changed.

Then she heard boots on the deck above, and her father's voice giving some order about the dive lights, and she understood that the conversation she'd been rehearsing her whole life was finally, horribly, due.

* * *

She didn't hide the file. She left it open on his chart table, weighted down with his own brass dividers, and she sat in his chair with her hands folded, the way he did when he was about to ruin someone's evening.

Edmund Voss came into his cabin mid-sentence—"Mara, tell Cortez the third light is *not* optional, the man dives like a mole"—and stopped.

For a moment, father and daughter regarded each other across forty years of immaculate paperwork.

"You picked my lock," he said.

"You hid a confession behind your socks."

"That's not—" He set down his hat with exaggerated care, buying himself time. She knew all his tells; she'd catalogued them the way she catalogued currents. "That is an old insurance matter. Settled. Closed. You're rummaging through my private papers over a rumor from a Sterling boy who'd say anything to get his hands on my wreck."

"*Your* wreck." Mara turned the file around so the second inventory faced him. "Twelve cases of perfume, Papa. Owner declines inspection. You initial here, here, and here. I've seen your initials my whole life. You press hard on the E."

Something moved behind his eyes—not guilt exactly. Guilt would have been easier. It was more like a man watching a tide come in over ground he'd thought was high and dry forever.

"Mara." He pulled out the chair opposite hers and sat, and for one treacherous second he looked tired, and old, and human, and she nearly forgave him out of pure reflex. "Sit properly. Listen to me."

"I'm standing."

"Fine. Stand." He folded his hands. "Whatever you think you've found, you don't understand what that woman was."

"Iris Sterling."

"She was going to burn this fleet down." His voice dropped into the register he used in storms—calm, absolute, the voice that made grown deckhands move without questioning why. "Not just me. Half the families in this port had money in that run. Judges. Harbor board. When she started pulling threads, she wasn't chasing truth, she was chasing leverage, and if she'd gotten it, do you know what happens? Every contract voided. Every pension gone. Your mother—" his voice caught, genuinely, and Mara hated herself for noticing, "—your mother was ill that year. Where did you think the treatments came from?"

"The insurance payout," Mara said quietly. "From a ship you sank full of opium."

"I did not sink anything. The storm sank her. I merely—" he gestured vaguely, a man trying to describe weather, "—declined to correct the record afterward."

"You sent a woman to drown in disgrace."

"I sent a woman to stop digging in a hole that would have buried this entire town!" He was on his feet now, and the mask was off, and underneath it Mara found not a monster but something sadder: a man who had made one enormous rotten decision and then spent forty years building a life on top of it, load-bearing lie by load-bearing lie, until the lie *was* the life. "You think I wanted any of this? I was twenty-nine years old and someone offered me a fortune to look away, and I looked, and then there was no way to stop looking. That is how it works, Mara. That is how it has always worked. You inherit a hull with rot in it and you keep sailing, because the alternative is everyone drowns."

"And Jonah Sterling?" she asked. "What did he inherit?"

Edmund stopped.

"He inherited your lie," she said. "Every day of it. His mother died with the whole port calling her a thief, and her son grew up running riverboats because he can't stand to be anywhere people remember her name. That's what your rot did. Not to judges. To a boy."

"He'll use you." Her father's voice went soft and cold, and this was the part she'd been bracing for, the part that had teeth. "You think that man approached you by accident? He found the wreck, and then he found *you*, my daughter, my navigator, because you're worth more to him than any dive crew. He'll let you decode his mother's little treasure map, and the moment the hold is open he'll take what's in it and lose you like ballast. They're Sterlings, Mara. Taking things is what they do. Ask anyone in this port whose fault the last forty years were."

"I did ask," Mara said. "I asked the insurance file."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, Cortez was shouting cheerfully about the third light, having apparently located it himself.

Edmund straightened his cuffs. Reassembled himself, plank by plank, into Captain Voss, and when he spoke again it was with terrible gentleness, which was always his final weapon.

"You're my daughter," he said. "Everything I built is yours. The fleet, the name, all of it—clean, legitimate, yours. One word from you and I'll cut that Sterling boy loose tonight, no harm done, and we'll bring up that cargo proper and legal and never speak of perfume again. That's the offer. It's the only offer, sweetheart, and it expires when the monsoon does."

"And if I say no?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him decide not to answer honestly.

"Get some sleep," he said. "We break the cave open at first light. Weather window's closing."

He left. The door clicked shut behind him, and Mara stood alone in the cabin that had been her whole world, holding a file that weighed less than a gull's feather and more than the fleet.

Break the cave open. At first light. With explosives, if the dive crews couldn't cut it—and she'd seen the crates they'd loaded yesterday, and they weren't carrying cutting gear.

If the cave came down, the hold came down. The log, the proof, whatever else lay in the dark with the Meridian Star—all of it buried under a hundred tons of rock, and the only man who could testify otherwise standing right here, offering her everything except the truth.

Mara looked at the porthole. Beyond it, low and bruise-colored, the first outer band of the monsoon was dragging itself across the horizon like something waking up angry.

And beyond the headland, past the point, a single lamp burned on the deck of an aging riverboat, where a man who never stopped moving was probably, at this very moment, doing something reckless and half-prepared and completely sincere.

She grabbed her coat, her slate, and her father's tide tables—the real ones, the ones that matched the wreck—and went out into the wind.
