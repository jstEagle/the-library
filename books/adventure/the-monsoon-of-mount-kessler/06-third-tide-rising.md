## 6. Third Tide, Rising

Jonah had dived in worse water. He'd dived in the Mirelle Channel with a busted regulator and a jellyfish bloom so thick it looked like the sea had grown a second sky. But he had never dived into a passage his mother had drawn on a chart in pencil—pencil, because she'd known even then that ink was too certain for what she was planning.

"Third tide," he said, checking the gauge strapped to his wrist. The water in the cave throat had dropped two feet since noon, and the submerged passage beneath the eastern shelf was showing its teeth: a black slot maybe four feet high, exhaling cold air like something sleeping.

Mara stood beside him on the wet stone, wetsuit half-zipped, hair tied back with what appeared to be a piece of rigging line. She'd brought her own gear from the fleet's stores without asking, which Jonah decided was either trust or theft, and with Vosses he suspected the line between the two ran thin.

"You're smiling," she said. "Stop it."

"I'm not smiling."

"Your whole face is doing something. It's unsettling."

"That's just my face."

"It's worse than I imagined." She zipped up and tested her regulator with the brisk efficiency of someone who had been told her whole life she was the best at this and had mostly believed it. "For the record, I think this is a bad idea. The passage narrows to under three feet for eleven meters, there's no direct ascent, and if the tide turns early we're corked in a bottle."

"Noted."

"And you're going anyway."

"My mother drew this chart," Jonah said. "She died forty yards from where we're standing. I'm not going to stand here and be sensible about it."

Mara looked at the black slot for a long moment. Then she pulled her hood up. "Fine. But if we die, I want it noted that I said 'fine' sarcastically."

* * *

The water took them the way cold water always does—a hand around the chest, a shout swallowed. Jonah went first, lamp beam cutting a cone through the green murk, following the pencil line his mother had left him: *hug the left wall, count your kicks, don't look back until the light changes.*

He counted his kicks. At thirty-one the light changed—from dead black to a strange, silvery gray, and then he was through, rising, breaking surface into an air pocket that shouldn't have existed.

It was the hold.

Not the hold of a ship, exactly—the Meridian Star had settled on her side, and what had been her cargo deck was now a tilted cathedral of ribbed steel and coral, half above water, half below, lit by shafts of light falling through a crack in the cave ceiling far overhead. The air smelled of salt and old wood and something faintly sweet, like tea left in a tin for forty years.

Mara surfaced beside him, spitting her regulator out. "Oh," she said, very quietly. That was all. Just *oh.*

"Yeah," Jonah said.

They climbed out onto the upturned hull, boots finding purchase on barnacled plating, and stood in the silver light looking down the length of the wreck. Cargo crates fanned across the floor like dropped cards, most of them split and empty, their contents long since claimed by the sea or by whoever had gotten here first.

"There's nothing," Mara said. "Forty years of salvage rights fought over, and there's nothing here."

Jonah didn't answer. He was looking at the far end of the hold, where a small iron door hung open—the strongroom—and beside it, wedged against the frame as if placed there rather than dropped, a canvas satchel gone stiff with salt.

His mother's initials were still legible on the flap. *E.S.*

"She got inside," he said. His voice came out strange. "She opened the strongroom. She was right here."

* * *

Mara had seen wrecks before. Her father kept photographs of them in his cabin like other men kept photographs of their children, and she had grown up learning to read a hull the way other girls read faces. But standing inside the Meridian Star was different. Standing inside it felt like being read.

While Jonah pried at the strongroom door, she drifted along the cargo deck, lamp low. And that was how she found the captain's log—not in the strongroom at all but lashed to a bracket near what had been the chart table, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with tar, done properly by someone who had expected the ship to be found eventually and wanted the finding to mean something.

"Jonah."

He came over fast, forgetting to be careful, banging his shoulder on a stanchion and swearing with feeling. She handed him the log without a word. The tar cracked away under his knife like old bread.

The pages were warped but legible. Captain Aldous Wren's hand was a schoolmaster's hand—precise, patient, faintly disapproving of everything, including the weather. They read by lamplight while the tide clock ticked somewhere behind Jonah's eyes.

*Day 14 out of Callao. Took aboard six crates at night anchorage, no manifest, no bills. Mr. Voss insists the paperwork will follow. Paperwork has followed us for fourteen days and caught us yet.*

Mara made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "He never could write anything down honestly. Even then."

*Day 19. Voss has moved the crates himself, twice. I no longer believe this is tobacco. I have told him the Star does not run contraband and he has laughed at me. A captain should not be laughed at on his own deck.*

Then, three pages later, the handwriting changed—shakier, pressed hard:

*Day 22. Storm coming early. Voss has ordered us into the Kessler coast despite my protest. He says the cave is deep enough to hide in until the search passes. He means to scuttle her, I am sure of it now. If any soul reads this: the cargo is his. The loss is his design. God forgive me for letting him aboard.*

And then, in the margin of the last entry, in a different hand entirely—smaller, slanted, furious—a single line that stopped Jonah's breath:

*Voss testified the Sterling woman had the manifests. She never had them. He burned them himself. — E.S.*

His mother's hand. He knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat. She had been here. She had found this log, read it, written in its margin—and then she had drowned trying to carry it out.

"She wasn't stealing," Jonah said. The words came out rough. "Everyone said she stripped the wreck and lied about it, and she was *here*, she was *documenting*—" He had to stop. The silver light wavered in front of him, and he blamed the water in his eyes on the dive.

Mara touched his arm. Briefly. Like setting a stitch. "My father testified that she had the manifests," she said slowly. "I've heard the story a thousand times. He said she produced papers proving ownership of the cargo. Papers nobody ever saw again."

"Because he burned them."

"Because he burned them." She looked down at the log, and Jonah watched something settle in her face—not grief exactly, more like a load-bearing wall finally admitting what it had been carrying. "Jonah. This log names him. In his own crew's hearing, effectively. This isn't suspicion. This is a confession with a witness list."

"Proof," Jonah said.

"Yes." She hesitated. "But proof of what happened forty years ago needs somebody alive to care about it. Salvage courts don't convict dead men. And my father—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening.

"Your father what?"

"Nothing. We have maybe twenty minutes before the passage closes. Take the log. Let's argue about my father on dry land."

* * *

Mara had spent her whole life navigating by her father's numbers, and it occurred to her, swimming back through the black slot with the captain's log sealed in a waterproof bag against her chest, that she had never once checked his arithmetic. Not once. Children don't. You assume the mapmaker loves you enough to be accurate.

The passage was tighter going out—the tide had turned, and the last ten meters were a squeeze of moving water and shifting gravel that scraped along her tank like fingernails. At one point her lamp caught Jonah ahead of her, wedged, kicking hard, and for three full seconds she watched him not move, and understood in her body what the word *corked* meant.

Then he popped free like a champagne cork and the gray light of the cave mouth opened above them, and they broke surface into ordinary evening air, gasping, alive, ridiculous.

"We're never doing that again," Mara said.

"Agreed."

"The tide tables say we have one more window. One. Tomorrow morning, third tide, and then the monsoon closes the book."

"Mm." Jonah was already hauling himself onto the rocks, pulling off his hood, holding the bag up to the dying light like a man checking whether a lottery ticket was real. "One more window."

"Jonah. Did you hear me? *One.*"

"I heard you." He sat down heavily on the wet stone, and the grin that spread across his face was so wide and so unguarded that she forgot, briefly, to find it unsettling. "Mara. Forty years. My mother died for this. Your father built a life on top of it. And it's been sitting in a bracket next to a chart table the whole time, waiting for somebody to check the arithmetic."

She sat down beside him. The bay was turning copper; out past the point, the fleet's lights were coming on one by one, neat and orderly, her father's little empire lighting itself for the evening. Somewhere over the horizon, the first bands of the monsoon were stacking up like furniture being moved into place.

"He'll know," she said quietly. "When we come forward with this, he'll know it was me. He'll know I found the insurance claim, and the log, and—" She shook her head. "You don't come back from that. There's no version of tomorrow where I'm still his navigator."

Jonah was quiet a moment. Then: "There's no version of tomorrow where I'm still broke and anonymous, either. Seems like we're both about to lose our jobs."

"Yours was losing itself. Your boat leaks."

"It doesn't leak. It *breathes*."

Despite everything—despite the log against her ribs and the wreck still humming in her bones—Mara laughed. It surprised her, the sound of it, like finding a room in a house she'd thought she knew completely.

Then Jonah went still. He was looking past her, toward the point, where a launch had put out from the fleet—running lights dark, engine low, cutting a straight line across the copper water directly toward the cave mouth.

"Mara," he said. "Does your father send boats out here at night?"

Her laughter died in her throat. "No," she said. "He sends them when he already knows something's here."

The launch was five minutes out, and the tide was already turning.
