## 7. The Fleet Weighs Anchor

Jonah Sterling had a theory about harbors: they were like people, and the bad ones always smelled of diesel and regret. The Kessler harbor smelled of both that morning, thick enough to chew, and he stood at the rail of the *Magpie's Rest* chewing on neither, because his mouth was too busy forming words he'd be ashamed of later.

"They took the propeller shaft coupling," Mara said from below, her voice flat in the way that flat things are when they're sitting on top of an earthquake.

Jonah went down into the engine room to see for himself, though there wasn't much point. There it was—or rather, there it wasn't. The coupling sat where it should have been like a tooth socket after a bad night. In its place, a greasy rag stuffed with what appeared to be deliberate, insulting care.

"They left me a rag," Jonah said. "A courtesy rag."

"That's not courtesy. That's a message."

"I know what it is. It's a message written in *rag*." He crouched, ran his thumb along the mounting bolts. Clean threads. Recent work. "Somebody who knew boats did this. Not a dockside thief—thieves take things worth taking. This is somebody saying *sit down, boy*."

Mara leaned against the bulkhead, arms crossed, and for a long moment neither of them said anything. Above them, through the deck plating, they could hear the harbor coming apart at the seams—the fleet's tenders hauling chain, the crane barge groaning out to its station off the cave mouth, men shouting numbers at each other like the numbers were prayers.

"He knows," Jonah said finally. It wasn't a question.

"He knows." Mara's jaw worked. "The log was in my cabin. I thought—I hid it under the chart drawer, which in hindsight is exactly where a navigator would look first, because it's where *I* would look first." She laughed, one dry note with no music in it. "I taught him everything about searching a boat. He taught me, rather. Same thing."

"Is he going to come after you?"

"No." She said it quickly, then slower, testing the word's weight. "No. That's the worst part. He won't even be angry at me, not really. He'll be *disappointed*, which is worse, because disappointment means he still thinks he owns the outcome of my life." She pushed off the bulkhead. "He'll just remove the obstacles. You're an obstacle."

"I've been called worse. Usually by creditors."

"This isn't funny, Jonah."

"It's a little funny. A man stole my propeller shaft and left a rag. Somewhere in this world there's a comedy writer taking notes."

But his hands weren't laughing. They were already moving, pulling the toolbox from its bracket, checking what he had and what he didn't, and what he didn't was a spare coupling, because nobody carried a spare coupling, because couplings don't fail, because *people make them fail*, and he'd spent forty-one years on the water and never once been sabotaged because he'd never once been close enough to anything worth sabotaging.

His mother's tide chart hung in the wheelhouse, weighted at four corners with coffee mugs. Six tides. The cave flooded completely on the sixth. They were somewhere in the fifth now—he could feel it in the light, that bruised green-gray creeping up the eastern sky, the monsoon assembling itself offshore like a debt collector who knew you were home.

"How long to fix?" Mara asked.

"If I had the part? Four hours. Without it?" He looked at the empty socket again. "There's a machine shop in Port Anselm. Two days' sail. Or—" he hated the sentence before he finished it, "—or I can fabricate something ugly out of the spare bronze stock and prayer, and it'll hold for maybe thirty hours if the sea stays calm."

"And will the sea stay calm?"

Outside, as if summoned by the question, the first band of the storm came across the harbor mouth—not rain yet, just wind, a long low moan through the breakwater gap that made every halyard in the port ring like a badly tuned choir.

"No," Jonah said. "It will not."

* * *

In the main cabin of the salvage vessel *Iron Meridian*, Edmund Voss stood before the chart table and did not look at his daughter's empty chair.

He had been looking at it all morning, actually, in the way a man looks at a hole in a fence he keeps meaning to fix. The captain's log lay open on the table before him, recovered from beneath her chart drawer by a man who had taught her everything she knew about hiding things, and it might have been the most painful irony of his long career that she'd hidden it so well precisely because he'd been such a thorough teacher.

*Voss arranged the manifest,* the dead captain had written, in a hand that shook even across forty years. *Voss signed the false report. The woman Sterling saw nothing but the truth, and the truth was worth more than the cargo.*

Edmund closed the log gently, the way you close a coffin.

Around him the fleet worked. Through the ports he could see the crane barge taking position over the cave, the drilling rig being swung aboard, the divers' tender circling like a gull that had learned ambition. His chief engineer, Okafor, stood waiting with a clipboard and the particular patience of a man who has asked a question twice already.

"The charges, Mr. Voss. Weather service says landfall by tomorrow morning. The rock face is sound, but blasting in a rising sea—"

"Blast it."

Okafor blinked. "Sir, the cave interior floods on the high tides. If we breach the far wall while the water's up, we lose the strongroom entirely. Everything in it goes to soup."

"Then blast it before the water comes up." Edmund rolled the log closed and held it a moment, feeling its weight, feeling forty years of weight behind it. "We have until the sixth tide. After that, the sea takes the evidence, and I spend whatever years I have left knowing a dead woman's son is holding a story about me."

"You could give it back to him, sir. The log. Let it go."

Edmund looked at his engineer for a long moment. Okafor was a good man, honest the way ballast is honest—it just sits there keeping you upright whether you want it or not.

"There are men in this fleet," Edmund said quietly, "who helped me move that cargo twenty years ago when I bought the claim. Men with pensions. Men with names. If the truth surfaces, it doesn't drown alone." He set the log down. "Blast the cave, Mr. Okafor. And send someone to check that riverboat captain's mooring lines. I want to know if he's going anywhere."

Okafor hesitated at the door. "And your daughter, sir?"

Something moved behind Edmund's eyes, some old weather system turning over. "My daughter," he said, "made her choice. I'm making mine."

* * *

By late afternoon the harbor had emptied of everything with sense. Fishing boats had run upriver; the harbor master had boarded his windows and posted a notice that read, in its entirety, GOOD LUCK. Jonah appreciated the honesty.

He was elbow-deep in the engine room, covered in grease to the elbows and beyond—there was grease behind his ears somehow, which defied physics—and cursing the bronze stock in a tone he'd learned from his mother, who had cursed like a poet. The fabricated coupling was ugly. It was the ugliest thing ever to bear his file marks, and he loved it the way you love a child who bites.

"Hold this," he said, and Mara held it, and they worked in the rhythm of two people who had been working together for three days and had already developed the shorthand of ten years.

"Tell me about her," Mara said suddenly. "Your mother. While we do this."

Jonah turned the wrench. "She sang off-key. On purpose, I think, eventually, because she liked watching me wince. She could read water better than any pilot on the coast—she'd stand at the bow and tell you what the bottom looked like fifty feet down, just from the surface wrinkles. Called it 'the sea showing its homework.'" He smiled despite himself. "Everyone called her a thief, and she never once defended herself. Not once. I used to think it was pride. Now I think—" the wrench stopped, "—now I think she knew defending herself would burn other people down with the lie. People who helped her. So she let it sit on her."

Mara was quiet a moment. "That sounds familiar."

"Yeah," Jonah said. "It does."

Above them, boots on the deck. Jonah froze. Mara put a finger to her lips and slid toward the companionway ladder, and Jonah picked up the largest wrench within reach, which is the universal mariner's response to unexpected footsteps.

Two figures came down the ladder. One was Okafor, whom Jonah recognized from the fleet. The other was young, nervous, holding a canvas bag.

"Captain Sterling." Okafor raised both hands, palms out, the international sign for *please don't swing that*. "I'm not here to fight. I'm here because I've got a daughter in college and a conscience, and only one of those pays tuition." He nodded at the youth, who dug into the bag and produced—a propeller shaft coupling. New. Brass-bright. Genuine.

Jonah stared at it. "Where did—"

"Stores. Fleet stores. Your original part is currently at the bottom of a tool chest on the *Iron Meridian*, wrapped in a rag, which I understand was meant as a message." Okafor's mouth twitched. "Voss has a sense of humor like a bilge pump. Here's mine: install that, get your boat running, and be gone from this harbor before dawn. Because at first light we're setting charges on that cave, and the weather's coming in behind them, and I have seen what happens to small boats that stay for Voss operations gone wrong. I've cleaned up after three of them."

"Why?" Mara said from the shadows of the ladder. "Why help us?"

Okafor looked at her, and his face did something complicated—pity and respect wrestling in a small room. "Because twenty years ago I helped move crates off a wreck that wasn't supposed to exist, and I told myself it was scrap metal, and I've been telling myself that ever since. I'm tired, Miss Voss. Tired men do honest things at strange hours." He turned back up the ladder, then paused. "One more thing. The old man ordered us to check your mooring lines. Officially it's concern for safety." His voice dropped. "Unofficially, a boat that can't leave is a boat that can't testify. Fix your boat. Leave. Or don't—but know that staying means the storm and the fleet both get a vote on whether you see sunrise."

Then they were gone, and the engine room was quiet except for the wind beginning to sing through the harbor gap, lower now, hungrier.

Jonah set the new coupling on the workbench and looked at it for a long time.

"We could run," Mara said. "Port Anselm. Ride it out. Come back after."

"After what? After the cave's rubble and the log's story dies with it?" He wiped his hands slowly. "My whole life I've run. Every port, every debt, every hard conversation—I ran. It's the family business, apparently. Mom ran from the lie, Dad ran before I could remember him, and I ran from everything else." He picked up the coupling, turned it in the lamplight. "You know what running costs? Everything, continuously, forever. Stopping costs once."

Mara studied him. Outside, the first fat drops of rain hit the deck overhead, spaced far apart, like applause before the show.

"So we stay," she said. "Through the storm. Against the fleet. To reach a flooding cave."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"It sounded insane the way I said it too."

Jonah grinned, and it felt strange on his face, like a muscle remembering an old job. "Good. Then help me install this coupling, because we're sailing into a monsoon tonight, and I'd like the boat to still be attached to its propeller when we get there."

He was reaching for the wrench when the harbor lights died—all of them, at once—and in the sudden dark, out past the breakwater, they heard the fleet's drill rig begin to turn.
