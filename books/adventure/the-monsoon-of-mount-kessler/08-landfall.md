## 8. Landfall

The monsoon came early, the way bad news always does.

Jonah felt it before he saw it—a pressure change in his ears, a taste of copper on the wind, the gulls abandoning the harbor all at once like a congregation fleeing a fire-and-brimstone sermon. The sky over Mount Kessler had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong, green-black and swollen, and the first rain came down in ropes so thick they seemed to have weight, as though the ocean had reached up and was pulling itself ashore by handfuls.

"Harbor's flooding," Mara shouted over the din. She stood at the rail of the *Kestrel's Grace*, hair plastered to her face, watching the water climb the pilings of the old fish pier. "Look at the tide gauge. That's three feet above high water mark and it's not even landfall yet."

"It landed already," Jonah said. "It just didn't send word ahead."

The harbor that had been dying for forty years was now drowning in earnest. Fishing boats strained at their moorings, hulls knocking together like nervous teeth. Water sheeted across the quay where Jonah had spent exactly one afternoon of his childhood—the last time he'd let himself stop anywhere longer than it took to take on fuel. He'd sworn off harbors after his mother drowned. Harbors were where you waited, and waiting was where hope went to die of exposure.

And now here he was, ankle-deep in one, with a sabotaged engine, a stolen captain's log wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, and a woman who should have been his enemy holding the other end of the wrench.

"We can't fix this here," he said. "Not with the fleet—"

"The fleet has bigger problems than us right now." Mara hauled the tarp tighter over the engine housing. "Edmund committed every barge and tender to the cave approach. We're a footnote."

"A footnote with a boat he wants at the bottom of the bay."

"Which is why we fix it fast." She wiped rain from her eyes with the back of her wrist. "There's a covered slip under the cannery. My father used it during the '89 storm. It'll hold until the worst passes."

"And then?"

Mara looked at him. Rain ran off her chin, and beneath all that weather there was something steady in her face that hadn't been there a week ago. Something that had chosen its heading.

"And then," she said, "we sail into it."

* * *

The slip under the cannery smelled of rust, old fish, and forty years of other people's bad decisions—which, Jonah reflected, made it the most honest room in Port Calloway. They worked by lantern light while the storm tried the roof overhead like a burglar testing windows.

The sabotage had been simple and mean: seawater in the fuel line, a sheared impeller pin, and something wedged into the intake grate that Jonah pulled out with two fingers and held up to the lantern. A length of chain, knotted tight.

"He wanted us stranded," Mara said quietly. "Not dead. Dead is quick. Stranded means you watch them win."

"That's Edmund all over." Jonah fed the new impeller pin into place. His hands knew this work the way they knew nothing else—better than navigation, better than conversation. An engine didn't lie to you. It broke or it ran, and either way it told you plainly. "He never once raised his voice at my mother's trial. Did you know that? Soft-spoken man, testifying about thieves and liars, while my mother sat there with salt-cracked hands and no lawyer worth the name."

"I've read the transcript," Mara said. "I found it when I was sixteen. I asked him about it and he said—" She stopped, jaw tightening. "He said some cargoes are worth more sunk."

Jonah set down the wrench. "That's an odd thing to say."

"It's an odd thing to raise a daughter on."

They worked in silence after that, but it was the good kind of silence, the kind two people can share without either of them checking whether the other is still there. Jonah replaced the fuel filter. Mara re-seated the intake grate and wired it double. Outside, the wind rose through registers Jonah had only heard described by men who'd survived the big storms and never quite stopped flinching at low ones.

By midnight the *Kestrel's Grace* would run. By midnight, the barometer had fallen so far the needle seemed to be trying to escape the instrument entirely.

"You know this is a suicide run," Jonah said. He said it mildly, the way you mention the weather. "Ninety minutes to the point in clear water. In this, call it four hours, if the sea doesn't fold us in half first. And even if we make it, Edmund's crew will be breaking through the cave wall with shaped charges while we're still fighting the swell."

"Yes."

"Mara. I need to hear you say the rest of it."

She sat down on the engine housing, dripping, exhausted, and laughed—a short, startled sound, as if it had escaped custody. "The rest of it is that if the charges bring the roof down, the Meridian Star is buried under ten thousand tons of rock and the log in your shirt proves nothing to anyone but us. Two people nobody believes, holding paper that says their parents were monsters and martyrs respectively." She looked up at him. "The rest of it is that I spent thirty-one years navigating by my father's charts, and last week I finally checked his math, and I can't un-know what I know. So yes. Suicide run. Except I've done the numbers, Jonah, and it isn't one. There's a window behind the headland between squall bands—my mother sailed it twice. Four hours of ugly, survivable ugly. And after that—" she gestured at the roaring dark beyond the slip, "—after that it's just a question of how much ugly a person can carry."

"I can carry quite a lot," Jonah said. "It's the stopping that scares me."

"Then don't stop." She stood. "Steer."

* * *

They came out of the cannery slip at 12:40 in the morning, into a sea that had forgotten it was ever calm.

Jonah took the wheel first. The rain hit the wheelhouse sideways with a sound like gravel thrown at glass, and the swells came out of the southeast in long black hills, and the *Kestrel's Grace* climbed each one and slid down the far side with her old timbers groaning prayers her builder had never taught her. Lightning turned the world white for half a second at a time—instant photographs of chaos, gone before the eye could file them.

"Compass is wandering," Mara called from the chart table, lashed in with a harness she'd rigged herself. "Iron in the hull plus the storm. I'm running dead reckoning off the log and the clock."

"Give me a heading and a prayer."

"You get the heading. The prayer's your department—I've seen how you talk to this engine."

He grinned into the dark. It was absurd, grinning. It was also the truest thing he'd done in years. Somewhere behind them lay a harbor full of boats smart enough to stay tied up. Somewhere ahead lay a cave that wanted to kill them and a fleet that wanted it more. And here, in the narrow wet space between, was a woman reading the storm the way his mother used to read tide charts—not fighting the water, but asking it politely where it intended to go, and going there first.

"Starboard ten," Mara said. "There's a lull behind the second band. My mother called it the seam. Ride the seam and we gain an hour."

"How do you know where the seam is?"

"Because I've been drawing charts since I was nine years old, and because—" she hesitated, and he heard her choose honesty over habit, "—because I found her logs too. In his cabin. He kept them. Forty years, and he never threw them away."

Jonah steered starboard ten. The boat leaned into the turn like a horse answering a familiar voice.

"What was her name?" he asked. "Your mother. You've never said it."

"Elena." The word came out small. "Elena Voss. She died when I was four. Diving. Off the Kessler point." A pause, long enough for a swell to pass beneath them. "My father told me it was an accident. I believed him for twenty-seven years."

"And now?"

"Now I think she found the cave too. Early. Before anyone." Mara's voice stayed level the way a deck stays level—by constant invisible correction. "I think she told him what was in the hold. And I think that's why he testified against your mother, and why he's willing to blow the mountain apart rather than let anyone else read that log. Jonah—he wasn't protecting a secret from strangers. He was protecting it from *her*. From what she'd have wanted. From the truth getting out the way she'd have let it out."

Lightning flashed. For one white instant Jonah saw the whole coast laid bare—the headland, the hidden mouth of the bay, the smudge of the salvage fleet's lights clustered at the point like flies on a wound.

"Third squall band in eleven minutes," Mara said. "After that, the seam opens for maybe twenty. Then we're in the lee of the point and it's the last mile by feel."

"By feel," Jonah repeated. "You navigators and your poetry."

"You riverboat captains and your superstition. You haven't touched the rail since we left the slip. I've watched you. You touch wood every time the lightning gets close."

"That's not superstition, that's engineering. This boat is mostly wood. I'm reminding her of our shared heritage."

Mara laughed—really laughed, once, bright and brief as the lightning—and in the dark of the wheelhouse, with the storm doing its best to erase them, Jonah understood that this was the whole of it. Not words. Not declarations. Just two people who had spent their whole lives running—one across open water, one inside a family—and had both, in the same terrible night, chosen to steer toward the same rock.

"Ten minutes," she said. "Get ready to give her everything."

"She hasn't got everything. She's got about seventy percent and a strong work ethic."

"Then we ask for seventy percent and gratitude." Mara clipped her harness to the rail beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, and stayed there. Neither of them moved away. "Whatever's in that cave," she said quietly, "we go in together or not at all. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"Not for the cargo. Not for the claim. For the log. For Elena. For your mother."

"For both of them," Jonah said. "The tide owes my family a debt. Tonight it pays."

Ahead, through the ragged curtain of rain, the headland rose out of the sea like the shoulder of something waking. And on its far side, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that was not thunder—too rhythmic, too deliberate, too human.

A hammering. Or a drilling. Or the careful, patient work of men placing explosives in the dark.

"They're already at the wall," Mara breathed.

Jonah put the wheel hard over. "Then we're late," he said, "and we arrive anyway."
