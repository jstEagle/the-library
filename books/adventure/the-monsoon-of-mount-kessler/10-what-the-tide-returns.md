## 10. What the Tide Returns

The storm left the way a drunk leaves a party—loudly, apologetically, and taking more than it should have with it.

Jonah stood on the deck of the *Pelican's Grace* three mornings later, watching the harbor of Port Calloway put itself back together. Fishing boats that had been stacked like kindling were being righted by crews of men who cursed with the easy rhythm of long practice. The chandlery had lost its roof but not its stock, which now sat under tarps in the street like a yard sale nobody had planned. A dog was swimming laps around a floating crate of oranges, deeply pleased with itself.

It was, Jonah had to admit, not the worst thing he'd ever seen.

For eleven years he'd avoided this port. He'd told himself it was because the harbor fees were extortionate, which they were, or because the coffee at the dockside café tasted like boiled regret, which it did. But the truth was simpler and he'd known it for years: Port Calloway was where his mother had been tried in absentia by gossip, where her name had been dragged behind boats until nothing was left of it but a stain. He hadn't stopped here because stopping meant being seen, and being seen meant answering questions he couldn't answer.

Now the questions had answered themselves, mostly. The captain's log of the *Meridian Star* sat wrapped in oilcloth below decks, dry as a sermon, its final pages naming Edmund Voss in a hand that shook with either fear or fury. The harbormaster had read it twice, gone pale, and made three telephone calls. By evening, the story would be everywhere. By week's end, it would be official.

His mother's name, coming home.

"You're going to wear a groove in those boards," Mara said from the cabin doorway, two mugs in her hands. She handed him one. It was the good coffee—the real stuff she'd produced from somewhere in her kit like a magician producing a dove—and Jonah drank it and felt something in his chest unclench by a degree.

"I'm thinking," he said.

"That's what worries me. You think like a man loading a gun."

He almost smiled. "Habit."

"Break it." She leaned on the rail beside him, shoulder warm against his, and looked out at the bay. The sea cave mouth was visible from here at low water, a dark seam in the cliff face, quiet now. Edmund Voss's fleet still rode at anchor beyond the point—four ships, sails furled, going nowhere. Nobody had worked them since the storm. Nobody, Jonah suspected, quite knew what to do with themselves.

Below them, the tide came in over the sandbar, unhurried, covering the place where everything had started. The sea giving back what it had taken, then taking it again. That was the arrangement. You didn't get to keep things. You only got to know them.

* * *

The salvage office occupied a narrow building near the customs house, and it smelled of ink, mildew, and forty years of men arguing about percentages. Jonah had been inside exactly once before, at nineteen, to file a claim against a sunken barge, and he'd walked out without filing it because the clerk had recognized his surname and said, "Sterling? Any relation to—" and Jonah had said no, and left.

This time he walked in carrying the claim himself: the *Meridian Star*, wreck number 4471-C, salvage rights pending, applicant Jonah Sterling.

The clerk—a different clerk, young, with the hopeful look of a man who still believed paperwork could save people—took the document and began stamping things.

"I'm withdrawing it," Jonah said.

The stamp paused mid-air. "Withdrawing?"

"Surrendering. Whatever word you use when you don't want it anymore."

The clerk looked up. Behind him, through the open window, the whole harbor seemed to lean in to listen. "Sir, the *Meridian Star*'s cargo manifest lists—well, it lists a great deal of value. Contraband aside, there's registered bullion, ship's plate, the insurance recovery alone—"

"I know what it lists," Jonah said. "I've been down there. I've held it." He set his hand flat on the counter. "What I want is this: the record corrected. My mother's name cleared in the registry, in the newspaper archive, in whatever dusty drawer you keep the truth in. The log goes to the maritime court as evidence. Everything else stays with the wreck."

The clerk blinked at him. "That's... highly irregular."

"It is," Jonah agreed. "I'm given to understand I come from a long line of irregular women."

Somewhere behind him, someone laughed—not cruelly. Word had already gotten out, the way word does in small ports, faster than telegraph and more accurate than newspapers. The daughter of the thief who wasn't a thief. The son who gave it all back.

The clerk stamped the withdrawal, then stamped the correction request, then, after a moment's visible internal struggle, stamped a third form just because it felt good. "There'll be questions," he warned. "Hearings. Men in better coats than mine will want statements."

"They can find me," Jonah said. "I'm not hard to locate these days."

And that was the strange part, standing there in the ink-smelling room: it was true. For the first time in eleven years, he was not hard to locate. He had an address. He had a berth. He had, God help him, a mooring.

* * *

Mara found him at the dockside café that afternoon, the one with the regrettable coffee, sitting at the table by the window where anyone passing could see him, which was the entire point and which he was still getting used to.

She sat down across from him without asking, which he liked, and stole half his pastry, which he liked less but had learned to accept as the tax on her company.

"The fleet's breaking up," she said.

Jonah set down his cup. "Breaking up?"

"Four ships, and no reason to stay. The cave's sealed itself again—third tide came and went, and the passage is silted shut, maybe for another decade, maybe forever. There's nothing to blast, even if anyone had the stomach for blasting after..." She turned her mug slowly on the table. "After my father."

They'd recovered what there was to recover, two days after the flood. The divers had found him at the old dive site, deep in the flooded gallery, his lamp still burning when they reached it, aimed at the spot where the *Meridian Star*'s strongroom door hung open. He'd had time, the divers said. Time to swim out. He'd chosen to sit down instead, in the dark, beside the evidence of what he'd done, and stay.

Mara had cried, Jonah knew—not for the man he'd been, but for the man she'd spent thirty years believing he might have been. She'd cried once, briefly, at the rail of the *Grace*, and then she'd gone below and copied out the relevant pages of the captain's log in her own precise navigator's hand, because grief, in her family, had always been something you did with a pen.

"There's a hearing Thursday," she said now. "You'll testify. So will I."

"I know."

"And after that?" She looked at him across the table, and Jonah saw that she was afraid—not of the hearing, but of the question underneath it. The one about what came next. Navigators hated open water with no bearing marked.

"After that," Jonah said slowly, "I thought I might take the *Grace* upriver. Run cargo. Oysters, mail, whatever pays." He watched her face. "There's a berth aboard. Two, actually. One for a navigator who's tired of working for men who lie about their coordinates."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. Outside the window, the dog from that morning trotted past with an orange in its mouth, victorious.

"You're offering me a job," she said.

"I'm offering you a chart table and first pick of the coffee."

"A terrible salary, I assume."

"Paid entirely in oysters."

She laughed—that short, surprised laugh he'd only heard a few times, always when she forgot to guard it—and then she reached across the table and took his hand, and neither of them said anything clever for a while, because some things are better left as ballast than converted to cargo.

* * *

They moored the *Pelican's Grace* at the north pier that evening, in the berth closest to the harbor light, and Jonah signed the lease for a full year with a hand that only shook a little.

Eleven years of never stopping. Eleven years of keeping moving so fast that no port could get a grip on him, no debt could find him, no memory could catch his sleeve. And now here he was, tied off, paid up, planted—watching the sunset turn the harbor into hammered copper while gulls argued overhead about jurisdiction—and the remarkable thing, the thing he kept turning over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit, was how little it cost.

Stopping cost less than running. That was the whole arithmetic of it, and it had taken him forty years and one monsoon to learn it. Running cost everything, every day, in installments. Stopping cost one signature and the willingness to be known.

Mara came up from below with the captain's log wrapped fresh in new oilcloth, ready for the court. She set it on the chart table between them, and beneath it, weighted at the corners, she spread out a chart—not his mother's tide chart, though that hung framed above the wheel now, salt-stained and sacred—but a fresh one, blank along the river's course except for her careful pencil marks.

"Depths," she said. "Snags. Three good anchorages and one terrible one that I've marked anyway because you'll ignore me and need it eventually." She tapped the pencil against the paper. "I've never drawn a chart for a boat I intended to stay on."

"No?"

"No." She looked up at him. "It's a first."

Jonah took the pencil from her hand and added one mark of his own: a small X at the north pier, Port Calloway, with the date beside it. Home port. The words felt foreign in his handwriting and correct anyway.

Above them, the harbor light came on, sweeping its slow white arm across the water, and out past the point the sea cave breathed in and out with the tide, holding its dead and its secrets and, somewhere in the dark, the last of the bullion that would wait for whoever came next.

Jonah found he didn't mind waiting. He found, in fact, that he was very good at it.

"Thursday," Mara said quietly. "We tell them the truth."

"We tell them the truth," Jonah agreed, and poured the coffee, and stayed.
