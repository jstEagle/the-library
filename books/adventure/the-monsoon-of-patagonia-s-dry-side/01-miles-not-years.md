## 1. Miles, Not Years

The wind hit eighty kilometers an hour at 3:47 in the afternoon, and Dominic Ishikawa wrote it down the way other men said their prayers.

*14:47 — gusts to 80 km/h from the WSW. Cell building over the meseta, rotation visible at base. Barometer falling like my credit rating.*

He was lying flat on the roof of his truck with the anemometer clamped to the rack above him, one boot hooked through the cargo bars, notebook braced against his knee. Below him, the Patagonian steppe ran out in every direction like a table nobody had finished setting — yellow grass, grey stone, a road that was less a road than a rumor of one. The sky overhead had gone the color of a bruise healing badly, green-black at the core where the storm cell was stacking itself up against the Andes.

"Come on," he murmured to it. "Be beautiful. Be *stupidly* dangerous. I need the data."

The truck shuddered under him. He'd named her *Constanza*, which had been a mistake, because now he talked to her and she ignored him exactly like everyone else he'd ever loved.

His phone buzzed against his chest. He didn't look. He knew who it was.

It buzzed again. Then again — three short pulses, patient as a metronome. The Voss brothers had a rhythm to their calling. Henrik called once and waited, because Henrik believed in letting a man sweat productively. Lukas called twice more, because Lukas believed in joy, and Dominic had learned over eight months that Lukas's joy was the most frightening thing on two continents.

On the fourth buzz, Dominic gave up, unhooked his boot, and slid down into the cab, where the air smelled of dust, old coffee, and the faint electrical tang of a storm that hadn't decided whether to kill him yet.

He answered without checking the screen. "You've reached Ishikawa Storm Data. We're currently experiencing high call volumes."

"Dominic!" Lukas's voice came through warm as fresh bread. "There he is. There's our guy. How's the weather?"

"Lovely. Terrible. Perfect."

"You sound happy." A pause, and then, gently, the way a surgeon says *this might sting*: "That worries me. Happy men don't answer their phones."

Dominic watched the cell through the windshield. It was doing something gorgeous at its leading edge — a shelf cloud rolling out like a wave deciding whether to break. He itched to be back on the roof with his instruments instead of here, performing calm for a man whose family business was, as far as Dominic could tell, lending money to people who would never be able to pay it back and then being *wonderful* about it.

"I'm working, Lukas."

"I know you're working. Henrik saw your invoice. That's actually why I'm calling." Paper rustled somewhere in Punta Arenas or Puerto Natales or wherever the Voss brothers kept their cheerful little office. "We love the invoices, Dominic. We frame them, practically. But we've noticed something interesting about them."

"They're itemized?"

"They're *small*." Lukas said it with genuine sorrow, like a father reporting that his son had been caught being mediocre. "Small invoices mean small jobs. Small jobs mean slow repayment. And you know what Henrik always says."

"Henrik says a lot of things."

"Henrik says: a debt is a seed. You water it with interest, but it only grows if the debtor keeps breathing comfortably." A cheerful sigh. "So we'd hate for anything to interfere with your breathing."

Through the windshield, the shelf cloud broke. Rain came across the steppe in a grey curtain, hammering the roof of the cab, and Dominic felt the old familiar lift in his chest — the one that had nothing to do with money and everything to do with standing next to something enormous and alive and not blinking first.

"Lukas, I'm going to have to call you back. Weather."

"Weather," Lukas repeated fondly. "Of course. Chase it well, Dominic. And hey — dinner soon? Henrik wants to cook. He got his hands on a Malbec that would make a grown man weep."

"I'll think about it."

"Don't think too long." The line clicked off, warm all the way down to the silence.

Dominic sat in the rain-noise for a moment, then flipped open his logbook and added a line under the wind speeds: *Debt outstanding: enough. Mood: excellent, inexplicably.*

Then he climbed back onto the roof, because the hail was starting, and hail meant the good numbers.

* * *

Three years ago, outside Calgary, a man at a gas station had asked Dominic where he was from, and Dominic had opened his mouth and discovered there was nothing there.

Not that he'd forgotten. He remembered Tokyo, where he was born; he remembered the apartment in Vancouver until he was nine; he remembered his mother's voice going quiet on long-distance calls until the calls stopped; he remembered the university in Colorado, the first job in Oklahoma, the second job everywhere. He had a decade of addresses folded into the back of his passport like pressed flowers. What he didn't have was an answer. *Where are you from* wanted a place, and places were things you left, and after ten years of leaving he'd gotten so fast at it that he'd left the question behind too.

He thought about that man sometimes. Gas station, Calgary, buying bad coffee at six in the morning while a supercell stacked up over the prairies like God stacking dishes. The man had a dog in his truck, a big dumb golden thing with its head out the window even though the truck wasn't moving. Dominic had looked at that dog and felt something crack slightly in his chest, and then he'd paid for his coffee and driven toward the storm, because that was the whole trick of his life: whenever anything cracked, he drove toward the weather, because weather never asked where he was from.

Weather just wanted to know if you could read it.

And he could. That was the one clean thing about him. Give Dominic Ishikawa a barometer and a horizon and he'd tell you what the sky intended before the sky knew itself. He'd chased through Tornado Alley and the Pampas and the Queensland outback and the flats of eastern Montana, logging winds and pressures and hail diameters for insurance companies, research outfits, anyone with a budget and a need. He was good at it in the way that made people say *how are you this calm* and he never told them the truth, which was that calm was the only thing he owned outright.

Everything else had liens on it.

* * *

By evening the cell had spent itself against the foothills and the steppe had gone gold and dripping, every blade of grass lit up like it was showing off. Dominic camped in the lee of a rock outcrop, ate cold noodles from the pot, and did the arithmetic he did every night, because misery loves routine.

Truck payment: overdue by eleven days. Insurance on the instruments: lapsed last month, which meant if Constanza rolled in a gust he was a man with a notebook and opinions. Interest accruing to the Voss brothers: compounding daily, cheerfully, like mold in a warm room. Total time until they took the truck, the gear, and — per Lukas's light-hearted mention of kneecaps during their last meeting, which Dominic had chosen to hear as a joke the way you choose to hear a smoke alarm as ambient music — possibly other things: weeks. Maybe two months if he landed a contract.

Two months. He looked at the fire, small and stubborn in the wind shadow of the rocks.

Ten years ago he'd have already been gone by now. Ten years ago, when a situation got heavy, Dominic simply evaporated and reformed somewhere with different weather. He'd left a girlfriend in Norman, Oklahoma, with a note that said *gone chasing, back soon,* and she'd married someone else within the year, and he'd sent a card, and that was the last time leaving had cost him anything he could still feel. Since then it had gotten easier and easier, which should have worried him more than the debt did.

The phone buzzed. Henrik this time — one buzz, patient, a man who let silence do his talking.

Dominic stared at it. Then, because the alternative was staring at the fire and doing the arithmetic again, he answered.

"Henrik."

"Dominic." Henrik's voice was lower than Lukas's, slower, the voice of a man reading a bedtime story to adults. "Lukas tells me you were chasing today."

"He tells you everything?"

"He tells me the parts that matter. He says you sounded happy." A pause, long enough that Dominic could hear ice shifting in a glass. "I find that interesting, because a man in your position shouldn't be happy. A man in your position should be hungry. Hungry men take work. Happy men take pictures of clouds."

"The pictures pay, Henrik."

"Barely." No anger in it. That was the terrible thing about Henrik — no anger anywhere, ever, just an enormous patience, like a glacier with a phone plan. "Dominic, I'm going to say something, and I want you to listen to it the way you listen to your sky. We like you. That is not a small thing. My brother likes you more than he likes most people, and my brother likes everyone, so understand the scale. We do not want your truck. Trucks are a nuisance. We do not want your knees. Knees are a metaphor, mostly. What we want is for you to stop running small jobs like a man planning his escape, and start thinking like a man planning his future."

"I'm listening to the sky right now, actually."

"Good," Henrik said, as if Dominic had agreed to something. "Dinner. Friday. Lukas is cooking. Wear something without hail dents in it."

The line went dead before Dominic could construct a refusal, which he suspected was the point. Henrik Voss ended conversations the way rivers ended valleys: gradually, then completely.

Dominic sat back against the rock. Above him the sky had scrubbed itself clean, and the stars came out the way they only did down here — dense, indifferent, southern, wrong-constellated. Somewhere out in the dark a fox screamed once and thought better of it.

*Friday,* he thought. Dinner with the optimists. Wine, warmth, laughter, and somewhere under all of it, laid out as neatly as cutlery, the hook.

He should run. That was the old move. Point Constanza north or south, cross a border, change the SIM card, start over in some new country with a new set of creditors who didn't know his face. He'd done it twice before and slept fine both times.

But the arithmetic followed him now. It lived in the truck. And besides — and this was the part he examined least, the way you don't probe a tooth — Henrik had said *work.* Not *payment.* Work. An actual job, offered by men who always paid on time, in a region where the season was turning and the passes would close soon and every salvage outfit on the coast was scrambling for crews.

A man planning his future. Dominic turned the phrase over. He hadn't planned a future since roughly the Obama administration. Futures were for people with addresses.

He wrote the day's final entry by headlamp, the page fluttering in the last of the wind:

*21:30 — skies clearing, pressure rising. Fox observed, cowardly. Debt unchanged. Dinner Friday. Note to self: eat before drinking their wine.*

He paused, pen hovering, then added:

*Note to self, addendum: figure out why the idea of saying no feels worse than the idea of saying yes.*

Out on the dark steppe, the wind changed direction, soft and warm off the mountains, carrying the smell of wet grass and distance. Dominic lay back on the tailgate, hands behind his head, and let it push at him like a hand on his shoulder — the same hand that had pushed him across four countries and ten years, patient as Henrik, cheerful as Lukas, asking nothing except that he keep moving.

For the first time he could remember, he considered pushing back.

Then his phone lit up with a message from Lukas — just a photograph of a bottle of Malbec and a single exclamation point, joyful as a landmine — and Dominic laughed out loud, alone under the wrong stars, and understood that Friday had already happened to him. He just hadn't arrived yet.
