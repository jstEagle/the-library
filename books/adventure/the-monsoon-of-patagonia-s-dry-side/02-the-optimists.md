## 2. The Optimists

The invitation arrived the way all their invitations arrived: hand-delivered, on cream cardstock, with a wax seal that cost more than Dominic's monthly fuel budget.

*Mr. Ishikawa — Dinner, Friday, seven o'clock. Dress is whatever you own. — E.V.*

He read it three times in the cab of his truck, parked outside a laundromat in Puerto Deseado where he was washing everything he owned at once because it was cheaper than two loads. The seal was burgundy. The paper smelled faintly of cedar. Somewhere, Elias Voss had chosen this stationery personally, and that was the part that made Dominic's stomach drop—not the debt itself, which had long ago stopped feeling like a number and started feeling like weather, but the fact that men like the Vosses *cared*. They paid attention. They remembered your mother's name and asked after your sinuses.

The cheerful ones were always worse.

* * *

The restaurant was the kind of place where the menu didn't have prices, which meant the prices were a formality for people who had never once considered them. Dominic arrived at 6:55 in his least-stained shirt and found both brothers already seated at a corner table with a view of the harbor, a bottle open, decanting.

"Dominic!" Erik Voss rose first—the elder by four years, broader, grayer at the temples, with the easy physicality of a man who rowed before breakfast and enjoyed telling you so. He shook Dominic's hand with both of his, warm and dry. "You look thin. Are you eating? Sit, sit."

"I eat," Dominic said.

"He eats," Elias confirmed from his chair. The younger brother hadn't stood. He never seemed to need to; the room arranged itself around him instead. He was slighter than Erik, quicker in the eyes, wearing a sweater that probably cost as much as Dominic's winch. "We checked. There's a place on Avenida San Martín that does a choripán for forty pesos and he's been there eleven times this month. We know because we know."

"That's not creepy at all," Dominic said, sitting.

"It's *care*," Erik said, pouring wine into Dominic's glass without asking. "There's a difference. Creepy is when nobody loves you enough to watch." He raised his glass. "To being watched."

They clinked. The wine was a Malbec from a valley Dominic couldn't have pronounced sober, let alone at whatever altitude of alcohol this bottle operated at. It was, annoyingly, magnificent.

For the first forty minutes they talked about nothing. Erik told a story about a horse that had opinions about bridges. Elias corrected three details of the story and improved two others until neither version could possibly have happened, and both brothers laughed so hard at their own revisionism that the waiter came over twice to check whether something was wrong. Dominic laughed too—he always did; it was reflexive, like flinching—and hated himself a little for how much he meant it.

This was the thing about the Voss brothers that no one believed until they'd sat across a table from them: they were genuinely pleasant. Not performatively pleasant, not smiling-with-cold-eyes pleasant. They liked people. They liked *him*, or they performed liking him so well that the distinction had dissolved somewhere around the second glass. And underneath all that warmth ran the simple arithmetic of what they were, which was patient men who owned things, and one of the things they owned, currently, was him.

"So," Erik said, when the plates had been cleared and the second bottle opened. "How's the truck?"

"She's fine."

"Is she? The transmission's been making a noise. Like a—Elias, what did we decide it sounded like?"

"A cat in a suitcase," Elias said.

"A cat in a suitcase," Erik agreed. "We heard it when you fueled up in Comodoro last Tuesday. You should get that looked at. Transmissions are expensive."

Dominic set down his glass. "Why are we talking about my transmission?"

"Because we're about to talk about your kneecaps," Elias said pleasantly, "and I wanted to establish first that we're coming at this from a place of affection."

Erik made a small chiding sound. "Elias. Manners." He turned back to Dominic, apologetic, avuncular, terrible. "What my brother means is that we've been reviewing your account. And we thought, rather than doing anything unpleasant, we'd offer you an opportunity. A job. Salvage work—which is, conveniently, what you do."

"I chase storms."

"You chase storms *badly*, financially speaking, which is why you owe us money. But you also do salvage. We've seen the invoices. River recoveries, wreck assessments, that business with the ferry up at Lago—" he waved a hand, "—the lake. You're good at finding things underwater. It turns out we need something found underwater."

Dominic waited. With the Vosses, waiting was free; talking cost extra.

Elias leaned forward, and for the first time all evening the performance thinned just slightly, enough to see the machinery behind it. "Upstream. On the Río Chico, past the gorge, into country nobody maps because nobody bothers. There's a site. Old wreck—or old something. Our information says low water this season has exposed more than anyone expected. The dry side's driest in sixty years, they say. Rivers giving up secrets." He smiled. "We want whatever's down there recovered, catalogued, and delivered to us before the season turns. You'd have thirty days, maybe less if the forecasts are honest, and your forecasts are the only ones we trust."

"What's down there?"

"If we knew that," Erik said warmly, "we wouldn't need you."

It was a good answer. It was also, Dominic noted, not an answer at all.

"And in exchange?" Dominic said. "Because I'm guessing this isn't charity."

"In exchange," Elias said, "we wipe the principal. All of it. Every peso, every point of interest, every one of those late fees we invented that one winter because we missed you." He spread his hands, magnanimous. "Clean slate. You walk away owing us nothing."

Dominic felt the trap close around him so gently he almost thanked it. That was the artistry of these two—they never sprang anything. They built rooms without doors and then acted delighted when you noticed.

"And if I say no?"

Erik refilled his glass. "Then we take the truck, the gear, and—as my brother mentioned—we take a sincere interest in your knees. Which I want to stress we would hate. Genuinely. I'd lose sleep. I have a bad shoulder myself, so I'm sympathetic to joint issues across the board." He shook his head sadly. "But business is business, and you're eleven weeks past due, Dominic. Eleven. We've carried you because we like you. But even affection has a fiscal year."

The waiter came and went. Out the window, the harbor lights came on one by one, and somewhere out on the steppe the wind was doing what it always did, and Dominic thought about the strange shape of his life: ten years of moving, chasing weather across two hemispheres, logging wind speeds in notebooks he kept because paper didn't crash, sleeping in the truck in forty-one towns he could name but none he could call home. Ten years since he'd had a door with his name on it. The debt wasn't the reason he never stopped moving. But it was the excuse, and excuses, he'd learned, were load-bearing.

"What exactly am I salvaging?" he asked. "Size? Weight? Hazardous materials? Because if someone wants me hauling drums of something off a riverbed—"

"We don't know," Elias repeated, patiently, as if to a child. "That's the entire job. Find it. Bring it to us. Don't open it, don't photograph it, don't tell anyone about it. Especially don't tell anyone about it." He smiled again, and this time the smile had edges. "Discretion is the largest single line item in your compensation."

"How much crew can I hire?"

"Whoever you need. Within reason. We'll fund it through our man in Deseado—you remember Señor Peña? Lovely family. Terrible poker face." Erik produced, from inside his jacket, a folded document. Cream paper. Cedar smell. "Standard terms. Read it if you like. Most people don't."

Dominic read it. It took four minutes. It was, structurally, a masterpiece: every clause that protected him was written in plain language, warm and generous, and every clause that gutted him was written in a legal dialect so dense it might have been a dead language, which, given the subject matter, felt thematic. Indemnification. Salvage rights assignment. A confidentiality provision whose penalty figure had clearly been chosen by someone who found large numbers funny.

"There's a clause here about 'all items of historical interest,'" Dominic said. "'Including but not limited to documents, seals, insignia, and instruments of state.'" He looked up. "Instruments of state?"

"Boilerplate," Erik said, waving.

"It's very specific boilerplate."

"Erik writes all our boilerplate himself," Elias said. "He's proud of it. Let the man have this."

Dominic looked at the pen Erik was holding out. It was a nice pen. Of course it was a nice pen. Everything about these two was nice, right down to the ruin they were offering him, gift-wrapped, with a bow made of his own signature.

Here was the thing nobody tells you about drowning: it doesn't feel like panic. It feels like tiredness. It feels like doing the math one more time and getting the same number, and then taking the deal, any deal, because the water is warm and the effort of swimming has started to seem like a personality flaw.

He thought about saying no. He genuinely did. He composed the sentence in his head—*I'll find another way*—and examined it honestly, and found nothing behind it. No plan B. No savings. No address to go home to, because home had been a word other people used for a decade now. The Vosses hadn't backed him into a corner. They'd just walked into the corner where he already lived and offered to redecorate.

"One condition," Dominic said.

Both brothers raised their eyebrows in identical, delighted unison. It was almost sweet. It was almost certainly rehearsed.

"Name it," Erik said.

"My crew answers to me. Whatever we find, however it goes—if it goes sideways, I make the calls on that river. You get the cargo and the silence. I get command."

Elias glanced at Erik. Something passed between them, quick and private, some silent audit of whether this mattered. Apparently it didn't, or apparently letting him believe it did was worth more.

"Done," Erik said. "Command of the river. We only ask for command of everything else, which, frankly, is the standard arrangement everywhere in the world." He slid the pen across. "Sign, Dominic. And then order dessert, because the flan here is genuinely life-changing and I refuse to let you sign away your knees on an empty stomach."

Dominic signed. His handwriting looked smaller than usual, which he decided not to read into.

The flan, infuriatingly, was life-changing.

* * *

He walked back to the truck through streets gone soft with harbor fog, and the night air smelled of salt and diesel and, faintly, of rain that hadn't fallen yet. His phone buzzed against his hip. A weather alert—the kind he'd signed up for years ago and never turned off, the way you keep a scar. *Low-pressure system forming over the cordillera. Unseasonal. Monitor.*

Unseasonal. In a drought year. In the driest season in sixty years.

Dominic stood by his truck—the truck with its cat-in-a-suitcase transmission, the truck the Vosses could describe the noises of better than he could—and looked north, toward the river, toward the gorge, toward whatever was waiting up there under the dropping water with a seal on it that a government archivist would fly across the country to bury.

He got out his logbook, out of habit, and wrote the date, and under it, because the page needed something:

*Day 3,652. Signed something tonight. Wind calm. Shouldn't have.*

Then he closed the book, got in, and drove toward a crew he didn't have yet, for a job he already wished he'd never taken, in country where the rivers were starting to remember things out loud.
