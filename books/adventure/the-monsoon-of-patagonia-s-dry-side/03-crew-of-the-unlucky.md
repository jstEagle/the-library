## 3. Crew of the Unlucky

The Voss brothers gave Dominic three weeks and a laminated card with a phone number on it, which was a joke, because everyone in Puerto Deseado knew everyone else's number, and the number on the card was Ilsa Rehn's, and nobody needed a card to find Ilsa Rehn. She found you.

So he had three weeks to assemble a crew for a salvage job he knew almost nothing about, up a river he'd never run, with equipment he'd have to beg, borrow, or sign away his remaining organs for.

He started, as all desperate men do, at the harbor bar.

* * *

Marisol Quenel was playing dominoes with three retired fishermen who were letting her win because they were afraid of her, and she was losing anyway, because she was drunk enough to be honest and honest enough to be furious.

"Ishikawa." She didn't look up from her tiles. "I can smell a Voss contract from across a room. You've got the stink all over you."

"Good nose."

"It's the cologne they wear. Money and menace." She finally looked at him. She had her grandmother's cheekbones and her grandfather's temper, and both were famous along the whole length of the Río Chico. "Whatever they've got you hauling, the answer is no."

"You don't even know what it is."

"Don't need to. Last man who took Voss money upriver came back with half a boat and no partner. The one before that didn't come back." She flipped a tile with unnecessary violence. "I'm done with rivers that eat people."

"You're done with rivers," said one of the fishermen gently, "but the river isn't done with you, niña. You sleep with your hand on the tiller of your chair."

"Shut up, Ernesto."

Dominic sat down, ordered two of whatever she was having, and pushed one across. "It's a wreck salvage. Low water season. Something old, they think. They want it before the rains come back."

"Everything old up there is either a sheep skeleton or a lie." But she was looking at the drink now instead of him, and her voice had changed key. "Where exactly?"

"Upper reaches. Past the old mission bend."

The bar went quiet in the particular way bars go quiet when someone has said something they shouldn't. Ernesto gathered his tiles. Marisol set down her glass very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"My grandmother sang about the mission bend," she said. "Songs about a city under the water. Twice under, she used to say. Went under twice." She laughed, short and hard. "There's no city up there. There's nothing up there but wind and my family's bad decisions."

"Then it'll be an easy job," Dominic said. "Boring, even. You pilot, I pay, we all get rich enough to be miserable somewhere warmer."

She studied him for a long moment. He'd been studied by better people—customs officers, creditors, an ex-wife—but Marisol Quenel had the most thorough eyes of the lot. She was checking whether he was a liar, and finding yes, and deciding that his lies were at least the ordinary kind.

"One condition," she said. "Nobody touches my wheel. Not you, not the Vosses' people, not God himself if he shows up with paperwork."

"Deal."

"And I keep my own log. If this goes sideways—and it will—I want a record of whose fault it was."

"That's two conditions."

"That's one condition stated twice, because you weren't listening the first time." She stood, drained her glass, and held out her hand. Her grip could crack walnuts. "Three days. I'll meet your boat sober, mostly. Don't make me regret this, Ishikawa."

He walked out into the cold harbor light feeling, for the first time in weeks, like a man with a plan instead of a man with a deadline. Then he remembered he still needed a drone operator, an archivist, and approximately everything else, and the feeling passed.

* * *

Teo found him, which was somehow worse.

Dominic was in the hardware store pricing rope when a voice at his elbow said, "You're the storm guy," with the reverence other teenagers reserved for footballers.

"I'm the debt guy," Dominic said. "Storms are a hobby."

"But you chase them. My cousin saw you on the steppe last March, standing next to your truck filming a cell wall like it was a movie star. That's so cool. That's the coolest thing anyone does in this entire province, and I'm including the guy who wrestles the feral boar behind the cannery." The kid fell into step beside him. He was maybe sixteen, all elbows and enthusiasm, with a drone case slung across his back like a sword. "Teo Fuentes. I fly drones. Well—fly is a small word. I *operate* them. I've got thermal imaging, I've got a long-range relay I built myself out of parts from three broken radios and a microwave, don't ask how, and I once tracked a lost flock of sheep for eleven hours through fog and found every single one except the one that had clearly been eaten, which wasn't my fault."

"Congratulations."

"So are you hiring? For the river thing? Because everyone's talking about the river thing. Marisol Quenel signed onto a Voss job, which means either the world is ending or the money's good, and honestly both are exciting."

Dominic looked down at him. The kid's eyes were enormous and guileless and completely without survival instinct. "How old are you?"

"Seventeen. Almost eighteen. Legally I can't tell you which is closer."

"That's not how age works."

"That's not how *legally* works, actually." Teo grinned. "Look, I know what you're thinking. Kid, liability, mother with opinions. But my mother works the night shift at the cannery and my older brother already left for Buenos Aires, and if I don't get off this dock soon I'm going to end up welding fish cans until my hands fall off. I'm good, Ishikawa. I'm really good. Give me five minutes of footage time and I'll prove it."

Against every instinct honed by ten years of bad decisions, Dominic heard himself say, "Five minutes."

They went out to the truck lot. Teo unfolded his drone with the tenderness of a surgeon and launched it into a wind that would have grounded anything with less love in its wiring. For four minutes the kid did things with the controls that made the machine seem less like a tool than a bird that had agreed to help out. He threaded between two trucks at speed, orbited a light pole while reading its serial number from forty meters, and then hovered the camera directly in front of Dominic's face, whereupon the speaker crackled:

"See? Told you. Also your left tire's low. And you should really get that rust on the chassis looked at, that's a structural—" 

"Okay. Okay." Dominic waved the drone down. "You talk too much."

"Everyone says that."

"They're right. But you fly well." He hated himself as he said it. "What do you know about rivers?"

"Nothing! Can't wait to learn."

* * *

Dr. Anneli Brandt was the hardest to find and the easiest to convince, which should have told Dominic something, and eventually did.

She worked out of a rented room above the pharmacy, surrounded by filing cabinets that hummed like contented bees. She was fifty-ish, precise, gray-blond hair cut like she'd done it herself with a bowl and good intentions, and when Dominic explained the job—a salvage upriver, needing someone to document and catalog whatever they raised—she said "yes" before he'd finished the sentence.

"That's it?" he asked. "No negotiation?"

"I've been waiting for someone to ask." She was already pulling a case from under her desk. Conservation gloves, acid-free sleeves, a field kit that looked military-grade. "What's the budget?"

"The budget is whatever the Voss brothers feel like giving us, minus their profit margin, minus my debt, which is substantial."

"Mm." She wrote something in a notebook. "And my funding?"

"Your funding?"

"For the expedition. Documentation costs, materials, my fee." She looked up, and her eyes behind the sensible glasses were perfectly calm. "I assume the Vosses are covering it. They cover everything, don't they? That's rather their whole approach."

"I... haven't discussed it yet."

"Then consider it discussed. I'll invoice them directly." She snapped the case shut. "When do we leave?"

Later—much later, on the river, in the dark—he would replay this conversation and understand exactly how much he'd missed. But that evening, standing in a room full of humming cabinets, all he felt was relief. An archivist who arranged her own funding. A dream employee.

One question nagged at him on the stairs down. "Dr. Brandt. Why does a government archivist want to run rapids with a bankrupt storm chaser and a teenager?"

She smiled. It was a nice smile. It reached all the way to her glasses and stopped precisely there.

"Because documents are only half the work, Mr. Ishikawa," she said. "The other half is being there when they're found. Opportunities like that come once in a career. Usually never." She opened the door for him, politely, and closed it just as politely, and Dominic stood on the landing feeling like a man who'd been interviewed for a job he thought he was offering.

* * *

They loaded the boat on a morning so cold the harbor ice creaked like old floorboards. Marisol arrived first, inspected every inch of the vessel, insulted three specific welds, and took the helm without asking. Teo arrived second, breathless, with more equipment than a person his size should be able to carry, and began narrating the loading process aloud for reasons no one could determine. Anneli arrived last, exactly on time, with two locked cases she did not explain and did not offer to explain.

Dominic stood on the dock with his manifest and watched his crew become a crew, or at least the rough sketch of one.

Marisol ran the checks like a woman defusing a bomb she personally built. Teo strapped his drones down and kept talking—"and if we see any condors I need thirty seconds of footage, thirty seconds, that's all I ask"—while Anneli stood at the rail, watching the upper river with an expression Dominic couldn't read. Not excitement. Something closer to recognition, like a woman returning to a house she'd only ever seen in photographs.

"We're going to die," Marisol announced cheerfully, casting off. "All of us. Probably slowly."

"Statistically unlikely," said Anneli.

"Statistically, sure. But the river doesn't read statistics. The river reads debts." She glanced back at Dominic with something that was almost a smile. "And we're all carrying plenty."

Teo laughed. Anneli didn't, quite, but her shoulders dropped a centimeter, which from her was practically a hug. And Dominic leaned against the rail and felt the strange, dangerous warmth of it—the wary, sideways camaraderie of four people who each had a reason to be somewhere else and had chosen, for now, to be here.

He told himself the tightness in his chest was just the cold.

Upstream, past the mission bend, the river ran low and quiet over country that no map admitted to remembering, and whatever waited there had already begun, in its patient way, waiting for them too.
