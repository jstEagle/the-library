## 4. Where the River Remembers

The river had a smell, and Marisol's body knew it before her mind agreed to.

It came through the open wheelhouse window at dawn—cold silt, wet stone, a green thread of willow—and for three full seconds she was nine years old again, standing on her grandmother's porch with a chipped mug of mate, watching mist lift off the water like the river exhaling after a long night's work.

Then Teo dropped a drone battery on the deck plates behind her and said, "Do you think fish get bored?" and she was thirty-four again, disgraced, underpaid, and piloting a salvage barge upriver for people she'd known less than a week.

"Bored," she repeated, easing the throttle forward. "Fish."

"Think about it. They live in the same water their whole lives. Same rocks. Same view." Teo wedged himself into the navigator's chair sideways, the way teenagers sit in chairs, as if chairs were a suggestion. "No movies. No books. Just swimming."

"They sleep," Marisol said. "That's their hobby."

"Sleeping isn't a hobby."

"It is when you do it eight hours a day and call it a lifestyle."

Teo considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, which was either endearing or exhausting depending on how much coffee she'd had. She'd had none. The barge's little generator had died overnight again, so there'd been no hot water, and Dominic Ishikawa had emerged from his bunk looking like a man who'd lost an argument in his dreams, which, knowing him, he probably had—with a bank, or a brother, or the ghost of whoever he used to be ten years ago. He drank his coffee cold without complaint, which told Marisol more about his finances than any credit report could have.

She brought the barge around the first bend, and the country opened up.

* * *

This was the part she hadn't prepared for: that it would still be beautiful.

She'd left this valley at nineteen with a duffel bag and a grudge, certain she'd never come back, and she'd built a whole life on that certainty—a life of other rivers, other towns, a license earned and then unearned, a name spoken carefully by other pilots in other bars. She had assumed that coming back would feel like failure. Instead it felt like putting on a coat she'd outgrown and discovering it still fit in all the places that mattered.

The valley ran wide here, terraced with old orchards gone feral, apple trees growing in rows nobody tended anymore, still fruiting out of pure stubbornness. Above them, the slopes rose gold and dry, stitched with ñire scrub, and above those the mountains stood in their blue distance like patient creditors.

"Those are wild apples?" Anneli Brandt stood at the rail with her binoculars, her voice carrying its usual archival mildness, as if she were cataloguing rather than marveling. "Someone planted these deliberately. Rows. Look at the spacing."

"Old homesteads," Marisol said. "There used to be more people up this way."

"What happened to them?"

Marisol adjusted the throttle. "Moved on," she said, which was true, and also not the answer, and Anneli looked at her for one second too long with those pale, patient eyes before writing something in her notebook.

Dominic came up from the deck, coiling rope with the automatic competence of a man who couldn't stand seeing gear untidy. "How far to the site?"

"Two days if the channel holds. Three if it doesn't." She nodded upstream, where the river narrowed between ridges. "Low water year. The charts are twenty years old and optimistic even then."

"You grew up here?"

"Downstream. Small town. You wouldn't have heard of it."

"Try me."

"Rincón de los Sauces."

He shook his head. "See? Told you." Then, because he was incapable of leaving anything alone: "Your family still there?"

"My grandmother is buried there. My mother sells pies to tourists who take the wrong road." She kept her eyes on the channel. "My grandfather ran cargo on this river until the river stopped being worth running."

"That why you took this job?"

"No. I took this job because you're paying me, and because no other pilot would." She glanced at him. "You should be flattered. Desperation is very flattering."

Something moved across his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if it could afford the rent. "Noted."

* * *

They made camp that first night on a gravel bar below an abandoned ferry landing, its pilings leaning like old men gossiping. Marisol cooked trout over a driftwood fire because Teo had caught four of them with the boundless, infuriating luck of the young, and because her hands needed something to do that wasn't trembling.

Because here was the thing she hadn't told anyone, the thing she'd been carrying since dawn:

Her grandmother used to sing songs about this stretch of river.

Not about it exactly—the songs were stranger than that. They were lullabies mostly, sung in a dialect Marisol had never heard anywhere else, a soft rolling speech her grandmother called *la lengua del río*, the river's tongue, and which everyone else in town called *cosas de viejas*, old women's nonsense. The songs were about a city. A city with white towers and a market square where they sold honey wine, a city with two suns on its flag, a city that—

Marisol poked the fire harder than necessary.

—that went under the water twice.

That was the line. It had a tune you couldn't forget, the way a fever has a temperature. *La ciudad que se hundió dos veces.* The city that drowned twice. As a child she'd asked her grandmother what it meant, and her grandmother had smiled in that way old women smile when they know exactly what they're withholding, and said, "Once with rain, mija. Once with paper."

Once with rain. Once with paper.

Marisol had grown up and filed it away with the rest of the nonsense, the charms against hail, the insistence that you must never whistle on the water because whistling calls the wind by name. And now she was piloting a salvage barge up this exact river, hired by men in debt to find a wreck that wasn't on any chart, carrying an archivist whose funding nobody could explain, and every kilometer upstream the songs got louder in her head.

"Mari." Teo crouched beside her at the fire, holding out his plate. "More trout?"

"You've had three."

"I'm growing. It's expensive." He sat, and for once didn't talk, just ate and watched the fire, and she was briefly, absurdly fond of him. "Can I ask you something weird?"

"You're going to regardless."

"The guy who hired us. Dominic. Is he okay? Like—" Teo waved his fork vaguely. "He checks his phone like it owes him money. Which, I guess maybe it does? But he does it like it might bite him."

"He owes the Voss brothers," Marisol said. "Everyone in this business owes somebody. At least he's honest about it."

"Are the Voss brothers bad?"

"They're cheerful," Marisol said. "Which is worse."

Across the fire, Anneli sat apart with a lantern and a folder of photocopied documents, reading with the focused calm of someone who already knew how the book ended. Marisol watched her over the flames and thought: *she knows something*. Not guessed. Knew. The woman had joined a salvage crew of nobodies with a budget that covered satellite time and a medical kit better than most clinics, and she asked questions like a person checking answers she already possessed.

And Dominic—Dominic watched Anneli right back, in the sidelong way of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

Well. Everyone aboard had something folded up inside them. That was the nature of crews like this. Marisol had hers; it sang in a dead language about a drowned city, and it was getting louder with every bend of the river.

* * *

The second day, the river turned strange.

It happened gradually, the way rivers do things—first the banks steepened, then the water deepened and slowed as though thinking hard about something, then the color changed. Not the milky glacier green of the upper reaches. A darker blue, almost indigo, cold in a way that felt architectural, as if the river were flowing over something that remembered being deep.

Marisol cut the engine to listen, an old habit, and in the sudden quiet Teo said, "Whoa," which was, she had to admit, the correct word.

The walls of the valley stepped down in terraces—too regular for geology, too worn for anything recent. Stone edges showed beneath the grass like bones under skin. And there, on the near bank, half-swallowed by a willow thicket: a staircase. Wide, shallow steps descending straight into the water, disappearing at the third step, continuing—who knew how far—below.

"Is that a dock?" Dominic asked, coming forward.

"That's a *landing*," Marisol said quietly. "You don't build stairs that wide for rowboats. That's for loading cargo. Barges. A lot of barges." Her voice sounded odd to her own ears, thin. "Nobody loads cargo here. There's nothing here."

"There was," Anneli said.

Marisol turned. The archivist stood at the rail with her binoculars lowered, and her face had gone carefully, professionally blank—the blankness of a person closing a door before anyone sees what's inside.

"How do you know?" Marisol asked.

"The stonework. The coursing is consistent with pre-annexation municipal construction." Anneli paused, and Marisol watched her choose her next words the way a gambler chooses which card to show. "There were settlements in this valley that don't appear in modern records. Administrative erasures. It happens."

"Erased settlements don't build staircases."

"No," Anneli agreed mildly. "They usually don't."

Teo already had his drone out, fingers flying over the controller, and the little machine lifted off with its insect whine and climbed toward the far bank. On his screen, Marisol saw the valley from above—and her stomach dropped, because from above it was obvious. The terraces weren't random. They followed lines. Straight lines, crossing at right angles, softened by a century of grass but unmistakable.

Streets.

Under the water, along the slow dark channel, the drone caught shadows that might have been foundations. A grid. Faint, drowned, patient.

"That's not a shipwreck," Teo said slowly. "Is it? We're not looking for a shipwreck."

"Charts say there's a wrecked steamer at the confluence," Dominic said, but his voice had the flat careful tone of a man reciting something he no longer believed. "The *Aurora del Sur*. 1911. That's the target."

"Sure," Teo said. "And that's a staircase to nowhere."

Marisol barely heard them. She was looking at the water, at the deep blue cold of it, and her grandmother's voice was singing in her skull in a language she'd mourned without ever learning to speak.

*La ciudad que se hundió dos veces.*

Once with rain. Once with paper.

"Grandmother," she murmured, "what did you know?"

"What?" Dominic asked.

"Nothing." She put her hand on the throttle. "Nothing yet."

* * *

They camped that night on a bench of flat ground above the drowned streets, and Marisol dreamed of her grandmother's kitchen, which meant she dreamed of the songs, because the kitchen and the songs were the same room in her memory. In the dream her grandmother stirred a pot and sang, and Marisol understood every word, and the words were—

She woke before the words arrived, heart hammering, and lay listening to the river move in the dark. It sounded different here. Slower. Deeper. Like something breathing under a blanket.

She got up quietly, wrapped herself in a blanket of the non-metaphorical kind, and climbed the small rise behind camp. The moon was up, three-quarters and generous, and it laid silver across the valley, and in that light Marisol saw what daylight had let her explain away.

The terraces marched down to the water in perfect order. And out in the channel, where the current ran smoothest, there was a disturbance in the flow—a long, low shape breaking the surface tension, dark stone just kissing the air, running parallel to the bank for fifty meters or more.

A wall. The top of a wall. Standing in the middle of the river.

Cities don't fall into rivers, she thought. Rivers rise over cities. Someone puts a dam across a valley, or diverts a flood, or signs a piece of paper in a capital far away, and the water comes up patient and obedient, and everything the paper says becomes true.

*Once with paper.*

She sat down hard on the cold ground.

Her grandmother had been born downstream of this. Had sung about it, coded and crooned, hiding a city inside lullabies the way you hide letters inside a name. And the town Marisol grew up in—the pie-selling, wrong-road, nobody's-heard-of-it town—it sat in the shadow of a valley that had been drowned on purpose, and nobody had ever said so, and her family had carried the memory in songs because songs were the only container small enough to survive.

Homesickness hit her then, sudden and physical, a fist under the ribs—but homesick for what? For a porch that belonged to a town that sat beside a lie? For a language that was a lifeboat? For a grandmother who had known and sung anyway, decade after decade, to children who fell asleep before the meaning arrived?

"You're up late."

Anneli stood at the bottom of the rise, lantern off, silhouetted against the stars. Of course it was Anneli. Marisol was beginning to suspect the woman slept in shifts timed to other people's insomnia.

"Couldn't sleep," Marisol said. "The river's loud."

"It's quiet."

"It's loud underneath the quiet." Marisol pulled the blanket tighter. "You knew. About the walls. Before we ever got here."

Anneli came up the slope and stood beside her, following her gaze to the dark line of the wall. For a long moment neither spoke.

"I knew there were questions here," Anneli said finally. "I didn't know the river would answer this fast."

"What's the target, Dr. Brandt? Really. Because I've been telling myself we're raising a steamship, and I can't tell myself that anymore. Not with a staircase in my headlights."

Anneli was silent long enough that Marisol turned to look at her. In the starlight the archivist's face was unreadable, but her hands—her hands were gripping the strap of her bag tight enough to whiten the knuckles, and Marisol had spent enough years reading water to read that.

"Ask me tomorrow," Anneli said. "After we reach the confluence. If I'm going to lie to you, I'd rather only do it once more."

"And if you're not going to lie?"

"Then tomorrow I'll tell you something that gets us all killed," Anneli said pleasantly, "and you'll wish I'd lied."

She walked back down the slope, footsteps soft on the dry grass, leaving Marisol alone with the wall and the water and the song rising in her chest like floodwater behind a dam.

Below, in the drowned dark, the city waited. It had waited a hundred years. It could wait one more night.

But Marisol found, lying awake under the generous moon, that she wasn't sure she could.
