## 5. Low Water

Teo had named all four of his drones after telenovela villains because, in his experience, anything you loved enough to fly over open water would eventually betray you. There was Rocío, the big survey quad with the cracked landing gear; there were the twins, Esteban and Esteban Dos, identical and constantly feuding over signal interference; and there was La Viuda, the little fixed-wing glider he'd built from a broken weather station and pure spite.

It was La Viuda he launched first that morning, off the flat of his palm, into air so still it felt embarrassed to be moving at all.

"Go on, my widow," Teo murmured. "Tell us what the river's hiding."

The river was hiding a lot. That was the whole problem.

They'd been on the water six days now, working upstream in Marisol's shallow-draft barge through country that got more beautiful and less apologetic about it every day — red cliffs, thorn scrub, guanacos watching them pass with the mild contempt of animals who had never once been in debt. And every day the river dropped. It had been dropping for months, apparently, this whole dry side of Patagonia shrugging off its water like a coat, and nobody had thought to mention to Teo that they'd be sailing down the middle of a drought with a salvage contract pinned to the bottom of a riverbed.

He stood at the bow with the controller cradled in both hands, watching La Viuda's feed on his tablet, and that was when the walls came into view.

* * *

"Walls," Teo said.

Nobody looked up. Dominic was at the stern arguing with an outboard motor in the low, patient voice of a man who had learned that machinery could smell fear. Anneli sat under the canopy with her knees drawn up, reading something in German on a battered e-reader. Marisol was poling them through a stretch of shallows with the bored grace of someone who had been doing it since before Teo was born.

"Walls," Teo said again, louder. "In the river. Under the river. Walls *under* the river."

That got Dominic's attention, mostly because anything that stopped Teo talking for ten seconds straight was by definition an emergency.

"Show me."

Teo turned the tablet around. The feed showed the river from forty meters up: brown-green water braiding itself thin across a wide gravel bed, and beneath the surface — clear as anything in the morning light, because there simply wasn't much water left to hide behind — long straight lines crossing each other at right angles. Streets. Or the ghosts of streets. A grid, drowned, patient, laid out by somebody who had believed the water would stay where they put it.

Dominic went very quiet. Dominic going very quiet was worse than Dominic yelling, which Teo had learned early and filed away as useful data.

"That's not a wreck," Dominic said.

"Nope," said Teo.

"That's not a ship, or a barge, or a cargo site. That's—" He turned to Marisol. "Marisol. What is that?"

Marisol shipped her pole and looked at the screen for a long moment. Her face did something complicated and then settled into nothing at all, which from Marisol was practically a scream.

"My grandmother used to sing about a city," she said. "Under the water twice, she said. I always figured it was one of those songs grandmothers sing so children stop asking questions." She pushed the pole back down into the gravel. "Apparently she wasn't singing to shut me up."

* * *

By noon they had put ashore on a gravel bar that no map admitted existed, and Teo had all three quads in the air at once, which was against his own personal safety regulations and also the most fun he'd had in a year.

Esteban flew the grid pattern, low and slow, camera pointed straight down. Esteban Dos orbited wide, catching the whole valley in context: the river's old channel, visible as a pale scar in the scrubland, curving around the drowned streets like a hand cupped to protect something. Rocío hovered close over the most intact structure — a rectangle of dressed stone maybe thirty meters long, its roofless walls standing chest-high above the silt, a doorway still framed in it, still facing east.

"They cut the stone," Teo reported, narrating for the benefit of the adults, who had gathered behind him like students. "You don't cut stone like that for a fishing camp. Look at the corners. Look at the— okay, hold on, hold on, everybody shut up, Rocío's found something."

Rocío had found a street. An actual street, cobbled, running between two lines of foundations, and at the end of it a set of steps leading down into deeper water, and at the top of the steps—

"A plaza," said Anneli quietly, over his shoulder. "There's a fountain base in the center. You can see the pedestal."

"You can see the pedestal," Teo agreed, delighted. "A whole city. A whole entire city, sitting in a river, and it's not on any map. I've checked every map. Satellite, topographic, hydrographic, the ones from the eighties, the ones from the fifties. Nothing. This valley is a blank spot with a city in it."

"How does a city not end up on maps?" Dominic asked. Nobody answered him, which was answer enough.

Anneli stood closest of anyone, close enough that Teo could feel her breath moving when she exhaled. She didn't say anything for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had the careful flatness of someone walking on ice they've personally tested.

"Teo. How many copies of these files exist?"

"Just the one card per drone, plus what streams back to my laptop." He shrugged. "Standard setup. Why?"

"I want you to understand something," Anneli said. She turned to face him fully, and Teo noticed — really noticed, for the first time since Puerto Natales — how tired her eyes were. Not expedition-tired. Older than that. "Whatever this place is, it was hidden on purpose. Maps don't just forget cities. Cities get forgotten by people with the power to decide what maps say. If we photograph it, we're involved. There's no version of this where we fly home and talk about it at parties."

Teo looked back at the tablet. On the screen, La Viuda's feed showed the plaza from above, the fountain pedestal casting a small crooked shadow across stones that hadn't seen sun in — how long? Decades? Generations? His thumbs rested light on the controls, and he felt the specific vertigo of a sixteen-year-old realizing that the toy in his hands had just become evidence.

"So what do we do?" he asked. "Not take pictures?"

"We already took pictures," said Anneli. "That's the point. Now we decide what happens to them."

* * *

They decided nothing, which is how expeditions usually handle decisions, and moved camp to the high bank above the ruins as the afternoon wind came up. Dominic wanted the chest — whatever chest, wherever the Voss brothers' coordinates actually pointed — located and raised before the river did anything unpredictable, and Marisol wanted to be gone from this stretch of water by tomorrow night, though she wouldn't say why, and everyone pretended not to notice that she wouldn't say why.

Teo spent the evening doing maintenance, which meant sitting cross-legged among his drones with a screwdriver and letting his brain run in the background. It was during this that he noticed Anneli walk past his laptop.

She didn't stop. That was the thing he kept coming back to later. She didn't hover, didn't glance, didn't do any of the things a person does when they're being sneaky. She walked past at exactly the pace she always walked, toward the water, with her tin mug, and she was gone perhaps four minutes, and when she came back she walked past again, and that was all.

But Teo's laptop lived in his tent, and his tent flap was half-open for the breeze, and his screen was angled toward the flap because he liked to watch his upload progress bars while he worked, and the progress bars were not moving anymore.

He waited until full dark, until Dominic was asleep in the cab of the truck and Marisol was somewhere out on the bank doing whatever Marisol did alone at night, and then he checked.

The raw files were gone. All of them. Every frame from Esteban and Esteban Dos and Rocío and La Viuda — the grids, the streets, the plaza, the doorway facing east. Deleted, and the recycle bin emptied, and the folder timestamps touched up so it looked like nothing had ever been written there at all. Clean work. Professional work. The kind of deletion you learn in an institution, not a hostel.

Except.

Teo was sixteen and had grown up poor and curious, which is to say he had never once in his life trusted a machine to do only what it appeared to be doing. His drones cached to their onboard cards. Every flight, automatically, before anything streamed anywhere. He popped Rocío's card, slotted it into his reader, and watched the drowned city come back pixel by pixel in the blue glow of his tent.

Streets. Steps. A fountain waiting for water that had been taken somewhere else on purpose.

He sat there a long time, thumb hovering over the copy command, feeling the shape of the situation settle onto him like a wet coat. Anneli Brandt, who shared her chocolate ration without being asked, who had taught him three words of German and laughed at his accent, who read books in languages he couldn't name — Anneli had erased a city tonight. Quietly, competently, between one sip of tea and the next.

Which meant either she was protecting them from something, or she was protecting something from them.

Teo copied the files twice — once to the drive taped inside his drone case, once to the microSD sewn into the lining of his jacket, because his mother had raised him to believe in redundancy and also in jackets with linings — and then he lay back on his sleeping pad and stared at the tent ceiling and listened to the river move over the streets below.

Wonder, he decided, was a lot like the river. Beautiful right up until you noticed how low it had gotten, and then all you could see was what it had been hiding the whole time.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he'd ask her. Not accusing. Just — asking. He was good at asking. He talked enough that one more question would never be noticed.

Out on the dark water, something shifted — a long, low groan of stone settling in the current, or maybe the river rearranging its secrets — and Teo pulled his jacket tighter around the little card stitched against his ribs, and did not sleep for a long time.
