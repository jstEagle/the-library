## 7. What Anneli Knew

Anneli Brandt had spent eleven years learning to sleep like an archivist: lightly, on her back, with one ear tuned to the sound of things being moved that shouldn't be.

So when the chest's tarp whispered at half past two in the morning, she was awake before the whisper finished. She lay still in her bag and listened to Dominic Ishikawa cross the camp on sock feet, crouch beside the chest, and—this was the part she found genuinely interesting—do nothing. He didn't open it. He didn't take anything. He just sat there in the dark, a large quiet shape, keeping it company.

She could have told him that guarding it was pointless. She could have told him a great many things, actually, and had been rehearsing them for three days against the inside of her eyelids, the way you rehearse a difficult phone call. *I have to tell you something. No—I need to tell you something.* Every draft sounded like a confession or a press release, and neither would survive contact with Marisol Reyes, who looked at Anneli the way customs officers look at luggage.

The problem was that Anneli had been lying by profession for so long that honesty now required equipment. A plan. A moment. Preferably witnesses, so that no single one of them could decide afterward that she'd made the whole thing up.

Dominic stood, patted the tarp once like a man soothing a horse, and went back to his bag.

Anneli stared at the stars and thought: *tonight*. Not because tonight was ideal. Because tomorrow they'd reach the confluence, and after the confluence there would be fuel stops and radio signals and other people, and every hour of delay made her confession less a gift and more a liability. Also—and she admitted this only grudgingly, the way you admit a leak in a roof—because she was tired. Bone-tired, in the specific way that comes from carrying a secret shaped like a government seal.

She got up, put on her boots without lacing them, and made tea over the banked coals. The kettle's small sounds woke Teo, whose sleeping pattern resembled a cat's: twenty short naps and total alertness during any human activity whatsoever.

"Are we leaving?" he whispered, already halfway out of his bag.

"No."

"Is someone here?"

"No."

"Then why are you making tea?"

"Because I can't sleep, and because if I'm going to ruin this expedition, I'd rather do it caffeinated."

Teo considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, which was considerable. "Ruin it how?"

"Go wake the others," she said. "Gently. And Teo—if Marisol reaches for something heavy, stand between them."

* * *

They gathered around the coals in various states of alarm dressed as casualness. Dominic sat with his arms crossed, watchful as weather. Marisol came last, wrapped in a wool blanket, hair loose, and took the seat farthest from Anneli, which Anneli noted was also the seat with the best view of her hands.

"I'll be brief," Anneli said, "because I suspect brevity is all the mercy I'm going to get."

"Already off to a strong start," Marisol murmured.

"My name is Dr. Anneli Brandt. That part is true. My doctorate is real; my funding is not." She turned her mug slowly in her hands. "Three months ago I was approached by an office within the national archive administration. It doesn't appear on org charts. The people in it don't give their surnames. They told me a salvage crew had applied for river permits on the Río Ceniza, upstream of the old dam country, and they wanted an archivist embedded. My job was simple: confirm whether a certain artifact existed, assess its contents, and ensure—" she paused, choosing the word carefully, hating each of its predecessors— "*that it doesn't reach the public.*"

The fire popped. Nobody breathed loudly.

"They gave me a cover story—a research grant, a university letterhead, a stipend routed through two foundations so clean they squeaked. In exchange, my debts were cleared. All of them. Which, given what I understand about this crew's relationship with debt, may be the most believable thing I've said tonight."

Dominic's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. Almost.

"When the divers raised the chest," Anneli went on, "and I saw the double sun on the lid, I sent a coded confirmation upriver on the supply run. Standard protocol. What happens next, per protocol, is that a recovery team meets us at the confluence. Officially they're cultural patrimony inspectors. Unofficially, the chest goes into a warehouse in the capital that officially doesn't exist, and everyone on this crew signs documents they can't read and forgets everything they've seen. There are clauses about disclosure. There are clauses about what happens to people who disclose."

"And you're telling us this because?" Marisol said. Her voice was flat, but her knuckles were white around the blanket's edge.

Anneli set down her mug. This was the part she'd rehearsed worst, because it was the part with no professional language for it.

"Because I've read the treaty drafts. The ones from before the erasure. I know what's in that chest isn't just paper proving a country existed. It's paper proving a country was *purchased*—quietly, clause by clause, by people who then flooded its valley and wrote it out of every atlas. And I know that some of the people in those clauses were never compensated, never relocated properly, never even renamed. Legally speaking, there are Valdorians alive today who are citizens of nowhere. The archive doesn't want the chest because it's dangerous history. They want it because it's dangerous *present*."

"And your office," Dominic said quietly, "is fine with that."

"My office exists to make sure no one ever has to be fine with anything out loud." She looked around the circle—at Dominic's steady, unreadable face, at Teo gone very still, at Marisol, whose expression had shifted from hostility into something more complicated and more dangerous: recognition. "I've spent three days telling myself I'm just an archivist doing archival work. But archivists preserve records. What I'm doing is helping bury one. And I watched Marisol's face yesterday when the seal came out of the water, and I thought—that woman knows something. Something older than my office. And I realized I had a choice between masters, and only one of them had ever lied to me politely."

Silence. The river talked to itself somewhere below the camp, patient as always.

"You sent the confirmation," Marisol said at last. "It's already done. They're coming."

"The recovery team mobilizes on my second signal. I haven't sent it." Anneli met her eyes. "Which means right now, as of this minute, I am the only person between that chest and a warehouse. And I am offering to stay that way. To burn my credentials, feed my handlers false coordinates, and help you move the chest somewhere they will never think to look—which, incidentally, is anywhere at all, because they believe I'm loyal."

"Why would you?" Teo asked—not suspiciously, just genuinely, the way kids ask the questions adults are too embarrassed to ask. "What's in it for you?"

Anneli smiled, and it surprised her by being real.

"Nothing," she said. "That's rather the point. For eleven years everything I've done has been in exchange for something—funding, access, absolution. I'd like to try one thing that costs me everything and buys nothing. Call it research."

Marisol stood. The blanket slid off her shoulders. For a long moment she looked down at Anneli, and Anneli braced for the verdict.

"You knew the songs," Marisol said quietly. "When I mentioned my grandmother's city under the water, you didn't ask what city. You asked which *valley*."

"Yes."

"How long have you known what they did to it?"

"Since before I met any of you."

Marisol nodded slowly, once, like a door deciding whether to open. Then she looked at Dominic. "Your call, jefe. You signed for the chest. But I'll say this much: I've been lied to by better people than her, and none of them ever volunteered the truth at two in the morning over bad tea."

Dominic hadn't spoken in four minutes, which Anneli had learned was his maximum processing time for betrayal. Now he uncrossed his arms.

"If we move tonight," he said, "where?"

"Downriver past the confluence, then cut south before the fuel stop at Puerto Hondo," Anneli said. "My handlers expect the crew to follow the permit route exactly. Deviation reads as incompetence, not evasion—for about thirty-six hours. After that, we're ghosts, and so am I."

"And the barge?"

"Light enough to run in the dark if someone pilots it who knows the channel blind." Anneli glanced at Marisol. "Someone grew up here, I believe."

Marisol was already rolling her blanket. "Wake me when it's time to be furious again," she said. "Right now I'm busy."

Dominic looked at the chest, at the river, at the sky going the color of gunmetal in the east. Then at Anneli.

"For the record," he said, "I started watching you yesterday. Politely. Constantly. Without blinking."

"I know," Anneli said. "You breathe differently when you're surveilling someone. Very slight whistle on the inhale. You might want to work on that."

Something that was almost a laugh went around the circle—the first one since the chest came up out of the water, and maybe the first one ever among the five of them, and it broke something loose that needed breaking.

"Move tonight," Dominic said. "Everyone sleeps in their clothes. Teo—drones packed, batteries charged, and if you tell anyone what you heard tonight, including strangers, including *fish*, I will personally introduce you to the concept of consequences."

"Understood," Teo said solemnly, and then, unable to help himself: "Was the double sun really a nation? Like, with stamps? Did they have—"

"*Fish,*" said Marisol.

By four o'clock the barge sat low and silent in the black water, the chest lashed amidships under its tarp, and Anneli stood at the rail watching the last of the camp disappear behind them. Somewhere downstream, men with clean foundations and no surnames were waiting for a signal that would never come. Somewhere ahead was a confluence, a lie about coordinates, and a country that had never officially existed, carrying its own paperwork home.

She had chosen her master. Now, like everyone else on this unlucky boat, she would find out what the choice cost.

At the first bend of the river, Marisol killed the running lights, and the dark took them the rest of the way.
