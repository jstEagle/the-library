## 8. Night Crossing

My grandmother used to say the river had two voices: the one it sang by day, and the one it muttered after dark when it thought nobody was listening. I grew up believing that was just something old women said to make children behave. Then I spent forty years on the water and learned she'd been giving me a navigation briefing.

The night voice was muttering now.

We loaded the chest onto the barge at dusk, which is a polite way of saying we wrestled a four-hundred-kilo iron box down a mud bank in failing light while Teo narrated like a sports commentator and Dominic pretended he wasn't counting how many times his feet slipped.

"Careful—careful—*pivot*—I said pivot, not pirouette—"

"I am pivoting," Dominic said through his teeth, "in an interpretive way."

The chest went over the gunwale on the third try, landing with a boom that I felt in my back teeth. The *Rosa del Río* — my father's barge, my grandfather's before him, named for a woman neither of them ever explained — settled under the weight and then forgave us, the way she always did. Shallow draft, flat bottom, hull of welded steel plates patched so many times she was more repair than boat. She'd carried cattle, contraband, and once, according to family legend, a complete circus tent. She could carry this.

Anneli stood on the bank holding the lantern, its light turned low, and watched the chest as if it might get up and walk away. Maybe she thought it would. Maybe she hoped.

"You're sure about this channel?" Dominic asked me, for the fourth time since lunch.

"No," I said. "Nobody's sure about any channel at night. That's what makes it a crossing instead of a cruise."

He looked at me. I looked at him. Somewhere behind us, thunder cleared its throat from beyond the eastern mesa — too far to see lightning yet, too close to ignore.

"That's a storm," Teo said helpfully.

"It's always a storm," I said. "It's Patagonia. Get aboard."

* * *

I took us out slow, engine idling just above a whisper, running on the memory in my hands. This stretch of river I'd known since I was small enough to fall in without anyone panicking — the bend below the drowned orchard, the gravel bar that migrated every flood season like a restless sleeper, the willow stand where my grandmother swore a man had once hidden a piano from the tax collectors. The water was low enough that the bones of the valley showed everywhere: stone walls breaking the surface like the backs of sleeping animals, a stairway descending into black water where no house stood anymore.

Valdoria's streets. Breathing air for the first time in who knew how long, and here we were, floating over them in the dark with their whole history in a box at our feet.

Teo crouched beside the chest with a rag, wiping river mud off the double-sun seal with the tenderness other teenagers reserved for phones.

"You know you don't have to polish it," Dominic said.

"It deserves respect."

"It's a box."

"It's a *historical* box." Teo buffed a patch of green patina. "Do you think there's treasure? Like, actual gold? Because Anneli said documents, but Anneli also said she deleted the drone files, and we all just sort of nodded like that was a normal thing a person says."

Anneli, at the stern with me, did not turn around. The lantern light caught the edge of her jaw. "The files are deleted," she said evenly.

"Right," Teo said. "Deleted. Sure. It's just — I have backups, is the thing, and—"

"*Teo.*"

"—which are also deleted," he finished, in the tone of a boy discovering diplomacy. "All of them. Every single one. Especially the ones on the cloud."

I smothered a laugh against my sleeve. Behind us, far off, the thunder spoke again, a little closer, a little longer. I checked the sky out of habit: no stars over the east, just a bruise-colored wall of cloud building up behind the mesas. A dry-side storm. They came down off the mountains fast and mean, dropping a month of rain on country that couldn't absorb a week of it, and every drop ran downhill into the same river we were sitting on.

"How far to the takeout?" Dominic asked.

"Eleven kilometers," I said. "Three hours if the river behaves."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then it's a shorter trip than that, one way or another."

He considered that. "You're very comforting, you know that?"

"My grandmother raised me on ghost stories and river lore. Comforting wasn't in the curriculum."

* * *

The first hour was almost beautiful, if you were the kind of person who found beauty in black water and cold wind and the smell of wet sagebrush coming off the banks. I am that kind of person, though I'd never say so out loud where the river could hear me being sentimental.

I ran us down the middle of the channel, reading the water by feel — the smooth glassy tongue where the current ran deep, the wrinkled V above a submerged rock, the sudden oily hump that meant a hole waiting to swallow a propeller. My hands did most of the thinking. That was fine. My hands had been doing this longer than my head had been doing anything.

Dominic came forward and stood beside me, one hand on the rail, watching the dark water with the expression of a man trying to read a language he'd only ever heard spoken.

"You grew up on this river," he said. Not a question.

"On this one and three others. We moved when the work moved."

"The songs your grandmother sang. The ones about the city under the water." He hesitated. "Marisol, when we were loading — you looked at that stairway coming out of the bank like you recognized it."

I kept my eyes on the channel. "Everyone from these parts knows those stories. City that went under the water twice. Songs about bells ringing underwater. Kids' stuff."

"Do you believe it now?"

The honest answer was that I didn't know what I believed, and that the not-knowing sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. My grandmother had sung those songs rocking me to sleep, her voice going low and strange on certain verses, verses she'd hum when my mother left the room. I'd always assumed they were just old-country nonsense, carried across some ocean from somewhere else. But we weren't near anywhere else. We were here. And here had walls coming up out of the river arranged in rows, like streets.

"Ask me tomorrow," I said. "Tonight I'm busy."

He nodded and let it go, which I was learning was one of his better qualities: he knew when a door was closed, even if he made a note of where it was.

That was when the river changed its mind.

* * *

I felt it before I heard it — a tremor in the hull, a shiver through the wheel, like the whole river had flinched. Then, downstream, a sound I knew from childhood nightmares: a long, rolling roar, like a freight train that had decided water was faster than rails.

"Flash flood," I said, and my voice came out flat and calm, which surprised me, because inside I was already eight years old again, standing on the bank watching the Río Blanco eat a bridge.

"What?" Dominic leaned forward. "From where? There's no rain here."

"There doesn't need to be. It's falling on the mountains right now and it's all coming to us. Everyone hold on! Teo — kill the lantern!"

The light died. Total dark, except the faint gray suggestion of the banks. I throttled up, hunting for the deep channel by sound and memory, because in about ninety seconds the shallow water we were floating on was going to become a moving wall of debris, and the only survival strategy on a flash flood is to be in the fastest, deepest part of the current, pointed downstream, surfing it like a log ride designed by someone who hates you.

The roar doubled. Tripled. Out of the black ahead came a smell — wet clay, torn grass, crushed flowers — the smell of a river carrying its own banks along with it.

Then the leading edge hit us.

The *Rosa* lurched, rose, and slewed sideways. Water came over the bow in a solid sheet. I heard the chest slide and Teo yell and Dominic shouting something that was either a prayer or profanity, possibly both, and then I stopped hearing anything individually because the world was all noise — the river roaring, the hull groaning, something enormous and soft — a stripped tree, a whole island of reeds — grinding past us close enough to touch.

"MARISOL!" Dominic's hand found my shoulder in the dark.

"I've got it!" I shouted back, lying completely, steering by the seat of my pants and the grace of whatever saint looks after idiots in steel barges. The trick with a flood wave is to stay on top of it, ride the crest, never let the nose dig in. The *Rosa* surfed, dropped, slammed, surfed again. Cold water swirled around our ankles. Somewhere below decks something broke loose and banged rhythmically against the hull like a panicked heart.

"We're taking on water!" Anneli's voice, sharp and precise even at the end of the world. Of course hers was precise. Hers would be precise at the actual end of the world.

"Bailing bucket's under the bench!"

"Why is there only ONE—"

"BECAUSE MY FATHER WAS AN OPTIMIST!"

And then — I swear this happened — Teo started laughing. High, wild, fifteen-year-old laughter bubbling up out of the dark like the river itself had gotten into him. "WE'RE GONNA DIE AND IT'S THE COOLEST THING THAT'S EVER HAPPENED TO ME!"

"TEO—"

"NO, LISTEN—" He was hauling on something, grunting. "The chest — it slid — I'm sitting ON it — I'm using it as an ANCHOR — Grandma Valdoria's got us, she's GOT US—"

Whether it was the boy's weight on the chest, or the old girl's hull remembering heavier days, or plain dumb luck — the *Rosa* came around. Nose downstream. Into the main channel, into the throat of the flood, riding the crest of the wave as it spread and slowed and spent itself across the wide flats downstream.

I steered. The river roared. And for maybe ten minutes, nobody talked, because there was nothing to say that the water wasn't already saying louder.

* * *

The flood passed through us and became, gradually, merely a river again — high, brown, fast, but a river, with manners. We limped into a side slough below the old ferry crossing and tied off to a dead willow, and the four of us stood in six inches of water on the deck, breathing hard, looking at each other in the gray pre-dawn like survivors of a shipwreck who hadn't quite decided to be grateful yet.

Teo was still straddling the chest. His hair was plastered to his skull. He looked like a small drowned cat guarding a national treasury.

"Everyone accounted for?" I asked.

Nods. Anneli wrung out her sleeve with grim efficiency. Dominic sat down heavily on the bench, put his face in his hands, and then — I saw it in the half-light — his shoulders started shaking. For one horrible second I thought he was crying.

He was laughing. Quietly, helplessly, the laugh of a man who has run out of everything else.

"We nearly died," he said, "polishing a box."

"Historical box," Teo corrected.

"In a flood. In the dark. In Patagonia." He lifted his head. "I want everyone to understand that ten years ago I chased tornadoes for a television network and I thought that was the edge. This is the edge. This is so far past the edge the edge needs binoculars."

Anneli sat down beside him, primly, avoiding the deepest puddle. "For the record," she said, "when I offered to help you move the chest tonight, 'nearly drowning' was not in my risk assessment."

"Your risk assessment had a section for tonight?"

"It had several sections. All of them are now marked 'obsolete.'"

I looked at the four of us — the disgraced pilot, the debtor, the spy, the kid — soaked, exhausted, alive, and something loosened in my chest that had been tight for years. Crews aren't made on calm water. Any group of strangers can share a boat when the river's behaving. What makes a crew is the moment after, when you look around at the people who held on next to you, and realize you'd hold on next to them again.

"Get the bilge pump working," I said, and my voice cracked on a smile. "We've still got five kilometers to go, and the takeout road floods worse than the river."

Teo saluted, dripping. Dominic hauled himself up. Anneli found the pump. And the *Rosa del Río*, riding low and stubborn, carried us the last stretch through the washed-clean dawn.

It was Teo, hauling the chest toward the truck at the takeout, who stopped mid-step and said, "Uh. Guys?"

"What?"

"There's tire tracks at the landing. Fresh ones." He looked up, and the morning light showed me his face going pale. "Two sets. Big vehicle. And somebody's been standing here long enough to crush a whole cigarette pack into the mud."

I looked at Dominic. Dominic looked at the tracks, and I watched ten years of running catch up with his eyes all at once.

"Ilsa," he said quietly. "She's ahead of us."
