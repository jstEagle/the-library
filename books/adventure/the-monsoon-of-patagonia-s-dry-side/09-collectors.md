## 9. Collectors

The fuel stop at Puerto Viejo del Río was less a town than a rumor with a gas pump. Two pumps, actually, one of which had been "almost fixed" since the Kirchner administration, according to a hand-painted sign that Marisol read aloud in a tone of deep anthropological interest. The other pump worked fine, provided you fed it pesos like a temperamental horse and didn't look directly at the attendant's dog.

Dominic stood under the corrugated awning watching the fuel gauge climb and doing the arithmetic he'd been doing for ten years: what he had, what he owed, and the distance between them, measured not in money but in days. The chest sat in the barge's hold under a tarp and three layers of fishing net, looking, he hoped, like nothing more interesting than engine parts.

His phone buzzed. He checked it out of habit before remembering there was no signal this far upriver, and felt the small, stupid relief of a man who has been granted a stay of execution by geography.

"Fuel's almost done," Teo said, orbiting him with his drone case slung across his back like a guitar. "Hey, do you think Ilsa Rehn is a real name? Because it sounds like a name someone invented to be scary. Like if you were designing a villain—"

"She's real," Dominic said.

"—you'd give her, like, a monosyllabic surname and a first name that sounds like weather. Ilsa Rehn. See? Weather."

"Teo."

"Right. Fueling. I'm helping." He went to help, which meant standing near Marisol while she worked.

Dominic leaned against the truck and let himself think about Ilsa Rehn properly, because she'd been in the back of his mind for two weeks like a stone in a boot. She'd come to the Voss brothers' dinner once, briefly, to deliver a folder. She wore gray the way other people wore uniforms. She'd shaken his hand and said, "Mr. Ishikawa. The brothers speak well of you," in a voice that made clear the brothers spoke well of very few people, and that being one of them was not the same as being safe.

She would have heard about the chest by now. Anneli's handlers knew; the syndicate knew; the river itself seemed to know, gossiping downstream in every eddy. It was only a matter of—

The satellite phone in the truck's glovebox rang.

Dominic looked at it for a long moment. Nobody called the satphone except people who already knew where they were calling, which defeated most of its purpose, or people who wanted him to know they could.

He answered. "Ishikawa."

"Good morning, Mr. Ishikawa." A woman's voice, unhurried, pleasant, with the faintest Germanic architecture underneath the Spanish. "You're at the fuel stop outside Puerto Viejo. The old pump is still broken, I see. Some things are dependable."

He turned slowly. The road into the stop ran along a low ridge, and on the ridge, half a kilometer off, a dust plume hung in the air like a held breath. A vehicle, parked, waiting. Not approaching. Just existing where he could see it.

"Ilsa Rehn," he said.

"You remembered. That's kind. Listen—I'm coming down. Don't run. Running would make this tedious for both of us, and I've had a long drive." A pause, and then, warmer: "Also, your pilot is excellent, but the river below the narrows is low, and your draft is wrong for a fast exit. I did check. Please don't make me be right about things in front of your crew. It sets a poor tone."

* * *

She came down alone, in a white Land Cruiser so clean it looked embarrassed to be in Patagonia. She got out, and she was exactly as Dominic remembered: mid-forties, gray blazer, hair pulled back with the severity of a woman who considered loose hair a security risk. She carried no weapon visibly, which in her line of work meant either that she didn't need one or that it was somewhere with better posture than her hip.

"Mr. Ishikawa." She offered her hand. Her grip was brief and dry. "You look well. Debt agrees with some people."

"It's the fresh air."

"Mm." She glanced around the fuel stop with the mild interest of a tourist. Marisol had stopped working and was leaning on the pump handle with her arms crossed, radiating the specific hostility of a woman protecting her river. Teo hovered near the drone case, vibrating. Anneli stood by the barge with a book she was holding upside down, which Dominic chose to interpret as a deliberate performance of harmless academic distraction rather than genuine panic.

"The rest of your crew," Ilsa observed. "Charming. May we talk privately? Just there, by the water. Ten minutes. I'll buy you a coffee afterward—the attendant tells me it's terrible, which is honest advertising at least."

They walked to the edge of the lot, where the bank dropped to the river. She stopped at a respectful distance and folded her hands, and for a moment she simply looked at the water, and Dominic understood that the pause was the point. The Vosses' people never rushed. Rushing meant you needed something. Needing something meant you could be bargained with, and the Vosses did not bargain from need.

"Here's my situation," she said finally. "The brothers sent me to recover an asset. I have recovered many assets. Boats, trucks, houses, once an entire fishing fleet, which was a fun week. This—" she gestured vaguely at the tarp-covered shape in the barge, "—is not an asset. This is a problem wearing an asset's clothes. Do you understand the difference?"

"Not yet."

"An asset has a price. A problem has consequences, and consequences don't have prices, they have owners." She turned to face him fully. "So here is what I am authorized to offer, and please believe me when I say I am offering it sincerely, because the alternative paperwork gives me a headache. Triple the contract. Your debt cleared—not deferred, cleared, stamped, gone. Plus a bonus sufficient that you never have to chase weather for money again. You could chase it for fun, like a hobbyist. All of it, for the chest, intact, delivered to me at the coast road junction, tomorrow evening."

The number she named was large enough that Dominic's brain did the thing it always did with large numbers: it started furnishing a life. A truck without liens. Gear without secondhand shame. A room somewhere with his name on a lease, plural years. He let it furnish for exactly four seconds, because he'd learned that the fantasy was part of the mechanism, and then he shut it off.

"And if I say no?"

"Then I keep following you," she said pleasantly. "At a distance. Comfortably. I have fuel and patience and you have neither, particularly the second. The desert will negotiate with you on my behalf, Mr. Ishikawa. I find it's very persuasive. And when you're tired enough, we'll have this same conversation again, and you'll say yes, and we'll both pretend the first conversation never happened. That's usually how it goes." She smiled, and it was a genuinely nice smile, which somehow made everything worse. "But I want to say one thing plainly, because I think you're a man who respects plainness. The brothers don't want to sell what's in that chest. They want to own the people who are afraid of it. That's a different business. Better margins. So when you're weighing your answer, weigh that too."

"Why tell me that?"

"Because you'll figure it out anyway, and I'd rather you thought well of me." She checked her watch—a plain steel thing, no jewelry anywhere on her, another uniform. "Ten minutes. I said ten. Think it over tonight. I'll call the satphone at dawn. Oh—and Mr. Ishikawa?" She paused at the Land Cruiser's door. "Your archivist is holding her book upside down. Either she's very nervous or very clever. In my experience it's rarely both."

She drove back up the ridge and parked there again, patient as weather.

* * *

They argued about it for three hours, which for this crew counted as remarkable unity.

Marisol wanted to run the river at night, consequences be damned. Anneli pointed out, with the book now right side up, that the narrows were genuinely too shallow and that drowning would render the entire debate academic. Teo wanted to rig the drone with flares and stage a diversion, a plan he described with such enthusiasm that everyone else quietly agreed never to speak of it again. Anneli wanted to surrender the chest to her archive contacts, an idea that received the collective warmth of a snowbank.

And Dominic sat on the tailgate turning Ilsa's words over: *they want to own the people who are afraid of it.*

"That's why the pay was so good," he said at last, into a lull. "From the start. The salvage contract. It was never about whatever we were supposed to find. They wanted us to find it. We're not couriers. We're prospectors they didn't have to pay salaries to."

"Cheerful thought," Marisol said. "Thank you. I'll treasure it."

"We should vote," Anneli said. "Formally. On whether to take her offer."

"No vote yet," Dominic said. "Not until we know all the options. Voting early is how you pick the least bad thing instead of the best available one." He hopped off the tailgate. "We move inland at first light, like planned. Overland. If she wants to follow us through the dry country, she can follow. Let's see how her Land Cruiser likes forty kilometers of salt flat."

Marisol grinned despite herself. "It'll love it. That's the problem."

"Everything's a problem," Dominic said. "Some problems just haven't met their match yet."

Nobody laughed, but Teo gave him a thumbs-up, which in the current economy was roughly equivalent.

* * *

They camped that night eight kilometers north, in a fold of ground behind a ridge of rust-colored rock, far enough off any track that a vehicle would have to be lucky or very good to find them. They set watches. Dominic took midnight to three, because he slept worst anyway and saw no reason to waste good sleep on himself.

The steppe at night was enormous. Stars in such quantity that they stopped being decoration and became terrain. Wind moving through the coiron grass with a sound like slow applause. Somewhere south, heat lightning flickered inside a cloud with no sound attached, a storm performing for an audience of nobody.

He liked it out here. That was the confession he never made aloud: for ten years, motion had been the only home he trusted. Every place he'd ever stayed had eventually asked him for something he couldn't pay—rent, commitment, an explanation of himself—and the road asked only for fuel and attention. Attention he had. He was, professionally, a man made of attention.

Which was why, at 1:40 in the morning, the silence changed key, and he noticed.

Wind doesn't stop. It dips, it gusts, it holds its breath—but a total absence of wind-sound on open steppe means something is standing between you and the grass. He turned his head slowly and saw, twenty meters out along the ridge line, a figure crouched at the edge of their camp. Then a second, lower, near the barge trailer. Neither moved fast. Both moved with the unhurried competence of people doing a job they'd done many times.

Dominic didn't reach for anything. He sat very still and watched, and after a moment he understood what he was seeing—or rather, what he wasn't. No flashlight beams swept toward the tents. Nothing was pried open. The figures moved around the camp the way a doctor moves around a sleeping patient: checking, reading, taking its pulse. One of them lifted the tarp corner over the chest, shone a penlight inside for perhaps five seconds, and let the tarp fall.

Then the nearest figure straightened, turned, and looked directly at Dominic across twenty meters of starlight.

It raised one hand—not a threat, not a wave. Something between the two. A greeting. A *we see you, you see us, isn't this civilized.*

And then both figures were gone, folded into the dark so cleanly that ten seconds later Dominic honestly couldn't have sworn which direction they'd left.

He sat there with his heart going like a drumline and did the math. They hadn't taken anything. They hadn't woken anyone. They'd come close enough to touch the chest and touched only the tarp, and the message in that was louder than any ransom note: *we could have. We chose not to. Yet.*

The trap hadn't snapped. It had just finished being built, quietly, while everyone slept, and now it sat around the whole camp, patient as the desert, waiting for morning and the phone call and the answer.

In the tent behind him, Teo murmured something in his sleep about battery levels. The heat lightning flickered on, silent, closer now.

Dominic watched the southern sky and thought: *that storm's going to beat everyone here to wherever it's going.*

He wrote the time in the logbook—01:47, visitors, two, professional, took nothing—and under it, because the logbook was the only place he told the truth, he added:

*We are not being chased. We are being kept.*
