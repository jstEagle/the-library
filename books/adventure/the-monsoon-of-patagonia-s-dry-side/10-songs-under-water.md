## 10. Songs Under Water

The chest had been sitting in the middle of camp for three days now, and everyone treated it the way you treat a sleeping dog of unknown temperament: respectfully, quietly, and with one eye on it at all times.

Marisol understood. She'd grown up around things like that—her grandmother's sewing box, which was never opened in front of guests; her uncle's second truck, which was never discussed at all; the songs.

The songs were the reason she couldn't sleep tonight.

They had made camp on a gravel shelf above the river, downriver from the drowned valley but not far enough downriver for comfort. Dominic had posted watches. Teo had rigged his drones to charge off the truck battery and then talked about battery chemistry until Anneli threatened to charge *him* off the truck battery instead. And Marisol had sat by the fire with her grandmother's notebook open on her knees, feeling like a woman who has been reading a letter upside down for thirty years.

Her grandmother, Abuela Rosa, had died when Marisol was nineteen, in a house that smelled of eucalyptus and diesel, leaving behind a notebook full of songs written in a language nobody in the family admitted to speaking. The family said the songs were "old river songs." That was the phrase they used, always the same phrase, delivered with the flat finality of people closing a door: *old river songs, mija, nothing more.*

Marisol had believed them because believing them was easier. Because asking questions in her family got you the look—the look her mother gave her father whenever anyone mentioned the year the water came up, or the town that used to be where the reservoir was, or why Great-Uncle Emilio refused to eat fish from that water his whole life.

She knew maybe forty words of the language. She'd thought she did, anyway. She'd sung the songs as a child without understanding them, the way children sing anything, and the melodies had lived in her body like scars live in skin: present, permanent, unexamined.

Then she'd seen the double-sun seal stamped into the lid of the chest, and something in her chest had gone cold and strange, because her grandmother's notebook had a drawing on its inside cover—a child's copy of a child's copy of two suns, one inside the other, drawn so many times the ink had worn grooves in the paper.

*Old river songs.*

She'd been humming the erasure her whole life.

* * *

"You're doing the thing," Teo said, dropping onto the rock beside her with a mug of something that claimed to be coffee. "The staring-at-nothing thing. Anneli does it too. Is it an archivist thing? Are you secretly an archivist?"

"I'm a river pilot."

"Right, but are you a *secret* archivist? Because that would explain a lot about your filing system on the barge."

"My filing system on the barge is 'whatever doesn't blow overboard.'"

"See, that's exactly what a secret archivist would say." He sipped his terrible coffee and made the face he always made, as if betrayed anew each time. Then, quieter: "You've been looking at that notebook since dinner. Since before dinner. You looked at it during dinner, which is rude, by the way, I made jokes."

"They were bad jokes."

"They were *great* jokes. There was one about the chest being a really committed tackle box." He followed her gaze to the notebook. His voice lost its bounce, which for Teo was like a lighthouse going dark. "That's the songbook, isn't it. The one you mentioned. On the river, day two. The one your grandmother sang."

Marisol turned a page. The handwriting looped and slanted, musical notation above lines of text in that alphabet she'd never been able to read—except that now, with the documents from the chest spread out in Anneli's careful plastic sleeves, some of the words had begun to resolve. Not all. But some. *Aguaviva.* Water-alive. A place name. She'd assumed it was a word for eddies, or springs, or something practical. It was in four of the songs.

It was in the treaty, too. Article Eleven. *The free city of Aguaviva, hereinafter dissolved.*

"Teo," she said, "get Anneli. And get Dominic. Don't make it a thing. Just—quietly."

"Quietly is my whole brand," Teo said, already standing, already loud enough to wake half of Patagonia.

* * *

Anneli came first, wrapped in a blanket against the steppe cold, hair escaping its braid in gray wisps. She knelt by the fire and looked at the notebook for a long time without touching it, the way she touched nothing without permission, as if every object were a document awaiting cataloguing.

"May I?"

"It's why I called you."

She turned pages with two fingers, slow, reverent. Dominic arrived last, arms crossed, radiating the particular patience of a man who has learned that waiting is usually cheaper than asking. He lowered himself onto a crate and said nothing, which from Dominic was practically a speech.

"Read me Article Eleven," Marisol said.

Anneli recited it from memory, which told Marisol exactly how many times she'd read it. "*The free city of Aguaviva, hereinafter dissolved, together with its port, its archives, its communal lands, and its registered inhabitants, shall pass into the administration of the signatory powers, such inhabitants retaining the right of residence and the obligation of silence regarding the former civic status of the same.*"

"The obligation of silence," Dominic repeated.

"That clause appears eleven times in the treaty," Anneli said. "In various forms. Silence, non-remembrance, the surrender of records. Whoever drafted it understood that conquest fails but forgetting can be administered." She paused. "Your grandmother's songs. How much of them do you understand?"

"Forty words. Maybe fifty. I thought—" Marisol stopped. Started again. "I thought they were about fishing. About the river rising. My mother said they were old river songs. Flood warnings, maybe. Songs women sang when the water came up, so the children would know to move to high ground."

"And now?"

"Now I know what *aguaviva* means. Now I know what *sol-dentro-sol* means." She touched the drawing inside the cover. Two suns, nested. "Sun-within-sun. It's not poetry. It's the seal. My grandmother drew the seal of Valdoria on the inside cover of her songbook and I thought it was a flower."

The fire popped. Down at the waterline, the river moved in the dark with that deep patient sound it made at night, like something enormous breathing slowly.

"Sing one," Anneli said softly.

"I don't—Anneli, I don't understand what I'm singing."

"I don't care. Sing it anyway. Sometimes the melody remembers what the mouth forgot."

So Marisol sang. Her voice came out rough at first, unused in this register, this language, and then found itself. It was a song about a woman walking down to the water with a lamp, looking for a house that wasn't there anymore, and everyone in her family had always said it was about the flood, about loss, about how the river takes things—and it *was*, but not the way they'd said. Not the way water takes things.

Because now, with the treaty vocabulary loose in her head, the words rearranged themselves. The woman wasn't mourning a house the river took. She was mourning a house men had put under the river. The song said so. It said it in the old words, the ones her family never translated, and it named the ones who signed, and it named the year, and it ended on a line Marisol had sung a hundred times as a lullaby without ever once hearing it:

*They wrote us out of the world in ink, but ink is only water that learned to lie.*

The last note hung over the fire. Nobody spoke. Teo had come back at some point and was standing at the edge of the light, uncharacteristically still, hugging his drone case like a teddy bear.

"The dam," Marisol said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "The songs aren't about a natural flood. They're about the dam. They're about—" She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "There's a song my grandmother sang about the engineers. We thought it was funny. It has this silly rhythm, we danced to it at weddings. It's about the surveyors who came to measure the valley. It lists their names. All their names. It's not a joke, it's a *record*. She was keeping a record of the men who drowned her city, and we danced to it."

"Marisol," Dominic said gently.

"No. Let me—there's more, there's so much more, it's been in front of me my whole life." She flipped through the notebook, pages trembling. "This one. This one we sang at planting time. I thought it was about seeds. Listen—it says *we buried the seed-cases under the new names, and the new names will crack in the spring.* It's about the families who took new surnames after the dissolution. New names. Cracking in the spring. It's a prophecy, or a promise, or—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "My grandfather's family name isn't our original name. My mother told me once we changed it 'after the water,' and when I asked what water, she made me go fetch eggs."

Anneli's hand settled on the notebook, steadying it, steadying her. "You understand what this makes you."

"A descendant of a country that doesn't exist."

"A descendant of a country that exists," Anneli corrected, quiet and precise, "and is not permitted to. There's a difference, and the treaty spent eleven clauses making sure nobody noticed it. Registered inhabitants. Retaining the right of residence. Your family didn't lose a homeland, Marisol. They were made stateless in place. Legally erased while standing on their own street."

The fury arrived then, and it arrived the way flash floods arrive on the dry side—not building, just suddenly *there*, filling every channel at once. Marisol stood up. She heard herself say, "They sang us to sleep. My own family. Three generations of singing the truth in a language nobody checked, because the treaty said be silent, so they were silent in the only way left—they hid it inside lullabies and wedding dances and planting songs, and they made the children carry it, and the children carried it without knowing, and I—" Her voice broke. "I have sung these songs to myself on the river for fifteen years. When I was scared. When I was lonely. They were the only home I had, and they were *home*, they were literally home, and I didn't know."

Nobody rushed to fix it. That was the thing about this crew, she was learning; they let grief stand up on its own legs before offering it a chair. Teo sat down heavily on her other side. Dominic fed the fire another branch, which was his version of a hug.

"The song about the lamp woman," he said finally. "The one you just sang. What's the last verse? The part after the ink line."

Marisol wiped her face and looked. She'd never gotten past the ink line—her forty words ran out there, always had. But beside the verse, in the margin, in smaller script, her grandmother had written a translation. Spanish, this time. Shy, cramped, as if even writing it felt dangerous.

Marisol read it aloud. "*But the lamp is passed hand to hand, and the water cannot drink a flame, and one day a granddaughter will walk down to the water and the houses will rise to meet her.*"

Silence again, but a different kind.

"She knew," Marisol whispered. "Abuela Rosa knew someone would come back. She wrote it in the margin like a letter she was mailing forty years forward." She looked up at the dark valley upstream, where the reservoir lay flat and secret under the stars, holding whatever it held. "Dominic. The chest. The documents. The letters inside—the family letters, the ones Anneli flagged. Some of those names will match this notebook. The songs list families. Planting lists, wedding lists, the engineers' names, the renamed families. This notebook is an index."

Anneli went very still, in the way Marisol was beginning to recognize as her version of alarm. "If the archive office learns that notebook exists—if they learn a descendant holds a keyed index to the chest's contents—"

"Then what?" Teo asked.

"Then we stop being a salvage crew," Anneli said, "and start being evidence."

Marisol closed the notebook and held it against her sternum, over the heartbeat, over the songs. Grief and fury braided together in her chest like rope, and rope, she knew from a life on the water, was good for exactly two things: holding on, and hauling.

Upstream, thunder muttered—too far to matter yet, too close to ignore. Dominic tilted his head at it, reading it the way he read everything, and frowned.

"That's early," he said. "That cell shouldn't be here for another two days."

Marisol hugged the notebook tighter and thought of her grandmother's margin note, of lamps passed hand to hand, of houses waiting under the water to rise.

The storm, at least, she understood. Storms never pretended to be anything else.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Around three in the morning she gave up pretending and walked down to the waterline, boots in hand, the notebook zipped inside her jacket. The river ran black and quick. Somewhere up there, under all that held-back water, was Aguaviva—streets, doorframes, a church bell her great-grandmother had supposedly rung on the last morning, though the family told that story the way they told everything, sideways, with the door left open exactly one crack.

Marisol crouched and touched the current with two fingers, the way her grandmother had taught her to greet any water: *good evening, old neighbor.*

And because no one was awake to hear, and because some debts of silence can only be paid aloud, she sang the lamp-woman's song—all of it, both languages, the margin included—to the river, to the drowned streets, to the grandmother, to the granddaughter she'd apparently been writing letters to across forty years of water.

When she finished, the thunder upstream rolled again, closer now, and the river shivered against her fingers like something answering.

Behind her, gravel crunched. She turned, heart hammering, and found Dominic standing at the edge of the firelight, jacket on, watching the southern sky with an expression she'd learned to dread.

"We have a problem," he said. "Wake the others."
