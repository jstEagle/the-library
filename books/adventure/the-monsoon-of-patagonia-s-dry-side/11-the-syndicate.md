## 11. The Syndicate

Teo had named all four of his drones after saints, which was funny because he wasn't Catholic and neither were the drones. There was Santa Lucía, the big fixed-wing surveyor with the cracked prop guard; San Jorge, his workhorse quad; La Dolorosa, a battered little racer he'd won in a card game he still felt guilty about; and El Milagro, the one held together mostly by zip ties and prayer.

He was running El Milagro up the river canyon at dawn because nobody else was awake to tell him not to, and that was when he saw the boats.

Three of them. Dark green, low in the water, engines idling behind a bend two kilometers downstream of their camp. Not fishing boats — fishing boats didn't carry men in matching jackets, and they definitely didn't carry a man on the bow of the lead boat holding what Teo's grandmother would have called *una escopeta* and what Teo's own brain called *oh no oh no oh no*.

"Okay," Teo whispered to El Milagro. "Okay okay okay."

He brought her home fast, skimming the canyon wall at an altitude that would have made Marisol box his ears, and jogged back into camp with the controller clutched to his chest like a hymnal.

Dominic was awake, of course. Dominic was always awake before everyone, standing at the edge of the water with his coffee like a man waiting for a phone call he dreaded. That was basically his whole personality as far as Teo could tell, and honestly it was kind of comforting, having one person whose job was to dread things so nobody else had to.

"Boats," Teo said. "Three. Downstream. Guns."

Dominic didn't spill his coffee, which Teo thought showed real discipline. "How far?"

"Two klicks. Maybe twenty minutes if they're lazy. Ten if they're not."

Dominic set down the cup. "Wake them up."

*

Waking people up was one of Teo's actual skills. He'd perfected it during the years his mother worked night shifts and he had to get himself and his little brother to school without waking the neighbor whose dog hated children. So he did Anneli first, gently, because she slept like a museum piece, then Marisol, who came up swinging and connected with his shoulder hard enough to leave a bruise he planned to milk for sympathy later.

"The syndicate," Dominic was saying, already kicking dirt over the fire pit. "They found us."

"How?" Marisol demanded, pulling her boots on with the fury of a woman who considered footwear a moral issue.

Everyone looked at Anneli.

Anneli went very still, the way she did when someone mentioned the archive office or asked her to pass the salt. "I deleted those files," she said.

"You said you deleted those files," Marisol said.

"I did delete those files." A pause. "Deleted files can be recovered. I knew that. I hoped—" She stopped, jaw tight. "I hoped nobody would bother looking."

"Well," Teo offered into the silence, "somebody bothered."

The plan came together the way their plans always did: fast, ugly, and held together with arguments. They couldn't outrun motorboats with a barge full of chest. They couldn't fight three boats' worth of hired muscle with one shotgun, Marisol's flare pistol, and whatever Teo could improvise. So they'd do what the river did when it didn't want to be crossed.

They'd disappear.

"There's a side channel," Marisol said, crouching over her hand-drawn chart, the one her grandfather had inked on butcher paper. "Half a kilometer up. It braids out through the reed beds and comes back to the main river below the old ferry crossing. In high water it's suicide. Right now, with the river this low—" she tapped a snaking line, "—it's maybe forty centimeters deep in places. The barge will scrape bottom the whole way. But so will anything bigger than a canoe."

"And the syndicate knows these waters?"

"Some of them do. Not better than my family does."

Teo raised his hand, which made everyone look at him, which was the point. "Question from the kid. What do we do when they just... follow us into the reeds?"

Marisol smiled, and it was not a nice smile. "Then you're going to earn your keep, *milagrito*."

*

The barge groaned into the side channel just as the sun cleared the eastern rim of the canyon, and the sound it made dragging over the shallows was like a whale being personally insulted. Teo stood at the stern with San Jorge hovering overhead, feeding him a live view of the main channel behind them.

"They've stopped at the mouth," he reported. "Two guys got out. They're looking at the water. One of them is pointing at our wake."

"Of course they are," Dominic muttered. He was poling alongside the barge in waist-deep water, which meant he was cold, wet, and pretending to be fine about both. "Anneli, how much farther to where the channel rejoins?"

"If my map's right, six kilometers. If Marisol's map is right, five. If Marisol's grandfather's map is right, four and a half, but there's a sandbar where he wrote *no pasar* in letters I can feel through the paper."

"*No pasar* means don't pass," Teo helpfully supplied.

"Thank you, Teo."

"Anytime."

The reeds closed over them like a green cathedral. It should have been beautiful — herons lifting off in slow flaps, dragonflies stitching the air, light coming down gold through the stalks — but Teo couldn't enjoy any of it because San Jorge's camera kept showing him the three boats entering the channel mouth in single file, and the lead boat was moving faster than theirs could ever dream of.

"Contact," he said. "They're in. Lead boat's doing maybe fifteen knots."

"Fifteen knots in this?" Marisol called from the bow. "They'll ground out inside a kilometer."

The lead boat grounded out inside eight hundred meters.

Teo actually cheered, then immediately regretted it because the men in the second boat didn't ground out. They cut their engine, jumped into the shallows, and started wading — fast, angled, cutting across a gravel bar toward the point where the channel bent and the barge would pass within thirty meters of open water.

"Second boat's beached! Four guys on foot, coming along the bar, they'll reach the bend before we do!"

Dominic hauled himself aboard, dripping. "Options."

"Flares," said Marisol.

"That's not an option, that's a fire hazard."

"A *distracting* fire hazard."

Teo looked at his controller. Looked at the gravel bar. Looked at the supply crates lashed midships — the diesel cans, the propane cylinder for cooking, the crate of signal flares Marisol had bought off a retired coast guard man who'd charged her double and thrown in a hug for free.

"Actually," Teo said slowly, "I have an idea, and you're going to hate it."

"I already hate it," said Dominic. "Go."

*

Here is what Teo had learned in three years of flying drones over rivers, canyons, wedding parties, and one disastrous quinceañera: people will always, always watch the thing that's watching them. Point a camera at a crowd and every face turns up. It was practically a law of nature. Men with guns were no different — worse, actually, because men with guns had been trained to treat drones as threats, and threats deserved attention.

So while the barge scraped its slow way around the bend, Teo flew San Jorge low and loud directly at the four men on the gravel bar, banking it in wide aggressive arcs, buzzing them like the world's most expensive horsefly. And they watched it. All four of them, rifles half-raised, faces tilted up, walking backwards away from the waterline because that's what trained men did when something unknown circled them — they gave it space.

Which left their backs to the river.

"Now," Teo said, and Marisol fired the flare pistol.

Not at the men. At the reeds.

The dry-season reeds went up like the curtain at the end of a very bad play. Flame roared thirty feet into the morning air with a sound like applause from God, and the four men on the bar turned around to find themselves between a burning wall and a barge full of strangers, and they did the sensible thing, which was to run the other way, which put them exactly where Marisol wanted them: splashing into the shallows, dropping their rifles into knee-deep water, scrambling back toward their beached boat.

"San Jorge, come home," Teo said, hands shaking only a little. "Good boy. Good saint."

The barge slid past the bend, past the burning reeds, past the syndicate men floundering in the shallows shouting into radios, and Teo allowed himself exactly four seconds of feeling like a hero.

That was when the third boat appeared ahead of them.

It hadn't gone into the channel at all. It had run the main river, fast and clean, and it was sitting squarely at the confluence where the side channel rejoined — right where Marisol's grandfather's map said the water got deep again and their options ran out. A man stood on its bow with a megaphone, which Teo thought was almost charmingly old-fashioned until the man spoke.

"Barge crew!" The voice boomed off the canyon walls. "This is the Cóndor Salvage Company. You're carrying property that doesn't belong to you. Cut your engine and drift in, and nobody gets hurt!"

Nobody said anything on the barge. The fire crackled behind them. The chest sat under its tarp in the middle of everything, patient as a sleeping animal.

"Cóndor," Marisol spat. "Vultures with a marketing budget."

"We can't go forward," Anneli said quietly. She had her arms wrapped around herself, and she wasn't looking at the boat — she was looking at the map, at the *no pasar*, at the sandbar. "We can't go back. And we can't fight them."

"No," Dominic agreed. His eyes had gone somewhere flat and calculating that Teo had seen once before, the night the flash flood nearly took them, the night Dominic read the water like a page. "But we can sink."

Everyone turned to look at him.

"Not us. The barge." He was already moving, pulling the knife from his belt, dropping to his knees beside the hull. "She's taking water anyway — listen to her. We scuttle her here, in the deep channel, with the chest lashed to the buoyancy bags. She goes down in four meters of water. Cóndor can't lift her out — they don't have the equipment, and even if they did, it takes hours, and by then we're gone overland with everything that matters."

"Overland," Marisol repeated. "Across the plateau. With half our supplies going down with the ship."

"With *half* our supplies," Dominic said. "That's why we choose now, before they choose for us. Teo — the buoyancy bags. Can you rig them to float free when she settles?"

Teo swallowed. "If I tie them off with slip lines, yeah. Chest comes up with the bags, barge stays down. I'd need two minutes."

"You have ninety seconds, because they're coming alongside."

It was true. The Cóndor boat was moving, easing toward them with the smooth confidence of people who believed the outcome was already decided. Through the megaphone, the man's voice had shifted from official to friendly, which was somehow worse: "Last chance, friends. Whatever they're paying you, we'll double it."

"Everybody lies about money," Marisol muttered, and cut the lashings on the supply crates.

What followed was the fastest ninety seconds of Teo's life. He knelt on the wet deck with his fingers cramping in the cold, tying slip knots he'd learned from a YouTube video titled *Knots for Absolute Idiots (Part 3)*, while around him the others threw overboard everything they couldn't carry — the spare fuel, most of the food, the cook stove, the tent, Marisol's entire collection of paperback novels, which she surrendered with the expression of a woman handing over a child. Anneli packed the documents they'd separated from the chest into a dry bag no bigger than a pillowcase. Dominic lashed the chest itself to the buoyancy bags with a speed that suggested he'd done salvage work before and hadn't enjoyed it.

"On three!" Dominic shouted. "One—"

The Cóndor boat hooked alongside, and a grapnel line sailed over their rail.

"—three!"

They cut her loose and jumped.

The water hit Teo like a fist made of ice. He came up gasping, grabbed the buoyancy bag rope as the barge rolled, sighed out its last breath of air with a long gurgling moan, and slid under the brown water with the dignity of a dowager fainting onto a chaise. The chest bobbed once, twice, and settled — visible, tantalizingly, as a dark rectangle four feet beneath the surface, wrapped in orange bags like a present nobody could unwrap.

Teo hauled himself onto the gravel shoal beside the others, coughing, and looked back.

The Cóndor men were leaning over their rails, staring down at the sunken barge, arguing. One of them was already on a radio. The man with the megaphone had lowered it and was watching the four of them — soaked, shivering, standing on a gravel bar with a dry bag, a shotgun, a flare pistol, and nothing else — with an expression Teo decided to interpret as respect.

"He's going to send divers," Anneli said.

"It'll take him an hour to rig for it," Dominic said. "Maybe two. By then—" He looked east, at the plateau rising beyond the canyon rim, brown and endless and utterly indifferent to all of them. "By then we need to be a very long way from here."

They climbed. It took the rest of the day. Teo carried El Milagro against his chest the whole way, wrapped in his shirt to keep the grit out of her motors, and if anyone noticed that he treated the drone more tenderly than his own blistered feet, nobody said anything, because everybody had lost something on that barge and grief makes people polite.

They camped that night on the first shelf of the plateau, under a sky so crowded with stars it looked like it was showing off. No tent. No stove. Half a bag of food between four people. Marisol divided it with the grim precision of a surgeon, and when Teo complained that his portion was smaller than Anneli's, she pointed out that Anneli was taller, and that fairness was a luxury item currently sitting at the bottom of a river.

But here was the thing Teo kept turning over as he lay on the cold ground with his arms around his knees: they had done it. All four of them, together, without a plan that survived contact and without anyone telling anyone else what to do — well, mostly Dominic told everyone what to do, but it had *felt* collaborative. When the grapnel came over the rail, nobody had hesitated. Nobody had looked at the chest and then looked at the Cóndor boat and done the math out loud.

Even Anneli, who was probably a government spy, had jumped.

"You did good today," Dominic said quietly, from somewhere in the dark. "The drone. The reeds. The knots."

"Knots for Absolute Idiots," Teo said. "Part three."

"Whatever works." A pause. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we walk."

Teo closed his eyes. Somewhere below them, in the black water of the channel, a barge full of Cóndor's ambitions sat on the riverbed, and above them the stars wheeled on, indifferent and generous, and Teo thought: *we lost everything comfortable, and somehow I feel less unlucky than I did last week.*

He was almost asleep when El Milagro — propped on a rock beside him, charging off the little solar panel — buzzed once with a motion alert from her idle camera.

He checked it because he was seventeen and incapable of not checking it.

On the screen, far below on the plain, headlights. Not following their trail. Running parallel to it, steady and patient, three vehicles moving north along the old coast road.

Moving toward the pass. Their pass. The only way home.

"Um," Teo said, into the dark. "Guys? You're going to want to see this."
