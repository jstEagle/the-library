## 12. Dry Side

The river gave us up on a Tuesday, or what Teo insisted was a Tuesday, though none of us had kept track of days since the flash flood took the barge and half our food and all of Marisol's patience.

We beached the raft in a stand of dead coihue trees that looked like they'd been waiting a century for someone foolish enough to arrive. Beyond them, the land simply stopped being river country. The plateau began—flat, rust-colored, stitched with thorn scrub and the bones of old fences—and it ran west toward the coast road for something like ninety kilometers as the condor flew, which was roughly twice that as the exhausted man dragged a chest through it.

We built a travois out of the raft's frame and two wheels salvaged from the syndicate's abandoned quad bike, which had been abandoned because its owner had made the mistake of underestimating us, and I intended to honor his sacrifice by using every part of it.

"Two hundred liters of water," Marisol announced, standing over our remaining supplies with her hands on her hips like a general reviewing troops she already distrusted. "Four people. Ninety kilometers. In this."

She gestured at the sky, which was doing its best impression of a white-hot anvil.

"Sixty liters is survival rationing," Anneli said. She'd done the math already; she always did the math first, quietly, in the margins of her notebook. "Two liters a day per person for drinking, plus a liter shared for cooking. We can stretch it to five days if we walk long hours and rest at midday."

"And if we're slow?" Teo asked.

"Then we learn new things about thirst," Marisol said.

I crouched by the chest. It sat there in the dirt, patient as a bank manager, its double-sun seal facing up at the indifferent sky. Three hundred years of a nation's proof, and it weighed about as much as my regrets, which is to say: considerably more than I could comfortably carry but not nearly enough to leave behind.

"We rig it on the travois," I said. "Two pullers, one pusher, rotate every hour. We walk at dawn and dusk, we sleep through the worst heat, and nobody drinks like they mean it."

"Nobody ever drinks like they mean it," Teo said mournfully. "That's the whole tragedy of rationing."

* * *

The first day was almost pleasant, in the way that the opening miles of anything are pleasant, before the thing reveals its true nature.

The plateau had a rhythm to it. Thorn scrub, then gravel, then a dry creek bed running north-south like a scar, then thorn scrub again. The wind came out of the west carrying the smell of salt and distance, and Teo flew his last functioning drone in wide lazy circles above us, calling out terrain like a sports commentator.

"Flat for two klicks! Then flat! After that, some flat!"

"Fascinating," Marisol said.

"There's a rock."

"That's not a rock," Anneli said, peering through her binoculars. "That's a guanaco."

"It's standing very still."

"They do that. It's how they judge people."

The guanaco judged us and found us wanting, and trotted off with the easy insolence of an animal that has never once worried about debt.

I pulled the travois with Marisol for the first hour. The chest rode between us, bumping softly over the gravel, and I found myself talking to it without meaning to. Not words, exactly. More like the way you acknowledge a heavy passenger—little shifts of apology when the wheel caught, small grunts of solidarity on the rises.

"You know he does that," Marisol said.

"Does what?"

"Talks to it. You've been muttering at that chest since we raised it."

"I have not."

"'Almost there, sweetheart,'" she said, in a frankly devastating impression of my voice. "'Easy over this one.'"

"That was strategic communication with the cargo."

"That was courtship."

I let it go, because arguing with Marisol while hauling three hundred pounds across a desert is a losing proposition in every sense, and also because she wasn't entirely wrong, and neither of us wanted to examine what that meant about me.

At noon we stopped in the shadow of the travois itself, which cast a shadow approximately the width of a beach towel, and we four grown adults arranged ourselves around it like survivors of a shipwreck huddling under a postcard. Anneli measured out water with the gravity of a pharmacist dispensing morphine. A liter bottle cap, filled twice each. Teo drank his in one go and then held the empty cap upside down over his mouth for a full ten seconds, hoping for rain.

"It's a desert, Teo."

"I'm aware. I'm being optimistic. It's called resilience."

* * *

The second day, the desert started working.

Not dramatically. That's the thing about the dry side that no one warns you about—it doesn't attack you. It just subtracts. It takes the moisture out of your lips, then your temper, then your sentences, until by afternoon you're communicating in gestures and single syllables, and everyone looks beautiful to everyone else purely because they are standing near water.

My logbook entry from that evening, reconstructed later from smears:

*Day 2. Wind W 30 gusting 45. Temp 34°C. Water down 40L. Everyone hates everyone. Normal.*

The truth was slightly warmer than that. Marisol had gotten sunburned along the part in her hair because she refused hats on principle—"hats are for people who've given up"—and Anneli had silently walked beside her for an hour holding her own hat out like an offering until Marisol snatched it with a wordless snarl that both of them understood to be gratitude.

Teo had discovered that if you licked your finger and held it up, you could feel the wind shift twenty minutes before it arrived, and he would not stop announcing it. "Shift coming! West-northwest! Mark my words!" He was right every time, which somehow made it worse.

And Ilsa Rehn was still back there.

We'd seen the dust plume at midday, hanging on the southern horizon like a question nobody wanted to answer. Through the binoculars: a single vehicle, keeping pace, never closing. Two kilometers back, then three, then two again. Patient as interest accruing.

"She's not even trying to catch us," I said.

"Why would she?" Anneli said. She didn't look up from her notebook. "She has a vehicle, fuel, and full water. We have a travois and optimism. She only needs to follow us until the desert finishes her work. Then she collects the chest and drives home."

"Cheerful."

"I'm an archivist. Accuracy is my love language."

But here's what I noticed, watching that dust plume while pretending to check the wheel bearings: she never came closer than two kilometers, and she never fell further behind than five. That's not pursuit. Pursuit closes distance or loses it. That's escort. That's someone making sure we don't turn north or south, herding us along the one line she wants us to walk—the line that leads to the coast road, where her employers' larger arrangements wait.

Or it's someone who wants us to think so.

Ten years of storm chasing teaches you one transferable skill above all others: reading a system's behavior instead of its appearance. A cell that looks calm on radar and keeps perfect formation is either weak or lying, and you don't find out which until you're inside it.

I didn't say any of this to the crew. There's a limit to how much dread a group can carry on top of a chest, and I'd appointed myself the mule for that particular load years ago. It's the one job I've never rotated.

* * *

The third night was the cold one.

Everyone imagines deserts as hot, and they are, ferociously, between ten and four. But the dry side of Patagonia gives the heat back the moment the sun leaves, the way a bad debtor returns nothing but the envelope. By nine o'clock the temperature had fallen fifteen degrees. By midnight our breath was fogging and the thorn scrub crackled with frost, and we huddled around a fire the size of a cupped hand because bigger fires show up on horizons, and horizons were currently occupied.

Teo's teeth were chattering in a rhythm, and being Teo, he'd turned it into percussion.

"Stop that," Marisol said.

"I'm cold."

"So am I. Stop being cold *rhythmically.*"

Anneli had the watch, and I lay with my bedroll open to the stars listening to her move around the camp—her footsteps were precise, evenly spaced, an archivist's footsteps, cataloguing the dark. Around two she crouched next to me.

"You're awake."

"I'm resting my eyes strategically."

"The vehicle stopped moving an hour ago. Four kilometers back." She paused. "It hasn't moved since."

I sat up. "Out of fuel?"

"Maybe. Or she's decided she no longer needs to follow us." Anneli fed a twig to the fire. Her face did something complicated in the light. "Dominic, if I told you I knew why she'd stop following us, and that the reason was worse than her following us—would you want to hear it tonight, or in the morning?"

I looked at her for a long moment. Frost formed on the chest's iron bands, and the double-sun seal caught the firelight like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"In the morning," I said. "Tonight I'm just a man sleeping next to his friends in the cold. Let me keep it another six hours."

She nodded, and I understood that this was itself a kind of gift she was giving me, and that she was paying for it, and that whatever she knew had a price tag I hadn't seen yet.

I slept anyway. Exhaustion is a mercy that way. It doesn't care what's coming; it clocks out on schedule.

* * *

The fourth day, the water math got honest.

Forty-one liters left, by Anneli's count, and sixty kilometers to go, and the middle third of the route crossed a stretch of plateau with no shade taller than a rabbit. We walked the dawn hours in silence, because silence conserves everything—water, breath, goodwill. Even Teo had gone quiet, which frightened me more than the dust plume ever had.

Around midmorning we found the well.

It wasn't marked. It was a ring of stones older than the fences, with a wooden cover rotted to lace, and Marisol saw it first and made a sound I'd never heard from her—not a word, not quite a cry, something between. She dropped the travois rope and walked to it and stood there with her hand flat on the stones like she was taking a pulse.

"My grandfather's route," she said. "He ran this track. He told me once—when I was small, I thought it was a story—that there was water every thirty kilometers if you knew the old names for the places." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet, which out here felt almost obscene, spending moisture like that. "The old names, Dominic. Valdorian names. He was singing maps to me my whole childhood and I thought they were lullabies."

We drew water with a bucket Anneli rigged from a tarp scrap and a length of rope. It came up cloudy and tasted of stone and iron, and it was the best thing I have ever drunk, including champagne at the Voss brothers' table, which had been excellent and had tasted like a trap.

We filled everything. We drank like we meant it, finally, all four of us, heads down at the well rim like horses, and nobody rationed anybody, and for about ten minutes the desert lost.

Then Teo, wiping his mouth, said: "So if there's a well every thirty klicks on the old route... whoever built them expected travelers. Regular ones. For a long time."

Nobody answered him. Nobody had to. The plateau was suddenly full of ghosts, and all of them seemed to be pointing west, and Ilsa Rehn's vehicle sat motionless four kilometers behind us, and I found myself wondering—with the specific professional unease of a man who reads weather for a living—what kind of system goes quiet right before it changes everything.

* * *

That night, Anneli didn't wait for morning.

She sat down across the fire from me while the other two slept, and she held her notebook against her chest like a shield, and she said, "Tomorrow, when we reach the ridge above the coast road, I need you to trust me for about twenty minutes without asking questions."

"Anneli."

"I know."

"Do you understand that 'trust me for twenty minutes' is exactly what a government archivist says right before the government arrives?"

"Yes," she said. "And I understand that I'm about to burn my own credentials to make sure it isn't. But I couldn't sleep knowing you might bolt at the ridge, so I'm telling you now, badly, in advance, so you can decide whether to tie me up tonight." She almost smiled. "Marisol has rope."

I looked at her across the coals—this woman who had deleted files and confessed betrayals and carried our water math and never once complained—and I realized I'd already made the decision somewhere around the well, or maybe around the night she offered to help us move, or maybe, if I was honest, the day she signed on to a salvage crew of the unlucky with funding nobody could explain.

"What happens in the twenty minutes?" I asked.

She opened the notebook, tore out a page, and held it toward the fire without letting it go.

"Something that should have happened eighty years ago," she said. "And after it does, Ilsa Rehn won't be following us anymore. She'll be running—from us, or toward us, I genuinely don't know which. Either way, Dominic, we need to be off this plateau by noon."

Beyond the firelight, the frost was forming on the chest again, and the old stones of the well stood black against the stars, and somewhere west, past sixty kilometers of thorn and gravel, the coast road waited with whatever the Voss brothers had arranged to be waiting on it.

I reached out and took the page from her hand before she could burn it alone.

"Show me first," I said. "Then we burn it together."
