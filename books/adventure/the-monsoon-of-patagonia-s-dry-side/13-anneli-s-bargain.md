## 13. Anneli's Bargain

The satellite phone woke Anneli Brandt at four in the morning, which was how she knew it was official business. Nobody called her at four in the morning for anything kind.

She had pitched her tent forty meters from the others—professional habit, she told herself, though honestly it was because Teo snored like a small landslide and Dominic talked in his sleep in three languages, none of them coherently. She crawled out into the cold, wrapped herself in the expedition's one good blanket, and answered on the fourth ring, giving her voice time to sound awake and unbothered.

"Brandt."

"Dr. Brandt." The voice was Director Halvorsen's, or rather the version of Director Halvorsen that existed on encrypted channels: flatter, colder, stripped of the avuncular warmth he wore at conferences. "You're two days behind schedule."

"The terrain is two days behind schedule," Anneli said. "I'm merely accompanying it."

A pause. On the encrypted channel, pauses were weapons. She had been trained to fill them. She had also, over the past week, been quietly learning not to.

"Confirm the chest is intact," Halvorsen said. "Then you'll do the rest tonight."

*The rest.* The phrase sat in the transmission like a stone in a shoe. They never said *destroy the documents*. They said *render the materials administratively inert*. They said *manage the narrative*. Tonight they had apparently gotten tired of euphemism.

"Define 'the rest,'" she said, though she knew. She had known since the day her funding appeared—overnight, uncommented upon, attached to an expedition she hadn't applied to join. Archives did not work that way. Archives were chronically, pathologically broke. Money that arrived without asking was not money; it was a leash.

"You know what it is. You've read the preliminary inventory." A rustle of paper on his end, absurdly domestic, as if he were shuffling grocery lists between orders. "Treaties. The purchase instruments. If those reach any court, any journalist, any descendant with a lawyer and a grudge, we spend thirty years in litigation and the ministry spends its credibility. The chest goes into the river or the fire. Your choice of method. Report completion by 0600 tomorrow."

"And the crew?"

"What about them?"

"Halvorsen." She used his name without the title deliberately, a small knife. "Four people are going to watch me burn the most significant archival discovery of the century. What happens to them?"

Another pause, longer this time. When he spoke again, the flatness had developed a fine crack of something that might have been regret, or might have been indigestion.

"They'll be compensated. Or they'll be managed. That's above your clearance now, Doctor. Congratulations, incidentally. Most archivists go their whole careers without being trusted with this."

*Trusted.* She looked out across the dark plateau, where the wind was combing the grass flat, and where, in the tent nearest hers, Dominic Ishikawa was presumably dreaming badly about interest rates.

"I'll need until tomorrow night," she said. "There's a river crossing. The documents are water-damaged; burning wet paper is inefficient." It was even true. She was getting better at building truths out of true parts.

"Tomorrow night," Halvorsen agreed. "Brandt—you've done good work for us. Don't make this complicated."

He hung up before she could answer, which was considerate of him, because what she would have said was: *It was already complicated. You made it so eleven years ago when you hired a homesick graduate student and taught her that truth was a filing problem.*

She sat with the phone in her lap for a long time. Then she opened her field kit, took out her credentials folder—the laminated archive identification, the authorization letters, the little card that made her, on paper, exactly what she claimed to be—and held it over the camp stove burner until the lamination curled and browned and let go of itself.

Burning credentials in the dark while everyone slept. It felt less like rebellion than like taking off a coat she'd forgotten she was wearing. Underneath, apparently, was just a woman who was very cold and very tired and had no idea what she was anymore.

She fed the ashes to the wind and went back to bed, where she lay awake composing lies with excellent posture.

* * *

The lie went out at dawn, typed carefully on her laptop and queued through the relay she'd used a dozen times without guilt: *Chest located, contents confirmed. Documents severely degraded by immersion. Controlled burn scheduled 2100 local at grid reference—*

And here she paused, pen hovering metaphorically, and gave her handlers coordinates that were real in every respect except the important one: nine kilometers south of their actual position, in a box canyon with no road access and, according to Marisol's muttered commentary the previous evening, a resident population of very territorial guanacos. Let the government's retrieval team spend a night arguing with camelids. It wasn't sabotage. It was scheduling.

She hit send and felt nothing dramatic at all. That was the strange part. Eleven years of careful, invisible service, and the moment of treason registered in her body as roughly equivalent to declining a second helping of potatoes.

Teo found her at breakfast, which meant Teo found her crouched over the stove rehydrating noodles with the focused intensity of someone defusing a bomb.

"You look weird," he announced, dropping beside her with his drone controller and a mouthful of crackers.

"I always look weird. I'm an archivist. We cultivate it."

"No, weirder. You've got your *deciding something* face. I've seen it. You get a little line—" he pointed at his own forehead, "—right there, like the paperwork disagrees with you."

Anneli touched her forehead. There was, in fact, a line there. It had been deepening all week.

"Eat your crackers, Teo."

"Marisol says we should be worried about you," he continued, with the tactless honesty of the young, which was either a genuine warning or Marisol's way of starting a conversation she didn't want to have directly. "She says government people don't fund expeditions out of love."

"Marisol is right. Government people fund expeditions out of fear." Anneli stirred her noodles. "The question I keep failing to answer is whether I'm more afraid of them or for them."

Teo considered this with his mouth full. "That's not a real question. That's a question-shaped thing people say when they already know."

She looked at him—sixteen years old, wanted by two different creditors' offices in two different countries, flying a drone worth more than his life insurance (of which he had none) over the bones of a drowned nation—and thought: *when did the teenager become the wisest person on this expedition.*

"Don't tell anyone I said this," she said, "but you should write that down somewhere. It's better than most of my dissertation."

* * *

She found Dominic at midday, checking the straps on the chest's carrying harness with the grim tenderness of a man adjusting a seatbelt for a passenger he resented but respected.

"We need to talk," she said. "Not here."

He glanced up. Whatever he saw in her face made him nod without argument, which she was learning was characteristic: Dominic saved his arguments for things worth arguing about, and mostly they turned out to be fewer than his creditors hoped.

They walked out along the dry wash, far enough from camp that the wind owned their words. Anneli stopped where a shelf of rock gave them a view of the whole gray plateau, and she took a breath, and discovered that eleven years of training had not prepared a single sentence for this moment.

"I lied to you," she said finally. "From the beginning. Not about everything—I really am an archivist, the degrees are real, the dissertation on colonial-era river settlements is real and boring enough to use as anesthesia—but about why I'm here."

Dominic kept his eyes on the horizon. "You were sent to make sure the chest disappears."

It was her turn to be surprised. "How long have you known?"

"Since the fuel stop. Ilsa Rehn knew our route too well, and the only person who's had our route memorized since day one is you." He shrugged. "Also you delete files like someone who wants to be seen deleting files. Teo's drones log everything, by the way. He doesn't know what half the metadata means yet, but I do."

Anneli laughed—a short, helpless sound. "Of course he does. Of course you do. I've spent eleven years being the most careful person in every room, and I get undone by a teenager's backup habits."

"To be fair, he's very annoying about backups." Dominic finally turned to face her, and his expression held no anger, which somehow was worse. "So. Are you here to tell me you're going through with it?"

"No." She said it fast, before her nerve went. "No. I burned my credentials last night. I sent my handlers false coordinates—they think the destruction happens tomorrow night, nine kilometers south of here, in a canyon full of angry guanacos. As of 0400 this morning I am unemployed, stateless in the professional sense, and almost certainly on some list." She spread her hands. "This is me defecting. I'd have prepared a speech, but I ran out of masters to rehearse it for."

Dominic was quiet for a moment. The wind moved through the grass with its long dry whispering, the sound the whole plateau made, all day, like the country clearing its throat before saying something.

"Why?" he asked. Not suspiciously. Genuinely. As if the answer mattered to him beyond tactics.

And that—that was the question that undid her, because no handler had ever asked it. Handlers asked *what*, *when*, *how deniable*. Nobody in eleven years had asked her *why*.

So she told him the truth, all of it, standing in the wind.

"When I was doing my doctorate, I found a fragment. A shipping manifest from the 1890s listing a port city called Valdoria—tonnage figures, customs duties, a population. Real numbers. A real place. And it appears nowhere else. No maps, no treaties, no successor-state records. I brought it to my supervisor, my supervisor brought it to the ministry, and six months later I had funding I'd never applied for and a career I never chose." She hugged her arms against the wind. "I told myself for years that I was protecting the documents. Stabilizing the collection. Responsible custody. And then I watched your divers open that chest, Dominic, and I saw the letters inside—not the treaties, the *letters*, the ones from ordinary people, a schoolteacher writing to her sister about the price of flour in a city that officially never existed—and I understood what I'd actually been doing for eleven years."

"Which was?"

"Sitting on a grave and calling it an archive." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "But here's the part Halvorsen didn't send me to find out, and the part you need to understand, because it changes everything about what that chest is worth. The erasure didn't just cover land. Valdoria wasn't conquered—it was *purchased*. Article Fourteen of the treaty in that chest transfers sovereignty, yes, but it also transfers *persons*. The population was legally reclassified as assets of the purchasing power. Their nationality was dissolved by clause. Anyone descended from Valdorian citizens—anyone who can trace their family to that valley—is, in the eyes of every government currently recognizing that treaty, legally nobody. Stateless. Not citizens of the successor state. Not refugees, because there was no war to flee. Just—unwritten."

Dominic had gone very still. "Marisol," he said quietly.

"Her grandmother sang songs about a city that went under the water twice. Songs aren't evidence in any court. But bloodlines are, if the right document survives to anchor them." Anneli nodded toward camp, toward the chest. "The family records in there—the letters, the parish registers, the citizenship rolls—are the only proof on earth that those people existed as *people*, under their own flag, with legal standing. Burn the treaties and the ledgers and you destroy the leverage. Destroy the leverage and the treaty can't be enforced or weaponized. But the family records? Those are the only thing in that chest that isn't money or power. They're just *true*."

"And your handlers want all of it gone."

"My handlers want the *possibility* of trouble gone. All of it. Including, eventually, I suspect, the descendants—who are much easier to ignore while they have no papers." She met his eyes. "I'm telling you this knowing you owe the Voss brothers more than you'll earn in ten years, and knowing that chest is the single most valuable object either of us will ever stand next to. I'm telling you because I have no safety net left, and because you're the only person I know whose debts are worse than mine, and I figured—if anyone understands choosing the cliff over the cage, it's you."

Dominic looked out at the plateau for a long moment. When he spoke, there was something in his voice she hadn't heard before—not the competent weariness, not the dread. Something drier and more alive.

"Guanacos," he said.

"Pardon?"

"Your false coordinates. You put the government retrieval team in a canyon with territorial guanacos." The corner of his mouth moved. "Eleven years of spy craft, and your act of rebellion involves camelid harassment."

"It was efficient," Anneli said with dignity. "The canyon has no road access. The guanacos were a bonus. I contain multitudes."

He laughed then, actually laughed, and the sound seemed to surprise him, like finding money in an old coat. Then he sobered and looked at her with an expression she suspected he rarely spent on anyone: plain, unguarded, weighing.

"If we run with this," he said, "there's no version where you go back. You understand that. Rehn will figure out the coordinates were false within a day. Halvorsen will disown you publicly and hunt you privately. You'll be a footnote in other people's courage."

"I've been a footnote my whole career," Anneli said. "I'd like to try being a page."

Dominic studied her a moment longer, then stuck out his hand—an odd, formal gesture, out of place on a windswept plateau, and completely sincere.

"Welcome to the crew of the unlucky," he said. "Fair warning: we vote on things. It's infuriating."

"So I've heard. Teo told me about the rationing vote. He's still bitter about the coffee."

"The coffee was a strategic resource."

"It was *terrible*, Dominic. Strategic terribleness is still terrible."

They walked back toward camp together, and Anneli noticed that her hands had finally stopped shaking—or perhaps they'd started, honestly, for the first time in years, and she simply couldn't tell the difference anymore.

Behind them, far to the south, a smudge of cloud was building over the mountains, earlier in the season than any cloud had a right to be. Neither of them saw it yet.

But the plateau did. The whole dry side of Patagonia had begun, very quietly, to hold its breath.
