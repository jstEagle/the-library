## 14. The Pass Closes Early

The first sign was the birds.

Marisol noticed them before anyone else—she always had, a habit from a childhood spent reading her grandmother's moods in the behavior of animals. The bandurrias had gone quiet and were moving south in tight, purposeful lines, like commuters who'd heard a rumor about the trains. Guanacos were drifting down off the high ground toward the river flats, unhurried but definite about it.

"Teo," she called across the camp. "When did you last check the forecast?"

"I checked it this morning." He didn't look up from his drone case, which he was cleaning with the devotion other boys reserved for sports cars. "It said clear for five days."

"When did you check the *birds*?"

He looked up. He followed her finger to the sky. To his credit—and Teo's credit was mostly made of moments exactly like this—he stopped cleaning immediately.

"That's... a lot of birds going one direction."

"That's a lot of birds going one direction with luggage."

Dominic came over wiping his hands on a rag, Anneli a step behind him with her ever-present notebook. They'd been repacking the sledge harnesses, preparing for another day of hauling the chest across the plateau on runners that groaned like an old man standing up. The chest itself sat under a tarp at the center of camp, radiating the particular gravity it had acquired since the night they'd opened it—the gravity of something everyone pretended not to look at too often.

"What have we got?" Dominic asked.

"Storm," Marisol said. "Early. Wrong season, wrong direction, wrong everything. My grandmother would've called it *el huésped sin invitación*—the guest without an invitation."

"And what does your grandmother say we do about uninvited guests?"

"She says you feed them or outrun them. We can't feed this one."

Anneli was already flipping through her weather notes, frowning at them as though they'd personally lied to her. "Every model I ran put the seasonal closure at least two weeks out. The passes don't usually seal until—"

"The passes don't usually do anything 'usually' anymore," Marisol said. "That's the whole point of this century so far. Look west."

They looked west. All morning there had been a cloud bank building along the cordillera, the kind of white, innocent-looking pile that tourists photograph and locals distrust. It had grown while they worked. Now it had a bruised underside, and above it, faint as breath on glass, stretched the long smooth cap of a storm being fed by something very high and very cold.

Dominic did the thing he did when he shifted into his other self—the storm chaser, the man who read skies the way Marisol read rivers. He went still. His eyes went slightly unfocused. Somewhere behind them, she knew, he was doing arithmetic with pressure gradients and temperature differentials, and the answer was making his jaw tighten.

"That's not a squall," he said quietly. "That's a system. Cold air dropping out of the Andes ahead of schedule, meeting whatever moisture is left in the basin. If it sets up over the pass—" He stopped.

"Say it," Anneli said.

"It seals. Snow above three thousand meters, maybe snow lower than that if the cold air pools. And once it seals, it stays sealed until spring. We're talking about a door closing, not a curtain."

Marisol felt the words land in her chest with a weight that had nothing to do with meteorology. Behind them lay the river, the syndicate, the burned barge, half their supplies, and every route they'd already used up. Ahead lay the Puerto Ventana pass—the only road home to the coast, the only way to deliver what remained of the chest to anywhere that mattered. A door. Closing.

"How fast?" she asked.

Dominic looked at the sky again, then at his watch, then at the sky, the way he did when he hated his own answer. "If we push hard today and tomorrow, we might beat it to the top. Might. But we're hauling eight hundred kilos of chest and sledge through broken country, and we're down to four people and one horse."

"We could leave the chest," Anneli said. It wasn't a serious suggestion—it was the kind of thing you said aloud just to hear how terrible it sounded—but everyone flinched anyway, including Anneli herself.

"No," Marisol said, and heard her own voice come out flat and final in a way that surprised her. She thought of the letters inside, the handwriting of people whose names her grandmother had sung into her before she could walk. "We're not leaving it. Not to the snow, not to whoever finds it after."

Nobody argued. That was the strange, hard-won thing about their little crew of the unlucky: arguments happened fast and then ended, because everyone had learned somewhere—in disgrace, in debt, in exile—that time spent arguing was time the world used against you.

* * *

They broke camp in forty minutes, which was a record, and made eleven kilometers by midday, which was not. The plateau fought them the way it always did: not dramatically, but persistently, like a bureaucrat. The wind came in long horizontal shoves. The sledge runners found every buried stone. The horse—a sour, intelligent mare named Constanza whom Marisol privately considered the most competent member of the expedition—pulled with the resigned strength of someone who had been lied to about retirement.

By late afternoon the western sky had gone the color of a healing bruise, and the temperature had dropped enough that their breath hung around them like small ghosts refusing to move on.

Teo flew the drone low ahead of their line, scouting the ground toward the pass road, and it was Teo who saw it first. He landed the drone, walked over to where Marisol was coaxing the sledge around a rock shelf, and had the unusual decency to look uncomfortable.

"So," he said. "Don't panic."

"That is the single least calming sentence in any language."

"The main road's gone. There's a slide—mud and rock, whole hillside let go, probably yesterday when we weren't looking. It's across the valley floor for maybe three hundred meters, and above it the switchbacks are already white. Like, *white* white. First snow sitting on them."

Marisol closed her eyes. She let herself feel, for exactly three seconds, the full luxurious misery of the situation: exhausted, under-supplied, hunted by two separate groups of well-funded predators, and now formally locked out of the only civilized way home. Then she opened her eyes, because three seconds was the family allowance and no more.

"Then we don't take the road," she said.

"There's another road?" Dominic asked, arriving with Constanza in tow.

"There's no road. There's a track." She hesitated. This was the part she had been carrying alone since the chest came out of the river, the part she hadn't yet found the courage to say plainly, because saying it would make real a thing she'd only recently allowed herself to believe. "My grandfather's track."

* * *

That night, camped in the lee of a basalt ridge with the storm chewing visibly at the western horizon, Marisol took out the one object she'd brought from home that wasn't practical: a small notebook bound in cracked green leather, her grandmother's hand filling it in pencil, in the old alphabet—the Valdorian script, the one that appeared in the chest's ledgers and nowhere else in the living world, except here, in a fishing family's kitchen, disguised as lullabies.

She had shown the crew fragments before. Tonight she showed them everything.

"My grandmother sang me to sleep with these," she said. "I thought they were songs. Songs about a city under the water twice, about a merchant who carried salt and secrets, about a road that goes where roads don't go. I sang them on the river my whole life. It wasn't until we opened the chest that I understood—I could *read* the ledgers, Dominic. Do you know why? Because I'd been sounding out this alphabet since I was four years old, thinking it was nonsense syllables my grandmother liked the sound of."

Anneli leaned forward with an expression Marisol recognized from mirrors: the hunger of a scholar meeting primary sources in the wild.

"They're not all songs," Marisol went on. "Some of them are. But some of them are instructions. My grandfather didn't just sing these—he *ran* them. He was a smuggler, back when the border was a suggestion. When the government flooded the valley and moved the maps around, some people never accepted the new geography. They kept the old routes alive. In the family. In the songs, because nobody confiscates a lullaby."

She turned to a page near the end and began to translate aloud, slowly, feeling each phrase settle into place like a key into a lock it had been cut for:

"*Where the condor drinks but the sheep will not, turn your back on the evening sun.* That's a landmark—there's a seep on the north face of the ridge where the water's mineral-heavy; livestock won't touch it. *Count nine bends of the dry creek and sleep under the stone that has a hat.* A hat. A capstone. I used to think that was just a funny image." She laughed, and it came out rougher than she intended. "It's a cairn. He marked his own caches. The songs are a map, drawn in meter so children would carry it without knowing. Every verse is a leg of the route. There's even a verse about where to hide if the *hombres de la oficina* come—the office men. Government men. It rhymes, so you remember it under stress."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Teo said, reverently, "Your grandma was a spymaster."

"My grandma was a tired woman who liked singing to her granddaughter and refused, on principle, to let a republic die just because powerful men said it had." Marisol closed the notebook gently. "There's a difference. Barely."

"And the track," Dominic said. "Does it get us over?"

"It gets us over the way water gets downhill—not straight, not comfortable, but reliably. It stays low longer than the road does, threads through country the surveyors never bothered mapping because there was nothing in it worth taxing. My grandfather's rule was: *the state can only close what the state can see.*" She looked west, at the storm. "If the songs are true—and they've been true every single time I've tested them on this trip—then we can be over the divide before the snow reaches the low passes. After that, the storm becomes our problem instead of our wall."

"Assuming," Anneli said, "that a track abandoned for sixty years still exists."

"Assuming that," Marisol agreed. "But my grandfather maintained it wasn't abandoned. He said a route doesn't die while someone remembers it. He said memory is maintenance." She tapped the green notebook. "This is sixty years of deferred maintenance, due tomorrow."

* * *

They climbed out of camp before dawn, by headlamp and starlight, following the third verse of a song about a merchant who carried salt and secrets.

It was the strangest navigation Marisol had ever done, and she had once piloted a barge through fog using only the sound of her own engine echoing off canyon walls. She would walk fifty meters, stop, hum a bar of melody under her breath to fix the next image in her mind—*where the rock leans like a drunkard, keep his company*—and then find it: a leaning slab of basalt, unmistakable, patient, waiting sixty years for someone to lean on it in return. The landscape and the song fit together with a click she felt in her teeth. Her grandfather had never seen this exact stretch—or had he? Had he walked it as a boy, learning the verses the same way, matching word to stone? She suddenly understood that she was walking inside a conversation between generations, conducted entirely in geography, and that she was the first in her family in sixty years to hear both sides of it.

"You're smiling," Teo observed, trudging beside her with the drone case strapped to his back like a turtle shell.

"I'm hallucinating from exhaustion. Keep walking."

"No, it's a good smile. It's the smile you get when the drone footage comes back better than expected."

Marisol considered this. "Yes. It's exactly that smile."

By midmorning they reached the first cache. Or the place where a cache had been. The capstone with its "hat" still stood above a dry creek bend, exactly nine bends from the condor's seep, and beneath it, wedged in the scree, they found an iron box gone to rust, and inside the box: nothing but a tin cup, a stub of candle, and a strip of oilcloth on which her grandfather's handwriting—his real handwriting, not the song-hand—had faded to ghosts. Marisol held the cup and couldn't speak for a moment.

"He left supplies," she managed finally. "For whoever came after. He always said a route isn't yours, you just borrow it. You leave it ready for the next runner."

"Sixty years ago," Anneli said softly, "he was leaving it for someone. Maybe he hoped it would be you."

"He hoped it would be *someone*. That was the whole religion of it." Marisol put the cup back, replaced the stone, and added, per family custom, a small offering of her own: a cartridge of drone batteries from Teo's case, donated under protest, and one of Anneli's chocolate ration bars, donated with genuine grief. "There. Route maintained. The next runner eats chocolate and charges their equipment. Grandfather would be so confused and so pleased."

Behind them, the storm arrived at the high passes exactly as Dominic had predicted, and they watched it from below with the peculiar gratitude of the nearly-drowned watching rain fall on the far bank. White plumes ripped off the summits. The road they would have taken vanished into the murk. Somewhere up there, the door slammed shut for the season, and every soul who'd bet on beating it lost—except, by the grace of a dead smuggler and a grandmother's bedtime voice, them.

"Any chance our friends are stuck on the wrong side of that?" Teo asked hopefully.

"Ilsa Rehn," Dominic said, "does not get stuck. She gets *delayed*, and she bills for it." But he said it lightly, and for the first time in days he sounded more tired than afraid.

They made camp early in the shelter of the drunkard's rock, and Marisol sang the fourth verse aloud—actually sang it, badly, while Teo filmed her and Anneli transcribed and Dominic stood watch with his binoculars on the white chaos of the pass. It was a walking song, she realized as she sang, built for exactly this: a steady pace, a load on your back, a long way to go. Her grandmother's tempo. Her grandfather's stride.

Near midnight, Dominic shook her awake and handed her the binoculars without a word.

South, far off across the moonlit plateau, a light was moving. One light, steady, patient, keeping pace with them along the contour of the land—not approaching, not falling behind. Just following. The way a debt follows.

"She found the track too," Marisol breathed.

"No," Dominic said quietly. "I don't think she needed to find it. I think she's been waiting to see where it goes."
