## 15. Ilsa's Terms

The wind on the plateau had a way of making every decision feel urgent. It came off the cordillera in long, patient shoves, the kind of pressure that didn't want anything from you except movement, and after nine days of hauling the chest across the dry side, Dominic had started to think of it as a creditor too.

He was checking the truck's rear axle—third time that morning, because the axle was the kind of problem you checked until it went away or you did—when Teo came skidding down the slope with his tablet held out like a live grenade.

"Truck," Teo said. "One truck. Parked at the pass mouth since dawn. Nobody in it."

Dominic straightened up slowly, keeping his hands on the axle housing where they looked busy. "Color?"

"Dust-colored. Everything out here is dust-colored." Teo scrolled. "But there's a second vehicle behind the ridge line, thermal only. Two people sitting very still. Like people who've been told to sit still."

Marisol appeared from behind the water trailer, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime around day four. "Rehn?"

"Could be the syndicate," Dominic said, though he already knew it wasn't. The syndicate drove loud vehicles and made entrances. This was patience. This was furniture arranged in advance.

Anneli emerged last, as she always did, carrying her field case like a woman attending a funeral she'd scheduled herself. "If it's Rehn," she said quietly, "she's had three days to take the chest and hasn't. That means she wants something we haven't offered yet."

"That's what worries me," Marisol said.

Dominic closed the axle panel. "Then let's not keep her waiting. People who wait get creative."

* * *

Ilsa Rehn had set up camp the way she did everything: neatly, without apology, and slightly better than yours. A folding table. A kettle already steaming. Three enamel cups set out, plus a fourth, which she turned upside down when she saw Anneli coming—the small cruelty of it so precise it was almost elegant.

"Dr. Brandt," Ilsa said pleasantly. "We'll skip the part where you pretend you're not here."

"I usually do," Anneli said, and stopped at the edge of the camp anyway, arms folded, a border drawn in body language.

Teo hovered at the perimeter with the drone controller, and Ilsa nodded at him like an aunt acknowledging a child at a wedding. "Still flying, I see. Good. Keep your hands where I can see them, though. Metaphorically."

Marisol stayed by the trucks. She always stayed by the trucks. Dominic had learned that about her: give Marisol an exit and a vehicle, and she could be generous with everything else.

"So," Dominic said, taking the cup she poured him. It was good coffee. Of course it was good coffee. The Voss brothers' money bought good coffee the way other people's money bought groceries. "You've been following us for two hundred kilometers."

"Two hundred and forty," Ilsa said. "You took the long way around the salt flats. Sensible. Ugly country, though. You look terrible, by the way. Everyone does. That's not an insult, it's an observation. Desert does that."

"What do you want, Ilsa?"

She sat back, crossed her ankles, and for a moment just looked at him with something he almost mistook for kindness. That was the thing about Ilsa Rehn that had unsettled him since the fuel stop outside Puerto Deseado: she never performed menace. She performed *friendliness*, and somehow that was worse, because friendliness could be meant, and menace was just a job.

"The Vosses sent me to collect a debt," she said. "That was the original assignment. Truck, gear, kneecaps—you remember the menu. But my employers are adaptable men. Cheerful men. You've met them. They laugh easily."

"They laugh at funerals," Dominic said.

"They laugh *especially* at funerals. It's how they know things went well." She sipped her own coffee. "Here's what changed. When you pulled that chest out of the river, you didn't just find salvage. You found a question. And the Vosses have spent their whole lives learning that questions are worth more than answers."

Dominic kept his face still. Behind him, he heard Teo shift his weight, and Marisol's boots scrape closer to the truck bed.

"Explain it to me like I'm slow," he said.

"You're not slow. That's why they like you." Ilsa set her cup down. "Everyone assumes the chest is treasure. Treaties, ledgers, provenance—a nation's death certificate, signed and stamped. Sell it to a museum, sell it to a collector, sell it to the government that buried Valdoria and would pay handsomely to keep it buried. All of that is money. Money is fine. Money is *boring*." She leaned forward. "What the Vosses want is the chest itself. Intact. Every page. Delivered to them, unopened by anyone else, before anyone else can open it. Because a document like that isn't worth what it says. It's worth what it lets you *do* to the people who don't want it read."

"Leverage," Dominic said.

"Now you're caught up." She smiled, warm as the kettle steam. "A government spent sixty years pretending a country never existed. There are people alive today—stateless, landless, legally ghosts—who were erased by a signature. If the Vosses hold the proof of that signature, they don't need to blackmail anyone. They just need to be *seen holding it*. Ministers will come to them. Quietly, hat in hand. That's not extortion, Dominic. That's *hospitality*. You'd be amazed what governments offer when you own the receipt for their worst sin."

The wind pushed at the tent flap. Somewhere behind the ridge, the second vehicle's occupants shifted, thermal ghosts deciding whether to become real ones.

"And my debt?" Dominic asked. His voice came out level, which surprised him, because inside his chest something had gone very quiet and very bright, the way it used to go before a storm cell dropped.

"Gone." Ilsa spread her hands. "All of it. Eleven years of interest, the truck, the gear, the—well, the other arrangements. Wiped. Not deferred. *Wiped*. You walk away owing nothing to anyone. You could drive to the coast tomorrow and start over with a clean slate and a full tank." She tilted her head. "When was the last time someone offered you that?"

He thought about it honestly, because lying to Ilsa Rehn seemed both pointless and rude. Ten years. Ten years of logging wind speeds in countries where he knew nobody's name past the length of a chase, ten years of forwarding addresses that were just post office boxes, ten years of the phone ringing with the Vosses' cheerful voices on the other end, patient as weather. He couldn't remember the last time he'd owed nothing. He wasn't sure there had been a last time.

"That's the trap," he said finally. "It's too easy."

"It's not a trap. It's a transaction." Ilsa refilled his cup without asking, which was its own kind of message. "You think I enjoy this? Following decent people through a desert? I'd rather close this file today. Hand over the chest, and everyone drives home rich in the only currency that matters, which is *finished*."

Behind him, Marisol spoke for the first time. "And the people in the chest? The ones who are still legally ghosts?"

Ilsa didn't turn around. "They stay exactly as ghostly as they were. Nothing changes for them. Nothing changes for anyone, except you four, who stop suffering." She paused. "Isn't that the arithmetic you've been doing since the river? Who gets hurt if you keep it? Because I promise you, the list is longer than you think, and it has your names on it now."

* * *

They regrouped fifty meters upwind, out of earshot, which on the plateau meant shouting distance but not listening distance, the wind taking their words apart and handing them to nobody.

"He's going to say yes," Teo said.

"He hasn't said yes," Dominic said.

"You haven't said no either, and your face is doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look like a man reading a menu at a restaurant he can't afford." Teo hugged the drone controller to his chest. "I've seen it. My uncle had that face. Right up until he sold the boat."

Marisol crouched, drawing lines in the dust with a stick—the pass, the ridge, the two vehicles, the long scar of the smugglers' track that only she and her grandfather's memory knew was there. "If we hand it over, we're done. Truly done. Fed, paid, free." She drew a circle around the chest's position on their little dust map. "If we don't, we have maybe two days of water, a pass that's closing early, and two vehicles full of professionals who've been polite so far because politeness is cheaper than bullets."

"Anneli?" Dominic said.

Anneli stood a little apart, looking at the upside-down cup she was still holding, as if it might explain itself. "She's telling the truth," she said. "That's what frightens me. The Vosses don't need to lie. The offer is real. The debt would vanish." She looked up. "And in ten years, the archive I worked for buried Valdoria a little deeper every time someone got curious. Ilsa's version costs you nothing and costs *them* nothing. That's how erasure works, Dominic. It doesn't need villains. It needs tired people accepting reasonable terms."

Nobody said anything for a while. The wind filled in.

"She said nothing changes for them," Marisol said quietly. "For the descendants. My grandmother sang songs about a city under the water twice, and I spent thirty years thinking they were just songs." She snapped the stick and threw the halves away. "Something changes for them. It changes the moment that chest leaves our hands."

Dominic looked back down the slope. Ilsa was packing up the coffee things with unhurried competence, and she caught him watching and raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. *Take your time,* the gesture said. *I'm patient. Patience is what they pay me for.*

And God help him, the offer sat in his chest like warmth. Clean slate. Full tank. No more 3 a.m. calls from cheerful men who laughed at funerals. He could be a storm chaser again instead of a debtor with a hobby. He could—

No. That was the trick of it, and he saw it clearly for the first time, standing in the wind with his crew watching him: escape had never been the hard part. Escape was *easy*. Escape was a road and a reason. What was hard was arriving somewhere and staying. What was hard was a name meaning something. He'd been running for ten years and calling it freedom, and here was Ilsa Rehn offering him a beautifully paved exit, and the only honest thing he could say about it was that it smelled like every other door he'd ever walked through.

"Not yet," he said aloud.

Marisol looked up. "Not yet what?"

"Not yet an answer. We vote tonight. All of us. Even Teo, even if he abstains, even if he abstains *loudly*." He rubbed the grit from his eyes. "But Ilsa should know we're considering it. Politely. Because the moment she thinks we're stalling, this stops being a conversation."

"I'll tell her," Anneli said, and started down the slope before anyone could argue, which was, Dominic was beginning to understand, simply how Anneli moved through the world—toward the uncomfortable thing, at speed.

* * *

They broke camp at dusk, not because they were fleeing—Ilsa's vehicles made no move to follow—but because Marisol wanted the ridge between them and the pass mouth before full dark, and because moving felt better than deciding.

The chest rode in the truck bed under tarps and spare rope, and Dominic found himself riding back there with it at the first rest stop, his palm flat against the wood, feeling the road's vibration come up through the planks like a pulse. Inside were letters. He hadn't opened that compartment—hadn't dared—but Anneli had catalogued enough to tell him: personal papers, family records, names. A nation's paperwork was leverage. A nation's *letters* were people.

"Hey." Teo climbed up beside him, tablet glowing. "Drone's up. Ridge line's clear. Also, uh." He hesitated, which Teo almost never did. "Also, there's smoke. North-northwest, maybe eight klicks. Small. Cook fire size. Somebody's living out here."

"On the dry side? Nobody lives on the dry side."

"Somebody's cooking dinner on the dry side." Teo turned the screen toward him: a gray-green smudge against the darkening slope, flickering. "Should I log it?"

Dominic looked at the smudge for a long moment. Cook fire size. In the middle of nowhere, on a smugglers' track that officially didn't exist, in the same direction Marisol's grandfather's route bent before it climbed.

"Yeah," he said. "Log it. And wake Marisol."

"Why?"

"Because I think somebody out there just lit a lamp for us," Dominic said, "and I'd rather knock than break in."

Below them, far behind, Ilsa's camp light burned steady and patient, a single point of warmth in all that cold country—temptation wearing a friendly face, waiting to see which way the wind would turn.
