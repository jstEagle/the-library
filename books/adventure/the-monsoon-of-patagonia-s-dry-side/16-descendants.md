## 16. Descendants

The smugglers' track was less a road than a rumor with gravel in it.

Teo had named his drone *Paloma*—dove, because Marisol said it, and because naming a machine something hopeful felt like a small act of rebellion against the last two weeks. He flew her low over the track now, skimming the scrub, watching the screen while the convoy groaned along behind him at the speed of tired mules and tireder men. The chest rode in the bed of Dominic's truck under a tarp that fooled absolutely no one, least of all the sky.

"Paloma says the track forks ahead," Teo called back through the window. "Left goes down into a canyon. Right just... stops."

"Tracks don't stop," Marisol said from the passenger seat. "They get shy."

"That's not a real thing."

"My grandfather ran this route for thirty years. Everything on it is real and most of it is shy." She leaned forward and tapped the windshield where the canyon mouth opened like a held breath. "Left."

Dominic took the left without argument, which Teo had learned was how you could tell he was scared. When Dominic argued, he was fine. When he went quiet and agreeable, somewhere inside him a man with a ledger was doing arithmetic, and the arithmetic never came out friendly.

They descended into the canyon as the light went long and copper-colored. And then Paloma did something she had never done.

She lost signal.

Not gradually—the way she usually stuttered and whined when the wind bullied her—but all at once, clean, like a door closing. Teo stared at the blank screen, then up, then around, and realized what he was looking at.

The canyon walls were threaded with wire.

Thin, dull, almost invisible wire, strung between outcrops in patterns that meant nothing until you saw them from above—and then they meant everything. Antennas. A whole passive array, woven into the rock like a spider had studied electrical engineering.

"Um," Teo said.

"I see it," Anneli said quietly, from the back seat. She had gone very still, the way she did when her professional self and her human self were fighting for the wheel.

"Those are listening wires," Teo said. "Or—they're not listening, they're *absorbing*, they'd kill radar reflection, you couldn't see this place from—" He stopped. His heart was going fast and happy, the way it only did when he found something he wasn't supposed to find. "You couldn't see this place at all."

"Turn off your drone," Anneli said.

"It's already dark. It went dark by itself. That's what I'm telling you—"

"Then turn off everything else."

Around the next bend, the canyon opened into a bowl of green that had no business existing on the dry side of Patagonia. Terraces climbed the walls. Water moved through them in channels old enough to have worn the stone smooth, fed by a spring that came out of the rock face under a lintel of carved stone—and the carving, weathered almost to nothing, was unmistakably a sun with two faces.

There were houses. Low, stone-footed, roofed in sod and tin, maybe forty of them, arranged not randomly but in the ghost of a grid—a street plan, the same one Teo had photographed lying drowned under forty feet of river water three hundred kilometers south.

Nobody spoke for a full minute.

Then people started coming out of the houses. Not running. Walking, unhurried, the way you come out when you've been expecting something for a long time and it finally bothered to arrive. There were maybe sixty of them, all ages, and an old woman at the front of them leaning on a stick, and every single one of them was looking at the truck bed.

At the chest.

The old woman reached them first. Up close she was small and iron-colored, with hands like tree roots and eyes that went from the tarp to Marisol's face and stayed there.

"You sing," she said. Not a question. In a language Teo didn't know, but understood anyway, the way you understand rain.

Marisol made a sound Teo had never heard from her—not words, just air, like something unlocking. Then she answered, haltingly, in the same language, and her accent was terrible and the old woman's smile said she didn't care even slightly.

"My grandmother," Marisol managed, switching back to Spanish. "She sang songs about a city under the water twice. I thought—I thought they were just songs. I thought they were *sad* songs."

"They are sad songs," the old woman said. "They are also directions. Come. You have driven a long way carrying our grandmother's house, and my knees are too old to stand in your dust."

* * *

They put the chest down in the largest building, which was part meeting hall, part schoolroom, and entirely full of people who touched it the way you touch a sleeping relative—with both hands and an apology. The double-sun seal on the lid made several of the older ones close their eyes.

An old man knelt beside it and read the seal aloud in Valdorian, slow and formal, and Teo watched sixty faces do the same thing at once: the small involuntary movement of people hearing their own name spoken after decades of being called something else.

"We kept the language," the old woman—who was called Serafina, and who ran things with the gentle totality of weather—told them afterward, over food that appeared from nowhere and was better than anything Teo had eaten since leaving the coast. "Three generations. My grandmother's rule: the language at home, Spanish outside, and never once, not ever, write it down where it could be found. So we carry it." She tapped her own temple. "Forty years I carried the land registry of a drowned country in my head. Do you know what that does to a person's memory? I can recite the names of every family that owned orchards in the valley. I have never seen the orchards. They are under the reservoir."

"The treaty clause," Anneli said softly. "Article Nine. The flooding provision."

Serafina looked at her for a long moment. "You know Article Nine."

"I work for the people who wrote the footnote that hides Article Nine."

The hall got quiet in a specific way. Teo recognized it from home, from the moment before his mother decided whether a stranger was trouble or not. But Serafina only nodded slowly, as if Anneli had confirmed something she'd already priced in.

"And yet here you are," Serafina said, "eating our bread."

"Here I am," Anneli agreed, "eating your bread."

It seemed to settle something. Teo ate more bread.

* * *

Later, when the hall had emptied and the fire had gone down to its thinking stage, Serafina sat with the four of them and opened the chest herself. Her hands knew the latches. That was the thing that undid Teo a little—she didn't fumble, didn't study it. Her hands remembered.

Inside, under the treaties and ledgers that had caused every problem any of them had ever had, were the letters. Family correspondence. Birth records. A child's drawing of the double sun done in charcoal, preserved under glass. Photographs of streets that existed now only as drowned foundations and as the layout of this canyon settlement, reproduced exactly, terrace for terrace, street for street.

"They rebuilt it," Teo said. He hadn't meant to say it out loud. "Here. You rebuilt the whole street plan. You've been living in the map of the city this whole time."

"Where else would we live?" Serafina said, as if he'd asked why they breathed.

Marisol had the letters in her lap and was crying without any noise about it, which somehow made it worse, and Dominic sat beside her with his hand hovering near her shoulder, not quite landing, a man who had spent ten years learning how to leave and forgotten how to stay.

And Teo—Teo felt something happen in his chest that he didn't have vocabulary for yet, so he did what he always did, which was reach for the wrong tool first.

"I should show you," he said. "The drone footage. Of the old city. Under the water. The drought dropped the river and you can see the streets, the actual streets, and there's a fountain in the main square and the basin is still—" His voice cracked in the middle like a fourteen-year-old's, which was unfair, because he was seventeen, thank you. "I mean. If you want. Nobody's seen it in eighty years."

Serafina turned and looked at him fully, and it was like being looked at by a lighthouse.

"You flew a machine over our city," she said, "and brought its bones home to us."

"Well. I mean. It's just a drone. It's a consumer model, honestly, the gimbal is third-party—"

"You brought our city home." She took his hand, both of hers around his one, root-hands and all. "Child, in this room tonight, you are not a boy with a toy. You are the keeper of the only images of Valdoria that exist. Do you understand me?"

He understood that his throat had closed. He nodded.

"Good," she said. "Tomorrow you will show everyone. All of them. Every street." A pause, and then the gentleness came back into her voice like water into a channel. "And then, child, you will tell us why there is a woman camped on the ridge above our canyon who has been there since noon, watching us through good optics, and why she smells of money and patience."

* * *

Teo found out later that Serafina's youngest grandson, a wiry kid of about ten named Bruno who had adopted Teo within four minutes of his arrival, maintained the wire array as a chore, the way other kids took out trash. Bruno had spotted the watcher at 12:40 in the afternoon and reported it with the bored tone of a professional.

Through Paloma's lens, flying high and silent on the far side of the ridge, Teo confirmed it: a single figure at a cold camp on the ridgeline, no fire, vehicle tucked into a ravine. Good optics. Patient posture.

He knew the posture. Everyone did. Ilsa Rehn had found them.

He brought the image to the others, and the hall filled again, and this time the mood was different—older, harder, practiced. These were people whose grandparents had survived an erasure by becoming invisible. Being found was not an abstraction to them. It was the oldest story they had.

"She won't come down tonight," Dominic said, studying the image. "That's not how she works. She'll wait. She'll let us sleep on it. Tomorrow she'll walk down alone, unarmed, smiling, and offer us everything we want, and it will be the most dangerous conversation of our lives."

"How do you know?" Bruno asked, wide-eyed.

"Because that's how it worked on me," Dominic said. "Once upon a time, I signed."

Serafina stood. The hall quieted the way water quiets.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we will receive her the way Valdoria received everyone. With bread, and open hands, and nothing hidden." Her eyes went around the room, and landed last on the chest, and softened. "We have been invisible for eighty years. We are finished with it. Whatever she offers, whatever comes down that canyon behind her—we decide together. All of us. That is the one thing they never managed to buy."

Outside, the stars over the canyon were ferocious and close. Teo lay on the schoolroom floor with Paloma charging beside him and Bruno asleep against his arm, and he thought about the fountain under the water, still holding its shape after eighty years, waiting for someone to look.

In the morning, Ilsa Rehn walked down into the canyon alone, unarmed, smiling—

—and stopped dead at the sight of sixty people standing in the street, waiting for her, singing.
