## 17. The Vote

The settlement had a word for what we were doing that evening, and Marisol taught it to me while we carried benches out into the square: *consejo abierto*. Open council. Anyone could speak; anyone could be spoken to; and when it was done, everyone had to live inside whatever was decided.

"It's how they did it in Valdoria," she said, setting her bench down with a thump. "Before."

"Before," I repeated. It was becoming our favorite word up here on the dry side of the world. Before the treaty. Before the water. Before three generations of people learned to sing their own history in a language no government would put on a form.

The old woman who had first recognized the chest—her name was Beatriz, and she held herself like a lighthouse holds a storm—had asked for the consejo with perfect courtesy and zero flexibility. The chest sat between us and them now, literally: we'd placed it on a table in the middle of the square, under an awning strung with drying peppers, as if it were a guest of honor at a wedding where nobody was sure the groom would show.

The letters inside it were Valdorian. The treaties were leverage. And by nightfall, one way or another, all four of us were going to have to say out loud which one mattered more.

* * *

I didn't sleep much, which was fine, because neither did anyone else.

I found Teo on the roof of the granary at two in the morning, his drone cradled in his lap like a cat he'd rescued from a drain. The settlement below us was dark except for one window—the house where they'd given Anneli a room—and a scatter of stars so thick it looked like someone had spilled the sky.

"Can't sleep either?" I said.

"I'm running scenarios." He patted the drone. "Not flying it. Just... thinking about flying it. There's a difference. Legally."

"There is?"

"There is if you're seventeen and somebody's already yelled at you once this week." He was quiet for a moment. "Dom. If we sell to the Vosses, I pay off my mom's medical thing. Like, completely. She stops getting the letters. You know the letters I mean."

I knew the letters. I'd been getting my own versions of them for ten years, until the Voss brothers upgraded to phone calls, and then to Ilsa Rehn appearing at fuel stops with coffee and a smile like a drawn bow.

"And if we give it away," Teo went on, "she keeps getting them. For years. Maybe forever." He looked at me with that terrible directness teenagers have, the kind adults spend decades learning to disguise. "I'm not saying what I want. I'm saying I know exactly what everything costs. That's the part nobody says out loud, right? Everything costs."

"Yeah," I said. "That's the part."

He nodded slowly, then added, "Also I think Marisol sharpened her knife again tonight. Third time since dinner. You can hear it if you listen."

I listened. Somewhere down the lane, faint and rhythmic, came the whisper of steel on stone.

"Good to know," I said.

* * *

The consejo opened at mid-morning under the pepper awning, and I'll give the Voss brothers' collectors this much: they had excellent timing.

Ilsa Rehn arrived at nine-forty, alone, on foot, hands visible, wearing the expression of a woman arriving slightly early for a dentist appointment she fully intended to enjoy. Two of her people stayed back at the tree line with the trucks, close enough to matter, far enough to pretend otherwise.

"Mr. Ishikawa," she said pleasantly. "Lovely village. Very well hidden. Took us eleven hours to find it, which I respect enormously."

"You're not invited to this one," Marisol said, and she had the knife out now, openly, cleaning her nails with it in a way that fooled absolutely no one.

"I'm not invited to anything, Ms. Ferreyra. I'm invited to everything. It's the nature of my work." Ilsa settled onto a bench at the edge of the square like she owned the bench and possibly the tree it came from. "Please, continue your little democracy. I wouldn't dream of interrupting. I only ask that before anyone decides anything, they remember that I am sitting right here, and that I have made Mr. Ishikawa an offer that involves him never seeing any of us again. That's worth something. Ask anyone who's ever seen us twice."

Beatriz, magnificent, unbothered, rapped a spoon against a cup and called the consejo to order in Valdorian, then translated herself, because half the village spoke both and wanted the performance anyway.

"The guests will speak," she said. "Then the guests will vote. Then we will see."

* * *

Anneli went first, and she was honest, which after everything cost her more than lying would have.

"I've spent eleven years in a government archive," she said, standing, hands folded, voice steady in the way of someone who has rehearsed steadiness. "My job was to make sure things like this chest stay theoretical. And I told myself it was fine, because most of what we buried deserved burying—there are things in those vaults that would hurt living people if they surfaced. But this—" she touched the chest lid, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal "—this isn't that. This is people who were never conquered, never defeated, never even asked. They were purchased. Line items. And their grandchildren are legally stateless right now, today, because a treaty clause says their country doesn't exist."

"So surrender it to your archive," Ilsa said sweetly, "and let the professionals handle it. Safely. Slowly. Over decades. In a vault."

"No," Anneli said. "That's the point. Not the archive. Not the syndicate. Not a museum lobby in a capital that signed the treaty." She turned to the villagers. "You. Or nowhere. But I won't pretend it's free. If we do this, the archive burns me. My career, my credentials, probably my freedom—they'll find a charge. They always find a charge. I'm asking you to understand that I know what I'm voting to lose."

Marisol stood up next, and she didn't fold her hands.

"My grandmother sang songs about a city that went under the water twice," she said. "I thought they were songs. They were testimony. These people are my family—I found that out on a riverbank three weeks ago, and I'm still finding pieces of it every morning. So yes. Give it to them. Obviously. Except—" and here her voice did something complicated, and I watched her fight it and lose, "—except Teo's mother gets the letters. And Dom walks out of this owing men who break kneecaps. And I'm done pretending I don't care about that. I care about it a lot. I just care about this more, and I hate that it's a choice at all."

She sat down hard. Teo stared at his boots.

"That's two for the settlement," Beatriz observed, dry as the plateau. "One of whom has just volunteered to feel guilty forever. Continue."

* * *

Here's the thing nobody tells you about democracy at gunpoint: it still works. It just hurts more.

Teo stood last. He'd written notes on his forearm, which he kept trying to read without being obvious about it, which was extremely obvious.

"So," he said. "Option one, we sell to the Vosses. Everyone walks away rich-ish, Ilsa wipes Dom's debt, my mom's fine, and these guys—" he gestured at the whole square, the peppers, the children watching from doorways, Beatriz's lighthouse posture "—get nothing, and the chest goes into a private collection somewhere, and in forty years some guy buys it at auction without knowing what it is. Option two, we give it to the archive, Anneli keeps her job, everybody else loses, same auction in forty years but with a plaque. Option three—"

He stopped. Looked at his arm. Looked up.

"Option three, we give it to them, and it's right, and it costs everything, and I can't—I keep trying to pick and I can't, because my mom's medical thing isn't abstract, okay? It's a real person in a real apartment who cries on the phone sometimes and pretends she doesn't." His voice cracked and he pushed through it, furious at it. "So I abstain. I abstain and I'm sorry and I'll live with whatever you pick. That's my vote. No vote."

Silence. Even Ilsa didn't fill it, though I caught her watching Teo with something that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite contempt. Recognition, maybe. The look of a professional noting a young person learning what things cost.

"Two for the settlement," Beatriz said. "One abstention. One vote outstanding." She looked at me. "Mr. Ishikawa. The tie is yours."

* * *

I want to tell you I weighed it carefully. I want to tell you there was a ledger in my head, columns balanced, interest calculated.

There wasn't. There was just a decade of running, laid out flat in the sun.

I thought about the Voss brothers at their dinner table, pouring wine, laughing warmly, telling me about salvage work upriver—cheerful, patient men who had built an entire philosophy on the idea that everyone has a price, and who had spent ten years proving it on me, patiently, cheerfully, one loan at a time. I thought about Ilsa Rehn sitting right there on that bench, having offered me a clean slate in exchange for a nation's chains, and meaning it kindly, the way you'd offer a drowning man a rope tied to an anchor.

And I thought about the letters in the chest. Not the treaties—the treaties were weapons, and weapons belong to whoever pays. The letters. A customs clerk named Ester writing to her sister about the last harvest before the water came. A schoolteacher complaining about the new maps that left his country blank. Ordinary mail from ordinary people who woke up one morning inside a paragraph of fine print and never got out.

Nobody had ever paid me enough to matter. That was the whole joke of my debt—ten years of payments and the number barely moved. Which meant the debt was never really the thing. The cage was the thing. And cages only work if you believe the door is locked.

"Here's my problem with selling," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I felt. "It's not morality. I want to be clear about that, because I've got very little morality left and I need it for later. My problem is that the Vosses don't buy things. They buy *people*. The day I hand this over, I'm not out of debt—I'm their employee, forever, the man who knows where the leverage lives. There's no version of that where I walk away. I've read that contract before. I wrote it, basically, one signature at a time."

Ilsa raised an eyebrow, genuinely interested, the way a chess player is interested.

"And the archive?" Beatriz prompted.

"The archive buries things. Burying is how we got here." I looked at Anneli, who was watching me with her whole face. "I burned half my life getting out of one hole. I'm not digging another."

I put my hand flat on the chest.

"The settlement. That's my vote. And Ilsa—" I turned to her, and here's where I did the arithmetic after all, the only sum that had ever actually mattered. "Tell your brothers the deal's dead. Tell them Dominic Ishikawa chose wrong, on purpose, with witnesses. Tell them whatever you want. But nobody touches this village, and nobody touches my crew, or the next storm cell that comes over this plateau is going to be the least of their problems, because I will spend the rest of my life making their lives very, very expensive. I'm good at expensive. Ask anyone."

For a long moment Ilsa Rehn just looked at me. Then she smiled—not the drawn-bow smile, something smaller and stranger—and stood, and brushed off her trousers.

"Eleven hours to find this place," she said. "Worth every one." She walked to the tree line without looking back, and her trucks, remarkably, drove away.

* * *

The village fed us that night like we'd won something, and maybe we had, though it didn't feel like winning. It felt like the moment after you jump—too late to change your mind, too early to know if there's water in the pool.

Anneli found me by the fire. "They'll come," she said quietly. "Rehn's withdrawal was real—I know her tells—but the syndicate lost a barge to us, and the archive lost me. Desperate institutions don't accept votes. They send weather."

"Weather I can read," I said.

"Can you outrun it?"

I looked east, where the horizon had gone the color of a bruise, and where the wind had started carrying that smell I knew better than my own name—the green, metallic promise of a big cell building beyond the mountains, weeks early, exactly like the pass closing early, exactly like everything else this season refusing to follow the map.

"No," I said. "But I can tell this village exactly where it's going to hit. Get Teo. Wake up whoever runs their shelter. We've got maybe six hours, and I intend to use every one of them."

Above us, the first thunder rolled down off the plateau like a drum announcing the only vote that hadn't been cast yet.
