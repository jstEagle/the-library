## 18. Storm Over the Plateau

**Dominic**

The sky had been lying to him all morning, and Dominic Ishikawa had finally caught it in the act.

He stood on the ridge above the settlement with his anemometer held up like a small brass prayer, watching the western horizon do something no honest Patagonian sky should do in late autumn. The cloud deck wasn't moving with the wind at ground level. It was moving *against* it—sliding southeast while the dust at his boots streamed northwest, which meant two air masses were arguing, and when air masses argued over a dry plateau, they didn't bicker. They settled things.

"Teo," he called down the slope. "Kill the drone and get up here. I need your eyes on the radar feed."

"You said don't fly it in wind over forty," Teo shouted back, already climbing, already flying it anyway, because Teo's relationship with instructions was the same as Dominic's relationship with debt: technically acknowledged, practically ignored.

"That was before. Get up here."

The settlement below them didn't look like much from the ridge—a scatter of stone houses and tin roofs tucked into a fold of the plateau, smoke rising straight up from chimneys because even the wind seemed to be holding its breath. Forty-odd people. Grandmothers who sang songs that were actually maps. Children who spoke a language that officially did not exist, fluently and with attitude.

Three generations of hiding, about to be interrupted by everyone at once.

Because that was the other thing the sky was doing. It wasn't just brewing a storm. It was providing cover. Dominic had spent ten years reading weather as an escape route—for himself, for other people's money, for anyone willing to pay a man who could tell you exactly when the passes would close. He knew what a convenient catastrophe looked like. And this one had *convenience* written all over its leading edge: a storm cell big enough to ground aircraft, flood roads, kill radios, and turn a plateau into a maze—which was precisely what you'd want if you were Ilsa Rehn or the salvage syndicate and you'd decided today was the day to stop being polite.

Ilsa had been shadowing them for days at a respectful distance, patient as arithmetic. The syndicate had lost their barge but not their appetite. And now both of them were converging on forty people who had done nothing wrong except remember.

Dominic checked his watch. Checked the pressure gauge he'd zip-tied to his pack strap years ago and never removed, because a man's tools outlive his excuses.

"Dominic." Marisol came up the trail fast, her grandmother's songbook wrapped in oilcloth under one arm like a scripture she'd only just learned to read. "Elder Sosa says the old shelter under the chapel holds sixty. Maybe seventy if we're friendly about it."

"How long to get everyone in?"

"With warning? An hour. Without warning?" She looked at the horizon and her jaw set. "Without warning, some of them won't go at all. They've spent three generations not being told what to do by outsiders."

"Then we'd better make sure the warning comes from inside the family." He handed her the anemometer. "Hold this. Tell me what you feel."

She frowned, held it up, turned slowly. "Wind's shifting. Coming around to the north?"

"North-northwest, and backing. That's the inflow." He pointed west, where the cloud deck had gone a color he could only describe as bruised pewter, with a greenish tinge underneath that made his stomach drop the way it always did—the good drop, the *oh, you're a big one* drop, the drop that had gotten him through a decade of chasing monsters across two hemispheres. "That cell is going to rotate. When it does, everything within twenty kilometers is going to get hit by rain you can't see through, hail, and wind that treats fences as suggestions. And the arroyo—" he pointed at the dry wash snaking past the settlement's eastern edge— "is going to become a river again. Briefly. Violently."

"The one my grandfather used to run supplies through."

"The very one. Which means it'll also be the one our friends try to come up, right until it tries to drown them." He allowed himself one grim smile. "Weather doesn't take sides, Mari. But it does keep a schedule, and I intend to read it faster than they do."

Below them, Teo had landed the drone and was staring at his tablet with an expression Dominic recognized from mirrors: the look of a man doing math he doesn't like.

"Radar's garbage," Teo reported. "Terrain clutter, plus the government archive keeps pinging my uplink. Anneli says she can spoof it, but—"

"Tell Anneli yes to whatever she wants. Today everybody gets to be useful and nobody gets to be proud." He clapped the kid's shoulder. "Now. You know every roofline in that village from your flights. I need you to map me the shelter routes—all of them, including the ones the elders think are secret. Because in ninety minutes, secret is going to matter less than *fast*."

Teo swallowed, nodded, and for once in his young life didn't say anything at all, which from Teo was roughly equivalent to a moment of silence.

Dominic turned back to the west and did the thing he was best at in all the world, the thing that had kept him fed and hated and alive: he read the sky, and he started counting down.

* * *

**Marisol**

Her grandmother had sung about this. That was the thought Marisol couldn't shake, standing in Elder Sosa's kitchen while the settlement mobilized around her with the calm efficiency of people who had practiced disaster the way other people practiced holidays.

*"When the sky forgets its manners,"* Abuela used to sing, rocking, *"the river remembers its road. Hide the children where the water cannot argue, and wait for the world to be washed honest."*

She'd thought it was just a song. A pretty nonsense thing about a city under a lake. Everything in that book had been just a song until six weeks ago, and now every verse was a survival manual and every lullaby was a land deed, and Marisol Oyelaran's family had been keeping the truth alive in three-quarter time without knowing it.

"They're coming up the arroyo," said a boy at the door—young, breathless, one of the spotters Sosa had posted on the ridges the moment Dominic gave the word. "Two trucks. Not Rehn's. Different people. They stopped at the mouth of the wash and they're arguing."

"Arguing about what?" Sosa asked. The old woman moved through her kitchen like weather herself, unhurried, total.

"About whether the arroyo floods." The boy shrugged. "One of them says no. One of them is looking at the sky."

Marisol almost laughed. Almost. "The one looking at the sky is smarter. Tell the ridge watchers: nobody engages, everybody observes. If the syndicate drowns in the wash, that's between them and the water."

"And Rehn?" Sosa asked quietly.

Marisol glanced out the window toward the south, where a single vehicle sat parked on a rise, engine off, patient as a headstone. Ilsa Rehn had been there since dawn. Watching. Waiting. Doing nothing, which from Ilsa Rehn was louder than most people's shouting.

"She's waiting for the storm to do her work," Marisol said. "Or for us to run into it. Either way—" She stopped. Outside, the light had changed. Gone green-gray, submarine, wrong. The first fat drops hit the window like thrown gravel.

Either way, it was starting.

Sosa put a hand on her arm. "The man with the instruments. Your storm-reader. Can he truly tell us how long?"

Marisol thought of Dominic on the ridge, wired into the sky like he'd been born with a cable in his spine, and felt something she hadn't let herself feel in weeks: trust. Not hope—trust. Hope was a wish. Trust was based on data.

"He's never once been wrong about when the sky would break," she said. "He's only ever been wrong about why he was running. Come on. We have children to move."

* * *

**Teo**

The problem with being the youngest person in any crisis, Teo had learned, was that everyone assumed your fear was bigger than theirs, when actually yours was just better organized.

He crouched in the chapel's undercroft—an old smugglers' cellar that smelled of dust and lamp oil and decades of deliberate forgetting—with his tablet propped against a wine barrel, flying four drones in rotation because one drone could be lost and four drones was a system. Rain hammered the trapdoor above him like applause from a giant. Through the drone feeds he watched the settlement dissolve into gray, roofs vanishing, the arroyo—his God, the *arroyo*—going from a dry scar to a brown torrent in eleven minutes flat, exactly like Dominic had said, to the minute, and Teo was going to bring that up later, repeatedly, forever.

On the southern feed: Ilsa Rehn's vehicle, headlights cutting cones through the deluge, moving now. Slowly. Toward the settlement.

On the western feed: the syndicate trucks, half-swallowed by the flooded wash, one of them tilted at an angle that suggested its driver had recently learned a hard lesson about hydrology. Men were out in the rain, hauling gear, shouting words the drone mic couldn't quite resolve but whose gestures translated perfectly in any language.

And on the northern feed, high up where the hail hadn't reached yet: the storm itself, seen from above, a rotating cathedral of cloud with a tail of rain sweeping the plateau like the eraser end of a pencil.

"Teo." Anneli's voice from the stairs, tight. "The syndicate has a winch truck. They're rigging to cross the arroyo upstream of the ford."

"In *this*?"

"They're professionals. Professionals are the most dangerous kind of stupid." She came down into the lamplight, hair plastered flat, holding a radio she'd rebuilt from parts that morning. "Dominic needs this information and the surface radios are drowning in static."

"I've got line-of-sight relay. Give me thirty seconds." His fingers flew. This was the part he was good at—not brave, not strong, but *fast*, brain and thumbs working together while older, calmer people did the actual courage. He bounced the signal off drone two, patched it to Dominic's handset, and heard his own voice crack slightly as he reported: "Syndicate crossing upstream, maybe fifteen minutes from your position. Rehn's moving too. Also—also the cell's rotation just tightened, Dominic, I can see it from above, it's—it's beautiful, honestly, it's the most beautiful horrible thing I've ever—"

"Teo." Dominic's voice came through calm as a metronome. "Breathe. Then tell me the hook echo's bearing."

Teo breathed. Looked. Reported the bearing.

A pause, and then Dominic said the sentence Teo would retell for the rest of his life, usually after two drinks:

"Good lad. Now everybody listen—here's how we buy this village an hour."

* * *

**Dominic**

The trick, Dominic explained to the ring of soaked faces huddled under the chapel eave—elders, crew, a couple of teenagers who refused to be left out—was simple, and it was a lie.

"You can't outrun a storm like this," he said, rain streaming off his jaw. "Nobody can. But you can convince people to run in the wrong direction, and that's nearly as good."

Sosa's eyes narrowed. "You want to deceive armed men during a natural disaster."

"I want to *inconvenience* armed men during a natural disaster. Deception implies elegance." He spread his map on a crate, weighted the corners with stones, and drew with a grease pencil. "The cell's core tracks northeast along this line. In forty minutes, the worst of it—the hail core, the embedded rotation—passes directly over the arroyo's middle reach. Anyone in that wash in forty minutes is having a very bad final afternoon."

"So we let the weather fight them," Marisol said.

"We *invite* the weather to fight them. There's a difference, and the difference is that we choose the invitation." He tapped the map. "Their scouts will have seen lights moving in the village—we make sure of that, lanterns in the east windows, movement silhouettes, the whole theater of it. They'll assume we're evacuating east, away from the storm track, toward the coast road. Every instinct says prey runs from the predator, and right now the biggest predator on this plateau is that cloud. So they'll push hard to cut us off before we 'escape'—which means pushing up the arroyo, in the dark, in a flood, directly into the path of a hail core I can time to within five minutes."

Anneli, quiet at the edge of the group, said: "And Rehn?"

"Rehn's smart enough to stay out of the wash. She'll come high, along the ridge road." He met the old woman's eyes. "Which is why someone has to be visible on the ridge road. A decoy. Lights, noise, a vehicle that looks like it's carrying a chest."

Silence. Rain drummed. Somewhere a child cried and was hushed.

"I'll drive it," Dominic said.

"No," said Marisol, Anneli, and Teo, in unison and three different keys.

"It's my skill set. Ten years of driving away from things in bad weather. I am, professionally speaking, the best-qualified coward on this continent." He rolled up the map. "But I'm not driving away this time. I'm driving *toward* the clock. Every minute Rehn follows my taillights is a minute she's not following yours. Every minute the syndicate spends fighting the arroyo is a minute the shelter fills and the chest goes underground where no contract, no treaty, and no collector can price it."

Sosa studied him for a long moment, rain dripping off the eaves between them, and then she did something he didn't expect. She took his hand in both of hers, old and dry and strong.

"My grandmother's song says the sky washes the world honest," she said. "It does not say the world helps. You are helping." She squeezed once. "Come back. That is also part of the song, though it is in the second verse, which nobody sings because it is difficult."

"I'll learn the second verse," Dominic said, and went out into the rain.

* * *

**Marisol**

They moved through the storm like a congregation, and Marisol sang to keep the children's feet quick and their fear small—her grandmother's songs, the real ones now, the ones with directions hidden in the refrains. *Where the water argues, walk the high stone. Where the sky forgets its manners, hold the hand beside you.* The Valdorian descendants picked up the verses without being taught; the words lived in them somewhere deeper than memory, and hearing forty voices answer hers through hammering rain, Marisol understood that a nation erased from maps had simply relocated into music, where no treaty clause could reach it.

Behind them, the settlement emptied into the undercroft and the old smuggling tunnels beyond. Ahead, on the ridge road, a pair of headlights swung and dipped—Dominic's truck, the empty chest-shaped crate lashed in its bed, taillights bleeding red through the rain as it climbed away from them, drawing the hunter after the decoy.

"Go on, you idiot," she whispered. "Be lucky."

Then the hail came.

It arrived the way Dominic had promised—suddenly, vertically, white stones the size of eggs hammering the plateau flat, and the column of people broke into a run for the tunnel mouth, Marisol hauling two children under her coat, Anneli dragging an elder by the arm, the world gone white and deafening. And through it, absurdly, Teo's voice in her earpiece, relaying Dominic's calm countdown from the ridge: *"Core's on schedule. Syndicate's turning back—they've lost a truck to the wash, casualties unknown, they're retreating west. Rehn's vehicle is following the decoy. Following the decoy. She's bought it. Everyone underground has twelve minutes. Twelve minutes and this whole plateau belongs to the rain."*

Marisol stood at the tunnel mouth, counted her people in—one, two, forty-one, forty-two—and looked back once at the drowned, blazing, magnificent chaos of the plateau where every enemy of the truth was busy losing a fight with the sky.

Then she went underground with them, and waited for the second verse.

* * *

**Dominic**

The hail ended as abruptly as it began, and in the sudden relative quiet—just rain now, vast and silver—Dominic's mirror filled with headlights.

Ilsa Rehn's vehicle swung alongside him on the ridge road, matching speed, wipers flogging. Her window dropped. Rain gusted into his cab.

"Mr. Ishikawa," she called, unhurried as ever, as though they were passing on a country lane in June. "Lovely evening for a drive."

"Rehn! Weather's terrible, sorry, can't talk!"

"Of course." Her eyes flicked to the crate in his truck bed, then back to him, and something in her face shifted—not anger. Recalculation. "You know, I've chased you across four hundred kilometers of the driest country on earth, and this is the first time you've driven *toward* me. Curious, that."

"New management philosophy!"

She smiled—genuinely, he thought—and fell back a length, letting him lead, and Dominic understood with cold clarity that she hadn't bought the decoy at all. She'd simply decided the storm had already cost her enough, and that whatever game he was playing, the board would still be there when the rain stopped.

Ahead, lightning walked the plateau on white legs, and behind him, beneath forty tons of wet stone, a chest full of letters slept among singing people.

For the first time in ten years, Dominic Ishikawa drove into a storm with nothing to run from—and no idea at all whether that would be enough.
