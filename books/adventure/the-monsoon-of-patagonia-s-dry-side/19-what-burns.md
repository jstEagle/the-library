## 19. What Burns

The storm hit the settlement like a fist through a paper wall.

Dominic had given them eleven minutes. It turned out to be nine and a half, because the cell did what cells on the dry side always did when he wasn't looking—cheated. The gust front came down off the plateau in a brown curtain of dust and rain that arrived all at once, flattening the grass roofs, snapping a corral pole like a matchstick, and sending forty Valdorian descendants, one disgraced river pilot, one teenage drone operator with a bleeding eyebrow, and one archivist running for the stone chapel at the center of the village.

He stood in it. He couldn't help himself. Ten years of habit had wired him to stand in weather while everyone else ran from it, and even now—with the wind trying to peel his jacket off his shoulders and hail the size of chickpeas stinging the back of his neck—he found himself reading the sky with something close to affection.

*Rotating base. Rain-wrapped core. She'll blow herself out over the eastern ridge in twenty minutes if nothing re-feeds her.*

"DOMINIC!" Marisol's voice, torn sideways by the wind. "The chest!"

Right. The chest.

It sat where they'd left it, under an overturned wagon beside the meeting house, because there hadn't been time to do anything cleverer. The tarp had already gone sailing off across the plateau like a wounded bird. Rain hammered the old wood, darkening it, and the double-sun seal of Valdoria gleamed up at him through the water as if it were pleased about all this.

Behind him, down the slope toward the dry riverbed, engines. Two of them. The syndicate's trucks, fighting the mud now, their headlights swinging wild through the murk. And further back, patient as arithmetic, a third set of lights that didn't fight the mud at all—Ilsa Rehn, parked on high ground, watching.

Dominic hauled the chest up against his hip. It weighed maybe thirty kilos wet, which was nothing, except that it wasn't the weight of the wood he was carrying.

He got it inside the chapel just as the door banged open again and Teo blew in backwards, drone case clutched to his chest like a football, hair plastered flat, grinning like a lunatic.

"They're stuck!" Teo shouted over the drumming roof. "Both trucks! One of them's axle-deep in the arroyo, I can see it on thermal—the driver's out, he's swearing, I can't hear what but I can *see* the shape of it—"

"Good lad." Dominic set the chest down on the altar steps, which felt vaguely sacrilegious until he remembered the chapel had been built by smugglers and probably hadn't hosted anything holy in sixty years.

Anneli was already there, wringing out her scarf, and Marisol came in last with two of the settlement elders, an old man named Aurelio who walked like a mast, and a small fierce woman everyone called Abuela Ceniza though Dominic suspected that wasn't her name so much as her job description.

"The letters," Aurelio said. Not a question. His eyes went straight to the chest the way a man's eyes go to his own child in a crowd.

"In here," Dominic said. "All of it."

"All of it," Anneli repeated quietly, and looked at him.

That was the thing about Anneli Brandt. She never said much, but she had a way of repeating a word back to you with the emphasis shifted half a millimeter, and suddenly you could hear everything you'd been avoiding. *All of it.* The treaties. The ledgers. The purchase agreements with their neat columns of figures and their clause seventeen, the dam clause, the drowning clause. The documents that proved a nation had been erased—and, held in the right hands, the documents that could un-erase it, or hold it hostage, or sell it to the highest bidder for another hundred years.

The Voss brothers wanted those papers. Not for money—for leverage. Ilsa had told him that plainly, sitting in her truck with the engine ticking, offering to wipe ten years of debt off the books like wiping condensation off glass. *We don't want your money, Dominic. We want what governments will pay to keep buried.*

And the archive wanted them too. Anneli's handlers, whoever they answered to, wanted them confirmed, catalogued, and disappeared.

And the settlement wanted—what? Their names back. Their grandmothers' songs made true. A country that existed somewhere other than in melody.

There wasn't enough chest for everybody. There never was.

"Dominic." Anneli touched his sleeve. Outside, thunder rolled along the plateau like furniture being dragged across heaven. "Whatever you're thinking—think it fast."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about storms: they are honest.

Every other hour of Dominic Ishikawa's life had been negotiation. Debt was negotiation. The Vosses' dinner invitations were negotiation. Even friendship, the little he'd allowed himself in ten years of moving, had carried fine print. But a storm doesn't negotiate. It tells you exactly what it is, exactly how strong, exactly where it's going, and then it does it. He had loved that about weather since he was nineteen years old and lying in a ditch outside Amarillo while the sky tried to kill him, feeling—for the first time in his life—like he understood the terms.

So he stood at the chapel window and read the cell one more time, because the terms were the only thing he trusted right now.

The core was passing north of the village. The rear flank was wrapping clean. In fifteen minutes the rain would slacken to nothing and the syndicate would dig their truck out of the arroyo, and after that the arithmetic got ugly: four of them, exhausted, against a syndicate crew plus whatever Ilsa Rehn decided she wanted, plus the archive's people, who were probably already driving.

Unless there was nothing left to drive for.

"Treaties," he said aloud, testing the shape of it.

Nobody answered. Marisol stopped wringing her scarf. Teo looked up from his drone case.

"The ledgers. The purchase agreements. Clause seventeen." He turned around. "Everything that makes this chest worth stealing."

Anneli went very still. "You're talking about destroying evidence. Historical evidence. Irreplaceable—"

"I'm talking about the difference between a country and a weapon." His voice came out steadier than he expected. "Those papers prove Valdoria was bought and drowned. You know who else knows how to read? Governments. Syndicates. The Voss brothers. Right now every one of them wants this chest because it's the only copy. Burn it, and what's left?" He put his hand flat on the wet lid. "Letters. Family records. Birth registers. Songs somebody wrote down. Things that belong to *them*"—he nodded at Aurelio and Abuela Ceniza—"and nobody else. No leverage. No price tag. Just... proof they exist."

Abuela Ceniza said something soft in Valdorian, three syllables that sounded like water going over stones, and Aurelio translated without being asked, his voice rough: "She says, 'A debt paid in fire is still paid.'"

"That's not—" Anneli pressed her fingers to the bridge of her nose. "Do you understand what you're proposing? I spent six years being told these documents might exist. My entire career is built on the idea that the record matters. That erasure only wins when the record dies." She looked up, and her eyes were wet, and not entirely from the rain. "And you're right. God help me, you're right. As long as those treaties exist, Valdoria is a bargaining chip. Destroy them and Valdoria becomes..." She exhaled. "Becomes theirs. Just theirs."

"Marisol?" Dominic asked.

Marisol had gone to the window. Her grandmother's songs had been maps; her family had been stateless for three generations without knowing why; and Dominic had watched her translate the flooding verse by lamplight with her jaw set like someone holding a door shut against a flood. Now she watched the rain come down on land that had once been hers, and when she spoke she didn't turn around.

"My grandmother sang about the city going under the water twice," she said. "Once with the dam. Once every time some government office stamped a file CLOSED and put it in a drawer." She turned. Her face was calm in the way of deep water. "Burn the drawer."

"Teo?"

The kid blinked. "Me? I abstain. I mean—no. Wait." He stood up, dripping, sixteen years old and suddenly older than that. "I've been deleting files since day one because adults told me to. So. For once." He swallowed. "Burn it. But let me film the seal first. Somebody should remember what it looked like."

* * *

They did it in the chapel yard, in the dying rain, because Dominic refused to burn anything under a roof.

The chest opened with a groan of swollen hinges. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had kept its secret for eighty years, the documents lay in bundles tied with tarred string: treaties with signatures of men whose nations no longer admitted signing; ledgers in a clerk's immaculate hand, column after column of a republic's assets converted line by line into someone else's; the purchase agreement itself, clause seventeen underlined twice in pencil by some long-dead hand that must have known exactly what it was underlining.

And beneath all of it, softer paper, thinner bundles. Letters. A birth register with names in a flowing alphabet. A child's drawing of the double sun, done in charcoal, kept all these years by someone who loved somebody.

Dominic built the fire with his own hands. Kindling from the wrecked corral pole, then the oilcloth, then the treaties, one bundle at a time, feeding them in while the rain thinned to mist and the eastern sky began, absurdly, to clear.

Teo filmed. Anneli read each document's first line aloud before it went in—a eulogy, or an inventory, or both. Marisol held the lantern. Aurelio sang, low, one of the songs, and halfway through the second verse Abuela Ceniza joined him, and then other voices from the settlement, gathered in the yard without anyone inviting them, singing a country back into existence by refusing to let its funeral be silent.

The ledgers burned longest. Paper holds ink the way bone holds marrow; the flames had to work at it. Dominic fed the pages in steadily, and with each bundle he felt something leave him—not grief, exactly. More like ballast. Like cutting loose weight you'd been carrying so long you'd mistaken it for a spine.

Ten years of debt. Ten years of the Voss brothers' cheerful phone calls, their patient interest calculations, their excellent wine and their cheerful, patient, utterly reasonable threats. Ten years of measuring life in miles because miles were the only unit that never asked him to stay. All of it had been built on the assumption that Dominic Ishikawa would always owe somebody, and that the somebody would always be able to tell him what to do.

The last ledger page curled and went up in a bloom of orange. Somewhere in the ash, clause seventeen died.

"You know," said a voice behind him, "that was the single most expensive bonfire I have ever witnessed."

Ilsa Rehn stood at the edge of the yard, hands in her coat pockets, rain-dark and unhurried, as if she'd been there for hours. Maybe she had. Behind her, down the slope, the syndicate's second truck sat stalled in the mud with its hazards blinking, and nobody from it was coming up the hill, because Ilsa had evidently told them not to.

Dominic didn't turn fully. He kept his eyes on the fire. "Come to arrest me?"

"For arson of documents that, according to every registry I have access to, never officially existed?" She came closer and stood beside him at the fire, warming her hands with theatrical irony. "No. I came to watch."

"Why?"

"Because I told Henrik and Lukas this would happen." She said it lightly, but there was something underneath, something almost tired. "Three years ago. When they bought your debt. I said, *that one isn't going to pay us, he's going to do something interesting instead.* They laughed. They love laughing, the boys. It's their best quality." She watched a treaty corner catch and flare. "Do you know why we lend to people like you, Dominic? Not gamblers—chasers. People who run toward the thing everyone else runs from. Because money never moves them. You can squeeze a gambler forever. But a man who stands in hail to measure wind speed?" She shook her head. "You have to find out what he'll burn for. And then you own that knowledge instead of him."

"And did you find out?"

Ilsa was quiet for a moment. The fire popped. Down in the settlement, someone had started bringing out food, because apparently the correct response to nearly dying in a storm followed by a document cremation was soup.

"I found out the debt was never really about money," she said. "For you, or for us. Henrik and Lukas collect debts the way you chase storms—it's not the thing itself, it's the *terms*. Knowing exactly what things cost." She reached into her coat and produced a folded sheet of paper, dry, protected from the rain with the care of a woman who respected paperwork even while watching it burn. "Your account. Marked settled. In full. Signed an hour ago, before I climbed the hill, because I already knew how the fire was going to go."

Dominic took it. His hands, he noticed distantly, were steady. Ten years of dreading this exact piece of paper in either direction—owing it, or needing it—and now it weighed nothing at all.

"Why let me?" he asked. "The chest was worth millions intact. To you. To the archive. To half the governments on the continent."

Ilsa smiled, and for once it wasn't the collector's smile. It was smaller, and older, and it made her look briefly like someone's sister rather than someone's reckoning.

"Because I watched you feed the ledgers in first," she said, "before the letters. Anybody saving what was valuable would have done it the other way around." She turned up her collar and stepped back toward the dark. "You kept the wrong half, Dominic. By every valuation I know. Which means you finally value the right things." At the edge of the light she paused. "The syndicate won't quit tonight—they're angry and muddy and men like that don't understand fires. Get your people ready. And get the letters somewhere safe by morning."

Then she was gone, downhill into the blue dark, and Dominic stood alone at the fire with a zero balance and a chest full of names.

Marisol came and stood beside him. Neither of them said anything for a while. The rain had stopped completely now, and the clouds were tearing open along the east, showing stars, showing the whole washed sky.

"So," she said finally. "What does a man with no debts do tomorrow?"

Dominic looked at the fire, at the last embers of clause seventeen glowing down to nothing, at the chest on the ground with its soft bundles of letters safe inside, and heard himself answer before he'd decided to:

"I hear the fishing's good on this river."

Marisol laughed—really laughed, the first time since the songs—and above them the stars kept coming out, more of them every minute, as if the sky were also settling its accounts.

Behind them, in the doorway of the chapel, Anneli cleared her throat. She had her satellite phone in her hand, and her face was doing something complicated.

"Dominic," she said. "My handlers just called. They know the fire happened." She held up the phone. "And they're sending someone to make sure there's nothing left to suppress. Someone who'll want to talk to the witnesses." She glanced at the settlement, at the children peering out of doorways, at Aurelio still humming. "By sunrise, we need to decide what these people are going to say exists here. Because I think—" she took a breath, and for the first time since the river, she sounded less like an archivist and more like a woman choosing a side, "—I think I know how to make sure the answer is *nothing at all*."
