## 1. The River Keeps Its Receipts

The Serei Delta at dawn smelled of diesel and rotting lotus, and Theo Zhou loved it the way you love a thing that has never once loved you back.

She stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel's Debt* — her boat, her mortgage, her whole crooked kingdom — and threaded the channel markers as the fog lifted off the water in long gray ribbons. Somewhere behind her, the river was already keeping its receipts: every crate she'd moved without papers, every toll she'd paid to men who weren't officials, every mile of water she'd claimed as neutral ground between herself and a family name that got people shot.

"Captain." Bao appeared at her elbow with two cups of tea, which was his way of saying he'd been awake all night worrying. He was nineteen and built like a bundle of reeds, and he had the delta kid's gift of appearing on any deck he pleased without making a sound. "Tide's turning early."

"Tide's always early. It's the clocks that are late." She took the cup. "Net's down?"

"Down since four. Imara says if we pull it empty again she's going to start charging us for the disappointment."

Theo grinned despite herself. Three years running salvage and light cargo out of Port Serei, and this was what she'd built: one deckhand who read currents like scripture, one cook who could make rice taste like an argument worth having, and a boat that floated. It wasn't much. It was exactly enough, and enough was the whole point.

They hauled the net off the sandbar shoals where the old trade route used to run, before the war upstream closed the passes and turned the delta into a graveyard for other people's ambitions. The winch groaned. The cable went taut, then *wrong* — a deep, dragging heaviness that made Bao look up sharply.

"That's not fish," he said.

"No," Theo said. "It isn't."

It took them an hour to bring it up, and by then Imara had abandoned the galley and Jun-less quiet of the morning to stand at the rail with her arms crossed, watching the water boil around the cable. What came out of the green murk was a chest — no, not a chest, Theo corrected herself as it swung dripping over the gunwale and crashed onto the deck. A strongbox. Iron-banded, lead-lined by its weight, sealed shut with wax poured over the lock like a wound cauterized.

And stamped into the lid, pressed deep enough to survive thirty years of silt, was a crest: a mountain split by a river, crowned with seven stars.

Theo stopped breathing.

She knew that crest. Every child of the old exile families knew it, the way children know the shape of a scar on a parent's body — half memory, half warning. The Republic of Vhalen. Gone since before she was born, drowned in the same winter her mother had walked out of the mountains carrying nothing but a coat and a name that wasn't hers anymore. Her mother had served that dead government. Had served it so well, according to the histories nobody said out loud, that when it died, everything it touched died with it.

"Captain?" Bao's voice came from very far away. "You've gone white as ballast."

"It's nothing." She set down her tea because her hands had decided they couldn't be trusted with it. "It's salvage. We'll sell it."

"To who?" Imara asked flatly. She was a broad woman from the coast with forearms like mooring line and the unimpressed stare of someone who had cooked for pirates and found them tedious. "Nobody buys lead-lined boxes with dead-country seals on them except people who already know what's inside. And those people don't pay. They collect."

The word landed in Theo's stomach like an anchor.

*Collect.*

"We pull it up, we sell it, we move on," Theo said, and heard how thin her own voice sounded. "That's the job. That's always the job."

Imara looked at her for a long moment — the look of a woman cataloguing something for later — and went back to the galley without another word. Bao lingered, chewing his lip, then touched the crest once with one finger, quick, like checking whether a stove was hot.

"The seven stars," he said quietly. "My grandmother used to say that star pattern meant *the truth keeps*. That it was carved on couriers' boxes during the war. Things too important to burn were put under seven stars and sent into the mountains."

"And your grandmother is a reliable source?"

"My grandmother is the reason I can read currents. So yes." He shrugged. "Also she lied constantly about everything else. But not about stars."

Theo crouched beside the box. Up close, the seal was worse — the wax had been stamped twice, once with the crest and once with a courier's mark she recognized from her mother's few surviving papers, the ones she kept in a oilcloth packet at the bottom of a locked drawer and never, ever looked at more than once a year. Her mother's mark. Or one identical to it.

Thirty years in the river, and here it was, surfacing in her net, in her stretch of water, on her boat. The river kept its receipts. Apparently it had been holding this one just for her.

"Get it below," she said finally. "Chain it to the mast step. If anyone asks, we pulled up engine parts."

"And if the person asking doesn't believe us?" Bao asked.

Theo looked downstream, toward Port Serei, where the market smoke was rising and the day's business was beginning, and felt the particular cold that had lived in her chest for three years shift slightly, like ice deciding whether to crack.

"Then we'll find out how good a liar I still am," she said.

* * *

They docked at Pier Nine past noon, in the shadow of the customs house whose officials Theo paid precisely enough to remain invisible. Unloading went smooth. Too smooth, she thought later; the delta never gave you a clean day without sending the bill after.

She was in the hold, wedged against the chained strongbox with a crowbar she wasn't using and couldn't stop touching, when Bao's whistle came down the hatch — two short, one long. *Strangers.*

She climbed up into the light and saw them coming along the pier: three men in matching gray coats that cost more than her boat's engine, walking with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no by anything smaller than a government. The leader carried a leather folio under one arm. None of them looked at the cargo, the cranes, or the fish stalls. All three of them looked directly at the *Kestrel's Debt*, and at her.

"Captain Zhou," the leader called, pleasant as a knife going in. "We're hoping you can help us with an inquiry."

Theo leaned on the rail and made her face do nothing at all. "Pier Nine's public. Ask away."

"We're agents of Mr. Maraval." The man opened the folio with a flourish that suggested he'd practiced it. "The collector. Perhaps you know the name."

Everyone knew the name. Maraval bought debts the way other men bought wine — by the case, for pleasure — and collected them the way other men collected teeth. Half the boats in the delta owed him something, and the half that didn't were careful to keep it that way.

"Heard of him," Theo said. "Never had the pleasure."

"No," the agent agreed, smiling. "Not yet. Mr. Maraval is an archivist, Captain. A passionate one. He recently learned that a certain item may have surfaced in these waters — an iron-bound strongbox, lead-lined, bearing the seal of the old Republic. Seven stars, split mountain. Ringing any bells?"

The cold in Theo's chest cracked clean through. Below her feet, through two decks of planking, the box sat chained to her mast like a heart she hadn't agreed to carry.

"Fishing's been poor," she said. "Mostly we pull up mud and other people's regrets."

"Mm." The agent consulted his folio, though Theo would have bet her boat he already knew every word in it. "Mr. Maraval pays generously for historical materials. Generously enough, I'm told, to clear certain... outstanding obligations. Mortgages, for instance." His eyes lifted, mild and terrible. "Yours, Captain. Held now by our employer's bank. He'd hate for anything to happen to a vessel with such a hardworking owner."

Behind her, she heard Bao go very still at the tiller, and Imara's footsteps stop in the galley doorway.

"I'll mention it to him," Theo said, "if I ever dig up anything with stars on it."

"Of course." The agent closed the folio. "You do that. Oh — and Captain? Mr. Maraval will be in Port Serei through the end of the week. He does so prefer to conclude these conversations in person." He smiled again, and it reached all the way to his eyes, which was somehow worse. "He's very thorough. You'll find he always gets what's owed to him. One way or another, the river keeps its receipts."

They walked back up the pier, three gray coats in the afternoon sun, and Theo stood at the rail watching them go, her hand resting on the rail above the place where the box lay hidden, feeling the tide turn beneath her keel — early again, always early — and understanding, with the calm clarity of a woman standing on the last dry plank of her life, that the river had finally come to collect.
