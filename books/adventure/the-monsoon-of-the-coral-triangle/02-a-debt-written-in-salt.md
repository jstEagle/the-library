## 2. A Debt Written in Salt

The men from Maraval's office wore river boots, which was how Theo knew they were trouble. Anyone who came to the Serei Delta dressed for the water they'd actually be standing in was either a fisherman or a liar, and these two had the soft hands of men who signed papers for a living.

They arrived at dusk, when the dock lamps were just guttering awake and the tide was pulling the smell of brackish mud up through the pilings. Theo was on her knees at the *Kestrel's* stern, scraping barnacles with a dull chisel, when their shadows fell across her deck.

"Captain Zhou." The taller one held a leather folio against his chest like a hymnal. "Mr. Maraval requests the pleasure of your company."

"The *Kestrel* doesn't do pleasure," Theo said, not looking up. A barnacle came free with a wet pop. "She does cargo."

"He anticipated that answer." The man smiled without warmth. "He also anticipated you'd say no to an invitation, so he's made it an appointment instead. Tomorrow, ten o'clock, the Salt House. He asks that you bring the chest."

Theo finally stood, slow, letting the chisel hang loose at her side where they could see it. "And if I don't come?"

"Then he'll come to you." The man glanced past her shoulder, at the boat — at the patched hull, the salt-scoured rails, the name half worn away. "He owns more of this delta than the delta knows. I'd think about what that means for a woman who sleeps on the water."

They left the way they'd come, unhurried, their footsteps keeping time on the planks. Theo watched them go, then looked down at the chisel in her hand and realized it had gone cold.

Behind her, from the galley hatch, Imara's voice drifted out, low and even as always: "I'm making enough for three. If it's going to be one of those nights, we should eat first."

It was always going to be one of those nights now.

* * *

The Salt House sat on stilts above the delta's widest channel, all teak and brass and windows angled to catch the light off the water without ever letting the water see in. Theo arrived at ten o'clock precisely because arriving late would look like fear and early would look like eagerness, and she refused to give Maraval either.

He received her in a room lined with maps. Not charts — maps. Old ones, framed under glass, of coastlines that had changed names two and three times over. She recognized a Vhalen survey among them before she'd taken her second breath, and hated herself for the flinch it cost her.

Maraval himself was smaller than she'd imagined. Collectors usually were; the money did the looming for them. He had white hair cut close as a crewman's and eyes the color of tea left too long, and he poured her a cup of something dark and spiced before she'd said a word.

"Captain Zhou." He gestured to a chair. "You have your mother's posture."

"I have my own posture."

"Of course." He sat across from her, folding his hands. "Let's not waste each other's morning. You pulled a chest out of the Serei three weeks ago. Sealed. Stamped with the crest of the Republic of Vhalen. I want it."

"It's salvage. Salvage law says—"

"Salvage law says it's yours, yes. I wrote half those statutes; I know them better than the judges do." He smiled, thin as paper. "Which is why I'm not asking you to sell it. I'm offering you a trade. Your debt—"

"I don't have a debt."

"Your *boat* has a debt." He slid a folio across the table and opened it with one finger. "The *Kestrel*. Mortgaged through the Delta Consolidated Bank, called due by its holder, purchased by me eight months ago through an intermediary in Port Anselm. You've been paying interest to a dead letter address, Captain. Charming arrangement. Very you."

Theo looked at the numbers and felt the deck tilt under her, though the floor was stone-still. Eight months. Eight months of payments into nothing, of believing she was slowly buying her way free. Someone had been unwinding every coin she sent.

"Forgive it," Maraval said. "All of it. The *Kestrel* becomes yours outright, clear title, this morning. In exchange for the chest. Unopened."

Something in the room went very quiet — the kind of quiet that lives inside large sums of money.

"You could burn the mortgage in front of me," Theo said carefully, "and I still wouldn't believe you'd let me keep the boat. Men like you forgive debts the way fishermen chum water."

"A reasonable suspicion."

"What's in the chest?"

His tea-colored eyes didn't move. Didn't blink, almost. "Records. Correspondence. The paper ghosts of a government that died badly. I am, by reputation, a collector of exactly such things."

"Reputations are cargo too," Theo said. "Easy to relabel." She leaned forward. "You didn't send two men to my dock over paper ghosts. You sent them because whatever's in that chest isn't dead yet. And things that aren't dead can still hurt people. So here's my counteroffer, Mr. Maraval. I keep the chest. I keep my debt. And you keep wondering."

For the first time, something moved behind his face — not anger. Interest. Which was worse.

"Pride," he said softly, "is an expensive cargo, Captain. It sinks more boats than weather does."

"So does rot," Theo said, standing. "And I know rot when I smell it. Thank you for the tea."

At the door, his voice followed her, pleasant as a hook through the gills: "The delta is a small place, Captain Zhou. But the mountains—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid down beside a plate, "—the mountains are full of snow this time of year. Do give my regards to whoever ends up holding that chest when you're done running."

* * *

She burned the mooring papers that night.

Not because it was smart — Bao told her, in his flat, unimpressed way, that it was possibly the single least smart thing she'd done since he'd known her, and Bao had known her four years. She burned them because Maraval owned the mooring, and the bank, and probably the chandler who sold her rope, and every tether she had in the delta was a hand around her ankle. Pride over prudence, Imara called it later, shaking her head, but her hands kept ladling stew while she said it, and nobody left the galley.

Bao found her at the stern rail afterward, watching the ash drift down onto black water.

"You know what happens now," he said. "We're nomads. No port rights, no moorage, no protection. Just the river."

"The river's been good to me."

"The river drowned my cousin." He leaned on the rail beside her. He was twenty-three and built like a coiled hawser, with a face that gave away nothing except when he was eating, when it gave away everything. "So. Where are we running?"

Theo looked at him. "You don't have to come. Maraval's quarrel is with me. I can drop you at Kessing Town tonight—"

"You can drop me nowhere." Bao picked a fleck of ash off the rail. "Four years you've paid me late and fed me well and never once asked where I slept when we were in port, which was nowhere, because I didn't have anywhere. This boat is the only address I've got." He shrugged. "Besides. Somebody competent should be aboard when you drown us."

From the galley doorway, Imara said, "Amen," and handed them both bowls.

Imara had come aboard two years ago fleeing something she never named, with nothing but a knife roll and a spice tin, and she cooked like a woman settling scores with every meal. She was sixty if she was a day, broad as a bollard, and she had opinions about Theo's judgment that she expressed primarily through portion sizes. Tonight everyone's bowl was generous. Reconciliation stew. Or farewell stew. With Imara it was hard to tell, and she wasn't saying.

"We'll need supplies," Theo said, into the steam. "Real ones. Water, rice, lamp oil, rope. Enough for a long run upstream."

"Upstream." Bao set down his bowl. "Nobody goes upstream this season. The Kessing Rapids are running high, and past them there's nothing but mountain country and customs men with bad tempers."

"There's something past the rapids," Theo said. "There's always something past the rapids. That's the whole problem with rivers — they insist on going somewhere."

Imara snorted. Bao stared at his captain for a long moment, reading her the way he read weather, and whatever he saw made him sigh the sigh of a man boarding a sinking ship on purpose.

"I'll check the fuel lines," he said, and went.

Imara stayed, elbows on the rail, watching the delta breathe around them.

"That man in the fine room," she said quietly. "He knew your mother."

It wasn't a question. Theo's silence answered it anyway.

"My mother's dead," Theo said at last. "Eleven years. And everything she touched went into the ground with her, and I intend to keep it there."

"Mm." Imara collected the bowls. At the hatch she paused. "Child, I've buried two husbands and a revolution. Things don't stay in the ground. They wait for someone foolish enough to dig." She tapped the deck once with her sandal, twice, a little rhythm she did when she meant something. "This boat floats. That's all a home needs to do. Wherever you point her, we eat."

She went below. Theo stood alone at the rail until the lamps along the far shore went out one by one, and the delta became water and stars and nothing else.

In the hold, under tarp and chain and a false floor she'd built herself, the chest waited with its extinct republic stamped on its lid, patient as a debt.

By dawn the *Kestrel* had cast off, burning her own papers' ashes off her decks, and pointed her bow upriver — toward the rapids, the mountains, and the snows.

They were two days past Kessing Town when Theo spotted the skiff trailing them: a single figure, rowing with the tireless stroke of someone who'd learned boats in a library rather than on water, gaining on them with maddening patience.

Bao lowered the glass, frowning. "He's flying no colors. But he's carrying books, Captain. Actual books. Lashed down like cargo."

Theo watched the stranger come on and felt, beneath her ribs, the old dread stir — the feeling that the river had decided to return something she'd thrown in it years ago.

"Let him board," she heard herself say. "But I want to see his eyes when he does."
