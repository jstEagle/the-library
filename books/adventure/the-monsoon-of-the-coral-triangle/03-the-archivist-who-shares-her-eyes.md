## 3. The Archivist Who Shares Her Eyes

Jun Zhou had been waiting on the dock at Serei Landing for three days, long enough that the fishmongers had stopped trying to sell him dried eel and started trying to sell him sympathy. He sat on a coil of rope with a leather case across his knees and a cataloguer's brass badge pinned to his coat, and he watched the *Kestrel's Debt* come downriver with her hull riding too low, the way a boat rides when she's carrying something her captain doesn't want anyone to weigh.

He knew the boat. He knew the captain. He had spent eleven years learning both from paper, and paper, he had found, was more patient than any teacher.

When the gangplank dropped, he stood, brushed the dock dust from his coat, and walked toward the woman at the rail with the practiced gait of a man who had rehearsed this moment in a hundred mirrors and was still, underneath all of it, terrified.

"Captain Zhou," he called. "I'm told you salvage. I'm told you sell. I'm told you don't ask where things come from."

Theo looked down at him the way she looked down at everything — a long, flat assessment, like a hawk deciding whether the thing below it was prey, weather, or a waste of altitude. "You're told a lot of things."

"I'm Jun Wei. I catalogue for the Meridian Archive in Port Callas." He held up the badge. It was genuine; that was the first thing he'd spent money on. "I pay in advance, in silver, and I never ask where things come from."

That was the second thing he'd spent money on: learning exactly what a woman running from her own name most wanted to hear.

* * *

Theo didn't like him. That was her first and most durable instinct, and she had learned to trust her instincts the way other people trusted charts. The archivist had soft hands and a soft voice and eyes that were the wrong color to be a stranger's — a dark amber flecked with green, the exact amber-flecked green she saw every morning in the river's mirror, the eyes her mother had worn, the eyes her mother had passed down like a debt.

She let him aboard anyway, because silver was silver and Bao had been eating through the stores like a boy with a hollow leg.

He worked for two days before he made his mistake. Or his move. She never decided which.

He was cataloguing the salvage hold on the second evening — she watched him through the hatch, because she watched him always — and he lifted a brass fitting from a drowned river chapel, turned it over, and said, quietly, to no one, in a dialect of Vhaleni that had been dead for fifteen years: "Mother used to sing this. The fitting-song. She said the chapel bells were tuned to it."

Theo came down the ladder fast enough to make the rungs ring.

"Say that again."

Jun turned. He didn't flinch, which she noted, and didn't apologize, which she noted harder. "You heard me."

"Nobody speaks chapel-Vhaleni. Nobody under forty. Nobody outside the mountains."

"I'm not outside the mountains," he said. "I'm not under forty. And I'm not nobody." He reached into his coat, slow, telegraphing every inch of the motion the way you'd move near a dog you didn't know, and drew out an oilcloth packet. He set it on the crate between them and stepped back. "Letters. Forty-one of them. Written by Lin Zhou between the fall of Vhalen and the year she died."

Theo looked at the packet and did not touch it. Her heart was doing something complicated and humiliating behind her ribs, and she kept her face still because her face was the only thing on this river that had never been for sale.

"Where did you get those."

"From her. Before she died. I was nine."

The hold went very quiet. Somewhere above, Imara was banging a pot, and the sound came down the hatch like weather from another country.

"You were nine," Theo repeated. "When my mother died, I was nine."

"I know," Jun said. "I've done the arithmetic more times than you'd believe."

* * *

He told her the rest in the galley, over Imara's fish stew, which he ate with the careful appetite of a man who knew the meal might be his last. Bao sat at the end of the bench, spoon frozen halfway to his mouth, watching the two of them like a spectator at a knife fight. Imara stood at the stove with her back turned, which fooled no one; Imara missed nothing.

"She was a river pilot too, before the Republic fell," Jun said. "She ran the northern channels. She met my father in Port Callas — a shipping clerk, nobody, a man who kept his head down so well he eventually disappeared into it. She stayed until I was ten. Then she went back upriver." His hands were steady around the bowl. "She wrote to me every month until she couldn't."

"She never wrote to me," Theo said. It came out flat, and she hated the flatness, because underneath it was the thing she'd carried since she was small enough to still believe her mother would come back up the river one season with a chest full of explanations.

"No," Jun said. "She wrote *about* you. Every letter. 'My Theo is running the boat alone this season, she's fourteen and the crew obeys her like a tide.' 'My Theo burned her hand on the boiler and didn't cry, she laughed, which frightened the doctor.'" He paused. "I can recite them. All forty-one. I had nothing else to memorize for a long time."

"That's not proof," Theo said. "That's a performance. Anyone who studied her could write that. Anyone who'd bought her letters off a dead man's estate could write that."

"Ask me something," Jun said. "Something a stranger couldn't buy. Something from before the letters."

Theo set down her spoon. She looked at him across the galley table, at the amber-green eyes that were hers, that were her mother's, and she chose the question the way you'd choose a blade.

"What did she call the boat we lived on before the *Kestrel*? Not its real name. The name she used."

Jun didn't hesitate, and that was the worst part. He didn't hesitate, and he didn't smile, and his voice didn't do anything clever. He just said, "The *Slow Answer*. Because she said the river always gave one, eventually, and never the one you wanted."

The galley tilted. Or Theo did. She gripped the edge of the table and stared at a man who had just reached into the sealed room of her childhood, past every lock she'd built, and set one small true thing down on the table like a candle.

Nobody knew that name. She had never spoken it aloud to another living soul. Her mother had only ever used it at night, on the water, in the low voice you use for things that are holy or embarrassing.

"Get off my boat," Theo said.

"Captain—" Bao started.

"Not you." She was still looking at Jun. "You. Off. At the next landing."

"All right," Jun said, and to her lasting fury he didn't argue, didn't plead, didn't perform heartbreak. He gathered his letters, stood, and said, "But I'll tell you why I came, first, because you deserve to hear it from me and not from Maraval's men, who are two days behind me and asking the same questions at every dock. He knows what was in that chest you pulled up. He knows what it leads to. And when he catches you — and he will catch you, Captain, he's never once failed to catch anyone — you'll want one person aboard who isn't there for the silver."

"And you're not?"

"I'm there for her letters," Jun said. "And for the truth about how she died. Maraval can't offer me either." He paused at the ladder. "You have her eyes, you know. I used to think that meant something. I still do."

* * *

He didn't get off at the next landing. He didn't get off at the one after that, either, because Theo never actually told him to again, and they both understood the shape of the silence she'd left where the order should have been.

She told herself it was tactics. A man who could recite forty-one letters was a man who knew where the rest of her mother's paper trail lay, and Maraval was coming, and knowledge was ammunition. She told herself that watching him was a security measure: she posted him in the salvage hold by day and slept with the galley door open by night, one ear tuned to his breathing through the bulkhead, cataloguing his sounds the way he catalogued brass. She told herself a great many things, and the telling had the desperate fluency of a woman arguing with a river current.

The truth was smaller and worse. The truth was that when he read the letters aloud in the evenings — and he read them, at her grudging demand, one per night, like a dose of medicine — his voice did something to the air in the galley. Her mother's voice, reconstructed, the cadences coming back to her across fifteen years of silence: *the river keeps its receipts, Theo-girl, never forget the river keeps its receipts.* Theo would sit with her arms crossed and her face carved into indifference, and inside she was nine years old again, standing on a dock, watching a boat get smaller.

Grief, she was learning, could be used as a weapon. It was just a question of who was holding it.

"He sleeps with his boots on," Bao reported to her on the fourth night, crouched by the wheel while Theo took the middle watch. "Boots on, coat folded, case under his head. Like a man expecting to be thrown off a boat any minute."

"Good," Theo said. "Let him expect it."

"He cried in the hold today. Thought nobody saw. Found one of the fittings from the chapel and held it a long time."

Theo said nothing. The river said nothing. Somewhere ahead, dark water moved around dark banks, and two days behind them, if the archivist was honest, a collector's men were moving dock to dock like a slow tide with a warrant.

"You could just ask him," Bao said. "About her. Instead of watching him like he's cargo that might spoil."

"I don't ask questions I'm afraid of the answers to, Bao. It's how I've stayed alive."

Bao looked at her for a long moment with that open, unguarded face of his, the face that had no idea what a liability it was. "Maybe," he said, "that's also how you've stayed alone."

She sent him below. She stood at the wheel with the dark water sliding by, and behind her, through the open galley door, she could hear the archivist's breathing, slow and even, and the oilcloth crackle of letters being handled in the dark. He wasn't asleep either. Neither of them was ever asleep, really. They were two people standing in the wreckage of the same woman, taking inventory.

At dawn, Imara came up the ladder two steps at a time, which Imara never did, and her face had gone the color of old ash.

"Smoke," she said. "Astern. A gunboat, running lights dark, and she's not slowing for the shallows." A pause. "Captain — she's flying no colors at all. And she's gaining."
