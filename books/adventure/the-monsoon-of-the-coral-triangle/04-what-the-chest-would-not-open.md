## 4. What the Chest Would Not Open

Imara had cooked for admirals and for men who ate with their knives, and she could tell the difference between a crew that was afraid and a crew that was pretending not to be. For three days since the gunboat appeared astern — there, then gone, then there again like a shark's fin circling a slow whale — the *Serei Rose* had been pretending.

Theo ran them upstream through water no cargo boat had business in, shallows braiding and rebraiding, the pilot charts twenty years out of date. Bao worked the winches without being asked twice. Jun Zhou sat wherever he was told to sit and catalogued salvage in a ledger he kept angled away from everyone, which fooled nobody. And Imara cooked. That was her answer to most things: when the world went wrong, you fed people anyway, because hunger made fear worse and fear made people stupid, and stupid got you dead on a river full of sandbars.

The chest sat in the hold where it had sat since the delta, lashed under netting like something they were ashamed of. Theo wouldn't let anyone touch it. Jun asked about it once a day, politely, the way a man asks about a wound. And every night Imara came down past it with her lantern and found herself slowing, the way you slow past a door you can hear breathing behind.

On the fourth night, she stopped pretending too.

* * *

She went down after the galley was scrubbed, carrying the ring of keys she'd inherited from three previous cooks on three previous boats — keys to storerooms, spice chests, a strongbox some dead captain had left bolted under a bunk, keys whose locks had long since rusted into other shapes. Cooks collect keys. Nobody notices a cook collecting keys, because everybody assumes she's just organized.

The chest was iron-banded teak, salt-scoured, its lid carved with the crest of Vhalen: a river forked around an island, crowned with nine stars. A nation that had drowned itself forty years ago rather than be conquered, and taken its archives with it. Imara knew the story the way everyone on these rivers knew it — as a warning, mostly. Don't keep records. Records are what they come for.

The lock was a brass plate the size of her palm, set flush into the lid, with a single keyhole at its center. She tried her smallest key first. It slid in and turned a quarter and stopped, gentle as a hand on a wrist. She tried the next. The next. Nine keys, nine refusals, each one stopping at exactly the same quarter-turn, each one stopping *softly*, without the grind of tumblers refusing.

Imara crouched there a long moment, lantern low, listening to the boat work around her — timbers ticking, water hissing along the hull, Bao snoring somewhere aft like a landslide in miniature.

Then she looked closer at the plate.

Around the keyhole, in concentric rings, someone had engraved numbers. Tiny ones, worn nearly smooth, but her eyes were good — thirty years of reading spice labels by bad light. Three rings. Outer ring: numerals one through twelve. Middle ring: one through thirty. Inner ring: smaller marks, finer divisions.

Not tumblers. Not wards. A calendar? No —

She'd grown up on a tidal coast, before the war took her village and the sea took everything else. Her grandmother had read tides off a carved board every morning of her life, three numbers spoken like a prayer: the hour, the day, the height.

Tide-tables. The lock wanted tide-tables.

But tides of *where*?

Imara sat back on her heels. Somewhere above her, faint through the deck, she heard Theo and Jun arguing in low voices — the same argument they'd been having for days, wearing different words. She couldn't make out what they said. She didn't need to. It was always the same argument, on every boat she'd ever served, in every war she'd ever survived: *what do we owe the dead, and what do we owe the living, and which debt comes due first.*

She put her keys away. She did not tell anyone yet. A cook knows the value of a thing best when she's the only one who knows it exists.

* * *

The gunboat showed itself at dusk two days later, standing off a mile astern in the last light, close enough now that Imara could see figures on its deck through her spyglass — no, through Bao's spyglass, which she'd borrowed without asking, another perk of being the cook. Dark hull. No colors. A single figure at the rail who never moved, watching them the way Maraval's agents had watched the docks back in the delta: patiently, proprietarily, like a man looking at property.

"They're not attacking," Bao said beside her. He'd come up for air and stayed, chewing dried fish with the steady rhythm of a boy who processed the world through his jaw. "Why aren't they attacking?"

"Because they don't need to," Imara said. "They know where we're going."

Bao stopped chewing. "We're going somewhere?"

"We're going wherever that chest tells us to go," Imara said, "and so are they, and Captain Zhou knows it, and that's why she hasn't slept since the delta." She handed him the spyglass. "Eat your fish."

That night the argument in the wheelhouse finally came down to the galley table, because Theo made it come down there. She spread the chart of the upper Serei on the table between the stew bowls and said, "We can't outrun her forever. The river narrows ahead. Two more days and there's nowhere to hide a boat this size. So we decide now: we scuttle the chest in deep water and run for the border, or we open it and find out what everyone is willing to kill us for."

"Open it," Bao said immediately.

"You don't know what's in it."

"Don't want to. That's why I want to open it." He said it with total sincerity, and Imara loved him a little for it, the way you love a dog that barks at thunder.

Jun said nothing at first. He sat with his hands flat on the table, and Imara watched him decide, visibly, whether to spend the coin he'd been hoarding. "My mother's letters mention a place," he said finally. "An archive in the ice. Vhalen didn't burn its records when the Republic fell. They carried them up into the mountains and sealed them in a glacier, to be opened when it was safe to be Vhalen again." His voice was very even, the evenness of a man holding something heavy at arm's length. "If the chest holds a route to that archive, then it holds whatever your mother thought was worth sealing in ice. Including, possibly, the truth about her."

"The truth about her," Theo repeated, flat. "You mean the charge. You mean the treason."

"I mean both versions," Jun said. "There are always both versions. That's what archives are *for*."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the river muttered over its stones, and far astern — Imara felt it more than heard it, a pressure in the ear like weather coming — the gunboat held its distance, patient as arithmetic.

"And if the truth ruins her?" Theo asked. "If I open that chest and find my mother was exactly what the histories say she was?"

Imara set down her ladle. She hadn't planned to speak. But the question hung there wrong-shaped, and wrong-shaped questions in a galley were her jurisdiction. "Captain," she said, "I've served eleven boats and two wars, and I'll tell you what I've learned about truth. It's like salt pork. It keeps whether you want it or not. You can leave it in the barrel and pretend it isn't there, but it's still in the hold, and it's still heavier than anything else you're carrying." She wiped her hands. "Only question is whether you carry it knowing, or carry it blind."

Theo looked at her for a long moment. Then at Bao, who nodded with his mouth full. Then at Jun, who hadn't blinked since he'd started talking, as if blinking might cost him the floor.

"All right," Theo said quietly. "Tomorrow. We open it tomorrow."

* * *

Except Imara opened it that night.

She hadn't decided to. She'd gone down to salt the pork — genuinely, actually, to salt the pork; the hold was cold and the barrels were there — and the chest was there, and the lantern was in her hand, and her feet simply made the decision her head had been circling for a week.

Because here was the thing she hadn't said at the table: she already knew how the lock worked. She'd known for two days. And a lock that reads tide-tables is a lock built for a particular kind of person — not soldiers, not thieves, not collectors with their careful gloved hands. A lock built for someone who grew up reading water. Someone coastal. Someone who learned the sea's arithmetic before she learned her letters.

Someone like a woman from the drowned delta towns, who had once, before the treason charges and the exile and the daughter born on a riverboat, served a government that governed by tide and star.

The chest wasn't locked against strangers. It was locked against everyone except family.

Imara knelt in the cold hold and set the rings with fingers that shook only a little. High tide at the old Vhalen harbor roads: she'd have to guess the station, but the delta towns all shared their tide within minutes, and her grandmother's board had given her the pattern — spring tide, the third day of the waxing moon, the great high water that came twice a month and flooded the salt fields.

Hour: seven. Day: seventeen. Height: the ninth mark, the flood mark, the one her grandmother called *the sea remembering itself.*

The inner ring clicked home like a knuckle cracking.

The lid rose six inches on its own, released by a counterweight, exhaling cold air that smelled of cedar oil and forty years of dark. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark: a tube of rolled vellum, and beneath it a book bound in grey leather, unmarked, its pages furred at the edges with frost-crystals that had survived every climate between here and wherever it had been.

Imara unrolled the vellum halfway across the hold floor. A route map — not river charts but mountain ones, contour lines stacked like fingerprints, a glacier drawn in blue ink running down a valley between two walls of stone. Along its length, in a small precise hand, annotations: dates. Calving windows. *Pass opens: six days. Closes: four days after.* And at the glacier's head, circled twice, a mark like the crest on the lid — the forked river, the nine stars — with a word beneath it in old Vhaleni script that Imara couldn't read but didn't need to. She'd seen enough maps to know a destination when it was circled twice.

The Ice Archive. Real. Reachable. Six days' climb beyond the river's end, if the ice gave them the days.

She should have called them. All of them, right then, lantern raised, voice ringing off the beams. Instead she opened the book, because the book was lying there and she was Imara, and Imara had never in her life left a closed thing closed.

The first page was a confession. She recognized the handwriting instantly, though she'd only seen it once — on the mooring papers Theo had burned, the signature she'd glimpsed as the flames took them. *Lin Zhou.* The same slanted capitals, the same fierce downward stroke.

She read it by lantern-light while the boat rocked and the river ran and the gunboat, unseen, held its patient mile of dark water astern. It was short. Confessions usually are. It named names, dates, a massacre in a valley whose name Imara didn't know, orders given and orders refused, and at the bottom, in lines that pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*I did not burn the valley. I carried the proof of who did, and I will carry it until the ice takes it, and when the ice gives it back, let my daughter be the one to read it. Let her know which account survives.*

Let my daughter.

Imara sat back in the freezing hold with her heart going like a drum in a procession, and upstairs she heard the wheelhouse door open, boots on the deck, Theo's voice calling down, sharp with alarm — because of course Theo had counted the lanterns, of course she'd noticed the hold lit at three in the morning, of course she came armed.

She appeared at the foot of the ladder with a knife in her fist and her mother's eyes blazing, and Imara, cook of the *Serei Rose*, keeper of keys and salt and now of one impossible secret, stepped aside so the lantern-light fell full on the open chest, the map, the book, the name.

"Captain," Imara said. "It was waiting for you. It was only ever waiting for you."
