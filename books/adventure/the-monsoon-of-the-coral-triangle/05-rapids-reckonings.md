## 5. Rapids, Reckonings

Bao had run the Kessing Rapids twice in his life, both times by daylight, both times with a pilot who knew the water like his own mother's face. Tonight there was no daylight, no pilot, and the woman at the wheel was running on a chart drawn from memory and a stubbornness that bordered on theology.

"You're sure about the channel," he said, for the third time.

"I'm sure about the moon," Theo said. "It sets in forty minutes. After that, we're blind."

The *Kestrel's Debt* — Imara had named her that as a joke, and the name had stuck the way names do, like burrs — slid through the black water with her lamps hooded and her engine muffled to a mutter. Behind them, maybe two miles back, the gunboat's searchlight swept the banks in long white strokes, patient as a lighthouse that had learned to hunt.

Bao crouched at the bow with a pole he didn't trust and listened to the river. That was his job, or the job Theo had given him when she'd pulled him out of a debtors' cell in Port Serei: listen to the water, tell her what it said. Most nights the water said ordinary things. Tonight it was saying something with teeth in it.

"Drop," he called back. "Big one. Then it goes quiet — too quiet."

"Quiet is good."

"Quiet before the Kessing isn't good. Quiet means the channel narrows and the water stacks up."

Theo was silent a moment. He could see her silhouette at the wheel, one hand on the spokes, the other flat against the chart case like she could hold the map down through sheer will. "How narrow?"

"Boat-and-a-half wide, if the old charts are right. And they're thirty years old."

"Then we go through single file with ourselves." She raised her voice without raising its volume, which was a trick Bao had never figured out. "Imara — kill the galley fire. Jun — get forward and be ballast, not opinion."

Jun Zhou came up the deck in the dark, moving carefully, and took his place beside Bao at the rail. The archivist had been aboard eleven days now, and Bao still hadn't decided what he was. He claimed to be Theo's half-brother. He carried letters in their mother's hand. He knew things — small things, terrible small things, like the way Theo hummed when she thought no one could hear, like the name of a dog she'd had when she was seven. But knowing wasn't being, and Bao kept his own counsel.

"The gunboat's closing," Jun said quietly. "I can hear her engines over ours."

"Then she can hear us too."

"No. We're downwind of the noise. She's chasing an echo." He paused. "Your captain knows that. She's been steering toward the rapids since dusk."

Bao looked back at the wheel. Theo's face was a pale smudge above the collar of her coat, and she wasn't looking at the gunboat at all. She was looking upstream, into the dark where the river went loud.

*She means to run it,* he realized. *She'd rather risk the Kessing than let Maraval take the chest.* And beneath that thought, colder: *she'd rather risk us.*

Except that wasn't quite true either, and the not-quite-true part was what scared him.

* * *

The drop came exactly where Bao had said it would, and it came like the end of the world in miniature. The bow lifted, hung, and then the river reached up and took them.

For ninety seconds Bao stopped being a person and became cargo. Water hit the deck in a solid wall, tore the tarp off the aft hold, and threw him against the rail hard enough to rearrange his understanding of his own ribs. Somewhere behind him Imara was shouting words in three languages, none of them prayers. The engine screamed, caught, screamed again. And Theo — Theo stood at the wheel like she'd grown out of the deck, hands moving, correcting, reading the dark water by feel alone, and brought them through the standing wave with the hull groaning but whole.

Then the quiet. The bad quiet.

"Channel," Bao gasped, hauling himself upright. "It's here. I can hear it — the water's stacking—"

Ahead, the moonlight — the last of it, dying on the ridge — showed him the truth all at once: the river pinching between black rock walls, the surface smooth as poured glass and lying about it completely, the current underneath moving like a freight train through a doorway. Thirty-year-old charts. Boat-and-a-half wide.

They were a boat and a quarter.

"Hold her center!" Theo shouted. "Bao — pole starboard, push us left—"

He got the pole down and the current took it like it owed it money. The wrench nearly took his shoulder out of its socket, and then the boat's stern kissed the rock wall with a sound like a struck bell, and everything after that happened in pieces:

The wheel spinning. Theo's voice, sharp, wordless now, just command-shaped noise. The port side swinging toward the far wall. A rock tooth rising out of the water dead ahead, black and wet and patient. And Bao — off balance, still holding a pole that was holding nothing — going over the rail the way water goes over a weir, without decision, without ceremony.

The cold closed on him like a fist.

He had grown up on this river. He had swum it in flood season for money, for dares, for reasons he couldn't reconstruct. None of it prepared him for the Kessing at night, for water that didn't want him to swim so much as it wanted him to be somewhere else, immediately, preferably against rock. He surfaced once, saw the boat sliding past him — saw Theo's face turned toward him over the rail, mouth open — and then the river folded him under and began the long business of deciding what to do with him.

He hit rocks. He remembered that in flashes: shoulder, hip, the heel of his hand. He remembered trying to breathe and getting the river instead. He remembered thinking, with strange clarity, *the chest is going to make it and I'm not*, and feeling no bitterness about it, only a kind of distant bookkeeping regret.

Then something hit the water near him. Not debris. Something that had jumped.

* * *

Later — much later, when he could stand to think about it — Bao would try to build the moment into sense and fail. What he knew was this: a second splash, close enough to drench his face. Then an arm across his chest, iron-hard, and a voice in his ear saying, with insane calm, "Feet up. Feet UP, Bao. Point them downstream."

Theo. Theo, who had handed the wheel to Jun — Jun, who had never steered anything bigger than a filing cabinet — and gone over the rail after him. Into the Kessing. At night. Against every law of seamanship, self-preservation, and common sense that Bao had ever heard spoken aloud.

They went through the rest of the rapid together, which is to say the river went through them. She kept his head above water by main force, taking hits across the back that he felt her body flinch from, her arm never loosening. When the channel finally spat them out into the slow braided shallows below the gorge, she hauled him onto a gravel bar and collapsed beside him, coughing river, shaking so hard he could feel it through the ground.

"You—" Bao got a breath. Another. "You gave Jun the wheel."

"He's a fast learner."

"He's an *archivist*."

"He's my brother," Theo said, and then coughed again, as if the word itself needed clearing from her throat. "Probably. Possibly. It was the only wheel there was."

Bao lay on the gravel and stared at the stars wheeling overhead, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since Port Serei. This woman had taken him out of a cell because no one else had. She paid Imara double and pretended it was standard rate. She'd burned her own mooring papers rather than trade the chest for her boat's freedom — her *home*, the only thing she owned. And tonight she had jumped into a rapid that killed pilots with thirty years of charts, for him. For Bao, who poled cargo and listened to water and had never once been worth drowning for.

And she would not do any of it for herself. That was the part that sat in his chest like swallowed stone. He'd watched her for weeks now. She rationed her own food without announcing it. She slept in shifts that always gave her the short end. If the boat went down, Bao understood with total certainty, Theo Zhou would get every soul aboard to shore and then stand on the bank and watch her life's work sink with an expression of mild inconvenience, and she would call it fair.

"That was the stupidest thing anyone has ever done for me," he said.

"Noted."

"They'll put it on your grave marker. 'Also stupid.'"

"It'll be a crowded marker." She rolled onto her back beside him. Her breathing was evening out. "Jun's bringing the boat down to pick us up. He sounded terrified. It suited him."

Bao laughed, and it hurt, and he laughed anyway. Upstream, faint but unmistakable, came a different sound: a deep, grinding crack, followed by a boom that rolled down the gorge like thunder trapped in stone. Both of them sat up.

Through the dark they watched the gunboat's searchlight swing wild — panicking, sweeping sky, bank, water — and then die altogether. A minute later the sound reached them again, softer: metal on rock, many times, in sequence. The Kessing had found the hull that had been hunting them, and the Kessing kept better receipts than any bank.

"She ran the narrows with her lights up," Theo said slowly. "Full speed. Chasing us."

"She won't chase anything for a while."

"No." Theo was quiet a moment. "Maraval won't stop, though. Men like him don't drown. They walk around the obstacle." She looked downriver, toward the mountains just visible as a darker darkness against the sky. "He'll go overland. Which means we've bought time, not safety."

"Then we spend it well."

She glanced at him. In the starlight her expression was unreadable, but Bao had spent weeks learning to read her, and what he saw underneath was exhaustion, and relief, and something almost like surprise — as though she genuinely could not understand why any of them were still aboard.

That settled it, whatever *it* was. Bao didn't ask permission from Imara or Jun later, because none was needed; some decisions a crew makes silently, the way water decides to be a river. Theo belonged to them now. She could pretend otherwise. She could keep rationing her own supper and giving away the safe sleeping shifts. It changed nothing. You did not jump into the Kessing for hired help, and you did not get jumped-for and remain unclaimed.

When the *Kestrel's Debt* slid out of the dark with Jun white-knuckled at the wheel and Imara hanging over the rail already shouting about hypothermia and hot tea, Bao let himself be hauled aboard, and he watched Theo climb up last, dripping, refusing Imara's blanket until Imara simply wrapped it around her and tied it, daring her to object.

Ahead, the river bent east, toward the foothills, toward the range where the snows were already falling and a glacier waited with a pass that opened for days, not weeks. Somewhere on the moraine beyond, a man who didn't drown was walking.

Theo stood at the wheel again, wrapped in someone else's blanket, and steered into the dark like she owned it.

Bao went below to dry out, and to sleep, for the first time in his adult life, without checking where the exits were.
