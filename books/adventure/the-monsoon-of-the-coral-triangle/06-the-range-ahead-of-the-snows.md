## 6. The Range Ahead of the Snows

The boat died at a place called Kettle Landing, which was not a landing at all anymore — just a shelf of gray shingle where the Serei gave up pretending to be navigable and became something white and furious instead. Theo ran her aground with the tenderness of a woman putting down an old dog, hand on the wheel long after the engine was off.

"She kept us alive," Bao said.

"She kept us afloat. Different thing." Theo stepped over the rail onto stone that crunched with frost. "Boats are for rivers. We're done with rivers."

Jun stood at the stern with the rest of them, watching the current take the wake apart, and understood that this was the moment he had been dreading since Maraval first put his name on a contract in a room that smelled of cedar and money. On the river, Jun had been useful. He could read charts, negotiate passage, speak four of the six dialects they'd need below the pass. On the mountain, usefulness would have to be earned in a currency he'd never carried: rope callus, lung capacity, the willingness to be cold for days without complaint.

Imara came up beside him with the galley pack already shouldered, absurdly calm, as if abandoning their home of two months were a matter of closing a cupboard. "You've never climbed anything," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I climbed the stairs of the Vhalen Consulate in Corvessa once. Fourteen flights."

"Was anyone shooting at you?"

"Not yet."

"Then no," she said, "you haven't," and walked down the plank.

* * *

They made camp the first night in the lee of a moraine ridge, three hours above the landing, and it was there — with the fire small and blue because everything burned fast and mean at altitude — that Jun told them.

He had rehearsed it forty times on the climb. He had rehearsed it on the river, too, in the dark of the hold among the crates, and every rehearsal had ended with him keeping silent. But the mountain changed the arithmetic. Up here there would be moments when someone's life hung on whether the person holding the other end of the rope wanted them alive. He would not carry Maraval's secret up a glacier like ballast.

"I was sent to find you," he said.

Theo looked up from the map she was studying by firelight. She didn't move otherwise. Imara stopped stirring the pot. Bao, who had been re-lashing a strap, went still with the buckle half-closed.

"Maraval hired me eight months ago," Jun said. "Not to take the chest. To find the person who'd eventually salvage it. He knew about the tide-lock — he's known about the Ice Archive for years, but the route dies with the people who mapped it, and your mother was one of those people. He reasoned the map would come home to blood." He turned his hands palm-up over the fire, an old archivist's gesture, showing what he held. "I was paid to board your boat, catalogue your salvage, and report your heading. I did all three. For six weeks."

Bao set the buckle down very carefully, the way a man does when he is deciding whether to throw something.

"The letters," Theo said. Her voice was flat as slate. "Our mother's letters. Were those part of the assignment?"

"No." The word came out of him faster than he meant it to, raw-edged. "Those are mine. Those were always mine. She wrote to my father for eleven years — I found them after he died, in a crate marked *kitchenware*. I spent two years reading them before I ever heard Maraval's name. When he offered me the job, I took it because it put me on your river. That was the sin of it. I used her letters as a door key." He swallowed. "I'm not asking you to forgive that. I'm telling you so you know exactly what you're roping yourself to tomorrow."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, ice shifted in the dark with a sound like a door opening in an empty house.

"You reported our heading," Theo said. "To the man who burned my mooring papers out from under me."

"Yes."

"And then the gunboat broke its hull in the Kessing Rapids, and you stayed aboard a sinking expedition with no pay coming and no way home."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Jun had expected anger. He had prepared for anger; anger had a shape he could answer. What he hadn't prepared for was the question itself, asked without heat, the way she might ask the depth of a ford.

"Because somewhere between the delta and the rapids," he said slowly, "I stopped working for him. I don't know the exact day. I know it was before Bao went under the water, because when Theo dove in after him I remember thinking: *this is what the letters were describing.* Not the words. The woman. And I couldn't sell that to a man who collects dead governments the way other men collect stamps."

Theo held his eyes for a long moment. Then she looked back down at the map, and said only, "Sleep. We move at first light."

It wasn't absolution. Jun lay in his bedroll listening to the glacier tick and groan, and understood that it wasn't even trust. It was a stay of judgment, granted provisionally, reviewable daily. He found, to his surprise, that he could live on that.

* * *

She tested him the next morning, and she was not subtle about it.

At the base of the first pitch — a wall of wet granite veined with running meltwater, the kind of climb that punished hesitation — Theo divided the gear. Bao got the good static line, yellow and supple. Imara got the new carabiners, still bright with factory grease. Jun got the rope from the bottom of the boat's locker: a length of kernmantle gone gray and fuzzy, its sheath abraded to fuzz along a full arm's span, stiff with old water damage, the kind of line any sensible climber retires to tie around luggage.

He weighed it in his hands and understood immediately. This was the trial. Not the wall — the rope.

"Check your knots," Theo said, not looking at him. "All of them. Twice."

He checked them twice. Then a third time, because the rope deserved it. He flaked it out coil by coil, feeling for soft spots, and found one near the middle — a core shot hidden under the fuzz, the inner strands pale and crushed. He cut six feet off either side of the weak point with his knife, sacrificed the length, re-tied, re-checked, and when Theo glanced over, he was simply coiling the shortened line as if nothing had happened.

She watched him do it. Said nothing.

The pitch took them four hours. It should have taken two, but the rock wept meltwater and Bao's boots were wrong for granite, and twice Jun anchored himself in a crack and lowered the bad rope down to haul Bao's pack the last ten meters rather than watch the boy fight for footing under its weight. By the top his forearms were shaking and his palms had opened in three places despite the gloves, and the gray rope had bitten a groove into his shoulder where he'd looped it for the haul.

Theo stood at the rim of the col, wind pulling at her collar, and looked at the rope. Then at his hands. Then at him.

"It had a core shot," she said.

"I know. I cut it out."

"You shortened your own protection by six meters on a climb like this."

"I shortened the part that would have killed me," Jun said. "The rest still holds."

Something moved behind her eyes — not warmth, not yet, but the place where warmth would go if it ever arrived, like a room with the furniture covered in sheets. "Tomorrow you get the good rope," she said, and turned up the col.

From Bao, walking beside him, a low whistle. From Imara, ahead, without turning: "Told you he'd never climbed anything. Now he has."

* * *

Three more days took them out of the world of brown things.

The valley walls narrowed to a corridor, then the corridor filled with ice — the Kessing Glacier, eleven kilometers of frozen river pouring down from the saddle they needed, gray-green and ancient, cracked into a maze of seracs the size of houses. In high summer the calving season shook it loose and the route closed entirely. In deep winter the crevasses bridged with snow too thin to trust. There was a window between — ten days, maybe twelve, when the summer melt had drained and the winter bridges hadn't yet formed, when the glacier briefly became passable to anyone mad enough to try.

Maraval's men had reached the moraine behind them that morning. Jun had seen them through his glass: seven figures in matching gray, roped in pairs, moving with the unhurried competence of professionals. Behind them, lower on the trail, porters with supply sleds. Maraval had lost a gunboat. He had not lost the war.

"We have four days of margin," Theo said that night, crouched over the route map with the others gathered close. Her finger traced the glacier's spine to a notch in the far wall marked in her mother's handwriting — a single word in old Vhaleni script that Jun translated aloud, his voice strange in his own ears: "*Threshold.*"

"And past the threshold?" Bao asked.

"Past the threshold," Theo said, "is the archive. And past the archive is whatever Maraval does when he runs out of patience." She rolled the map. "We cross fast, we cross light, and nobody tells me they're tired, because I don't want to hear it."

That night Jun dreamed of his father's kitchen, of the crate marked *kitchenware*, of lifting out letters tied in ribbon the color of glacier ice. In the dream his mother's handwriting crawled off the page and up the walls and across the ceiling, and every line said the same thing: *the truth is heavy, little one, but it floats.*

He woke before dawn to a sound he had read about in a hundred expedition accounts and never heard — a deep, tectonic groan rolling down the glacier's length, followed by silence so complete it rang.

Above them, in the first gray light, the ice was moving.

Not melting. Moving. A serac the size of a chapel had begun to lean, slow as a felled tree, at the head of the route they were meant to take at midmorning — and far below on the moraine, tiny with distance, seven figures in gray had stopped climbing and were watching it too.

Theo stood at the tent flap with the wind tearing her hair sideways, and Jun saw her do the same arithmetic he was doing, and saw the moment the numbers came out wrong.

"That's early," she said quietly. "Calving's early this year. We just lost our margin."
