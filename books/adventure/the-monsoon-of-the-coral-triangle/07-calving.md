## 7. Calving

The mountain told them first.

Theo had spent eleven years reading water — the way a river darkened over a snag, the way foam braided before a drop — and she recognized the same grammar in the glacier. All morning it had been speaking in small voices: a hiss under the ice bridge, a tick-tick-tick of stones settling in the moraine, meltwater running louder than it had any right to run this early in the season. The calving was supposed to be days away. The pass stayed open for a handful of days at most, and every guide's account agreed the ice held until the high sun of late summer.

The ice had not read the accounts.

"Listen," Jun said, and stopped so abruptly that Bao walked into his pack.

They all listened. Beneath their boots, deep down where the glacier ground against bedrock, something groaned — long and low, like a boat's hull flexing in heavy swell. Theo had heard that sound once before, on a river steamer taking water, forty seconds before the deck tilted.

"Off the ice," she said. "Now. To the lateral moraine."

They ran. It wasn't running like the rapids, where speed was a choice you made with your whole body; it was the stumbling, lunging flight of people who knew they were standing on something dying. Imara was ahead with the rope coil, Bao behind her, Jun after, and Theo took the rear because that was where captains stood — the last one off, always, because if the ship went down she went down knowing exactly how.

The crack came at midday, when the sun stood highest and the melt ran hardest.

It did not sound like thunder. Thunder rolls and fades. This was a single report, clean as a rifle shot, and then a sound Theo felt in her teeth rather than her ears: the deep tearing of ten thousand tons of ice letting go of the mountainside above them.

She turned in time to see the serac fall.

It came off the face above the route like a white whale breaching in reverse — rotating, ponderous, beautiful in the way that only enormous destructive things are beautiful. It hit the glacier tongue below and the whole valley answered. The air went solid with pressure. Snow blasted outward in a horizontal wall, and Theo threw herself flat behind a boulder as the world turned to flying white.

When it cleared, the route was gone.

Where the ice bridge had spanned the crevasse field — the narrow spine of blue glacial ice they'd crossed at dawn, the one thread through the broken country ahead — there was now open air, and beneath the air, a chasm going down into green-black depths that no light had ever touched. The far side, where the route climbed toward the col and the archive beyond, might have been another country. Another century.

And Imara was on it.

* * *

"I'm fine," Imara's voice came across the gap, thin and steady in the way that meant she was neither. "I'm fine. I'm on solid ice. Well. Ice."

She had been fifty meters ahead when the serac fell — past the bridge, onto the shelf beyond — and the collapse had simply erased everything between them. She stood now at the edge of the far side, cook's apron still tied over her expedition gear because she'd refused to take it off since the Kessing Rapids, a superstition none of them had dared question. Behind her the route continued up toward the col, clear and cruelly close.

Behind Theo, back the way they'd come, the moraine offered no route forward at all. Only retreat, down-valley, into the arms of the snows and Maraval's men.

"How wide?" Theo called.

Imara paced the edge, kicking at the lip. "Forty meters. Maybe less. There's debris — look, the fallen ice made a ramp on my side, but it ends at the gap." She crouched, and even across the distance Theo could see her testing the edge with her boot heel, professional, unhurried, terrified. "There's a block wedged in the middle. I can see it. If someone could get to it—"

"If," Bao said quietly.

They spent the rest of that day and all of the next doing the impossible arithmetic of rescue.

The rope wouldn't reach. They had ninety meters between them and nothing to anchor to on the near side but boulders that shifted when tested — moraine is not rock, it's rock's unfinished sentence. Jun found an old piton scar in the cliff above the gap, hammered home by some earlier expedition, rusted red as dried blood. Theo didn't trust it. She trusted it more than she trusted waiting.

"We go around," Bao said for the fourth time. "Down the crevasse field, along the edge, up the other side. Two days maybe."

"Two days," Theo said, "and Maraval crosses the moraine behind us in one." She'd seen them at dusk through Jun's glass: six figures in matching expedition gear, roped and disciplined, moving up the lateral moraine with the unhurried confidence of men who were paid by the hour and feared nothing in the world except their employer's disappointment. "We don't have two days. We barely have one."

"So we leave her." Bao's voice cracked on it. He was twenty-three and he had never learned to say hard things smoothly, which Theo privately considered his best quality. "We signal from the col, we come back with—"

"With what? No one comes into the Vhalen passes. That's the entire point of the archive." Theo looked at the gap. Forty meters of empty air over a hole in the world. "Nobody gets left."

"You almost drowned pulling me out of the Kessing," Bao said. "You'd have died. For me. And now you're telling me we risk the same for—"

"Yes," Theo said. "That's the arithmetic. I've done it both ways, Bao. I spent eleven years doing the other math — the math where you cut loose anything that slows you down, where you travel light, where nobody on the river knows your name because names are debts. You know what it bought me?" She gestured at the four of them, at the ruined route, at the whole frozen roof of the world. "A boat with a false name and a mortgage I couldn't pay. That's what invisible buys."

Jun said nothing. He'd been quiet since the serac fell, working the piton with a file, cleaning rust out of the eye with the patience of a man cataloguing something fragile. Now he looked up. "I can cross it," he said. "On the rope. If we anchor it right, tension it right, I can cross hand-over-hand and bring her back."

"The piton won't hold us both falling."

"It doesn't have to hold us both. It has to hold one of us while the other doesn't fall." He smiled, and it was his mother's smile, and Theo hated him a little for wearing it at a time like this. "You gave me the worst rope on the climb, Captain. I noticed. I didn't complain. Give me this too."

* * *

They anchored the line at dusk of the second day, when the melt slowed and the ice stopped talking quite so loudly. Ninety meters of rope doubled over the piton, tensioned between three bodies hauling on the near side, sagging in a shallow arc across the gap. Below it, the crevasse breathed its cold breath upward, smelling of stone and ancient time.

Jun went out over the edge feet-first, upside down, spidering along the underside of the rope with his boots hooked over it, and Theo hauled against the anchor with Bao's weight added to hers and watched the piton with her whole soul. The rust flaked. The metal sang a thin note in the wind. Jun moved anyway, meter by meter, unhurried, and halfway across he stopped and looked down into the crevasse — Theo saw him do it, saw him choose to look — and then kept moving.

When his boots touched the far side, Imara grabbed his collar and kissed his forehead like a grandmother, and then they were both shouting things across the gap that the wind took most of.

Coming back was worse. Two on the rope meant double the load, double the swing, and the piton groaned like a mooring cleat in a storm. Theo hauled until her shoulders screamed and her palms left blood on the line, and when Imara finally rolled over the lip of the near side — apron torn, face frost-burned, laughing — Theo sat down hard on the moraine and discovered she was shaking.

"Told you I was fine," Imara said.

"You were never fine."

"No," Imara agreed, and pulled Theo's head against her shoulder the way you comfort a child, or a soldier, or a captain who has forgotten she is allowed to be either. "But I knew you'd come. That's different from fine. That's better."

Theo wanted to say something. That she hadn't chosen this, that she'd been trying to stay unattached, that a captain keeps her cargo dry and her heart drier. But the words wouldn't form, because underneath them was the truth, and the truth was this: somewhere between the delta and the ice, these three people had quietly annexed her. They had decided she was theirs — not her skills, not her boat, not her usefulness, but her — and they enforced the decision daily, with rope and rice and terrible jokes and now, apparently, with their lives. She had spent eleven years making herself invisible, and it had taken these idiots four months to render the effort void.

Terror was still in her, cold as the crevasse. But it had changed state, the way water changes to ice. It had become ferocity. Whatever came up that moraine behind them would find her done with hiding.

* * *

They lost the second day entirely, and with it most of what remained in their packs. The emergency rations went first — eaten cold on the move, because stopping was a luxury Maraval's schedule didn't permit. Then the spare rope, cut free to save weight. Then, hardest, half the fuel, because the col would demand everything their legs had left and the stove was heavier than hunger.

Bao carried the chest now. He'd insisted, and none of them argued, because the chest was why any of them were on this ice at all and because watching the smallest of them haul thirty kilos of dead government up a glacier was the kind of thing that bound a crew tighter than any articles.

At dusk they gained the shoulder below the col and Theo turned once, because turning back was a captain's vice and she permitted herself one look per day.

Maraval's party had reached the crevasse field.

Through the glass she watched them stop at the gap, tiny figures at the edge of the broken ice, and she watched the tall one — Maraval himself, she was certain of it now, the way he stood with his hands clasped behind him like a man inspecting property — study the anchor point, the piton, the rope-scarred boulders. Studying them the way he'd studied her mortgage papers. Finding the weakness.

Then one of the figures began to cross.

"They'll be a day behind us," Jun said at her shoulder. "Maybe less. The col is half a day ahead. We sleep four hours and move."

Theo lowered the glass. Far below, the crossing figure swung out over the void, patient as a spider, and the wind coming off the ice carried a sound she knew too well — the glacier, still talking, still counting down.

Four hours, then. She turned uphill, and her crew turned with her, and together they climbed toward whatever waited in the archive under the ice — with the collector's shadow lengthening up the moraine behind them, gaining ground with every hour the sun refused to set.
