## 8. The Archive Under the Ice

Imara had cooked for admirals and for thieves, for wedding feasts and for funerals where the food was the only kindness anyone could afford, and she had learned long ago that you could read a person's soul in what they ate when they were afraid. Theo Zhou, afraid, ate nothing. Bao, afraid, ate everything, methodically, like a man laying in stores against a siege. Jun, afraid, forgot to eat entirely until Imara put a bowl in his hands and stood over him until it was empty.

So when they came over the last moraine at dawn and saw the Vhalen Ice Archive standing in the blue shadow of the glacier's snout — a door of black iron in a face of ancient ice, frost-ferns climbing the rock above it like the ice itself was trying to write something — Imara cooked.

She built the stove out of the wind, fried the last of the rice with the last of the dried fish and the last of the pickled greens she'd hoarded like a miser, and she fed them all before anyone said a word about what waited behind that door.

"Eat," she said, when Theo pushed her bowl away after three bites. "You don't walk into your mother's grave on an empty stomach."

Theo looked at her with something raw in her face, and then she picked up the bowl and finished it.

That was the thing about Theo that Imara had understood since the rapids, since the night she'd gone over the side after Bao without a rope, without a thought, without even shouting — Theo would spend herself down to nothing for other people and call it nothing. And the four of them had quietly, without ever saying it aloud, appointed themselves the ones who kept her fed, roped, and alive, whether she liked it or not.

They ate. Then they walked to the door.

* * *

The lock was the chest's lock, writ large — the same tide-table mechanism, the same patient Vhaleni engineering that had outlived the nation that built it. Jun worked it with the master key they'd found sewn into the confession's binding, his breath fogging, his hands steadier than Imara would have been. The iron groaned. Frost rained from the lintel. And the door swung inward onto a stair cut down through the glacier's living heart.

The cold inside was different. Older. The walls of the passage were ice so compressed it had gone the color of deep water, and things were suspended in it — a bird, wings spread, mid-fall for two hundred years. A lantern. A boot.

"They built it to outlast the Republic," Jun said quietly. His voice did that thing it did around anything of their mother's — went soft and reverent and slightly broken. "They knew Vhalen would fall. They built the archive so the fall wouldn't get to decide what the truth was."

The vault itself was a chamber perhaps forty feet long, shelved in black iron, and it was full. Ledgers. Dispatch cases. Sealed cylinders of film and paper. Dispatches with wax stamps Imara recognized from the chest — the crowned tide of the Republic of Vhalen, the crest of a country that existed now only in here, in the cold, in the care of the ice.

Theo walked the shelves like a woman in a dream. She didn't touch anything at first. Then she pulled one ledger free, opened it, and Imara watched her read her own name in a dead language.

"Here." Theo's voice came out strange. "This is the Kessing garrison log. The massacre at Kessing — the one they say she ordered." Her finger moved down a column. "This is her report. Filed six days *before* the massacre. She reported the garrison commander's plan. She reported it to the Assembly. She named names." Theo looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "They had it. They always had it. They just buried it with her."

"The victors write the histories," Imara said. "The losers' children pay the interest."

"Then we're taking the whole vault."

"We can't take the whole vault," Bao said from the door. He'd been on watch — he was always on watch now, since the rapids, since Maraval's gunboat broke its hull on the rocks and Maraval went overland with his men like a patient wolf. "We can carry maybe forty pounds each. We have to choose."

"No," Theo said. "We don't choose. We don't get to be the ones who choose which truth survives. That's how this started — someone choosing." She pressed her palm flat against the ledger. "We take everything."

Imara exchanged a look with Bao. Forty pounds each, over a glacier, ahead of the snows, with a collector's armed party somewhere behind them on the moraine. It was impossible and they both knew it, and they both also knew that Theo had already decided, and that arguing with Theo about the shape of an impossible thing was like arguing with the tide.

So Imara did what she always did. She started packing.

* * *

They worked for hours. Jun catalogued as they went — he couldn't help himself, the archivist in him cataloguing even as the smuggler in him packed — and Bao rigged a sledge from the sled runners they'd hauled over the pass and lengths of their spare rope. Imara wrapped the most fragile cylinders in her spare clothing, in the galley linens, in anything soft, and when she ran out of soft things she used her own coat.

It was Bao, on watch at the mouth of the passage, who saw them first.

"Smoke," he said. "Two fires. On the moraine. And — flags. Maraval's colors."

They had gained more ground than any of them had guessed. The overland route had been faster than the glacier, or Maraval had pushed his men through the nights, or both. Imara did the arithmetic she didn't want to do: half a day, maybe less. Maybe much less.

Theo closed the ledger in her hands with a sound like a small door shutting.

"Options," she said, and her voice was captain's-voice now, flat and clear. "We run with what we have. We hide the rest and come back. Or—"

"Or we're still here when he arrives," Imara said, "because the sledge is heavy and the crevasse field is between us and the pass."

Silence, except for the glacier. Imara had lived her whole life around water and she had never heard anything talk like ice — long groans, deep and slow, like a ship's hull in a storm, like the mountain clearing its throat.

Then, from the mouth of the passage, iron on iron. A knock. Polite. Almost gentle.

"Captain Zhou," came Maraval's voice, muffled by the cold and the stone, but perfectly, horribly pleasant. "I see your lantern light. Might I come in out of the wind?"

* * *

He came in with six men and rifles slung across their backs, and he came in alone to the vault itself, leaving his men at the stairhead, which was either courtesy or theater — Imara suspected theater, carefully staged so that what he said next would be heard by all of them and could be refused by all of them, individually, one at a time.

He looked older than he had on the dock in the Serei Delta, a lifetime ago. The mountain had gotten into him — frost in the creases of his face, a new stiffness in his walk. But his eyes were the same: the eyes of a man who priced everything and had never once been surprised by the number.

"Remarkable," he said, turning slowly, taking in the shelves. "Two hundred years, and the paper's perfect. Cold is honest that way. Cold doesn't editorialize." He let his gloved fingers hover over a dispatch case without touching it, a collector's piety. "You've done me a great service, Captain. You found what four expeditions and thirty years of my life could not."

"You financed my debt to get me here," Theo said. "You can skip the gratitude."

"Gratitude, then an offer." He turned to face them all — Theo at the shelves, Jun by the stair, Bao at the passage mouth, Imara with her arms full of wrapped cylinders. "The documents in this room can clear your mother's name, Captain. They can also restart a war. There are three governments that would burn this vault and the valley it sits in, and two of them know I'm here. If I leave empty-handed, they come themselves, and they will not knock politely." He smiled, thin and reasonable. "So. My offer. To each of you, separately, because I've found that groups decide bravely and individuals decide honestly."

He looked at Bao first. "Deckhand Bao. Passage home, wages for a year, and your sister's hospital bills — yes, I know about your sister — paid in full. All you must do is carry nothing out."

Imara saw Bao's jaw tighten. She saw him think of his sister — he'd told them about her one night on the river, the fever that had taken her hearing, the surgery the doctors said might give it back if they could ever afford it. She saw him want it, whole and visible, like heat off a stove.

"No," Bao said. His voice cracked on it and he said it again, steadier. "No. I've carried things for money my whole life. I'm not carrying this away empty."

Maraval inclined his head, unbothered, and turned to Jun. "Archivist. You came to me first, if you'll recall. You wrote me letters for two years. I have them all — every one, in my safe, in your handwriting, promising me exactly this vault. Walk away now and I'll burn the letters. No one ever needs to know you were mine before you were hers."

Jun's face went white. Imara hadn't known that — none of them had known that, and she felt the knowledge land in the room like a dropped knife. But Jun lifted his chin.

"Burn them," he said. "I was hers before I ever wrote to you. I just didn't know it yet."

Maraval studied him a moment, and something almost like respect crossed his face, and Imara hated that she noticed it. He turned to her last, and she knew what was coming, because Maraval did his homework and Imara had never once in her life pretended to be a mystery.

"Imara Osei. Cook, salvage-diver, the only person on this crew who's been to prison and come out the other side." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "You have a daughter in Kessing Port you haven't seen in six years. Passage, a letter of credit, and my personal word — which is worth more than any government's — that no one ever comes looking for the woman who was on this mountain. You'd be free. Truly free. Isn't that what you told the captain you wanted, the night you signed on?"

The vault was very quiet. Imara could hear her own heart. She could hear the glacier, groaning its long slow syllables. She thought of Sefa, eleven now, probably tall, probably angry, probably singing anyway, because Sefa sang the way other people breathed.

And she thought of Theo, three years ago, buying Imara's debt off a dockside broker not because Imara was useful but because Imara was cold, and Theo had said, *my boat feeds everyone aboard or it feeds no one.*

"No," Imara said. And then, because Maraval had done his homework, she gave him her whole answer: "I've been free my whole life, Mr. Maraval. Free and alone. I'm not trading the second boat for the first."

Maraval looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at Theo, and all the pleasantness drained out of his face like water out of a cracked hull, and what was underneath was just tired, and old, and certain.

"You'd throw all of it away," he said. "Your mother's name, your lives, this — the only complete record of the Kessing truth in existence. For each other."

"You keep saying it like it's a small thing," Theo said. "It's the largest thing I own."

Maraval nodded slowly. He stepped back toward the stair, and his men came down at his gesture, rifles unslung now, and Imara understood with a cold, sliding certainty that the offer had never been the plan — the offer was the record, the proof he'd tried, the story he'd tell the governments who came after.

"Well," Maraval said, from the foot of the stairs. He drew something from his coat — a small, squat object, brass and glass, and Imara knew what it was before he thumbed the cap off the fuse because she'd seen men use them in the Kessing docks riots, and her whole body went cold past cold.

"The cold is honest," Maraval said. "But fire is *thorough*."

He struck the fuse against the wall, and it hissed to life, and he threw it onto the shelves.
