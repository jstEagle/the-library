## 9. Truth Is a Heavy Cargo

**Theo**

The archive breathed cold.

That was Theo's first thought, standing in the vault carved into the glacier's heart — that the room was alive, that the ice around them inhaled and exhaled in slow centuries, and that they were trespassing inside its lungs. Blue light came through the walls as if through water. Shelves of oilcloth-wrapped bundles rose into the dark, and the air smelled of paper and stone and something older than either.

"Vhalen kept everything," Jun whispered. He had his gloves off despite the cold, running his fingers along a shelf spine like a man touching scripture. "Every decree. Every dispatch. Every lie."

"And every proof," Imara said.

Theo found the ledger before she was ready to. It sat at chest height on the third shelf, wrapped in waxed canvas gone amber with age, and when she peeled it back her mother's name looked up at her from the first page in a clerk's careful hand: *Sera Zhou, Attaché, Office of the Interior.* Not traitor. Not hero. A woman who had signed her name to testimony that a massacre happened at Kel Vora, and who had named the men who ordered it, and whose government had buried the record so deep in the ice that even the nation's death could not dig it out.

Theo had spent thirty years building herself into someone with no past. The past had been waiting here the whole time, patient as a glacier.

The sound came down the entry tunnel like a dropped anvil — boots, many boots, and then Maraval's voice, pleasant as ever, echoing off the blue walls.

"Captain Zhou. You've saved me the climb."

* * *

**Jun**

Jun counted six armed men behind Maraval and did not like the arithmetic. The crew had two rifles between them and Bao's knife, and Bao's knife was mostly ceremonial.

Maraval walked the vault the way he'd walked Theo's deck months ago — hands clasped behind his back, admiring, proprietary. "Remarkable preservation. The Republic understood that some truths are too dangerous for warm rooms." He stopped before the open ledger in Theo's arms. "You're holding the master record of the Kel Vora inquiry. Do you know what happens if that document surfaces? Half the families who built their fortunes on the aftermath go to war over it. Borders move. People die."

"People already died," Theo said. "They just didn't get a receipt."

"There it is." Maraval smiled. "Your mother's mouth on your face."

He turned to the crew then, and Jun watched him do what Maraval did best: make each offer small enough to accept. Safe passage down the mountain, individually, by name. Bao first — debts forgiven, a berth on any ship in the delta. Then Imara. Then himself.

"You were mine once, Jun."

"I catalogued your purchases," Jun said. "I never belonged to you."

Bao spat — a small, defiant gesture that would have been funnier if Jun's heart weren't hammering. Imara stepped closer to the shelf she stood beside, one hand resting on the bundles there like a shieldmaiden claiming ground. And Theo simply shifted her weight so the ledger was further from all of them, and said nothing, and her silence refused harder than any speech.

Maraval's smile thinned. "Sentiment is expensive cargo, Captain."

"So is truth," Theo said. "I've carried both. I know which one sinks you."

He nodded to his men. "Burn it."

* * *

**Imara**

Imara had cooked on three rivers and survived two sinkings, and she knew fire the way she knew salt — intimately, and with respect. So when the first canister of accelerant hit the floor and bloomed orange against the blue ice, she was already moving.

"Not the shelves near the door!" she shouted. "Smoke goes up — take from the low racks!"

The vault became a bellows. Heat found the ice ceiling and the whole chamber groaned, a whale-song moan that Imara felt in her back teeth. Water began to weep down the walls. Somewhere above them, the glacier shifted its enormous attention toward the noise.

Bao threw her an armload of oilcloth bundles; she stuffed them into the expedition sledge. Jun worked the far wall with a speed that looked almost holy, pulling records two-handed, choosing in half-seconds what deserved to live. Theo stayed at the center with the master ledger strapped across her chest like armor, feeding the fire's appetite nothing but her shadow.

"The beacon," Theo called. "The emergency transmitter — where's the case?"

"Entry tunnel, under the rope cache!"

"Get it here. We broadcast from inside."

"You want to stand in a burning ice cave and send documents to every receiver on the coast?" Bao's voice cracked upward. "Captain, that's—"

"That's the only way this survives us. Maraval burns paper. He can't burn a signal." She met each of their eyes in turn, and Imara saw it plainly now, the thing she'd suspected since the rapids: Theo Zhou had finally decided her life was worth spending on something. "Go. Both of you. I'll hold the line."

"No," Imara said, and was surprised by how steady her own voice came out. "You'll hold the line *with us*. That's the whole point of a crew, girl. Nobody holds alone."

For one heartbeat Theo looked like she might argue. Then the glacier groaned again, louder, and argument became a luxury none of them could afford.

* * *

**Bao**

Bao ran the tunnel with the beacon case banging his hip and came back into hell.

Fire had climbed the shelving on the east side, and the smoke hung in layers, and through it he could see Theo at the transmitter with pages in her free hand, reading coordinates and dates aloud into the microphone while Jun fed her bundles and Imara keyed the frequency bands. The signal light blinked green. Somewhere out there, every listening station from the delta to the straits was receiving the Republic of Vhalen's dead confession, line by line, in a dead woman's clerk-hand.

Then Maraval came through the smoke.

Bao saw him before anyone else — a silhouette resolving out of the haze, coat scorched, face no longer pleasant at all. His men were scattered or fled or buried under falling ice; it didn't matter. What mattered was that Maraval had eyes only for the ledger on Theo's chest, and a pistol in his hand, and nothing left to lose that wasn't already burning.

"Theo—" Bao shouted.

She turned. The shot went wide and took out a shelf of forty-year-old shipping manifests, which burst into flame like a small angry sunset. Maraval lunged. Theo sidestepped — riverboat reflexes, a lifetime of shifting decks — and the collector stumbled past her, caught himself on the sledge, and came up swinging a length of burning shelf-iron instead of the gun.

What followed was not a fight so much as a demolition. They crashed into the central rack. Bundles rained down. The ledger strap tore half loose. And the floor — the floor that had been groaning since the first canister opened — finally answered.

The crack ran under Maraval's feet like lightning deciding.

Bao would remember it in pieces forever: the fissure opening with a sound like the world clearing its throat. Maraval dropping the iron and grabbing for the master ledger as it swung free of Theo's chest. Theo letting it go — actually letting it go, opening her hands, giving him the weight of it. Maraval clutching the thing he'd crossed a mountain range for as the ice tilted beneath him, and his face in the last second not furious but astonished, a man discovering the true price of a receipt.

Then the fissure took him, and the ledger, and closed like water.

Silence, except fire.

"He had it," Bao whispered. "You gave him the—"

"I gave him what he came for," Theo said. Her voice was ash. "It's done its work. Everything that matters is already in the air."

* * *

**Theo**

They climbed out of the dying vault into a night that smelled of snow and cinders, carrying four sledge-loads of rescued records and nothing else worth counting. Behind them the glacier calved — a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, then a boom, then the long white roar of the ice face giving way, and the pass they'd bled across sealed itself behind them as if the mountain had read the last page and closed the book.

Theo sat down hard on the sledge and discovered her hands were shaking.

It was done. The beacon had burned through its power cell broadcasting until the vault's collapse cut the antenna; the coastal stations had acknowledged; within a week, every port from Serei to the straits would have the Kel Vora testimony in print. Her mother's name would come home wearing its true clothes. Maraval was a story now, a cautionary one, told about collectors who mistook paper for power.

And the chest — the chest that had started everything — was somewhere under a hundred meters of fresh ice, holding nothing but a lock that read tides.

"We lost the boat," Bao said quietly, sitting beside her. "The debt's paid, though. Funny how that works."

"We lost more than the boat." Theo looked at her hands. "Two years of my life. My name, my hiding place, my—" She stopped. Started again, because these people had earned the truth even when it cost. "I spent thirty years making sure no one could ever be taken from me by keeping no one close. And then you four happened to me like weather."

Imara laughed, low and warm, and put a blanket around her shoulders. Jun said nothing, but he sat down on her other side, close enough that their shoulders touched, and that was its own kind of speech.

Above them, the sky was doing something extraordinary — the aurora spilling green down the new calving face, lighting the ice field like a hall of mirrors. Somewhere in that glow, the snows were coming early, and the pass was gone, and spring was a long way off.

"So," Bao said. "Anybody know the way home?"

Theo looked east, where the mountains stepped down toward rivers she knew better than her own heartbeat, and felt something in her chest unclench for the first time since a sealed chest had landed in her net.

"Downhill," she said. "Everything does, eventually. Even us."

She shouldered the first sledge rope, and her crew — her family, chosen twice over now, once by her and once by themselves — took up the others, and they started walking toward the water.
