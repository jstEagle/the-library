## 10. Where the Water Goes After

The melt came down from the mountains the way grief leaves a body — slowly, and all at once, and never entirely.

Theo stood at the rail of the *Meridian's Grace*, a borrowed barge wide as a barn and about as graceful, and watched the Serei Delta unspool ahead of them in braids of brown and green. The river was high. It carried whole trees in its current, and silt, and the pale ghosts of ice that had begun their journey as the glacier they'd crossed on their hands and knees. Somewhere behind them, up in that white country, the Vhalen Ice Archive burned or didn't, buried or didn't, and it no longer mattered which. The beacon had done its work. Every port from Kessing to the open sea had heard her mother's name spoken not as a curse but as a question — and questions, unlike verdicts, could be answered.

Her family's name was cleared. Her pockets were empty.

She found she preferred it this way, though she'd have fought anyone who said so out loud.

"You're doing the thing again," Bao said, appearing at her elbow with two cups of Imara's tea. He handed her one without asking. He'd stopped asking weeks ago; that was what family meant, she was learning. It meant someone knew how you took your tea and simply brought it.

"What thing?"

"The staring-at-the-water-like-it-owes-you-money thing." He leaned on the rail beside her, small and wiry and permanently sunburned across the nose since the moraine. "It owes you nothing, Captain. You're square with the river."

"Nobody's square with the river."

"No," he agreed comfortably. "But some of us are on speaking terms."

Downriver, the delta towns were waking into spring. Fishermen mending nets on the mudflats. Children running the levees. A temple bell somewhere, thin and bright over the water. Theo drank her tea and let the ordinary world wash against her like a tide against a hull that had finally stopped taking on water.

* * *

They'd spent nine days in Kessing after coming down off the mountain, nine days of depositions and clerks and a government assessor who kept apologizing for the coffee. The Republic of Vhalen had been dead forty years, but its paperwork, it turned out, had heirs — and those heirs wanted very much to know what Maraval had been willing to kill for. When Theo laid the salvaged pages on the assessor's desk, water-stained and smoke-kissed but legible, the man had gone quiet for a long time. Then he'd read the master ledger's final entry aloud, his voice cracking on the numbers: eleven villages, one order, one woman who'd smuggled the proof out under a diplomatic seal and paid for it with everything.

"That was your mother," the assessor had said. Not a question either.

"That was my mother," Theo had said, and discovered that saying it plainly, without flinching, was its own kind of arrival.

The reward came through in the form of recovered assets — Maraval's estate, frozen, then liquidated, then portioned out to claimants with the cheerful indifference of all bureaucracies. Theo's share, after the lawyers, was enough to buy one used riverboat of modest tonnage and questionable paint. She signed the papers with a hand that only shook a little.

Jun had watched her sign from the doorway of the assessor's office, arms crossed, wearing the same coat he'd worn up the glacier, now missing two buttons and bearing a scar of melted synthetic along one sleeve where he'd pulled Imara clear of the crevasse lip. He hadn't asked to come along on whatever came next. He hadn't asked for anything since the archive. That, more than the letters, more than the eyes they shared — their mother's eyes, narrow and dark and always slightly amused at the world's expense — was what had finally convinced her.

You could fake a letter. You couldn't fake standing in a burning vault and choosing the ledgers over the exit.

* * *

The new boat sat at the Kessing public docks, third slip from the end, looking like what she was: thirty years old, diesel-hearted, honest as a mule. Bao walked her deck twice, kicking tires that weren't there, checking welds with a knuckle-rap and an ear pressed to steel like a doctor listening to a chest. Imara stood at the gangway with her arms folded, evaluating the galley through the open hatch with the expression of a woman being asked to love a stranger.

"The stove is criminal," Imara announced.

"It's a boat, not a restaurant," Theo said.

"It's a *home*, if you mean to live on it, and homes require stoves that don't try to kill you before breakfast." She marched aboard, already listing replacements under her breath. By evening she'd claimed the galley so thoroughly that the boat seemed to have grown around her, the way a tree grows around a fence wire.

Bao surfaced from the engine compartment at dusk, grease to the elbows, grinning. "She'll run. She'll run ugly, but she'll run." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up long ago. "So. What are we calling her?"

Theo had known the answer for nine days. She'd known it on the mountain, actually, in the white hour before dawn when Jun had shown her the last letter — the one their mother had written to a daughter she'd never meet, sealed inside the ledger itself, tucked between massacre records like a flower pressed in a book of horrors. *If you are reading this, you found the truth. I'm sorry it cost you. Name something after me that goes where I couldn't.*

A boat went where she couldn't. A boat went everywhere.

"*Anwei*," Theo said.

Bao went still. Even Imara stopped mid-inventory in the galley, one hand resting on the criminal stove.

Their mother's name. Spoken aloud on open water for the first time in twenty years, not whispered in archives or screamed across gunboat decks, just — said. Simple. Afloat.

"The *Anwei*," Bao repeated, testing it, and smiled his crooked smile. "Yeah. Yeah, that's right."

Imara came out onto the deck wiping her hands. "Then we need a proper naming ceremony. Salt over the bow. Something with rum in it." She looked at Theo, and her voice gentled in the way it did perhaps twice a year. "She'd have liked her, I think. This boat. Stubborn. Ugly in the right places."

Theo laughed — really laughed, the first time since the fissure — and it startled her, the sound of it, like hearing her own voice played back from deep water.

* * *

Jun stayed.

He didn't announce it. On their last morning in Kessing, while Bao argued fuel prices and Imara provisioned with the ruthlessness of a woman who'd once eaten glacier-melt porridge for eleven days, Jun simply appeared on the *Anwei*'s deck with his cataloguing case and his mother's letters in their oilcloth wrap, and began arranging his things in the second cabin as though the arrangement had been decided by committee months ago.

Theo stood in the doorway and watched him set a framed photograph on the shelf — the only photograph either of them owned of Anwei Zhou, salvaged from a Vhalen-era identity file, grainy as a memory.

"There's a berth aft," Theo said. "Smaller. Better light in the morning."

Jun looked up. Something moved through his face — relief, maybe, or the beginning of it, the thaw starting at the edges the way spring started on a river, imperceptibly and then all at once.

"I get the better cabin?"

"You get the cabin where I can hear you snoring. Consider it surveillance."

"I don't snore."

"We'll see." She turned to go, then stopped, hand on the doorframe. "Jun. The letters. When we're settled — I'd like to read them. All of them. With you."

He nodded, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence between siblings who were learning the word held steady as ballast.

* * *

They cast off from Kessing on a morning so clear the mountains still showed on the northern horizon, white teeth against blue, already letting go of their winter. Bao took the helm first shift. Imara produced, from somewhere, actual rum, and poured a measure over the bow rail while muttering something in three languages that was probably a blessing and definitely included an insult for the stove. Jun stood at the stern, watching Kessing shrink, watching the country that had condemned their mother grow small enough to forgive — or at least to leave behind for a season.

Theo took the wheel from Bao near noon, when the delta opened up ahead of them, all its channels glittering, every braid of the river running silver toward the sea.

For twenty years she had run this water as a fugitive from her own name, reading every current as a threat, every mooring as a trap, every stranger at a dock as the harbinger of the next debt. She had been fast because slowing down meant being caught. She had been alone because alone was safe.

Now the *Anwei* moved at cargo speed, unhurried, riding the melt-swollen current with a full hold of legitimate freight and a crew of three who had chosen her in a burning vault, on a crevasse lip, in the worst rope on the climb. Nobody was chasing them. Nothing was burning. The river ran ahead, and for the first time in her life, Theo looked down its length and felt what she could only describe as welcome.

"Captain," Bao called from the deck, pointing. "Traffic ahead. Grain barge, heavy load, hugging the far channel."

"I see it."

"And a skiff off the port bank — two fishermen, waving."

Theo raised one hand from the wheel and waved back. The fishermen waved again, ordinary and easy, and turned back to their nets.

She eased the throttle forward a notch, no more, and steered the *Anwei* into the broadening water, toward the sea, toward wherever the river went after — which, she understood now, was not away from anything at all.

It was simply home, arriving one bend at a time.
