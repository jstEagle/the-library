## 1. The Debt Collector's Tide

The tide that morning went out like a man pulling a tablecloth from under the dishes.

Odile Sandoval felt it before he saw it — the harbor sucking back on itself, the water dropping a hand's width every few minutes, the moored barges groaning as their lines went slack. He was knee-deep in a drowned fishing trawler at the time, prying copper sheathing off her hull with a flat bar, and the water that had been sloshing at his chest simply left. It slid down his overalls and drained away toward the channel, and for a long moment he stood in the trawler's ruined gut with his mouth open, listening to the gulls scream about it.

"Spring tide," said Halim, the salvage boss, from the dock above. He was a squat man with forearms like mooring rope and the permanent squint of someone who had spent forty years looking at glare. "Biggest of the year. Moon's lined up with the sun like coins in a palm." He spat thoughtfully into the retreating water. "Get what you can off her before she floats again. And Odile — look at that."

Odile climbed the ladder and followed Halim's pointing finger.

The seabed was exposed. Not the usual ribbon of wet sand and stranded jellyfish, but a full quarter-mile of it, gray-green and glistening, running out toward the headland where the coastal range came down to meet the sea in a wall of black rock. Tide pools lay scattered across it like dropped mirrors. Old anchors, three of them, lay in a line like sleeping men. And at the base of the headland, where the water had never been shallow enough to wade, let alone walk—

"There's a hole in the mountain," Odile said.

"There's a hole in the mountain," Halim agreed, with the satisfaction of a man whose predictions were always grim and usually right. "Never seen it in my life. Nobody has. That water's been over it since before my grandfather's grandfather."

The cave mouth was maybe eight meters across, a dark arch cut into the cliff at the exact line where the sea had gnawed at the rock for ten thousand years. Even at this distance Odile could see that it wasn't a natural fracture. Natural fractures were jagged, chaotic. This had edges. This had been *made*, or at least made larger, widened, dressed.

And around it, ringing the arch like a halo, were carvings.

He didn't know that yet. At first he thought it was lichen, a pale crust on the black stone. Then the sun cleared the headland and the light raked across the cliff face, and the lichen became lines, and the lines became figures — spirals, rows of dots, a long procession of small carved shapes that seemed to be walking, in single file, into the mouth of the cave.

Odile's hands went cold inside his gloves.

He knew those figures. He had known them since he was nine years old, since the last summer before everything, when his mother had sat cross-legged on the floor of their apartment with photocopies spread around her like a card game and told him, *Look, Odile, look at this — they're walking into the mountain. Every site in the region, every carving, and they're all walking into the mountain. What did they think was in there?*

Marisol Sandoval had spent eleven years and her entire reputation looking for that mountain.

*Don't go down there,* he thought, and was surprised by the violence of it. The thought arrived whole, fully armed, like something that had been waiting years for the door to open. *Don't. It's a hole in the world and everything that goes into it doesn't come out.*

"Odile." Halim snapped his fingers. "You're white as the foam. Sit down before you fall down."

"I'm fine." He wasn't. His heart was going like a pump with a clogged intake. He sat down anyway, on a coil of fouled netting, and looked at the cave, and the cave looked back, and between them lay a quarter mile of seabed that would be underwater again by nightfall.

His mother had written about a cave like this. Of course she had — she'd written about a dozen, in the years when the letters still came, each one more certain than the last, each one followed by silence and then a new theory and a new letter. The Empty Quarter's coastal range. A drowned entrance. Carvings of a procession walking inward. *They didn't bury their dead facing the sky, Odile. They sent them into the rock. There's a whole civilization in there and nobody's looked because nobody believed the sea could keep a door.*

The last letter had been four years old. After that, the letters stopped and the creditors started.

* * *

The creditors arrived at noon, which was the tide's doing too, though nobody said so. Low water meant exposed wreck, exposed wreck meant salvage, salvage meant the harbor authority's claim officers came down to the dock to make sure the percentages were being honored. And where the harbor authority went, the debt collectors went, because Odile Sandoval's name was on a list that traveled with every one of them like a smell.

Her name, really. His by inheritance.

He was hauling the trawler's bronze fittings up the ladder in a canvas sack when the man came down the dock — a narrow, dry person in a coat too clean for the harbor, carrying a document case the way a priest carries a reliquary. Odile knew the type. He'd met nine of them in four years. They always had the same look: the look of men who had learned that the most efficient way to collect from a proud person was to be polite about it.

"Sandoval." The man consulted a tablet without really reading it. "Odile Sandoval. I'm Ferro, representing the consolidated interests of the Marisol Sandoval estate creditors. You're working the Halim concession this season?"

"Third season."

"Good. Good. Productive." Ferro smiled the way an invoice smiles. "I'll be brief, because I know your time is money, and money, as we both know, is what you don't have." He opened the document case. "The estate's outstanding obligations now stand at two hundred and six thousand, four hundred and twelve. Interest accrues. However — and this is why I've come in person, rather than sending the usual letter — an opportunity has arisen."

Odile set down the sack. "What kind of opportunity."

"A client has purchased the salvage and survey rights to this stretch of coast. All of it." Ferro gestured at the exposed seabed with a dry finger. "The whole shelf, the headland, the range frontage. Eleven kilometers. He's mounting an expedition — archaeological, salvage, and recovery — and he requires local labor with maritime experience. The pay is triple the concession rate. And there's a provision."

"There's always a provision."

"The client is willing to apply a portion of your wages directly against the estate's debt. Substantially more than a portion, actually. Work the full season and the debt is settled. Cleared. Zero." Ferro let that sit in the salt air like bait in clear water. "You'd walk away clean, Sandoval. First time in four years."

Odile looked at the cave. The carvings were clearer now in the high light — he could count the figures, or start to, a procession of small carved people marching into the dark with their arms full of things he couldn't identify from here. His mother had a photograph of carvings like these. Not these exactly — hers were from a shrine site two hundred kilometers south, degraded, half-eroded. But the same style. The same procession. The same direction.

"Who's the client," he said.

Ferro's smile didn't move, but something behind it did — a small recalculation, the look of a man who had hoped not to be asked. "The expedition is organized under the Vey Foundation for Maritime Antiquity."

The harbor noise seemed to drop away. The gulls, the winches, the slap of hulls — all of it went distant, and what remained was very small and very loud: the sound of his own blood.

"Cassian Vey," Odile said.

"The foundation's director, yes."

Cassian Vey. The protégé. The brilliant young man his mother had championed, funded out of her own grant money, brought into the field, treated — Odile had understood this even at fifteen, watching the man eat at their table with his careful manners and his hungry eyes — treated like a son. Cassian Vey, who had stood in front of the academic review board four years ago and testified, in a calm and reasonable voice, that Marisol Sandoval had falsified the dating of her primary site. That she had known the chronology was wrong and published it anyway. That her life's work was a fraud.

The board had believed him. The funders had believed him. The world had believed him, because the world always believes the young man over the loud woman, and his mother had died — not that week, not that year, but soon enough, out in the Empty Quarter chasing the site nobody would fund her to dig properly — with her name in ruins and her son not speaking to her.

Odile hadn't gone to the funeral. There hadn't been one. There hadn't been a body. Just a search that was called off after nine days, a box of effects forwarded to an apartment he was about to lose, and a brass compass wrapped in a sock at the bottom of it.

"Mr. Sandoval?" Ferro was watching him with the careful attention of a man who has seen people cry on docks before. "You've gone quite pale. If the association with the foundation is a difficulty—"

"No." Odile heard his own voice from a long way off. It sounded calm. It sounded like someone else's voice, someone reasonable, someone who had already done the arithmetic. Two hundred and six thousand. Triple wages. A season's work. And the cave — *her* cave, the one she'd died looking for — sitting there in the cliff with its door open for one day, and Cassian Vey's name on the permit to walk through it.

If he said no, Vey's people would go in without him. They would take whatever was in there and the truth about his mother would stay buried under eleven kilometers of purchased coastline, and the debt would still be there, and he would still be prying copper off dead boats at forty.

If he said yes, he would spend a season working for the man who had killed her — not with a knife, with a *testimony* — and he would be inside the one place on earth where the truth about her might still be sitting in the dark, waiting.

It wasn't a choice. It had never been a choice. It was a trap with good pay, and he could feel it closing, and the worst part — the part he would lie awake with that night — was that some cold practical animal deep in his chest was *grateful* for it.

"Show me the contract," Odile said.

* * *

Ferro laid it out on an upturned crate: heavy paper, a foundation letterhead with a stylized wave, clauses in small type. Odile read it the way he read everything now, the way his mother had taught him without meaning to — looking for what was missing. Wages, schedule, hazard waiver, the debt-offset provision spelled out to the decimal. The expedition chief's signature block was pre-signed: a name in blue ink, small and precise, the handwriting of a man who never pressed hard enough to leave an impression.

*C. Vey.*

"Survey and recovery of prehistoric coastal sites," Odile read aloud. "Including — " he found it, third page, a subsection — "the karst feature provisionally designated Site Zero, exposed at the headland frontage."

"They have a name for it already," Odile said.

"They've had a name for it for some time, I'd imagine." Ferro checked his watch. "The client's advance team arrives within the week. The spring tide won't hold — the site will be underwater again by tonight — but the foundation's surveyors have bathymetric data going back years. They knew where it was, Sandoval." A small dry pause. "They've always known where it was. What they needed was the tide, the permits, and the money. The money arrived last month."

*They've always known.* Odile stared at the blue-ink signature. His mother had written to Cassian Vey about this coast. Of course she had — the letters proved it, he'd reread them a hundred times, she'd laid out the whole theory for him in her last year, the drowned entrance, the procession carvings, the chronology that would have rewritten the region's history. And Vey had testified against her, and then, quietly, with foundation money and foundation permits, had gone looking for the cave himself.

He'd used her map. He'd used her map and buried her name.

"Sign there," Ferro said, "and the offset provision activates immediately. Your first payment against the estate posts within the month."

Odile picked up the pen. His hand was steady, which surprised him. Outside, the tide had turned; he could see it in the channel, the water creeping back up the exposed seabed, reclaiming the anchors one by one, sliding toward the cave mouth with the patient certainty of something that had been guarding it for ten thousand years and could wait a little longer.

He signed.

"Excellent." Ferro gathered the papers with the efficiency of long practice. "Report to the staging camp at Qalat Bend on the first of the month. The foundation will provide transport from there into the range." He paused at the top of the dock, and for one moment the invoice-smile slipped and something almost human looked out. "For what it's worth, Sandoval — and it's worth nothing, I know — the director spoke about your mother at the planning sessions. More than once. He called her the best field archaeologist he ever worked under."

Odile said nothing.

Ferro nodded, as if the silence were an answer, and went back up the dock the way he'd come, dry and narrow and clean, carrying his reliquary.

The seabed was nearly gone now. Odile stood at the rail and watched the water close over the procession of carved figures, over the anchors, over the dark arch at the mountain's foot — watched it seal the door the way it had sealed it for ten thousand years, and felt in his pocket, where he always kept it, the small brass weight of his mother's compass.

That night, in his room above the chandlery, he would take it out and hold it under the lamp, and the needle would swing away from north and settle, trembling, pointing south and east — out toward the headland, out toward the drowned door, out toward whatever his mother had loved enough to die for.

But that was later. For now there was only the dock, and the rising water, and the contract drying in his breast pocket with a traitor's name on it — and the debt, at last, with a way out that led straight through the one place he had sworn, at fifteen, at nineteen, at twenty-five, at every age since, that he would never go.

He went back to work. The trawler wasn't going to strip herself, and the tide, which had given, was now taking back everything it had lent.

It always did. That was the one lesson of the coast he'd never needed his mother to teach him.
