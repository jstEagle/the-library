## 3. The Crew of Small Betrayals

The staging camp sat where the last asphalt gave up and the wadi took over — six canvas shelters the color of old bone, a generator coughing in the shade of a rock overhang, and crates stacked with the optimism of people who had never met this coastline. Odile arrived on the supply truck from the port town, wedged between two pallets of drilling mud, and stepped down into air that smelled of diesel, dust, and something green trying to happen after the first rains.

He'd been told to report to the quartermaster's tent. The quartermaster turned out to be a boy.

Not quite a boy. Sixteen, maybe seventeen, all elbows and certainty, wearing a crew jacket two sizes too large with the sleeves rolled back four times. He was reorganizing a shelf of ration tins by some private taxonomy when Odile ducked through the flap, and he looked up with the particular alertness of someone who has been practicing looking unremarkable for a long time.

"You're Sandoval," the boy said.

"Word travels."

"Vey told us. He said you were coming." A pause, a flicker of something — curiosity, or its careful suppression. "I'm Naji. I do stores."

"Where's the actual quartermaster?"

Naji smiled like a card he hadn't decided whether to play. "You're looking at him. Previous one got appendicitis in Salalah. I have excellent handwriting and no criminal record." A beat. "That can be verified."

Odile let it go. Everyone at this camp would need letting things go; he could feel it already, the way you feel weather coming through a bad tooth. He signed the manifest Naji slid across the crate-lid desk, and noticed the boy watched his signature the way other people watched fire.

"Sandoval," Naji said again, testing it. "Like the archaeologist?"

"My mother."

"I read her book. The one about the drowned trade routes." He said it quickly, defensively, as if expecting mockery. "It was in my school library. Somebody had underlined all the parts about the monsoon winds."

"She'd have liked that," Odile said, and found it was almost true. Marisol had liked being underlined. It was everything that came after the underlining she couldn't survive.

* * *

Yusuf found him at dusk, or rather Yusuf was simply there, the way terrain is there. A broad man in his fifties with a beard going to salt and a walking stick that had clearly been a tree once, he materialized beside Odile at the cook fire and handed him a tin cup of tea so sweet it qualified as dessert.

"You are Vey's new salvage clerk," Yusuf said. Not a question.

"Debt collector's clerk, technically. Vey pays better than the bank."

"Everyone pays better than the bank." Yusuf lowered himself onto a rock with the economy of a man who had spent fifty years learning exactly how much energy any given motion required. "You have done mountains before?"

"Some. The Alborz, when I was younger. Nothing like this."

"This is not like anything. This is the place where the land keeps its own accounts." He sipped his tea. "We leave in three days if the weather holds. Four if it doesn't. Never five."

"That specific?"

"The passes close when they close. The satellites say one thing. My knees say another. My knees have a better record." He studied Odile across the fire with eyes the color of wet slate. "Your mother came through here. Twelve years ago. She hired my cousin Rashid."

Odile kept his face still. "I didn't know that."

"She was good in the field. Rashid said she listened to the mountain instead of arguing with it. That is rare in doctors of rocks." Yusuf set down his cup. "Rashid does not guide anymore. After what happened to her expedition, he decided the mountain had said what it wanted to say to our family." He rose, joints announcing themselves. "Sleep well, clerk. Tomorrow I will teach you which rocks lie."

* * *

The rocks, it turned out, lied constantly. Yusuf spent the morning demonstrating: scree that looked solid and ran like water, a ledge that looked like nothing and held like bedrock, a dry wash that was a river twice a year and had killed three men Odile didn't ask about. Odile learned fast because the alternative was educational in a permanent way, and by noon he'd earned a single nod from Yusuf, which he suspected was worth more than most certifications.

Petra he met in the explosives tent, and the meeting began with her pointing a finger at him like a sidearm.

"Feet," she said. "You're standing on the detonator case."

He stepped back. She was small and wiry, forty-something, hair cropped short enough to be practical rather than political, and she moved through the tent with a precision that made him think of a watchmaker who also did surgery. Crates of emulsion and boosters were racked in perfect rows. Everything labeled. Everything squared.

"Petra Lindqvist," she said, offering a hand gloved in leather. Her grip was brief and strong. "Blasting. You'll hear me before you see me, usually."

"What's the plan — road cuts?"

"Mostly. There's a slide choke on the ascent route Vey wants cleared, and we may need to open the cave approach if the tide doesn't cooperate." She said the word *cave* without inflection, and Odile noted the absence the way you note a missing picture on a wall.

She caught him noting it. People like Petra always caught you.

"You're the son," she said. "Of Marisol Sandoval."

"Allegedly."

"Her dating methodology was in my university syllabus. Before." Before what, she didn't say, and he didn't ask, because *before* in this profession meant before the disgrace, and everyone knew which disgrace. Petra turned back to her crates, squaring a row that was already square. "For what it's worth, the numbers she published were clean. I checked them myself, years ago, out of boredom. Clean numbers don't get you discredited. Something else does."

The words landed in Odile's chest and stayed there, warm and dangerous, all day.

He saw the tremor that evening. They were breaking down a crate together — she'd asked him to hold a pry bar steady while she levered nails — and when she reached up to take the bar back, her right hand shook. Not much. A fine, fast vibration, like a plucked string still ringing. She saw him see it. The tent went very quiet in the way tents do, canvas holding its breath.

"Cold," she said.

"It's thirty degrees out."

"Then it's the altitude."

"We're at sea level."

Petra looked at him for a long moment, and something passed behind her eyes — an assessment, a weighing, the quick arithmetic of a person deciding whether a stranger is a threat or merely a witness. "Essential tremor," she said finally, flatly, like ripping off a bandage to show the wound was smaller than feared. "Runs in the family. Gets worse with fatigue and stress. I've placed charges with it for six years and my error rate is zero." She took the pry bar. The hand steadied itself through visible effort. "But if Vey finds out, he'll bench me, and I need this contract the way drowning people need boats. So."

"So," Odile agreed.

"Why are you agreeing? You don't know me."

Because I know what it is to be one bad fact away from losing everything, he thought. Because my whole life is a tremor someone could report. What he said was: "Zero's a good error rate."

She almost smiled. It was clear she hadn't practiced the expression recently.

* * *

The stowaway came out of the third supply truck on the morning they broke camp, which meant Naji had ridden folded into a crate of rope for eleven hours, which meant Naji had planned it, which meant the excellent handwriting and the missing quartermaster and the jacket with rolled sleeves were all part of something older and more desperate than a job.

Yusuf found him first. Of course Yusuf found him first; the man probably smelled stowaways the way camels smell water. By the time Odile reached the truck, the boy was standing in the road dust with his jacket clutched in both hands, chin up, doing the arithmetic of a cornered animal deciding whether to be small or loud.

"There is no stores position," Yusuf was saying, not unkindly. "There never was. I checked the payroll."

"I can carry. I can cook. I know engines—"

"You are sixteen."

"Fifteen and ten months."

"You are sixteen," Yusuf repeated, "and your family is in Salalah, and they will be reporting you missing by tonight."

Something went out of the boy then — not the defiance, but whatever was holding the defiance upright. He sat down abruptly on the truck's rear wheel, in the dust, like a puppet with a cut string. And Odile, watching, recognized the posture. He'd worn it himself at nineteen, in a lawyer's office, hearing the terms of an estate that was mostly debts.

"Arranged marriage?" Odile asked quietly.

Naji's head came up. "How—"

"You packed like someone who isn't planning to come back. People running toward something pack light and stupid. People running away pack like they're never going home." He nodded at the boy's duffel, which bulged with the unmistakable shape of books. "Also nobody fleeing toward something brings that many novels."

A shaky, involuntary laugh escaped Naji, half sob. "His name is Tariq. He's forty-one. He owns fishing boats. My father says it's security." His jaw tightened. "Security. Like I'm cargo."

The camp had gone quiet around them. Crews drifted closer without appearing to move, the way crowds do. Odile glanced at Yusuf, whose slate eyes were unreadable, and at Petra, who had stopped mid-stride with a coil of det cord over her shoulder and was watching the boy with an expression Odile couldn't file yet.

"He stays," Odile heard himself say.

Yusuf's eyebrows rose a centimeter, which on that face constituted open rebellion. "This is not your decision, clerk."

"No. It's Vey's." Odile turned to face the circle of faces. "So let's make it expensive for Vey to say no. He needs a stores man, the last one left, and a kid who can fix the generator — can you fix the generator?"

"It's a fuel line," Naji said instantly. "It's been a fuel line since Salalah. I can fix it in twenty minutes."

"There. Twenty minutes of competence versus a week of paperwork sending him home. Vey's funding deadline is in—" Odile did the math he'd memorized off the contract, the same brutal calendar that had marched his mother into these mountains. "Twenty-six days. He doesn't have time to lose a mechanic."

Silence. Then Petra shifted the det cord on her shoulder and said, to no one in particular, "The generator has been driving me insane."

Yusuf exhaled through his nose, a sound like wind through a canyon, which Odile was beginning to understand was the full range of the man's emotional vocabulary. "When Vey asks," Yusuf said, "and he will ask, I will tell him the boy knows the goat paths above the second wadi. Which is true. My grandmother taught them to me, and I have taught no one, and my knees are fifty-three years old." He looked down at Naji. "This makes you useful. Useful is the only shelter on this mountain. Do you understand? Useful, and quiet, and lucky. Be all three."

Naji nodded so hard his whole body moved. Then, scrambling up, dusting himself off with terrible dignity: "I'll start on the fuel line."

He lasted four steps before turning back. "Sandoval. Your mother's book. The underlining — that was mine. I'm the somebody." And he was gone toward the generator before Odile could decide what to do with that.

* * *

They left for the range two days later, and the first night on the trail Odile lay in his bag listening to the camp breathe. Petra's tent, silent, though he'd seen the light burning late — she checked her charge kit every night, he'd realized, counting and recounting, steadying her hands against the dark. Yusuf's low voice drifting from the fire, telling Naji something in the cadence of a story, the boy's laughter cracking once and going quiet. Somewhere upslope, a hyena laughed back, and the mountain did its own breathing underneath everything, vast and indifferent and, Yusuf insisted, attentive.

Four people in this crew carrying secrets, he thought. Five counting Vey, whose secret was the size of a testimony. Six counting himself, whose secret was a brass compass wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of his rucksack, and the letters, and the growing suspicion that his mother had not been ruined by a liar so much as surrounded by them.

That was the thing about a crew of small betrayals, he was beginning to understand. Nobody here had come for the money alone. Petra needed the contract to outrun something. Yusuf was guiding an expedition toward a cave his family had spent generations not talking about. Naji had traded one cage for a crate of rope. And Odile himself was working off his mother's debts inside the expedition of the man who'd buried her name — which made him either the most honest member of the party or the least.

Wary camaraderie, he thought. People who expect to be abandoned, practicing abandonment on each other in advance, and calling it professionalism.

He was nearly asleep when boots crunched outside his tent, and Vey's voice came through the canvas, smooth as poured concrete.

"Asleep, Sandoval?"

"Not yet."

"Good. Come walk with me. There's something about your mother's final season you should hear — my version of it, before someone else gives you theirs." A pause, and Odile could hear the smile in it, or thought he could. "You'll want to hear it from me first. Trust me on that."

The compass, in its oilcloth, seemed to grow heavier against his spine.
