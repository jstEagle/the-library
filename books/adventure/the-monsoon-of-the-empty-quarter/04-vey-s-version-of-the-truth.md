## 4. Vey's Version of the Truth

The tent smelled of diesel and old paper, and Cassian Vey sat behind a folding table like a man who had rehearsed this conversation for eleven years.

Odile knew the face from news footage, from the hearing transcripts his mother kept in a box he'd never opened until last month. Older now. The hair gone grey at the temples in that deliberate way powerful men let themselves go grey, as if aging were a credential. He wore field clothes that cost more than Odile's truck and wore them badly, like a costume he'd been fitted for by someone who'd only read about deserts.

"Sit," Vey said.

"I'll stand."

Something flickered across Vey's face — not anger. Recognition. *She used to say that too.* Odile saw him swallow it.

"Suit yourself." Vey slid a folder across the table. "Your contract. Partnership terms, not laborer terms. Fifteen percent of salvage value, plus first refusal on publication rights to anything we find bearing Marisol Sandoval's name."

"You don't have publication rights to her name."

"Not yet." Vey leaned back. "That's rather the point of this conversation."

Outside, the staging camp went about its noise — generators, the winch crew testing lines, Naji somewhere laughing at something Yusuf said, a sound so unguarded it made Odile's chest hurt for reasons he didn't examine. Inside the tent, a lantern hissed. The compass sat against Odile's ribs in its oilcloth wrap, and he was suddenly, acutely aware of it, the way you're aware of a loaded thing in a room full of strangers.

"Eleven years ago," Vey began, "your mother stood in front of a review board and told them she'd found a cave in the coastal range with carvings dating to the third millennium before Christ. Third millennium. Do you understand what that would have made her?"

"The most important archaeologist of her generation."

"The most important archaeologist of *anyone's* generation," Vey said. "And it was a lie."

The word landed the way it always landed, even secondhand, even eleven years downstream. Odile kept his hands still on the table. He'd learned stillness working salvage docks, where the men who flinched lost fingers.

"Her samples came back contaminated," Vey continued. "I know because I ran them. I was her postdoc. I ran them twice, three times, and I told her the results, and I watched her look at me — do you know how she looked at people when they disappointed her? Not angry. *Disappointed*, like you were a specimen that hadn't turned out. And then she submitted anyway. She submitted dates she knew were wrong because if she didn't, the funding died, and if the funding died, the cave went to a mining consortium, and she couldn't bear that. So she chose fraud over the consortium. That's your mother's legacy, Mr. Sandoval. A choice between two corruptions, and she picked the one with better lighting."

"You testified."

"I reported. There's a difference."

"There really isn't."

Vey smiled, thin and tired. "No. There isn't. I've had eleven years to learn there isn't." He tapped the folder. "But here's what I've also learned: everything she claimed is sitting under four hundred meters of limestone waiting for us, and if even half of it is real, the dating question stops mattering. You can't falsify star charts carved into bedrock. Either they're there or they aren't. So I'm offering you this: come with me, help me prove her right or prove her wrong, and either way, at the end of it, you'll know. Isn't that what you want? Not money. Not vindication. *To know.*"

It was exactly what Odile wanted, which was why he distrusted it.

"And what do you want?"

"Redemption is cheap," Vey said. "I want the site protected before someone else strips it. I'm not the only one who knows those coordinates exist. Marisol's notes circulated further than the hearing record did. There are men with capital equipment who would take that cave apart and sell it in pieces to collectors in three hemispheres. I'm trying to get there first and lock the door behind us."

"Altruism."

"Debt," Vey said simply. "You'd know about that."

* * *

He didn't sleep. At midnight he unwrapped the compass on his cot and held it flat in his palm, and the needle swung once, twice, and settled — pointing at the tent wall, past it, toward the command tent where the light still burned.

He told himself it was iron in the ground. He told himself the mechanism was damaged, that grief had made him superstitious about a dead woman's toy. He told himself all of it while he dressed and crossed the fifty meters of frozen gravel between the tents, because telling himself things had never once worked.

Vey was awake. Of course he was. He looked up from a table spread with survey maps and didn't seem surprised.

"Couldn't sleep either?"

Odile set the compass down between them, open, the needle trembling toward Vey's chest like a dog scenting its master.

"Do you know what this is?" Odile asked.

Vey went very still. It was the stillness of a man seeing a ghost, and Odile understood, in that moment, that Vey recognized the instrument. Had held it. Maybe had held *her* hand around it.

"Where did you get that?" Quiet. All the performance gone out of his voice.

"It came with the debts. Apparently it was hers. Apparently it points north." Odile watched his face. "Except it doesn't. It points at whatever its holder loved most. Right now it's pointing at you."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere outside, the wind found the guy-lines and played them like a bad instrument.

"That's not possible," Vey said.

"No," Odile agreed. "Neither were the carvings, according to the review board. And yet here we both are, flying into the range tomorrow morning." He picked up the compass, closed the lid. "Here's my question, Vey, and think carefully, because I'll know if you lie — I'm told lying to the family runs in your professional history. If this thing points at what a person loves most, and right now it points at you — what did you love? What's left in you that it can find?"

For a long moment Vey looked at the closed brass lid as if it were a door he'd sealed himself behind.

"A woman," he said finally. "Once. Before any of it. Before the funding and the hearings and the testimony." His jaw worked. "She died hating me, and I have spent eleven years telling myself the hatred was deserved, because the alternative was admitting it wasn't, and then I'd have to do something about it." He met Odile's eyes. "Sign the contract, Odile. Come into the mountain. And when you find what your mother left in there — and she left *something*, I'd stake my life on it, I already have staked everything else — ask yourself whether a woman who lied about carbon dates would also lie about everything else. Ask yourself whether the version of her you inherited is the true one, or just the one the survivors could live with."

* * *

Back in his cot, Odile held the compass again. The needle swung away from Vey's direction, wandered, and settled — steady, certain — toward the sea. Toward the letters. Toward her.

He lay in the dark doing arithmetic he hated. If the compass pointed at what its holder loved most, and it pointed at Vey, then somewhere inside that polished, ruined man was a genuine love, buried but alive. Which meant Vey was capable of loving something truly. Which meant —

Which meant the same test could run the other way. He thought of his mother's letters, the ones he'd reread forty times looking for an apology that never came, and tried to remember whether she had ever, anywhere, written *I lied*. Not *they ruined me*. Not *the board was corrupt*. *I lied.*

He couldn't remember one.

He fell asleep with the needle's image burned behind his eyes and woke to Yusuf shaking the tent pole, saying the weather satellites had changed their minds about the monsoon, and that Vey wanted everyone in the command tent by six, and that the mountain, in Yusuf's opinion, was already deciding whether to let them up at all.

The contract was still on the table where Vey had left it. Odile signed it in the grey light before departure, his signature small and hard, like something braced against impact.

Whatever the truth was, it was uphill.
