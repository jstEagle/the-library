## 5. Into the Range

The goats had known the way before Yusuf did.

That was the first thing he understood on the morning they left the staging camp — that he was navigating by animals thirty years dead. His grandfather's herd had climbed these switchbacks when the paths were still paths, before three floods and a road crew's abandonment had let the mountain take them back. Yusuf had been seven years old then, barefoot, carrying a water skin too big for him, memorizing without knowing he was memorizing: the lean of this boulder, the smell of that thornbush after rain, the place where the trail ducked under an overhang so low even the goats had to fold their ears.

Now he stood at the mouth of the first wadi with a laminated satellite printout in his hand and none of it matching anything beneath his boots.

"You're sure this is the route?" Petra asked behind him. She had her pack straps doubled over her shoulders like she expected the mountain to try to steal it.

"I'm sure it was the route," Yusuf said. "The mountain has opinions."

He heard Naji laugh — a quick, startled sound, the boy still surprised by his own permission to laugh — and then Vey's voice cutting across it, flat and dry as old paper. "Then we follow your opinions instead. Lead."

Yusuf led.

The wadi floor was a riverbed nine months out of the year and a river for the other three, and today it was something worse: a riverbed pretending to be innocent. The gravel was sorted wrong. He could read it the way other men read print — coarse stones at the edges, fine ones down the centerline, all of it laid down by water moving fast and recently. Not monsoon-fast. Something earlier, something impatient.

He crouched and pressed two fingers into the wet sand between stones. It came away dark.

"Water through here inside the last week," he said.

Vey consulted the satellite sheet without breaking stride. "Nothing on the forecast until next month. The models have the system forming south of the shipping lanes."

"The models," Yusuf said, standing, "have never met this mountain."

* * *

They made the first camp in the lee of a ridge the old people called the Sleeping Camel, because from the valley it looked like exactly that, if you squinted and were willing to be generous. From up close it looked like any other pile of rock, which was the way of names given by people who loved a place — accurate from far away, useless up close, true anyway.

Yusuf got the tents pitched while Petra ran the stove and Naji hauled water from a seep he'd found by following a line of green that shouldn't have existed in this heat. The boy worked like someone paying off a debt to be allowed to stay. He hadn't stopped doing that since the camp, since the night Yusuf had found him folded into a supply crate among the rope coils, sixteen years old and certain he was about to be sent back to a wedding he'd rather die than attend. Nobody had sent him back. That was the whole economy of this expedition, Yusuf thought: everyone aboard had bought their seat with something they couldn't afford, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it, and somehow that held them together better than paychecks would.

Odile Sandoval came down the slope at dusk with the compass in his hand.

Yusuf watched him the way he'd watched him since the dock — not suspiciously, exactly. Curiously. The man walked like a debt collector was behind him, which Yusuf supposed one always was. But there was something else. When Odile stopped at the edge of camp and looked back the way they'd come, toward the coast gone gray-blue in the evening haze, he didn't look like a man checking his escape route. He looked like a man saying goodbye to something.

"Needle behaving?" Yusuf asked, when Odile passed close enough.

Odile's hand closed over the compass, quick, almost guilty. "It points where it points."

"Everything does," Yusuf said. "The trick is knowing what it's pointing at."

Something moved behind Odile's eyes — recognition, or its cousin — and then he nodded once and went to help Naji with the water, and Yusuf filed the exchange away with all the other small things the mountain was letting him see.

* * *

On the third day the land began refusing them.

Not dramatically. The mountain didn't roar or shrug or send down stone. It simply, politely, withdrew every path it had ever offered. The goat track along the second ridge — the one Yusuf had promised Vey would carry them two days' march in comfort — ended at a scar of collapsed slope where a whole hillside had decided, sometime in the last few years, to become a different hillside somewhere lower down.

Yusuf stood at the lip of the collapse and felt thirty years fall away and then come rushing back, heavier.

There had been a cairn here. His grandfather had built it, or rebuilt it, stacking stones the way his people had stacked them for longer than anyone could count — not to mark the safe way but to thank the mountain for allowing one. The cairn was gone. In its place, scattered down the fresh scree, were its stones, each one lying where gravity had judged it should lie.

"We go around," Yusuf said. "East, then up through the notch. It costs us half a day."

"It costs us more than that." Vey had the schedule out, the laminated one, the one with dates circled in red ink that Yusuf had already begun to hate on principle. "The funding window—"

"Is a window," Yusuf said. "This is a wall. Windows you can argue with."

For a moment he thought Vey would push it, and he watched the man do the arithmetic of a man who had never carried a pack up a mountain in his life deciding whether to pretend otherwise. Then Odile said, quietly, "Half a day," in a voice that wasn't loud but landed like a tent peg, and Vey put the schedule away.

They went around.

And it was on the detour — climbing through the notch where the wind funneled cold despite the season, where the rock walls closed in close enough that the party had to go single file — that Yusuf saw the carvings again.

He had seen them first at the cave mouth, on the coast, in the hour after the tide peeled back the seabed. Spirals within spirals. A figure with arms raised, headless, or maybe the head was the sun above it. Rows of marks like rain falling upward. He had looked at them and felt a coldness in his stomach that he had told himself was the sea wind.

Here they were again, cut into the notch walls at shoulder height, worn shallow by centuries of wind, and the coldness came back and this time it brought its grandmother with it.

Because he knew these. Not from archaeology. From a kitchen.

His grandmother had told stories in that kitchen, in the low years, when the power failed and the kerosene lamp made everyone's shadows into giants. Stories about the high country. About the old people who had lived in the caves before caves were places anyone admitted to living in. She had called them the Rain-Counters, and she had described their marks — the spirals, the upward rain, the figure with its arms open to a sky that owed it something — and she had described them in a whisper, and she had finished every telling the same way:

*Never point at them. Never name them to strangers. And never, never follow their marks uphill.*

"Why?" Yusuf had asked, seven years old, holding a water skin too big for him.

"Because the marks are not directions," she had said. "They are requests. And the mountain answers requests. You don't want to find out what answer it's been keeping for the last thousand years."

He had forgotten the stories for thirty years. Standing in the notch with his palm an inch from the spiral, he forgot nothing at all.

"You know what these are."

Petra's voice, low, beside him. She was looking at the carvings too, and her hands — Yusuf had noticed the tremor the first day and pretended he hadn't, the way everyone had agreed to pretend — her hands were very still, which he'd learned meant she was concentrating hard on something that wasn't her hands.

"My grandmother told stories," Yusuf said. "About the people who made them."

"And?"

"And she said they weren't decoration." He took his hand back from the rock. "She said they were prayers. Or warnings. She used both words."

"Those can be the same word," Petra said, "depending on who's listening."

Above them, Vey called down that the notch opened onto the high shelf, and the column moved, and Yusuf went with it, but he looked back once at the spiral — the rain falling upward, the figure with its arms open — and he did not point at it, and he did not say its name, and he caught himself walking faster without having decided to.

* * *

That evening the satellite phone brought the weather.

Yusuf was on cook duty, which meant he was the only one with an excuse to sit near the radio tarp and listen. Vey took the call from the expedition office in the city, and Yusuf heard only Vey's side of it, which was mostly "confirm that" and "run it again" and, twice, "that can't be right."

When Vey came out of the tarp his face was doing something complicated. Trying to be calm. Almost managing it.

"The system's forming early," he said. "Weeks early. The models are revising. We may see precipitation in the high country well ahead of schedule."

"How far ahead?" Yusuf asked.

"They don't know. That's the point of 'revising.'"

Yusuf stirred the pot and said nothing, and around the fire the others did the arithmetic silently, the way people do when the numbers are bad. Naji looked at Odile. Odile looked at the fire. Petra checked the charge bags on her pack frame, counting them, though she must have known the number by heart.

Only Vey sat looking at the schedule, and Yusuf watched him look at it, and understood suddenly that the man wasn't reading the dates. He was pleading with them. As if the red circles were a contract the weather had signed too, and some clause could be invoked, and the mountain would honor paperwork.

"My grandmother had a saying about forecasts," Yusuf said, into the quiet. "She said the sky doesn't read."

Nobody laughed. It hadn't been a joke.

* * *

He dreamed of the kitchen that night.

In the dream his grandmother was grinding grain and talking without looking up, the way she talked about important things, saving eye contact for matters that could wait. She was describing the high country again, the forbidden path, the one the carvings marked — the one that went over the shoulder of the range where no shepherd grazed his flock, where the grass grew in patterns too regular to be accidental, where the springs tasted of stone.

"There are two ways through the mountain," she said in the dream, in her story-voice, the one that meant the words were older than she was. "The way the goats use, and the way the marks use. The goat way is for going. The mark way is for asking."

"Asking what?"

She looked up then, and in the dream her face was the cave mouth, ringed with spirals, and she said: "Asking to leave."

Yusuf woke with his heart going hard and the temperature dropped — actually dropped, the canvas breathing cold against his hand — and he lay in the dark listening to the mountain make sounds it hadn't made three days ago. Water somewhere below the ground, moving. Rock settling, somewhere above, thinking about its options.

He got up and stood outside the tents. The stars were out, sharp and indifferent, and against them the ridge line of the high country showed black, and along that black line, where the moonlight caught the exposed faces just enough, he could see them — or believed he could, which amounted to the same thing at one in the morning. Marks. Row after row of them, ascending toward the col like footsteps.

Rain falling upward. A figure with its arms open.

*Requests,* his grandmother had said. *The mountain answers requests.*

And Vey, tomorrow, would lead them straight up into the middle of a thousand-year-old question, dragging a schedule written by men who had never stood on this stone, chasing a deadline that had already killed one Sandoval — and the sky to the south, when Yusuf turned and looked, was not empty anymore. There was a weight to it, a bruised darkness gathering along the horizon like a tide deciding.

He stood a long time. Then he went and woke Odile.

The younger man surfaced from sleep fast, silent, one hand already finding the compass — and Yusuf noted that, filed it, said only: "Come look at the sky."

Odile came. They stood together in the cold, two men who barely trusted each other, looking south at the weather being born.

"That's early," Odile said finally. "Isn't it."

"That's early."

"Can we outrun it?"

Yusuf considered the ridge line, the black ascending marks, the distance to the cave, the distance back. He thought about his grandmother's voice saying *never follow their marks uphill*, and he thought about Marisol Sandoval, whom he had never met, whose letters he had never read, but whose son stood beside him wearing the same expression Yusuf had seen on his own face in mirrors after his grandfather died — the expression of someone carrying a question they'd trade years of life to stop carrying.

"No," Yusuf said. "But we might outrun it partway. And partway is where all the decisions live."

Below them, faint under the wind, the mountain made the water-sound again. Moving. Rising.

Coming up to meet them.
