## 7. The Journal's Missing Pages

The journal dried badly. Odile laid its pages out on the mess tent table like a man laying out a body, weighting the corners with stones from the wadi, and watched the water stains bloom brown as old blood across his mother's handwriting.

She wrote small. She always had — a habit from grant applications, fitting her arguments into margins and boxes that were never meant to hold them. Even now, dead two years and waterlogged, she was compressing herself into whatever space remained.

He read until the lamp guttered low on paraffin Yusuf had grumbled about sparing. The early entries were almost cheerful: survey notes, sketches of the carvings above the tide cave, a joke about the muleteer's singing. Then the handwriting tightened. The dates compressed. And on the last legible page, in letters pressed so hard they'd scored through three sheets beneath:

*The chamber is older than anyone will accept. I have the proof. I will not let them bury it with me.*

Then nothing. Not an ending — a severance. The final pages had been torn out at the spine, close and deliberate, by someone who knew what they were doing.

Odile turned the stub of the binding over in the lamplight. Four ragged edges. A single clean stroke per page, done fast, done once, no hesitation marks.

He'd spent two years learning what betrayal looked like from the outside — the court transcripts, the news footage, Vey's composed face on the stand. He had never learned what it looked like up close, in the hand.

He sat with the journal until the stones went cold, and he did not tell anyone.

* * *

Petra counted charges by touch.

It was a habit from her first demolition job, a quarry in a country she didn't name at parties: lights out, hands in the crate, fingers reading the paper casings like braille. Sixty-one sticks, primers wrapped separately, det cord coiled in figure-eights. If the count came back right in the dark, the day could start.

This morning the count came back right, but her left hand didn't. It had taken her four tries to strike the camp stove lighter, and the tremor had been running up into her wrist since the rockfall two days ago — no, that wasn't honest, she corrected herself, since before the rockfall, since the range itself, since the moment she'd looked up at those cliffs and understood what kind of ground Vey had bought her silence to enter.

She flexed her fingers. Counted again. Sixty-one.

"Your hand's worse."

Naji, crouched by the crate, chin on his knees, watching her the way he watched everything — openly, incuriously, like weather.

"It's fine."

"You dropped a primer yesterday."

"I set it down."

"You set it down twice." Naji said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Yusuf pretends not to see. Odile doesn't pretend anything, he just watches you the way he watches Vey. I'm the only one who'll say it."

"And why would you say it?"

Naji shrugged. "Because everyone here keeps pretending things, and I got tired of pretending at fourteen. It's exhausting. You should try quitting."

Petra almost laughed. Almost. She closed the crate instead and latched it, and her hand shook on the hasp, and Naji saw that too, and mercifully said nothing more.

* * *

That evening Odile spread the journal pages again, this time under better light, photographing each one with the battered field camera he'd brought for exactly this purpose — documentation, evidence, the habit of a man raised by a woman who believed nothing existed unless it was recorded twice.

He was photographing the torn spine when the tent flap moved.

Naji stood in the gap with a tin of tea he clearly hadn't been invited to bring, wearing the expression of someone who had already seen too much to pretend otherwise.

"The door," Odile said.

"There isn't one. There's a flap." Naji came in anyway, set the tea down, and crouched opposite him. "You've been photographing that journal for two days like it's going to testify. I can read upside down. I learned it sitting across from my father while he read my aunt's letters to decide who I'd marry."

Odile should have been angry. He catalogued the anger, found it, noted its shape — and found underneath it something colder and more useful. This boy had crossed a mountain inside a supply crate to escape being treated as property. He knew what secrets cost and what they weighed.

"How much did you see?"

"The last page. Before you covered it." Naji wrapped both hands around his own tea, uninvited. "*I will not let them bury it with me.* That's her? Your mother?"

"That's her."

"She thought she was going to die."

"She thought someone wanted her to." Odile heard his own voice go flat and careful. "The last pages are gone. Torn out."

Naji leaned forward without touching anything, the discipline of someone raised around valuable things he wasn't allowed to handle. "By her?"

"No. Look at the edges." Odile tilted the spine toward him. "One stroke. Downward pull. Right-handed, strong grip, done in a hurry or done coldly — I can't tell which."

"Can you tell what kind of knife?"

Odile blinked. "What?"

"A blade leaves a signature. My uncle slaughters goats; I've held every knife in his house. Serrated leaves whiskers. Scissors leave a flat double line. A straight edge with a slight recurve—" Naji traced the air above the tear, not touching, "—leaves a mark that curves out at the end, like the cut is reaching for something."

Odile photographed the spine again, closer, the macro attachment shuddering slightly in his grip. When he pulled the image up on the camera's screen, enlarged past usefulness into suggestion, there it was: the faint outward flare at the terminus of each tear.

"Recurve," Naji said quietly. "Like a karambit. Like the knives Petra sells."

The tent felt suddenly smaller.

"You know her blades?"

"I know everyone's blades. I inventory the stores because nobody asked me to and it makes me useful." Naji met his eyes, sixteen years old and entirely unafraid. "I'm telling you because you're the only person on this expedition who isn't lying to himself yet. Everyone else has decided their lie is load-bearing."

* * *

Petra stood at the stores tent an hour later, ostensibly checking primer storage against the damp, actually standing where she could see the mess tent through the gap in the canvas.

She'd seen Naji go in. She'd seen the boy come out walking carefully, the way people walk when they're carrying something breakable that isn't physical.

She knew what Odile had. She'd known since the descent, since he'd come up out of the antechamber white-faced with a waterlogged book wrapped in his shirt like an infant. And she knew — had known since the second night of the staging camp, when she'd inventoried Vey's personal kit against the manifest — that Vey carried a knife with a recurved blade, damascus steel, a thing too beautiful for fieldwork and exactly the kind of thing men like Vey bought from her in the years when selling to him had seemed like business rather than complicity.

She had sold him three of that pattern. One had come back to her for resharpening after Marisol Sandoval's last expedition. She remembered because the edge had a nick in it, as if it had been driven into something harder than paper. Into bone, she'd thought at the time, and then made herself stop thinking.

Now a dead woman's journal sat in the next tent with tears in its spine that curved like her own handiwork reaching for something.

Petra counted her breaths. In for four. Hold for seven. Out for eight. The tremor in her left hand answered none of it.

Here was the arithmetic she lived inside: if she told Odile, the expedition fractured now, nine days — no, eight — before the passes closed, with half a ton of cached supplies and a crew that had just begun, dangerously, to feel like a crew. If she said nothing, she kept her place in it, kept Vey's trust, kept the arrangement that was clearing her debts — the reports, the coded check-ins, the little betrayals paid out weekly like wages.

If she said nothing, she was doing it again. Being the hands. Selling the blade.

"Petra." Yusuf at the tent flap, tea in hand, offering her a glass without entering. He had a way of appearing with tea at exactly the moments silence became unbearable. "You look like a woman doing math she hates."

"All my math is like that."

"Mm." He drank. "The boy is with Odile. Whatever he's carrying in there, it's heavier than tea."

"Yes."

"You know what it is."

Petra looked at him. Yusuf's face gave away nothing except the fact that he expected to be lied to and had budgeted for it.

"I know some of it," she said.

"Good." He handed her the glass and turned to go. "Then you know which part to keep, and which part to put down before it burns through your pockets. Mountain rule. You carry what saves you. The rest you cache, and you mark the spot honestly, or the next party dies looking for it."

He left her with the tea going cold in her shaking hand.

* * *

Odile worked past midnight.

He built a file: photographs of every page, cross-referenced against Marisol's published work, against the trial transcript he'd memorized out of spite, against Vey's testimony — *she falsified the dating, I merely reported what I found* — and against the physical evidence of the tear marks. He wrote nothing down that could be read. He photographed his own notes and deleted the text.

Paranoia, he told himself, was just grief that had learned logistics.

Near the end, he did the thing he'd been avoiding. He took out the brass compass and held it over the journal's remains.

The needle swung once, twice — and settled, steady as a struck bell, pointing at the torn spine. At the missing pages. At whatever his mother had loved enough to hide, and someone else had loved — or feared — enough to take.

He was still staring at it when he heard the faint scrape outside, and the tent flap lifted, and it was not Naji this time.

Vey stood silhouetted against the generator light, hands visible, voice pitched low and reasonable, the voice of a man who had never once entered a room without knowing how he'd exit it.

"You should sleep," Vey said. "Tomorrow we make the high traverse, and I need everyone sharp."

Behind him, in the dark of the tent, the compass needle trembled — and began, very slowly, to turn toward the doorway.
