## 8. Monsoon Warning

The village had no name on any map Yusuf trusted, which was how he knew it was a good village. Maps named things so that governments could tax them and armies could burn them. This one sat in a fold of the high valley like a coin dropped in grass — forty-odd houses of dry stone, roofs weighted with river rocks against the wind, and terraces of barley climbing the slope in steps so narrow they looked like the mountain's own ribs showing through its skin.

The dogs announced them an hour before they arrived. By the time the expedition crested the last rise, half the village was standing in the dust of the track, watching with the particular stillness of people who lived where weather was a neighbor you either respected or buried.

An old man came forward. He walked like the terrain was a language he'd been fluent in since before he could speak, and he stopped three paces from Yusuf and looked him over — boots, rope coil, the sun-scourged face of someone who worked other people's mountains for money.

"You are going up," the old man said. Not a question.

"Over the col at Cold Fork, down into the coastal range." Yusuf kept his voice easy. "We're provisioned for six weeks."

The old man's face did something complicated. It wasn't quite pity. It was closer to what a man looks like when he watches a child count coins that have already been stolen from his pocket.

"The passes close in nine days."

Yusuf laughed, because the alternative was to be rude. "Grandfather, it's early summer. The monsoon is three weeks out at minimum. The forecast—"

"I know what your forecasts say." The old man turned his face east, toward the wall of peaks where the clouds were born. "I have watched sixty of these seasons come up my valley. My grandmother watched them before me, and hers before her. The birds went quiet four days ago. The barley has gone flat on the stalks — it does that when the air gets heavy. And last night the wind came down off the glacier instead of up from the sea." He said it all without drama, the way a man reads a bill of lading. "When the wind turns downhill, the rain is already on its way. Nine days. Ten if the mountain is generous. The mountain is not generous."

Behind Yusuf, he heard the expedition stop pretending to rest. Petra shifted the weight of her charge bag. Naji had gone very still beside the mules, the way he did when adults started talking about things that would decide his life without asking him.

"We'll take it under advisement," Vey said, coming forward with his expedition smile already fixed in place — the one that had opened doors and closed arguments across three continents. "Thank you for the warning. We'll pay for water and grazing, and we'll want to hire two of your young men as porters as far as the fork."

The old man didn't look at Vey. He looked at Yusuf, and there was a whole negotiation in it, one side of it anyway.

"You are the guide?"

"I am."

"Then hear me as a guide hears another guide." The old man spoke slowly now, laying each word down like a stone in a wall. "There are two ways over this range. The low way, through Cold Fork — wide, gentle, the way caravans go. It closes first, because it fills with water. And the high way, the goat paths above the ice line. That one stays open longer, but it is not a path. It is a permission. My people do not use it. There are markings on it older than my people, and my grandmother said the ones who cut those marks were not making decoration. They were making a record. A warning, or an invitation. She never said which, and she forbade us to find out." He paused. "You will take the low way, because you have mules and men who are not goats. And you will be over the col in nine days or you will winter here eating my barley and owing me until your grandchildren are old."

* * *

Vey commandeered the largest house — the headman's meeting room, actually, offered with the wary hospitality of people who wanted the strangers gone quickly — and spread his maps on the floor while the crew crowded in around the lamplight. Outside, the light was already wrong. Yusuf had noticed it on the walk in: a yellow-green tinge to the afternoon, the kind of sky that made old shepherds count their animals twice.

"Nine days is their number, not ours," Vey said, tapping the contour lines around Cold Fork. "Village elders in every range on earth pad these estimates. It builds caution into the culture — you tell outsiders the worst case so they leave margin. Perfectly rational. But we're not a caravan of pilgrims. We move fast, we travel light after the cache drop, and the fork is thirty-six hours' march from here. We can be over the col in four days, five with bad luck, and down into the coastal drainage before the first real rain arrives."

"And if the real rain arrives early?" Petra asked. She was sitting with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she hadn't drunk, and Yusuf saw her flexing the right one inside its fold of fingers, testing it, the way she did when she thought no one was watching.

"Then we'll have spent five days gaining ground instead of nine days losing it." Vey straightened. "I understand the concern. But the funding window closes on the fifteenth. If we're not on site with the descent party equipped by then, the grant reverts, the salvage rights transfer, and everything we've spent getting here becomes a donation to this mountain. I didn't bring eleven people across a desert to watch a deadline expire in a tea house."

"The deadline," Yusuf said, "is a piece of paper."

"The deadline," Vey said, without heat, which somehow made it worse, "is the only reason any of us are being paid. Including you, Yusuf. Your license may be forged, but your invoice isn't."

It was the first time anyone had said it aloud. Yusuf felt the crew's attention swing to him and away again, quick as birds — everyone had known, nobody had said, and now the secret was currency on the table whether he liked it or not. Vey hadn't even looked up from the map. That was the skill of the man: he could gut you and keep reading contours.

Naji, curled near the door with his knees drawn up, said nothing. But his eyes went from Vey to Odile, and Yusuf followed the look.

Odile Sandoval had been quiet all evening, sitting slightly apart with Marisol's letters in a oilcloth packet on his knee, and when he finally spoke his voice was mild in a way that made the room go still.

"When did my mother get her funding deadline?"

Vey's pen stopped moving.

"It's a simple question," Odile said. "She had one too, didn't she? A window. A date after which the money vanished. And she pushed into the range against the season to meet it, because the alternative was losing everything. Isn't that exactly what happened?"

"That was different."

"How?"

Vey set the pen down with great care, the way a man sets down something he wants to throw. "Your mother made errors of judgment. I've told you what I believe those were. I'm not going to relitigate them in front of my crew."

"No," Odile agreed. "You're just going to repeat them. Same range. Same season. Same impossible date. Same refusal to turn back when the people who actually know the mountain tell you it's turning." He stood, unhurried, and Yusuf watched the whole room understand at once what he was saying. "You're not running an expedition, Cassian. You're reenacting one. You're walking her route, on her schedule, toward her cave, and you're calling it a deadline."

The lamp flame leaned in some draft from under the door. Nobody moved.

"Sit down, Sandoval," Vey said quietly.

"Give the order to turn back and I will."

"I can't do that."

"Can't," Odile said, "or won't."

For a moment — one long moment, long enough for Yusuf to measure it — something moved behind Vey's eyes that was not ambition and not stubbornness. It looked almost like grief wearing a uniform. Then it was gone, buttoned away, and the expedition chief was back, smooth and reasonable and immovable.

"Here is what's going to happen," Vey said. "Tomorrow we march at first light. Anyone who wants to stay in this village and wait out the season is welcome to — I'll mark their contract suspended, not broken, and there'll be work for them on the next phase. Anyone who comes with me crosses the col in plenty of time, because I have planned this route down to the hour and I don't plan badly." He rolled the map. "Get some sleep. We've got a long day."

He left. The crew stayed where they were, arranged around the dead lamp space like survivors of something, though nothing had happened yet. Nothing except the sound of a family discovering it had a fault line running through the middle of the floor.

Petra spoke first, to her untouched tea. "Five days over the col. Even if he's right about the pace, that leaves us four days of margin on a nine-day estimate from a man who's counted sixty seasons."

"He's not wrong about the deadline, though," Naji said, surprising everyone. "Deadlines are real. Debts are real. I ran away from one; I'd know." He hugged his knees tighter. "But my grandmother used to say, the mountain doesn't care whose name is on the paper."

Yusuf looked at each of them in turn — the forger, the tremorer, the runaway, the son of the disgraced woman — and understood that none of them would stay behind. Not because they believed Vey's arithmetic. Because they had all, at some point in their lives, been left behind by someone who chose a schedule over a person, and they could not make themselves do it to each other.

That was the fracture. And that was also, he realized with something close to dread, the thing that would keep them together all the way into the teeth of it.

* * *

Yusuf climbed out of the village before dawn, alone, to look at the sky.

He went up the barley terraces to the shrine stone at the top — a slab of granite rubbed smooth by centuries of hands, hung with faded ribbons and one bright new one — and sat with his back against it the way he'd sat against stones his whole life, waiting for the light.

The east was banded black and iron. Above the great peaks, a single cloud had formed in the night, high and white and anvil-headed, and it was not drifting. It was building. He had seen that shape maybe ten times in his life, and every time, within days, the valleys below it had run brown with water and the passes had filled with snow that didn't melt until spring.

His grandmother's voice came back to him across thirty years, in the cadence she used for the forbidden stories, the ones told only after the children were supposed to be asleep. *The mountain keeps a door,* she used to say, *and the door has a mouth, and the mouth has markings, and the markings say: we were here when the sky broke, and we went into the rock, and the rock held.* He had always assumed it was a flood story, the kind every culture carries. Standing here now, watching the storm being born over the coastal range — the same range, the same walls of stone, the same folded country where the tide had peeled back the seabed to show a carved mouth — he felt the story shift under him. Not a memory. An instruction.

Below him, the camp was stirring. He could see Vey already awake, already at the maps, a small upright figure burning through the dark hours with his schedule. And moving between the tents, quieter, Odile — standing once at the edge of the fire-pit with the brass compass open in his palm, tilting it at the storm cloud as if the needle might have an opinion about the weather.

Yusuf shouldered his pack and went down to earn his forged license.

They marched at first light, as ordered. The mules balked at the ford and Yusuf talked them across; the porters sang the walking songs and the pace held; and by midday they were in the throat of the valley with the walls rising on both sides and the anvil cloud now visible even to the men who didn't want to see it.

It was Naji, riding drag, who spotted it first and called out in a voice cracked thin with alarm.

High on the south wall, where the morning sun hit it full, stood a line of markings cut deep into the living rock — the same spirals and star-charts and long-legged figures that ringed the cave mouth by the sea, duplicated here, fifty kilometers inland, a thousand meters up, facing the pass.

Facing the way they had come. Marking the escape route.

Yusuf reined in and looked at them for a long time, and the old man's words came back to him: *a warning, or an invitation. She never said which.*

Now, with nine days on the clock and the sky assembling its argument overhead, he knew.

It was both.
