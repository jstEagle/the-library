## 9. Ninety Meters of Bad Rock

Petra felt the tremor start in her left hand at 4:40 in the morning, before anything else went wrong.

She'd learned to read it like weather. Some days it was a fine shiver, a moth beating against the inside of her skin, and she could work through it — steady the wrist against her thigh, brace the elbow on rock, let the right hand do the talking. Other days it was a storm. She woke with it now, lying in her bag in the high camp below the cave mouth, and she lay still and watched her fingers jump against the nylon and thought: *not today. Please not today.*

The tremor had a name she never used out loud. Essential tremor, progressive, the neurologist in Antalya had said, three years ago, back when she could still afford neurologists. He'd said it would get worse. He'd said the word *essential* as if it were a kindness, as if the shaking were essential to her, part of the architecture. What he meant was that nobody knew why. Her hands were simply learning to disagree with each other, and no one could say when they'd stop listening altogether.

She'd been an explosives specialist for eleven years. Blasting was a trade of millimeters. You drilled your charge holes to spec, you loaded to the gram, you timed the sequence so the rock fell where you told it to fall and nowhere else. The mountain didn't care about your intentions. It cared about your fractions of a second. A hand that shook was a hand that killed — hers or someone else's, usually someone else's first.

So she hid it. She'd gotten good at hiding it. Sleeves rolled down even in heat. Charges set at night when no one watched. The old trick of pressing her forearm flat against stone until the vibration burned itself out against something solid. And when it got bad enough that she couldn't trust herself near a primer, she found reasons. Equipment checks. Wire runs. Anything that put distance between Petra's fingers and the detonator.

Yusuf knew. She'd seen him know — the way his eyes tracked her sleeve instead of her face, the way he'd started handing her the rope end instead of the drill. He'd never said a word. That was its own kind of debt, and Petra kept a ledger of debts the way other people kept prayers.

Naji didn't know. Odile didn't know. Vey — well. Vey knew everything about everyone; that was what made him useful and unbearable in equal measure. But Vey hadn't said anything either, which meant he hadn't decided yet whether her secret was leverage or liability. With Vey those were always the same thing, just priced differently depending on the day.

At 4:52 the ground spoke.

Not an earthquake. Something smaller and more personal — a deep structural groan from the ridge above the cave, the sound wet rock makes when the temperature swings hard overnight and water finds new ways into old fractures. They'd been hearing it for two days. Yusuf had been listening to it the way a doctor listens to a heart he doesn't like.

Then a second sound, sharper, closer, and Petra was out of her bag and into her boots before her brain finished the sentence.

*That's the gallery.*

* * *

They reached the side gallery's mouth at first light, and the mountain had closed it like a fist.

The side gallery ran off the main descent passage — thirty meters of tunnel the survey called Gallery C, the one Naji had been mapping because Vey wanted every meter documented before the funding deadline, and Odile had gone in with him because Odile couldn't stand letting the kid work alone in the dark. Two people. One headlamp between them, because Naji's had died the day before and the spares were in the cache at Cold Fork, and Odile had said *we'll share, it's thirty meters, we'll be twenty minutes.*

Twenty minutes had been nine hours ago.

Now the entrance was a wall. Not a collapse exactly — a slump, a shoulder of the ceiling letting go, maybe forty tons of limestone and marl slumped down across the passage in a wedge that tapered to nothing somewhere in the dark. Forty tons sounded like a lot. In blasting terms it was nothing. In rescue terms it was everything, because you couldn't dig through it — the material above it was hanging, cracked, waiting for an excuse — and you couldn't go around, because the survey showed solid rock on both flanks for sixty meters in either direction.

You went through. Or they died in there.

"Odile!" Yusuf's voice hit the rubble wall and came back flattened. "Naji!"

Nothing. Then — faint, muffled, coming through forty tons of stone like a voice through a pillow — a knock. Three knocks. Pause. Three knocks.

"They're alive," Odile said, and Petra heard something crack open in his voice, some door he'd been keeping shut since the dock, since the contract, since the whole cold arithmetic of this expedition. "They're alive."

"Air?" Yusuf asked, already moving, pulling line from his coil with those quick goat-herd hands.

"The gallery has a chimney at the far end," Petra said. She'd memorized the survey because memorizing surveys was how she stayed alive in this trade; you didn't blast what you hadn't walked in your head. "Fissure running up toward the sink. Narrow but it'll move air. They've got air." She looked at the slump wall, at the fresh cracks feathering up into the ceiling above it, at the fine dust still sifting down in threads. "What they don't have is time. That face is live. Every hour we spend, more of it wants to come down."

Vey arrived last, of course, because Vey arrived precisely when arriving made the best theater. He stood at the rubble with his headlamp throwing his shadow huge across the wall, and Petra watched him do the math she was doing — except Vey's math had different variables. Vey's math had a funding deadline in it. Nine days until the passes closed, per the shepherd village. Two days already burned on the rockfall rescue in the original schedule. Vey's math had a number where other men had a conscience, and the number was winning, Petra could see it winning behind his eyes.

"Can you clear it?" he asked her. Not *can we.* Can *you.* He knew. Of course he knew.

"I can clear it," Petra said.

Her left hand chose that moment to shiver against her thigh, a small traitorous flutter, and she pressed it flat and pretended to be studying the rock.

* * *

Here is what placing a charge looks like when your hands are trying to leave you:

You drill the charge hole first — or in this case, you find the natural fracture and clean it, because there was no time to drill and the slump had helpfully opened a seam along its own spine, a finger-width gap running diagonally through the mass like a fault line in a marriage. You pack the cartridge in with a wooden tamping rod, never metal, never anything that sparks. You run your detonating cord and your delay elements. You tie the knots. And every knot is a small negotiation between you and your own nervous system, because a knot tied by a shaking hand is a knot that might hold or might not, and the difference is measured in other people's children.

Naji was sixteen. Petra kept doing that arithmetic too, against her will. Sixteen. She'd been nineteen her first blast, on a quarry crew outside Izmir, and the foreman had put her hand on the plunger and said *feel it, girl, feel the mountain answer,* and she had loved it, God help her, she had loved it for eleven years, the one thing her body had ever agreed to do perfectly. Now the mountain was answering again and her hands were arguing about it.

She worked through the morning. Yusuf anchored the safety line across the chamber — a traverse line strung between two sound pillars so anyone working the face could clip in if the ceiling shed. He rigged it without being asked, without discussion, the way he did everything, moving through the chaos of a rescue with the unhurried economy of a man who had grown up on paths where hurry got you killed. When Petra needed the tamping rod held steady against the seam, Yusuf held it. When her left hand jumped mid-tie and the cord slipped, Yusuf's hand was already there covering hers, steadying it against the rock, saying nothing, looking at nothing, giving her the fiction that she'd done it herself.

"You don't have to do that," she said once, low, not stopping work.

"Do what?" he said, and moved away to check an anchor.

That was the whole conversation. It was the most anyone had given her in years.

By noon the charge was set: a single row of relief shots along the seam, light charges, millisecond delays, designed to peel the slump apart along its own fracture and drop the debris into the chamber floor where it could be shoveled, rather than bringing the hanging ceiling down with it. Light charges meant precision. Precision meant her hands.

She sat back on her heels and looked at them. Left and right. Old friends, old enemies.

"Petra." Odile crouched beside her. He'd spent the morning hauling spoil and shouting encouragement through the wall and getting three-knock answers back, and his face had gone through grief and hope so many times it had settled into something raw and simple. "You've been shaking since dawn. I'm not blind."

"No," she said. "You're just late."

"Give me the primer. I'll place it. Tell me where."

And there it was — the offer, plain and stupid and kind, from a man who had never touched a charge in his life, who would as likely kill them all as free them, offering to take the one thing she had left that was hers.

"It's not a shovel, Odile. You don't hand it off." She flexed her fingers. Watched them obey, mostly. "It's mine. I placed it, I fire it. That's the rule. My rule. If it goes wrong, it goes wrong on my name."

"That's a stupid rule."

"Yeah," she said. "Most of mine are."

She fired at 12:40. She braced her right wrist in the crook of her left elbow, locked both arms against her ribs, and pressed the firing switch with her thumb, and for the half-second between her thumb and the boom her hands were the steadiest they'd been in a year, because terror, it turned out, was also a stabilizer.

The seam took the shot beautifully. The slump sighed, split along its spine, and folded outward into the chamber in two great slabs that settled with a sound like a book closing. Dust boiled out. Through it, gray figures moving — and then a lamp beam, swinging wildly, and a voice cracking up an octave.

"We're here! We're here, we're okay—"

Naji came through the gap first, filthy, dehydrated, fourteen hours underground with one lamp and a boy's stubborn refusal to be frightened properly, and Odile grabbed him and held him and Petra saw the older man's whole body shake worse than her hands ever had. Behind him a second figure — because there had been two, Petra had almost forgotten, the arithmetic of panic keeps dropping terms — a second figure, limping, one arm cradled.

It wasn't Odile's.

It was a stranger. A man in modern boots, a synthetic jacket, a face none of them knew, dust-caked, bleeding from the scalp, blinking at them like a man waking from someone else's dream.

"He was already in there," Naji said. His voice came out thin. "When the rock came down. He was already in the gallery. He says he's been in the cave two days."

Petra looked at the man's boots. Modern tread. Clean laces. And at the slump wall, at the seam she'd just blasted, and thought with terrible clarity: *nobody walks into a sealed side gallery of an unexplored cave by accident.*

Behind her, very quietly, Cassian Vey stopped breathing for a moment.

* * *

They spent the rest of that day and all of the next digging the chamber floor clear enough to move the camp forward, because the collapse had eaten their margin and Vey refused — absolutely, finally, with the whole dead weight of the funding deadline behind him — to give any of it back. Two days. Their window had been nine. It was seven now, and the sky over the coastal range had begun doing things skies shouldn't do in this season, stacking itself into towers, going the color of a bruise deciding where to land.

But something else happened in those two days, and Petra kept noticing it sideways, the way you notice weather changing.

Yusuf taught Naji knots on the second evening, sitting by the stove, the boy's frostbitten-looking fingers guided patiently through a bowline until he had it. Nobody assigned the lesson. It just happened, the way it happens in families.

Odile gave Naji his spare socks without being asked and lied about having others.

Petra caught herself, on the second night, checking that Naji was sleeping warm before she checked her own gear — an action with no operational value whatsoever, pure waste motion, and she did it anyway, and then stood there in the cold feeling like a burglar in her own chest.

This was the danger. She understood that with professional clarity. Crews were built on distance; you kept the payroll a payroll and everyone went home. But these people had stopped being a crew somewhere in the last forty-eight hours, sometime between the three knocks through the rock and the bowline lesson, and now there were seven days left and a live ceiling and a stranger with clean boots, and Petra knew — the way you know a slope is loading up before it slides — that the family they'd accidentally become was exactly the thing that would get them killed.

People who love each other stop counting the cost. Mountains count everything.

On the second night she sat apart, cleaning primers by lamplight, and her left hand shook so badly she had to hold the wrist with the right, and she looked at the stranger across camp — fed now, bandaged, watching Vey with an attention he thought was hidden — and she thought about the blade she'd sold to Vey's expedition two years ago. The knife with the distinctive guard. The one whose marks she'd recognized in Marisol Sandoval's journal and said nothing about.

Everyone at this fire was carrying something. She'd told herself that made it fair.

Across the flames, Odile looked up from the compass in his hands and caught her watching the stranger, and for a long moment neither of them looked away, and Petra understood that whatever the boy had found in that journal, whatever the needle pointed at, the ledger was about to come due — and every one of them, the whole accidental family, was standing on the account.
