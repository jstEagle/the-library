## 10. The Compass Points at Naji

Two days after the rockfall, Naji was still sleeping like the dead.

Not literally — Yusuf had checked his breathing so many times in the first night that Petra finally threatened to sew a bell to the boy's chest so everyone could stop hovering. But he slept fourteen hours at a stretch, woke to drink broth and complain about it, and slept again. The rock had caught him across the ribs and one shoulder; the bruise bloomed down his side in colors that moved through the spectrum as the days passed, purple going green at the edges like something ripening.

Odile sat outside the medical tent — a canvas lean-to really, more storage than surgery — and turned the compass over in his hands for perhaps the hundredth time since the gallery came down on them.

The needle pointed at Naji.

It had been pointing at Naji for two days, steady as a rivet, and Odile still hadn't said anything to anyone about it.

He told himself there was nothing to say yet. That he needed more data. That a needle could stick, could catch on a pivot worn loose by forty years of salt air and bad handling. He'd watched compasses fail before; he worked salvage docks, half the instruments that came through were liars of one kind or another. A stuck needle was a mechanical problem, and mechanical problems had mechanical answers.

Except the needle hadn't been stuck when the rockfall happened.

He'd been carrying it in his breast pocket when the gallery groaned and dropped. In the blackness afterward, with Naji's hand locked around his wrist and dust sifting down like gray snow, Odile had pulled the compass out mostly from habit — the way some men reach for cigarettes, or prayer. And in the dark, by feel alone, he'd felt the needle swinging. Restless. Hunting. It had swung toward the rubble wall where the sound of digging was coming from, then back toward Naji, then toward the wall again, as if it couldn't decide which rescue mattered more: the way out, or the boy bleeding against him.

At the time he'd thought it was concussion. His own.

Now, in daylight, with Naji breathing slow and even ten feet away inside the tent, the needle lay flat and certain, aimed through the canvas at the kid's chest like a small brass accusation.

* * *

"You're doing the thing," Petra said.

Odile looked up. She'd come around the tent's flank with two tin mugs and the particular walk she used when her hands were bothering her — careful, elbows tucked, as if carrying something full to the brim even when she wasn't. She set one mug beside him without asking what he wanted. It was tea. It was always tea. Yusuf bought it in quantities that suggested he expected the expedition to be evacuated by camel at any moment.

"What thing?"

"The staring-at-nothing thing." She lowered herself onto a supply crate with a grunt. "You've done it three times today. Once at the tent, once at your boots, once at me, which I didn't care for."

"Sorry."

"Don't apologize, just talk." She blew on her tea. "Vey wants a progress meeting at eighteen hundred. He's already talking about making up the lost days. I want to know what mood to bring."

"He can't make up the lost days."

"He knows that. He wants us to think he doesn't know that." Petra drank. "There's a difference between a man who lies to you and a man who lies to himself, and the second kind is worse company on a mountain."

Odile almost smiled. Then the weight in his pocket shifted against his hip and the smile died in the crib.

"Petra. Can I ask you something strange?"

"I'd be disappointed otherwise."

He took out the compass. Held it flat on his palm between them. "What do you see it doing?"

She glanced down, ready to be unimpressed, and he watched the readiness curdle into something else. Her eyes narrowed. She leaned closer without touching it — Petra never touched other people's instruments, an old blaster's superstition — and her lips moved slightly, reading the dial.

"It's pointing at the tent," she said slowly. "At—" She stopped. Looked up at him. "That's north-ish. Isn't it? Give me a second, my bearings are all—"

"It's not north." He kept his voice low. The camp was quiet — mid-afternoon lull, most of the crew down at the cache site repacking what the rockfall had scattered. "I've checked it against the sun, against the survey map, against Yusuf's GPS unit which he pretends is a cigarette case. It hasn't pointed north once since we entered the range."

Petra set her mug down with exaggerated care, the way she did when her tremor was talking to her and she refused to let it win. "Then what's it pointing at?"

"A person."

* * *

He told her all of it, because if he didn't tell someone he was going to start talking to the compass itself, and there were already enough people on this mountain who thought he was strange.

The night after Marisol's funeral — no, before that. The first time, years ago, sorting her effects in the storage unit the university had stopped paying for. He'd held the compass and the needle had swung in a slow circle and settled on his own chest, and he'd assumed magnetism, assumed his belt buckle, assumed anything but the truth, because the truth was a door he wasn't ready to walk through. On the dock, the day the tide peeled back the seabed, the needle had strained seaward — toward the cave, though he hadn't known it yet. Toward whatever was in the letters he'd been reading on the breakwater that morning. And in camp, that first week, he'd caught it pointing at Vey. Steady. Certain. For hours at a time.

"I thought it meant Vey knew something," Odile said. "A fix on the guilty man. Like it was evidence."

"And now?"

"Now I don't think it points at guilt. Or truth. Or north, or anything useful." He turned the instrument so the needle quivered, held, quivered again, and re-locked on the tent. "I think it points at love."

Petra was quiet long enough that he heard the wind change pitch in the pass above them, a thin whistle through the col that Yusuf had started checking twice a day.

"That's a hell of an instrument," she said finally.

"My mother built it. Modified it, anyway. There are papers in her effects — old ones, from before the disgrace, before anyone was watching her closely enough to matter. She was working on resonance. Not magnetic north — *personal* north. She wrote that every person has one. A fixed point. The thing they orient by." He swallowed. "She called it the true bearing. Everything else, she wrote, is declination."

"And hers was—"

"I don't know. That's the problem." He closed his fist around the compass, and through the brass he could feel the needle pressing against its housing like a small live thing. "When she disappeared, the last entry in her logbook gives a bearing. Everyone assumed it was navigation. Search teams used it. They went exactly where it pointed and found nothing, because they were looking for a cave and the bearing wasn't pointing at a cave."

Petra went very still. Petra, who had placed charges under fire, who had crawled into a collapsing gallery two days ago with her hands shaking and her jaw set like poured concrete — that woman looked at Odile as if he'd shown her a wound.

"It was pointing at a person," she said.

"At whoever she loved most. Whoever her true bearing was." He made himself say it plainly, because plainness was the only way through. "And the searchers followed that bearing into the range, and the range killed two of them, and the inquiry needed someone to blame, and the man who gave them someone was Cassian Vey."

* * *

They sat with that while the light went long and amber across the scree. Down at the cache site, someone laughed — Naji's laugh, actually, thinner than usual but unmistakable, and both of them turned toward the sound at the same time.

"He's up," Petra said.

"He shouldn't be up."

"He's sixteen and bored and concussed. Being up is the least dangerous thing he'll do today." She stood, joints cracking, and paused. "You're not going to tell Vey about this."

It wasn't a question, but he answered it anyway. "No."

"Good. Because here's what I know about instruments that measure people instead of rocks." She looked down at her own hands, resting at her sides, and flexed them once, deliberately, the tremor visible and unhidden. "People find out they're being measured, they change. You put this thing on the table at Vey's meeting, and everyone in that tent becomes a liar in a new direction. Keep it close. Keep it *quiet*."

She walked off toward the cache site, and Odile stayed on the crate a while longer, watching the needle strain against its glass toward a sixteen-year-old laughing at something Yusuf had said.

* * *

Naji found him at dusk.

The boy came up the slope from the cache site moving carefully, one arm cradled against his ribs, but moving — and there was something in his face that Odile had learned to read over the past weeks, the look he got when he'd decided to ask for something and was rehearsing how. He lowered himself onto the crate Petra had vacated with a hiss of pain he tried to disguise as a sigh.

"Yusuf says I'm supposed to rest."

"Yusuf is right."

"Yusuf also smokes four cigarettes a day at five thousand meters, so his authority on health is compromised." Naji settled back against the tent pole. For a while he just breathed, watching the first stars come out over the col, sharp and cold in the thinning blue. Then: "You've been sitting out here holding that thing for two days. Staring at it."

Odile's hand had gone to his pocket without his deciding to. He noticed, and Naji noticed him notice, and the boy smiled — the crooked, too-old smile that had made Odile trust him long before he'd decided to.

"It's a compass," Odile said.

"It's not a compass." Naji said it simply, without drama, the way he said most true things. "My grandmother had a ring like that. Not magic — I'm not saying magic. But things carry weight. Things carry *people*. Anyone who grew up around old objects knows that." He nodded at the pocket. "What does yours carry?"

Odile took it out. Held it between them in the last light.

And because it was Naji — because the boy had read over his shoulder at the journal and instead of anger Odile had felt relief, had felt *seen*, had spent two nights telling him pieces of Marisol he'd never told anyone — because of all of that, he did the honest thing.

"Take it," he said. "Hold it flat. Tell me what it does."

Naji took it with his good arm, cupping it in both palms like something rescued from water. And the needle — the needle that had lain locked on him for two days, that had fought its housing like a tethered animal — the needle swung.

It swept a wide, lazy arc across the whole dial, east to west to south, unhurried, almost drowsy. Then it slowed. Wobbled. And settled.

Pointing straight back at Odile's chest.

Neither of them spoke. Above the col, a wind came through and died again, and somewhere below, the camp generator coughed and steadied. Naji stared down at the needle, then up at Odile, and Odile watched understanding arrive on the boy's face in stages — confusion, then wonder, then something softer and more frightened than either.

"It's pointing at you," Naji whispered.

"I know."

"Why?"

"Because right now," Odile said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended, "I think I'm what it loves most. What its holder loves most. It's mine now. It was hers." He reached out and touched the brass, and Naji let him, and for a moment their hands overlapped on the instrument and the needle trembled between two claims, quivering like a struck string. "Marisol carried this for thirty years. Whatever it pointed at, in the end — that was her true bearing. And the search teams followed it into these mountains thinking it was a heading."

Naji's breath caught. "They were following her heart."

"They were following her heart with shovels and rope and no idea what they were looking for." Odile took the compass back gently. The needle swung home to Naji immediately, eager, certain. "And when they found nothing, someone had to be blamed. And the man who supplied the blame was standing right next to her the whole time."

* * *

They sat in the dark a long while. Naji's breathing evened out; the painkillers and the altitude and the exhaustion pulled at him, but he fought it, blinking hard, the way teenagers do when they're afraid of missing something important.

"Your mother," he said finally, half-asleep, "loved somebody. At the end. And it wasn't your father, and it wasn't—" He frowned, surfacing. "Wasn't Vey. Because the needle doesn't point at Vey anymore. Does it?"

"No." Odile had checked, that morning, obsessively, while Vey lectured the crew about schedule recovery. The needle had given Vey a single indifferent sweep and returned to Naji. "Whatever Vey was to her, it's over. Whatever it was, it ended before she went into that mountain."

"So who's left?" Naji murmured. "Who does a woman love most, at the end, when she's walking into a cave she might not walk out of?"

And the answer arrived in Odile's chest before it arrived in his mind, arriving the way grief always arrived for him — sideways, physical, a pressure behind the sternum.

*A son,* he thought. *She would have loved her son.*

But the needle had never pointed at him. Not once, not in the storage unit, not on the dock, not anywhere in the weeks since. If the compass measured the holder's true bearing, and Marisol had been its holder until the day she vanished —

Then either she had stopped loving him.

Or the compass had changed hands before she went into the cave. Had been left behind, deliberately, for him to find — left behind the way you leave a letter. And her last fix, her final true bearing, taken in that chamber with the murals, had pointed at something in the dark that she loved more than life, more than him, more than escape.

Something worth staying for. Something worth dying beside.

"Odile?" Naji's voice, very small. "You've gone white."

"The journal," Odile said. He was already on his feet, already moving toward the tent where the waterlogged pages lay drying between sheets of blotting paper, each one a small brown ghost of his mother's handwriting. "The pages Vey didn't tear out. The last entries. She wrote a bearing, Naji — the searchers' bearing — but she also wrote coordinates, and times, and I've been reading them as navigation because that's what they look like." He ducked into the tent, scrabbled for the lantern, thumbed it on. "But if they're fixes. If every entry is a fix of her true bearing, taken over months—"

The pages were there. He spread them with shaking hands, and Naji crowded in beside him, ribs forgotten, and together they read the sequence of bearings Marisol Sandoval had recorded in her final season:

The sea. The sea. The university library. The sea again. A hotel in the port town. The sea.

And then, in the last dated entry, in handwriting gone hurried and deep, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the page beneath:

*Bearing changed today. It has never changed before. I don't know what it means yet. I only know I am no longer afraid, and that frightens me more than anything.*

Below it, a single line, almost illegible, written as if against her own will:

*I think it's pointing into the mountain.*

Odile looked up. Naji was watching him, and in the lantern light the boy's face held the expression of someone standing at the edge of a drop.

"Someone was in that cave," Naji said quietly. "Before her. Someone she loved."

"Or someone who went in with her." Odile folded the pages with a care that felt like handling bone. Outside, far off across the darkening range, thunder muttered — the monsoon clearing its throat, days early, exactly as the shepherd village had warned. "And Vey tore out the pages that would have named them."

Above the camp, the first fat drops began to hit the canvas, and the needle in Odile's hand trembled — not toward Naji, not toward the tent, but briefly, wildly, toward the mountain itself, before settling home like a heart returning to rhythm.
