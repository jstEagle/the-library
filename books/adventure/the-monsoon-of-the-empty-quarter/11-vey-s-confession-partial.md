## 11. Vey's Confession, Partial

Cassian Vey had rehearsed this conversation for eleven years.

He had rehearsed it in the shower of a rented flat in Marseille, in the back of taxis between funding meetings, once — memorably, humiliatingly — into the voicemail of a woman named Delphine who had stopped returning his calls in 2019 and whose number he still knew by heart. In every rehearsal he was eloquent. In every rehearsal Odile Sandoval listened with a face like cut stone and then said something that forgave him, or didn't, and either way Cassian walked out of the imagined scene lighter than he'd entered it.

The real version began at four in the morning, in a tent that smelled of kerosene and wet wool, with the boy asleep twenty meters away and the mountain making sounds it had no business making.

He hadn't planned to do it tonight. That was the thing about confessions — they were like charges, Petra would have told him. You could pack them carefully, set the fuse on your own schedule, and then some fracture line you'd never mapped decided the timing for you.

It had been the compass. It was always going to be the compass.

* * *

Vey had watched Odile test it for the first time outside the staging camp, before the range, when things were still simple enough to be called logistics. The boy had held the brass instrument flat on his palm and the needle had swung past north, past everything, and settled pointing directly at Vey's chest across forty meters of gravel.

Odile had looked up. Their eyes had met. And then the boy had done something worse than anger: he had smiled, politely, the way you smile at a stranger who has stepped on your foot, and put the compass away.

That smile had been living in Vey's ribs ever since, through the goat paths and the rockfall and the nine-day warning from the shepherd village, growing heavier by the hour, until tonight, when he'd found himself standing outside Odile's tent with no memory of deciding to walk there.

Inside, the boy was awake. Of course he was awake. Nobody slept well on this mountain anymore; the weather reports had seen to that. He sat cross-legged on his sleeping bag with Marisol's waterlogged journal open on his knees, drying page by page under a headlamp, and when Vey ducked through the flap he didn't even startle. Just looked up with those eyes — Marisol's eyes, God help him, the exact gray-green of a sea right before weather — and waited.

"You should be sleeping," Vey said. Idiot's opening. He heard it leave his mouth and hated it.

"So should you."

"I need to tell you something." His voice came out lower than he intended. "About the journal. About what happened to it."

Odile closed the journal. Not quickly, not protectively — deliberately, the way his mother used to close a notebook when she'd caught someone lying to her and wanted them to know she'd caught them. Vey felt eleven years of rehearsal dissolve like sugar in rain.

"Sit down," Odile said.

There was no second chair. There was never a second chair in a salvage tent. Vey sat on the supply crate by the door, which put him below the boy, looking up, and he understood that Odile had arranged it that way without moving anything at all.

* * *

Here is what Cassian Vey had told himself for eleven years, in the version where he could live with himself:

Marisol Sandoval had been reckless. Brilliant, yes — he had never denied brilliant, not to anyone, not under oath — but reckless with other people's money and other people's careers, chasing a cave that three surveys said couldn't exist where her letters claimed it did. When the funding committee asked him directly whether her dating methodology was sound, he had answered honestly. She had published a chronology she couldn't defend. He had defended the truth instead of his mentor. It had cost him everything too — her friendship, his reputation as a man who stood by his people, the quiet certainty that he was good. He had paid. Everyone always forgot he had paid.

That was the version he'd carried into every taxi and every shower. It had the great advantage of being almost true, in the way that a map is almost the territory, if you don't look too closely at the rivers.

Here is what actually happened:

The funder's name was Aldous Renner, though the money moved through three shells and a foundation with a name like a hymn. Renner had come to their first survey season uninvited, charming, generous, asking questions that sounded like enthusiasm and were actually appraisal. How extensive were the galleries. Who else knew the coordinates. What would it take to keep the discovery quiet until title could be secured.

Marisol had laughed at him. That was the beginning of the end — not the falsified dates, not the missing pages, but a laugh, bright and contemptuous, in a hotel bar in Salalah, in front of two of Renner's associates. She had told him, in effect, that the Empty Quarter's dead were not for sale, and she had made it funny, and funny was the one cruelty rich men never forgive.

After that, the machinery started turning. Vey saw it turn. He was twenty-nine years old and drowning — his father's clinic debts, his sister's tuition, a life assembled out of loans — and Renner had sat across from him at that same hotel bar a week later and spoken very quietly about how reputations worked. How fragile they were. How a young archaeologist's entire future could rest on whether he was perceived as rigorous or as loyal, and how loyalty to a sinking ship was just a way of drowning with company.

And then Renner had said the sentence. Vey had kept it verbatim for eleven years, polished smooth from handling:

*"I'm not asking you to lie, Mr. Vey. I'm asking you to decline to perjure yourself."*

Because that was the genius of it. Marisol's dating was honest. Her chronology was defensible — Vey had checked the numbers himself, twice, terrified, hoping to find the flaw that would make all this easy, and there wasn't one. But the methodology was new, and new methodologies could be made to look like fraud by a sufficiently hostile expert witness, and Vey was the only person alive positioned to be that witness credibly. All he had to do was testify to doubts he didn't hold. Decline to perjure himself. And Renner's foundation would clear his father's clinic debt, and the site would be tied up in litigation until Renner's people could strip it quietly, and Marisol Sandoval's name would be mud, and no one would ever look for her cave again.

He said yes. He told himself it was temporary. He told himself she'd understand — she, who had taught him that access to a site was a moral question, that provenance was a form of justice. He told himself so many things in that hotel bar that he'd half believed them by the time the ice melted in his glass.

The testimony took forty minutes. It destroyed her career in an afternoon. And then, because the universe is not subtle, it destroyed her in every other way it could reach: the funding gone, the university washing its hands, the creditors circling within the year. Within two years she was working salvage docks under other people's licenses. Within five she was writing letters to a son she barely saw, letters full of a cave she couldn't stop describing, as if the cave were the only thing left that believed in her.

She went back for it alone. She died alone. And Cassian Vey had helped make her name so poisonous that when the search parties read her final letters, they read them as evidence of obsession rather than discovery, and scaled the search down, and then stopped.

* * *

"And the pages," Odile said.

They had been sitting in silence for some time. Vey realized his hands were shaking — not Petra's tremor, nothing medical, just the body's ancient opinion of what it was doing.

"The pages," he said. "Yes."

After her death, the estate had been a bonfire of debts, and Vey had bought Marisol's field effects at auction — ostensibly to preserve them, and that much was even true. Among them, her last expedition journal. He had read it in one night in a hotel room in Muscat, and by dawn he understood that he was holding the thing Renner feared most in the world: proof. Proof the cave existed where she said. Proof the dating was sound. Proof, in her own hand, dated and witnessed by her own instruments, that would have overturned the testimony, restored her name, and exposed Renner's plan to strip the site — a plan she had somehow learned of, near the end, and documented with the cold precision of a woman building a case she expected to be read after she was dead.

Renner wanted the journal destroyed. Vey had already given his testimony; refusing now would accomplish nothing except confirming his own guilt. So he had torn out the final pages — her account of the inner chamber, her chronology, her notes on Renner's intermediaries — and burned them, and reported the rest of the journal as inconclusive fragments.

Except.

"I didn't burn them," Vey said. His voice cracked on the second word and he let it. "I told myself I would. I sat there with the lighter for — I don't know how long. And I couldn't. Because they weren't just evidence. They were her. Her handwriting, her jokes in the margins, her little arrows and abbreviations she thought nobody else could read. Burning them felt like burning her twice."

"So you hid them."

"I hid them." Eleven years in a bank vault in Geneva, in a deposit box under a false name, because a man who had already betrayed Marisol Sandoval once did not trust himself to do the right thing with the most dangerous paper in the world. "I told myself I was protecting them. Protecting *you*, maybe — because if they surfaced, Renner wouldn't just discredit you, he'd bury you next to her. That's what I told myself."

"And now?"

"Now I think I couldn't destroy them because destroying them meant admitting what I'd done completely. Keeping them let me feel like a custodian instead of a thief." He made himself look up. Made himself meet the gray-green eyes. "That's the truth of it. Not the version where I was protecting anyone. I was protecting myself. From her. From you. From the version of me that signed the testimony."

* * *

He had expected fury. He had rehearsed for fury — fury was clean, fury he could stand against. What he got instead was worse.

Odile was quiet for a long moment, and then he laughed — one short, terrible breath of a laugh, with no humor anywhere in it.

"Do you know what my mother's debts did to me?" the boy said. "Not the money. The money I could work off. Do you know what it was like, being her son? Every dock, every crew, every creditor's office — *Sandoval*. The fraudster's boy. I spent six years paying for a crime she never committed, and you knew. You sat across from me at that contract table and you knew, and you let me sign on to dig up the cave that killed her, and you said nothing for—" he gestured at the tent wall, the mountain, the whole black world beyond it, "—for weeks. Until four in the morning, when it became convenient for *you*."

"Yes," Vey said. Because it was true, and because the boy deserved one word that cost something.

"Why now?"

"Because the needle pointed at me." He heard how it sounded and pressed on anyway. "Your compass. Outside the staging camp. Whatever that thing measures — it measured me and found something. And I've been carrying that like a stone ever since, because I don't know what it found. Love? Guilt? They weigh the same at four in the morning." He rubbed his face. "And because the passes close in eight days, and if we die on this mountain with the truth still in a vault in Geneva, then Renner wins forever, and I will not — I cannot — let that be the ending."

"The pages are in Geneva."

"They're in Geneva. Box 4471, Banque Helvétique du Commerce, under the name Corvel. The key—" He reached inside his jacket and set it on the crate between them. A small brass key, absurdly ordinary, gleaming in the headlamp light. "I've carried it for eleven years. I told myself I was waiting for the right moment. There is no right moment. There's only this one."

Odile looked at the key for a long time. He didn't pick it up.

"You testified against her," he said finally, "to protect yourself. Then you stole her proof to protect yourself. Then you hid it for a decade to protect yourself. And now you're telling me — why? So I'll think better of you? So you can walk out of this story forgiven?"

"No," Vey said. "I'm telling you because you're going to need it. Renner's people aren't behind us by accident, Odile. Petra's radio traffic — I've seen the patterns. Someone is feeding our position to someone, and the only person on this mountain worth tracking is the person holding her journal." He stood, knees cracking, old at thirty-eight. "Whatever happens in the next eight days, you need to know exactly what I am, so you can decide what I'm worth. That's the only honest reason I've ever had for anything. It took me long enough to find it."

At the tent flap he paused. He didn't turn around.

"She named you Odile," he said. "She told me once it was from a ballet — a girl who looks like the heroine but isn't, until the last act, when everyone finds out she was the real one all along. She said she'd bet on the last act." A breath. "So do I."

He ducked out into the dark.

Behind him, in the lamplight, he heard the small metallic sound of the key being picked up — and beneath it, fainter, from the direction of the supply crates where the boy kept his mother's effects, the almost inaudible tick of a brass compass needle swinging, settling, and holding steady on the sound of a man walking away.
