## 12. The Split at Cold Fork

**Yusuf**

The fork announced itself the way bad news always did in the high country: quietly, then all at once.

One moment they were climbing a spine of gray rock with the wind flattening them against the stone, and the next the ridge simply split — one branch bending east toward the cave's upper approach, the other dropping west into a saddle of black scree that fell away toward the descent route. Between them stood a cairn so old its stones had fused with lichen into something less like a marker and more like a grave.

Yusuf stopped and let the others catch up. He had been dreading this place for two days, since the shepherd's boy in the village had drawn it for him in the dirt of a courtyard: *the mountain splits like a man deciding*. The boy had laughed when he said it. Yusuf had not.

He looked west first. The descent route was still open — a brown thread of trail stitching down through the scree toward the cache point they'd scouted on the way up. Below it, far below, the valley floor was already wearing a different color. Not the green of rain. The bruised violet of air that had too much water in it.

Then he looked east. The cave's approach climbed another four hundred meters along an exposed rib of rock, and above that rib the clouds were doing something he had only seen twice in his life, both times before disasters. They were stacking. Building themselves upward instead of sideways, like a wall being raised by hands too large to see.

"Vey." He had to shout over the wind. "We need to talk about the sky."

Cassian Vey turned from the eastern branch. Even hunched against the weather he managed to look like a man presiding over something — a boardroom, a courtroom, a funeral. "We're close," he said. "Two days' work. Maybe three."

"Look at the clouds."

"I've looked at the clouds."

"Then look again." Yusuf pointed with his whole arm, the way you pointed at a fire. "That is not a monsoon yet. That is a monsoon *deciding*. And when it decides, this rib—" he stabbed a finger at the exposed climb— "becomes a waterslide with rocks in it. We go up there tomorrow, we may not come back down."

Behind him he heard Petra shift her pack, the wet creak of straps. Naji said nothing, but Yusuf could feel the boy's attention like a hand on his shoulder blade.

Vey walked back along the ridge until the wind let them speak at normal volume, which somehow made it worse. "The funding window closes in eleven days," he said. "If we're not out with documentation by then, the concession reverts. You know what reverts means."

"It means someone else comes," Yusuf said. "Someone worse."

"It means everything Marisol did was for nothing."

And there it was. Yusuf had watched men use dead women as arguments before; it was a specialty of certain kinds of ambitious people. But Vey said it differently — not as leverage but as a wound he kept reopening himself, checking whether it still bled.

"The mountain doesn't care about her funding," Yusuf said. "I'm telling you what my grandmother told me about this range: it gives you one warning and then it stops giving."

* * *

**Naji**

Naji had learned to read adults the way other children learned to read — slowly, painfully, with a lot of punishment for mistakes. His father had been easy: anger first, then the blow. The marriage brokers had been harder: smiles first, then the trap.

Vey was the hardest of all, because Vey seemed genuinely unsure which one he was.

They stood at the fork while the guide and the expedition chief argued, and Naji used the time to watch Odile Sandoval, who was watching the eastern climb with an expression Naji recognized from mirrors. It was the face of someone walking toward a thing they'd sworn never to touch.

Odile caught him looking. "You have an opinion?" he asked. He didn't say it unkindly. That was the strange thing about Odile — he asked questions like he actually wanted the answers, even from a sixteen-year-old stowaway who was supposed to be a cook's assistant.

"My opinion," Naji said, "is that everyone here keeps saying *we* when they mean *I*."

Petra snorted. Even Vey glanced over.

"Vey says *we're* close because *he* needs the chamber," Naji went on, warming to it, the way he always warmed to trouble. "Yusuf says *we* should turn back because *he* promised his grandmother's ghost he'd keep fools alive. And Odile—" he hesitated, then committed— "Odile says nothing, because Odile needs the truth more than any of us need to live."

The wind gusted. Nobody laughed. That was how Naji knew he'd landed it.

"That's very perceptive," Vey said finally, in a tone that meant the opposite.

"He's right, though," Odile said quietly. "About me, anyway."

And Naji felt the small hot pride of being believed, and immediately after it the colder thought: *being believed is dangerous. People who believe you will take you seriously, and people who take you seriously will include you in things.* Like decisions. Like sacrifices.

Like a list of who goes up the rib and who goes down the scree.

* * *

**Petra**

Petra's hands were shaking, and not just from the cold.

She sat on a boulder slightly apart from the argument, pretending to check the charges in her kit, which was a lie she'd been performing for three days now. The tremor had gotten worse with altitude. This morning she'd dropped a detonator. She'd caught it against her boot before it hit rock, and her heart hadn't stopped hammering since, and no one had noticed, which was either luck or proof of how little these people actually saw her.

Except Naji had noticed. Of course Naji had noticed. The boy missed nothing; it was going to get him killed or make him rich.

And Odile — Odile had started standing downwind of her when she worked, casually, like a man adjusting his position for the view, and she understood that he was positioning himself to grab the kit if she dropped it. He thought she didn't know. She let him think it. Gratitude was easier than conversation.

Now the expedition was splitting, she could smell it, the way you smelled a charge about to go — the silence before the circuit closed. Vey wanted the inner door. Yusuf wanted the low route. And the crew of five was about to become a crew of two and three, and every split she'd ever been part of ended with somebody abandoned on the wrong side of a decision.

She'd been on the wrong side before. She'd put herself there, selling information to the funder's people, one report at a time, telling herself each one was harmless, each one was the last. Her debts had a way of renewing themselves like the tide.

"So here's what I propose," Vey was saying. "A support party holds the descent route. Caches supplies, maintains the line, stays ready to receive us. The ascent party pushes to the inner door. Minimal weight, maximum speed."

"And if the storm closes the rib behind the ascent party?" Yusuf asked.

"Then the support party holds the route until it opens."

"And if it doesn't open?"

Vey didn't answer. The silence did the answering for him, and Petra watched Yusuf hear it — watched the old guide's face do something complicated and final, like a man reading his own death warrant and finding the handwriting familiar.

* * *

**Odile**

The compass had been restless all morning.

Odile had taken to carrying it inside his jacket, against his chest, where he could feel it through the fabric — a faint tug, a living weight, like holding a bird that wanted to fly somewhere. All morning it had pulled east, toward the cave, hard enough that he'd begun to wonder if it wasn't pointing at the chamber at all but at whatever waited inside it. At whoever.

At the fork, with the argument grinding around him, he took it out under cover of checking his gloves.

The needle swung once, twice, and settled east. Steady. Certain. The way it had steadied on Vey that night in camp, the way it locked onto Naji after the rockfall. Whatever the compass loved, it loved without ambiguity, and right now it loved the dark ahead of them.

His mother had come up this same rib. Had stood at this same fork, maybe touched this same lichen-eaten cairn. Had gone on when every sensible instinct said turn back — and Odile was beginning to understand that she hadn't gone on despite the danger. She'd gone on because of what she was carrying. Evidence. A message. A thing worth dying next to, if dying was the price of delivery.

"Odile." Vey's voice, low. "You're with me?"

It wasn't really a question, and they both knew it, and Odile hated him a little for framing it as one — for making Odile choose in front of the others, so that choosing would look like loyalty instead of obsession.

But that was the thing. It *was* loyalty. Just not to Vey.

"I'm with her," Odile said. "You happen to be going the same direction."

Something moved through Vey's face — hurt, maybe, or relief, or the particular pain of a man hearing the truth said plainly for the first time in years. "Fair enough," he said.

* * *

**Yusuf**

Yusuf made the decision the way he made all his decisions: by imagining his grandmother standing behind him, arms crossed, waiting to see if he'd been paying attention all those years.

She would have said: *you cannot stop a fool from climbing. You can only be standing at the bottom when he falls.*

"All right," he said, and the argument died mid-breath, because they'd all been waiting for his voice without admitting it. "Here's how it is. I'm not letting you go up that rib alone, and I'm not taking Naji and Petra up it either. So nobody gets what they want, which is how you know it's fair."

He laid it out fast, before the wind could argue: he would take the descent party down to the cache point — food, rope, the spare tent, the medical kit — and hold the route open. Petra, because her charges might be needed to clear the path if the rockfall shifted. Naji, because—

Because Naji was sixteen, and Yusuf had buried enough of the young to know the arithmetic of it. Because the boy had run from one arranged life already and Yusuf would not hand him to a mountain.

"Naji goes down," he said.

"No," Naji said.

"Yes."

"You don't get to decide—"

"I'm the licensed guide," Yusuf said, and then, because the license was forged and they all knew it, he amended: "I'm the oldest person on this mountain, and on this mountain that's the law."

Naji's jaw worked. Yusuf watched the boy fight it — watched him look at Odile, at Petra, at the eastern climb where the stacked clouds were building their wall higher — and Yusuf understood, with a pang, that the boy wasn't fighting for adventure. He was fighting because every family he'd ever had had split apart in front of him, and he'd learned that splits were just abandonments wearing boots.

So Yusuf did the unforgivable kind thing. He took Naji by the shoulders, turned him away from the others, and spoke quietly enough that the wind took most of it.

"Listen to me. Someone has to hold the bottom of the rope. That's not the small job. That's the *only* job. Everything else is decoration. If we come off that rib — when we come off that rib — there are exactly three people in the world positioned to catch us, and you're one of them. Your grandmother—" he caught himself, adjusted— "whoever you ran from, whoever you're running to: neither of them gets anything if you freeze to death proving a point. Live. Hold the rope. Be there."

Naji stared at him with wet furious eyes. Then he nodded, once, the way stones nod downhill.

* * *

**Petra**

They cached the supplies at the saddle in ninety minutes of increasingly horizontal rain, working in the wordless rhythm of people who had stopped being strangers without ever discussing it. Petra set the cache under an overhang, sealed it in groundsheet and stone, and marked it with a cairn and a strip of orange tape that would survive anything short of the end of the world.

Her hands shook the whole time. She hid it inside the work, the way she'd hidden everything her whole life — the debts, the reports, the tremor, the slow rot of being owned by people who had never once gotten dirt under their nails for her.

At the edge of the saddle, where the descent route dropped away west, she paused. Below, the violet air had thickened. Somewhere beyond the ridgelines, water was gathering itself into a fist.

*Three days,* she thought. *Give us three days and we walk out of here clean.*

She didn't believe it. She was an explosives expert; disbelief was her profession. Every charge she'd ever placed had been placed on the assumption that the world wanted to collapse and required persuading otherwise.

She looked back up the ridge. Odile and Vey were already small figures on the eastern rib, moving with the deliberate economy of people who had stopped debating and started surviving. Yusuf stood at the fork like a gatepost, watching them go, and Naji stood beside him with his fists in his pockets and his chin up, refusing to cry in front of anyone, which Petra privately considered the bravest act performed on the mountain that day.

*Hold the rope,* she told herself. *Be there.*

* * *

**Odile**

The rib was worse than it looked, which was the rib's whole personality.

Four hundred meters of exposed climbing, the rock slicking under the first real rain, the drop on their left side deepening from serious to theological. Vey climbed well — better than Odile expected, with a grim economy that suggested mountains in his past he'd never mentioned. They roped together without discussing it. Some decisions made themselves.

Halfway up, Odile paused on a ledge barely wide enough for both boots and looked back.

The fork was invisible now, lost in cloud. The descent party was a theory. The world had contracted to the width of this ledge, the weight of his pack, the pull of the compass against his chest — steady, insistent, east.

"Your mother came up here in better weather," Vey called from above, "and it nearly killed her. I read her route notes."

"Then you know why she kept going."

"I know why *you're* keeping going." Vey's head appeared over the lip of the ledge, rain streaming off his jaw. "It's not the murals. It's not the science. You want to stand where she stood. You want to finish her sentence."

Odile gripped the wet rock and said nothing, because the man was right, and because saying so out loud would have cost something he wasn't ready to pay in front of Cassian Vey.

They climbed on. Above them the stacked clouds had finished their wall, and the light had gone the color of deep water, and the first thunder rolled down the range like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Behind and below, at the bottom of everything, three people were holding a rope.

Ahead, the compass pulled.

And somewhere over the western ridges, hours early, with no warning left to give — the monsoon opened its hand.
