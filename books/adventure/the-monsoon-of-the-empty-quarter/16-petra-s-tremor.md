## 16. Petra's Tremor

The path had no name in any language Yusuf was willing to speak aloud, which told Petra everything she needed to know about it.

They climbed out of the flooded cache ravine at first light, single file along a ledge the width of a coffin lid, and Petra kept her eyes on Naji's heels ahead of her because looking down meant looking at the drop, and looking at the drop meant thinking about her hands.

Her hands were the problem. They'd been the problem for two years now, since the detonator accident outside Muscat that she told everyone was a car crash. A fine tremor, the doctor called it. Fine. As if the word were a compliment. Some mornings it was barely there — a whisper under the skin, a moth against the inside of her wrists. Other mornings, like this one, the left hand carried a rhythm of its own, small and insistent, like a second pulse that hadn't gotten the news that she got to decide things.

She flexed her fingers inside her gloves. The tremor settled. For now.

"Petra." Odile's voice from behind, low. "How many charges left?"

She didn't turn around. Turning around on this ledge was how people became headlines. "Four sticks and the det cord," she said. "After that I'm a geologist with opinions."

"And after that?"

"After that we pray to whatever carved those stars into the rock, and hope they were better engineers than the funder's men."

Ahead of them, Naji laughed — a bright, unguarded sound that seemed designed specifically to annoy the mountain. The kid had been laughing more since the rockfall. Since Odile dug him out and Yusuf held the line and nobody, not one person on this expedition, had suggested leaving him. Petra had watched that happen from behind a charge bag with shaking hands, and something in her chest had cracked along a fault line she'd been pretending wasn't there.

That was the trouble with found families. They made confession feel possible. And confession, in Petra's experience, was just demolition you did to yourself.

* * *

They reached the shelf by midmorning — a broad terrace where the forbidden path switchbacked up through a cleft in the range wall, sheltered enough from the rain to make camp. Yusuf called a halt with the particular grimness of a man who had argued against this route and lost to geography.

"My grandmother said the old ones cut this path as an escape," he said, crouching over a fire he'd coaxed from damp scrub. "Not a road. An exit. You only come here when staying is worse."

"Then we're using it correctly," Odile said. He was wringing out his socks with the solemnity of a man performing surgery. "Everything behind us is worse."

Naji was already asleep, curled against a pack, sixteen years old and running on nothing. Yusuf draped a tarp over him without waking. Petra watched him do it and felt the fault line widen.

She checked her charges again. Four sticks. She counted them twice, then a third time, because counting was a prayer she still believed in. And while she counted, she thought about the satellite phone zipped into the inner pocket of her pack, and the messages she'd sent on it, and the number of a man in a city with glass towers who paid by the kilo of information.

Three days ago: *Expedition split at Cold Fork. Vey and Sandoval proceeding to inner chamber. Descent party holding cache route.*

Two days ago: *Cache route flooding. Descending via secondary path. Will confirm rendezvous.*

Yesterday, nothing. Yesterday she'd sat with her thumb over the send key for an hour while Naji slept ten feet away, and in the end she'd put the phone away unsent, and hated herself for both options equally.

The debt was two hundred and forty thousand. Her sister's clinic in Salalah, the equipment lease, the loan sharks her brother-in-law had borrowed from when the clinic nearly folded — all of it consolidated into one number that lived in Petra's head like a tenant who never slept. The funder's man — Haddad, smooth-voiced, patient as erosion — had offered to clear it. All of it. In exchange for coordinates, schedules, headcounts. Just words, he'd said. Words are cheap. You're not hurting anyone.

She'd believed him the way you believe anything when the alternative is watching your sister lose everything.

"Petra." Odile again, settling onto the stone beside her, close enough to share the fire's heat. Up close his face had the specific exhaustion of a man carrying his mother's ghost up a mountain. "You've been checking that bag every twenty minutes since Cold Fork. Either you're in love with your explosives or something's wrong."

"Both can be true."

"Petra."

She looked at the fire. She looked at Naji sleeping. She looked at the compass clipped inside Odile's jacket, its brass case catching the light, pointing — she knew without asking — at the boy across the camp. Whatever its last holder loved most. Marisol Sandoval's compass, honest as gravity, and here was Petra surrounded by people whose needle would have pointed somewhere true, and hers would have pointed at a bank balance.

"I sold Vey the blade," she said.

Odile went very still. "What blade?"

"The knife that cut the pages out of your mother's journal. I recognized the marks in chapter seven of your little reading club. I know the steel, the grind, the way it bites paper at an angle. I sold that exact model to Vey's expeditions three times. I put the tool in his hand." She heard her own voice go flat, professional, the voice she used before a blast. "That's the small sin. Here's the large one."

She unzipped the inner pocket and took out the satellite phone and set it between them on the stone, screen up, message thread open. Odile read. Petra watched his face do what faces do when the ground moves — nothing, at first, then everything.

"You've been reporting to Haddad."

"Since staging camp. Coordinates, schedules, the split at Cold Fork. Everything except—" Her hand shook then, properly, visibly, and she let it. "Except yesterday. Yesterday I couldn't send it. Not after the rockfall. Not after Yusuf refused to leave Naji. I sat with my thumb on that button and I couldn't."

The silence stretched. Across the fire, Yusuf had stopped moving. He'd heard. Of course he'd heard; the mountain gave you ears whether you wanted them or not. He rose slowly, and Petra braced for it — the fury, the rifle-butt justice of a guide whose grandmother's forbidden path was now compromised by her treachery.

Yusuf looked at the phone. Then at her. His face was terrible and quiet.

"How many died?" he asked. "At the sites you sold. How many people did Haddad's crews displace, or worse? How many villages?"

"I don't know."

"You didn't ask."

"No."

He nodded once, as if filing it away, and turned back to the fire, and somehow that was worse than shouting. Naji stirred, blinked awake, read the temperature of the camp the way teenagers read rooms — instantly, ruthlessly. "What happened?"

"Petra's been selling us," Odile said simply. "To the men who want the cave stripped and us erased."

Naji stared at her. And the worst part — the part Petra knew she'd carry longer than any debt — was that the kid didn't look angry. He looked like someone recalculating. Like someone learning, at sixteen, that the arithmetic of trust has hidden fees.

"So the ambush at the pass," Naji said. "The funder's men tracking us. That was—"

"That was me," Petra said. "Yes."

* * *

It was Odile who broke first, and not the way she expected. He didn't rage. He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and asked the question that mattered: "Do they know about this path?"

"They know the cache route flooded. They know we're descending. They don't know this path exists — it's not on any survey, it's in your grandmother's stories, Yusuf. If they knew about it they'd be sitting on it."

"But they're converging on the lower galleries," Yusuf said. "Which means when we come out of the mountain, we come out into them."

"Probably."

"Wonderful," Naji said. "I escaped an arranged marriage for this."

"You could have had the marriage," Petra said.

"The marriage didn't have views like this."

And that — that stupid, brave joke offered to the woman who'd sold them — cracked something loose in the whole camp. Yusuf swore in three languages. Odile put his head in his hands. And Petra sat in the middle of it, guilty and unforgiven and, absurdly, still included, because the storm outside was louder than their anger and anger is a luxury weather doesn't permit.

"We deal with Haddad's men when we're off the mountain," Odile said finally. "Right now the enemy is water and rock. Petra — how many charges?"

"Four."

"Then you're our rear guard. Anything comes down that path behind us, you make it not come down." He met her eyes. "I'm not forgiving you. I want to be clear about that. But my mother spent her last months documenting a theft, and I'll be damned if I finish her work by throwing away people I might need. This isn't absolution. It's logistics."

"Logistics," Petra repeated. "From a Sandoval. Your mother would be proud."

Something flickered across his face — grief, quickly boarded up. "She'd have forgiven you faster than I will. That was her flaw."

* * *

The rockslide came ninety minutes later, and it came without negotiation.

Petra heard it before anyone — a deep subsonic groan above the cleft, the sound of a mountain clearing its throat — and she was moving before her conscious mind caught up, because twenty years of explosives work builds reflexes deeper than thought. "UP THE PATH! NOW! MOVE!"

They moved. Yusuf hauled Naji up by the collar; Odile grabbed the packs; and Petra turned and ran toward the slide instead of away from it, because that was the job, that had always been the job, the idiot romance of it — you run at the explosion so other people can run from it.

The cleft above the shelf was letting go. Not all of it — a wedge, maybe sixty tons of fractured limestone, loosened by the monsoon's saturation, poised above the switchback where the path narrowed to the coffin-lid ledge. Where they'd just been standing. Where they'd have been standing in four minutes when the rain-weakened anchor finally failed.

She had four sticks. She needed, ideally, eight, and a week of surveying, and a helicopter.

She had maybe ninety seconds.

Her hands were shaking. Both of them now, the tremor climbing her forearms like floodwater, adrenaline feeding the thing that had ended her career. She knelt in the mud at the base of the cleft and her fingers wouldn't behave — the primer cap skated across the detonator housing, slipped, skated again.

*Breathe.* Old instructor's voice, dead ten years: *The charge doesn't care about your feelings, Petra. Steady hands or no hands.*

She pressed her left hand flat against the rock, cold and wet and solid, and used the stone itself to steady her. Wedged the right hand's tremor against her own knee. And worked. Primer seat. Det cord run — thirty meters of it, laid fast along the seam where the wedge met the mother rock, because she couldn't bring the whole cliff down but she could persuade the wedge to fall early, fall now, fall into the empty gully instead of onto the path an hour from now when they were on it.

"PETRA!" Odile, from above. "COME ON!"

"Ninety seconds!" she screamed back, which was a lie; it was going to take two minutes, and they all knew it, and nobody left, and that fact hit her harder than the slide ever could. They were waiting. These people she'd betrayed, these people whose coordinates she'd sold for a number, were standing in the rain waiting for the traitor to finish her work.

She seated the last cap. Her hands were useless now, full tremor, tears or rain running down her face, impossible to tell. She bit the firing device's safety clip open with her teeth like the old days, like Muscat, before Muscat.

"Fire in the hole!" she yelled, and thumbed it.

The world went white and sideways. The crack rolled down the valley and came back off the far walls twice, and the wedge — her wedge, her beautiful doomed sixty tons — shrugged loose from the mother rock and fell exactly where she'd promised it would, into the gully, missing the ledge by the width of a prayer. The dust cloud swallowed the shelf whole. Rocks rained past her, one the size of a skull cracking off the stone beside her hip, and then the mountain exhaled, and it was done.

Silence, except the rain.

Petra stayed kneeling in the mud. Her hands lay in her lap, trembling, empty. Four sticks to zero. Whatever came down this path now — funder's men, weather, God — would arrive unopposed. She'd spent everything. It occurred to her, distantly, that she'd been spending everything her whole life, and that the difference tonight was that for once she'd spent it on the right side of the ledger.

Boots in the mud. Odile crouched in front of her, gray with dust, alive. Behind him Yusuf, Naji, the fire guttering, the path clear.

"You waited," she said. Her voice came out wrecked. "You absolute idiots. You waited."

"Yeah," Odile said. "We do that now. Apparently." He looked at her hands, still shaking, and didn't look away, which was its own strange mercy. "Is that all of them? The charges?"

"All of them. I'm done. I'm cargo now."

"You were never cargo," Naji said, appearing at Odile's shoulder, mud-faced, seventeen-going-on-eternal. "You're the reason the mountain didn't eat us."

"The mountain can still eat us," Yusuf said, pointing down-path into the rain. "Look."

Petra followed his finger. Far below, through the torn veil of the storm, lights. Three of them, maybe four, moving up the flooded cache route with the disciplined spacing of professionals. Headlamps. Haddad's men, hours ahead of any estimate, closing on the mouth of the forbidden path.

And behind them, faint but unmistakable over the rain, a sound Petra knew from a hundred blasted quarries: the whine of a portable drill, and beneath it, the flat percussive knock of someone else's charges being set.

"They're not following us," she said quietly. "They're sealing the exits."

Odile stood, and pulled the compass from his jacket, and held it up against the dark. Even in the rain-light, Petra could see the needle straining — not toward the cave below, not toward the sea beyond it, but down-path, toward the lights, quivering like a living thing.

"It's pointing at them," he said. "Why is it pointing at them?"

Petra looked at the needle, and at the lights, and felt the answer arrive cold as groundwater. "Because someone down there loved your mother," she said. "Or someone down there is carrying something she loved."
