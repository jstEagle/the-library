## 17. The Funder's Men

**Odile**

The descent from the inner chamber should have been a celebration.

Odile felt it in his hands first — the way they wouldn't stop shaking, not from fear but from the sheer weight of what he carried. Marisol's journal, complete now, the torn pages recovered from the resin-sealed cache she'd built into the chamber wall like a woman burying a time capsule addressed to no one and everyone. The murals' photographs on his phone, three hundred of them, each frame a nail in the coffin of the lie that had eaten his childhood whole. His mother had told the truth. She had told the truth and died for it, and the proof was riding against his ribs in a waterproof roll like a second heartbeat.

Vey climbed ahead of him through the narrow gallery, his headlamp beam jittering off wet stone. The man moved badly — favoring his left knee, breathing in short controlled pulls — but he moved with purpose, checking anchors, testing holds, calling back the depth of each drop before Odile committed to it. Twenty years of expeditions lived in those hands. Twenty years, and Odile was only now beginning to understand how much of that competence had been built on the foundation of one betrayal.

"Rope's good," Vey said, holding the line taut. "Watch the lip on your left. It shears."

Odile swung over the edge and rappelled down into the second gallery, the one they'd named the Gallery of Ships because the murals there showed vessels with sails like crescent moons riding swells carved so precisely you could almost hear them. His boots hit the ledge. Water ran somewhere below, a black whisper in the dark.

"How far to the junction?" he asked.

"Two hours if we're careful. Three if we're alive." Vey dropped down beside him, unclipping, coiling. He paused, headlamp angled at the mural wall, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. In the light, a painted figure stood at a ship's prow, arms raised — greeting or warning, the artists hadn't bothered to say.

"She saw this," Vey said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"She documented all of it." Odile patted the roll at his chest. "Pigment analysis, stratigraphy, radiocarbon samples she processed herself when the funding dried up. She knew exactly what she'd found. And she knew someone would come to take it."

Vey's jaw worked. "I keep telling myself I didn't know they'd go as far as they did."

"You keep telling yourself that." Odile clipped back onto the rope. "Tell me something true instead. How long have they known we were here?"

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Odile had spent two weeks learning to read this crew's silences — Yusuf's meant *I'm calculating*, Naji's meant *I'm afraid to say it*, Petra's meant *I've already said too much*. Vey's silence, he realized now, meant *I've known for days and hoped it would matter less*.

"Petra's reports," Odile said. "That's how."

"Satellite check-ins every forty-eight hours. Encrypted channel. I thought—" Vey stopped himself, pressed his palm flat against the cold rock as if steadying himself against it. "I thought if we moved fast enough it wouldn't matter. That we'd be gone before anyone decided we were worth the trouble."

"And the chamber contents are worth the trouble."

"The chamber contents are worth more than either of us will earn in a lifetime." Vey turned, and in the flat white light of the headlamps his face looked older than it had at the staging camp, older than it had in any photograph Odile had studied with hatred. "We need to move. Now. Not at the junction — past it, down the flood route, even if it's running. If they're already in the range—"

A sound came up the gallery behind them.

Not water. Not rockfall. Metal, kissing metal — a carabiner clipping onto a line that wasn't theirs.

Both of them killed their headlamps at the same instant, and the dark came down like a lid.

* * *

**Vey**

In the black, Cassian Vey did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for six days.

Three hours to the junction. Below the junction, the flood galleries — running water, maybe waist-deep by now with the early monsoon feeding the aquifer, passable but slow, and slow was a word that belonged to other people's funerals. Above, the descent route Yusuf had cached: four hours to the fork, then the forbidden path, then open ground. But above was also where the sound had come from. Whoever had just entered the upper gallery had come down the descent route. Which meant the descent route was compromised, which meant Yusuf and the others—

He shut that thought in a box and sat on it. Later. Everything could be dealt with later except the next ninety seconds.

"Odile," he breathed, barely voicing it. "Count with me. On my hand."

He found the younger man's wrist in the dark — thin, corded, trembling with adrenaline — and pressed out a rhythm against the pulse point. One. Two. Three. A cadence Odile would recognize from a week of ropework: *move on three, freeze on my grip.*

Above them, lights bloomed. Not headlamps — proper expedition floods, wide-angled, expensive, the kind of gear that made Vey's own equipment look like a student field kit. Four beams, moving with the unhurried discipline of people who were being paid by the hour and had been told there was no rush. Voices drifted down, echoing off stone, flattened by distance into something almost gentle.

"—chamber's confirmed. Drone footage matches the survey. Boss wants the panels cut in sections, crated tonight—"

"—the kid? And Vey?"

"Orders are orders."

Vey closed his eyes. *Orders are orders.* He'd used those exact words once, eleven years ago, in a deposition room with a lawyer's hand on his shoulder and a funder's money in his account, explaining why he'd testified that Marisol Sandoval fabricated her dates. He'd slept fine afterward. That was the part he'd never confessed to anyone, the part that shamed him more than the act itself: how easily he'd slept.

His hand tightened on Odile's wrist. *Move.*

They went, blind, by touch and memory — thirty meters along the ledge to where the gallery narrowed, then down through the squeeze Vey had cursed on the way up, shoulders scraping, the journal roll between them passed hand to hand like something newborn. Behind them the floodlights swept the Gallery of Ships, and Vey heard one of the men give a low whistle at the murals, the whistle of an appraiser, and something in his chest turned over and began, finally, to burn.

He had spent eleven years telling himself a story: that he'd reported Marisol to protect the integrity of the record, then protected Odile from her mother's debts, then joined this expedition to salvage whatever could be salvaged of her reputation. Each version had enough truth in it to survive a sleepless night. But crouched in a squeeze of limestone with a paid salvage team cutting his escape route off above him, Vey understood the story had always been simpler and worse. He had traded her because trading her was easy, and he had spent every year since buying his way back toward her with other people's money, and none of it — not the funding, not the testimony he'd drafted and redrafted, not this expedition — none of it had ever been about her work.

It had been about standing in front of her son without flinching.

They reached the junction chamber, a vaulted room where four passages met around a natural pillar carved with spirals. Vey clicked his lamp on for half a second, shielding it with his hand. The lower passage — the flood route — gushed audibly in the dark, higher than yesterday. The eastern passage dead-ended at a collapse. The western passage climbed toward the descent route and the men with the floodlights.

And the fourth passage, south, barely a crack in the wall, was the one Marisol's own notes had marked with a single word in her cramped handwriting: *if cornered.*

She had known. Eleven years ago, she had stood in this chamber and mapped herself an escape, and written it down for whoever came after her, and Vey had helped make sure that whoever came after her would be her son.

"There," he whispered, and pointed.

* * *

**Odile**

The crack was Marisol-sized, which was to say it was a cruelty. Odile went in sideways, exhaling everything, dragging the journal roll ahead of him through a slot of stone that scraped his jacket to ribbons and his ribs nearly to the bone. Behind him Vey grunted, wedged, swore with a fluency that would have shocked the academic committees, and followed.

The crack opened after twenty meters into a low crawl that sloped downward, air moving through it in cold threads — moving, which meant it went somewhere. Odile crawled. His mind crawled faster.

*Orders are orders.* He kept hearing it. He kept seeing the men's shadows on the gallery wall, four of them, maybe five, professional and patient. They hadn't rushed. Rushing was for people who expected resistance. These men had been told there would be none, or that whatever resistance existed could be handled the way you handled weather — waited out, worked around, noted in the report.

They had asked about him. *The kid? And Vey?* And the answer had been *orders are orders,* which was not an answer at all.

His fingers found the compass in his pocket without deciding to. He drew it out, cupped it against his chest, and tilted the lid just enough to read the needle by feel of its swing. In the total dark it didn't matter; he couldn't see the card. But he held it anyway, the way you hold a hand in a crowd, and the needle pressed against its pivot toward the southeast — toward the lower mountain, toward the fork, toward Yusuf and Naji and Petra, wherever they were in this drowned stone labyrinth.

Toward the people he loved most. Toward home, if home was a direction and not a wound.

"Vey," he whispered. "If they take us — if it goes wrong — the journal goes with me. Into the water, into the fire, whatever it takes. You understand? It doesn't get sold. It doesn't get traded. Not for our lives."

Ahead of him, in the dark, Vey was quiet for three full breaths.

"I know," Vey said. "That's why they'll kill us. And it's why I'm done letting them."

There was something new in the man's voice. Odile had catalogued Vey's tones over these weeks — the chairman's warmth, the veteran's briskness, the confessor's careful sorrow. This was none of them. This was flat and hard and quiet, a voice with nothing left to protect.

The crawl ended at a shelf overlooking the upper flood gallery, and Odile looked down into running black water and saw, fifty meters below and around a bend of stone, a faint gray suggestion of daylight. An exit. The storm-light, dimmed by rain, leaking in through some fissure in the mountainside.

Also below them, between the shelf and the bend: lantern light. Two men, stationed at the gallery's mouth, guarding the exit. Of course. You didn't run an operation like this without covering the doors.

Vey eased up beside him. For a moment they lay side by side on the stone like brothers, watching the guards, and Odile thought absurdly of camping trips that had never happened, of a childhood in which this man had been Uncle Cassian, and hated himself for the thought, and kept it anyway.

"Two guards," Vey murmured. "Rest are in the chamber, cutting panels. They'll want the heavy crates moved before the water rises further, which means the guards stay put until the work party comes down." He checked his watch by feel. "Which means we have maybe ten minutes before this gallery is a loading dock."

"What's the play?"

Vey was silent. Then, in the darkness, he began unbuckling his pack.

"I'm going to walk down there," he said, "with my hands visible and my credentials in my pocket, and I'm going to tell them I'm the one who sold them this site. Eleven years ago. I'm going to tell them I can deliver the journal and the boy quietly, no mess, no bodies, provided my cut is renegotiated." He pulled out the flare gun from the emergency kit — three cartridges, the ones Yusuf had insisted on packing against regulation — and pressed it into Odile's hands. "And while they're deciding whether to believe me, you're going to go over that shelf, into the water, around the bend, and out. You're going to find Yusuf's crew, and you're going to bring them back down the forbidden path with every charge Petra has left, and you're going to seal this cave with those men inside it if that's what it takes."

"That's insane. They'll shoot you the moment you—"

"They won't. Not immediately." Vey's smile in the dark was terrible and calm. "I'm worth more talking. That has always been the problem with me, Odile. I've never once been worth more than what I say." He took a breath. "Your mother testified to the truth and lost everything. I testified to a lie and gained everything. Consider this the exchange rate correcting itself."

"Vey—"

"Cassian." He said it softly. "You might as well. If I'm going to die in your mother's cave, I'd rather die having been on a first-name basis with her son."

Odile grabbed his sleeve. For one suspended second, in the cold breath of the mountain, everything he wanted to say — *you destroyed us, you coward, you're doing this for yourself, don't you dare die down here, my mother loved you, did she love you, tell me* — crowded his throat at once.

What came out was: "The compass points at you. Did you know that? It pointed at you the day we met. Whatever you loved most — it's real. Whatever else you are, that's real."

Vey went very still. When he spoke, his voice had cracked open somewhere inside. "It was her," he said. "For thirty years, it was her. Go."

Then he stood up out of the dark, raised both hands high, and walked down into the lantern light, saying in a clear carrying voice, "Gentlemen. We should talk about the terms of my surrender — and about how much the man who built this deal is actually worth."

Below, the guards spun, weapons rising. Odile rolled off the shelf into the freezing water.

* * *

**Vey**

They put him on his knees at the gallery mouth with his hands laced behind his head, and the lead man — a broad, patient-faced type with a Slavic accent and mud to the knees — crouched in front of him and studied him like a specimen.

"Cassian Vey," the man said. "My employer speaks of you. Not fondly."

"My employer speaks of me the same way," Vey said. "That's what made us rich."

The man almost smiled. Behind him, the second guard kept his weapon level at Vey's chest with the bored precision of a man who had done this many times in places with worse lighting. Upstream, faintly, came the grind of generators and the ring of chisels — the chamber being dismantled panel by panel, eleven thousand years of human memory going into foam-lined crates.

"Where is the journal?" the lead man asked.

"In the cave. Where it's always been. Along with something else your employer doesn't know about." Vey let the pause stretch. Let them lean in. He had spent a career making funders lean in. "Marisol documented the sale negotiations. Names, dates, transfer agreements — proof that the site was being stripped illegally under heritage-law exemptions your lawyers forged. It's all in her final pages. If anything happens to me or the boy, those pages reach three ministries and two newspapers within the week. Dead-man arrangements. Her idea, actually. She was always better at this than I was."

The lead man's expression didn't change, but his weight shifted back half an inch, and Vey, who had read a thousand faces across negotiation tables, read the shift perfectly: *recalculate.*

"So here are my terms," Vey continued, and his voice did not shake, and he marveled at that, at the machinery of composure still running on its old rails even as the rest of him stood in the wreckage. "The boy walks out of this mountain untouched. The crew walks out. Your men repack their crates empty. And in exchange, I give you the location of the secondary cache — the duplicate documentation — and my silence, permanently, which you'll find is worth more than my death, because my death makes him a martyr and my silence makes him a partner."

"Lies," said the second guard, mildly.

"Probably," agreed the lead man, and drew a pistol from his hip with the unhurried motion of a man checking his watch.

And Cassian Vey, kneeling in the flooded gallery of the cave his ambition had condemned and his confession had failed to save, looked past the muzzle, past the lanterns, up into the black throat of the tunnel where a boy was escaping with his mother's truth against his ribs — and found, to his genuine surprise, that he was not afraid.

*Marisol,* he thought. *I'm coming to the end of the ledger. I hope the math embarrasses me.*

"Last chance to hear terms," he said aloud.

"No," said the lead man, and cocked the hammer.

From deep in the mountain — muffled, distant, impossible — came a rolling double-crack of detonation, and the floor of the Empty Quarter shrugged.
