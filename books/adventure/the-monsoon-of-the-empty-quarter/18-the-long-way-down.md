## 18. The Long Way Down

The water came for us before the light did.

We were eighty meters below the inner chamber when the sound reached us — not rain, not yet, but a deep arterial pulse through the rock, like the mountain had a heartbeat and it had just started racing. Vey stopped on the gallery floor, his lamp swinging, and I watched his face do something I'd never seen it do in all the weeks since the dock: it went honest. Pure animal honesty. The face of a man who understood, in his marrow, that the schedule was dead.

"The galleries flood from the bottom up," he said. "Marisol's notes said—"

"I know what her notes said." Her notes said the ancient builders had engineered drainage, that the lowest chambers emptied on a tide cycle, that the whole cave was a machine for surviving water. Her notes had also said she trusted no one's schedule but the mountain's. I'd read that line a dozen times in the waterlogged pages and thought it was archaeology. It was a warning.

The first water found us as a thin sheet across the floor, ankle-deep and shockingly cold, moving with purpose. Not pooling. Running. Toward the outer galleries, toward the descent, toward us.

"The murals," I said. "The escape route. The second chamber — the one with the procession."

Vey's lamp snapped toward me. "You want to follow a painting."

"I want to follow the people who painted it. They lived here, Cassian. They watched this same monsoon come down this same range, and they didn't die. They built themselves a way out." I was already moving, splashing through the rising sheet, the compass in my fist. The needle didn't waver. It held steady on a bearing that led deeper — down and east, away from the route we'd come by, toward a part of the cave I'd only seen in pigment and soot.

He followed. Of course he followed. Whatever else Cassian Vey was — bought, broken, half a traitor and half a penitent — he was not a man who wanted to drown alone.

* * *

The procession chamber was exactly as I remembered it from the first descent: a long vault of painted figures, hundreds of them, walking in lines along the walls. I'd catalogued them in my head as ritual. Devotional. The kind of thing you write a paper about.

Standing in the chamber now, water to our knees, I saw it differently.

They weren't worshipping anything. They were *leaving*. The figures walked in ordered files — elders carried, children bound to chests, animals led by ropes — and the lines of them all converged, generation after generation of painted people, toward a single point on the eastern wall where the artists had ground away the rock face itself. Behind the figures, painted in the same ochre, the artists had left a record of why: black clouds, a mountain weeping white lines, water rendered in a style so old it predated the script in the star chamber. A storm. *This* storm. The same monsoon, arriving on the same range, centuries or millennia ago, and the painted people had answered it by walking.

"They faced it," Vey said quietly. His lamp light trembled across the black clouds. "They stood where we're standing and they painted their own escape before they needed it."

"Marisol understood that." My throat was tight. "That's what the funder never got. You can't strip a site like this and sell it in pieces. It isn't a collection. It's an *instruction*."

The eastern wall's single point was a fissure, half-choked with flowstone, and it was exactly compass-width wide. I pressed the brass casing into the gap the way I'd pressed it to the outer door, half expecting nothing, and the mountain answered with a sound like a held breath releasing — stone shifting somewhere in the dark, old mechanisms doing old work, and the fissure widened by a hand's width, then two. Cold air moved through it. Moving air meant an opening. An opening meant a way.

"Odile." Vey had gone still, his lamp aimed down the gallery we'd come from. The water there had climbed past our knees in the minutes we'd stood reading the walls. And in the lamplight, far back the way we'd entered, something caught the beam and threw it back — a pale, tumbling gleam. Not water.

The resin canisters from the inner chamber. The funder's men had marked their extraction points, and the flood was lifting their gear and sending it ahead of us like a vanguard. Which meant the flood had reached the chamber above. Which meant the funder's men were either running too, or they weren't running at all.

I didn't say it out loud. Some knowledge you carry in your body instead.

"Through," I said, and we went.

* * *

The passage beyond the fissure was nothing like the engineered galleries. It was raw, narrow, the builders' work giving way to the mountain's own architecture — and then, forty meters in, it changed again, and I understood that the ancient people had done their finest work in the places no one would ever think to look. Steps cut into the living rock, steep and shallow-risen, made for climbing with a child on your back. Handholds worn smooth by generations of gripping palms. At intervals, soot stains on the ceiling where lamps had hung, and beside them, carved small and low — at a child's eye height, I realized, so a child could read them — marks. Arrows. Waves. A figure standing upright in water, unafraid.

An instruction manual, written for the smallest and most frightened people in the tribe. *Follow the marks. The water rises. Walk anyway.*

"Odile." Vey's voice came from behind me, and it was wrong — flattened, careful. "How fast can you climb?"

I looked back. Below him, in the passage's throat, the water was no longer a sheet. It was a current, waist-deep and accelerating, carrying grit and a dead lamp and something that might have been a boot. The mountain had stopped breathing and started swallowing.

We climbed.

I won't pretend I remember it as sequence. I remember it as fragments: the compass lashed to my wrist with a bootlace, its needle holding true through every turn so I never had to think, only move. Vey's hand locking around my ankle when I slipped on a flowstone lip, hauling me back with a strength I'd have denied him existed. The water rising past our knees, past our waists as we climbed, so that we were swimming the last stretches, kicking against a current that wanted us back the way we'd come, back down into the drowned galleries, back into the mountain's stomach. The carved marks appearing in my lamplight again and again — the upright figure, the wave, the arrow — each one a small painted hand on my shoulder saying *this way, this way, they made it, you can too.*

And somewhere in the middle of it, in the worst of it — chest-deep in a flooded bend, my lamp dead, Vey's guttering, the compass the only fixed point in a world of black moving water — the terror turned. I can't describe it any other way. It turned like a key.

Because the marks kept coming. Because the builders had cut these steps for exactly this night, and their children had climbed them, and their children's children, and every one of them had lived long enough to come back and cut the next set. The cave wasn't trying to kill us. The cave was a ship, and we were in its oldest corridor, and the people who built it had left the lights on for us. My mother had read this mountain the way I'd never learned to read anything, and she had trusted it, and she had been right to trust it, and for one suspended moment, chest-deep in black water with a dead lamp, I loved her with a completeness I'd never managed while she lived.

The needle held. I climbed.

* * *

We came out gasping onto a slope of broken scree under a sky the color of a bruise, and the rain hit us like a decision. Not the coastal drizzle we'd been outrunning for days — this was the monsoon proper, vertical and total, a wall of water falling out of a sky that had no top to it. The range behind us had vanished. Not darkened. *Vanished*, erased behind rain, as if the mountain had been a rumor all along.

Vey collapsed onto his knees in the runoff and laughed, or wept, or both; at that distance, in that noise, they sound the same. I stood with the compass in my fist and the rain hammering my shoulders and did the only inventory that mattered: alive, alive, out.

Then the compass moved.

I'd been holding it the way you hold a thing you've stopped thinking about — and the needle, which had held true through every gallery and every flood-bend, swung. Not wildly. *Deliberately.* It came around through the rain and the dark and it locked, steady as a nail driven home, on a point downslope where the scree gave way to a black fold of land. And out of that fold, thin as a thread under the monsoon's roar, came a sound I would have known anywhere, in any storm, at the bottom of any sea.

Naji's voice. Calling a name.

Calling *mine*.

I was moving before I decided to move, down the scree with the water tearing at my legs, the needle locked on him the whole way — and I understood, with a clarity that had nothing to do with the flood or the cave or the men who'd followed us into it, exactly what my mother's compass had been trying to tell me from the beginning. It had never pointed at treasure. It had never pointed at truth. It pointed at the thing you would walk through a drowning mountain to reach, and mine was sixteen years old and standing in the rain with a lamp, and behind his lamp, as the fold opened up, other lights — Yusuf's broad silhouette braced against the wind, a second lamp that could only be Petra's, and between them the dark mouth of a path I knew from Yusuf's stories, the forbidden one, the one his grandmother had named and the carvings had marked.

The ancient escape route didn't end in the cave. It ended *here* — in the hands of the people who'd never forgotten it.

Naji caught me at the path's mouth, both of us nearly going down in the runoff, his hands gripping my jacket like he was checking I was real. His mouth was moving; it took me a moment to hear him over the storm.

"The cache route's gone — flooded out an hour ago, we watched it go. Yusuf says the only way off the range is the old path, the one the carvings show. But Odile—" His grip tightened, and his face, lit from below by his own lamp, went grim in a way no sixteen-year-old's face should have to be. "There's something you need to know before we take it. Petra's been talking to someone on the radio. She's been talking to them since base camp."

Behind him, Vey came limping down the scree into the lamplight, bleeding, half-drowned, and alive — and Petra turned her lamp toward us, and I saw her see the compass in my hand, and I saw her understand that whatever she'd sold and whoever she'd sold it to, the bill had just arrived on this mountain, in this storm, and there was no cache route left to run it down.

The rain fell. The old path waited, black and narrow, climbing into the dark.

Yusuf put a hand on my shoulder. "Whatever happens," he said, "no one goes down this path alone."
