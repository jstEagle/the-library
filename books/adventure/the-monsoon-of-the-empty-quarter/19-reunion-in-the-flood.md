## 19. Reunion in the Flood

**Yusuf**

The rain came down the mountain like something with intent.

Yusuf stood at the mouth of his grandmother's path — a slot of black rock between two leaning slabs, invisible unless you had been told stories about it since before you could walk — and watched the range vanish behind a curtain of water. Not fog. Not mist. Water, falling so hard and so continuous that the peaks simply stopped existing. The world contracted to the width of the path and the sound of it, which was not a sound so much as a pressure, a hand pressed flat against the whole mountain.

Behind him, Petra leaned against the slab, gray-faced, her hands wrapped in strips of shirt to stop them shaking where he could see. Naji crouched at the path's lip, watching the flood-churned wadi below.

"They're coming," Naji said. "Listen."

Yusuf listened. Under the rain, under the roar of the wadi now running brown and violent thirty meters below, there was another sound — voices, thin and ragged, carried up the gorge.

"Odile," Naji said, and started forward, and Yusuf caught him by the collar.

"Not yet. Look first." He pointed down the slope, where the rain thinned enough to show shapes: two figures descending the goat track at a speed that should have killed them, and behind them, further up, three more figures moving with the unhurried economy of men who were not running because they did not need to run. The funder's salvage team. He'd seen their type before, on other mountains, for other men. They moved like people who had already decided how this ended.

"Petra," Yusuf said quietly. "How many charges left?"

She didn't answer at once. When she did, her voice was flat as slate. "Two. Maybe. If I trust my hands, which I don't."

"Then we don't fight them on the slope." He pulled Naji back into the shadow of the slabs. "We let them come to us. All of them. And we decide what happens next standing together."

Naji looked up at him, rain streaming off his chin, sixteen years old and trying very hard to look like someone who had already decided things. "And if we can't?"

Yusuf thought of his grandmother's stories. The forbidden path was forbidden not because it was dangerous — though it was — but because the old people believed the mountain kept accounts, and you did not come to it asking for rescue twice.

"Then we decide anyway," he said. "Together. That's the part that matters."

* * *

**Odile**

The last hundred meters of descent Odile would remember afterward only in fragments: Vey's hand locked around his wrist hauling him off a ledge that wasn't there; the compass in his pocket burning cold against his hip; the murals' painted escape route matching the actual stone turn by turn, as if the builders had left him instructions across four thousand years. *This way. We survived this way.*

Then the slabs loomed out of the rain, and there were figures in the gap, and one of them was shouting his name in a voice cracked raw — Naji's voice — and then hands were pulling him into the dry dark of the path's mouth, and it was over, or the running part of it was.

He sat down hard on wet stone. Naji dropped beside him, gripping his arm hard enough to bruise, saying nothing, which from Naji was a speech. Petra pressed a canteen into his hands. Yusuf stood at the gap, watching the slope.

"Vey?" Yusuf asked.

Odile turned. Cassian Vey stood just inside the entrance, dripping, one arm hanging wrong — dislocated or broken in the scramble down — his face the color of the wet limestone. He looked older than he had a week ago. He looked like a man who had set something down and did not know yet whether it had shattered.

"The funder's men," Odile said. "Three of them. Behind us. They'll be here in minutes."

"I know," Yusuf said. "I saw."

"We have the documentation," Odile went on, and heard his own voice go strange and formal, the negotiator's register he'd learned from creditors. "All of it. Marisol's journal, restored pages, photographs of the chamber. It's the only copy. That's our leverage."

"It's also why they can't let us leave," Petra said. She said it without drama, the way she reported charge counts. "Dead men don't publish."

The rain filled the silence. Odile looked around at them — Naji white-knuckled and refusing to show it, Petra wrapping and unwrapping her hands, Yusuf motionless at the gap like something the mountain had grown — and felt the truth of the last nine days settle into him with the weight of ballast. Somewhere between the staging camp and this hole in the rock, these people had stopped being a payroll. He did not know when. He only knew that the thought of any of them dying on this slope was unbearable in a way his mother's death had never quite managed to be, because her death had been a wound and this would be a failure — his failure, chosen, avoidable.

Vey spoke from the shadows. "There's a way through this."

Everyone turned.

* * *

**Cassian Vey**

Cassian had rehearsed nothing. That was the strange thing. For eleven years he had rehearsed every version of this conversation — the confession, the justification, the elegant self-portrait in which circumstance rather than character was the villain — and now, standing half-crippled in a cave mouth while armed men climbed toward them, he found all of it useless, all of it ash, and what remained was simply the truth, unarranged.

"Marisol's dating was honest," he said. "The cave is what she said it was. Her chronology holds. I knew it held when I testified, and I testified anyway, because Aldous Renner paid me, and because I told myself I was protecting you, Odile — that if Marisol fell quietly, you'd inherit less ruin. That was a lie I told myself so I could sleep. I tore the final pages from her journal because they proved everything, and I gave them to no one. I kept them. I told myself I'd return them someday. Eleven years." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Eleven years, and a boy with a compass had to dig them out of a wall."

Nobody moved. Rain hammered the slabs.

"So here is what I'm worth," Cassian went on. "Renner's men want the documentation and no witnesses. I am a witness they particularly want — I know where the private buyers are, I know what Renner promised whom. If I walk out that gap alone, carrying the journal, they will follow me and only me. They'll believe it. A man like me running with the prize is exactly the story they've already written." He lifted his good arm, the gesture almost jaunty, and it cost him visibly. "You take the lower branch of the path — Yusuf, your grandmother's route, the one the carvings mark. It comes out at the shepherd village by morning. By the time anyone reports anything, you're over the col and the site is documented, published, protected. My name on the testimony, hers on the science. That was always the trade."

"You can't even hold a rope," Petra said. "Your arm—"

"I can hold a journal and run downhill. It's the one athletic event I've trained for my entire life."

It was meant as a joke. It landed as a eulogy, and he seemed to understand that, and went on anyway, because he had decided, and Cassian Vey's decisions, once finally made, were load-bearing.

"Naji," he said, gentler, "you get married or you don't, but you do it alive. Petra — your debts die with Renner's exposure, not with you. Yusuf — your grandmother's path stays secret, because no one follows you far enough to learn it." He turned last to Odile, and whatever he had prepared for that look, it failed him, and what showed underneath was just grief, plain and late. "Publish everything. Even the parts about me. Especially those."

He picked up the journal case.

Yusuf stepped into the gap and blocked it.

* * *

**Petra**

Petra had spent her adult life around men who talked about sacrifice the way other men talked about sports — abstractly, competitively, with no intention of playing. She knew the posture. She'd expected it from Vey since the day she'd sold him the blade, maybe before; men who betrayed people were always rehearsing grand redemptions to balance the books.

But Yusuf — Yusuf she had misread entirely, and the correction arrived like a charge going off.

"No one is left behind on this mountain," Yusuf said. He said it without raising his voice, which somehow made it worse, made it geological. "Not the traitor. Not the boy. Not the woman with shaking hands. Nobody. You want to give yourself to those men, fine — but you'll do it with us standing behind you, and they will not accept that bargain, and then we are four witnesses instead of five and you've bought nothing."

"He's right," Petra heard herself say, and was surprised. "Vey. Listen to him. Renner's lead man — Halvard — I've worked for his crews. He doesn't take trades. He takes everything in front of him and burns the rest. Your distraction gets you killed and delays them forty minutes."

"Forty minutes is enough," Vey started.

"For what? For us to descend a flooded gorge in monsoon rain?" She held up her wrapped hands. "Look at us. Count us. One guide with a forged license, one blaster with palsy, one stowaway child, one heir with a grudge, and you. We are not a sacrifice waiting for a volunteer. We are a mess. And messes survive by staying messy — all of us, together, being too many problems to solve at once."

Something shifted in Vey's face. Hope, maybe, arriving like pain.

"There's another way," Odile said slowly. Petra turned. The boy — no, not a boy anymore, not since the inner chamber — had the compass out, its brass case streaked with mud, the needle inside swinging and settling, swinging and settling. "Halvard wants the documentation. Fine. But what he actually needs is for none of it to exist publicly. What if it exists publicly *before* he catches us? Petra — can you get a signal from here? Any signal?"

Petra blinked. Then she was already moving, digging the satellite phone from its waterproof sleeve, holding it up toward the gap where the sky might conceivably reach. One bar flickered. Died. Flickered.

"Burst transmission," she said. "If I can hold a bar for ninety seconds, I can push the scans to three addresses — a journal editor in Sana'a, an antiquities board, and a lawyer who owes me. After that, killing us protects nothing. Killing us becomes the story."

"And if the bar dies at second eighty-nine?" Naji asked.

"Then we're back to running," Petra said. "So somebody pray, or whatever it is you people do instead."

* * *

**Naji**

Naji had never prayed properly in his life. His family had arranged a marriage for him, which suggested they believed in God, but the belief had never been passed down along with the dowry negotiations. So instead of praying he did the thing he did instead of most things: he watched.

He watched Petra stand in the rain at the gap's edge, phone raised above her head like an offering, cursing steadily under her breath with a fluency he intended to memorize. He watched Yusuf position the others — Vey against the slab, Odile at the path mouth with the compass open in his palm — with small economical gestures, a shepherd arranging a flock he refused to call his own. He watched the slope below, where three dark figures had stopped moving.

They had stopped because they could see the phone. Naji understood it a full second before anyone said it: Halvard's men couldn't advance. Whatever happened next, they needed it to happen away from a transmitting antenna.

"They see it," Naji said. "They're waiting. They think we're calling for extraction — they'll wait for the call to end, then close."

"Then the call must not end," Yusuf said.

"It doesn't have to be real," Odile said. His eyes had gone bright in the way Naji had learned to distrust and love in equal measure. "Petra — send the burst. Real data, real addresses, do it fast. But keep the connection alive after. Keep talking. Read them the shipping manifest, read them poetry, read them your grocery list. As long as the phone shows active, they sit on that slope in the rain."

"And when the battery dies?" Vey asked.

"By then," Odile said, "the documents exist in three inboxes on three continents, and Halvard has a choice between retreating and becoming famous." He looked around at all of them, rain sheeting off his jaw, and Naji saw him arrive at the sentence the way you arrive at a ledge edge — knowing the drop, jumping anyway. "We walk out of here together. All five. Down the forbidden path, past the carvings, to the village. Let Halvard explain to his employer why five witnesses reached civilization with the story already filed."

Silence, except the rain.

It was Vey who broke it, laughing again — that same broken sound, but warmer now, thawing at the edges. "Marisol would have hated this plan. Too many variables." He paused. "She would also have been the first one moving."

"She'd have gone first," Odile said quietly. "Down the path. To make sure it held."

"Then we honor her badly," Yusuf said, "by going together."

* * *

**Odile**

The burst took eleven seconds. Petra fed the phone a stream of nonsense afterward — readings from a gauge she invented on the spot, delivered in the dead monotone of a field report — and the bar held, and held, and the three figures on the slope stayed exactly where they were, patient as stones, and the expedition became, for twenty minutes, a strange little congregation huddled in a cave mouth while a woman swore at a satellite in four languages.

When the battery finally gave out, nobody panicked. The documents were out. The needle, Odile noticed, had settled in his palm — steady, certain, pointing not north but at the four people crowded around him, swinging faintly from one to another as if unable to choose, as if refusing to choose.

He closed the compass and put it away.

"Path's flooded to the knee for the first stretch," Yusuf said, shouldering his pack. "After that, my grandmother's stories say it climbs. Cold, narrow, and it comes out above the village by dawn." He looked at each of them in turn, and his voice did something Odile had never heard it do — softened, just slightly, like ground giving underfoot. "My grandmother said the mountain keeps accounts. So we go up owing it nothing, and we pay attention to everything it tells us. Move when I move. Touch nothing carved. And nobody—" the ghost of a smile, "—nobody is left behind."

They went into the dark of the forbidden path single file, five heartbeats in the mountain's throat, while behind them the range drowned and the men on the slope began, at last, to climb.

Two hours in, by the light of a single lamp, the passage opened into a gallery the flood had never reached — and the walls were covered in carvings, hundreds of them, spiraling, and among them, fresh enough to still hold chisel-bright edges, a line of marks that no prehistoric hand had made.

Naji's lamp found them first. Five symbols. Recent. Deliberate.

Someone had walked this path before them — recently — and left word.
