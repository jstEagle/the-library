## 20. The Bargain at the Pass

The storm came down the col like something being poured.

Odile felt it before he saw it — a pressure change that made his ears pop, the taste of iron in the back of his throat, and then the first fat drops hitting the scree with an audible slap, as if the mountain itself were being struck. Behind them, three hundred meters down the switchbacks, the funder's men had stopped climbing. They didn't need to hurry anymore. The weather was doing their work for them.

"They'll wait for it to pin us," Vey said. He was leaning against a boulder, one hand pressed to his ribs where a ricocheted stone had caught him on the descent from the inner chamber. His face was gray under the tan. "Then they'll come up and take what's left of us."

"Then we don't get pinned." Odile scanned the pass. It wasn't really a pass — not anymore, not in this weather. It was a saddle of broken rock between two shoulders of black stone, maybe forty meters across, with the descent route falling away to the south and nothing but cloud to the north. There was a shallow overhang where the shepherds' goats used to shelter, according to Yusuf, and beneath it a wind-carved hollow deep enough for four people to crouch in and pretend at cover.

Four people. He counted them again out of habit, the way you check your pockets. Himself. Vey, bleeding slowly and pretending not to. The satchel with Marisol's journal, the recovered pages, the photographs Odile had taken in the inner chamber by headlamp while the murals watched him with their painted eyes. And the compass, riding against his sternum in its oilcloth wrap, warm as a held hand.

Below, figures moved through the rain. Six of them, maybe seven. Professional spacing. One carried a satellite phone in a waterproof case, holding it up like a censer.

"They've been tracking us since Cold Fork," Odile said.

"Since before Cold Fork." Vey's laugh turned into a cough. "Petra's reports were good product. He knew our route before we did."

*He.* The funder. For twenty chapters of his mother's life — no, Odile corrected himself, for twenty years of his own — the man had been a shape without a face: the anonymous benefactor whose grant money had built Marisol up and then torn her down, whose lawyers had taken the house, whose name appeared in her letters only as *H.* or *our friend in the north* or, once, memorably, *the spider*. Now he had sent his body instead of his checks.

The lead climber reached the base of the pass and stopped. Even at this distance Odile could read the gesture: a raised fist, the team halting, then a single man starting up alone. Unarmed hands visible. A negotiator's walk.

"He wants to talk," Vey said.

"He wants me tired." Odile checked the satchel straps anyway. "Tired people take bad deals."

"Odile." Vey caught his sleeve. Up close, the man looked older than his fifty years, older than stone. "Whatever you're about to do — remember that everything you have in that bag is the only copy in existence. Once it leaves your hands, you have nothing left to bargain with. Ever."

"That's why I'm going to make sure I never leave empty-handed."

---

The man who climbed up to them was not what Odile expected, which was itself a kind of information. He'd imagined the spider as old, soft, upholstered — a man who bought destruction the way other men bought wine. Instead the figure pulling himself over the last rocks was perhaps forty-five, lean, weathered, with the rope-scarred hands of someone who had actually worked sites before graduating to owning them. Gray-flecked beard clipped short. Rain jacket worth more than everything Odile owned, worn like it had been earned rather than purchased.

"You're the son," the man said. Not a question. His eyes went to Vey, lingered with something that might have been contempt or might have been accounting, and returned to Odile. "Cassian told me you were stubborn. He undersold it."

"And you're H."

A flicker. Genuine surprise, quickly banked. "She called me that?"

"She called you worse."

"I imagine she did." The man lowered himself onto a rock opposite the overhang, unhurried, making himself comfortable in the mouth of a storm. "My name is Halvard Reine. And you have something of mine."

"No," Odile said. "You have something of hers. Her reputation. Her career. Eleven years of her life that your lawyers shredded. I have the receipts."

Reine smiled without warmth. "The boy has a ledger. Good. Ledgers make negotiations fast." He nodded at the satchel. "Marisol Sandoval's complete documentation of the site. The murals, the chronology, the star charts, whatever she hid behind that door. My surveyors tell me the chamber is intact and that you photographed everything. I want the originals, the negatives, the copies, and any testimony you've collected. In exchange—"

"In exchange for what? You've got six men and a storm. Why would you offer anything?"

"Because dead men drop things into crevasses." Reine said it mildly, the way you'd discuss freight rates. "Because documentation recovered from a corpse has provenance problems. Because I've spent eleven years and a great deal of money making sure the world believes Marisol Sandoval was a fraud, and a fraud's papers are worthless — unless they come with a living heir's authentication. Your signature makes my collection legitimate, Mr. Sandoval. Your silence makes it exclusive. Those are worth paying for."

There it was. Odile felt the trap's full geometry settle around him, elegant as the cave's star charts. Reine didn't just want the documents. He wanted Odile's complicity, laundered through a purchase price, so that the last person who could cry fraud became the seller of the fraud.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we wait for the storm to finish, we recover what we can, and your mother's name stays exactly where I put it." Reine spread his hands. "I'm not a monster, whatever she wrote. I'm a curator. Things survive better in my care than in the ground. Ask anyone who's sold to me."

"I did ask. Petra."

Something moved behind Reine's eyes — the small recalculation of a man discovering his information had leaked. "Then you know she regrets it."

"I know she bled for us on the forbidden path. That's more than you've done for anyone."

Vey made a sound that might have been a warning or a prayer. Odile ignored it. His heart was going hard, but his hands were steady, and he understood suddenly — with the strange clarity that arrives when there is nothing left to protect but the thing itself — that he was not afraid. Whatever happened on this rock in the next hour, his mother had already survived worse. She had survived being right and punished for it. All he had to do was be half as brave as a dead woman.

So he opened the satchel.

Reine leaned forward despite himself. Odile saw it — the hunger, quick and involuntary, the collector's reflex. He lifted out the journal, water-swollen and re-dried, its covers warped, Marisol's handwriting marching across every page in that fierce leftward slant. He set it on the rock between them, in the rain, and let the water begin to spot the first page.

Reine's hand twitched. Stopped.

"Careful," Odile said softly. "It's the only one."

"You're damaging it."

"I'm demonstrating it. There's a difference, and the difference is price." He kept his voice level, almost conversational, the way Yusuf talked about goat paths. "Here's my counteroffer. You don't get the documents. Nobody gets the documents — not as property. What you get is the chance to be on the right side of history for once, publicly, verifiably, with your name attached to the rescue instead of the burial."

Reine sat very still. Rain ran off the brim of his hood. "Go on."

"The site gets declared protected. Formal designation, international registry, enforcement funding — your money, your name on the endowment if you like, I genuinely don't care how you buy your redemption. The cave, the murals, the approach routes, all of it closed to salvage permanently. Second: every finding publishes under Marisol Sandoval's name. Sole authorship. Posthumous, obviously, but complete. Her chronology, her interpretations, her field notes quoted in full — including the parts that describe certain funding arrangements." He let that land. "Third: the record of her disbarment is reopened. Your testimony, given honestly this time, or at minimum your written acknowledgment of what actually happened. Cassian will provide his own. Won't you, Cassian."

Vey, gray-faced against the boulder, said: "I've already written it. It's in my pack. Signed, dated, witnessed by Naji, who apparently can't stop reading other people's documents, which in this case is the most useful flaw I've ever encountered."

Reine looked from one of them to the other, and Odile watched the arithmetic run behind his eyes — the cost of the concession weighed against the cost of six men and a storm and a recovery operation with provenance problems, weighed against the thing collectors fear more than any rival: irrelevance. A site declared protected and famous under a martyred woman's name would be visited, studied, photographed, enshrined. Its contents would belong to everyone, which meant they could belong to no one, which meant Halvard Reine would own the one thing he couldn't buy: a footnote in someone else's legacy.

Or he could own a locked room full of paper that the world had been taught to distrust.

"You're asking me to spend a fortune unmaking my own investment," Reine said.

"I'm asking you to stop throwing good money after a lie. You're a curator. Curate."

For a long moment there was only the rain, hammering the scree, filling the silences between them like sand poured into cracks.

"There's a problem with your leverage," Reine said finally, and his voice had changed — cooler now, the negotiator surfacing through the collector. "You need me alive and agreeable. I only need you stationary. If talks fail, my men take the satchel off your corpse and I take my chances with the papers. Water damage, fire, accidents — documents are fragile, Mr. Sandoval. People are fragile. I've outlasted both before."

"Yes," Odile said. "But you haven't outlasted this."

He took the compass from inside his jacket.

---

He hadn't planned it. That was the truth he would carry afterward — that the idea arrived whole, the way his mother's best insights must have arrived in the dark of the cave, and that once it arrived he couldn't not do it. He unwrapped the oilcloth and held the brass instrument up between them, rain beading on its casing, the needle trembling inside the glass.

"What is that?" Reine asked. And then, because he was a scholar once, before he was a spider: "That's a period piece. Nineteenth century? The housing looks—"

"It doesn't point north." Odile turned it slowly so Reine could see the needle swing, ignore magnetic north entirely, and settle — as it always settled now — toward the southeast, toward the flooded path where Yusuf and Naji and Petra were somewhere alive in the rain. "It points at whatever its holder loves most. My mother's compass. She used it to find her way home. It's the last thing she touched."

Reine's expression didn't change, but his breathing did — a fractional shallowing, the tell of a man doing new math.

"Take it," Odile said. "Hold it. I want you to see something."

And here was the gamble, naked on the wet rock between them: because if the stories were wrong, if the needle pointed at some ordinary thing — a wife in Geneva, a son at boarding school, a yacht, a dog — then Reine would see a sentimental trinket and the moment would collapse into embarrassment, and Odile would have shown the spider his one soft thing.

Reine hesitated. Then, because men who collect cannot resist touching the object, he took it.

The needle spun.

Not swung — *spun*, wild and fast, whipping around the dial like a thing in pain, around and around and around, catching no bearing, fixing on nothing, the brass housing buzzing faintly in Reine's grip as though the instrument itself were trying to shake him off. Odile watched the man's face as he watched it. Watched the confusion come first, then the dawning, horrible understanding of what the instrument was telling him, in front of witnesses, in front of the son of the woman he had destroyed.

The needle would not stop. It had nothing to stop *for*.

"Well," Reine said. His voice was perfectly controlled. His knuckles were white. "A novelty."

"It's not a novelty." Odile held out his hand. "Give it back."

Reine gave it back. His fingers released it quickly, too quickly, the way you release something hot. And in the second before Odile wrapped it away, the needle steadied instantly, swinging back to its true bearing — southeast, the flood, the family — like a dog returning to its master's side.

Silence. The rain filled it.

"My surveyors found boot prints in the chamber," Reine said at last, and it was not the sentence Odile expected. It was quieter. "Modern resin. Recent. I assumed Vey had been back." His eyes cut to Vey. "But Vey was in prison when those prints were made. Which means someone else entered my site after her. Someone I didn't send." He paused. "Did she die of exposure, Mr. Sandoval?"

The question hung there, and Odile understood that he was watching something rare: a man hearing, for the first time, the echo of his own crime coming back off the walls of the place he'd tried to bury. Whether it was remorse or merely risk assessment, Odile couldn't tell, and decided he didn't need to. Remorse was not required for the transaction. Only agreement.

"Read the journal," Odile said. "All of it. Then decide whether you want your name on the rescue or on the second betrayal. But decide fast, because in about—" he glanced at the sky, where the clouds had gone the color of wet slate, "—an hour, this pass becomes a river, and none of us decides anything ever again."

Reine stood. Rain streamed off him. He looked at the journal lying spotted with water on the rock, at Vey's written confession visible in the open flap of the pack, at Odile's face, and Odile saw him arrive — visibly, joint by joint, like a structure settling — at the conclusion that the needle had already reached.

"Protected status," Reine said. "Full publication under her name. My endowment funds the designation, and my statement reopens the record." He drew a breath that shuddered slightly at its edges. "In exchange, the originals come to me for conservation. Documented, insured, publicly accessible within five years. I keep the spider's web, Mr. Sandoval. You keep the fly's story."

"The fly's story is the only part worth anything," Odile said. "Done."

They shook on it, glove to glove, in the rain, and it was the coldest handshake of Odile's life and the warmest thing that had happened to his family in eleven years.

Reine turned to go, then stopped. "Your mother," he said, without turning around. "In her letters. Did she ever write what she loved most? What the needle would have found?" 

Odile thought of the final fix in the journal's margin, the bearing recorded in Marisol's slanting hand, pointing into the sealed heart of the cave. Thought of the second set of boot prints. Thought of the answer he might never have.

"Yeah," he said. "I think she did. I think that's what killed her."

Reine nodded once, as a man nods at a debt he can never repay, and started down into the rain toward his men.

* * *

They moved within minutes, because the pass was dying around them. Water ran in the switchback gullies like veins opening; somewhere above, with a sound like a door slamming, a slope of scree let go and went down in a brown rope of mud. Vey stumbled twice and Odile hauled him upright both times, the satchel strapped tight across his chest, the compass against his sternum beating its steady southeast pulse.

At the southern lip of the saddle, where the forbidden path dropped away into cloud, a light blinked through the rain. Three flashes. Pause. Three flashes.

Naji's signal. Alive. Waiting.

Odile grabbed Vey's collar and pulled him toward it, into the dark, into the storm, while behind them the mountain came apart in the rain — and ahead, faint through the roar, he could hear Yusuf shouting the old words his grandmother had taught him, the forbidden names, calling them home.
