## 1. The Man Who Owns No Address

There are three ways to disappear in Papua New Guinea, and I had tried all of them.

The first is the obvious one: go somewhere remote. This fails immediately, because remote places have the best memories in the world. A man who vanishes into the Sepik Basin discovers that every village along every tributary knows not only who he is, but who his mother was, and whether his grandfather once stole a pig. You cannot outrun a memory that lives in the ground.

The second is to become someone new. New name, new papers, new story. This works better, but it has a cost that took me years to understand: eventually you forget which parts of the borrowed man are costume and which are skin. I have been Jonas Petersen the surveyor for eleven months now, and some mornings I wake up and have to lie there for a while, doing the arithmetic of who I am, before I can get out of bed.

The third way is the only one that works, and it is the one nobody chooses on purpose: make yourself so unremarkable that forgetting you is effortless. Be the man who fixes the outboard motor. The man who can read a river. The man who is useful and quiet and has no address, no family, no story worth the telling. Be furniture.

I had gotten very good at being furniture. Which is why, when Okuk found me on the Wewak waterfront that morning, I knew immediately that I had made a mistake somewhere — because furniture does not get found.

* * *

The morning had started the way all my mornings started, with the tide and a borrowed theodolite. The Catholic mission was extending its seawall, and Brother Anselm — no relation to anyone important, a mild man who smelled of mimeograph fluid — paid me forty kina a day to tell him where the wall would stand longest against the sea. It was honest work in the sense that the numbers were honest, which is more than I could say for most of my professional history.

I had the instrument leveled and the first sight taken when I felt it: that particular prickle on the back of the neck that a man develops when he has spent years being looked for by people with reasons. I did not turn around. Turning around is an amateur's mistake. I finished the sight, wrote the figure in the log with a steady hand, and only then glanced down the beach.

He was standing at the edge of the seawall works like a man waiting for a bus that ran on rumor. Big, broad through the shoulders, wearing a shirt that had been ironed by someone who cared about him, and a wristwatch that was either very good or a very good copy. He was not looking at me. He was looking at the theodolite, and he was looking at it the way a man looks at a tool he understands — which was my first clue that this was not a simple man, whatever he was pretending to be.

I finished the morning's sights. I packed the instrument in its case with the care of a man who has nothing else in the world he treats gently. Then I walked down the beach toward him, because the alternative was to keep walking the other way, and I had learned — expensively — that running from a fixer in Wewak is like running from the tide. You just get tired before it does.

"You're the surveyor," he said. Not a question.

"I'm a surveyor," I said. "There are several."

"There's one who reads water." He smiled. It was a good smile, wide and warm, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Brother Anselm says you told him the seawall should curve east, against the obvious line, because the current is changing. He thought it was superstition. I checked the channel charts." He paused, letting me understand that he had actually done it, that he was a man who checked things. "You were right. The channel's moved forty meters since the charts were drawn. The official charts."

"Charts get old," I said.

"Charts get old," he agreed. "People get old too. People get found, even." He said it lightly, the way you'd comment on the weather, and I felt the ground shift under me the way it shifts under a structure whose footing you thought you knew. "My name is Okuk. I buy and sell. Sometimes cargo. Sometimes favors. Today, maybe, a job."

"I'm not looking for work."

"Nobody who says that is telling the truth," Okuk said, "and nobody who says otherwise is either. Come and drink something cold. It's hot, and I hate talking business standing up. Standing up is for funerals."

* * *

The place he took me was one of those Wewak establishments that exists in the gap between the guidebooks and the police reports — a veranda over the water, a fridge that hummed like a mosquito with ambition, and SP lager at a price that told you exactly how much of the cost was beer and how much was the view of who else came there. Okuk greeted three separate tables by name. Two of them greeted him back with the careful warmth of men who owed him money and hoped he'd forgotten. He had not forgotten. I could see him not forgetting, the way you can see a crocodile not moving.

He ordered without asking me. Two beers, and a plate of sago and tinned fish that arrived as if the kitchen had been expecting him since dawn.

"You were a railroad man," he said.

"I'm a surveyor."

"You were a railroad man," he repeated, with the mild patience of a man laying bricks, "in a country where the railroads are mostly a rumor now. You came through Port Moresby four years ago. Before that —" he waved his hand, a small gesture that contained an entire dossier — "well. Before that is your business. But I know a man who once rode a train in the Sepik hills, before the war took the rails, and he says the man who surveyed that line could read country like a priest reads scripture. And now there is a surveyor in Wewak who reads water the same way." He drank. "I don't believe in coincidence. Coincidence is what lazy men call pattern."

I turned my bottle a quarter-turn on the table. A small movement. The kind of movement that buys time.

"Whatever you think you know about me," I said, "you know it wrong."

"Probably," Okuk said cheerfully. "Half of what I know is wrong. That's why I check the other half twice. The point is not what you were, Jonas. The point — and I will call you Jonas until you give me a better name, and I suspect you won't — the point is what you can do. And what you can do is move through country that eats other men. Yes?"

I said nothing, which in that establishment was as good as a signature.

"Good." He leaned back. Below us, the tide was going out, uncovering the mudflats where the Wewak kids dug for shells, and beyond it the water lay flat and silver all the way to the horizon, hiding everything it hid. "There is an expedition. Scientific. Hydrological — they measure rivers. They are going up the Sepik, to an old gauge station near the headwaters, decommissioned since the Australians left. They need supplies carried up. Diesel, food, instruments, the usual. And they need a man to get the convoy there, because the river between here and there is not a river, it is an argument, and the river is winning."

"Every boat operator in Wewak wants that contract."

"Every boat operator in Wewak wants the contract I would give them," Okuk said. "This one is different. This one is quiet. No radio chatter, no port agents, no manifest filed with the harbormaster. The expedition's lead is a man named Hargreave — English, or English enough — and he has money and no patience and a funder who prefers discretion. They want a man who is himself discreet." His eyes came around to me, and for the first time they were not smiling at all. "A man, in fact, with practice at not being found."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I will buy you lunch, tell you the fish was excellent, and never trouble you again." He said it so smoothly that I almost believed it, and the fact that I almost believed it told me exactly how good he was. "But you will say yes."

"Why would I say yes?"

"Because," Okuk said, "you have been in Wewak eleven months, and a man like you can live on nothing, but you cannot live on nothing *forever*. Because the money is very good — I will write the number on a napkin and you will pretend not to be impressed, and I will pretend to believe you. And because" — he tapped the table once, gently, like a doctor finding the spot that hurts — "you are bored, Jonas. Bored men die of it. Slowly, usually, but surely. You have been furniture for a year. Furniture doesn't dream. You dream. I can tell. You have the face of a man who is somewhere else even when he is here."

The worst part was that he was right, and the second-worst part was that he knew he was right, and the third-worst part, which I did not understand until later, much later, was that he had been sent.

I made him write the number on a napkin. I pretended not to be impressed. He pretended to believe me.

* * *

That night I did not sleep, which was not unusual. What was unusual was what I did not sleep *about*.

I lay in the room I rented above the Chinese trade store — a room with a mattress, a mosquito coil, and a window that looked out on the trade store's corrugated fence, in case you were wondering what eleven months of discretion buys — and I thought about a trestle.

It is strange, the things that stay. You would think a man haunted by a dead bridge would be haunted by the falling of it, the noise, the water, the terrible slow way a structure comes apart when it has been lied about. But no. What stays is smaller. A clipboard. The weight of the clipboard in my hand, and the form on it, and the line at the bottom where a signature goes, and the voice of the regional manager — a decent man, a tired man, a man with a mortgage and a daughter at university — saying, *Casper, the inspection's clean, the report's clean, sign it and we all go home for Christmas.*

The trestle at Kilometer 214 had been rated for loads it could not carry. I knew it. I had written a report saying so, a real one, with numbers in it, and the numbers had been removed by people whose job was removing numbers, and what came back down the chain to me was a form with a clean box and a voice saying *sign it and we all go home for Christmas.*

Fourteen months later it went down under a work train. Three men. I knew two of their wives' names, because I had eaten at their tables, because that is what you do on a rail crew — you eat at each other's tables, and you learn the names, and then one day the names go on a list and the list goes in a report and the report says *structural fatigue, unforeseeable,* and the company survives, and the man who signed the clean form keeps his certification for another two years until the tribunal finds the original numbers in a file cabinet in a regional office, at which point the company survives anyway, and the man who signed does not.

I did not go to prison. That is the detail nobody believes. There was no crime, technically. There was a signature, and pressure, and a form, and the law — which is a structure too, and is rated for far less than it carries — held. I lost the license, the career, the name, and the ability to sign anything without my hand knowing what my hand knows. I lost the right to be in rooms where load ratings are discussed. What I kept was the arithmetic: that a structure fails exactly as hard as the lies in its paperwork, and that the men at the bottom of the structure are always the ones underneath when it comes down.

So I came to a country with no railroads. You may laugh. I did, once, on the plane into Port Moresby, a short ugly sound that made the woman beside me move her bag. A country of rivers and roads that wash away and footpaths and boats, and not a single kilometer of track — a whole nation where nothing I knew how to do could kill anybody. I took survey work under a borrowed name because a theodolite doesn't collapse. It just measures. It tells you where things stand, and it doesn't care what you wish were true, and nobody has ever asked a theodolite to sign a clean form.

Eleven months of that. Forty kina a day and a room above a trade store and a name I wear like a shirt I can't take off.

And now a fixer with an ironed shirt and a good copy of a good watch was telling me the river had a job for me, and the number on the napkin was six months of Brother Anselm's seawall, and the route went up the Sepik to a decommissioned gauge station near the headwaters, and something in my chest that I had been keeping very still for four years had turned over like a sleeping man hearing his name.

Because here is the thing about the headwaters. Here is the thing I had not told Okuk, and would not tell him, and would not tell anyone.

I knew that station. Not the station itself — I'd never been there — but the *lines*. The old colonial survey sheets, the ones from before the war, the ones that show a spiderweb of narrow-gauge spurs running up out of the Sepik Basin toward the hills, mining spurs, labor spurs, lines with names like the companies that built them and companies that ate them. I had studied those sheets for years, in libraries, in archives, in the long insomniac years when a man without a profession studies the corpse of his own. And on every modern map, every chart, every government survey since, those spurs are gone. Not abandoned — abandoned lines get dotted, get marked *disused,* get remembered. These were *erased.* Somebody had paid, more than once, in more than one country, to make a spiderweb of steel disappear from paper.

And now a well-funded expedition was going upriver to the exact country where those lines used to be, with crates I hadn't seen and a funder who preferred discretion, and the only man in Wewak who knew what used to be under all that water and jungle had just been offered six months' wages to carry them there.

I got up. I lit the mosquito coil. I sat at the table by the window with my old survey sheets — the copies I kept in a waterproof map case under the floorboard, because a man who owns no address still owns his obsessions — and I spread them out under the coil's small red light, and I looked at the ghost lines running up toward the headwaters like veins under skin.

Okuk had said I was bored. He was wrong, and he was right. It wasn't boredom. It was the other thing, the thing that had been waiting under the boredom all along, patient as water: the knowledge that somewhere in this country there was a structure that had failed, and that somewhere in the paperwork of the world there was a form that had been signed clean, and that the men underneath it were still underneath it.

I did not sleep. Toward dawn I took a pencil and marked the gauge station's coordinates on the old sheet, and the nearest ghost spur, and the distance between them, and I sat looking at the two marks like a man looking at a fuse.

At seven I went down to the waterfront and found Okuk already there, drinking tea, unsurprised, watching the tide come in over the mudflats as if he had ordered it personally.

"Well?" he said.

I looked at the river. I looked at the hills beyond it, where the erased lines ran, where the water had been covering something for fifty years and was, at this moment, in this season, getting ready to give some of it back.

"Tell me about the cargo," I said.
