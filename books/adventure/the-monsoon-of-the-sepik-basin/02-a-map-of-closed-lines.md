## 2. A Map of Closed Lines

Okuk left the contract on my table the way a man leaves a lit cigarette near an open fuel drum — casually, and with full knowledge of what happens next.

I told myself I wouldn't touch it until morning. I lasted forty minutes, which for me is a personal record in self-restraint and says more about my character than I'd like it to.

The paper was good stock, the kind that survives humidity without going soft, which already told me money was behind it. Real money, not the missionary-donation kind or the government-budget kind that arrives three years late and forty percent lighter. The letterhead said *Meridian Logistics Solutions*, which is the sort of name companies choose when they want you to think about anything except what they actually do. Beneath it, in smaller type: *Provisioning & River Transport Services — Confidential Engagement*.

Confidential. Of course. Everything worth doing upriver was confidential. The fish were confidential. The rain was confidential. Somewhere a man was being paid to keep the fog off the books.

I read the terms twice. The second time was not because I'd missed anything but because I was hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something less strange. They didn't.

The job: deliver supplies to a hydrological expedition working out of a decommissioned gauge station near the headwaters of a tributary the contract declined to name, referring to it only as "Site K." Four runs over six weeks. Fuel, food, instruments, spare parts. My boat, my hands, my discretion. Payment in advance, half now, half on completion, wired through an account in Singapore that would make the transaction look like agricultural equipment sales.

Agricultural equipment. To a hydrology station. In the Sepik Basin.

I set the pages down and looked at the rain coming off the roof of the trade store across the street, and I did the thing I always do when a deal smells wrong, which is to ask myself not *what's wrong with it* but *what's right with it*, because the wrong parts are usually decoration and the right parts are usually the tell. What was right about this one: the pay was triple the going rate, which meant either danger or secrecy, and in my experience those two travel together like crocodiles and warm water. What was also right: they wanted someone who could read water and keep his mouth shut, and those are the two things I am genuinely good at, if we don't count bridge engineering, which history suggests we shouldn't.

And then there was the map.

It came folded inside the contract, a survey sheet on heavy paper, and whoever had prepared it had made a mistake that I suspect Okuk's principals would have paid real money to avoid. Because maps are like confessions — people redact the words and forget the handwriting.

* * *

Here is what most people don't understand about railway maps, and I'll forgive them for it, because I didn't understand it either until a collapsed trestle took my career and left me with nothing but the understanding. A rail line is never just a line. It's a decision. Every curve says *we chose to go around that hill*. Every gradient says *our engines could climb this much and no more*. And every spur — every little dead-end finger reaching off the main line toward nowhere — says *there was something there worth reaching*.

This map showed the Sepik tributaries in blue, the roads in red where roads existed at all, and the old colonial-era rail alignments in dashed black, marked *ABANDONED — NO PUBLIC RECORD*. That last phrase was doing a lot of work. Lines aren't abandoned by accident. They're abandoned by paperwork, and paperwork is written by people, and people have reasons.

There were four spurs. One ran from the main alignment down to the river at a point roughly thirty kilometers below Site K. Another climbed into the foothills toward what the map coyly labeled "quarry — disused." The third just stopped, mid-slope, at coordinates with no annotation whatsoever, as though the surveyor had run out of ink or nerve. And the fourth —

The fourth went to Site K.

Not near it. Not past it. To it. The dashed line terminated precisely at the decommissioned gauge station, and then, as if that weren't enough, continued — fainter, almost apologetic, a ghost under the ghost — another two kilometers upstream before dissolving into blank paper.

I got out my own maps. I keep a steel cash box under my bunk with everything I own that matters, which is to say paper: certificates under names that are mostly mine, a tide table for a coast I haven't seen in two years, and a collection of survey sheets I've copied or bought or been given by men who wanted them forgotten. I spread them across the table and weighted the corners with beer bottles, because in Wewak that is what beer bottles are for.

The commercial charts showed nothing. No spur, no station, no Site K — just the river doing its braided, indecisive thing through country too green and too far from anywhere to interest anyone with a printer. The wartime navigation notes mentioned a "lighterage landing, upper reach, condition unknown." Condition unknown. Two words that in this country can mean anything from silted-in to shot-at.

But my oldest sheet — a 1938 mining concession plan I'd traded a Japanese officer's binoculars for in Lae, back when I still traded things instead of hiding them — showed the line. All of it. Main alignment, the four spurs, and beyond the gauge station, continuing upstream to a terminus marked with a symbol I knew well from other maps, other countries, other lives: a circle with a cross in it. An adit. A mine entrance driven horizontally into a hillside, the kind of entrance men go into and ore comes out of, and sometimes the order of those operations reverses.

The 1938 map named the operator. *Neuguinea Bergbau und Handels Gesellschaft*, which rolls off the tongue like a debt rolling downhill. I knew the name the way you know the name of a dog that bit you in a town you no longer live in. They'd operated all through the highlands and the coast in the colonial years, moved labor by rail and river, kept books in triplicate, and dissolved after the war into a succession of successor companies, each one inheriting the assets and shedding the liabilities like a snake moving through a series of increasingly clean skins.

I sat back and looked at the two maps side by side — the new one paid for by Meridian Logistics Solutions, the old one paid for by men who counted coolies as line items — and I asked myself the question that has kept me alive longer than competence has:

Who pays to erase a rail line?

Because erasure costs money. Surveyors cost money. New charts cost money. Bureaucrats cost money, though in this case perhaps less than usual. Nobody spends that kind of effort on a line that leads to nothing. You erase a line when the line leads somewhere you don't want found — or somewhere you do want found, but only by yourself.

And here was the part that made me put the beer bottle down carefully instead of drinking from it: the contract wasn't asking me to find anything. It was asking me to supply an expedition that was already there. Hydrologists. Water-measurers. Men and women with current meters and sediment bottles, whose entire professional vocabulary is flow rates and discharge volumes and turbidity, none of which requires a decommissioned gauge station sitting at the end of an erased railway spur next to an unmarked mine adit.

Unless they weren't measuring the water.

Unless they were measuring what the water was trying to say.

* * *

I slept badly, which is standard, and dreamed about the trestle, which is also standard, so let's skip both and move to the morning, when I took the contract down to the wharf and read it again with salt air in my nose and diesel smoke in my eyes, because documents lie differently depending on where you read them.

At the wharf I did arithmetic. The route to Site K, as best I could reconstruct it from the dashed line and the blue water, ran up through channels I knew partially and channels I didn't know at all. Six weeks, four runs, monsoon season building toward its peak. Crocodiles fat and stupid on the easy hunting of the lower reaches, and lean and clever above them. Log rafts drifting down without lights. Middlemen at every junction who taxed cargo by eye and temper. And at the end of it, a station full of scientists who had hired their escort through a fixer rather than hiring him directly, which meant somebody in that chain didn't want their faces known to each other.

That's not how you provision a hydrology survey. That's how you provision something you intend to deny later.

I should have walked away. Let me be clear about that, since this account will be read — if it's read at all — by people deciding what kind of man I was. The correct decision, the decision made by any reasonable person with functioning survival instincts and no outstanding debts, was to carry the contract back to Okuk, decline with thanks, and spend the season running fuel drums to mission stations and telling myself I was honest.

But I looked at that dashed line for a long time. The fourth spur. The one that ended at Site K and then kept going, faintly, upstream, to a circle with a cross in it.

I've spent eleven years being nobody from nowhere, and I'll tell you what nobody tells you about invisibility: it isn't peace. It's a room you build around yourself, brick by brick, alias by alias, until one day you notice the room has no windows and you built it yourself and you can't remember what you were hiding from. I told myself I hid because hiding was safe. But safety is just fear with better furniture, and some mornings, watching the river take everything downstream without asking permission, I felt less like a man who had escaped and more like freight that had slipped its manifest.

The trestle took my name. Fine. But it hadn't taken my eyes, and my eyes were telling me that this contract, this confidential, triple-rate, agricultural-equipment contract, was a door standing open in a wall I'd spent a decade believing was solid.

Curiosity is supposed to be a small vice, a cat-killing trifle. Nobody warns you it's structural. That once it gets into the foundations of a man, the whole building leans toward whatever he isn't supposed to know.

I found Okuk at his usual table behind the trade store, under the awning, playing cards with two men who scattered like sparrows when I approached, which flattered neither of us.

"You read it," he said. Not a question. He poured coffee from a thermos into a tin cup and pushed it across without looking up, which in Okuk's language is a contract of its own.

"I read it."

"And?"

"There's a rail spur on your map that ends at the gauge station."

Okuk's face did nothing, which for Okuk is a great deal. Most men, hearing that sentence, would blink, or frown, or glance away. Okuk drank his coffee with the serenity of a man who has already decided how much of the truth he can afford, and the answer is usually *some*.

"Old lines," he said. "War damage. Nobody uses them."

"Somebody paid to have them removed from the charts."

"Somebody pays for lots of things." He set the cup down and finally looked at me, and there it was — the thing beneath the fixer's calm, the flicker of a man who knows more than he's selling. "You taking the job or not?"

"The expedition lead," I said. "Who are they?"

"A doctor. Hargreave. Hydrologist, big reputation, does work for universities and governments and people who pay university prices." He shrugged. "There's a cartographer too. Norwegian, argues with rivers. The rest you meet at the staging camp."

"Staging camp where?"

"Up the coast road. Boat leaves day after tomorrow, first light." He gathered the cards, squared them against the table with a snap. "You're asking a lot of questions for a man who hasn't signed."

"I'm asking exactly one question. I'm just asking it several ways."

"Then ask it straight."

I looked out past the awning at the harbor, at the water lying brown and patient under the morning haze, going about its business of carrying everything away. Somewhere up that water, at the end of an erased line, a circle with a cross in it waited under sixty years of jungle, and a crew of water-measurers was camped beside it pretending to care about sediment.

"Why me?" I said. "You know a dozen boatmen in Wewak. Cheaper ones. Louder ones. Ones who don't ask about rail spurs."

Okuk smiled, and it was the saddest smile I ever saw him wear, before or since.

"Because the client asked for a man with engineering papers," he said, "who doesn't exist on any registry. And Casper—" he used my real name, quietly, the way you'd handle something with an edge, "—men like that are rare. Men like that are usually hiding from something. And clients who ask for hidden men," he tapped the contract once, "are usually hiding something too. So. Are you taking the job?"

The rain started again, soft on the awning, and I thought about the room I'd built with no windows, and the door standing open in the wall, and the faint dashed line running upstream to a place where somebody once counted bodies as ballast and called it logistics.

I picked up the pen.

"First light, day after tomorrow," I said, and signed the name that wasn't mine on the line provided, and felt — for the first time in eleven years — like the signature might actually fit somebody.

Okuk took the contract, checked the ink the way he'd check a banknote, and stood. "One more thing," he said, shrugging into his shirt. "The cartographer. Ellingsen. She'll look at you like she's reading a chart."

"They all do."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "She'll read you *right*. That's different."

He walked out into the rain, and I sat there a while with the cold coffee, listening to the water come off the awning, thinking that of everything Okuk had told me, that was the part I should probably have feared the most.
