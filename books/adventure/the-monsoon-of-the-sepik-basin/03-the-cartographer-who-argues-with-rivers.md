## 3. The Cartographer Who Argues With Rivers

The official chart said the channel ran along the eastern bank below the second bend. The river disagreed. The river had been disagreeing for years, patiently, the way rivers do, and nobody at the Hydrological Survey had thought to ask it why.

I asked it why.

I'd been at the staging camp four days, camped at the end of a gravel spit where the Sepik's tributary widened into something between a river and a rumor, and in four days I had redrawn eleven kilometers of channel chart from direct observation, a sounding line, a borrowed aluminum boat, and a level of stubbornness that Dr. Hargreave had already begun to call, in that voice he used when he wanted to be called reasonable, "energetic."

"Mara." He stood over my table in the mess tent, holding the official chart the way a priest holds a text he suspects of heresy but can't quite say so out loud. "The Survey issued this in 2019."

"The Survey issued this from a helicopter in 2019," I said. "At midday. In the dry season. With a camera that cost more than my boat." I tapped the mylar sheet I'd been working on, where I'd inked the true channel — a deep, sinuous throat hugging the western bank, choked on its eastern side with a sandbar the size of a parish. "Rivers don't hold still for cameras, Doctor. This one moves. It's been cutting west for at least six years. See the cut bank, see the point bar, see the way the snags lie? The water's telling you where it's been and where it's going. The chart just isn't listening."

"The channel depth on the official figure is two point one meters."

"The channel depth at low water, on the line I've drawn, is two point four. On the official line it's forty centimeters and a propeller's worth of grief." I pulled the chart toward me and made the mark I'd been waiting all morning to make — a bold red X over the Survey's navigation line. "If we run the convoy where this says to run it, we will put two boats on that bar and spend a day and a half getting them off, and we will do it in crocodile water, because everything here is crocodile water."

Hargreave looked at the X for a long moment. He was a tall man who had spent his career being the smartest person in whatever room he stood in, and I think it genuinely confused him that the room had stopped agreeing.

"You've been here four days."

"I've been reading water for eleven years." I said it without heat. There was no point in heat; heat just gave him something to be reasonable about. "The river is the most honest colleague I've ever had. It never inflates a result. It never fudges a margin to keep a donor happy." I let that sit for exactly one second. "Speaking of which. You've got us loaded to ninety percent of rated displacement on the big boat, and you've got the schedule built on eight hours of running time a day, and you've got no fuel cache between here and the gauge station. Any one of those I'd call optimism. All three together I'd call a margin of error with a hole in it."

"The window is short," Hargreave said. "The calving season opens the high route for a few weeks. After that—"

"After that we wait a year, I know. I've read the briefing. I wrote part of the briefing." I capped my pen. "Short windows are an argument for better margins, not worse ones. A tight schedule is exactly when you want to know where the deep water is."

He gathered the official chart off my table with the air of a man confiscating a toy, and paused. "I'll consider the western line," he said, which was Hargreave for *I will not consider the western line but I have learned not to say so to your face*.

"Consider it out loud," I said to his back. "The river will consider it either way."

He left. The tent flap settled. Somewhere down the bank, the outboard engines ticked as they cooled, and the river went on saying what it always said, which was everything, in no particular hurry.

* * *

I want to be fair to Hargreave, because fairness is a discipline like any other, and because the man was not a fool. He was a hydrologist of genuine distinction who had spent twenty years persuading ministries and foundations to fund work in places where the funding was the first thing to evaporate and the work was the second. He had learned to run lean and fast because lean and fast had always, so far, worked. He believed in the mission the way some men believe in God — sincerely, and with an unfortunate tolerance for what could be done in its name.

But he did not argue with rivers. He negotiated with them, briefly, and then he published.

I argued with rivers. My father had been a pilot on the Hardangervidda lakes, and he taught me before I could read that water is a document, that every riffle is a sentence and every eddy a footnote, and that the people who drown are almost always the people who read the summary instead of the text. I had come to the Sepik because the charts here were the worst I'd ever seen professionally — beautiful, confident, and wrong in ways that would kill someone eventually — and because a Norwegian cartographer who redraws other people's rivers tends to run out of patience for her own country's fjords, which at least have the decency to stay where they're put.

I finished inking the western line by lamplight, and I was still angry, which was how I noticed the man at the water's edge.

* * *

He was standing on the gravel spit below the boat ramp, alone, in the last gray hour before full dark, and he was doing something I had never seen a hired escort do.

He was reading.

Not a book. The water. He stood with his hands in his pockets and his weight on his heels and he watched the current the way my father had watched it — the long, patient, slightly tilted stare, eyes moving bank to bank, reading the surface like a page he already half knew. I watched him track a line of foam as it came down the channel, saw him mark where it held straight and where it bent, saw him glance up at the cut bank and then back to the foam, and I understood that he was doing in his head what I did on mylar: fitting the observed river against some chart he carried in there, and finding the disagreements.

There was a boat at the ramp — one of the older ones, a long aluminum hull with a temperamental sixty-horse Evinrude that had been sulking since Wewak. He went to it, crouched at the transom, and did something to the fuel line with the unhurried economy of a man who had done it a thousand times, and then he stood and looked at the water again, and I saw him look specifically at the sandbar, at the eastern bank, at the place where the official chart sent boats to die.

Then he looked up at me.

I don't know how long I'd been standing at the tent flap. Long enough. He didn't startle and he didn't wave. He just looked at me across forty meters of gravel and darkening water with an expression I recognized because I'd seen it in mirrors and in the faces of the two or three other people I'd ever met who shared the affliction: the look of a person caught doing the thing they're best at, who isn't sorry, and who is deciding, in real time, how much of it to admit to.

I walked down to the ramp. It was the obvious move and I've never been good at not making the obvious move.

"That boat's been flooding the carburetor since Wewak," I said.

"Float valve sticks," he said. "I cleaned it. It'll stick again in about three days. There's grit in the fuel down here." He said it in the flat tone of a man reporting the weather, and then, because I didn't answer, he added, "You're the cartographer."

"I'm the cartographer."

"Okuk said you'd look at me like you were reading a chart."

"Did he." I looked at the river, then at him. He was maybe forty, hard-used, with the kind of face that had been deliberately arranged to be forgettable and had mostly succeeded, except for the eyes, which were doing far too much reading to be forgettable. "And what did Okuk say I'd conclude?"

"He didn't say." A pause. The current made its long hiss against the gravel. "He said you'd read me right. He seemed to think that was different from looking."

"Okuk is a very wise man," I said, "who charges by the wisdom. What's your name? Your real one, if it's convenient. I'll settle for the working one if it's not."

"Casper Iversen."

"Casper Iversen." I tried it against the evening. "And what are you, Casper Iversen, besides a man who fixes float valves in the dark?"

"Escort."

"You were reading the channel."

"Old habit." He turned back to the water, and I watched him decide something — watched it happen in the set of his shoulders, a small surrender, a man letting one card show because the alternative was a longer conversation. "The Survey chart has the channel on the east bank below the second bend. It's wrong. It's been wrong for years. The deep water's on the west, inside the cut, and the bar's growing every wet season. If your convoy runs the official line, you'll lose a boat on the bar."

I kept my face still, which cost me something. "That's my finding. Exactly. To the meter."

"It's not a finding," he said. "It's just what the water does. Any boatman on this river could tell you."

"Then why hasn't anyone?"

He was quiet for a moment. Down the bank, one of the crew was laughing at something in Tok Pisin, the sound carrying flat across the water. "Because the people who draw the charts don't ask the boatmen," he said finally, "and the boatmen have stopped offering. It saves everyone embarrassment."

"That's the most cynical thing I've heard all week," I said, "and I spent Tuesday with Dr. Hargreave's budget spreadsheet."

The corner of his mouth moved. Not a smile. The place where a smile would be kept, in storage, for a better year. "The western line," he said. "You've drawn it already."

It wasn't a question. "How do you know that?"

"Because you argued with Hargreave about it at dinner, loud enough that the cook heard, and because you're the kind of person who draws the line before she asks permission." He shrugged. "It's what I'd have done."

We stood there a moment longer, two people on a gravel bar in the middle of the largest unbroken swamp in the Pacific, discovering that we were the same specific kind of strange. I did not like it. Liking things is how you lose them, and I had a rule about the people I worked with: I could admire them, rely on them, mourn them if it came to that, but I didn't let them become interesting, because interesting people got inside the chart and moved the lines.

He was already interesting. That was the problem.

"Your margin analysis," he said, "the displacement, the fuel, the running hours. You're right about all of it. He'll listen to the fuel one. He won't listen to the other two until something goes wrong, and then he'll listen completely, and it will be too late, and he'll blame the river."

"You've worked with his type before."

"I've *been* his type before," he said, and the flat way he said it closed the subject like a door.

* * *

I didn't sleep well that night, which was not unusual, but the shape of the sleeplessness was new. I lay in my tent with the rain starting on the fly — the first real rain of the build-up, fat, warm drops that sounded like applause from a very large and unimpressed audience — and I thought about the man on the gravel spit.

Here is what I knew, by morning: he'd arrived two days ago on Okuk's boat with one duffel bag and no history. He'd spent those two days mostly alone, and mostly at the water. The crew had already started leaving tools where he could find them, which is how boatmen vote. And he had looked at the same river I had looked at, with the same instruments — none — and reached the same line, to the meter.

There were two explanations. One was that Casper Iversen had spent enough years on this river to know its habits the way a village knows its elders. The other was that Casper Iversen read water the way I did — as a text, as a discipline, as the one honest document in any room — which meant he'd learned it somewhere, formally, somewhere that issued charts and certified people, and that somewhere was not on any of the papers Okuk had given Hargreave.

I got up in the gray light and walked down to the spit with my coffee, and he was there again, and this time he had the old Survey chart, Hargreave's confiscated copy, spread on the gravel under a stone at each corner.

"You stole the doctor's chart," I said.

"I borrowed it from the mess tent. He took mine." He pointed at the eastern line, the official one, the doomed one. "It's not just wrong here. Look at the tributary junction, twelve kilometers up. The chart shows the channel entering from the northeast. It enters from the north. And the chart shows the gauge station on the true left bank." He paused, and something moved behind his face, quick and gone, like a fish turning under the surface. "The station's on the right bank. Or it was. Rivers move. Stations don't."

I crouched over the chart. He was right. Of course he was right. I'd flagged the junction myself and hadn't gotten to the station yet.

"You've been up there," I said.

"I've been a lot of places." He gathered the chart, folded it along its original creases with the care people give to things they respect, and held it out to me. "Give this back to him. Tell him the western line, the northern junction, and add a day to the schedule for the bar at the confluence. He'll take the schedule change from you. He won't take it from me."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm the help," he said, "and the help doesn't have findings." He looked at me then, straight on, and for the first time the flat delivery cracked and I saw something underneath it — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe, with the safety off. "You redraw their rivers because you can't stand watching them be wrong. I read their water because being right is the only thing nobody's ever been able to take off me. We're going to be on the same boats for three weeks, Ellingsen. Let's not pretend we don't know what each other is."

I took the chart. Our hands didn't touch and both of us made sure of it.

"And what is it," I said, "that we know what each other is?"

"Careful," he said. "And lonely about it. And about to be a problem for Dr. Hargreave, because neither of us knows how to let a wrong chart stand."

He walked off up the bank toward the boats, and I stood there with the chart under my arm and the rain starting again, and I thought about what Okuk had told him — that I'd read him right — and how it hadn't occurred to either of us that being read right is a door that swings both ways.

I redrew the junction before breakfast. I added the day to the schedule, and Hargreave, to my genuine surprise, took it, and I decided not to wonder why until later.

Later came sooner than I wanted. Because when I went down to the boats to check the stowage, the mislabeled crates were already loaded — the ones marked *INSTRUMENTS — FRAGILE* in fresh stenciled paint — and Casper Iversen was standing over the open lid of one of them with a pry bar in his hand and an expression on his face that I recognized, because I'd been wearing a version of it for two days.

It was the look of a man who had just read something on a chart that should not have been there.
