## 4. Manifest of Small Lies

The manifest said forty-one crates. I counted forty-three.

That was the first lie, and it wasn't even a good one. Anyone who has ever moved freight through a place where nobody signs anything knows that the manifest is not a record of what you're carrying — it's a record of what someone is willing to admit to. The extra two crates sat at the end of the row under a tarp weighted with river stones, and they had been there long enough for the tarp to grow a green beard of algae on its north side.

I stood in the staging shed at Wewak's back wharf with a clipboard I hadn't asked for, in air so humid it felt less like weather than like swimming slowly, and I did the thing I have spent most of my adult life trying not to do.

I got curious.

Curiosity is a luxury item. It's the first thing you sell off when your name becomes something you carry instead of something you own. For six years I had been a man who read manifests the way other men read horoscopes — quickly, skeptically, and without letting any of it touch me. You count the crates, you check the weights against the waybill, you note the discrepancies in pencil on the back page where nobody looks, and then you forget all of it, because forgetting is a professional skill and I had been very good at my profession once.

But forty-one versus forty-three is the kind of arithmetic that sits in your chest like a swallowed fishhook.

* * *

The staging camp itself was a study in careful money. Not flashy money — careful money. The generators were quiet European models, the fuel drums were stacked on pallets instead of mud, and the expedition's boats — two aluminum workhorses and one long black fiberglass thing with an engine that cost more than the rest of the convoy combined — were tied off with proper knots. Somebody with a budget and a conscience about budgets had put this together. In my experience, those two qualities rarely travel together, and when they do, it usually means the budget is serving a purpose the conscience hasn't been told about.

Dr. Hargreave appeared mid-morning, as I'd known he would, because men like Hargreave can feel an audit happening the way dogs feel weather. He came down the plank walk with his shirt pressed and his smile deployed at half-power, the setting he used for people whose cooperation he needed but whose opinions he didn't.

"Iversen. Making yourself useful. Excellent."

"Counting," I said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" He glanced at the manifest in my hand with the mild interest of a man looking at a menu in a language he doesn't speak. "Everything should be in order. The logistics firm in Lae handled the packing."

"The logistics firm packed forty-one crates."

"Then we're two crates richer than advertised. A rare event in this country." His smile went up a notch, testing. "You'll find hydrological equipment is heavy, tedious, and deeply boring. Core samplers. Flow gauges. The kind of machinery that makes strong men weep with tedium."

He said the word *boring* twice, which was once more than necessary, and I wrote that down too, in the pencil place.

Hargreave was a good talker, which I want to be clear is not the same as a liar. Liars rehearse. Hargreave improvised, smoothly, the way water finds a downhill, and if you weren't watching the ground he was flowing around, you'd never notice how much of it he was avoiding. He wanted to know what I'd found. He wanted to know it casually. And he wanted me to conclude, on my own, that whatever I'd found was dull.

I gave him the dull face. Six years of borrowed names will teach you a whole wardrobe of faces, and the dull one fits me best anyway.

"Core samplers," I agreed. "Can't wait."

He lingered another thirty seconds — long enough to establish that lingering was his idea — and then went back up the plank walk toward the radio tent, where a man was shouting into a handset in Tok Pisin about fuel prices, and I turned back to the crates.

* * *

The manifest was typed, which already made it unusual. Most cargo moving through the Sepik these days travels with a handwritten list and a handshake, and both dissolve in the rain. This one had been produced on a proper form, with letterhead from a shipping agent in Lae I'd actually heard of, and every line item was plausible. Sediment corers. Water quality kits. Spare propellers. Rice, diesel, medical stores, tinned meat enough to feed twelve people for six weeks. Twelve people, though the expedition roster I'd been shown named nine. Another small surplus. Camps run by careful money always over-provision; camps run by honest ones don't need to.

I worked down the row, checking stencils against line items. Crate 1 through 9: instruments, hydrological, as declared. I pried one open because that was nominally my job, and found exactly what the manifest promised — a sediment corer wrapped in wax paper, gleaming, expensive, boring. Crates 10 through 14: consumables. Rice, and the particular sweet-rot smell of diesel drums breathing in the heat. All true. All dull.

Crate 15 was where the weight started lying.

The stencil said INSTRUMENTS — METEOROLOGICAL, gross weight 60 kg. I've loaded enough crates to weigh them with my knees before my hands ever get involved, and this one came off the ground like it was full of wet iron. I cut the strapping and lifted the lid.

Anvil. A drilling anvil, forged steel, the kind you set under a core drill rig so the bit doesn't argue with the ground. Alongside it, coiled in oiled cloth, drill steel — six lengths of it, and a bit head with carbide teeth sized for rock, not river mud. You do not take rock-drilling gear on a hydrological survey. Rivers are made of the one substance on earth that forgives you for not drilling it.

I sat on the crate's edge and looked at the anvil for a while. Somewhere out on the water a canoe went past, a woman standing amidships with a paddle, and the sound she made was the oldest sound in the world, and I remember thinking: somebody paid to ship sixty kilograms of forged steel four thousand kilometers to drive a hole into stone, and then wrote *meteorological instruments* on the outside, and the distance between those two facts was where the whole story lived.

Crate 16 was worse. The stencil said SAMPLE VAULTS — GLASSWARE, and inside, nested in straw, were three steel cylinders the size of beer kegs, each with a gasketed lid, a pressure seal, and a padlock hasp. Sample vaults, yes. But sample vaults are built for one purpose: keeping what's inside from being seen or opened by anyone who doesn't hold the key. Glassware doesn't need a lock. Bones don't need a lock either, unless somebody is afraid of who might look at them.

I'm not going to pretend I knew what they were for. I want to be honest about the shape of my ignorance at that stage, because later, when everything was plain, people would assume I'd understood it all from the start. I hadn't. What I had was a feeling — the specific, cold feeling of a man reading a document that has been written by someone who expects never to meet the reader. Freight tells you things. Freight is honest in aggregate even when it lies in particulars, because weight doesn't take direction, and steel doesn't care whose name is on the waybill. Forty-one crates of measuring equipment don't include a rock drill. Measuring expeditions don't bring vaults with locks. And nobody — no one, in twenty years of moving things up and down rivers — ships a satellite telephone and forgets to mention it.

Which brings me to crate 22.

* * *

Crate 22 was listed as SPARE RADIO EQUIPMENT, and it did contain spare radio equipment: a backup HF set still in factory film, antenna wire, a hand-crank charger. All legitimate. All accounted for. But beneath the radio, wedged into a false bottom that some careful packer had built with real craftsmanship — dado joints, felt lining, the works — was a satellite handset in a hard case, charged, with a prepaid SIM slot already loaded and a laminated card taped inside the lid bearing a single number and a single word: *URGENT.*

Not a contact name. Not a company. One number, one word, and a phone that could call anywhere on earth from anywhere on earth, outside every radio net, outside every log, outside every ear that might be listening — including, presumably, the ears of the expedition's own crew.

I put it back exactly as I'd found it. That took longer than finding it. Then I sat on an empty rice drum in the heat and thought about what I was going to do with the fact that I now owned a piece of knowledge nobody had sold me, given me, or authorized me to have.

Here is what a sensible man does with such a fact: nothing. A sensible man finishes the count, notes his forty-three against their forty-one, hands back the clipboard, collects his pay in Wewak like a normal person, and lets the river keep its secrets. I had built an entire life on the principle that the sensible move is almost always available, and that taking it costs you nothing except, eventually, yourself.

I picked up the clipboard and kept counting.

* * *

Mara Ellingsen found me at the last crate, near dusk.

I heard her before I saw her — the particular unhurried step of someone who walks on ground she has already decided to trust — and then she was standing in the shed doorway with the last of the light behind her, holding two cups of coffee that smelled like it had been brewed from despair and recycled twice.

"You've been out here eight hours," she said. "Hargreave thinks you're slow. I think you're thorough. Which is it?"

"Yes," I said.

She handed me a cup anyway. That was the first data point about Mara Ellingsen: she didn't require conversation to perform kindness, which in my experience puts a person in roughly the top tenth of a percent of the species.

She looked at the open crates — the anvil, the drill steel, the vaults — with the flat professional gaze of someone who reads terrain the way I read freight. She didn't ask what they were. She looked at them the way you look at a word in a sentence that doesn't belong, and I watched her file it away somewhere behind her eyes, next to, I suspected, a great many other filed observations about this expedition.

"Hargreave says it's all coring equipment," she offered.

"He said meteorological, mostly."

"Mm." She sipped her terrible coffee. "We're mapping channels. Sediment loads, flow rates, gauge calibration. There is no version of that science that requires a rock drill." She said it lightly, conversationally, the way you'd remark on the weather, and then she looked at me directly for the first time, and her eyes were the color of deep water over sand, and they were doing arithmetic of their own. "You noticed the weight discrepancy in the manifest before you opened anything. I watched you lift crate 15 with your knees first."

"Old habit."

"It's a surveyor's habit," she said. "Railroad men lift with their backs and their pride. Surveyors lift with their knees and their suspicion."

I let that sit. Out on the wharf, the tide was turning, and the pilons knocked against each other in their slow wooden language.

"There are two crates under the tarp at the end," I said. "Unmanifested."

"I know. I helped load them in Lae."

That landed harder than it should have. I set the coffee down. "Helped load them."

"I carried the smaller one." She shrugged, unapologetic. "I also read the labels on the inside before the lids went on. One is more drill consumables. The other—" she paused, and for the first time since she'd walked in, something in her posture shifted, some weight redistributing, "—the other is chain and padlocks. A lot of chain. Enough for a site, not enough for cargo."

"A site," I repeated.

"For keeping people out of," she said. "Or keeping something in." She finished her coffee in one swallow, grimaced at it on principle, and looked at me again. "I'm telling you this because you counted forty-three and didn't stop counting, and because tomorrow we all get on the same boats for eleven days, and I'd rather know now whether you're the kind of man who notices things and sells them, or the kind who notices things and keeps them."

"And if I'm the first kind?"

"Then I'll know before you do anything with them," she said, "because I'll be watching. I chart rivers for a living, Iversen. People are just channels with worse manners."

She left the way she'd come, unhurried, and I sat in the failing light among the crates with her words and my clipboard and the cold little satellite phone sleeping in its false bottom, and I understood that I had just been recruited into something without anyone asking me, and that the something had at least two factions already, and that I had apparently joined hers by the simple act of being curious in front of a witness.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, which was standard, but the texture of it was new. Usually my insomnia was empty — the same gray loop, the trestle, the report, the signature that wasn't quite mine and was entirely mine. Tonight the loop had furniture in it. An anvil. A locked vault. A phone with one number and the word *urgent*. Chain, enough for a site.

I got up, took the oil lamp into the shed, and pulled the manifest's back page out where I keep my pencil notes. I added the last entries to the list I'd been building all day, and looking at the list whole, I finally saw its shape:

Two crates undeclared. Drill gear labeled as weather instruments. Vaults with locks labeled as glassware. A satellite phone hidden beneath a radio, with an escape hatch of a phone number. Chain for a site. Twelve places provisioned for nine names. And a doctor who used the word *boring* like a man holding a door shut.

None of it proved anything. That was the elegant part. Every single item, taken alone, had an innocent explanation, and I could hear Hargreave producing each one with that half-power smile. But innocence doesn't arrive in patterns. Guilt does. Guilt is the only thing in this world that consistently over-prepares, mislabels, and hides its communications, and I recognized the handwriting, because I had once signed documents written in it.

A measuring expedition measures. This one brought tools for opening the earth and containers for hiding what comes out, and a private line home for whoever needed to say *urgent* without witnesses.

I folded the page into my shirt pocket, next to the map of closed lines I'd carried for six years like a scar I'd grown fond of, and I stood in the doorway of the shed and looked at the black river.

Tomorrow at first light we'd load the boats. Nine scientists, one escort, forty-three crates, and whatever it was at the end of the channel charts that all this careful money had been sent to find — or to bury deeper.

Out on the water, something large turned over without hurrying, and the ripples came ashore a long time later, like a message sent from the far side of the dark.

I went back to my bunk and lay down with my boots on. Old habit. But from tonight on, I'd be honest about why.
