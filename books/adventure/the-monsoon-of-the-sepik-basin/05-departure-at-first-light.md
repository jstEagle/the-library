## 5. Departure at First Light

There is a particular hour on the Sepik when the river stops pretending to be water and admits it's a living thing. Just before dawn the mist comes off the surface in slow ropes, and the whole basin breathes out — warm, vegetal, faintly rotten — and if you stand in a boat with your hand on a tiller you can feel the current thinking beneath you, deciding where it wants to go today regardless of what the charts say.

I was up before that hour because I don't sleep well in places I intend to leave. Old habit. You pack your head the night before like you'd pack a bag: nothing left behind, nothing you can't carry.

The staging camp sat on a mud bank downstream of Angoram, three boats lashed to saplings and loaded to their gunwales with the expedition's ambitions. Fuel drums. Rice sacks. Crates marked INSTRUMENTS — FRAGILE that I knew for a fact contained drilling gear and a satellite phone nobody would discuss. Somewhere under all of it, presumably, were the actual instruments.

Mara Ellingsen was already awake. Of course she was. She stood at the waterline with a mug of something black and terrible, watching the current the way other people watch television — with total absorption and occasional disapproval.

"You're up," she said, without turning around. She'd identified my footsteps already. That should have worried me more than it did.

"Someone has to check the boats."

"I checked them at four."

"Then someone has to check them again, so we both feel useful."

She glanced back at me then, and there it was again — that assessing look, the one that made me feel like a contour line she hadn't finished drawing. "You re-tied the stern lines," she said. "Different knots than last night."

"Better knots."

"On purpose?"

"On habit." I crouched by the lead boat's transom and checked the engine mounts. "Knots are like handwriting. You stop choosing them after a while."

"Hm," she said, which from her seemed to mean *noted, filed, cross-referenced*. She drank her terrible coffee and watched me work, and neither of us said anything else, and it was somehow the most comfortable conversation I'd had in years.

* * *

Dr. Edmund Hargreave arrived at first light the way men arrive who have never once been late for anything that mattered to them: loudly, freshly shaved, and radiating the confidence of a man whose risks have always been paid for by someone else.

"Beautiful morning!" he announced to the camp generally, as though he had personally arranged it. He wore a canvas hat with the brim snapped down and a shirt with more pockets than fabric, and he clapped his hands together like a man starting a sporting event. "Tide's with us, weather window holds, and Okuk tells me the channels are running clean above the junction. We'll make the station in four days if the river cooperates."

"And if it doesn't?" asked Tomas.

Tomas Vidal was the youngest of the hydrologists, twenty-four or thereabouts, all elbows and enthusiasm, with a sunburn peeling off his nose in sheets. He had introduced himself to me the previous evening by asking whether the outboard on boat two was a Yamaha or a knockoff, which is, in my experience, the fastest way to a mechanic's heart.

"If the river doesn't cooperate," Hargreave said grandly, "we persuade it. That's rather the point of an expedition, isn't it?" He beamed at us all, and I noticed — the way you notice things when your job used to be noticing things — that his confidence had a seam in it. A place where it was stitched on over something else. Men who have actually been tested wear their confidence differently. Hargreave's was a costume he'd grown into, the way a boy grows into his father's coat, and it fit almost well enough that most people wouldn't see the sleeves hanging long.

Almost.

"We load in fifteen minutes," he declared. "Casper — you'll take the lead boat. Mara with you for charting. Tomas, you're with me. Ferro and the rest in the third."

Ferro. Dr. Lucia Ferro, sedimentologist, a compact woman in her fifties who had spent the entire staging period saying almost nothing and looking at everything. She nodded now, once, and went on checking the lashings on her sample cases with the thoroughness of a woman packing evidence rather than equipment.

At the time I filed that under *scientists are strange*. Later I would file it somewhere else entirely.

* * *

We pushed off into a river the color of strong tea, and within an hour I understood why Mara argued with charts.

The Sepik doesn't run so much as wander. It braids itself into a dozen channels, abandons half of them overnight, and grows islands in its sleep. The official survey — done, judging by its vintage, by men in shorts and pith helmets with a theodolite and a great deal of confidence — showed our route as a single confident blue line. The actual river offered us six options in the first two hours, four of which ended in sandbars and one of which ended, briefly, in a wall of floating vegetation that moved against the current like it had somewhere to be.

"Left channel," Mara said, not looking up from her field board. She'd been sketching since dawn, freehand, correcting the map in real time. "The right looks wider but it shoals — see the color change? Tea to milk. That's silt coming off a bar."

I looked. The right channel did look wider. It also had that pale, thick look of water dragging bottom, and a slick of foam curling along its edge in a way that meant current piling into something shallow.

"Left," I agreed, and swung the tiller.

Hargreave, in the boat behind, shouted something about the main channel being the main channel for a reason. Mara didn't even turn around. "The main channel was the main channel in 1973," she called back pleasantly. "Rivers resign."

I liked her enormously, which was inconvenient. I had a policy about liking people. It was a short policy. It consisted mostly of *don't*.

But there's a specific pleasure in working water with someone who reads it the same way you do — calling the channel before you've seen the reason for it, both of you watching the same foam-line and reaching the same conclusion at the same moment. By midmorning we'd stopped narrating entirely. She'd tap her pencil twice for left, once for right, flat palm for slow down, and I'd answer with the tiller. The boats behind us followed our wake like ducklings, and Hargreave's commentary gradually subsided into grudging silence, and the basin opened up around us — gallery forest leaning over dark water, birds lifting in white flurries, crocodiles logging on the mud banks with the patient menace of tax auditors.

It was, I will admit, the best day I'd had in years. I resented this.

* * *

We made camp that first night on a high bank that Mara chose and I confirmed — she for sightlines, me for the sound of the water, which tells you at night whether a channel is shifting. Tomas appointed himself fire-tender and cook's assistant and general morale officer, and by the time the rice was done he had extracted my life story down to approximately 1998, at which point I'd fed him a version involving a survey firm in Darwin and a disagreement about safety margins that was true in every particular except the ones that mattered.

"A railroad man," he said, delighted, poking the fire. "That explains it."

"Explains what?"

"How you handle the boat. You steer like the river's on rails. Like there's a correct line and everything else is derailing."

I turned my tin plate in my hands. "Rivers don't have rails."

"No," he said, with the serene confidence of youth, "but they have physics. Same thing, basically."

Mara, across the fire, snorted into her coffee. Tomas grinned like he'd scored a point in a game whose rules I hadn't been told.

He found his real opening the next afternoon, though, when the number-two engine began to die.

It started as a stutter — a hiccup in the rhythm every few minutes, then every few seconds, until Hargreave's boat was falling behind and he was shouting something about warranty. I waved them toward a gravel bar and we hauled out in the shallows, and Tomas had the cowling off before I'd even got my boots dry.

"Fuel," he said. "Has to be. Listen—" and he tilted his head at the carburetor like a doctor listening to a chest.

"It's not fuel." I pulled the plug wire and grounded it against the block, then pointed at the flywheel. "Spark's weak and intermittent. Look at the points."

He looked. His face fell. "Corroded."

"Corroded and gapped wrong. Someone ran this engine hard with the cover off in rain, then closed it up wet." I did not say *someone at the staging camp*, because the someone had probably been Hargreave himself, showing off for the hired help. "File, emery cloth, and about ten minutes of patience."

"I have emery cloth!" Tomas produced it from a pocket with the triumph of a magician producing a dove. "And a file. And—" he dug deeper— "a spare condenser, because I don't trust anyone."

"Tomas," I said, "you and I are going to get along."

We worked side by side while the light went amber and the mosquitoes mustered in the shade. He talked the way young men talk when they finally find an audience they trust — about his degree, about how he'd come into hydrology because his grandfather had worked water his whole life, some kind of hydraulic engineering back in Europe, dead before Tomas could remember him clearly except through tools. There was a photograph of the man in Tomas's wallet, worn soft at the corners: a heavyset figure squinting into sun, standing in front of machinery.

"He came out here, actually," Tomas said, wiping his hands on a rag. "During the war. Or after — the dates are confusing, everything from that time is confusing. Some kind of contract work. He never talked about it. My grandmother said he came back different. Quieter." He shrugged, embarrassed suddenly, as if he'd said too much. "Anyway. That's why rivers. Family tradition, sort of."

"Sort of," I said, and handed him the spark plug to gap, and said nothing else, because there was nothing useful to say. The war had left a lot of quiet men scattered through a lot of families like shrapnel nobody could locate on an X-ray. I knew something about being one of those men, and something about causing others, and I kept both knowledges where I kept everything else — packed tight, nothing left behind.

The engine caught on the second pull, smooth and clean, and Tomas let out a whoop that scattered birds off three trees, and Hargreave clapped him on the shoulder and told him he was worth his weight in fuel, and for a moment — a whole moment, maybe two — I stood on that gravel bar in the middle of nowhere with grease to my elbows and thought: *this is fine. This is just a job. Four days upriver, drop the cargo, turn around, disappear.*

I want it on the record that I believed that, more or less, right up until the evening I didn't.

* * *

The third day the river narrowed and the country changed. The forest came closer, older, and the channels braided tighter, and Mara's corrections to the chart grew more frequent and more emphatic. Twice she had us take passages that weren't passages at all according to the official map — slots between islands that appeared and vanished with the water level — and twice they saved us an hour and a grounding.

"The survey party missed this whole delta system," she said, tapping her board. "Or it formed after. Either way, half these channels are younger than my mother."

"Rivers resign," I said.

"They do." She looked up at me, and for a second neither of us did any navigating at all, which on moving water is a luxury you pay for eventually.

We paid that evening.

The storm came down out of the hills an hour before dusk — no warning but a greenish light and a smell like iron, and then the sky simply emptied. We ran for the nearest high bank and got the boats tied and tarped, and sat under a lean-to of drum covers and rope while the rain hit the river so hard it seemed to bounce. Lightning walked along the far ridge. Somewhere upstream, thunder rolled with a depth that wasn't weather.

"That's not a storm rolling," Mara said quietly, beside me. Her shoulder was nearly touching mine under the tarp; I was aware of it the way you're aware of a compass needle. "That's a bank giving way. Listen to the interval."

I listened. She was right. Between the lightning flashes, the long rumble had a texture to it — earth moving, tons of it, sliding into water somewhere above us.

"Channel's going to change below us by morning," I said.

"Channel's changing *now*." She was already reaching for her board, sketching by lightning-flash, and I sat beside her in the drumming dark and held the tarp edge down against the wind and watched her draw a river that didn't exist yet.

Around the fire-smoke smell and the wet canvas, Hargreave's voice carried from the other shelter, cheerful as ever, telling Ferro and the others about the publication schedule, the funding cycle, the *opportunity* this expedition represented. Ferro's answers were monosyllables. Tomas laughed in the right places, loyal as a dog.

Mara finished her sketch, looked at it, and tore it out and tucked it into the waterproof tube with the others. Then she said, without looking at me:

"You've done this before. Not this exactly. Something like it. Moving heavy things through bad country with people who trust you more than they should."

"Escort work," I said. "It's in the contract."

"That's not what I asked."

Lightning flashed. In the white instant I saw her profile, steady, waiting, and the river beyond her running high and brown and full of everything it was carrying downstream — silt, branches, secrets.

"I did railroads," I said. "Then I did other things. The order matters less than you'd think."

She studied me for another flash-cycle, two, three. Then she nodded slowly, the way she nodded at a channel that finally matched her chart.

"One day," she said, "you'll tell me the real order."

"Maybe."

"And maybe I'll tell you why I really argue with Hargreave about the safety margins." She pulled the tarp down against a gust. "It's not the margins."

The rain eased near midnight. I took first watch, because I always do, and sat with the river talking to itself in the dark, rearranging itself below us, and I thought about what she'd said and what she hadn't, and about the crates of drilling gear riding in the third boat, and about Dr. Ferro packing her samples like evidence.

Four days to the gauge station, Hargreave had promised.

The river, as it turned out, had other plans — and so, it would soon become clear, did somebody else. Because when the storm finally blew itself out and the moon came through ragged, I swept my eyes downstream out of pure habit, the way a man checks a mirror on a road he thinks is empty.

Far down the braided channels, where the water caught the moonlight in broken silver threads, something moved against the current.

A boat. Running dark. Coming upriver.
