## 6. The Village That Isn't on Any Chart

The village did not appear on any chart I owned, and I owned charts that other men had paid good money to make me believe were complete. It sat where the river split around a long gravel island and came back together again like a sentence that had briefly lost its nerve, on the left bank, under a stand of sago palms that looked as if they'd been holding the place up out of politeness for two hundred years.

I found it the way you find most things worth finding in the Sepik: by being wrong.

The channel I'd chosen that afternoon had been narrowing for an hour, the way channels do when they're about to embarrass you. Hargreave stood at the bow of the lead boat with his hands on his hips, radiating the confidence of a man who has read a report about a river rather than met one. Mara sat beside me in the second boat with her chart board across her knees, redrawing the channel in pencil for the third time since noon, each version less flattering to the official survey than the last.

"Your map says four meters," she said, not looking up.

"My map says a lot of things," I said. "My map was drawn by a man who probably never left Wewak."

"Then why do you keep consulting it?"

"Because it's the only thing aboard that admits it might be wrong."

She almost smiled at that. Almost. With Mara, an almost-smile was worth more than most people's full production, so I banked it and turned back to the water, which had gone from brown to the color of strong tea and was doing something at the far bend that I didn't like — a smoothness, a glassy indifference, the river's way of saying *nothing to see here* right before showing you exactly what there is to see.

"Sandbar," I said, and cut the engine, and we drifted, and the water went quiet enough that you could hear the birds arguing in the canopy about whose fault the afternoon was.

That was when I saw the smoke. Not campfire smoke — cooking smoke, steady and domestic, rising through the palms on the left bank where no chart gave anyone reason to be.

"There," Tomas said, pointing. The kid had eyes like a hawk that had been raised by sparrows; he noticed everything and assumed it all meant well.

Hargreave consulted his GPS unit, which told him, with the serene authority of all instruments, that we were in the middle of nowhere. "There's nothing marked here."

"No," I agreed. "There isn't." I restarted the engine and swung us toward the smoke. "Which is usually how you know the mapmaker never asked anybody."

* * *

The village was maybe forty houses on stilts, built of sago palm and bamboo, connected by walkways that creaked in a language older than any of us. Dogs announced us before we touched the bank, and by the time our boots hit mud, half the population had arranged itself along the walkways to watch — not hostile, not welcoming, just *watching*, the way you'd watch weather come up the river.

A man came down to meet us. Middle-aged, broad through the shoulders, wearing shorts and a shirt that had once been somebody's idea of a uniform. He looked at our boats, our crates, our rifles — because yes, we carried rifles, and no, nobody had said what they were for, which is its own kind of answer — and then he looked at me longest, which I've learned means either trouble or opportunity, and in my experience they travel together like crocodiles travel with water.

"You are going up?" he asked, in Tok Pisin, slow enough for me.

"Upriver," I said. "To the old gauge station."

Something happened to his face. I want to be precise about this, because it's the sort of thing you can miss if you're not paying attention, and I have spent my whole life paying attention to faces because they're the only maps that don't lie outright. What happened was: nothing. A deliberate nothing. He took the name of the station, folded it up, and put it away somewhere behind his eyes, and the shutters came down over the windows.

"I am Waima," he said. "You will eat with us tonight. Your boats will be safe."

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't entirely an offer. It was closer to a ruling.

* * *

They fed us fish and sago and greens from gardens I couldn't see, on a long platform open to the river, and the food was better than anything we'd carried ourselves, which is the usual joke of travel: you haul a tonne of supplies into paradise and paradise feeds you anyway.

Hargreave held court, as he does, telling stories about hydrology to people who had lived their entire lives inside the subject. To his credit — and I was beginning, grudgingly, to extend him credit in small denominations — he knew when to stop talking and listen, even if what he heard mostly got filed away as color for some future paper. Tomas charmed the children by letting them inspect the outboard motor, which they treated with the reverence due a captured god. Mara sat slightly apart, sketching, because Mara always sat slightly apart; apartness was her native country and she kept her citizenship current.

And I watched Waima.

He ate little and watched much. When Hargreave mentioned the gauge station again — casually, the way you'd mention a landmark, a lighthouse, a thing without weight — the conversation around him didn't stop, but it changed key. Subtle. A half-tone drop. The old woman ladling fish moved a step further from him. A younger man who'd been laughing at Tomas's motor suddenly found something fascinating in his own hands.

Nobody said a word. That was the thing. In my experience silence comes in flavors, and this one had a taste I knew from the wrong side of a career: the silence of people who have decided, collectively and without discussion, that a certain subject is a hole in the floor, and everyone will walk around it, and no one will look down.

Later, when the plates were cleared and Hargreave had retired to argue with his own instruments, an old man came onto the platform.

I'd seen him earlier at the edge of things, sitting outside the largest house, not joining the meal, not excluded either — positioned, the way elders get positioned, at the exact point where presence becomes authority. He walked slowly, leaning on a stick of ironwood worn smooth as glass, and he sat down across from me without ceremony, the way you sit with someone you've already decided about.

Waima brought him betel nut and then, notably, stayed. So did two or three others, drifting to the edges of the platform until we had an audience arranged in a loose crescent, and I understood that whatever was about to happen was not private. It was, in fact, possibly the entire reason we'd been invited to stay the night.

The old man chewed his betel and spat red against the boards and looked at me for a long time.

"You are not a government man," he said finally. It wasn't a question either. This family apparently didn't ask them.

"No."

"You are not police. Not church. Not company."

"No."

"What are you, then?"

I considered the honest answers. *Disgraced.* *Unemployed.* *A man who owns no address.* I settled on the truest one available. "I move boats," I said. "I read water. That's all."

He nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something. "The others," he said, tilting his chin at Hargreave's tent, "they measure the river."

"They try."

"And you?" His eyes were yellowed and sharp and missed nothing, including, I suspected, the rifle across my knees. "What do you do with the water?"

"I listen to it," I said. "Mostly it tells me where not to go."

Something moved behind his face then — not a smile, but the ancestor of one. He spat again, and looked out at the river, dark now, running full and indifferent under the first stars.

"The white men who came before you," he said, "also listened to the water. But they did not like what it said. So they made the water say something else."

Mara had appeared at the edge of the crescent at some point — I hadn't heard her, which should have been impossible given the creaking walkways, and told me something about how quietly she moved through the world. She sat down beside me without asking, chart board still under her arm, and said nothing. Good instincts, both of us. You don't interrupt a door opening.

"The gauge station," I said carefully. "Do you know it?"

The change in him was different from the change in Waima. Waima had put the name away. The old man took it out instead, turned it over, examined it like a stone he'd been carrying in his pocket for sixty years.

"We knew it when it had another name," he said. "Before the war, it had a company name. After the war, it had a government name. Now it has your name, I suppose, since you are going there." He shrugged, a small dry movement. "Names are cheap. The place stays the same."

"What was the company?" Mara asked. Her voice was level, professional, and I could feel the tension in it anyway, the cartographer's hunger for coordinates, for facts, for things that could be pinned to paper.

The old man looked at her for a while. Then at me. Then he made his decision, and I watched it happen, and I want to record that it cost him something, because decisions like that always do.

"The river used to run there," he said, and pointed — not at the main channel, but at a line of country south of the village, a low green wall of forest with a certain regularity to it that I'd noticed coming in and hadn't liked. Regular vegetation means regular ground. Regular ground near a river means works of man. "Where the tall trees grow in rows, that was the channel. My father fished it as a boy. Then the company wanted the water for their workings, up at the hill, and the water was in the wrong place. So the men came with machines, and the water was moved."

"Moved," Mara repeated. "The whole channel?"

"The whole channel." He said it simply, the way you'd say *it rained*. "Rivers can be moved. Everyone knows this. What people forget is what the moving costs." He chewed, spat, watched the red juice fall. "The laborers who dug the new course — they were counted like cargo. Loaded and unloaded. Some seasons the company wrote down how many arrived. They did not write down how many stayed."

"Stayed," I said.

"In the ground," he said. "In the banks. In the new channel they dug." He looked at me then, directly, and his voice didn't rise at all, which made it worse. "The river was moved by men who counted bodies as ballast."

Nobody spoke. Down at the water a night bird called, once, twice, and got no answer. Somewhere behind us a child laughed and was hushed.

Mara's hand had gone still on her chart board. I could see her wanting — physically wanting, the way you want air — to ask names, dates, numbers, the raw material of maps and tribunals. She was smart enough not to. There are questions you ask a man and questions you let him answer, and the difference between them is whether you ever hear the truth.

"How many laborers?" she asked instead, softly. Not *how many died*. I could have kissed her.

The old man considered. "My father said the company's book had a column for it," he said. "Expended, the word was. Like fuel." He smiled then, briefly, and it was the worst smile I have ever been shown, because there was no bitterness in it at all, only a very old, very finished grief. "We remember the number differently. We remember it in families. Every family here is missing a branch. That is our arithmetic."

* * *

They gave us a house for the night — a guest house, raised high, with woven walls that breathed. Hargreave slept like a man with a clean conscience, which I was beginning to suspect was less innocence than practice. Tomas fell asleep mid-sentence, the way the young do, still talking about the old man's story and what it might mean for sediment cores.

Mara and I ended up on the platform outside, because neither of us could sleep, and neither of us pretended otherwise.

"He lied to us," she said after a while. "Hargreave. 'Heritage recovery.' He knew."

"Maybe."

"Casper."

"Maybe he knew a piece of it," I said. "Men like Hargreave know exactly as much as keeps the money flowing and not one gram more. It's a survival adaptation. Very common in the species."

She was quiet. Out on the river, the water ran on, doing its ancient work over ground that remembered everything.

"You heard what he said," she murmured. "About the channel. If the river was diverted, the sediment record will show it. It'll scream it. Discontinuous stratigraphy, reworked gravels, fill horizons—" She stopped herself, laughed shortly. "Listen to me. A man tells us about a massacre and I'm excited about the stratigraphy."

"It's not excitement," I said. "It's armor. You put on the science so you can carry the rest of it." I knew this because I'd done the same thing with engineering for twenty years. A collapsed trestle is a load calculation until three o'clock in the morning, when it becomes eleven names, and then you need the load calculation back very badly.

She looked at me sideways. "And what do you put on?"

"Boats," I said. "I put on boats."

She almost smiled again. Almost. Then she unclipped a pencil from her board and began, in the starlight, to draw — not the channel, not the village, but the line the old man had pointed out, the row of tall trees south of us, the ghost of the old course. She drew it as a dashed line, tentative, the way you draw something you haven't proven yet but already believe.

"This goes on no official chart," she said. "Not yet."

"No," I agreed. "Not yet."

And I want to note, for the record I would one day wish existed, that I said *not yet* and not *never*. Even then, some part of me — the part I'd spent years drowning in borrowed names and odd jobs — had already signed off on where this was going. I just hadn't told the rest of me yet.

We sat until the fire burned low and the mosquitoes drove even Mara inside. Before she went, she paused at the doorway.

"The elder," she said. "Why tell us? Why now? Strangers with guns and cargo, and he just... opens the door."

I thought about it. I thought about the crescent of listeners, the deliberate seating, the meal offered before questions were earned. I thought about Waima staying close, guarding the moment like a coal.

"Because we asked the right question," I said. "Or because nobody's asked in sixty years. Or because—" I gestured vaguely upriver, toward the dark water and the dark hills and the station waiting at the end of them. "Because he knows where we're going. And he wanted us to arrive knowing."

Mara nodded slowly, and went inside, and I sat alone on the platform a while longer, listening to the river move over ground that remembered.

It was near dawn when the boy came — one of the children who'd admired Tomas's motor, sent barefoot and breathless from the big house. He tugged my sleeve and pointed upstream and said words I understood perfectly and would spend the rest of this story paying for:

"A boat came in the night. It did not stop. It did not show lights. It went up, toward the hill."

Toward the gauge station.

I stood on the platform watching the gray light come up over the water, doing the arithmetic I hated — speed, current, head start — and the numbers came out bad, and the day hadn't even started yet.
