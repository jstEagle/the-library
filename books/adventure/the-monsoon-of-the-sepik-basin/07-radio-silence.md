## 7. Radio Silence

The Sepik does not have a schedule. It has moods. I had learned this the way you learn most things on a river — by being wrong about them first.

We were three days out of the staging camp, camped on a mudbank the color of old tea, when Hargreave decided it was time to call home. He announced it the way he announced everything, as though the river had been waiting for his convenience.

"Check-in at eighteen hundred," he said, tapping his watch. "Wewak expects us on the hour. Keeps the office happy."

The office. I'd gathered by then that Dr. Edmund Hargreave had an office the way a bishop has a diocese — something he referred to often and protected fiercely, and which existed mostly in his own mind. The expedition was his, the funding was his, and the schedule was his, and if the Sepik disagreed with any of these facts, that was the river's failure, not his.

I said nothing. Saying nothing is a skill. I'd spent years perfecting it, the way other men perfect golf.

The evening came down the way it always did on the basin — fast, total, like a lid closing. By half past five the light was gone and the cicadas had taken over the airwaves, broadcasting at a volume that made me wonder why we bothered with electronics at all. Mosquitoes arrived in delegations. Tomas was tinkering with the second boat's carburetor by lantern light, humming something Scandinavian and mournful. Mara sat apart with her charts, redrawing the channel for the fourth time since Wewak, because the river kept changing its mind and she refused to let it win.

Hargreave set up the radio on an ammunition crate, uncoiled the antenna wire with the ceremony of a man unveiling a monument to himself, and began the ritual of modern exploration: pressing a switch and hoping.

"Wewak Base, Wewak Base, this is Sepik Survey One. Do you copy? Over."

Static. The thick kind, the kind that sounds like the inside of a shell.

He tried again. Adjusted a knob. Tried again, louder, as though volume were the problem — as though Wewak were simply hard of hearing rather than four hundred kilometers of drowned country away.

"Sepik Survey One calling Wewak Base. Scheduled check-in, eighteen hundred hours. Over."

More static. Somewhere in it, briefly, there was a sound that might have been a voice or might have been a bird dying, and then nothing.

Hargreave frowned at the set the way a man frowns at a waiter who's brought the wrong wine. "Equipment," he pronounced. "These sets are rubbish in the humidity. Condensation in the finals, probably."

"Probably," I said.

Mara looked up from her charts. She had one of those faces that didn't say much but filed everything, and I could see her filing this. "You checked in two days ago without trouble," she said. "Same weather."

"Weather isn't constant, Ms. Ellingsen."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "Neither is equipment."

Tomas looked up from his carburetor, wiping his hands on a rag that had started life white and now belonged to the swamp. "In my experience," he offered, "radios fail either all at once or not at all. Gradual failure is usually the operator." He said it cheerfully, the way only a twenty-four-year-old can deliver bad news to a superior and survive it.

Hargreave gave him a look that would have curdled milk in a kinder climate. "Thank you, Tomas. Your expertise in hydrology extends to electronics, does it?"

"I fixed my grandfather's set every winter for ten years. He never forgave me for being right about the aerial."

I almost smiled. Almost. There was a time — and I want to be honest here, because honesty is the one luxury I can still afford — when I would have laughed out loud and poured myself into the argument, because arguing about radios is a fine way to spend an evening and costs nothing. But I'd noticed something, and noticing things is what they used to pay me for, back when I had a name worth putting on documents.

The static was too clean.

That sounds like nonsense, so let me explain it the way I'd explain it to a client, if I still had clients. A radio that fails from moisture fails messily. The signal degrades, warbles, drops in and out, picks up ghosts — other stations bleeding through, whistles, harmonics, the whole haunted house of the shortwave bands. What we were getting was different. It was a flat, even hiss, uniform across the band, like a blanket laid over everything. That's what you get when the signal isn't arriving at all. When the wire that's supposed to be catching it out of the sky isn't catching anything, because it isn't there.

I didn't say any of this. Instead I finished my coffee, which Okuk had packed despite my instructions, because Okuk believed instructions were suggestions addressed to someone else, and I watched Hargreave pack the set away with the satisfied air of a man who has diagnosed the problem and scheduled its solution for a more convenient day.

"We'll try again at dawn," he said. "If it's still playing up, we'll troubleshoot properly."

* * *

Dawn on the Sepik comes up gray and sweating, mist lying on the water in long ropes until the sun burns it off around eight. I was up before it, which was normal. Sleep and I had been estranged for years; we met occasionally, awkwardly, like relatives at a funeral, and parted with relief.

I told myself I was up early to check the boats. This was even partly true. The third boat had been taking water overnight — a slow seepage around the shaft log that I'd stuffed with grease and hoped at, and hope is not a marine sealant — and I wanted to bail her before the bilge became a swimming pool.

But the truth, the part I didn't say out loud even to myself, was that I wanted to look at the antenna.

Here's what you need to know about me, or at least the relevant part: I spent eleven years building and maintaining infrastructure across some of the meanest country on earth, and when that career ended — abruptly, publicly, with a report and a hearing and a name I stopped using — I discovered that you don't stop seeing systems just because you've stopped being paid to. A trestle, a road, a radio mast: they're all the same animal. Load paths, weak points, the places where things fail first. I couldn't turn it off. Some men leave the priesthood and still cross themselves. I look at a guy-wire and calculate tension.

So while the others slept — Hargreave snoring with the confidence of a man whose problems were all condensation, Tomas curled like a comma, Mara already gone from her fly, because she rose early too, though neither of us acknowledged the coincidence — I walked the bank to where the antenna wire ran up into a pandanus tree.

The set itself lived in a rubberized case, and the lead ran from the case along the ground, up the trunk of the pandanus, and out along a branch to a longwire strung between trees, maybe thirty meters of copper-clad steel doing its quiet work of pulling voices out of the ionosphere.

Except it wasn't.

I saw it before I reached the tree, and I stood very still for a moment, the way you do when the bush goes quiet and you haven't yet worked out why.

The wire came up the trunk and ended. Just — ended. Below the branch, at chest height, exactly where a man standing on the ground could reach it comfortably. The upper length was gone. Not fallen, not sagging, not torn loose by wind or a passing crocodile's tail or any of the hundred accidents a river invents daily. Gone. And where it ended, the remaining wire was cut.

I crouched and looked at it the way I used to look at fractured rail. Clean cut, single stroke, sharp blade. No fraying, no repeated hacking, no sign of an animal. And here was the detail that moved it out of accident entirely and into intention: whoever cut it had taken the loose end with them. Thirty meters of wire doesn't walk off. Wind doesn't coil it up and pocket it. You cut an antenna to silence a radio. You take the wire so nobody splices it back together and starts asking how long it had been silent.

Because that was the question, wasn't it. The check-in two days ago had gone through — Hargreave had logged it himself, smug about it, punctuality being next to godliness in his private church. Which meant the wire had been whole two days ago. Which meant someone had come into our camp, in the night, within reach of sleeping men, and cut it.

Or — and this was the version I liked even less — someone had done it earlier in the day, while we were all on the water, and had known exactly which tree, exactly which wire, exactly what it was for. Which meant they knew our rig. Knew our habits. Possibly knew our route.

I looked at the mud around the base of the tree. Rain had come through in the night, a brief hammering squall that had turned everything to soup and then left it to set like pudding, and footprints in pudding keep beautifully. There were prints, all right. Ours, dozens of them, going everywhere, because we were six people living on a mudbank and tidiness was a rumor. But near the tree, half-erased by the rain and older than ours by hours, there was a single line of prints approaching from downstream and leaving the same way. Bare feet. Long stride. Someone who moved through this country the way I moved through train yards — without wasted motion, without noise, without wanting to be seen.

I stood there a long time. Long enough for the mist to start burning off and the first kingfisher to start its morning argument with the world.

Here's the thing about dread. People think it arrives with noise — drums, gunfire, the works. It doesn't. Dread arrives quietly, ahead of the thing itself, the way cold air comes down a valley before the storm. It's the body knowing before the mind consents to know. I'd felt it twice before in my life: once on a bridge in the highlands, an hour before the span that killed four men sang a note I didn't understand until later; and once in a hearing room in Port Moresby, when a lawyer smiled at me a half-second before he destroyed me. Both times the feeling had been right. Both times I'd ignored it, because ignoring it was easier and because I was, at bottom, a man who preferred comfortable lies to inconvenient truths.

I was forty-one years old and I was done with that.

I walked back into camp carrying the cut end of the wire like evidence, which is what it was.

* * *

They gathered the way people do around bad news — slowly, reluctantly, pretending to be busy until curiosity beat dignity. Hargreave last of all, emerging from his fly with his hair flattened on one side and his authority already bristling.

"That's sabotage," Mara said, before anyone else spoke. She took the wire from me and examined the cut end, turning it in the light. "Clean blade. Single pass."

"It's a broken aerial," Hargreave said. "Wire fatigues. Crocodiles drag lines off—"

"Crocodiles don't use knives, Doctor. And they don't steal the evidence." She handed it back to me. Her eyes came up to mine and stayed there, and I understood that she had already worked through everything I had, probably faster, and was watching to see whether I'd say it out loud.

"The wire was intact two days ago," I said. "Your check-in went through. So it was cut sometime in the last forty-eight hours, by someone on foot, barefoot, coming from downstream." I turned the cut end over in my fingers. "And they took the rest of the wire with them."

Silence. The river made its noises behind us — water over a snag, birds, the endless wet breathing of the basin — and none of it helped.

"That's absurd," Hargreave said, but his voice had changed key. Authority is mostly pitch, and his had dropped. "Who would cut our aerial? We're a scientific survey. We're nobody."

"We're not nobody," Mara said quietly. "We're a survey that keeps missing its check-ins, in a place where somebody once went to a great deal of trouble to make sure nothing was ever found." She looked at Hargreave. "You know more about who funded this dig than you've told us. Maybe it's time you thought carefully about who else knows."

Hargreave's face did something complicated — a small civil war fought entirely in the muscles around his mouth. I'd seen that face before, on men holding ledgers. I filed it away with the footprints.

"Paranoia," he said finally. "It's the humidity. It gets into people worse than the radios." But he said it to the river, not to us, and he didn't meet anyone's eyes, and when he turned away he was rubbing the back of his neck the way men do when the collar has suddenly gotten tight.

Tomas crouched by the tree, reading the prints with a young man's quick fascination. "Barefoot. Big. Coming from downstream and going back." He glanced up at me. "Could be a villager."

"Could be," I said. Villagers walked these banks, fished these channels, and most of them would sooner touch a spirit stone than another man's property. But there was another possibility I didn't say, because saying it would make it more real: that somewhere behind us, between us and Wewak, someone had decided our expedition was worth watching. Worth following. Worth silencing — quietly, professionally, one clean cut at a time — before whatever we were hunting could be reported home.

I spliced the wire. It took twenty minutes with a knife, a lighter, and a length of scavenged copper from Tomas's parts box, and when it was done the radio crackled back to life with a chorus of Indonesian pop music bleeding in from some distant coast, proof of nothing except that the sky was still there. Hargreave attempted the check-in anyway. Static answered, flat and total, and he shut the set off with a snap and declared we'd raise Wewak from higher ground tomorrow, and everyone pretended to believe him, because pretending is cheaper than the alternative.

Mara found me at the boats while I was bailing the third one for the second time that morning.

"You knew last night," she said. Not accusing. Checking.

"I suspected. Knowing requires proof, and proof requires—" I gestured at the general catastrophe of the world.

"Requires someone listening." She crouched on the gunwale, forearms on knees, watching the water run past brown and patient. "My father fished the Hardangerfjord. He said the sea tells you everything, if you ask it politely. Rivers too, I think." She nodded upstream, toward the headwaters, toward the gauge station and whatever waited there. "This one's been trying to tell us something since Wewak. The maps that lie. The channels that don't match. And now this."

"And now this," I agreed.

She looked at me then, straight on, and hers were the palest eyes I'd seen in the basin, gray-green like the water over gravel, and they missed nothing. "You're not frightened," she said.

It wasn't flattery. It was diagnosis, and it was accurate, and it unsettled me more than the cut wire had. Because the truth was I wasn't frightened — not yet — and that frightened me, in a distant, academic way. Fear is a survival instrument. A man who's stopped reading his own gauges is a man headed for a collapsed trestle.

"Not yet," I said. "Ask me again tonight."

She almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile she'd given me, and I found I'd been waiting for it without admitting it, which was its own small alarm bell, ringing somewhere far below the waterline.

That night we anchored midstream instead of camping on the bank — my suggestion, dressed up as mosquito avoidance, which was even true. I took first watch. The moon came up late and thin, and the river ran silver and black, and somewhere downstream, in the dark, I imagined a man with a knife walking our route ahead of us, patient as the water, cutting the wires of the world one clean stroke at a time.

At midnight, far off in the direction we were headed, I heard an engine.

Not a village engine — not the puttering longboats that stitched the basin together. Something bigger, deeper, running slow and steady against the current, in the middle of the night, with no lights showing.

I stood in the bow and listened until it faded, and I did not wake the others, because there was nothing yet to say that wouldn't sound like madness.

But I marked the bearing. And I did not sleep.
