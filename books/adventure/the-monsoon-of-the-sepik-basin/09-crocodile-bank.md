## 9. Crocodile Bank

There is a particular kind of silence a river makes at night when it has decided not to kill you yet. It isn't peace. Peace implies an ending. This is more like a landlord who hasn't come to collect.

I was thinking about that in the stern of the second boat, watching the black water slide past the running lights we weren't supposed to have on. Hargreave had ordered them doused at dusk — "we don't advertise" — which was the first sensible thing he'd said since Wewak, and I'd obeyed it the way I obey most orders: technically, with reservations. We ran instead on the faint green wash of Mara's chart light and the phosphorescence the river itself provided, little cold sparks where the bow broke the surface, as if the Sepik were trying to tell us something in a language made of dying plankton.

Tomas sat beside me with his hands around a thermos he wasn't drinking from. The boy — I kept calling him a boy, though he was probably twenty-four and had published more than I ever would — had spent the afternoon elbow-deep in the outboard with me, learning why you never trust a fuel line that looks fine. He had grease to the elbows and a smear across one cheekbone like war paint applied by someone with no artistic convictions, and he was happy. There's a species of happiness that only exists in people who haven't yet learned what rivers cost.

"Casper," he said, "the way you bled that carburetor — where'd you learn that?"

"Railroads."

"Railroads have carburetors?"

"Railroads have everything," I said. "They just call it by different names so the accountants can't find it."

He laughed, and the laugh went out over the water and didn't come back, and I remember thinking: file that away. Not because anything was wrong, but because happy moments on this river were going to be scarce enough that I wanted receipts.

Ahead, the lead boat's single shaded lamp winked twice — Hargreave's signal for *channel check*. Mara stood up in our bow, chart board braced against her hip, and studied the water ahead of us with that expression she wore when arguing with geography. She'd been doing it for three days. The official charts said the channel bent left around a sandbar; the river disagreed, having moved the bar sometime during the last government administration. Mara had redrawn it from soundings taken off the moving boats, which is either very good science or very bad seamanship depending on whether you survive it.

"Bank's migrated," she called back to me. "The old cut's silted. We go wide right, along the far bank."

"The far bank," I said, "is the crocodile bank."

"The near bank is the crocodile bank too. They're crocodiles, Casper, not real estate agents. They don't hold title."

She had me there. That was the trouble with Mara Ellingsen: she had me there about once an hour, and each time it landed somewhere I hadn't armored.

* * *

We went wide right.

You should understand what a night crossing means on the middle Sepik. By day the river is braided — channels splitting and rejoining around islands of cane and silt, some of them here this morning and gone by Thursday. At night all of that becomes rumor. You navigate by the sound of water over bars, by the smell of mud versus vegetation, by the way current wrinkles under moonlight that may or may not be present. The locals do it without thinking, the way you walk your own hallway in the dark. I did it with thinking, lots of it, and a lifetime of reading water that started with culverts and ended — I'd hoped — nowhere near here.

Hargreave took the lead boat wide, exactly as instructed, because Hargreave always followed instructions that came with confidence attached. Confidence was the only cargo he personally handled. Everything else he delegated.

The problem arrived as problems on rivers do: quietly, from underneath.

It was a log — a whole tree, actually, root ball up like a drowned fist, riding just awash where the new channel bent. The kind of thing Mara's soundings should have caught and might have caught if the echo had returned anything but mud-mud-mud-log, and if anyone aboard had been listening for *log* rather than *bar*. I saw it late. Later than I'd have liked. A paler seam in the black, a place where the phosphorescence broke wrong.

"Hargreave — hard left! Log, hard left!"

His boat swung. Too slow, too wide, and the current — which had been helping us all day and now remembered its other job — took the stern and walked it sideways into the thing. I heard the crack of the hull finding wood, not a splintering crack but a deep structural complaint, the sound a boat makes when it files for divorce. Then the lead boat's lamp swung wildly, and there was shouting, and then there was water coming over their gunwale in the unhurried way water does, as though it had all the time in the world, which it does.

Our boat came alongside chaos. In the green wash I could see them: Hargreave clinging to the console with the dignity of a man who believed drowning was negotiable. Two of the local crew already in the water, swimming for the near bank with the calm of men who had grown up being lied to by rivers. And Tomas — Tomas was not swimming. Tomas was tangled. Something had him — a net line, a rope off the swamped gear, something — and every time he kicked for the surface the thing pulled him back down, and the phosphorescence showed me his face for half a second, mouth open, eyes enormous, and then the river closed over him like a book.

Now. Here is what I know about crocodile water, and I know it the way you know things you've paid for. Salties hunt at night along the banks, in the shallows, at the seams between current and slack. A man splashing in open channel is mostly noise to them, mostly ignorable. But a man struggling — a man making that irregular wounded-animal commotion — that registers. And the bank was forty meters off, and the water between here and there was exactly the temperature and depth they prefer, and every second Tomas spent thrashing was a second he spent becoming interesting.

I didn't think. Thinking is for afterward; it's how I justify things to myself at two in the morning, which is a service I provide regularly. My body simply did what bodies do when trained long enough by other people's emergencies: I stripped nothing, hesitated over nothing, and went in.

The water hit like a fist full of cold silk. I came up beside the tangle — it was the anchor line, snagged over a submerged branch with Tomas's leg looped through the bight — and I did the thing you're supposed to do and almost nobody does: I went down instead of up. Down to him, hand finding his collar, my knife out and sawing through the line above the knot rather than fighting the knot, because knots are patient and knives are not. His eyes found mine underwater. Even down there, even in that brown-black murk lit by nothing, I could see the question in them. *Are you real?*

"Not yet," I told him, which came out as bubbles, and meant nothing to anybody, and meant everything to me.

The line parted. I shoved him up ahead of me, breaking surface behind him, and got an arm across his chest before he could do the thing drowning men do, which is climb you like a ladder into the sky. "Stop fighting me or we both stop," I said, and he stopped, because there is a register of voice that bypasses language entirely and goes straight to the mammal brain, and I have been told I own that register. I've never been proud of it. It's the voice of a man who has pulled people out of things before, and the fact that I have pulled people out of things before is not a happy biography.

We swam. Not for the near bank — the crocodile bank, the one with the cane and the slides worn smooth into the mud, each slide a signature, each signature a promise. We swam for the boat, or what was left of it riding low with the bow wedged on the log, and the crew hauled Tomas over the gunwale like a sack of wet rice, and then me, and then we were all breathing in that ragged communal way that people breathe after the river has shown them its teeth and, for reasons of its own, declined to use them.

Hargreave looked at me across the flooded deck. Water to his knees, dignity to his ankles.

"You went in," he said. "At night. Into known crocodile water. For a geologist."

"He was closer than the crocodiles."

"That's not—" He stopped. Recalibrated. Hargreave recalibrating was like watching a large ship decide to be a smaller one. "That's not escort work."

"No," I agreed. "Escort work is standing on the bank explaining what went wrong."

Mara's boat came alongside ours, and she climbed across without asking permission, which was characteristic, and put two fingers to Tomas's throat, which was professional, and then looked at me — soaked, shaking slightly, pretending not to be — with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. It wasn't gratitude exactly. Gratitude is simple. This had architecture.

"You dove straight to him," she said. "Everyone goes up. You went down."

"It's where he was."

"You cut the line above the knot." Her eyes were doing the thing they did with charts, taking measurements of something she intended to keep. "Who taught you that?"

"A river," I said. "A different one. It charged tuition."

* * *

We grounded both boats on the near bank anyway — the crocodile bank, yes, but crocodiles respect fire, and fire respects dry wood, and there was plenty of that. The local crew built a blaze the size of a small religion while the rest of us salvaged. The lead boat was done; she'd taken the log below the waterline and her engine was a museum piece now. We redistributed cargo, redistributed people, and redistributed blame, the last of which Hargreave attempted to conduct formally until Mara suggested, pleasantly, that the log had not been on any chart, and that charts were her department, and that if he wished to assign fault he was welcome to begin with the colonial survey office, dead these sixty years.

Tomas sat wrapped in a blanket by the fire, shivering in the way you shiver when the cold is only half of it. I brought him the thermos, finally opened, sweet tea gone lukewarm, and sat down next to him without ceremony. Sitting with people is a skill. Most people talk instead.

"I saw it," he said eventually. "Underwater. Just for a second — a shape. Big. Moving along the bottom like it owned the deed." His hands shook around the cup. "It didn't even turn. Like I wasn't worth turning for."

"Best news you'll get all week."

"How do you do it?" He turned to look at me, and the firelight made him younger and older at once. "The water. The engines. Tonight. Okuk said you were an escort. Escorts don't—"

"Okuk says a lot of things. It's how he charges."

"But you knew. Down there, you knew exactly what to do, like you'd done it a hundred times." He wasn't accusing me. That was the strange part. He was hungry for it — for competence, for someone on this expedition who was what the manifest claimed. "Who are you, Casper?"

I watched the fire. Somewhere out in the dark, the river continued its monologue, and beneath it, fainter, something else — a shape sliding off a mudbank with a sound like a heavy book closing.

"I'm a man who used to certify things," I said. "Bridges, mainly. Trestles. You sign your name saying a structure will hold, and then one day it holds exactly as well as you signed it would, and everyone acts surprised." I handed him back the thermos. "After that, names get heavy. So I travel light."

He absorbed that with the seriousness of the young, who still believe that answers are given whole. Then, because he was twenty-four and not stupid: "The trestle. Was it your fault?"

The honest answer was *yes and no*, which is the answer to most questions worth asking. What I said was: "The river moved. I signed for where it used to be. Somewhere between those two facts, eleven men died, and a company survived, and I got to leave town." I shrugged, and the shrug cost more than I let it show. "So now I read water for a living. All of it. Every kind."

Tomas nodded slowly, and then did the thing that undid me a little, the thing I hadn't priced in: he put his hand on my forearm, grease and all, and squeezed once. No speech. Just pressure. Acknowledgment between two people who had both, in different decades, been underwater.

Across the fire, Mara was re-drawing the channel by firelight, working fast, committing tonight's corrections to paper while they were still true. She glanced up at us — at Tomas's hand, at my face — and went back to her work, but I caught the corner of her mouth doing something unauthorized.

Hargreave stood apart, at the edge of the firelight, radio handset in hand, calling Wewak on the scheduled frequency. Static answered him, patient and total. He tried again. Static again. He lowered the handset and stood looking upriver into the dark for a long moment, and I watched him decide — visibly, the way a man decides to wear a coat — that it was equipment failure, atmospheric interference, nothing, tomorrow's problem.

I had spent enough years around signals to know static from silence. Static is noise. Silence is a decision.

But that was tomorrow's problem too, and the fire was warm, and Tomas was alive and asleep within minutes, snoring with the shameless vigor of the recently rescued. Mara moved down the bank to sit beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, neither of us making an issue of it.

"You went down," she said again, quietly. "I keep thinking about that."

"There wasn't time to go up."

"That's not what I mean." She drew her knees up, chin on them, firelight moving on her face. "Most people, in that water, would have grabbed for the surface. Instinct. You went toward the drowning part. You've done this before, Casper. More than once. And whatever it was you did before — the railroads, the bridges, the name you don't use — you're good at it. Genuinely, dangerously good." She turned her head, and her eyes held mine with that surveyor's steadiness. "I'm a cartographer. I notice where things really are. Including people."

"And where am I, then? On your map?"

"Uncharted," she said. "Which is my favorite kind of country."

Out in the dark, the river said nothing, which is how it says everything. And downstream — I wouldn't confirm it until morning, when we found the antenna wire cut clean as a surgeon's stitch — something had already begun deciding what we would be allowed to hear.

But that night there was only the fire, and the boy breathing, and the woman beside me mapping the one territory I'd spent ten years keeping blank, and the slow cold knowledge settling into me like silt: the river had spared us tonight for its own reasons, and rivers always send a bill.
