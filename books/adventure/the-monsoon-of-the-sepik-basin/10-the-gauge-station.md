## 10. The Gauge Station

The river told us we were close before we saw it. That's the thing about water — it keeps a diary. Two kilometers below the station the current went strange, braiding out into threads that couldn't decide where they belonged, and I knew we were entering water that had been argued with. Natural rivers don't hesitate. They commit. This one hedged, and hedging water means engineering, and engineering in the Sepik Basin in the year of our Lord whatever-it-was meant somebody had wanted something moved and hadn't cared what got moved with it.

"Half a klick," I said, and killed the engine to a murmur.

Hargreave stood in the bow of the lead boat like a man arriving at his own coronation, binoculars up, khaki pressed into creases that the humidity had been trying to negotiate out of him for six days. "There. The tower. You see it, Iversen?"

I saw it. A lattice of rusted steel rising out of the pandanus, the old gauge tower, the kind the colonial water boards had bolted up every forty miles like stitches in a wound. On my closed-line map it was a pencil cross and a date — 1953, decommissioned. On the contract it was a dot with coordinates and the word *delivery*. On Mara's charts it was a small careful X with three question marks beside it, because Mara did not believe in dots.

What I didn't see was anything else. No smoke. No laundry line. No radio mast. A station that had checked in on schedule for three weeks does not go quiet and leave its antenna standing, and a station with nine people in it does not fail to put a single tin cup on a bank to catch the morning sun.

"Something's wrong," Mara said. She was beside me at the tiller, which she had annexed sometime around day two without asking and which I had permitted without asking her why. Her binoculars were cheaper than Hargreave's and better used. "No movement. No birds on the roof."

"Birds?"

"Swiftlets nest under the eaves of every structure on this river. If people had been coming and going, there'd be droppings, noise, a mess. That roof is too clean." She lowered the glasses. "Casper. That roof is *clean*."

There it was. My name, in her mouth, without the ironic distance she usually kept on it like a coat she'd lend but not give. Six days on the water had worn a groove between us, and I noticed I'd started standing in it.

"Could be maintenance," Tomas offered from the engine well, where he was perpetually elbow-deep in our second boat's carburetor, which he had named Gerald. "Could be they cleaned up for—"

"Tomas," Hargreave said, not unkindly, "not everything requires an explanation yet."

That was Hargreave's whole method in a sentence. Not everything requires an explanation *yet*. He was a man who believed explanations were a resource to be spent carefully, like fuel, and he'd been rationing his since Wewak. I'd catalogued it and filed it and said nothing, because my contract said *escort*, not *audit*, and because a man with my history learns to keep his observations where they can't be subpoenaed.

"Approach from downstream," I said. "Slow. Mara, keep us in the deep channel until the last two hundred meters. If anyone's watching, I'd rather they watch us arrive than watch us think about arriving."

"And if no one's watching?" Hargreave asked.

"Then we've lost nothing but a little time, and time is the only thing on this river I've never been able to buy."

He almost smiled. Almost. Hargreave's smiles were like the station's radio signal — reported to exist, never personally observed.

We came in slow, engine at idle, the boats sliding through water the color of strong tea. The bank rose gently to a shelf of gravel and clay, and above it the station resolved itself piece by piece, and each piece was worse than the last.

* * *

The gauge tower still stood, but the instrument shed beneath it had been opened the way a man opens an oyster he doesn't care to eat — prised, gutted, discarded. The door hung on one hinge. Through it I could see shelving stripped bare, papers scattered like molted feathers, and a workbench with a vice still clamping a piece of pipe nobody had bothered to remove, which told me the ransackers had been in a hurry or had been interrupted, and I was not yet sure which possibility I preferred.

Hargreave was ashore before the boat touched gravel, which was stupid, and I said so.

"Dr. Hargreave—"

"I am the expedition lead, Iversen."

"And I'm the man whose job description begins with keeping you alive long enough to file your reports. Wait for the bank to be walked."

He waited, which surprised me, and I walked the bank, which did not. Old habit. You read ground the way you read water — for what it carries and what it refuses to. The gravel told me boots, several sets, recent — the edges of the prints still crisp, the clay between them not yet skinned over by drying. Two days, maybe three. The prints went up from the landing, into the station, and out again toward the treeline, and one set came back out and did not return, and that set was dragged on the left side, and I stood over it for a long moment before I called the others ashore.

Mara came up beside me and looked down at the dragged print and did not ask what it meant, because she'd already read it. That was the thing about Mara. She read everything — channels, sediment, men — and she read it the same way: patiently, suspiciously, with a pencil behind her ear and a refusal to accept the first story the ground offered.

"Somebody left on their feet," she said quietly. "Somebody didn't."

"Maybe."

"Maybe isn't a word you use, Casper. I've watched you. You use it when you don't want to say the other thing."

"The other thing costs extra."

She didn't laugh. She looked at the shed, and the tower, and the clean roof, and she said, "Where is everyone?"

* * *

We found the first answer in the radio room, if you could call it that — a lean-to attached to the shed, corrugated iron and termite-chewed plywood, and inside it the expedition's HF set, or what had been the expedition's HF set. Someone had taken a machete or a fire axe to it with a thoroughness that went beyond vandalism. The case was split, the chassis bent double, the valves — Tomas picked one up and turned it in his palm with a look I recognized, the look of a man finding a dead friend — crushed to glitter.

"That's not looting," Tomas said. "You loot a radio, you *take* the radio. Radios are worth money out here. This is—" He turned the crushed valve over again. "This is making sure it never talks again."

"Same thing we found at the antenna," I said.

I'd shown them the antenna that morning of the silence, back at the last camp — the coaxial cable cut clean, one stroke, no fraying, no animal gnaw. Equipment failure, Hargreave had insisted, and I had let him insist, because there is a time for arguments and a river crossing with a swamped boat and a near-drowned geologist behind you is not it. But the cut cable and the murdered radio were having a conversation, and I was done pretending I couldn't hear it.

"Three weeks of scheduled check-ins," Mara said, "and then silence, and then this." She was standing in the doorway with the light behind her, and for once she wasn't looking at anything, just thinking, her eyes unfocused on the middle distance where her anomalies lived. "Whoever did this knew the schedule. They knew when the silence would start to worry people in Wewak, and they timed it so we'd arrive after they'd finished."

"Or they didn't finish," Hargreave said.

No one answered him, because the grave answered for all of us.

* * *

Tomas found it. He'd wandered — Tomas wandered, it was his nature, the boy had the attention span of a mayfly and the loyalty of a dog — and he came back down the slope at a walk that was trying very hard not to be a run, and his face had gone the color of the clay.

"There's a—" He stopped. Started again. "Behind the station. There's a grave."

The grave sat on the rise behind the instrument shed, where the ground dried first after rain, which is where you dig when you know what you're doing. It was fresh — the fill still dark, not yet settled, a week old at most — and it was marked not with a cross or a name but with a survey stake, the orange-painted kind, the kind that says *here is a measurement* rather than *here is a man*. Someone had written on the stake in black marker, in a careful engineer's hand:

**F. OKONKWO — 61° — TEMP.**

"Temp," Mara read. "Temporary."

"They were in a hurry," I said.

"They were *counting*," she said. "Look at it. Bearing, number, temporary marker. That's not grief, Casper. That's an inventory. That's someone logging a body the way you'd log a lost instrument, so it can be found and *processed* later."

I looked at the stake and I thought about ledgers. I thought about the way men with clipboards have always been more dangerous than men with guns, because guns end things and clipboards file them away where they can be denied forever. I didn't say any of this. I'd learned on the trestle, years ago, what happens to a man who says the thing out loud before he knows who's holding the clipboard.

Hargreave crouched at the grave's edge for a long time. When he stood, his face had done something complicated, and I watched him do arithmetic with it — I watched him weigh nine missing colleagues against whatever it was he wasn't telling us about who was paying for this expedition — and I watched the arithmetic come out wrong, and I watched him know it had come out wrong, and I watched him decide to keep the wrong answer anyway.

For now.

"Dr. Ferro," he said. "Has anyone seen Dr. Ferro?"

* * *

That was the question, wasn't it. Nine souls at the station, per the manifest Hargreave carried in a leather folder he touched the way other men touch rosaries. Nine souls, and the station had been hit, and we had found — we counted twice, in the gathering dark, by lamplight, with the river talking to itself below the bank — we had found four.

Four, sheltering in the pump house downstream of the compound: two hydrologists, a cook, and a surveyor named Anaya who had a rifle and the good sense to have kept it loaded and the better sense to have not fired it. They came out to our lanterns with their hands half-raised, half-reaching, the way people do when they can't decide whether they're being rescued or arrested, and when Anaya saw Hargreave she said, "You came," in a voice that made it an accusation.

"We came," Hargreave said.

"Six days. We heard the boats two days ago and thought it was them coming back."

"Who coming back?"

Anaya looked at him for a long moment, and I watched her decide how much of the truth this man could carry, and I watched her arrive at the same number I had.

"Men," she said. "Eight, maybe ten. Boats with no markings. They came up at night, two days after the radio died. They took Dr. Ferro."

"Took him where?"

"They asked him questions first." Anaya's hands were very still around the rifle, the way hands go still when stillness is all that's holding them together. "About the tunnel. About what he'd found in the tunnel. He told them he'd found nothing. They didn't believe him, because he's a terrible liar — Ferro, he blushes when he lies about liking someone's cooking." Her voice caught on the present tense and she corrected it, and the correction was the worst thing I'd heard all week. "Was. He *was* a terrible liar. They took him downriver. And the others—" She stopped. Started again, in the flat voice people find when the round voice won't hold. "Okonkwo tried to stop them. He had a machete. He was sixty-one years old and he had a machete and he tried."

Nobody said anything. The river said it for us, the way rivers do, patiently, over and over.

"And you four hid," Hargreave said, and there was no judgment in it, or if there was, it was the cheap kind, and Anaya spent none of herself on it.

"We hid, and we stayed off the radio because they'd smashed it, and we marked the grave so that if anyone ever came, someone would know to look for the rest of us." She turned and looked at me, and her eyes were dry and furious and older than the rest of her. "The stake says temporary. That was Ferro's doing — he marked it before they took him. Temporary. He wanted whoever found it to know it wasn't finished. That none of this was finished."

* * *

We made camp in the pump house because it had walls and a roof and because the four of them could not be persuaded to sleep anywhere the men in the unmarked boats had already looked, which was everywhere else. I set watches — mine first, because I don't sleep well in the best of years and this was not shaping up to be one — and I sat on the pump house steps with the river running black and silver below me, and I took out my closed-line map and looked at it for a long time.

The pencil cross. 1953, decommissioned. And beneath it, in the surveyor's hand of whoever had drawn the original — a hand I had studied for years the way other men study the faces of the dead — a notation I'd always taken for a drafting quirk: a small loop in the contour lines above the station, a place where the elevation seemed to double back on itself, impossible, unless the ground had been asked to lie.

Ferro had asked questions about a tunnel. Anaya had said so. A tunnel, above the gauge station, and a river that hedged, and rail spurs erased from the record, and a grave marked like a line item.

Mara came out and sat beside me on the steps. She didn't say anything for a while. That was one of the things I was learning about her, and learning it the way you learn a river — not by asking where it goes but by watching what it carries.

"Anaya says Ferro kept notebooks," she said finally. "Everything went in the notebooks. If they took Ferro and didn't find the notebooks—"

"Then the notebooks are still here."

"Or the men in the boats have them."

"Then they'd have taken the station apart differently. You don't gut a radio room and miss a man's kit." I turned the pencil cross toward her. "And you don't mark a grave temporary unless you expect someone to come finish the record."

She looked at the map, then at me. In the lantern light leaking through the pump house door her face was half gold and half dark, and I remember thinking that was about right for both of us.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I'm going to walk that contour loop."

"Alone?"

"Are you offering to come?"

"I'm offering to stand behind you while you do it and complain about the slope."

The almost-smile again. Hers was real when it came, which made it worse, or better, I hadn't decided. "Casper. Whatever's up there — Ferro found it, and men came in the night for it, and a sixty-one-year-old man died holding a machete against whatever it was. This stopped being a delivery two days ago. You know that."

I looked down at the river. Somewhere below us, down all those braided, hedging channels, a man was tied up in a boat being asked questions by people who counted bodies as ballast, and the only map to him was a loop in a contour line that a dead surveyor had drawn in 1953 and a living one had marked *temporary*.

"Yeah," I said. "I know that."

I folded the map along its old creases and put it away, and above the station the dark was very quiet, the particular quiet of a place that had been searched by professionals and found wanting, and I sat my watch and did not sleep, and in the morning we were going up the slope to find out what the water had been moved to hide.

Behind us, in the pump house, Anaya was teaching Tomas how to strip the rifle, and her hands were steady now, and that, I thought, was the only good news this river had given anybody all week.
