## 11. Ferro's Last Notebook

The grave bothered me more than the smashed radios.

Radios are easy to explain. Men who want a place quiet break the things that talk. I'd done it myself once, in another life, on a night I don't put in reports. But the grave — the grave was a courtesy, and courtesies from violent men always have a price tag attached. You don't dig a hole for someone you killed in anger. You dig it because you want the body found, or because you want the living to understand that finding bodies is part of the service.

Mara stood over it for a long time, her boot toe an inch from the survey stake. Someone had written on it in grease pencil: *F. FERRO — 14/6*. The date was four days old.

"That's not right," she said quietly.

"No?"

"Ferro isn't dead." She said it with the flat certainty of a woman who has argued with rivers and won at least some of the time. "I've shared a tent with that man for six weeks. He complains about the humidity, he hoards chocolate, he alphabetizes his sample vials when he's nervous. If Ferro were dead, someone would have had to kill him twice."

"You think they buried something else."

"I think," Mara said, "that whoever dug this wanted us to stop looking. And I've never once stopped looking because a man with a shovel told me to."

Hargreave, meanwhile, had retreated into the gauge station's office and was going through the wreckage with the methodical despair of a man auditing his own failure. The building itself was timber and corrugated iron, colonial bones with newer skin — a stilling well down to the water, a chart room, a bunkhouse whose door hung by one hinge. The radio bench looked like it had been attacked with a machete and then, for thoroughness, a boot. Glass from the barometer crunched underfoot like river ice.

Tomas stayed outside. He hadn't said much since we landed — since the fresh-turned earth and the stake — but he'd taken up a position where he could see both the river and the treeline, arms folded, jaw set in a way that made him look suddenly older than his twenty-six years. The boy who'd talked engines with me over the outboard now watched the jungle like it owed him money.

I went back to the boats.

Not because I'm brave about graves. Because cargo men like me learn one thing early: when a story doesn't add up, count what's missing. The station had been ransacked, yes — drawers pulled, charts torn, the radio murdered. But ransacking is a performance as often as it is a search. I wanted to know what the searchers had *not* touched.

* * *

Fuel drums tell you everything about an expedition's true intentions. Food stores say how long you plan to stay. Ammunition says how welcome you expect to be. Fuel says how far you intend to run.

There were six drums racked behind the station on a timber stand above the flood line, which told me whoever built this rack knew the Sepik's moods. Five of them were full — I rocked each one, read the slosh. The sixth was light. Too light for forty-four gallons of diesel.

I popped the cap and looked inside with my torch.

Empty, except for a canvas satchel wedged at the bottom, wrapped in a plastic sheet tied off with electrical tape. Somebody had thought about water. Somebody who worked around rivers thinks about water the way priests think about God — constantly, and with a certain dread.

I hauled the satchel up onto the rack and sat there a moment with my hand on it, feeling like a man reading another man's mail, which is exactly what I was doing, and which I have never once let stop me.

"Casper." Mara's voice behind me. She'd followed without my hearing her, which should have worried me more than it did. "What have you got?"

"Either Ferro's laundry," I said, "or the reason six men with good boots came upriver to break a radio."

We unwrapped it together on the drum rack. Inside: a notebook, cheap and swollen with damp, its cover soft as cloth; a second, smaller book bound in oilcloth; a pencil stub; and a folded square of heavy paper that turned out to be a section of a survey map, hand-annotated, older than either of us.

The notebook's first page said, in cramped engineer's lettering: *R. Ferro — hydrology log, continued. If found, do not give this to Hargreave.*

"Well," Mara said. "That's awkward."

"Hargreave's in the office counting his broken instruments."

"Then we have perhaps ten minutes." She sat down on the drum beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and neither of us moved away. "Read it aloud. I want to hear it in your voice. Mine will make it sound like science."

* * *

Ferro wrote the way good field scientists write — fast, precise, increasingly frightened. The early entries were almost boring, and I mean that as praise. Gauge calibrations. Sediment cores. Flow rates that didn't match the historical record. Then, eleven days ago, the record stops being about water.

*Core 14 shows an unconformity at 2.3 meters. Not a natural sequence. There is a fill layer — crushed rock, ash, and below it, bone fragments. Small mammal? I am not equipped to say. But the stratigraphy is wrong for this channel. This river has not always run here.*

Mara made a sound low in her throat when I read that. Not surprise. Confirmation. Whatever she'd been sketching in her own quiet maps since the sampling began, Ferro had arrived at the same country from the other direction.

*Took a walk upstream along the old cutbank. Found the diversion works. Earth embankment, now forested, but the geometry is unmistakable — someone moved this river. Moved it deliberately, with labor, decades ago. The question a hydrologist asks is why. The question I am starting to be afraid to ask is what needed hiding badly enough to drown a valley.*

*Found the adit.*

The handwriting changed here. Larger. Faster. The pencil pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages.

*Behind the gauge station, upslope, screened by strangler fig — a tunnel mouth, timbered, collapsed by design. Wartime work or styled to look like it. Japanese occupation era, or just before. A mine adit. But nobody mines this geology — there's nothing here worth a shaft. Unless what they were mining wasn't ore.*

*I got through. The collapse is partial, maybe two meters of crawl. Inside: rails. Narrow gauge, hand-propelled skips. And a chamber — a gallery, cut back thirty meters at least — with shelving. Company shelving. Ledgers. Dozens of them. Payroll books, requisitions, tonnage reports. Photographs in oilcloth packets. And bones. Bones stacked like cordwood against the north wall, catalogued in chalk on the rock itself. Numbers. They numbered them.*

I stopped reading. My mouth had gone dry in a way the Sepik humidity couldn't account for.

Mara took the notebook from my hands gently, the way you take something from a child, and read the next passage herself, aloud, her voice steady in the way voices go steady when steadiness is a decision rather than a condition.

*"The ledgers list laborers. Recruitment rolls from the coast, transport manifests, and then a column headed 'expended.' Expended. Like fuel. Like ballast. Three hundred and forty-one names in the first book alone. The photographs—"*

She stopped.

"The photographs," I said.

"There are descriptions." Her thumb rested on the page. "Executions. Trenches behind the diversion works. Men lined up at the cutbank. Dated. Stamped with a company mark — a consortium mark. Casper, these aren't war records. These are receipts."

Outside, Tomas shifted his weight at the treeline, and somewhere downriver a bird made its idiotic laughing call, and the world went on being the world, indifferent to the fact that a dead-seeming man's notebook had just turned sixty years of quiet water into evidence.

* * *

The last entries were written in the final day, and they read like a man packing a suitcase in a burning house.

*Someone came. Boat ran dark past the station at 0200. Four men, maybe five. Not police — no uniforms, too much equipment. They went upslope toward the fig tree. They know about the adit. They didn't find my cache, but they'll come back, and next time they'll search properly.*

*I have hidden the smaller book separately. If you're reading this, look where fuel goes to die.*

Mara glanced at the empty drum. "He means the sixth drum."

"He means we haven't found the second book yet." I tipped the drum again, ran my torch around inside, and there — welded to the underside of the rim, held by a magnet that had been a speaker once — a slim oilcloth packet. Ferro had been an engineer before he was a hydrologist. Engineers hide things the way other people pray.

Inside was the smaller book, and this one was worse, because it wasn't data. It was reasoning.

*Working theory, and I hate it:* Ferro wrote. *The consortium that recruited the labor — the records carry the mark of the old Sepik Development Consortium — didn't dissolve after the war. It was absorbed. Restructured. Its assets rolled forward into successor entities. Companies are like rivers that way. Change the name, keep the channel.*

*The current development push up this basin — roads, dredging, the whole bright future they're selling — traces back through holding companies to the same families. Same capital. Same mark, restyled. Whoever funded Hargreave's expedition didn't fund it to find this. They funded it hoping we'd measure water and go home. But the sediment wouldn't lie, and now they need the adit emptied and sealed properly, and they need witnesses accounted for.*

*A buyer is coming. I intercepted a fragment of transmission on the backup set before they cut the antenna — a schedule, coordinates for a floatplane landing on the broad water below the station. A name was spoken twice. Roos. Anselm Roos. He is coming to collect the ledgers himself. Which tells me how much they're worth, and tells you what will happen to anyone holding them when he arrives.*

*If I'm gone when you read this: I am not gone. I have watched these men work. They are thorough, not omnipotent. There is country west of here I know better than they do. I have taken the photographs' location data and one ledger volume and I will move at night. Do not come after me. Come after the truth. It weighs less and it floats.*

Below that, one last line, the pencil dragging, hurried:

*Tell Ellingsen the anomaly map was right. Tell her the river remembers. Rivers are the only honest witnesses left — everything else can be bought.*

Mara closed the book. For a while neither of us said anything. The river talked instead, patient and enormous, running over ground that remembered.

"He's alive," she said finally. "West of here. Moving at night."

"If he's alive. That entry's four days old."

"He's alive." She said it like a fact she was prepared to defend with instruments. Then her voice cracked, just once, just slightly, the way ice cracks before it becomes a lead of open water. "*Expended.* Three hundred and forty-one names in the first book. Casper, my grandfather worked coastal recruitment in those years. He used to say there were villages that stopped answering letters."

"And now we know why."

"Now we know why." She looked at the notebook in her lap, then at me, and something passed between us that had nothing to do with ledgers — the recognition of two people who had both spent their lives drawing accurate pictures of things other people preferred drawn inaccurately. "Hargreave can't see this. Not yet. He'll do something noble and stupid, or stupid and stupid."

"And if he finds out we hid it?"

"Then he'll be angry for a day and grateful for the rest of his life, assuming we all live long enough for gratitude." She wrapped the notebooks back in their oilcloth with quick, sure hands. "Where do we keep them?"

"Not on the boats. Boats sink, boats get searched." I looked up the slope, toward the gauge station, toward the strangler fig I could just make out against the ridge, its silhouette like a fist of dark rope. Toward the tunnel behind it, where a company had shelved its sins in the dark and chalked numbers on stone. "And not up there. Everyone will look up there."

"So where?"

I thought about it the way I think about water — where does it go when it wants to disappear. Then I smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile, and Mara noticed, and didn't ask, which is one of the hundred reasons I was already halfway in love with her.

"The stilling well," I said. "It's a pipe that runs twelve feet down into the riverbed, watertight, capped. Nobody breaks a gauge station's plumbing. Even vandals have professional respect."

We hid the notebooks in fifteen minutes, and I won't describe how, because a hiding place described is a hiding place lost. What I will say is that when we walked back into the chart room, Hargreave looked up from the wrecked radio bench with red eyes and said, "Anything?" and Mara said, "Nothing but broken glass," and I kept my face arranged into the honest blankness of a man who owns nothing, comes from nowhere, and knows nothing worth knowing.

It was the best acting of my life, and I'd had practice.

But that night, lying in the bunkhouse listening to the river run full over its buried secret, I kept coming back to one line in Ferro's small book, the one that had nothing to do with ledgers or bones or the arithmetic of the dead.

*A buyer is coming. Roos. He is coming to collect them himself.*

Men send employees for things that don't matter. They come themselves for the things that could ruin them — and they come with whatever force it takes to make collection permanent.

Four days ago, a floatplane had been scheduled for the broad water below the station.

I lay in the dark and listened, very carefully, to the river.

Somewhere downstream, faint beneath the water's voice, I heard an engine that wasn't a boat.
