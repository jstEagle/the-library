## 12. Hargreave's Real Employer

The grave was still there in the morning, which meant it hadn't been a dream, and neither had Ferro's notebook, and neither had the name at the bottom of the last entry — *R. comes Thursday* — written in a hand that had stopped bothering to be neat somewhere around the third page.

I'd slept maybe two hours on the station's veranda floor, which was generous of me, since the veranda also hosted a colony of ants who regarded my forearm as a highway and my sweat as a toll booth. I lay there listening to the river do its patient work against the pilings and thought about the word *expended*. Not killed. Not murdered. Expended — the way you'd write fuel or blasting powder into a ledger. Somebody had sat at a desk, probably a good desk, teak maybe, with a ceiling fan turning overhead, and chosen a word that let him sleep at night.

I knew that desk. I'd sat behind one like it once.

By six the camp was awake in the way camps wake — badly, in stages, with complaints. Mara was already at the water table with her samples, Tomas was nursing a burn on his palm from the night crossing, and Hargreave was standing at the rail of the main boat with a mug of tea he wasn't drinking, looking upriver at the gauge station as if staring hard enough might reorganize what we'd found inside it.

It doesn't work that way. I've tried.

I made coffee — real coffee, hoarded from Wewak, because if I was about to have the conversation I was about to have, I wanted to be caffeinated for it — and carried two mugs down the bank.

"Doctor," I said.

"Casper." He took the mug without looking at me. "Beautiful morning."

"It's a massacre morning."

He flinched. Good. A man who flinches can still be talked to; a man who doesn't has already decided what he is.

"Look," he said, "what happened here was — it was wartime. Terrible things happened all over this basin during the war. That's history. Tragic, but history."

"That's not what the notebook says."

"The notebook says a lot of things. Dr. Ferro was — he was imaginative. Brilliant man, but he saw patterns where—"

"Hargreave." I said his name the way you set something heavy down on a table. "I read survey reports for eleven years. I know what fabricated data looks like, because I've signed some. You're doing it right now, out loud, with your face."

That landed. He turned and looked at me properly for the first time, and I saw the thing I'd been half-suspecting since Wewak: not a liar. A man carrying something heavy and telling himself it was luggage.

"Walk with me," he said.

* * *

We walked up the overgrown spur line behind the station. The rails were still there under the mud and the kunai grass, rusted orange, running straight back toward the hills until the jungle swallowed them. My map had these lines on it. Closed since before independence. Someone had paid good money to have them struck from every official chart, and someone else had paid better money to keep them forgotten.

"You want to know who's paying for this expedition," Hargreave said.

"The thought had crossed the river."

He laughed — a short, unhappy sound. "You don't miss much, do you?"

"I miss plenty. I just file it differently." I sipped my coffee. "Who, Doctor?"

He stopped walking. Put his hands in his pockets. Took them out again. A man rehearsing.

"When I applied for funding," he said, "the grant came through a foundation. The Meridian Trust. Hydrological research, heritage recovery, cultural documentation. Very respectable letterhead. Very respectable people." He paused. "It wasn't until the second tranche cleared that I did the digging I should have done first. The Trust is a shell. It rolls up into a holding company, and the holding company rolls up into—" He exhaled. "Kessler Development Group."

The name meant nothing to me, which is how names like that prefer it.

"Should I know them?"

"They're building the new port at Vanimo. Roads. The resource corridor through the foothills. Half the development money moving through this country in the next decade goes through their books." He was talking fast now, the way men talk when they've been alone with a fact too long. "And before all of that — going back — they were the consortium. Sepik Consolidated Mining. The company that ran the labor lines up this valley in '43 and '44."

I looked down at the rusted rail under my boot.

"And the ledgers in that tunnel," I said. "The photographs. The bones."

"Are evidence," Hargreave said, "that could end everything they're building now. Or—" and here his voice changed, went smooth, went salesman, and I felt my stomach drop, because I recognized that voice too, "—or they could be handled. Contained. Contextualized."

"Contextualized."

"There are negotiations happening. There have been feelers put out. If the material surfaces in a controlled way — an academic publication, carefully framed, decades-old wartime conduct, no living culpability — the Group will fund a memorial. A research center. They'll fund *this expedition* for another ten years, Casper. Properly funded. Equipment, salaries, security. We could do real work here. Real hydrology. And the dead get their memorial, and nobody else has to die over papers that are eighty years old."

I let the silence sit between us long enough for him to hear how it sounded.

"You believe that," I said finally. "That's the remarkable part. You actually believe it."

"I believe in being *practical*—"

"You believe a company that diverted a river to hide eight hundred bodies is going to fund a memorial to the eight hundred bodies." I shook my head. "Hargreave, I've worked for companies. Companies don't build memorials to their own crimes. They build parking lots."

"You don't understand the scale of what they're offering—"

"No, I understand exactly. I understand it better than you do." I turned to face him fully. "Eleven years ago I signed a report. A trestle on the highland line came down, forty-one tons of rolling stock, three men dead. The report said soil saturation. Act of God. I signed it because my director told me the alternative was the railway's insolvency, four thousand jobs, and my signature on a different kind of report — one where I named names and ended up unemployable on three continents. So I signed. And you know what the company gave the three dead men?"

He didn't answer.

"A plaque. Small one. Brass. Behind the maintenance shed at Kundiawa where nobody would see it unless they were already looking." I tapped the rusted rail with my boot. "That's what containment looks like, Doctor. That's the memorial. That's the *context*. And then everyone keeps drawing their salary, and the water keeps running over the same ground, and twenty years later somebody's grandson finds the brass plaque and starts asking questions anyway. Because the truth doesn't stay contained. It just waits for a wet season."

Hargreave's jaw worked. "You're a fine one to lecture me about signatures."

"Yes," I said. "I am. That's rather the point."

* * *

He walked back toward the boats ahead of me, shoulders up around his ears, and I stayed on the spur line a while longer, because there was something I needed to check and I didn't want an audience.

Ferro's notebook had mentioned the adit — the tunnel mouth — sealed behind the gauge station. But it hadn't mentioned the rails. The rails were new information, or old information newly relevant, and I wanted to look at them the way Mara looked at channels: patiently, letting the pattern come to me.

I crouched and brushed the mud away. Gauge was narrow — fifty centimeters, mine gauge, labor gauge, the cheap kind you laid when you expected your workforce to be expendable and your operation to be temporary. The ties were hardwood, termite-eaten but sound in places. And here, where the spur bent toward the hillside, the ballast changed — darker stone, crushed finer, laid by different hands than the section nearer the station.

Two construction phases. Two budgets. One of them in a hurry.

I followed the dark ballast back until the jungle closed in, and there it was: the adit. Timbered mouth, collapsed by design — the charge had been set inside, not outside; the rubble cone fanned *outward*, which any demolition man will tell you means someone wanted it buried deep, not merely closed. And stenciled on the last surviving timber, faint as breath on glass:

**S.C.M. — LINE 4 — PROPERTY OF THE CONSORTIUM**

Sepik Consolidated Mining. The consortium. Kessler's grandfather, or great-grandfather, depending on how you counted generations of money.

I stood there in the green dark with the flies singing and understood the shape of the whole thing at last. This wasn't an expedition that had stumbled onto a crime. This was a cleanup crew wearing lab coats. The supplies, the drilling gear mislabeled as instruments, the satellite phone nobody admitted to owning, the buyer coming Thursday — we weren't the delivery. We were the disposal team. And the disposal team's contract always ends the same way: the waste gets buried, and so does anyone who's seen too much of it.

Except Ferro had seen too much first, and run, and hidden, and left a notebook in a fuel drum like a message in a bottle thrown at the future.

And Mara had started her own map.

* * *

She found me at the adit, of course. She had a talent for finding me when I was in the middle of a thought I'd rather finish alone.

"Tomas says you and Hargreave had words."

"We had a conversation with sharp edges."

"Mm." She crouched beside me, examined the stencil, ran her thumb along the letters. "Kessler."

"He told you."

"He told me at breakfast, after you told him. He tells everyone everything within an hour; it's his one redeeming quality." She sat back on her heels. "He offered me a research center."

"Of course he did."

"He genuinely thinks he can negotiate with them." She looked at the collapsed tunnel mouth. "That's the frightening part. He's not corrupt. He's *reasonable*. He's reasonable all the way down the line, each individual step sensible — take the funding, frame the narrative, sign the clause — and the sum of all those reasonable steps is that eight hundred people stay buried under a river twice."

"That's how it's done," I said. "Nobody ever chooses the horror. They choose the sensible thing, forty times."

"Is that what you did? With your trestle?" She asked it plainly, no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. Hargreave had told her that much too, apparently. The man leaked like a dugout canoe.

I considered lying to her. I considered it seriously. But she was sitting in the dirt at the mouth of a mass grave reading a demolition pattern off a rubble cone, and lying to a woman like that seemed not only wrong but pointless.

"Yes," I said. "That's what I did. Each step sensible. And I've spent eleven years being invisible to make up for it, which is its own kind of cowardice, dressed up as penance."

She was quiet for a moment. "Invisibility isn't cowardice, Casper. Sometimes it's survival."

"For me it was both, and I never sorted out the ratio." I stood, knees cracking. "Here's what matters now. Roos — or whoever R. is — comes Thursday. Today is Monday, if the ants have kept accurate calendar. That gives us three days, and in those three days we have to decide what we are. Delivery crew, disposal crew, or witnesses."

"Witnesses," Mara said. No hesitation. As if she'd been waiting days for someone to offer the word.

"And when the witnesses start getting shot at — because they will — you'll wish we'd been disposals."

"Then I'll wish it," she said, "and I'll still be right."

There it was again, that thing between us — not agreement, exactly, but alignment. Two instruments measuring the same river from different banks. I wanted to tell her about the plan forming in the back of my head, the one involving the backup transmitter and the decoy camps, and I didn't, because plans are like eggs: they break easily and shouldn't be shown around before they're ready.

Instead I said, "Keep your anomaly map current. And keep it hidden."

"Always."

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing escort things — checking mooring lines, rotating watch, showing Tomas how to true a propeller shaft with a file and patience — ordinary work, the kind that keeps a crew calm, while underneath it my mind ran the ledger of our position like a bookkeeper who'd found a discrepancy he didn't like.

Assets: one boat, functional. One second boat, swamped but salvageable. Supplies, mostly intact. Ledgers' location, known to four of us. Ferro, missing, possibly alive. Crew morale, fragile but genuine — Tomas had taken to following me around asking questions about engines, and I'd taken to answering, which surprised us both.

Liabilities: one employer whose employer wanted us all quiet. One buyer arriving Thursday with unknown resources. One radio antenna cut clean, meaning someone upstream or downstream had done deliberate work. And a river with exactly two directions, both of them visible for miles.

Then, near dusk, the discrepancy resolved itself.

Tomas was on watch. He came down the bank at a jog, trying to be casual and failing completely. "Casper. Downstream point. There's a boat."

"Fisherman?"

"At this hour, running without lights?"

I went up the bank quietly and glassed the downstream bend through the binoculars I'd bought in Wewak with Okuk's advance and never told Okuk I'd bought. The light was going, but the river gave it back silver, and against that silver: a hull. Long, low, aluminum, twin outboards, canvas over something amidships. Running dark, hugging the far channel, moving *upstream*.

Prospectors don't run dark. Prospectors don't hug channels at dusk. Prospectors don't buy twin forty-horse Yamahas. That was security — corporate security, the expensive kind, the kind that arrives when a company decides a problem needs to stop existing before a certain date.

Thursday had come early. Or the buyer had sent his teeth ahead of him.

I lowered the binoculars and felt the old familiar sensation settle over me, the one I'd carried out of the wreckage at Kundiawa: the specific cold clarity of a man who has just realized the schedule is no longer his.

Three days had become one night.

I climbed down the bank, found Mara at the water table, and said the sentence I'd been assembling all day:

"Get your maps. Kill the fires. We're about to become very difficult to find."
