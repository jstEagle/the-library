## 13. Downstream Eyes

The trouble with running dark on a river is that darkness is a two-way street.

I'd learned that lesson years ago on the Benguet line, when a night inspection train I was riding went lights-out through a tunnel and we nearly collected a maintenance crew with our cowcatcher. The foreman afterward said something I've carried around like a stone in my boot ever since: *The man who turns off his lights so no one can see him has also turned off his lights so he can't see anyone.* He said it while looking at me in a way that suggested the sentence had a second meaning. It did. Most sentences about hiding do.

So at half past nine that night, lying flat in the bow of our lead boat with the engine ticking as it cooled and the whole camp banked behind me like a held breath, I wasn't just watching for what the river might send us. I was listening to my own silence and asking it questions.

It answered.

* * *

We had made eleven kilometers past the gauge station's turnoff before Hargreave finally agreed to stop — not because he believed me, but because the fuel math stopped caring about his beliefs. We'd burned through a third of our reserve running the channels by moonlight after I told him what I'd seen at dusk: a shape upstream, low, unlit, moving against the current with the kind of disciplined patience that prospectors don't have and drunk fishermen don't sustain.

Hargreave had wanted to push on to the station. Of course he had. The station was where his career lived now, where Ferro's notebook pointed, where whatever Kessler Development Group had paid him to find sat waiting in the dark like an unpaid invoice. Every hour we idled was an hour someone else might arrive first.

"Prospectors," he'd said, when I described the boat. "Illegal dredgers, probably. They run dark all the time to dodge the fisheries patrol."

"Fisheries patrol boats have outboard motors and a spotlight zip-tied to a bicycle handlebar," I said. "This one had a hull you could land aircraft on and an exhaust note I've only heard from military diesels. And it was holding station against four knots of current without wandering. That's not dodging anyone. That's *looking* for someone."

Mara had been the one who settled it, in her way — not by arguing but by unfolding her chart of the braids downstream and saying, quietly, "If they're between us and Wewak, we can't go back anyway." Which was true, and which she knew was true, and which she delivered with the mild tone of a woman reading a weather report. Hargreave looked at the chart, then at the fuel gauge, then at the black water, and gave the order to bank.

Now the camp lay in a cut of the bank beneath a stand of sago, three boats nosed into the reeds, engines cold, cook fire long dead and buried under wet mud. No lanterns. No cigarettes. Even Tomas, who at nineteen treated silence as a personal insult, had gone quiet, crouched beside me in the bow with a machete across his knees and his breathing too deliberate, the way young men breathe when they're performing calm for an audience of one older man they've decided to trust.

I hated that. Not the trust — the timing of it. Trust is a fine thing to earn over years and a terrible thing to be handed at night on a crocodile river by a boy who thinks you know what you're doing.

"You see anything?" he whispered.

"I see the inside of my own eyelids and a lot of wishful thinking," I said. "Which is more than most people get."

He almost laughed. Almost. Then the river gave us both something better than a joke.

* * *

It came as a sound first — or rather, as the absence of one. The Sepik at night is never truly quiet. Frogs run their circuits, insects stitch the air together, and somewhere in the reeds something is always dying or being born, usually loudly. What I heard was a hole in all of it, a moving pocket of hush maybe two hundred meters out, sliding along the far channel. Animals don't go quiet for nothing. They go quiet for things bigger than them.

Then the shape resolved against the faint shine the sky leaves on water even on moonless nights — a long, low silhouette, bow squared off, superstructure barely above the gunwale. Running without navigation lights. Without even the red glow of a cigarette on deck. A boat that clean in its darkness isn't sneaking; it's *trained*. Somewhere aboard were men who had drilled this, men whose employer had paid for the drilling, and employers only pay for drilling when they expect to be seen and would prefer not to be.

"There," I breathed, and put two fingers on Tomas's wrist to keep him still.

We watched it pass. It didn't run the main channel — it took the secondary braid, the one Mara had flagged on her chart as seasonally shallow, the one no commercial traffic bothers with because the charts say it shouldn't hold a boat that size. Whoever was piloting either knew the water intimately or had a depth sounder worth more than every boat in our convoy combined. Both possibilities pointed the same direction: money, recent and serious.

The shape slowed near our cut — I felt Tomas stop breathing entirely — hung there for perhaps ninety seconds, and moved on upstream. Toward the gauge station. Toward Ferro's adit. Toward everything Roos's money wanted collected before anyone else touched it.

Except they hadn't come from downstream. They'd come from *upstream*, moving down, and now they were turning back up. Which meant they'd already made their sweep below us, found nothing, and were returning to sit on the prize. Which meant, if they were any good, they'd be back down again. Patrols are loops. Loops have periods. And a period, given patience, is a schedule.

I slid back over the gunwale and dropped into the shallows, mud sucking at my boots, and walked the twenty meters to where Hargreave stood in the black under the sagos, arms crossed, radiating the particular misery of a man who has been right about everything except the things that matter.

"Well?" he said.

"One boat, steel-hulled, twin diesel, trained crew, running a patrol loop through the back channels. They'll be back within the hour, and next time they'll be slower. First pass is always fast. Second pass is when they look."

"That's absurd. Who patrols a river for—"

"Dr. Hargreave." I kept my voice level, which took some doing, because the man's capacity for self-deception had begun to feel less like a flaw and more like a structural feature, load-bearing, something the whole expedition rested on. "You took money from a company that buried a massacre and moved a river to hide it. Did you imagine they'd sent a fruit basket?"

Silence. Somewhere behind him, I heard Mara shift her weight — she'd come forward without being called, chart tube under her arm like a rifle. She'd heard. Good. I was tired of being the only one carrying bad news in triplicate.

"What do you propose?" Hargreave asked finally, and there it was — the transfer, small and grudging, of command. He'd done it once before, at the swamped crossing, and each time it cost him something visible. I almost pitied him. Almost.

"They're hunting a campfire and a light show," I said. "So let's give them one. Just not ours."

* * *

Here is what forty years of infrastructure work teaches you that no one puts in the manuals: every landscape is full of things that want to burn, float, drift, or fall, and the professional's job is mostly deciding which of those serves you tonight.

Two kilometers downstream of our cut, on a low gravel spit the flood had stranded, sat the remains of an old fishing camp — a lean-to of bamboo gone silver with weather, a scatter of tins, and, crucially, a stack of split sago-palm kindling some fisherman had left against the day he returned. Fishermen always mean to return. Rivers count on it.

Tomas and I ran the lead boat down with the engine at idle, muffled by a blanket over the cowl — a trick I'd learned from a smuggler on the Fly River whose name I never learned and whose methods I never forgot. We loaded the kindling, a drum of waste diesel we'd been hauling since the staging camp because Hargreave refused to pollute and I'd privately known would be useful for exactly this, and a bundle of dried palm fronds that would catch hot and die fast. A fire that burns bright for ten minutes and collapses looks like an accident. A fire that burns steady looks like a camp. We wanted a camp.

Back at our cut, Mara had taken the other problem: our own traces. She worked down the bank with a torch shuttered to a coin of light, cataloguing everything we'd done wrong since landing — the mud scarred by our keels, the reeds broken where we'd hauled the boats, the wet ring where the cook pot had sat. She couldn't erase it all. Nobody erases a convoy. But she could make it read old, and she did, dragging palm fronds backward over the worst of it, scattering the mud, relocating our actual sleeping positions ten meters inland onto ground that would show nothing by morning.

"You've hidden before," she said, not quite a question, working beside me in the dark. Her shoulder brushed mine and neither of us adjusted away from it, which in the economy of that week counted as a significant transaction.

"Hiding's just surveying in reverse," I said. "You map what gives you away and then you break the map."

"And the rest? The engine work, the water-reading, knowing what a military diesel sounds like at two hundred meters?" She kept her eyes on the reeds. "Okuk told me you were an escort. Escorts don't usually come with a résumé."

"Okuk tells everyone what keeps the contract signed."

"Mm." She straightened, looked at me — really looked, the way she looked at a channel that the official charts got wrong, with that expression of a woman preparing to redraw something. "One day you'll tell me the truth about yourself, Casper Iversen. Probably at the least convenient moment available."

"It's the only kind of moment I book," I said, and she almost smiled, and I felt that almost-smile land somewhere under my ribs where I'd been keeping everything else, and I thought: *this is how it starts, this is exactly how it starts,* and I turned back to the fire kit before my face could file a report.

* * *

We built their camp for them at midnight.

The spit caught light beautifully — diesel-soaked kindling takes a match like it owes you money, and inside four minutes we had a blaze throwing shadows across the water, silhouettes of palm trunks leaning and swaying, and Tomas, who had a theatrical streak nobody had previously discovered, set two empty fuel drums on the gravel upwind so the wind played them into a low metallic moaning that carried across the channel like machinery. From two hundred meters, in the dark, through heat shimmer, it would read as a working camp: generators, cooking, men. From five hundred meters it would read as *the* camp, the one the patrol was looping to find.

Then we killed ourselves properly. Engines already cold. The last of Mara's trace-work done. We pulled the boats another fifty meters into the reed maze — deeper than safety strictly allowed, deep enough that getting out in a hurry would cost us skin and time, but hurry was a currency I was planning not to spend — and we lay down in the mud and the mosquitoes and waited.

Waiting is the part they never pay you enough for. In rail work it was waiting on weather, waiting on inspectors, waiting on the telegraph. Out here it was waiting on a boat full of armed strangers to decide whether we were interesting. I lay on my back in the shallows with my hat brim funneling river water gently past my ears and watched the stars do their slow rotation and thought, not for the first time, that I had built a life out of not being found, and that the strange thing about it was how much of my energy it left over for finding other people.

Mara lay close enough that I could hear her breathe. At some point her hand found my forearm in the dark — not a squeeze, not a declaration, just contact, the way you touch a railing on a moving deck. I didn't move. Some signals are best acknowledged by remaining perfectly still.

The patrol came back at ten minutes past one.

I knew before I saw it, because the frogs shut down along the far bank in a traveling wave, silence rolling toward us like weather. Then the shape again, slower this time, exactly as promised, and this time it had a searchlight — hooded, shuttered to a slit, sweeping the gravel spit where our fire roared and groaned and threw its beautiful lies.

The boat eased toward the burning camp. Through the reed stems I watched figures resolve on its deck: six, maybe seven, carbines slung, one man forward with a light, one aft at a mounted piece that I genuinely hoped I was misidentifying. Too many for dredgers. Too well-fed for pirates. Too organized for anyone whose payroll didn't originate in an office with air conditioning and a logo.

They circled the spit twice. The searchlight crawled over the lean-to, the drums, the collapsed heart of the fire. One of them called out — English, flat, bored, the voice of a man reading a script: *"Anybody home? This is a security patrol."*

Nobody answered. Nobody was home. Nobody had ever been home. The fire crackled on behalf of several people who did not exist, and the drums moaned their mechanical grief, and the patrol boat hung there in the channel while its crew performed due diligence for a report somebody upstairs would skim.

Then the light swung off the spit and began, lazily, to crawl along our bank.

Along *our* bank. Toward the fifty meters of reeds where three boats and seven living people lay holding their breath in crocodile country with mud in their teeth.

Time does strange things in those seconds. I remember noticing, with insane clarity, a beetle crossing the back of my hand. I remember calculating — uselessly, compulsively — the distance from the searchlight slit to our nearest hull, the angle of the beam, whether the reed screen would hold at that height. I remember Mara's hand tightening on my arm, once, and the message in it, which was not fear. It was *together*, translated into pressure.

And then the river intervened, the way rivers do, without malice and without mercy. A hippo — we'd been hearing them all night, somewhere south, grunting their swamp opera — chose that moment to surface in the main channel forty meters off the patrol's stern with a blast of breath like a steam valve letting go. The searchlight whipped toward it. Voices snapped short commands. The diesel note climbed as the pilot backed water, putting distance between his hull and four hundred kilograms of territorial temper, and the beam swept high across the reeds, over our heads, painting the sago crowns white for one long heartbeat before it dropped and died.

The boat turned upstream. The frogs resumed. The beetle continued its commute across my hand, unbothered, having seen worse.

For a long time none of us moved. Then Hargreave, from somewhere behind me, exhaled the longest exhale of his professional life, and Tomas made a sound that was half laugh and half prayer, and Mara's grip loosened by degrees, though her hand stayed where it was.

"They bought it," she whispered.

"They bought the discount version," I said. "That fire will be dead in twenty minutes, and when it is, they'll know a camp doesn't pack itself up. We've bought hours, not days. Maybe not even that — the mounted gun means they report to someone who expects resistance, and someone who expects resistance sends a second boat."

"So we move."

"So we move. Tonight, while their loop is committed downstream and their eyes are full of a dead campfire. Upstream, to the station, before they decide to sit on it permanently." I rolled upright, mud sheeting off my shoulders, and looked at the black water where the hippo had already submerged, back to its opera, master of a river that belonged to no one and everyone and certainly not to Anselm Roos or whoever signed his invoices. "And Dr. Hargreave — when we get there, we do this my way. All of it. Because the men on that boat aren't the danger. They're the *symptom*. The disease is whatever's coming behind them, and I intend to see it coming first for once in my life."

Nobody argued. That alone should have frightened me more than the patrol boat did.

We slipped the boats out of the reeds into the current, running dark, lights dead, engines muffled — invisible, or pretending hard enough to pass. Ahead of us, upstream, the gauge station waited with its smashed radios and its fresh grave and its sealed adit full of bones and ledgers, and somewhere between us and it, a second boat was probably already loading.

Behind us, on the gravel spit, the last coals of our false camp ticked and died, and the river closed over the place where seven people had never been.
