## 14. Mara's Choice

I have always believed that a map is a confession. Every line on one is somebody admitting what they know and, more damningly, what they've decided to leave out. I learned that from my mother, who spent thirty years charting the Norwegian coast and used to say the blank spaces were where the bodies were buried. She meant it as a joke about hydrography. She would not have found it funny here.

The gauge station had been ours for two days now — two days of ransacked drawers, smashed radios, and a grave marked with a survey stake that none of us could look at for long. Two days since we'd found Ferro's notebook in the fuel drum, and two days since Casper Iversen had stopped pretending to be merely an escort. He moved through the station like a man reading a room he'd been in before, which was impossible, because no one had been in this room before except men who'd wanted it forgotten.

I spent those two days doing what I do best: arguing with paper.

The ledgers themselves were still sealed in the adit behind the station — Casper had insisted they stay there until we knew who else was coming for them — but Ferro's notebook gave me coordinates. Precise ones. Ferro had been a better surveyor than anyone credited him for being alive to appreciate, and his last entries described the tunnel chamber with grid references, depths, distances from the old rail spur. Enough that if the notebook survived and the tunnel didn't, the evidence could be relocated by any competent team.

So I photographed everything. Every page, every margin note, every smudge of Ferro's handwriting where his hand had shaken. I shot them in natural light against the grey concrete of the station floor, then again with the flash diffused under a cloth, then again at an angle to catch the indentations where he'd pressed hard. Three copies of every frame, on two cards, plus the prints I'd begun developing in the improvised darkroom I'd rigged from a storage crate and Tomas's chemical kit.

Tomas had asked me why I needed prints when the digital files existed. I told him that digital files can die quietly, but paper has to be murdered in front of witnesses. He'd looked at me like I'd said something profound. Maybe I had. Mostly I was scared.

* * *

Casper found me at dusk on the third evening, sitting on the loading platform with the notebook open on my knees and the river going copper below me. He didn't announce himself. He never did. He just appeared at the edge of my awareness the way weather does, and then he was sitting beside me, close enough that our shoulders almost touched, far enough that it could be called coincidence.

"You've been photographing that notebook for three days," he said.

"I've been photographing it for as long as it takes to do it properly."

"And you've made prints."

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. The Sepik muttered against the pilings. Somewhere across the water a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling.

"Mara." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "What are you planning?"

And there it was. The question underneath all the other questions we'd been circling since the grave with the survey stake. I could have lied to him. I considered it, briefly, the way you consider jumping off something high — aware of the drop, aware of the appeal.

"I'm planning to get this to the tribunal," I said. "Port Moresby. There's a session convening next month on colonial-era labor claims. Ferro knew about it — it's why he started digging in the first place, why he kept records the way he did. He built himself a chain of custody because he understood that finding the truth is worthless if you can't prove where it's been." I turned a page. Ferro's handwriting marched across it, neat even at the end. "The ledgers name companies. The photographs name men. The bones name everyone. That's not history, Casper. That's a case."

"Say that louder," Casper said, "so the people Roos hired can hear you."

"There's nobody within forty kilometres."

"You're certain of that the way you were certain the radio failure was equipment?"

That landed where he'd aimed it. I closed the notebook.

"Here's what I think," he went on, and his voice stayed level, conversational, the voice of a man discussing engine maintenance. "I think there are people downstream whose entire professional existence depends on this tunnel staying shut. I think they have boats, radios, and weapons, and we have two skiffs, a feverish geologist, and Dr. Hargreave, who still believes negotiation is a thing that happens between equals. And I think the moment any of this evidence exists in a form that can be seized, it will be seized, along with everyone carrying it."

"So your plan is to leave it buried."

"My plan is to keep everyone alive long enough to have a plan."

"That's not a plan, that's a postponement."

"It's kept me alive for eleven years."

I looked at him then. Really looked. He was watching the river the way he always watched it — not admiringly, but the way a chess player watches a board, tracking currents the way other men track intentions. Eleven years. I knew almost nothing about those years. A collapsed trestle somewhere, a career that ended, a man who owned no address and navigated by borrowed names. What I knew was that when Tomas went into the crocodile water, Casper went in after him without a half-second's arithmetic, and that a man who values his own survival that highly doesn't do that unless something in him has decided some things cost more than dying.

"Casper," I said. "Do you believe what's in that tunnel happened?"

He was quiet for a while. The bird across the water tried its hinge again.

"Yes," he said finally.

"Do you believe the men who did it should answer for it?"

"Yes."

"Then help me get it out. Not burned, not buried, not traded away in whatever quiet arrangement you're already building in that head of yours. Out. To the tribunal. With names attached and a chain of custody Ferro bled to build."

"And when Roos's security detail takes it off us at the first checkpoint? When they take us with it?" He turned to face me, and up close his eyes were lighter than I'd thought, grey-green, tired around the edges. "You're a cartographer, Mara. Run the route. Show me the line from here to Port Moresby that doesn't pass through hands Kessler owns. Take your time. I'll wait."

I hated him a little, because I'd run the route. I'd run it every night since the grave. Every line ended the same way: a checkpoint, a bought magistrate, a boat that never arrived. The map of this country, drawn honestly, was a map of everywhere the truth went to drown.

"That doesn't mean we bury it," I said. "That means we're smarter about how it travels."

"Smarter how?"

"Distributed. Copies. If the evidence exists in five places at once, seizing it becomes pointless. That's the whole logic of a chain of custody — you don't protect the object, you protect the record of the object. Photographs, coordinates, sworn statements. The ledgers are the anchor, yes, but the testimony is the hull. You can sink the anchor. You can't sink every copy."

Something shifted in his face. Not agreement — I want to be clear about that, it was not agreement — but recognition. The look of a man watching someone solve a problem he'd assumed unsolvable, and recalculating.

"You've thought about this," he said.

"I argue with rivers for a living. Bureaucracy is easier. Rivers don't hire lawyers."

That got the ghost of a smile out of him, there and gone. Then it faded, and he looked back at the water, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into a register I hadn't heard from him before. Lower. Unarmored.

"When I was twenty-nine," he said, "I signed a report that said a trestle was sound. I knew it wasn't. My superior knew it wasn't. The company knew. But the schedule held, and the report got signed, and four months later it came down with eleven men on it." The river filled the silence he left. "The inquiry found structural fatigue. Found nothing about the report, nothing about the pressure, nothing about me. The company survived. I got blamed anyway — not officially, but officially enough. No railroad in three countries would touch me after that." He flexed his hands, once, like a man testing old restraints. "I know exactly how Kessler will handle this, Mara. Not because I'm clever. Because I've been the pen they sign with. I know how it feels from the inside. And I know that the people who carry the truth get destroyed first, and the truth gets destroyed second, and the paperwork gets filed third."

"So we do nothing. We let them win by default."

"We let them win *later*. There's a difference. Later means alive."

"Alive doing what? Running supplies up dead rivers under borrowed names until the river takes you or the drink does?" I heard my own voice go sharp and didn't rein it in. "You talk about survival like it's the point. It isn't the point. It's the precondition. Something has to survive *for*, Casper, or you're just cargo that hasn't been delivered yet."

He flinched. Actually flinched, small, around the eyes, and I realized I'd hit something true, and I hated myself for the accuracy and loved myself for it too, which is the specific ugliness of arguing with someone you —

Well. Which is the specific ugliness of arguing with Casper Iversen.

"You think I haven't thought about that," he said quietly. "Every day for eleven years. You think I don't know exactly what I am. A man who keeps moving because stopping costs more. Because if I stop anywhere long enough, someone asks questions, and questions have answers, and the answers are that I signed the report." He shook his head. "You want to hand this to a tribunal. Fine. I believe you're right. Do you understand what that means? It means I stop being invisible. It means my name goes on a document next to Ferro's and Hargreave's and yours, and Kessler's lawyers read that document, and they find out what I did at that trestle, and they use it. They'll call me a disgraced engineer with a grudge. They'll call you a foreigner with an agenda. They'll take Ferro apart posthumously. And if that fails, they won't use lawyers."

"Then we make sure it doesn't fail."

"You can't guarantee that."

"No," I said. "I can't. Neither can you. Nobody can. That's what courage is, Casper — it's acting without the guarantee. You of all people should know that. You pulled Tomas out of that water without a guarantee. You didn't calculate the crocodiles first."

"That was different."

"It was exactly the same, and you know it."

We sat there, both breathing harder than the argument strictly required, close enough on the loading platform that I could feel the heat coming off his shoulder. The last light was leaving the river. Downstream, somewhere past the darkening bends, men with rifles were presumably running dark toward us, and upstream the mountain held its tunnel full of paper and bone, and here in the middle were the two of us, fighting about the only thing worth fighting about, which is what you owe the dead.

"Copies," Casper said at last. It wasn't surrender. It was a counteroffer. "Distributed, like you said. But the originals stay hidden until we control the delivery. And nobody — nobody — carries anything on their body that mentions the tribunal. Nothing written. If they search us and find intent, intent is enough to hang us."

"Agreed."

"And Hargreave hears none of this. He'd negotiate it away inside an hour."

"Agreed," I said again, and then, because honesty seemed suddenly cheap compared to what we'd just exchanged: "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I might still be wrong. You might still be right. One of us is going to find out badly." He stood, and offered me his hand up, and I took it, and neither of us let go quite as fast as the transaction required.

The platform was narrow. That's my story and I'm keeping it.

For a moment we stood closer than the argument had ended, his hand still around mine, the river going black below us, and I saw something in his face that I suspected he'd spent eleven years making sure nobody saw — not fear exactly, and not hope either, but the raw place where fear and hope share a wall. I knew the place. I'd been living in it for three days.

"If we do this," he said, very low, "we do it together. Not escort and client. Together."

"Together," I said.

He nodded once, and squeezed my hand — briefly, deliberately, a signature rather than a caress — and walked off toward the boats to check the engines a fourth time, because Casper Iversen expressed tenderness primarily through preventive maintenance.

I stayed on the platform a while longer with the notebook on my knees. And that was when I saw it: a light, far downstream, low on the water, moving without running lights, the way a boat moves when it doesn't want to be seen. It vanished behind a bend, then didn't reappear.

I was on my feet and moving before I'd finished deciding to move, calling his name into the dark.
