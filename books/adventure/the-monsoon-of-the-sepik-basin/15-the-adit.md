## 15. The Adit

The gauge station had told us what happened to the people. The hill behind it was going to tell us why.

Ferro's notebook lay open on the table between us, its last entry circled twice in pencil gone soft with damp: *Adit behind the station, 40 paces upslope from the water tank. Rails still in. They never pulled the rails.* Below that, in a hand that had stopped bothering with neatness: *Buyer coming. If I don't come back down, it's because the tunnel decided otherwise.*

"He had a flair for the dramatic," Mara said, but her voice didn't believe it.

"He had a flair for being right," I said. "Which is worse."

Tomas stood at the window with his hands jammed in his pockets, looking at the treeline the way a man looks at a dog that has bitten him once already. He'd nearly drowned two nights ago in croc water, and he'd stopped sleeping much since, and he hadn't complained about either. I was starting to think the kid was made of something they don't sell at supply depots.

"We could wait for Hargreave," Tomas said.

"Hargreave is on the radio trying to raise Wewak for the ninth time this morning," I said. "He'll be another hour convincing himself the static is weather." It wasn't weather. I'd checked the antenna myself and found the cut, clean as a surgeon's line, and I hadn't told him yet because there was no version of that conversation that ended with anyone calmer than we already were. "And if Ferro went up that hill alone, every hour we spend being careful is an hour he spends not being alive."

Mara folded the notebook closed with both hands, like closing a casket. "Then we go now. Three of us. Light packs."

"Rope, torches, the pistol," I said.

"You brought a pistol?" Tomas asked.

"I brought a pistol the way I bring a life jacket," I said. "Hoping to feel foolish about it later."

* * *

The adit wasn't hidden so much as forgotten by everything except the jungle, which had spent forty years trying to swallow it and only gotten as far as the collar. Fern grew out of the timber framing in green fists. A drainage ditch ran from the mouth downhill toward the old water tank, choked with silt but still legible — someone had engineered this, graded it, meant it to last. Colonial engineering always had that quality: built by men who assumed the future would be grateful.

The rails came out of the dark like two iron sentences.

I knelt and put my palm flat on one. Cool. No rust bloom worth mentioning. Gauge of maybe two feet — a tramway, not a proper line, the kind of track you laid into a hillside when you expected to pull something heavy out and didn't care what you left behind.

"Casper?" Mara said, because I'd gone quiet, and I get quiet in a particular way when a map starts talking to me.

I took the folded survey sheet from my shirt pocket — my map of closed lines, the one I'd been carrying since Wewak, the one Okuk's contract had been stapled to like a joke with a punchline nobody had gotten yet. I spread it against my knee. There, threading up from the river bend toward the headwaters, a spur marked in the draughtsman's shorthand for *abandoned, pre-war, records incomplete.*

"This is it," I said. "This exact alignment. Somebody drew this spur on a government survey in 1936 and then somebody else paid to have the drawing disappear, and the only reason I have a copy is that a drunk archivist in Lae sold me a misfiled duplicate for the price of two beers." I looked up the slope, into the black under the ferns. "I thought I was carrying a curiosity. Turns out I've been carrying a confession."

"Whose?" Tomas asked.

"That's what we're going up to find out."

Mara clipped her torch to her pack strap. She did things efficiently even when her hands weren't entirely steady; I'd noticed that about her the first day, watching her argue with Dr. Hargreave over channel charts while re-inking a contour line without looking at it. "Forty paces from the water tank," she said. "Which means the chamber should be—"

"Past where the daylight quits," I said. "Which means after that point, we're walking on their terms, not ours."

"Their terms being?"

"Men who moved a river to hide a crime," I said, "do not leave tidy tunnels behind."

* * *

The first twenty paces were almost polite. The rails ran straight, the timbers held, our torches found walls of cut stone sweating in the cool. Water dripped somewhere ahead with the patience of geological time. The air changed — cooler, then mineral, then faintly sweet in a way I didn't like, because sweet in an old tunnel usually means something organic is busy becoming something else.

At pace thirty, the collapse.

Not a collapse, exactly. A wall. Rubble stacked with intent — big stones at the base, smaller ones fitted above, the whole thing mortared with mud that had cured hard as pottery. Someone had sealed this end deliberately and recently enough that the mud hadn't grown moss.

"They walled it up," Tomas whispered. His torch beam wandered across the stonework. "Why would you seal your own mine?"

"You seal a mine when you're done taking things out," I said. "Or when you're hiding what's still inside."

Mara ran her fingers along the mortar seam. "There's a gap. Top left. Freshly opened — see the scrape marks? Ferro got in here. Recently. Days."

"And whoever walled it originally didn't come back to check," I said, "because who checks on a grave?"

We widened the gap carefully, passing stones out hand to hand, and I'll admit I worked faster than caution strictly recommended, because the sweetness in the air was getting stronger and I wanted very badly to be wrong about it.

The last stone came free and the darkness beyond breathed out at us — cold, wet, old.

Tomas went first. He was the smallest, and he volunteered before I could assign it, which told me the boy needed to do something braver than drowning. I followed. Mara came last, dragging the pack with the camera and the sample kit, because Mara did not enter rooms — even rooms underground, even rooms full of the dead — without her instruments.

* * *

The chamber opened off the tramway like a side pocket, maybe eight meters deep, shored with rotting timber. Our three torch beams crossed and uncrossed, and for a long moment none of us said anything, because there was nothing in any language prepared for us that fit.

Crates. Dozens of them, stenciled with a name I knew from my closed-line maps — *Neuguinea Bergbau Konsortium*, the colonial mining consortium, the one whose successor company was currently paving half the Sepik with gravel roads and calling it development. Most were open. Some had been opened recently, by Ferro, his fingerprints still visible in the dust.

Ledgers. Rows of them, bound in oilcloth, stacked with the terrible neatness of bureaucracy. And photographs, bundled in rubber bands gone brittle. And along the far wall, laid out with more care than anything else in the room —

Bones.

Not scattered. Arranged. Skulls in one row, long bones in another, someone — Ferro, it must have been Ferro — had begun cataloguing them, little paper tags wired to the ones he'd reached. He'd gotten through perhaps thirty.

"Bloody hell," Tomas said, very quietly, which is what people say when the ordinary profanities would be obscene.

Mara lifted a ledger onto a crate lid and opened it with both hands, the way you handle a thing that might break or might bite. Her torch hovered over the page. I watched her face do something complicated — the scientist's hunger arriving first, and then, underneath it, the human being catching up.

"It's a payroll," she said. "It's a *payroll*, Casper. Daily labor rolls. Names, villages, task assignments." Her finger moved down columns of typescript. "And then a separate column. Look at the column heading."

I looked. The heading was in German, abbreviated, efficient. Beside each laborer's name, in that final column, entries appeared in a code that repeated too often to be random.

"What does it say?" Tomas asked.

Mara was quiet for a moment. When she translated, her voice had gone flat, the way voices go flat when feeling would otherwise be fatal. "*Verbraucht.* Expended. Like fuel. Like ballast." She turned pages. Dates in 1943, 1944. Names. Names. Names. And then, interleaved with the payrolls, requisition forms. Lime. Timber. Chain. Diesel. The mundane shopping list of an atrocity, filed in triplicate.

"They counted them," I said. "That's the part that gets me. They didn't just kill them. They *accounted* for them. Somewhere a clerk sat down at the end of each week and reconciled the dead against the ledger, and initialed it, and went home to his dinner."

"Is that better or worse than chaos?" Mara asked. Not really a question. "People imagine massacre as madness. This—" she touched the page "—this is bookkeeping."

Tomas had drifted to the photograph bundles. He untied one with fingers that shook, and I watched him look, and I watched him stop looking, and I put my hand on his shoulder and turned him gently away, and I looked instead, because somebody had to and I'd seen enough ugly in my life that it mostly bounced.

Execution photos. Men kneeling at a cutting above the river — the old channel, the one the water no longer used. Company men standing behind them with rifles, some smiling, actually smiling, the way men smile in photos with fish they've caught. On the back of each print, a stamp and a number. Filed. Indexed. Somebody's archive of murder, kept the way companies keep everything: in case it's useful later.

And then I saw the diversion surveys, and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Engineering drawings. My own trade, staring back at me from 1944. Channel realignment plans, headworks designs, flow calculations in a neat engineer's hand — calculations that showed, in cold figures, how they'd cut a new course for the river and stranded the old one, burying the cutting and everyone in it under forty meters of redirected water. Signed. Approved. There was a signature block at the bottom of the master plan, and above the signature, a title: *Chief Engineer, Hydraulic Works.*

A name I recognized. Not from history books — from the letterhead of the Kessler Development Group, whose founder's biography hung in the lobby of every office they'd ever built. *Pioneer. Visionary. Father of modern Sepik infrastructure.*

"He designed it," I heard myself say. "The diversion wasn't a cover-up arranged afterward. It was the cover-up from the start. They planned the massacre and the river in the same set of drawings. One project. One budget."

Mara came to stand beside me. Her shoulder touched mine and stayed there, whether for her comfort or mine I couldn't have said. "Kessler," she said.

"Kessler," I agreed. "Grandfather, probably. And the grandson is funding Hargreave's expedition to make sure this stays buried before the tribunal season opens." I looked around the chamber — the ledgers, the bones, the smiling men in the photographs. "Except Ferro got here first. And Ferro wrote down a buyer. Which means Roos isn't coming to recover heritage. He's coming to collect evidence and burn it."

"Then we take it first," Mara said. All the argument had gone out of her voice, replaced by something harder. "All of it. Tonight."

"Mara." I turned to face her. In the torchlight her eyes were bright and furious and certain, and I want it recorded that I understood her completely in that moment, and loved her a little for it, and still had to say what I said next. "If we carry these ledgers out of here, we become the most valuable cargo on this river. More valuable than we are survivable. Every man Roos has will be pointed at us."

"Then we'd better be worth chasing," she said.

Tomas made a sound. Small. We both turned. He was standing at the row of bones, holding one of Ferro's paper tags close to his torch, and his face had gone the color of the mortar outside.

"Tomas?" Mara said.

He read the tag aloud, though his voice barely carried it. "Village of Ambunti district. Laborer roll 44-C." He swallowed. "My grandfather came from a village in the Ambunti district. He was taken for labor during the war. My mother was born after he never came home." He looked up at us, and he was crying without any noise at all, which was somehow worse than sobbing. "Roll 44-C. Casper, that's — these are —"

"I know," I said.

Because I did know. Not his grandfather specifically. But the arithmetic of it — the way a name in a ledger reaches forward through decades and puts its hand on the back of your neck. I'd signed a report once, years ago, that let a company survive and a trestle's dead stay officially guilty of their own deaths. I knew exactly what it cost to be a line item in someone else's paperwork. I'd been one.

I didn't say any of that. Instead I sat down on a crate lid beside him, in a room full of the expended, and put my hand flat on the cold rail that ran past our feet — the same rail, the same gauge, the same iron from my map of closed lines — and I said, "We're going to carry all of them out of here. Every name in these books. That's the job now. Not the supplies. Not the delivery contract. This."

Mara crouched on his other side. "All of them," she agreed.

Tomas wiped his face with the back of his wrist, leaving a stripe of dust, and nodded, and began — methodically, terribly — to read names aloud from the payroll, one by one, like a man learning the size of a debt.

That was when I heard it.

Above us. Beyond the wall of stone. A footfall on the old tramway bed, deliberate, unhurried — the tread of someone who knew the ground and wasn't afraid of what slept in it. Then a second footfall. Then a light, sweeping across the gap we'd opened in the masonry, throwing our shadows long and thin across the bones.

Mara killed her torch. Tomas killed his. We stood in the dark among the dead of 1944, listening to the present arrive.

From beyond the wall, a voice called down into the chamber — conversational, almost friendly, in English with an accent I'd last heard across a negotiating table in Wewak:

"I know you're in there. And I'd very much like to talk about the ledgers."
