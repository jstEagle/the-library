## 16. Bones and Ballast

The ledgers smelled of bat guano and machine oil, and Tomas carried them out of the adit like a man carrying an infant through a burning house.

He wouldn't let anyone else touch them. Not Mara, who had the driest hands and the most careful habits; not Hargreave, whose authority had curdled into something Tomas no longer pretended to respect; not me, though I was the one with the tarp and the rope and the general habit of keeping things from falling apart. Tomas cradled the four volumes against his chest on the walk back down from the tunnel mouth, stepping over the rusted rails like a man walking over sleeping dogs, and when we reached the gauge station he set them on the survey table under the one working lamp and just stood there, breathing.

It was late afternoon by then, the light going amber through the slats of the shutters, moths already testing the lamp glass. The others had drifted outside — Hargreave to smoke and rehearse whatever version of himself he intended to be tomorrow, Mara to her charts, Ferro still feverish in the back room, muttering about chain ladders and the specific gravity of lies. That left me and Tomas and the ledgers.

"You should eat," I said.

"In a minute."

I knew that minute. It's the minute a man buys for himself when he knows that once he starts moving again, he'll have to be a person, and right now being a person is more than the job requires. I'd bought that minute myself in a dozen station houses and shipping offices. So I didn't push. I found the methylated spirits and a rag and cleaned the mud off my boots, which needed doing anyway and gave him the room to do whatever he was going to do.

He opened the first ledger at random, which is what you do when you can't bear to open it deliberately.

The columns were beautiful, in the way that evil is often beautiful — ruled lines, copperplate headers, a clerk somewhere in Rabaul or Sydney with a fine hand and a steady nib. *Labour Roll — Northern District Operations.* Names. Numbers. Wages, mostly fictional, entered against names that were real. And then the other column, the one that made the whole book a confession disguised as accounting: *Disposition.*

*Expended: 3.*
*Expended: 1 (dysentery).*
*Expended: 11 (cave-in, No. 2 adit).*
*Expended: 6 (refused work).*

Not *deceased*. Not *lost*. Expended — the word you use for fuel, for fuse wire, for anything consumed in the doing of the thing. Somebody had sat at a desk and chosen that word, and somebody above him had approved it, and nobody in the whole chain had ever once had to look at what the word meant, because the whole architecture of the ledger existed precisely so that nobody would ever have to look.

Tomas read. His lips moved. He's twenty-six, maybe twenty-seven, a geologist with quick hands and a laugh that comes too easily, the kind of young man who'd befriended me over a carburetor within two days of meeting me because friendship, for him, is just curiosity pointed at a person instead of a rock. I watched the laugh leave him the way water leaves a channel when someone dams it upstream.

"Casper." His voice came out wrong, flattened. "What does *expended* mean here? Exactly?"

"It means they killed them," I said. "Or worked them to death and wrote it down as consumption. Same entry either way."

He nodded slowly, as if I'd explained a geological process. Then he turned pages. Faster now, hunting. I understood that too — the way grief goes looking for itself, the way a man who has lost something will search a crowd for the face even knowing the face isn't there.

Except his grandfather had been here. That was the thing Tomas had said in the tunnel, or half-said, before the collapse of himself started. His family came from a village up near Ambunti. His grandfather had been taken for labor under the wartime administration — conscripted, the family story went, and never heard from again. A grandmother who waited eleven years and then married a man from downriver. A father who grew up with a hole where a history should have been. Tomas had come on this expedition, he'd told me once over engine repairs, partly because the Sepik was good fieldwork and partly because he wanted to understand the river that had swallowed his grandfather without leaving so much as a record.

Well. There was a record.

*Yawan Kombengi. Recruited Ambunti district. Age 31. Disposition: expended, 14 March, cause: refused loading detail. Approved: R. Voss, Asst. Supt.*

Tomas made a sound I've only heard twice in my life, both times from men reading their own death warrants. Not a cry. Something smaller and worse — the sound of a man discovering that the world has been keeping meticulous notes about the worst thing that ever happened to his family, filed and ruled and initialed, and that the notes survived while the man did not.

"He refused," Tomas said. "He refused to load. They killed him for refusing to load ore for the company that—" He stopped. Started again. "My father told me he ran away. That he probably lived. That he probably had another family somewhere, up in the mountains, and that's why he never came home. My father built his whole—" The sentence fell off a cliff. He put both hands flat on the table, the way you brace against a jolt.

"Tomas—"

"Don't." Not angry. Just full. A cup that cannot take one more drop. "Don't say anything yet. Please."

So I didn't. I got the paraffin stove going and put water on for coffee, because there are moments when the only honest contribution you can make to another man's suffering is heat, and I made it badly and slowly and let the noise of the kettle fill the room.

* * *

Mara came in at dusk. She took one look at the table — the open ledger, the boy bent over it like a branch under snow — and she looked at me, and I shook my head slightly, and she nodded and did not ask. She set her notebook down quietly, poured herself coffee she didn't drink, and sat across from Tomas and turned the pages with him, translating where the clerk's hand degraded, reading aloud the names of villages, building, name by name, the geography of the thing. She did it the way she does everything — precisely, without flinching, but also without hurry, giving each name its full weight of syllables. It was, I thought, the kindest act of scholarship I'd ever witnessed. She was making a map of the dead, and she was making it gently.

It was nearly midnight when Tomas found the photographs.

They were loose in the back of the third volume, tucked into a fold of the binding — prints gone silver-grey at the edges, the kind of photograph a government camera takes, all official angles and no mercy. Labor lines at the mine head. Men with numbers on boards hung around their necks. And three pictures of a pit behind the adit, filled the way the ledger's arithmetic required, with a supervisor standing at the edge in a pith helmet, hands behind his back, posture relaxed, a man at the end of a satisfactory day.

Tomas held the last one at arm's length, then close, then at arm's length again, as if distance might change the arithmetic.

"That's him," he said. "Third row. My mother has a photograph from his wedding — the same jaw. The same—" His voice went. He set the picture face-down on the table with exaggerated care, the way you handle something that might detonate, and then he put his face in his hands and broke.

I don't mean he cried. Crying is a weather event; this was structural. Twenty-six years of a story — *he ran away, he probably lived, he chose not to come back to us* — collapsing all at once into the truth, which was that his grandfather had been murdered at thirty-one for the crime of refusing, and that his father had grown up believing himself descended from a deserter rather than a martyr, and had carried that small shame his whole life like a stone in the boot, and would carry it until someone told him, and telling him would not remove the years the stone had worn.

And underneath that, the thing that had Tomas shaking: the company that did this had a name, and the name had changed its letters but not its blood, and that name was currently paying for the boat Tomas slept on.

Mara gathered the photographs and squared them and put her hand flat over them, a small gesture, protective, like covering a bird. She caught my eye across the lamp. In her look there was the argument we hadn't finished — *this is why it goes public, Casper, this is why* — and for once I couldn't find the counterargument. I just nodded, and her eyes said *we'll fight about it tomorrow*, and I could live with that.

She stayed until two, then went to check on Ferro. I stayed.

* * *

Here is what I know about inherited guilt, and I know it the way you know a river you've wrecked yourself on.

Nine years ago I signed a report. That's the whole of it, if you want the short version — a report on the Karawari trestle, a report that said the timber was sound when I had measured it myself and knew it wasn't, signed because a district manager with a soft voice and a hard schedule stood in my office and explained, kindly, what happens to a subcontractor's family when a line shuts down. Eleven months later the trestle came down under a loaded ballast train. Two men died. The inquiry found fatigue fractures consistent with my report's findings, and my signature held the whole structure up the way a keystone holds an arch, and when it was over I was the engineer who had certified the span, and the company paid its fines, and the district manager retired to a house with a view, and I became a man who owned no address.

People assume the shame is in the signing. It isn't. The shame is subtler than that. The shame is that I understood, even as I signed, exactly what I was doing — that I performed the moral arithmetic in real time, weighed two hypothetical deaths against a certainty of ruin, and chose, and then spent nine years telling myself the choice had been forced on me. Forced. As though force removes authorship. The men on that train had families. I have never learned their names, because learning them felt like a penance I hadn't earned the right to perform. That's the trick of inherited guilt — it doesn't belong to you, and that's precisely why you can't put it down. You carry your father's silence, your company's euphemisms, your own small signatures, and none of it fits your hands, and all of it rides on your back.

Tomas was carrying his family's stone now, freshly discovered, still warm from the ground. And I knew — not from wisdom, God knows, but from mileage — that what he needed tonight was not comfort. Comfort implies the thing can be made comfortable. What he needed was a witness. Someone to sit in the room while the truth finished demolishing the old story, so that when dawn came and he began, brick by brick, building the new one — *my grandfather was a man who said no, and they killed him for it, and saying no was the bravest thing anyone in our family ever did* — there would be one person alive who had watched him hold the pieces.

So I sat. I made coffee at intervals that had nothing to do with thirst. Once, around three, he asked me, in a voice scraped raw, "How do you carry it? Knowing things like this?" — meaning the ledger, meaning the pit, meaning the whole machinery — and I almost told him about the trestle. The words were actually there, queued up, nine years overdue. But it was the wrong night. Tonight was his; my confession would have been a theft, turning his grief into my absolution. So I said, "Badly, some days. But you carry it toward something. That's the whole trick. You point it at something worth the weight."

He looked at me for a long time across the lamp. Young eyes, old question. Then he said, "The tribunal. Mara's tribunal."

"If we get there," I said, because I am constitutionally incapable of letting hope travel unescorted.

"We'll get there," he said, and there was iron in it, the first iron since dusk. "My grandfather was ballast to them. A number in a column. I'm going to stand in a room in Port Moresby and read his name out loud until it costs them something."

I refilled his cup. Outside, the river talked to itself in the dark, running over ground that remembered, and somewhere downstream — I felt it more than heard it, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth — the world was arranging its next move against us.

* * *

Dawn came grey and dripping. Tomas had fallen asleep at the table around five, cheek on the closed ledger, and I let him sleep an hour past light before I woke him, because a man should wake in a room where someone stayed.

He rose, washed his face in the rain barrel, and when he turned around his eyes were swollen but level. Changed. Not healed — nothing heals in a night — but reorganized, the way rock reorganizes after pressure. He picked up the ledger with the photograph of the pit and wrapped it in oilcloth himself, carefully, and put it in the bottom of his pack beneath his spare shirt, and said, "This one travels with me," and I said, "Understood," and that was the entire negotiation.

Mara appeared with tea and the particular brightness she wears when she has been awake since four making charts of things that frighten her. Hargreave emerged last, smoking, watching Tomas with an expression I couldn't parse — guilt, maybe, or its cheaper cousin, apprehension. Ferro's fever had broken in the night, Mara reported; he was asking for food and asking, twice, whether anyone had counted the ledgers.

"Fourteen volumes," Tomas said. "Plus the photographs. Plus Ferro's notebook. All accounted for."

"Good," Ferro called weakly from the back room. "Because I counted the boats, and we're one short, and I don't think that's a coincidence."

I stepped out onto the station platform to look. The river lay low and brown-green in the early light, braided channels glinting, mist standing up off the water in columns. Our remaining boat sat where we'd left it, tied to the old loading piling. And far downstream, at the limit of vision where the channels braided into haze, there was a smudge that was not mist — too low, too steady, too deliberate in its approach.

An engine, running quiet, coming upriver.

I stood there a moment doing the arithmetic I'm best at — distances, current, fuel, time — and then I went inside and said, quietly, in a voice I kept very flat:

"Everyone pack. Now. We're done being the ones who arrived."
