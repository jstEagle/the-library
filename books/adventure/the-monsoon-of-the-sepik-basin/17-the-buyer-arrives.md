## 17. The Buyer Arrives

The floatplane came out of the east at half past four in the afternoon, which told me more about Anselm Roos than any dossier could have. Men who arrive at dawn want something done before anyone wakes up enough to object. Men who arrive at dusk want you to have spent the whole day thinking about them.

He'd been thinking about us thinking about him. That's a kind of courtesy, I suppose. It was also a kind of threat, and I've learned over the years that the two are usually printed on the same card.

I heard the engine first — that flat, expensive drone that says the aircraft has been maintained by someone who charges by the hour and is worth it — and I was already moving before Mara called my name from the station porch. By the time the plane swung wide over the river and lined up on the long reach of open water below the gauge station, I had counted heads through the field glasses Tomas had liberated from Ferro's kit: pilot, Roos, and six security men in matching grey shirts with no logos. No logos is its own logo. Prospecting companies put their names on everything; they're proud and they're advertising. Men who strip the branding off their shirts are telling you exactly what kind of work they expect to do.

"Seven," I said, handing the glasses to Mara. "Plus whoever's been shadowing us since the sudd."

"You're sure someone has been?"

"I'm sure the birds on the far bank keep getting up when there's nothing to scare them but men trying to be quiet."

She took the glasses and studied the water for a long moment. The light was going amber and the river had that late-day glassiness that makes every reflection look like a second world lying just under this one, waiting for its turn. Somewhere behind us, inside the station, Hargreave was shaving. I'd watched him do it — watched him take his time with it, the way a man does when he knows he's about to be looked at closely and wants to control what gets seen. He'd heard the plane too. Everyone within twenty kilometres had heard the plane. That was rather the point of it.

"He's coming to buy," Mara said. Not a question.

"He's coming to make an offer," I said. "Whether it's a purchase or a down payment on something worse depends mostly on how the afternoon goes."

"And Hargreave?"

"Hargreave has spent three weeks being paid by people he never met to dig up things he wasn't supposed to understand." I kept my voice level, because she didn't need me editorializing. "Men in that position generally find out at some point that they were hired for their ignorance, and it lands on them like a dropped anvil. Some get angry. Some get righteous. And some decide the anvil would be lighter if they held it from the other side."

She lowered the glasses and looked at me. She has a way of looking at you that makes you aware of the exact number of things you're not saying, and she was doing it now.

"And which one is he?"

"That's what we're about to find out."

* * *

Roos came ashore the way money comes ashore: without hurry, without wet feet, and with other people carrying things. The floatplane idled in the channel while a aluminium boat brought him and four of the grey shirts to the station's collapsed jetty. Two more stayed with the plane, and I noted that one of them spent the entire crossing scanning the treeline with a pair of optics considerably better than ours. They'd expected us to run. Or they'd hoped we would. A running man tells you where he's going.

Anselm Roos himself was not what the word *fixer* prepares you for. Fixers, in my experience, come lean and quick, all elbows and watchfulness. Roos was a big, soft-spoken man in his fifties with a sunburned scalp under thinning white hair, dressed in khakis that had actually been worn in rather than bought pre-worn, and he climbed the bank with the unhurried competence of someone who has spent decades being comfortable in exactly these places. He smiled at Hargreave like a brother. He shook hands all around, remembered everyone's name from a briefing he'd clearly memorized, and complimented the station's old colonial stonework as though he hadn't helped arrange for its radios to be smashed against it.

Then he saw me, and something happened that I want to set down precisely, because it mattered.

His eyes went over me the way a customs officer goes over a suitcase — not hostile, just thorough — and stopped for a fraction of a second on my hands. On the calluses, the old rope burns, the particular wear pattern of a man who has spent his life holding things together that are trying very hard to come apart. Then the eyes came back up and the smile arrived right on schedule, and he said, "And you'll be Mr. Iversen. The escort. Dr. Hargreave speaks highly of your river sense."

Which was interesting, because Hargreave had never once spoken my name aloud in front of the crew, and I doubted he'd wasted it on a phone call to Wewak. Which meant Roos had known I was here before he ever left wherever he'd left from, and knew my name, and had decided to demonstrate both facts in front of witnesses. A small message, wrapped in politeness: *we know who you are, we know why you're really here, and we haven't decided yet whether that's a problem.*

I gave him the flat, slightly vacant smile of a man whose chief qualifications are engines and silence. "River's got plenty of sense on its own," I said. "I mostly stay out of its way."

Something flickered behind his eyes — disappointment, maybe, or recalibration. Men like Roos collect first impressions the way other men collect debts, and I wanted mine filed under *harmless*. Whether it worked, I couldn't say. He turned back to Hargreave with the warmth of a reunion.

* * *

They met in the station's main room, because Roos suggested it and because suggesting is what he did. We cleared a table of sediment samples and Mara's charts — I caught her rolling her anomaly map tight and sliding it into the ceiling joists with a smoothness that made me proud and frightened in roughly equal measure — and then the grey shirts posted themselves at the door and windows, and Roos sat down across from Hargreave like a man settling in for a pleasant evening of accounts.

Mara and I stood at the back. Nobody asked us to leave, which I found more ominous than being asked.

What followed was, I will grant it this, beautifully done. Roos didn't open with numbers. He opened with history. He talked about the war, about the consortium, about how hard those years had been on everyone including the company men, about how the record of that era was fragmentary and painful and deserved careful, professional handling. He used the phrase *legacy obligations* twice and *healing* once, and each time he did, I watched Hargreave's shoulders come down a centimetre, because Hargreave was an academic, and academics are trained from birth to believe that anyone who uses institutional language must be operating inside an institution with rules.

Then, and only then, the numbers. And I'll be honest: even sitting where I sat, knowing what I knew, hearing the figure out loud produced a small vertigo in me. It was more money than the expedition's entire budget. It was more money than Hargreave would earn in the rest of his career, multiplied by a factor he'd need a napkin to calculate. In exchange: the ledgers, the photographs, whatever remained in the adit, and a signature on a confidentiality agreement drafted by lawyers whose names alone probably cost more than our boats.

"The material stays with the family," Roos said, and his voice had gone gentle in a way that was somehow worse than if it had gone hard. "Dr. Hargreave, you've seen what's down there. You know what publication would do. Not to us — to *them*." He gestured vaguely toward the river, toward the villages, toward the descendants of men listed in ledgers as ballast. "You'd be handing grief to people who have built their lives on not having it confirmed. Is that a kindness? We don't think so. We think the decent thing — the *scholarly* thing — is controlled disclosure. A memorial, properly funded. Compensation, properly structured. Dignity, managed by professionals."

It was magnificent. I mean that sincerely. Every sentence was true in isolation and false in sequence, which is the highest form of the art. And Hargreave — I watched him, and I want to report it fairly — Hargreave wavered.

Not cravenly. That's the thing I couldn't stop seeing. He wavered the way a man wavers when the corrupt offer and the honest one are wearing each other's clothes so well that he genuinely cannot tell which is which. His funding had come from Kessler all along; he'd told me that himself, wrapped in the language of heritage recovery, and I think part of him had believed it. Now the same hand that fed him was offering to feed the dead, and the cheque was enormous, and the alternative was a tribunal in Port Moresby that might take years and might, in the end, change nothing except how many graves got opened.

"Controlled disclosure," Hargreave repeated. His voice had gone careful, the way voices do when they're testing whether a floor will hold.

"With your name on the memorial," Roos said. "As the historian who made it possible. Done properly, respectfully, with the families consulted — by us, through channels they trust." A pause, measured to the millimetre. "Or it happens the other way. Files leak badly. Contexts get distorted. People get hurt who never asked to be part of this. I'd rather it be you, Doctor. I'd rather it be dignified. But the material is coming home either way, and the only variable under your control is whether that process honors you or erases you."

There it was. The polite offer, and underneath it, quiet as silt, the threat. *Erases you.* I glanced at the grey shirt by the window and found him watching Hargreave's hands, not his face. Professionals watch hands.

Mara's breathing had gone shallow beside me. I put two fingers lightly on her forearm — not to restrain her, exactly, but to remind her arm that the rest of her had plans for later — and felt the muscle underneath jump like a hooked fish.

Hargreave looked at the table. He looked at the contract Roos slid across it, all clean paper and numbered clauses. And then — and this is the moment I'll carry out of that room — he looked up, past Roos, directly at me.

Just for a second. Half a second. A man checking whether the ice will hold, and finding only my face where the answer should have been.

I gave him nothing. Not because I'm cruel, and not because I'd decided his soul for him, but because a look like that is a debt and I refuse to lend men my face to sign their surrender with. If Hargreave sold the dead, he was going to do it believing it was his own idea, and live with it accordingly. If he didn't — well. Then he'd earned the next hour himself.

He looked back down at the contract. His finger moved along clause four. Outside, the light was failing, and the floatplane's running lights had come on, red and green, floating on the darkening water like the world's most expensive fishing lure.

"Could I have until morning?" Hargreave said.

Roos smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Of course. Of course. These decisions deserve rest." He stood, buttoned his jacket, and became once again the reasonable man, the family man, the man who only wanted dignity for everyone. At the door he paused and turned, and his gaze traveled around the room — the sediment cores, the empty radio shelf, Mara, me — with the mild thoroughness of an appraiser.

"Gentlemen. Doctor. Get some sleep," he said pleasantly. "Tomorrow we can all stop worrying about the past."

* * *

After the boat took them back to the plane and the plane lifted off into the last light and droned away east, the camp sat in a silence with weight to it. Tomas built a fire nobody asked for, because his hands needed occupation. The grey shirts who'd stayed behind — two of them, quartered in the old survey office with their rifles broken down and cleaned, ostentatiously visible — ate rations from foil packets and pretended not to listen to us, which meant they were listening to everything.

Mara found me at the waterline, where I was doing the thing I do when I need to think, which is pretending to check mooring lines that don't need checking.

"Until morning," she said.

"Until morning."

"He'll sign."

"Maybe."

"Casper." She stepped in front of me, and the firelight caught the fury in her face and made it beautiful, which seemed unfair to both of us. "You saw him. Clause four. He was reading it like scripture."

"I saw a drowning man reading a life raft that happens to be nailed shut." I coiled a line that was already coiled. "People aren't decisions, Mara. They're weather. Hargreave tonight isn't Hargreave at dawn, and neither decision is the real one. The real one comes after — after he signs or doesn't, after he finds out what the signature costs either way."

"And if he signs? If he hands them everything tomorrow?" Her voice dropped. "Then it's gone. The ledgers, the photographs, the bones' names — sealed in some family vault for another fifty years, and every village on this river keeps swallowing the same lie for another generation, and we stand here and *watch*, because we're the help."

"We photograph everything first," I said quietly. "We've already photographed everything. Nothing leaves this river clean, whatever Hargreave decides. But hear me on this one thing—" I took her hands, because the argument had gotten close enough to our skin that words alone weren't holding it. "If it goes wrong tomorrow, it needs to go wrong slowly. Roos has seven men and a floatplane and, by Thursday, whatever else a phone call buys him on this river. We have a boat, a feverish geologist, and a woman who can read water better than God wrote it. So we let the morning happen. We watch which way Hargreave breaks. And then we move — fast, quiet, and downstream, with everything that matters already copied and already hidden in three places Roos hasn't thought of."

She was quiet a moment. Down the bank, the fire cracked, and one of the grey shirts laughed at something, a short corporate laugh with no one in it.

"You're enjoying this," she said finally. "Some part of you. The scheming."

I considered lying to her, and rejected it, partly on principle and partly because she'd catch it. "A little. Yes. There's a version of me that spent fifteen years being invisible because visibility got people killed, and this week that version gets to be useful instead of just absent. It feels like drinking cold water after a long walk." I turned her hands over in mine, absently, the way you check a knot. "But enjoy is a strong word for a game where the losing side shoots people."

"It's not a game."

"No," I agreed. "That's why I'm good at it. I've never once enjoyed anything I was good at."

She almost smiled. Then her eyes went past my shoulder, upriver, and sharpened, and I turned to follow them.

On the black water, well out in the channel, the floatplane should have been a diminishing drone and a memory. Instead its running lights had reappeared — red and green, low, moving slowly north along the far bank, then stopping. Holding position. Out of our firelight, out of casual sight, parked on the river like a patient animal.

Watching the station through the night.

"They're not leaving," Mara said.

"No," I said. "Buyers like Roos don't leave merchandise unattended overnight. And Hargreave—" I looked at the station, at the lamplight in the main room where our expedition leader sat alone with a contract and a conscience, with two armed men between him and the door and a fortune on the table. "Hargreave has until morning. But I suspect Roos intends to make sure the morning arrives with the answer already written."

Behind us, unseen in the dark, the lamp in the station window went out — and then, a minute later, came back on. Someone in that room was awake, and pacing, and deciding.

I started, quietly, counting the hours until dawn.
