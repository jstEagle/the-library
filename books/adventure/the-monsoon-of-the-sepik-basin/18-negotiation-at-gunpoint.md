## 18. Negotiation at Gunpoint

The trouble with polite men is that you spend so long waiting for them to become impolite that when it finally happens, they've already won.

Anselm Roos had been polite for three days. He'd shaken Hargreave's hand on the landing like a bishop greeting a donor. He'd complimented the gauge station's colonial stonework — "they built things to last, back then; shame about what they built them *for*" — and he'd eaten our rice and tinned fish with genuine appetite, asking Tomas about sediment cores with the air of a man who found science charming the way some men find horses charming. He'd even laughed at one of my jokes, which should have been my first warning. Nobody laughs at my jokes unless they're buying something.

On the fourth morning, over instant coffee that tasted like hot cardboard and regret, Roos set down his cup, folded his hands, and stopped being polite.

"Dr. Hargreave. I've made you a generous offer. I've made it twice. I'm not going to make it a third time."

Hargreave opened his mouth — I could see the negotiation forming in him, the academic's reflex to reframe, to find the third option, the footnote that saves everyone — and one of Roos's men stepped out of the doorway behind him and put a hand on his shoulder, not hard, just present, the way weather is present.

"There is no version of this," Roos went on, in the same mild voice he'd used to discuss rainfall data, "where those ledgers leave this river. There are only versions where more or fewer of you leave with them. I'd prefer the larger number. It makes the paperwork easier."

I want to tell you I did something brave in that moment. I want to tell you I stood up, or said something cutting, or made a move toward the rifle rack by the door. What I actually did was look down at my coffee and think, with the cold clarity of a man who has spent years perfecting the art of being overlooked: *he hasn't looked at me once.*

That was the mistake. His, not mine. Though God knows I'd spent forty-odd years building myself into exactly the kind of man nobody bothers to count.

* * *

They detained us in the old instrument room, which was fitting, because it had bars on the windows from the war era and a concrete floor that held the cold like a grudge. Six of us: Hargreave, Mara, Tomas, Ferro — who'd been found feverish upstream and was now shivering through whatever was burning in him, wrapped in every blanket we owned — young Priya from the sampling team, and me.

Roos's men took the radios, the satellite phone, the rifles, the boat keys, and Mara's camera bag, which she surrendered with a face so blank I knew she'd already done what she needed to do with the film. The ledgers and photographs from the adit they carried out under Roos's personal supervision, stacked in a steel document case like communion silver.

"They'll burn them," Mara said quietly, an hour in. She was sitting against the wall with Ferro's head in her lap, feeding him water a spoonful at a time. "He'll burn them tonight. On the beach, probably. He'll want witnesses among his own men so no one can steal them back and sell them."

"You sound very sure."

"I've read his kind," she said. "In three countries' worth of archives. They always burn them. It's the only ritual they have left."

Tomas sat apart from us, knees drawn up, staring at the barred window where a strip of river light moved slowly across the floor like something alive. Since the adit — since he'd found his grandfather's name in a column headed *EXPENDED, 1943–44, SEE ATTACHED* — he hadn't said ten words. I knew that silence. I'd lived inside a version of it for eleven years. It's not grief exactly, and it's not shock. It's the sound of a man renegotiating the terms of his own history, line by line, and finding the contract was never in his favor.

Hargreave paced. That was his contribution. Up and down the room, hands working at each other, muttering fragments of argument — "there are protocols, there are *channels*, if I could just speak to Kessler directly, man to man—" as though the problem were one of etiquette rather than bones.

"Hargreave." I kept my voice low. "Sit down before you wear a groove in their floor."

"I could have handled him," he said, not hearing me, or hearing me and needing not to. "If the radio—"

"The radio was cut before we ever arrived. You know that now. This wasn't improvised, Doctor. Roos came upriver with a plan and a schedule, and we were a line item."

He stopped pacing. Looked at me with something raw in his face. "And what would you have done? You, the escort? You were hired to carry crates."

"Yes," I said. "And I carried them. And here we all are."

It was crueler than it needed to be, and I watched it land, and I didn't take it back. Some part of me — the part that had signed a report under pressure twenty years ago and let a company survive and a bridge inspector's family bury an empty coffin — needed Hargreave to feel the weight of what compromise costs. The rest of me just wanted him quiet, because quiet was going to matter in the next few hours, and I was already counting hours.

* * *

Here is what being presumed harmless buys you, if you're patient enough to collect:

They fed us twice a day, and the man who brought the food was the youngest of Roos's security detail — a local hire, big through the shoulders, bored through everything else. He propped the door open with his boot while we ate because the room had no ventilation and his boss had complained about the smell. He kept his rifle slung rather than held, because we were six civilians including two women and a sick man, and because nothing in his training had prepared him for the possibility that the gray-haired white man in the corner might once have loaded mules across the Ok Tedi in wet season.

They counted us at dusk and dawn, aloud, in Tok Pisin, and the count was always the same word repeated: *siks. Siks.* Six. As though the number itself were a lock.

And they left the door's hinge-side unwatched during shift change — four minutes, maybe five, when the evening man walked the perimeter and the night man came up from the beach, and the corridor outside the instrument room belonged to nobody.

Four minutes is nothing. Four minutes is also, if you've spent a career calculating load tolerances and failure points, an eternity with a floor plan drawn across it.

I didn't move yet. Moving on the first night is what amateurs do. Amateurs see an open door and think *escape*. Professionals see an open door and think *what does he want me to see through it*.

So I lay on the concrete with my hands behind my head and I listened, and I built the station in my mind the way I'd once built trestles — member by member, load by load. The generator shed, running loud until ten, then silent: they didn't post a sentry on their own fuel. The patrol boat at the mooring, two men aboard, one asleep by the second night because the river at night does half your sentry work for you, humming and slapping at the hull until any ear stops believing it means anything. The floatplane gone — Roos had flown out the morning after taking the ledgers, back downstream to wherever his real office was, leaving eight men and a timetable. Eight men guarding six prisoners and a steel case full of paper that their employer intended to feed to a fire.

Eight men is a lot. Unless you know which three of them sleep badly, which one drinks, and which door doesn't latch.

Mara figured out I was figuring before anyone else did. Of course she did. She caught me watching the light through the bars at the same hour two evenings running, timing the strip of sun across the floor like a clock, and she crossed the room on the pretext of checking Ferro's fever and crouched beside me and said, without moving her lips much, "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at a river and it tells you its secrets. Except it's the station."

"It's not a river."

"No," she said. "But it has current." Her eyes flicked to the corridor, to the generator shed, to the mooring, cataloguing, and I felt that familiar vertigo of being seen by someone who reads the same language. "Tell me you have a plan and not just a hobby."

"I have a hobby," I said. "The plan arrives Thursday."

"Why Thursday?"

"Because Thursday the supply launch comes up from Angoram with our food, and somebody has to sign for it, and it won't be one of Roos's men, because none of them can write a manifest. It'll be one of us, escorted, at the beach, in the open, away from the others." I paused. "And because Ferro can't travel before then anyway, and I refuse to design an escape that leaves him."

She looked at me for a long moment. In the bar-light her face was all planes and patience, and underneath the patience something was burning the way it had burned in the adit, the way it burned whenever the truth got within arm's reach of her.

"When it happens," she said, "the ledgers come with us. Not the copies — the originals. Chain of custody starts with physical possession, Casper. If we run and leave the case, everything we suffered was tourism."

"Mara—"

"And if you say publication will get us killed again, save it. I've done the arithmetic too. Dead men don't testify, but neither do free men who stayed quiet. I've decided which kind I'd rather be." She touched Ferro's forehead, professional, gentle, then stood. "Thursday," she said, and went back to her patient, and that was the whole conversation, and it was more binding than any contract I'd ever signed.

* * *

Wednesday night, Roos's men burned something on the beach.

We heard it before we saw it — the particular roar a fire makes when it eats paper, a rushing sound like wind through a tunnel — and Tomas was at the bars first, fingers wrapped through the iron, watching the orange light jump on the water. None of us spoke. There was nothing to say that the fire wasn't already saying.

Except.

Except that I had spent Tuesday afternoon, during my escorted trip to the latrine, noting that the document case had come back from the floatplane lighter than it went. Except that Roos had flown out with a leather attaché that hadn't existed before, and had left instructions — I heard the tail of it through the instrument room's thin wall — that the case be held for his return Friday. *Held*, not burned. A careful man burns copies and keeps originals close; a careful man also keeps insurance against his own employees. Or maybe it was simpler than that: maybe Anselm Roos, heir to sixty years of buried arithmetic, couldn't quite bring himself to feed the last of it to a river beach while his employer's lawyers still argued about liability.

Or maybe — and this was the thought I liked best, the thought I turned over all Wednesday night like a stone with something underneath it — maybe the case on the shelf in the survey office was empty, and the real ledgers were already in the floatplane's baggage hold, and the fire was theater for the benefit of eight men whose discretion could no longer be trusted.

It didn't matter which. That was the beautiful, terrible thing I'd learned from watching companies operate my whole ruined life: it didn't matter which, because either way, the case was in a room with a door that didn't latch, guarded by a rotation I'd memorized, and by Friday noon Roos would land again and the question would be settled permanently, one way or the other, by fire or by flight.

Thursday had to work.

* * *

The supply launch came upriver at half past nine, riding high and empty except for rice, diesel, and a crate of batteries someone in Wewak had billed as instruments, because apparently that lie traveled well. Two of Roos's men walked Hargreave down to the beach to sign — Hargreave, because he was the expedition lead and the manifest wanted a lead's signature, and because Roos's sergeant understood hierarchy even if he didn't understand men.

I watched from the barred window as Hargreave signed. I watched the sergeant check the crates. I watched the launch crew — two brothers from Angoram I'd shared cigarettes with at the staging camp, men who owed me nothing and knew the river better than any of us — cast off slow, engine idling, the younger one standing in the stern looking back at the station a beat longer than cargo-handlers look at anything.

Then I watched the older brother reach into the bilge and come up with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and pass it across the gunwale to the sergeant as payment for passage — the way you do, on the Sepik, the way everyone does, a courtesy so ordinary that the sergeant took it with one hand while waving the launch away with the other.

Inside the oilcloth, if my arrangement with the brothers held, was a hacksaw blade, a spool of wire, a file, and a note in my own handwriting that said: *Count again at dusk. It may not be six.*

Because here was the thing I'd been building toward, the thing four days of being invisible had bought me: Roos's men counted us. *Siks,* they said, every dusk, every dawn, confident in their number. But numbers are only locks if everyone agrees on what's being counted — and I had spent Wednesday teaching Priya the generator shed's blind side, and teaching Tomas how a padlock sounds when it's about to fail, and telling Mara, in the dark, in the fewest possible words, the shape of Thursday.

At dusk the sergeant would count six prisoners in the instrument room, and he would be right, and it wouldn't matter, because by then the question wouldn't be how many of us there were.

It would be whether the steel case on the shelf in the survey office was heavy with the dead men's names — or whether I'd be opening it to find Roos's private joke, empty as a promise, and have to invent a whole new plan before Friday's floatplane touched the water.

The sun went down behind the ridge. Somewhere below, boots on gravel, a bored voice calling the count.

*Siks.*

For now.
