## 19. The Radio Trick

There is an old rule among men who spend their lives being underestimated: never let them see you count.

Roos's security chief — a broad, sunburnt Afrikaner type who introduced himself as Venter and then immediately stopped using my name at all — had looked at me exactly once since the crew was detained at the gauge station. That once was enough for him to file me under *harmless infrastructure*. The engineer. The escort. The man hired to keep the boats running and the beer cold. They took Hargreave's satellite phone, Tomas's knife, Mara's camera bag, and every radio on the expedition except the one bolted to the wall of the gauge house, which they smashed with a rifle butt on the first evening, mostly, I think, because it felt decisive.

They left me unbound because I asked to be. Not in so many words. I simply stood very still near the fuel drums when they were tying the others and made myself the kind of man nobody ties up. It is a skill. It requires a certain slouch, a certain willingness to look like the least interesting object in the room, including the drums. I have spent eleven years perfecting it. There are worse legacies from a ruined career.

So: they left me unbound, and they put me to work, because a camp runs better when the man who understands generators is doing something with his hands instead of brooding. Venter assigned me to keep the station's power plant fed and the water pumps primed, and he did it with the airy contempt of a man delegating to furniture. Which meant that twice a day, alone, I walked into the pump shed at the back of the compound, and nobody watched me do it.

The pump shed shared a wall with the old signal room.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is what kept me alive that week. Colonial gauge stations on the upper Sepik weren't just hydrology posts. They were nodes. Water level, rainfall, river temperature — all of it telemetered back down the line, because a mining consortium that moves rivers wants to know where its rivers are going. And telemetry means transmitters. And a consortium paranoid enough to bury a massacre does not trust its primary transmitter to survive a lightning strike, a flood, or a disgruntled operator with a hammer. So they built backups. And then, when the war ended and the ledgers went into the tunnel and the whole enterprise was quietly amputated from the corporate record, somebody forgot to dig the backup out of the wall.

Somebody forgot. Or somebody hoped. Ferro's notebook had mentioned the signal room in passing — *station log references 'aux set' serviced 1959, location obscured* — and I had read that line four times before the radios got smashed, because reading other people's paperwork is the one vice I've never managed to quit.

* * *

The first two days of captivity, I did nothing but look.

This is the part that would have driven Mara mad, so it's fortunate she couldn't see me. She'd have wanted action by lunchtime of day one. But there is a grammar to searching a building that doesn't know it's being searched, and the first rule of the grammar is patience. You don't go to the wall. You let the wall come to you. You carry things past it. You lean on it. You learn its temperature, its dampness, the way sound comes off it when you knock a drum against it by accident-on-purpose.

The signal room's door had been painted over sometime in the sixties and again sometime in the eighties, both coats failing, and it sat flush in the north wall of the pump shed behind a rack of spare impellers that nobody had moved since the last Australian left. Flush doors are honest; fake ones sit proud or swing wrong. This one sat flush. But the paint around its frame had cracked in a pattern I recognized, because I'd spent a career looking at buildings that were pretending to be something else. Cracks radiate from movement. This frame hadn't moved in forty years, which meant the door behind it hadn't either, which meant whatever was inside had been sealed rather than abandoned.

Sealed rooms keep their secrets dry. Abandoned rooms give them to the termites.

On the third morning, while Venter's men ran their patrol boat upriver on another fruitless sweep for Ferro, I moved the impeller rack. It took me twenty minutes and cost me a splinter the length of my thumb, and when the rack came away, the painted door came with it — hinges long dead, the whole thing lifting free like a lid. Behind it: a steel cabinet door, green, weeping rust at the seams, with a handle that turned after some argument.

Inside, the aux set.

It was beautiful, in the way that only things built by people who expected them to matter can be beautiful. A valve transmitter, military surplus by the look of the chassis, hand-wound coils, a crystal oven, and a power supply that had been designed by someone who assumed the grid was a rumor. Alongside it, wrapped in waxed paper gone amber with age, a spare crystal set in labeled sleeves — frequencies chosen by a dead man for a world that no longer existed. And beneath the whole assembly, a maintenance log in a canvas cover, the last entry dated March 1961: *Aux set tested. All parameters nominal. Recommend continued quarterly checks.* Signed with initials. Quarterly checks that had stopped fifty-odd years ago, and yet here the set was, oiled, greased, sealed against the damp, waiting for a check that never came.

I stood in the dark of that little room and felt something I hadn't felt since before the trestle. Not hope exactly. Hope is for men whose problems are ahead of them. It was more like recognition. Here was a machine built to speak and silenced by neglect, tucked into a wall where everyone had forgotten it, keeping its frequency all these years.

I knew how it felt. And I knew, with the particular cold clarity that used to make my superiors call me difficult, exactly what I was going to make it say.

* * *

Repairing a 1961 valve transmitter with scavenged parts, in a locked shed, under the noses of fourteen mercenaries, takes a certain relationship with time. Fortunately, captivity provides it.

The problem wasn't the radio, not really. Valves are valves; they either hold vacuum or they don't, and only two of the eight had failed, and the spares drawer — because of course there was a spares drawer, this was German engineering applied to colonial paranoia — held replacements in wax. The problem was power. The set wanted 230 volts of clean AC, and the station generator was running a camp full of floodlights and a freezer full of Roos's imported sausages, and any draw I added would show up on somebody's fuel arithmetic. Venter counted his diesel the way misers count coins. I'd watched him do it every evening, pencil behind his ear, lips moving.

So I couldn't steal power. I had to borrow it invisibly.

The answer was sitting in the corner of the pump shed wearing a coat of dust: a battery bank, glass-cased, lead-acid, installed for the original telemetry system and never removed because removal costs money and abandonment doesn't. Six cells. Two were dead — sulfated white, finished — but four held charge, and the station still had a trickle charger wired to the mains that someone had never bothered to disconnect because it drew almost nothing. Almost nothing is the operative phrase. A trickle charger feeding a battery bank draws less than the freezer's door seal leaks cold. It disappears into the noise floor of Venter's arithmetic.

For three nights I worked by the light of a torch shielded through a slit in my shirt pocket — an old surveyor's trick, learned from a Norwegian who taught me more about honesty than any employer I ever had. I cleaned contacts with sandpaper torn to the size of a stamp. I re-tensioned the coil windings with a needle from the medical kit, boiled first. I rebuilt the antenna feed where forty years of moisture had turned the coax into green lace, splicing in a length scavenged from the smashed main set's remains, which Venter's men had left in a tangled heap behind the station like the skeleton of a murdered animal. The antenna itself was the easiest part: the original wire dipole was still strung between two coconut palms behind the compound, sagging, invisible against the sky, cut in half by whoever had killed the main radio — but a dipole cut in half is just two wires waiting to be rejoined, and I rejoined mine at two in the morning with a soldering iron heated over the pump shed's kerosene lamp, breathing the fumes and thinking, absurdly, of my father's workshop in Bergen, and how he'd said a man should always be able to fix three things: a fence, a friendship, and a radio.

He'd been joking about the third one. Or I'd decided he was. Either way.

By the fourth night the aux set hummed. The dial light glowed a soft, patient orange, the color of a lamp in a window you thought was empty. I keyed the test switch, listened to my own carrier come back off the ionosphere like an echo from a deeper room, and allowed myself thirty seconds of pure, undiluted satisfaction before the fear arrived, right on schedule, to ask the obvious question:

Now what do you say?

* * *

A lie is a load-bearing structure. Get one element wrong and the whole thing comes down on you.

I lay on my bunk that night — they'd given us the old laborers' quarters, a long room with mosquito nets and the smell of decades — and I built the lie the way I used to build trestles, member by member, testing each joint before trusting it with weight.

What did Roos's men fear? Not us. We were seven civilians, disarmed, outnumbered, surrounded by water they didn't understand. What they feared was complication. Specifically, they feared witnesses moving, evidence moving, and — above all — Ferro loose in the bush with whatever he'd carried out of that tunnel. They had spent four days sweeping the river for him and finding nothing, and I could hear it in the way Venter's voice had shortened each evening, the reports getting terser, the silences longer. Men on a failed search start hearing ghosts. My job was to give the ghosts a body.

Second question: what would a real distress call sound like? Not a Mayday — this wasn't open ocean, and a Mayday invites every ear in the basin, including Wewak, including the tribunal's friends, including people Roos could not afford. No. What I needed was something quieter and more credible: a routine check-in that goes wrong. The expedition had missed its scheduled contact with Wewak days ago — that silence was the whole reason we'd found the station ransacked — and Roos's men knew it. So the most believable thing in the world was a fragmentary, damaged transmission from downstream. Someone else's radio. Someone else's trouble.

Third question: where? Downstream, per the assignment my own instincts had already written. Because Roos's security detail was concentrated upriver and around the station, and because the one direction that threatened his operation was the direction evidence could travel — toward Port Moresby, toward lawyers, toward daylight. If I could convince him that trouble was brewing below, he would have to send men below. Half his force, ideally. Enough.

And the final element, the keystone: the transmission had to be imperfect. Real emergencies are messy. Clean lies smell like paint.

I spent the fifth day doing nothing whatsoever, hauling diesel and priming pumps, smiling my furniture smile at Venter, while inside my skull the message assembled itself word by word. On the sixth night, at 0230, with the camp asleep and the patrol boat riding dark at its mooring, I unlocked the pump shed with the key I'd copied from the ring Venter hung on a nail — copied in soap first, then filed from a tin lid, a trick from my railway days that I confess I performed with more pleasure than was strictly dignified — and I sat down in front of sixty years of dust and orange light, and I spoke.

Not as Casper Iversen. As nobody. A voice pitched flat, clipped, half-lost in static I generated by keying the microphone against the transformer hum. I gave a callsign that belonged to a mission boat that plied the middle Sepik — a real boat, a real callsign, lifted from a frequency list I'd memorized in Wewak — reporting, in broken fragments, that they had picked up survivors. Two men. One feverish, one with a broken arm. Picked up at the confluence, drifting on a raft of bamboo, saying they'd come from the gauge station, saying there had been violence, saying they needed to reach Angoram, needing to reach Angoram, say again, needing—

And then static. Then, after a ten-count that I stretched until my hands shook, a single repeat of the position and the words *moving downstream tonight*, and then I pulled the plug on the modulation circuit and let the carrier die mid-word, which is the single most convincing thing a dying radio ever does.

Then I sat in the dark and listened to my own heartbeat and understood that I had just fired a shot that could not be taken back. Somewhere in the night, if anyone had been listening — and someone was always listening; the basin was full of ears, mission sets, police sets, Roos's own monitoring gear — a ghost had just spoken. By dawn it would be a story. By noon it would be a decision.

I cleaned the shed, resealed the wall, replaced the impeller rack, and walked back across the compound a free man in every way except the one that mattered.

* * *

The decision arrived faster than I'd dared hope.

Venter came out of the command hut at 0740 with the monitoring log in his fist and a look on his face like a man who has found water in his fuel. I was stacking crates by the landing, visible, dull, essential. He walked past me without a glance, which was the finest review my acting had ever received, and twenty minutes later the camp changed shape.

I heard it in pieces, the way you hear weather. Voices at the floatplane dock. The patrol boat's engine turning over, that deep diesel cough. Venter's voice, sharp, giving numbers — six, I counted, six names called and six men wading out with kit bags and the big mounted gun unlashed from its cradle and swung aboard. Roos himself appeared on the station steps in a linen shirt pressed despite everything, and I watched him listen to Venter and watch the arithmetic move behind his eyes. Survivors. From the station. Drifting downstream with God knew what in their pockets. Every hour those men floated was an hour closer to a town, a phone, a newspaper.

"Take the boat," Roos said. "Sweep the confluence. If they're real, bring them to me. If they talk to anyone else first—" He didn't finish it. He didn't need to. The sentence finished itself in everyone's stomach.

"And the prisoners?" Venter asked, jerking his chin at our quarters without, I noted, calling us prisoners. Euphemism is the last luxury of guilty men.

"Hargreave stays. The woman stays. The boy stays." A pause, and then, with the mild contempt that was going to save all our lives: "Leave the engineer. Who's he going to call?"

Six men went downstream on the patrol boat at 0915, running fast, burning fuel, chasing my ghost into the braided channels below the confluence. Fourteen became eight. Eight guards, one Roos, one Venter, and a floatplane pilot who cared about nothing but his engine hours.

I stood at the landing and watched the wake flatten out around the bend, and I felt the specific, terrible lightness of a man who has moved a great deal of weight with one small lever and now has to live under whatever it drops on.

Because here is what the lie bought us and what it cost. It bought us a camp with holes in it. It bought us a night — maybe two — before Venter's sweep found nothing at the confluence and came back angry and suspicious, and suspicion is a dog that remembers. It cost us the truth that the moment the phantom survivors failed to materialize, every unsolved oddity in this camp would get re-examined, starting, if Venter had one grain of the sense his pay suggested, with the quiet man who fixed generators and asked for nothing.

One night. Maybe two. After that, the walls would start asking questions.

I found Mara at the water tank behind the quarters, ostensibly washing bandages, actually watching the empty channel with the expression of a woman composing a map in her head. I crouched beside her, kept my eyes on the river, and said, very quietly, the only sentence that mattered:

"The sudd, south channel. How far can you take a boat through it at night?"

She didn't look at me. She wrung out the bandage, and I saw her mind lay my words over her charts the way she laid everything — carefully, edge to edge, testing the fit.

"Far enough," she said. "If we leave before they come back."

Downstream, six of Roos's men were hunting a wreck that existed nowhere but inside a sixty-year-old radio and me. Upstream, the calving season was counting down toward a pass that would open for nine days and no more. And somewhere in the floating gardens of the south channel, in water Mara had charted in defiance of every official map, a boat waited with its engine cold and its tanks full.

We had one night. I intended to spend all of it.
