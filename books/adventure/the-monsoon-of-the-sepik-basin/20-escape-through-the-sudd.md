## 20. Escape Through the Sudd

I have spent eleven years arguing with rivers, and I want it on the record that this was the first time one argued back.

The sudd had come down in the night — that's what Casper called it, though the word belongs properly to the Nile, to the great floating barriers of vegetation that strangle whole channels for seasons at a time. Here in the Sepik it was smaller but no less malicious: a raft of water hyacinth and cut grass and uprooted pandanus, kilometers of it, riding the current down from some flooded oxbow and parking itself across every navigable line on my charts like a hand slapped over a mouth.

We stood on the bank in the grey before dawn and looked at it.

"That's new," Tomas said.

"Rivers are allowed to do that," I said. "It's one of their few pleasures."

Casper said nothing. He was doing his reading-the-water thing, head tilted slightly, eyes moving across the green mat the way other men read ledgers. Behind us, upstream, the gauge station still smoldered faintly where Roos's men had fired it — a thin column of smoke standing against the pale sky like an accusation nobody had gotten around to filing yet. We had maybe two hours before whoever set the fire came looking for whoever fled it.

One boat. The longboat, the one with the good engine and the bad manners. Everything else was ash or abandoned: the supply crates, the fuel drums, Hargreave's precious instrument cases, three weeks of food. All of it either burning or sitting in plain view for the Kessler Group's security detail to inventory at their leisure.

What we had instead was a waterproof map case pressed flat under my arm, Ferro's notebook wrapped in plastic inside Casper's shirt, and a roll of film canisters and a data card in a tin that had once held sardines. And Ferro himself — found alive at dawn, half a kilometer upstream, wedged into the root tangle of a fallen fig tree like something the river had decided, after consideration, to give back.

He was alive. That was the triumph part. He was also burning with fever, shivering so hard his teeth chattered in Morse code, and mumbling about adits and ledgers and *the buyer comes Thursday* in a voice scraped down to gravel. That was the cost part, and we hadn't even gotten to the actual costs yet.

"We can't go through that," Hargreave said. He'd barely slept. His hands wouldn't stay still. "Nobody goes through that. You'd ground us in ten meters and they'd pick us off from the bank like — "

"I know," I said.

"Mara." He said my name the way you'd say *please reconsider* if you'd forgotten how to beg. "Mara, listen to me. There are men upstream with rifles and a floatplane and a budget, and downstream there is nothing but river, and between those facts there is a wall of plants, and you're proposing — "

"I'm not proposing anything yet," I said. "I'm remembering."

That stopped him. It should have. Because here is the thing about cartographers that nobody tells you: we don't just draw what's there. We draw what was there, and what might be there again, and — when we're honest, which is rare — what everyone else has agreed to forget. For six weeks I had been making a second chart. Not the official channel survey, not the pretty laminated product Dr. Hargreave could present to funders. A working chart, pencil on tracing paper, of everything wrong with this river. Sediment layers that couldn't occur naturally. Banks that changed character mid-reach as if the water had been introduced to them recently and hadn't settled in. Anomalies. And threaded through all of it, because a river remembers its old beds the way a body remembers an amputated limb, were the ghost channels — the courses the Sepik had run before men with ledgers moved it.

And ghost channels, I knew, ran clear of sudd more often than main channels did. Floating vegetation follows the deep, slow, nutrient-rich water. The dead channels starve it out.

"Casper," I said. "Your closed-line map. The spur that runs along the north bank above the station. Where does it cross the floodplain?"

He didn't ask why. That was the thing about Casper Iversen; he rationed his questions like a man who'd learned the hard way that questions cost. He pulled the folded survey sheet from his jacket, opened it against his knee, and put his finger on a dotted line that died twice before reaching the escarpment.

"Here. And here. Causeway embankments, probably still standing. Railroads don't like water, so they'd have been built on the high ground between channels." He looked up at me, and I watched him get there a half-second before I explained it. "You're saying the old riverbed runs under the old railway."

"I'm saying the company that diverted a river also built a rail line beside its corpse, and both of them are still down there, and neither one of them is on any chart anyone else owns."

Tomas made a sound that was half laugh, half prayer. Hargreave looked at the sudd, at the smoke, at Ferro shaking in the bottom of the boat, and performed the arithmetic of a man whose coward's ledger had finally refused to balance.

"Do it," he said.

* * *

Getting into the sudd was easy. Getting through it was the work of the day.

The trick — and I use the word with full awareness that it sounds insane — was to treat the floating vegetation not as an obstacle but as a surface. You cannot plow through hyacinth mat with a propeller; the roots foul the intake in seconds and you die slowly and stupidly in sight of open water. But the mat itself floats on perhaps forty centimeters of slack water, and beneath the mat, in the old channels, there was sometimes a meter and a half of the Sepik's forgotten blood. If you could find the seams — the places where the mat thinned over the ghost channel, where the current underneath still breathed enough to keep a lane open — you could pole and winch and drag the boat along the top of the world like a sled over pack ice.

Casper poled. Tomas winched, using a block rigged to a sapling we carried aboard for exactly no reason anyone could name until today. I stood in the bow with my tracing paper and called the seams.

"Port. Ten degrees. Follow the brown water — see it? Where the mat's darker, that's deeper underneath. Trust the color."

"I trust the color," Casper said, "and I distrust everything else, which is why we're going to live."

It took us four hours to cover what the open river would have given us in twenty minutes. The sun climbed and turned the sudd into a furnace — heat rising off the vegetation in visible waves, insects rising off everything else. Twice we grounded hard and had to unload half the boat and haul it over a lip of matted root by hand, Casper waist-deep in the black water with a pole under the hull, saying nothing, which from Casper was a speech. Once a crocodile surfaced thirty meters off — just the eyes and the ridged snout, motionless, taking our measure — and slid under without a ripple, and nobody commented, because commenting would have meant admitting we'd all seen it.

Ferro lay in the stern under a tarp, and I checked him at every stop. The fever climbed through the morning. By noon he was talking to people who weren't there — I caught fragments, a woman's name, a university, *don't sign it, don't sign the report* — and his skin had gone past flushed into something greyer. We had antibiotics in the medical kit. The medical kit was in a crate at the bottom of the station storeroom, or possibly already ash. I wrote that fact down in my head and filed it under costs, and kept calling seams.

Somewhere in the early afternoon we passed directly over the place where, according to my anomaly chart, the diversion works began. I felt it before I could prove it — a change in the drag on the hull, a smoothness, the sense of moving over engineered water. And there, off the port side, breaking through the mat like a knuckle through skin: a line of dressed stone running straight as a rule across the floodplain. Cutoff wall. Nineteen-thirties concrete, colonial masonry, the finger of the company that had reached into the river and turned it.

Tomas stared at it. "They built that," he said. "To hide — "

"To move a river," Casper said quietly. "Hiding was a bonus."

I marked it. Of course I marked it. Coordinates, bearing, description, in pencil, on tracing paper, in a boat full of fever and fear. Some habits are the whole person.

* * *

We cleared the last of the sudd at dusk, sliding out of the green into a stretch of open brown water so wide and calm it felt like being forgiven. Behind us the mat stretched back to the horizon, unbroken, a country unto itself. If Roos's men had boats on the main channel, they were now looking for us in entirely the wrong geometry.

Casper shipped the pole and sat down heavily next to the engine, and for a moment he let his face do what it wanted, which was to look sixty years old and profoundly tired. Then he caught me watching and rearranged it into something more marketable.

"Not bad," he said, "for someone who argues with rivers."

"I won," I said.

"You negotiated a settlement. Rivers always leave something on the table."

I laughed — actually laughed, out loud, in the middle of everything — and the sound startled the birds out of the pandanus fringe, and for about ten seconds none of us were fugitives.

Then Ferro cried out.

It wasn't words. It was a single high sound, animal, and it emptied the boat of everything except urgency. We got the tarp back and found him rigid, eyes rolled white, hands clawing at the air. Malaria, almost certainly — cerebral, the kind that doesn't negotiate. The shivering had stopped, which was worse than the shivering. His skin burned under my hand like a stone left in the sun.

"He needs a hospital," I said. "Not tomorrow. Now."

"The mission station at Ambunti is eight hours downstream," Casper said. "If the engine holds and the checkpoints haven't grown overnight."

"And if they have?"

He didn't answer, which was an answer. Roos had bought the river police with a phone call — we'd heard it in Hargreave's panicked briefing before the fire, the way power shows how cheaply it purchases law. Every junction, every landing, every fuel stop between here and Wewak could have a boat with a blue light and a manifest of names. Ours were probably on it.

Hargreave crouched by Ferro and held his wrist, counting a pulse that was running away from him. When he looked up, his face had done something I hadn't seen it do in the entire expedition: it had become honest.

"There's a back channel," he said. "Below Ambunti. The Karawari branch — it rejoins the main river south of the police post. If we take it, we miss the checkpoint entirely." He swallowed. "It's not on the official charts. It's on yours, isn't it? On the other one."

I looked at him for a long moment. Six weeks of clashing over safety margins, over pride, over the difference between a map that flatters and a map that tells the truth — and here he was, finally, asking for the true one.

"It's on mine," I said.

"Then take us," Hargreave said. "Take us the way only you can."

I got out the tracing paper and smoothed it on my knee, and drew the line south while the light failed, and the boat ran on into the dark with the engine low, carrying a dying man, a stolen archive, and five people who had stopped pretending to be anything other than what they were.

* * *

Near midnight, Casper found me at the bow.

The others were asleep in the way exhausted people sleep in boats — badly, upright, waking at every change in the engine note. Ferro's breathing was shallow but steady, and Tomas had appointed himself nurse and would not be relieved. The moon was down. The river made its endless remarks to itself.

Casper leaned on the gunwale beside me and didn't say anything for a while. With him, silence isn't absence. It's a room he invites you into.

"They'll burn it," I said, finally. "Roos will find a way. Even if we make it, even if the tribunal gets the ledgers — chain of custody, provenance, they'll argue the documents are forged, that we planted them. Companies like that don't lose to paper. They lose to witnesses, and witnesses are expensive to keep alive."

"You've been planning the losing," he said. "All day, in the bow, drawing your lines. You've been rehearsing it."

"Somebody has to. You rehearse escape routes. I rehearse grief. We make a balanced crew."

He was quiet a moment longer. Then he said, "When the trestle went down — the one they blamed me for — there was a report. I signed it. Not because it was true. Because a man I trusted told me the truth would end four hundred jobs instead of one career, and I was young enough to think that was arithmetic." He said it evenly, without drama, the way you recite something you've paid off in installments for years. "Kessler's people wrote that report. Different name then. Same handwriting."

I turned to look at him. In the dark I could only see the outline of him, and somehow that made it easier for both of us.

"So you know," I said. "Exactly how they'll bury it."

"I know the shovel," Casper said. "Which means I know where they dig." He straightened, and I heard something in his voice I'd never heard there before — not resolve exactly; resolve is cheap. Certainty. "Give me three days and a mailing address, Mara. Give me the tribunal investigator I testified for, and the families' names from Ferro's notebook. We don't publish. We deliver. Copies, sealed, simultaneous, to people who already hate them. Let Kessler spend its fortune buying back a truth that's already been posted."

"And you?" I asked. "What does that cost you? Your invisibility — the whole architecture of your life. No address, no name on anything. You mail that evidence and you're permanent record forever."

"Yes," Casper said. And then, in a voice so quiet the river nearly kept it: "I'm starting to think I'd rather be found than safe."

I didn't answer. There was nothing to add that the dark between us wasn't already saying. I put my hand over his on the gunwale, and he turned his palm up and held on, and we ran the black river together toward a dawn neither of us had charted.

Behind us, far upstream, the sudd lay across the water like a closed door. Ahead, somewhere past the bend, a light blinked twice and went dark — too regular for a village, too brief for a mistake.

Casper saw it too. His hand tightened around mine.

"Karawari junction," he murmured. "Somebody's awake down there."

The engine ticked over, low and patient, carrying us toward whatever it was.
