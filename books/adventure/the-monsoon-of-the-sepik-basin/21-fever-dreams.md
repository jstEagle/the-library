## 21. Fever Dreams

Ferro talked in his sleep, and what he said was numbers.

Not names, not prayers, not the usual fever litany of mothers and missed chances. Numbers. Gauge readings. Elevations. Tonnages hauled out of a mountain by men who kept books on everything, including the people they spent to keep the books quiet. He lay in the stern of the beached boat under a tarp we'd rigged into something between an infirmary and a confession booth, and he recited figures to a ceiling of canvas as if somewhere in their arithmetic was a door he could walk through and come out the other side unburned.

"Thirty-one point four," he murmured. "They logged them at thirty-one point four per hundred."

I wrung out the cloth and laid it across his forehead. Thirty-one point four per hundred. I didn't want to know what unit that was. I knew anyway. I'd spent three days with Ferro's notebook open on my knees while Casper stood watch, and the notebook had taught me the consortium's vocabulary: *expended*, *attrited*, *written down*. A mining company's ledger is just a map of a different kind — one where human beings are contour lines, and some of them fall below sea level.

"He's climbing again," Casper said from the bow, where he sat with the repaired radio across his knees like a cat he didn't entirely trust.

"You say that like it's weather."

"It is weather." He nodded at Ferro without looking up. "Infection's a front. It moves or it doesn't. You just watch which way it goes."

I had learned over the past weeks that Casper Iversen expressed tenderness almost exclusively through meteorology. He couldn't say *I'm worried about him* any more than he could say *I'm worried about you* or *I have noticed that when you argue with rivers you smile* — but he could tell you the barometer was falling, and he meant all of it.

I pressed the cloth flat against Ferro's temple. Under the tarp the heat was a physical thing, the Sepik Basin breathing its wet breath over us, and Ferro burned inside it like a coal someone had banked too deep. We'd found him upstream of the station, wedged into a culvert with a tin of condensed milk and Ferro's own notebook wrapped in oilcloth, delirious, one ankle swollen to twice its size where something in the water had tested him and found him not yet ready. Three days of antibiotics scraped from the expedition med kit, three days of me playing nurse with hands better suited to drafting pens, and still the fever came back each evening like a tide that hadn't gotten the memo about retreat.

"Mara." His eyes opened partway, unfocused, finding my face without quite landing on it. "You have to log it. If you don't log it, it never happened."

"I'm logging it," I said. "Everything. Sleep now."

"The adit. Chainage four-two-oh. The rails are forty-two pound per yard, wartime standard, they match the—" A cough took him, wet and long, and I held the canteen to his lips until it passed. "They match the spur," he finished, thinner. "The one that isn't there anymore."

"I know. I've got it. Four-two-oh."

He smiled then, a small, cracked thing, and went under again. And I sat there in the heat holding a dying man's hand and thinking about chainage four-two-oh, because that was who I had become in the space of a month: a woman who measured her life now in the coordinates of a mass grave.

* * *

Casper found me an hour later at the waterline, where I'd spread my anomaly charts on a groundsheet weighted with stones. The river moved past us in its brown, patient way, carrying silt from a reroute sixty years old, and I sat cross-legged above it drawing lines that official hydrology would call impossible.

"Sediment cores from the east channel," I said, before he could ask. "I finished the last two this morning. Look." I tapped the chart. "Coarse gravels here, in a channel that today carries nothing but fine silt. That's a high-energy deposit. That channel used to carry serious water. Then it stops — hard stop, datable horizon — and the flow signature jumps to the main stem downstream of the gauge station. The river didn't move itself, Casper. Rivers don't resign. Someone redirected it, and whoever did it wanted the old channel dead."

"And the old channel runs past the mine."

"Past the adit. Which means before the diversion, the tunnel mouth either flooded seasonally or was visible from the water. Burying the massacre wasn't enough. They buried the *approach*. They turned a river into a locked door." I looked up at him. "You understand what this is? This isn't evidence of a crime. This is the crime's *architecture*. You can't divert a river by accident. The engineering alone—"

"I know what it is." He crouched beside the charts, and for a while he said nothing, which with Casper was never empty space; it was load-bearing silence. When he finally spoke, his voice had the flatness I'd learned to read as its most serious register. "There's a tribunal session convening in Port Moresby. End of next month. Natural resources and wartime conduct — it's been sitting on Kessler's development approvals like a dog on a bone, waiting for something with teeth."

I stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"Because I've been listening to the shortwave bands since Wewak, and because Okuk talks when he drinks, and because men like Roos don't charter floatplanes for heritage recovery." He picked up a stone and set it precisely on the corner of my easternmost chart, as if even his fidgeting had to be surveyed. "Roos has a deadline, Mara. That's why he came upriver himself instead of sending more hired muscle. That's why the security detail is so well-equipped and so impatient. Everything Kessler is doing right now — buying the river police, burning the station, chasing us through the sudd — it's all one operation with one objective."

"Destroy the evidence before the tribunal can see it."

"Before the tribunal can *subpoena* it. There's a difference. Evidence that exists is a problem. Evidence that's been formally entered into a proceeding is a catastrophe — you can't burn it without committing a second crime in front of witnesses. So they need it gone in the window. Before Port Moresby. After that, every day the ledgers survive makes them worth more and costs Kessler more to bury." He turned the stone under his thumb. "We're not running from Roos. We're racing his calendar."

I sat back and looked at the charts, at the neat contours of a buried thing, and felt the strange cold that comes in the tropics when understanding arrives faster than fear. "Then we stop thinking of this as an escape," I said slowly. "An escape just needs to survive. This needs to *arrive*. With provenance. With custody. With dates and signatures and a chain a lawyer can't snap."

Something changed in his face. Not much. With Casper it was never much — a fractional easing around the eyes, the way ice lets go of a bank. But I'd spent weeks learning to read him the way I read water, and I saw it.

"Yes," he said.

"Yes what?"

"Yes, that's the plan. Not survival. Arrival." He exhaled. "I've been planning escapes my whole adult life. Escapes are easy. You just make yourself small and keep moving. But you're right — small and moving gets the ledgers lost, or confiscated, or 'misplaced' by some well-paid customs officer, and then we did all this for nothing and Ferro's fever was for nothing and Tomas's grandfather stays a line item." He looked at me directly, and Casper Iversen looking at you directly was an event, rare enough that you marked the date. "So we do it your way. Openly. Together. No more of me deciding things alone at three in the morning and telling you afterward."

"That was one time."

"It was two times. I counted." The ghost of the wry mouth. "And both times you were right and I was wrong, and I'm told that pattern doesn't improve with repetition."

I laughed — actually laughed, there at the edge of a crocodile river with a fevered man behind me and mercenaries somewhere downstream — and the laugh surprised us both. Something loosened in my chest that I hadn't known was knotted.

"All right," I said. "Together. Then let's do the work. What do we actually have?"

He settled onto the groundsheet beside me, shoulder nearly touching mine, and pulled Ferro's notebook from the oilcloth. "Physical evidence: the ledgers from the chamber — eleven volumes, plus the execution photographs, plus the remains themselves, which we documented and left in place. Ferro's field notes with chainage references tying the adit to the surface survey. Your sediment cores and channel analysis proving the diversion. My closed-line maps showing the rail spurs built to service the mine and erased from record after the war. And—" he tapped his chest pocket "—this."

He drew out a folded sheaf of papers. Survey records, I saw. Originals, on paper that had yellowed along its folds. His name was on them. *C. Iversen, assistant engineer, certification of alignment and grade.* Dated twenty years ago. From another life, another country, another collapse.

"What are those?"

"The other half of the proof." His voice was very even, the way water is even just before it goes over the sill. "Kessler's predecessor didn't just erase the spurs from the maps. They re-surveyed the corridor afterward to launder the record — and the firm they hired subcontracted a junior engineer who didn't ask enough questions. The certification documents exist. They tie the company, by name and signature, to the exact corridor the ledgers describe. Without them, the ledgers are damning but deniable. With them, the deniability dies."

"Casper." I reached out and put my hand flat on the papers before he could fold them away. "Those are the records that ruined you. The ones you signed under pressure. The ones that ended your career."

"They're also the only independently authenticated engineering documents connecting Kessler's corporate ancestors to that tunnel," he said. "Which means the chain of custody starts with the man whose career they broke. Which means I can't stay invisible, Mara. Not this time. Invisible men can't sign affidavits."

The river ran past us, brown and patient, carrying the mountain down to the sea grain by grain, the way it had been doing since before anyone diverted it and would go on doing after everyone in this conversation was sediment. I thought about everything I knew about Casper Iversen — the borrowed names, the odd jobs, the talent for not being found that he wore like other men wear scars — and I understood suddenly that what he was proposing wasn't courage. Courage would have been easier. What he was proposing was the deliberate end of the only safety he had, and he was proposing it while looking at my charts instead of my face, because he still, after everything, expected to be alone with his decisions.

"We'll need copies," I said. "Certified. And originals travel separately — never in the same case, never on the same boat. If Roos takes one cache, the other survives."

He blinked. "That's exactly what I was going to—"

"I know. I've been watching you think for six weeks. I cheat." I pulled a fresh sheet toward me and uncapped my pen. "Split manifest. Three caches. One travels with the witnesses, one with us, one goes nowhere anyone expects — I'll decide where once I've seen the country. And the documentation of custody starts tonight: date, location, contents, signatures. Mine, yours, Hargreave's if he's lucid enough, Tomas's. Every hand that touches anything signs for it. If Kessler's lawyers want to attack the chain, let them find a chain welded shut."

For a moment he just looked at me. Then he laughed too — a real one, low and startled out of him, the sound of a man discovering a room in his own house he hadn't known was there.

"You've done this before," he said.

"I've argued with rivers my whole life. Rivers are just bureaucracies made of water. Same rules apply: know where everything is, trust nothing undocumented, and assume the map is lying until you've walked the ground yourself." I started writing. "Now. Route. Because knowing what we have is worthless if we can't get it to Port Moresby, and Roos owns the river."

"Roos owns the *legal* river," Casper said. "Checkpoints at the junctions, bought police, the works. Which means we don't take the river." He pulled his closed-line map from its waterproof sleeve and spread it beside my charts, and there it was again — that ghost network of spurs and tramways, drawn in his precise hand, the arteries of an industry that had officially never existed. His finger traced a line west from our position, up a tributary no one charted, into ridgeland where the ink switched from river-blue to the dashed brown of abandoned grade.

"Here. The old haul road follows the spur toward the high country. It's rough, it's slow, and it's completely off Roos's board, because as far as his intelligence goes these lines were torn up in 1946." His finger stopped at a mark near the head of the valley — a small square he'd annotated in pencil. *Airstrip (disused), est. 900m, condition unknown.*

"There's a strip up there?"

"There was. Mission supply, then plantation freight, then nothing. Condition unknown is honest — but unknown beats impossible, and impossible is what the river is right now." He glanced at me sideways. "It's a portage. Days of it, overland, with a sick man, with supplies we barely have, through country that eats equipment. I won't pretend it's good odds."

"Better than zero," I said. "Zero is what the river offers." I looked at the dashed brown line, at the little penciled square, and felt the plan assemble itself in my chest the way a channel assembles after floodwater finds its course — not chosen so much as *inevitable*, once you'd finally seen it. "We move at first light. Tonight we document, split the caches, and get Ferro stable enough to be carried. Can he travel?"

"With fever like that? No. Will he insist on it anyway?" Casper looked toward the tarp where the numbers were still being recited softly to the canvas. "Yes. He knows what's in his own notebook better than we do. Men like that don't stay behind."

* * *

We worked through the afternoon, and it was the strangest kind of happiness I have ever known — kneeling side by side over evidence bags and carbon paper while the basin steamed around us, dividing the bones of a massacre into triplicate so that no single act of violence could undo the truth of it. Casper handled the ledgers like they were live ordnance, which in every way that mattered they were. I drafted the custody log in my best hand, the hand I used for charts that had to survive being argued with. Tomas sat with Ferro and took dictation when the fever let him speak coherently, transcribing the oral chainage notes in a boy's careful script, and once, when Ferro drifted mid-sentence, Tomas kept writing from memory and got it right, and Casper watched him do it and said nothing, but I saw him file it away — the boy is becoming what we need him to become.

Hargreave sat apart under his own tarp, drinking water he didn't want and declining food he needed, and when I brought him the custody log to sign he held the pen for a long time.

"I knew," he said, not looking up. "About the cover-up. Not the details — I'm not going to insult you by claiming the details — but the shape of it. Heritage recovery, they told me. I signed the funding proposal, Mara. My name opened the door these men walked through."

"I know," I said.

"You should hate me. It would be efficient."

"Hargreave." I waited until he raised his eyes. "Sign the log. Hate yourself on your own time. Right now your signature is worth more than your remorse, and remorse doesn't testify."

Something moved through his face — pain, mostly, but underneath it, faintly, relief, the relief of a man who has been given permission to be useful instead of sorry. He signed. His hand shook, but the letters landed.

Dusk came down the way it does on the Sepik, fast and total, and with it Ferro's fever peaked. We sponged him and dosed him and Casper sat up with him through the worst hours while I slept in shifts I didn't believe in, and somewhere past midnight I woke to voices and crawled out of my net to find the two of them talking — really talking, Ferro gray-faced but present, propped against the gunwale with Casper's jacket over his shoulders.

"The buyer's name is Roos," Ferro was saying. "Anselm Roos. He came to the station three days before you arrived. I saw the floatplane. I hid in the culvert and watched them search, and I heard him give Hargreave's name to the security chief, and I understood that the dig was never ours. We were the digging implements. They intended to collect the ledgers quietly and burn the station quietly and write us down quietly." He coughed. "I ran upstream because a dead witness can't testify, and I am *tired*, Ms. Ellingsen, of being an implement."

"You're not an implement," I said. "You're a witness. There's a difference, and it's the entire point."

He looked at me then with the terrible clarity the fever sometimes grants, and said: "Then get me to Port Moresby alive, because I memorized the ledger numbers, and paper burns, but I don't. Not yet."

Casper caught my eye across the fire ring. Neither of us smiled. It wasn't that kind of moment. But something passed between us all the same — the recognition that the deadline Roos carried in his breast pocket had just acquired a face and a pulse and a voice that recited numbers, and that we were no longer escorting supplies, or rescuing survivors, or even escaping.

We were delivering testimony. All of us. Whether we lived or not.

At first light we broke camp. I stood at the bow of the loaded boat with the eastern sky going copper behind the ridge line and the dashed brown line of Casper's map waiting for us in the high country, and Casper came to stand beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and neither of us moved away.

"Portage at dawn tomorrow," he said. "Today we run the tributary as far as it goes and cache the third set where only a cartographer would think to look."

"Only a cartographer and the man who reads water like a local," I said. "Between us, Roos hasn't got a chance."

Downstream, faint on the morning air, came the sound we'd been listening for and dreading: an engine, running dark no longer, coming up the tributary fast — and Casper's hand closed on my arm as he said, quietly, "Change of plans. He found the river after all."
