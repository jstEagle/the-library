## 22. Roos Closes the River

The radio at the mission station crackled to life at dawn, and the voice on it belonged to a man I'd last seen offering money with the sincerity of a funeral director.

"This is a district advisory," the voice said, in the flat cadence of someone reading words he hadn't written and didn't believe. "All river traffic between Ambunti and the headwaters is suspended pending a security operation. Vessels will be subject to inspection at designated checkpoints. Compliance is mandatory."

I stood there with a mug of tea going cold in my hand and did the arithmetic that men like me are always doing. A security operation requires security forces. Security forces require paperwork, funding, authorization — weeks of it, in this country, if you follow the legal channels. Anselm Roos had been at the gauge station four days ago. The checkpoints were already staffed.

That's not bureaucracy. That's a phone call.

* * *

Father Ignatius — the missionary who'd taken us in after the takeoff through the squall line, a wiry Sepik-born priest who'd seen enough of the world to be unimpressed by all of it — listened to the broadcast twice, then switched off the set with the delicacy of a man closing a door on a sickroom.

"They came yesterday," he said. "Two boats. River police, but new uniforms. New outboards too, forty-horse Yamahas, still with the price stickers on the cowlings." He smiled without any humor in it. "In thirty years I have never seen the river police with new anything."

"Where are they checking?"

"Pagwi. Timbunke. The junction at the Korosameri." He counted them off on his fingers like sins. "And a boat anchored mid-channel below Ambunti that isn't police at all. Those men don't wear uniforms. They wear sunglasses and the particular expression of people being paid by the day to dislike everyone they meet."

Mara had come up behind me while he talked. She smelled of carbolic soap and Ferro's fever, because she'd been up half the night again with cool cloths and quinine, keeping our rescued hydrologist on the right side of the line between delirium and something worse.

"So he bought the river," she said.

"He rented it," I said. "Men like Roos never buy anything outright. Buying leaves a receipt."

Ferro, propped on a cot in the corner with a blanket around his shoulders like a man wearing his own ghost, laughed — a dry, cracked sound that turned into coughing. "He'll have paid the district commander directly," he managed when it passed. "Or the commander's cousin. Or the commander's cousin's wife's brother who holds the fuel contract. It only takes one signature and one envelope. I've watched it happen my whole career. Science in this country moves at the speed of paperwork; money moves at the speed of cash."

Hargreave sat apart from us, as he had since the airfield, since he'd walked toward Roos's floatplane with his hands visible and come back having learned exactly what his redemption was worth. He looked older than he had two weeks ago. Men do age quickly when they finally read their own invoices.

"The tribunal session is in eleven days," he said quietly. "Port Moresby. And every water route south now has a tollgate on it."

"Every *legal* route," Mara said.

I looked at her. She was looking at me, and there was something in her face that wasn't hope exactly — hope implies expectation, and Mara Ellingsen had spent long enough arguing with rivers to know better than to expect anything from them or from me. It was more like an audit. She was checking whether the idea forming behind my eyes was the same one forming behind hers.

It was.

* * *

We spread the map on Father Ignatius's dining table that afternoon, weighting the corners with mugs and a tin of biscuits nobody touched. The map was mine — the old one, the closed-line survey I'd carried for six years like a scar you keep showing people so they'll stop asking about it.

"There," I said, and put my finger on a thread-thin dashed line running southwest from a point above Ambunti, climbing away from the river into country marked with the cartographer's honest confession: *rel. ind.* Relief indicated. Which is map-speak for *we couldn't be bothered to measure it properly and neither should you.*

"What am I looking at?" Hargreave asked.

"A railway," I said. "Or the corpse of one. Built in '36 to haul copper ore down from the foothills to a barge landing that silted over before the first locomotive ever ran. The company went broke. The war ate what was left. Nobody ever pulled up the rails in the high sections because pulling up rails costs money and abandoning them costs nothing." I traced the line with my finger. "It runs to here — an airstrip. Mission-built, wartime, used for a decade and then forgotten. If the kunai hasn't eaten it entirely, and if the drainage culverts haven't gone, it's a corridor from the river to the highlands that no checkpoint covers, because no one remembers it exists except men who study maps of things other people paid to erase."

"And the distance?"

"Ninety kilometers as the crow lies. Call it a hundred and twenty on foot, with cargo, through ridge country in the build-up heat."

Silence around the table. Tomas, who'd been cleaning the rust off a machete with the patience of a boy who'd recently learned what machetes are actually for in this country, spoke first.

"I've walked worse," he said. "Everyone in my grandfather's village has walked worse. That's rather the point of us."

Nobody laughed, but something shifted in the room — the way weight shifts in a boat when everyone leans the same direction at once.

"It's not just the walking," I said, because I owed them honesty and honesty was the only currency I had left that hadn't been counterfeited. "It's the witnesses. We can't move the ledgers alone anymore. Roos knows we have them. The moment we surface anywhere — Port Moresby, a tribunal, a newspaper office — Kessler's lawyers will attack the chain of custody. Evidence without witnesses is a story. Evidence with forty descendants of the men in those photographs standing in the courtroom — that's a reckoning." I looked at Father Ignatius. "How many villages along this stretch trace back to the labor lines?"

He understood immediately. Of course he did; he'd buried half of them. "Ambunas. Yes. And three smaller settlements up the Yuat side. Families that were recruited in '37 and never came home, and families that came home without their fathers." He folded his hands. "You're asking whether they'll walk."

"I'm asking whether they'll walk *with us*. Ninety kilometers of ridge country carrying children and grandmothers, past checkpoints that have machine guns, to testify against a company whose lawyers will call them liars for six months before anyone says sorry."

The old priest was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the rain had started — early, even for the build-up, fat drops hitting the tin roof like slow applause.

"I won't answer for them," he said finally. "But I'll tell you what I know. For fifty years those villages have known what happened and said nothing, because saying nothing kept the peace and saying something got men disappeared. Silence was the tax they paid to exist. And every year the tax got heavier, and every year they paid it, and every year they hated themselves a little more for paying." He looked at the map, at my dead railway. "A man will carry almost anything if putting it down means becoming someone again. Ask them. Don't decide for them. They've had enough of that."

* * *

I asked them.

I went to Ambunas the next morning with Tomas — deliberately, because whatever else happened, this had to be asked by faces, not by a stranger with a map. The headman was a woman named Yawan, sixty-odd, with a voice like gravel in a tin and eyes that had audited better men than me and found them wanting.

Tomas told it. He told it in Tok Pisin and then in the village language, and I understood perhaps one word in five of the second telling, which was correct — it wasn't for me. He told them about the adit. About the ledgers with the columns of names and the column headed *expended*, which is what a company writes when it means *worked to death* and doesn't want the word to look like what it is. About the photographs. About his own grandfather's name in the third ledger, second page, written in a clerk's careful hand between a man who'd died of beriberi and a man listed only as *runaway, recovered, disposed*.

When he finished, the silence in that longhouse was the kind of silence that has furniture in it. You could live in it.

Yawan looked at me. "The white man with the map. You say there is a road the government forgot."

"A railway bed. Flat, graded, mostly walkable. It avoids every checkpoint on the river."

"You say the lawyers will call us liars."

"They will. For months. It's what they're paid for."

"And after?"

"After, if it works — the truth enters the record. Names get spoken in a courtroom instead of a grave. Compensation, maybe. Apology, maybe. Justice—" I shrugged, honestly. "Justice arrives imperfect or not at all. But the record survives either way, and records outlive companies. I've watched it happen once from the wrong side."

She studied me for a long time, the way you'd study a bridge before trusting it with your family.

"My grandmother was nine years old," she said, "when the recruiters came. She remembered the trucks. She remembered that her father waved from the back of one, and she remembered that he never waved from the front of one coming home." She turned to the room and said something in the village language, short and hard, and the room answered her — not all at once, but the way rain starts, scattered drops finding a rhythm until it's simply falling everywhere.

Then she looked back at me. "Forty-one adults will walk. Eleven elders insist on coming, and you will not win that argument, so do not waste your breath. The children go to the mission. We leave when the moon is dark." A pause, and then, with the ghost of something that might have been humor: "You will walk in the middle, map-man. Not the front. The front is where the shooting starts, and we have lost enough men at the front of things."

* * *

Roos closed the rest of the river that same week, and I'll give him this much: he did it beautifully.

By Thursday there were checkpoints at Pagwi and Timbunke flying the district colors, staffed by river police in uniforms so new the creases hadn't fallen out yet, waving down dugout canoes and inspecting bundles of sago with the gravity of customs officers at Heathrow. By Friday a notice had appeared — typed, letterheaded, laminated against the humidity, which must have cost extra — announcing the suspension of navigation permits pending review. By Saturday, the trading post at Angoram had received a shipment of outboard fuel that could only be purchased with permit documentation that no longer existed.

He hadn't seized the river. He'd *administrated* it. There's a difference, and the difference is that administration comes with forms, and forms come with queues, and queues come with the beautiful passive violence of making people wait until they give up. Somewhere in a Port Moresby office building a lawyer was drafting a press release about *temporary regulatory measures to ensure public safety during ongoing security operations*, and somewhere in Wewak a district officer was counting an envelope's contents and telling himself it was consulting fees, and somewhere Anselm Roos was sleeping eight hours a night on clean sheets, because cruelty administered through paperwork lets a man keep his conscience the way a corporation keeps its assets — laundered, distributed, and technically owned by no one.

Mara and I watched a checkpoint from the treeline above Pagwi on Friday night, lying in wet grass while mosquitoes wrote their memoirs on every inch of us.

"Look at them," she murmured. "Half those boys are from villages upriver. They grew up on this water. Now they're charging their own cousins a fee to cross it, and the fee goes into a bag, and the bag goes into a truck, and the truck goes to a man whose grandfather signed the ledger that lists their great-grandfathers as ballast." Her voice was level, but I've learned to hear the load-bearing walls under a level voice. "They don't even know what they're policing. That's the genius of it. You don't need to corrupt a whole system. You just need to make the corruption boring."

"That's the most frightening sentence anyone's said to me in years."

"I have others." She rolled onto her back, looking at the stars doing their indifferent rotation. "Casper. The window. The glacier pass. How long?"

"Nine days, maybe ten, if the calving opens it on schedule. After that it closes for the season and we're looking at spring."

"And the tribunal sits in eleven days."

"Yes."

"So we move forty-one witnesses, three crates of evidence, one feverish hydrologist, and one broken expedition lead over a hundred and twenty kilometers of forgotten railway, through ridge country, in the build-up heat, evading a mercenary detail with radios and helicopters, and arrive in time to testify." She said it without inflection, the way you'd read a shipping manifest. "I want you to appreciate that I've charted rivers that made more sense than this plan."

"Rivers always make sense. They're just honest about wanting to kill you."

That earned me half a smile in the dark. Then her hand found mine in the grass — not a declaration, just contact, the way you touch someone to confirm the world still contains them. We lay there a while longer, watching the checkpoint lanterns burn below us, small and orderly and bought.

* * *

Back at the mission, the news was waiting, and it was bad in the specific way bad news travels upriver — fast, downstream-biased, and slightly embellished.

Okuk had sent word.

It came via a fish trader, via a canoe, via a boy who handed Father Ignatius a cigarette tin with a scrap of paper inside, because Okuk communicated the way viruses do, through many small carriers none of whom knew what they carried. The note was in his handwriting, which I'd know anywhere — the letters of a man who learned English from shipping manifests and never saw a reason to beautify them.

*I HEAR YOU ARE ALIVE. GOOD. ALSO HEAR YOU HAVE PAPERS EVERYONE WANTS. BAD. RIVER IS CLOSED BUT NOT FOR ME. I CAN MOVE YOUR PEOPLE TO THE AIRFIELD. TRUCKS, DRIVERS, THE RIGHT FRIENDS AT THE CHECKPOINTS. MY PRICE IS NOT MONEY THIS TIME. COME TO THE POST AND WE WILL TALK LIKE OLD FRIENDS. — O*

I read it three times. Mara read it once and said, "No."

"You don't know what the price is yet."

"I know Okuk. Well — I know *of* Okuk, which in your case amounts to the same thing. You've told me enough. He's your oldest ally in this country, which means he's also the person best positioned to sell you, because betrayal requires access and strangers rarely have good access." She tapped the paper. "*My price is not money this time.* What else does Roos want? What's the one thing we have that can't be replaced?"

I looked at the note and felt the trap in it the way you feel a soft patch in a jungle trail — not visible, but registered in the soles of your feet, in some older part of the brain that predates maps.

"The ledgers," I said.

"The ledgers."

"Or the witnesses. Or both. Or—" I stopped, because the thought arrived fully formed and ugly. "Or me. Roos offered me reinstatement once already, through intermediaries, before the airfield. A clean name. My record expunged. Maybe Okuk's price is Casper Iversen, delivered breathing, and maybe Okuk thinks he's doing me a favor. That's the worst possibility, and therefore the most likely one. Okuk has never once betrayed me for profit. He'd only betray me for what he believes is my own good."

Mara stared at me. "That's somehow worse."

"Welcome to my entire adult life."

Hargreave, listening from the doorway with the hollowed-out look of a man auditing his own ruin, said quietly: "Then don't go."

"I have to go. If I don't, he sells to whoever asks next, and the next buyer won't offer trucks."

"Send someone else."

"Who? Who else does he trust?" I folded the note into my pocket. "Besides — a man who owns no address learns to walk into rooms full of people who want things from him. It's the one skill my disgrace left me. Everything else it took."

Outside, the rain kept falling on the tin roof, steady and patient, the monsoon rehearsing its opening act. Nine days to the glacier window. Eleven days to the tribunal. Forty-one witnesses waiting on a dark moon. And a hundred and twenty kilometers of dead railway between us and everything.

I checked my watch, though there was nothing to check it against, and began packing for a meeting with an old friend.
