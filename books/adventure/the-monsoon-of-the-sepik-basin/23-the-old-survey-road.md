## 23. The Old Survey Road

I spread the map on the gauge station's table at dawn, weighting the corners with a shell casing, a coffee tin, a stone, and Ferro's feverish hand, and I watched four people look at me like I'd suggested we walk to the moon.

"The old survey road," I said, and tapped the line. "Rail spur. Closed since before the war. It runs from here—" my finger moved northeast, through the hatched green that cartographers use for country they'd rather not think about, "—to here. Kandrian Depot. There's an airfield."

Mara leaned over it first, because Mara always leaned over maps the way other people leaned over railings above dangerous water. Her finger traced the line I'd drawn, then kept going past it, hunting for the thing that wasn't there.

"There's nothing," she said. "No road. No track. No contour data worth trusting. Casper, this is a colonial survey from 1938. Half these elevations were taken from a boat."

"Which is why nobody's watching it."

"Which is why nobody has walked it in eighty years."

"That too."

Hargreave stood at the edge of the table with his arms folded, and I could see the arithmetic running behind his eyes, the same sums he'd been doing since the radio died. Boats versus men. Distance versus fuel. The probability that a man who had already sold his conscience once could sell it a second time cheaply enough to sleep.

"It's forty kilometers of closed rail bed through primary jungle," he said. "With eleven people, two of them injured, one of them dying, carrying evidence that a billion-dollar development group wants burned. And at the end of it, a disused airfield you know about how?"

"I certified ferry flights out of it in another life. It's real. The strip's still there. Grass will be over the wheels, but the gravel base doesn't rot."

"And the plane?"

I turned the page of my map book to the pencil note I'd made two nights ago, when I'd first allowed myself to think about this. "There's a derelict. An old Islander, or what's left of one. I'll worry about that when we get there."

"You'll worry about it when we get there," Hargreave repeated. "That's your plan. That's the whole plan. Walk forty kilometers, and then *worry*."

"Every plan I've ever survived has looked exactly like that from the front end," I said. "The ones that looked good from the front end are the ones that killed people."

He didn't have an answer for that, which was itself an answer. He turned away and looked out at the river, at the brown water running full and indifferent past the station, and I watched him do the arithmetic again, and this time I watched the numbers come out wrong for him. Because the river was closed. Roos had bought the police, and the checkpoints were blooming at every junction like mushrooms after rain, and every hour we sat here the net drew tighter, and the only man at this table who had ever walked a closed line was the man nobody trusted.

Mara was still studying the spur. Not the line — the country around it.

"The gradient," she said slowly. "Rail gradients are what, one in fifty, one in eighty? If they built a line through here, it's the gentlest route through the ridges. It's not a road, it's a *corridor*. Someone already found the easy way."

"Someone found the easy way and then paid to have it forgotten," I said. "Which means it's the easy way twice over."

She looked up at me, and there was the thing I'd been hoping for and afraid of in equal measure: she believed me. Not because I was trustworthy — I'd given her no great reason — but because the map was true, and she was a woman who had spent her life learning that rivers lie and contours don't.

"The laborers built it," she said quietly. "The men in the ledgers. They cut this corridor."

"Yes."

"Then we're not escaping along it. We're walking home along it."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Tomas, who had been sitting on the floor with his bandaged arm cradled like a sleeping animal, looked up at that, and something moved in his face that I recognized and did not want to name in front of him.

"It's still forty kilometers," Hargreave said, but his voice had gone quieter, the objection becoming ritual rather than argument. "With what food? What medicine for Ferro?"

"Whatever we can carry. Which is why we leave today, not tomorrow. Every hour we spend debating is an hour the patrol boats spend narrowing."

Ferro stirred on the cot and said something in Italian that I chose not to translate, even for myself.

* * *

We spent the day becoming smaller.

That's the part nobody writes about, in the adventure novels — not the walking, but the arithmetic of leaving. Eleven people, and a station full of gear, and a boat that could carry a fraction of it. Everything had to be weighed against a shoulder and a knee and forty kilometers of jungle that had spent eight decades growing through the bones of a railway.

I made the lists, because lists were the one honest thing left in my skill set. Water in the jerrycans we could carry. Rice and tinned meat, counted by the meal. Ferro's antibiotics, counted by the dose, which was the number that frightened me most. The ledgers wrapped in oilcloth and split across four packs, so that no one man carried the whole truth — a lesson I'd learned from watching what happened to men who did. The photographs, Mara insisted on carrying herself, in a waterproof case against her chest, and I didn't argue, because I had seen her check that case the way other people check a child.

The boat we sank.

That was Tomas's idea, delivered with the flat pragmatism of a young man who had nearly drowned in crocodile water and come out of it with opinions. We ran her into the deep channel off the station pilings, opened the seacocks, and let her go down with the supplies we couldn't carry lashed in the cabin — a cache, if we ever came back, and a lie for anyone who came looking, because a sunken boat says *disaster* and a missing boat says *flight*.

Hargreave watched her go under with the expression of a man watching a coffin.

"You understand," he said, when the bubbles stopped, "that if the airfield doesn't work, there is no second option. The river is gone. The road is a guess. We are betting eleven lives on a pencil line drawn by a man who was fired for lying on surveys."

The others had gone inside. It was just the two of us and the brown water.

"I was fired for signing a report I didn't believe," I said. "The lying came later, from men with better tailors. But you're right. It's a pencil line, and I'm asking you to bet everything on it."

"Then why should I?"

I thought about giving him the honest answer, which was *because you have nothing else*, and I thought about giving him the kind answer, which was *because I have walked worse lines than this one*, and what came out instead was the true answer, which I hadn't planned.

"Because you knew," I said. "About the massacre. You told me on the portage — no. You haven't told me yet. But you will. I can see it sitting in you like a stone in a boot. You've known since before we left Wewak, and you took the money anyway, and every hour we sit at this station is an hour you get to pretend the choice hasn't arrived. Walking that spur is the first thing you can do that isn't for sale. I'm offering you the exit, Hargreave. Not from the jungle. From yourself."

He stared at me for a long time. The river ran. Somewhere up the bank a hornbill laughed its idiot laugh, the jungle's idea of a joke.

"You're a cruel man, Iversen."

"I'm an accurate one. It comes to the same thing, most days."

He turned and walked back toward the station, and his shoulders were different — not straighter, exactly, but carrying their weight in a new place. I had seen that change before, on men coming off a mountain, and I knew it for what it was. Not courage. Just the arithmetic finally failing, the numbers coming out in a direction he couldn't buy his way around.

I gave him ten minutes and went in to finish the packing.

* * *

Mara found me at dusk, checking the last of the fuel for the station generator — we'd run it one final hour to charge the torches and Ferro's thermometer, and now I was about to kill it for good.

"Walk with me," she said.

We walked the old platform, which the jungle had narrowed to a ribbon of concrete with ferns growing through the expansion joints. The river muttered below. Somewhere across the water, too far to matter yet but not too far to think about, a boat engine ran at the idle speed of a man pretending to fish.

"They're watching the station," she said.

"They're watching the river. There's a difference. By tomorrow they'll be bored, and bored men make mistakes."

"And if they don't get bored?"

"Then I'll make them bored."

She stopped at the end of the platform, where the old rail had rusted to a brown lacework in the ties, and put her hand on it, and I watched her do the thing she did with rivers — read it, argue with it, lose, come back tomorrow.

"Casper. If this goes wrong. If we're on that spur and Ferro's fever spikes and there's no helicopter coming because there's no clearing to land one — what's the plan then?"

"There isn't one."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one. I've spent fifteen years making plans, Mara. Contingency trees, risk matrices, the whole liturgy of men who get paid to predict disasters. And I'll tell you what I learned, the thing they don't put in the reports: every plan is a story you tell yourself so you can sleep before the bad day. On the bad day there's just the country, and the people next to you, and what you're willing to do. Everything else is decoration." I looked down the rusted line, into the dark coming up out of the jungle. "I'm not asking you to trust the plan. I'm asking you to trust that I'd rather die out there than hand Roos a clean river."

"That's a terrible thing to build a relationship on."

"It's the only thing I've got that isn't counterfeit."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly gone — and then she did something that undid me more thoroughly than any argument had. She reached over and straightened my collar, an absurd domestic gesture, there at the end of the world, and left her hand flat on my chest for a moment, over the oilcloth case of photographs.

"Then we walk," she said. "But Casper — the ledgers go to the tribunal. Whatever happens. Whatever it costs. You agreed to that in the adit and I'm holding you to it in the jungle. Swear it."

I looked at her hand on my chest, at the woman who had argued with a river until the river confessed, and I swore it, and for the first time in fifteen years the oath didn't taste like a signature on someone else's paper.

* * *

We burned the station at 0400, and it was not my idea, and that is the part I want on the record, wherever records survive.

We were loading the last packs in the grey half-light when the first flare went up from the far bank — a red star, hanging, the kind of thing that means *we see you* — and then the shooting started. Not at us, not at first. Into the station. Deliberate, methodical, the rounds walking through the generator shed and the fuel store, and then the fuel store caught, and the night turned orange.

"Down! Off the platform!" I got Ferro over my shoulder — the man weighed nothing, fever had hollowed him — and we went into the ditch behind the old coal bay while the station burned above us. I got a look, one look, between the flames: two boats at the pilings, men in the black kit of people who are paid rather than convinced, and on the near boat, a figure with a torch in a launcher, lighting the barracks roof with the unhurried air of a man closing out a work order.

Roos wasn't taking chances anymore. Roos was erasing.

"The evidence—" Hargreave shouted, and I grabbed his collar and put him in the mud.

"The evidence is on our backs. The station is just timber. Stay down."

We stayed down. The fire ate the barracks, the office, the gauge house with its hundred years of river measurements, the paper record of a watershed that had been moved by men who counted bodies as ballast. Heat came off it in waves you could feel through the ditch bank. Tomas was crying, quietly, the way you cry when you're too tired for the loud kind, and I put my hand on the back of his neck and left it there, because that was the whole of what I had to give him just then.

By the time the roof beams started going in, the boats had gone. No need to stay. Fire does the follow-up work for free.

We came up out of the ditch into the smell of wet ash and looked at what was left: the concrete platform, the rusted rails, and a black skeleton of a building with the fire still working through its ribs.

Hargreave stood in front of it a long time. When he turned around, his face was lit orange on one side and grey on the other, and he looked, for the first time since Wewak, like a man who had run out of arithmetic.

"They'd have burned it with us in it," he said. "If we'd stayed another day. If I'd argued another day."

"Yes."

"You knew they'd come."

"I knew men like Roos don't leave witnesses to a fire any more than to a ledger. It was a coin toss whether they'd come tonight or tomorrow night. I chose to stop flipping."

He looked at the burning station, and then at the eleven of us standing in the ash with our packs, and something in him settled — not bravely, not gracefully, but the way a man settles a debt he can no longer refinance.

"The spur," he said. "Lead out."

* * *

We walked into the jungle at first light, up the old survey road, along a corridor cut by men whose names appeared in ledgers under the column marked *expended*, following rails that rusted from lace to nothing and back to lace again as the ground rose and fell. The gradient held true — one in sixty, gentle as a promise, the easiest walking any of us had ever done in country this cruel, and Mara said once, not looking at me, "They were good engineers, the men who surveyed this," and I said, "The men who surveyed it, yes," and we let the difference lie there between us, unburied, and walked on.

Behind us, the smoke from the station stood up straight in the still morning air, a black column visible, I estimated, for thirty kilometers in every direction. A signal fire we hadn't chosen, telling Roos exactly where we'd been and roughly where we'd gone.

There was no help for it. But as I looked back at it one last time before the ridge took it, I found I wasn't afraid of the column.

I was reading it. And the wind, which had been still for three days, was beginning, very gently, to lean it toward the northeast.

Toward the spur. Toward us.

Toward the airfield, where a dead Islander sat in the kunai grass, waiting for a man who had once certified aircraft ferries under a different name to make it lie about being alive.

Three days' walk. Three days' work. And a hunter who could now read our heading as easily as we could.

I tightened Ferro's weight on my shoulders and lengthened my stride, and did not say any of this out loud, because there was no percentage in it, and because the woman walking beside me was already reading my face the way she read water, and had gone quiet in the way that meant she'd gotten the same answer.
