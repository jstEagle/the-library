## 24. Portage

The old survey road began where the jungle decided to forgive it.

That's the thing about a rail spur nobody maintained for sixty years — the forest doesn't destroy it so much as digest it slowly, keeping the bones. We walked on a raised bed of crushed basalt gone soft with moss, and every few hundred meters a culvert still ran true under our feet, carrying water the way some dead engineer had intended it to before anyone thought to ask him whether he wanted to be remembered this way. Cuttings rose on either side, ten meters of red clay furred with ferns, and in the cuttings you could still see the blast scars from the drilling charges, neat rows of them, like a signature.

I'd walked this line once before, on paper. Now I walked it with forty kilos of Ferro on my back and a feverish man muttering coordinates into my collar.

"Left bank," he said. "The intake. They moved it at night."

"I know," I told him.

"You don't know. Nobody knows. That's the whole point of knowing things — is that somebody has to carry them." He laughed, a dry papery sound against my neck. "You're carrying me. I'm carrying them. It's all very efficient."

Ferro had been hiding upstream of the gauge station for eleven days when we found him, living on sago and river water and whatever his mind was cooking instead of food. The fever came in waves. In the troughs he was lucid enough to walk twenty minutes at a time; in the peaks he was back in the tunnel, reading ledgers by headlamp, and he narrated it to whoever was nearest. Today that was me. I'd volunteered for Ferro duty partly because I was the biggest, and partly because Mara needed her hands free for the chart she was making as we walked — the portage route, drawn live, because no map of this country could be trusted that hadn't been made by someone currently standing on it.

Behind us came the rest: Tomas with his arm strapped to his chest and his grandfather's name folded into his breast pocket like a letter he couldn't decide whether to send; two of Hargreave's field techs, Nalo and Wari, who had signed on to measure rivers and were now hauling crates of evidence through the spine of the Sepik Basin; and Dr. Edmund Hargreave himself, fifty-eight years old, expedition lead, walking at the rear with the particular gait of a man who has stopped believing the ground will hold him.

We made four kilometers the first morning. On flat ground with a road under you, four kilometers is nothing. On a decommissioned rail bed climbing through jungle ridges with a stretcher case rotating between shoulders, it is a day's honest work and then some.

At noon we stopped in the shadow of a bridge that wasn't there anymore.

* * *

The piers stood. That was the eerie part. Two concrete abutments and a central pier, square-shouldered, unbothered by six decades of rain, and between them nothing but air and a stream running green over stones thirty feet down. The span itself had been dropped — not collapsed, *dropped* — and lay in the ravine below with its trusses bent like something kneeling. You don't get that from rot. Rot takes years and leaves the steel looking tired. This had been cut loose in an afternoon, probably with thermite or a dozer chain, by people who wanted the line unusable without wanting it to look demolished. Demolition attracts questions. A fallen bridge in the jungle is just weather.

Mara stood at the edge of the abutment and looked down at the fallen span for a long time.

"They brought the ore out over this," she said. "And they brought the men in."

"And then they took away the way back," I said. "Same bridge. Both directions."

She didn't answer right away. She was sketching — fast, sure lines, the pier, the ravine, the crossing point where the stream narrowed enough to ford. When she finished she tore the page free and handed it to Nalo without being asked, which was how she'd been running the portage since dawn: quietly, precisely, one instruction at a time, until I realized somewhere around midday that the column was moving to her rhythm and not mine, and that I didn't mind.

"We can't cross here with Ferro," she said, low, just for me. "The rocks are slick and the current's faster than it looks. There's a gravel bar two hundred meters downstream where the channel braids. We go there."

"You've already looked."

"I always look." She capped her pen. "You navigate, I chart. That was the deal."

It hadn't been a deal, exactly. It had been three in the morning outside the burning station, smoke rolling down the valley, and me saying *the survey road* and her saying *show me* and neither of us sleeping after that. But I let her have the word *deal*. A man adrift as long as I'd been learns to take structure wherever it's offered.

We crossed at the bar, boots off, trousers rolled, the water cold enough to make Ferro yelp — which I counted as a good sign, since pain meant the fever hadn't eaten everything yet. Tomas went last, favoring his strapped arm, and Wari stayed beside him the whole way without being told to. Good kid, Wari. Twenty-three, maybe, and already understood that you watch the weakest man's face, not the water.

The leeches found us on the far side.

They come up out of the leaf litter like small brown opinions, and by the time we halted for the afternoon they'd gotten into everyone. Nalo sat on a log swearing in three languages while Mara worked a bootlace knot around his ankle. I pulled nine off my own shins and showed Tomas the trick of it — fingernail edge, quick scrape, don't yank, yanking leaves the jaws in and the jaw in means infection, and infection out here means amputation or worse. He copied me carefully with his good hand, tongue between his teeth, and for a moment he looked about twelve years old, and then he looked at the strap on his arm and remembered what else he was carrying, and he was twenty-four again and heavy with it.

"Mr. Iversen." Hargreave's voice, from behind me. "A word."

* * *

He'd walked ahead alone to a cutting where the clay wall had slumped and exposed, of all things, a section of rail — one length of steel, half-buried, rusted to the color of dried blood. He was standing over it the way a man stands over a grave he isn't sure is the right one.

"A word," he repeated.

"It's been a lot of words today, Doctor."

"Yes. Well." He took off his glasses and cleaned them on his shirttail, a gesture I'd seen him perform a hundred times at the staging camp, usually before disagreeing with Mara about safety margins. It was his tell. It meant the disagreement was with himself this time. "I owe you an accounting, I think. Before we're any further into country where I might die and take it with me."

"You don't owe me anything."

"I owe *everyone* everything, Mr. Iversen. That's rather the problem." He put the glasses back on and looked at the buried rail instead of at me. "I knew."

The word sat there between us. I let it.

"Not the details. Not the ledgers, not the photographs, not the names. But I knew there was something. When Kessler Heritage approached me — that's what they called it, *heritage recovery*, a lovely phrase, it makes destruction sound like curating — they gave me the hydrological brief and a budget that made no sense. Four times what a sediment survey costs. And a clause. A confidentiality clause covering 'pre-contact cultural materials,' which is a legal fiction, because there are no pre-contact cultural materials in a decommissioned colonial mine, and everyone in that room knew it." His voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. Steady voices are how educated men confess. "I signed it anyway. Because the money funded three years of real science. Because I told myself I'd publish around it. Because I was sixty-one years old and tired and flattered, and flattery is the most dangerous thing they ever invented, more dangerous than guns, because a man will walk into gunfire for a principle but he'll walk into anything for the feeling of being taken seriously."

"Ferro figured it out."

"Ferro figured it out in nine days because Ferro is a better scientist than I ever was, and because Ferro doesn't want to be taken seriously, he wants to be *right*, and those are different hungers." Hargreave finally turned to look at me. His eyes were wet, but his voice held. "When the radio went silent I told you it was equipment. I knew it wasn't equipment. I knew someone had cut that antenna, and I knew who would want it cut, and I said *equipment* because saying the other thing meant admitting what my signature had bought. Every decision since — the slow pace, the caution, arguing against going into the adit, negotiating with Roos — you thought it was timidity. Some of it was. But most of it was a man trying to delay the moment he'd have to say out loud what he'd known from the start."

The column had stopped behind us. I could hear Mara talking softly to Ferro, and the stream, and the enormous indifferent noise of the jungle. I thought about a trestle outside Lae, nineteen years ago, and a report I signed under pressure, and how the company survived and the railway inspectorate lost a man named Casper Iversen and gained a ghost who did survey work under borrowed names. I thought: *there but for a different kind of cowardice goes.*

"Why tell me now?" I asked.

"Because you're leading this now, and you should know what your leader was." He straightened, and something in his spine seemed to physically transfer, some load shifting from one set of shoulders to another. "I'm done making decisions, Iversen. I'll carry, I'll walk, I'll testify until they put me in the ground — God willing they let me testify first — but I'm done deciding. Give me orders like the others. It's a relief. It's the first honest position I've held in years."

I looked at him for a long moment. Then I pointed at the buried rail.

"Then here's your first order. Look at that steel. Memorize the profile — the foot, the web, the head. Forty-pound rail, colonial standard, same as the spurs on my closed-line map. When we testify, and we will testify, you're going to say out loud that you walked this road and saw their rails with your own eyes. That's your penance. Not feeling bad. Saying it. Feeling bad is cheap and private. Saying it costs."

His mouth twitched — almost a smile, the first one in days. "You're crueler than Roos."

"Roos is crueler than me. That's why we're walking and he's flying." I shouldered past him toward the column. "Fall in, Doctor. Rear guard. Watch Tomas's feet — he won't complain, so you complain for him."

"Yes, sir," said Dr. Edmund Hargreave, and for the first time since I'd met him, he sounded like a man who meant what he said.

* * *

The second night we camped in a cutting with high clay walls on both sides, which was tactically stupid and comfortingly enclosed, and I compromised by posting Wari at the north end with the one working torch and myself at the south end with nothing but the dark and my own nerves.

Mara found me there around midnight. She didn't ask if she could sit; she just sat, close enough that our shoulders touched, which in the arithmetic of the last three weeks constituted a speech.

"He told you," she said.

"How do you know?"

"Because he's walking differently. He's been apologizing with his whole body since dawn." She pulled her knees up. Above the cutting rim, the stars were thick and indifferent. "Did you believe him?"

"The confession? Yes. Confessions are easy to fake and his wasn't performed. He wasn't asking forgiveness. He was handing over weight." I turned a stick over in my fingers. "What I don't know yet is whether a man like that is worth what he costs. He knew, Mara. From the start. Ferro nearly died for the truth while the man funding the dig was protecting his budget line."

"People are both things at once. That's the finding I keep failing to publish." She leaned her head against my shoulder, briefly, then not briefly. "You knew too. About what companies do. You've known since your trestle."

"That's different."

"It's exactly the same, and the fact that you can't see it is the only difference." She said it without heat, the way she corrected a chart. "You signed a report you didn't believe. He signed a contract he didn't believe. You both spent years paying for it. The only question is what you do next, and look at us — we're doing it. Walking his rails, carrying his ledgers, dragging his witnesses over his mountains. Maybe penance isn't a feeling. Maybe it's a portage."

I laughed, quietly, so as not to wake Ferro. "You've been saving that one."

"Since the gravel bar." Her hand found mine in the dark, and neither of us made anything complicated of it. There wasn't time, and there didn't need to be. "Sleep, Casper. Tomorrow's the ridge."

Tomorrow was the ridge.

* * *

The ridge took everything.

The survey road climbed out of the cutting onto a saddle between two jungle peaks, and for six hours we gained altitude over ground that had never heard of a switchback. The rail bed became a staircase of exposed ties, rotten and black, each one a separate negotiation. Ferro walked the flats and rode the climbs — we'd lashed two pack frames into a litter, and Nalo and I traded it front-to-back while our quads filed formal complaints. Tomas's arm swelled under the strap; Mara re-set the binding at the halfway cairn with a splint split from bamboo and a calm that I suspect was ninety percent performance. Leeches got inside everyone's boots. Wari developed blisters that opened and kept walking anyway, because he was twenty-three and proud, and I ordered him to sit at the top of the ridge for ten minutes and eat, and he obeyed with the wounded dignity of a soldier being protected from his own heroism.

At the crest, the jungle opened.

Below us, to the west, the country fell away in folds of green to a river valley we couldn't see but could hear — a low continuous hush, the Sepik's tributaries working their way down toward the main channel. To the east, kunai grassland rolled in gold waves to the horizon, and somewhere in that gold, twelve kilometers out, sat a runway and a dead airplane and the next act of our lives.

Hargreave stood at the top, breathing hard, and looked back the way we'd come. The whole route lay visible below: the cutting, the fallen bridge, the dark stitch of the road through the trees.

"My father surveyed this line," he said suddenly, to no one, to all of us. "Not the massacre part. The engineering part. Nineteen-fifties. He had the original alignment drawings in his study, and I used to lie on the floor and trace them. I never asked what the line was *for*." He wiped his face with the back of his wrist. "Children never ask what the money is for. That's how it keeps working. Generation after generation, nobody asks."

Tomas came and stood beside him. Said nothing. Put his good hand briefly on the old man's shoulder, and left it there, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

We descended into the kunai as the light went long and amber, grass whispering against our hips, the litter bumping along, Ferro murmuring intake coordinates to the sky. My feet had stopped reporting in, which is either a blessing or a warning. Behind us, far back along the ridgeline, I checked twice for dust or smoke or the glint of glass, and found nothing — which meant nothing, with Roos, and I knew it, and I walked faster anyway.

It was full dark when we smelled woodsmoke.

Not ours. Ahead, down in a fold of the grassland, a smudge of orange light and the shape of tin roofs, and strung between two posts, catching the fire glow, a string of drying fish and a hand-painted board that read, in English and Tok Pisin both:

**OKUK & SONS — TRADE STORE — COLD DRINK — GUIDES — ALL BUSINESS CONSIDERED**

*All business considered.* I stood in the dark looking at that sign and felt the temperature of the evening change. Okuk. My fixer, my recruiter, the man whose contract had started all of this, sitting squarely on the only route between us and the airfield — and Roos knew Okuk existed, because Roos's employers had hired through him once already, and a man like Okuk did not stay neutral when both sides were bidding.

Mara stopped beside me and read the sign, and then read my face, and went quiet in the way that meant she'd gotten the same answer.

"Well," she said. "How much do you trust him?"

"That," I said, "is the wrong question. The right one is what he trusts *us* for — and I'm about to find out."
