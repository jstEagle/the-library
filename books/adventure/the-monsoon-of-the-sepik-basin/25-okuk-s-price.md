## 25. Okuk's Price

The trading post announced itself the way all good lies do — with something familiar. A tin roof gone orange with rust, a veranda sagging at exactly the angle of every veranda I'd ever trusted, and a hand-painted sign that read OKUK & SONS, GENERAL MERCHANDISE, though everyone on the river knew Okuk had no sons and sold very little that was general.

"We can't stop here," Mara said from the stern, low, the way you talk in a house where the walls have ears and the ears have employers.

"We're stopping here," I said. "Ferro's fever needs quinine, Tomas needs his shoulder looked at properly, and Hargreave hasn't eaten since the station burned. Whatever else Okuk is, he keeps medicine behind the counter and grudges in a drawer where they belong."

"And which one are we to him?"

"That," I said, cutting the engine as the current took us sideways toward the landing, "is the question I've been avoiding since Angoram."

* * *

Okuk was waiting on the veranda, which told me he'd already been told we were coming. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges the way a rope goes soft — still strong underneath, but no longer shaped for any single purpose. He wore a shirt with a fishing company's logo on it from a company that had never fished, and he smiled at me with the particular warmth of a man calculating what my trouble would fetch by weight.

"Casper Iversen," he said. "Or is it Larsen this month? I lose track."

"Thornbury today."

"A fine name. Sounds like it owns land." He embraced me, patted my back twice — once for friendship, once for inventory — and stepped back to look at the boat. His eyes did what eyes do when they've been paid to see: counted heads, catalogued postures, lingered half a second too long on the oilskin-wrapped case lashed under the thwart. "You bring me customers and corpses both. The river has been generous to you."

"The river's been generous to nobody. It just moves things around."

Inside, the post smelled of kerosene, dried fish, and the sweet rot of sugar stored past its arguments. Okuk's wife — or a woman who performed the role convincingly enough that it didn't matter — brought quinine without being asked, which meant Ferro's condition had been discussed before we arrived, which meant someone downstream had radioed ahead, which meant the net was already drawn and we were swimming inside it. I filed that away and drank the tea she gave me anyway, because paranoia is a luxury and thirst is not.

Tomas lay on the back bench while the woman cleaned the gash in his shoulder. He'd gone quiet since the trestle — not broken quiet, but the quiet of a man who has found something heavy inside himself and is learning its weight. He watched Okuk the way the rest of us pretended not to.

Hargreave sat hunched over a bowl of rice like a man eating under protest. Since the fire, since his confession on the portage trail, something had been draining out of him daily, some essential pressure, and what remained was smaller and more honest and far less useful. He looked up at Okuk with the expression of a man meeting his own reflection in a dirtier mirror.

"You know him?" I asked Hargreave quietly.

"Everyone in the basin knows Okuk." He set down his spoon. "That's rather the point of him."

* * *

Okuk drew me out to the veranda at dusk, alone, with two bottles of green ginger wine and the studied casualness of a man about to ruin something. Below us the river ran brown and patient. Somewhere across it, in the dark, Roos's checkpoints were multiplying like mold.

"I can move you," Okuk said. "All of you. Boat, crew, papers, the sick one carried if he must be carried. To the old airfield in the grass, two days' walk from the spur, three days' run from anything Kessler owns. By the time Roos learns which direction you went, you'll be a rumor."

I turned the bottle in my hands. Green glass, warm from sitting in the sun, the label printed in a language nobody on this river spoke. "And the price?"

"Ah." He smiled again, and this time there was nothing underneath it, which was almost a relief after years of reading his layered faces. "The ledgers."

The word sat between us like a third man on the veranda.

"Not copies," Okuk went on, reasonably, the way a crocodile is reasonable. "Not photographs. The originals. The whole case. You give them to me here, tonight, and tomorrow morning my boys load their own supplies aboard your boat so the weight looks right, and you go north light and clean and alive."

"To sell them back to Kessler."

"To sell them *forward* to Kessler. There's a difference. Backward implies you took something from them first." He shrugged. "Roos pays for silence. I provide silence. It is the oldest trade on this river, older than the mission, older than the mine. Your tribunal, your Port Moresby, your justice — these are new things. New things drown. Silence floats."

"Silence floats because it's dead," I said. "Everything that floats is dead. Fish, logs, men who kept quiet. The living things swim against it."

Okuk laughed — genuinely, warmly, the laugh I'd drunk with in Wewak a dozen times, the laugh that had bought me survey jobs under borrowed names when no one else would hire a disgraced engineer. That was the cruelty of it. He wasn't a stranger betraying me. He was the only man in Papua New Guinea who had ever vouched for me without asking what I'd done, and now he was pricing my friends' graves by the kilo.

"You think I don't understand what's in that case," he said. "Casper. I grew up forty kilometers from that gauge station. My grandfather carried rail ties for the consortium until his lungs filled with the dust they didn't put in any ledger. I know exactly what's in the case. That's why it's worth so much."

"Then how—"

"How can I sell it back to the men who did it?" He looked out at the water, and for a moment the fixer fell away and something older and wearier stood in his place. "Because Kessler will pay more for it than a tribunal will. Because if I sell it to the tribunal, Roos burns this post down with my family in it, and the ledgers end up in evidence storage in Moresby for nine years while lawyers argue about chain of custody, and then a minister whose campaign Kessler funded loses the file. I have seen how your justice works, Casper. It works slowly, and it works for people who can wait. My family cannot wait. But Kessler pays *now*, and Kessler pays in things that keep my family fed whether the file survives or not."

"And the dead men in the tunnel? Do they get fed?"

"The dead men," Okuk said gently, "have been dead for eighty years. They will be dead when the tribunal rules and dead when the appeal fails and dead when the development project opens with a plaque. I am sorry for them. I am sorrier for the living."

He let that sit. Downriver a night bird started up, the one that sounds like a hinge that will never be oiled.

"So that's the offer," I said. "Ledgers for passage."

"Ledgers for passage, and my word that none of my buyers learn your faces or your route. Which, I remind you, is worth more than the money. Roos's security chief is a man named Vassek who once worked for me and learned everything I know about moving quietly through this basin. If you go north without my help, Vassek finds you. Not because he's clever. Because there is only one north."

I looked at the bottle. I thought about Tomas on the bench inside, holding his grandfather's name like a coal he couldn't put down. About Mara, who had photographed everything twice and slept with the film rolls knotted into her hair. About Ferro, burning at thirty-nine degrees and murmuring coordinates in Italian. About the villagers downstream who had agreed to walk the old rail bed as witnesses — the community that had stayed silent for eighty years choosing, finally, to speak, and how much silence like that costs to buy back.

"You've thought about this," I said. "How long?"

"Since the day you left Wewak." He said it without shame. "I knew what you were carrying before you did. I always know. It's the trade."

"One question, then, friend to friend, whatever's left of that." I set the bottle down. "If I say yes — if I hand you the case tonight — do I walk out of here tomorrow? Honestly. Not the answer you'd give Roos. The true one."

Okuk was quiet for a long moment. And that was the answer, really, before he opened his mouth. A man planning to let you live answers quickly, because the lie is easy. A man planning otherwise takes his time, because he's weighing how much truth you'll believe.

"You would walk out," he said at last. "Probably."

"*Probably.*"

"Casper." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register he used for prices. "You are asking me to promise you a thing I may not be able to deliver. Vassek's buyers want the ledgers *and* confirmation of who held them. If I refuse that part, they find another seller and come here anyway. If I agree to it and break my word, I am a man who broke his word to Casper Iversen, which on this river is worth something, but not worth Vassek's patience. So. Probably. I would do everything in my power. My power is not infinite. Nothing on this river is infinite except the water."

I nodded slowly, as if considering, as if the offer were still open, as if my heart weren't already made up and hammering.

"Let me talk to the others," I said.

"Of course." He rose, gathered the bottles, paused at the door. "Casper. One more thing, free of charge, because I liked your father's stories and I like you. Whatever you decide — decide it before midnight. After midnight, I make calls. And after midnight, the decision stops being yours."

* * *

We crowded into the storeroom behind the counter, among sacks of rice and crates of soap, with a hurricane lamp turned so low it was mostly a rumor of light. I laid it out plainly: the offer, the price, and the probably.

"He's selling us," Hargreave said flatly. "He's selling us and dressing it as arithmetic."

"He's doing both," I said. "They're not mutually exclusive. That's what makes him dangerous. Everything he says is true. That's how he gets you to sign."

Mara sat on an overturned crate, arms wrapped around her knees, and I could see her running the map behind her eyes — channels, checkpoints, the airfield, the spur, the geometry of escape with and without Okuk. She'd been doing it since the veranda; I could tell by the way her fingers moved against her shin, tracing lines only she could see.

"If we refuse," she said, "we're two days' walk from the airfield through country Vassek knows better than we do, carrying a feverish man and a wounded one, with every junction checkpointed. Our odds are bad."

"If we accept," Tomas said, "the ledgers die. My grandfather dies again. Everyone in that tunnel dies again, politely, in a filing cabinet." His voice cracked on the last word and he stared at the floor, furious at it.

"Not necessarily," I said.

Five faces turned toward me. Even Ferro, half-conscious on a mat in the corner, opened his eyes.

"Okuk wants a case of ledgers," I said. "A specific case, oilskin-wrapped, lashed under the thwart, seen by his spotters, weighed by his eyes. He wants *that* case. Nobody said the river only carries one."

Hargreave frowned. "You mean copies—"

"I mean a decoy. We build a duplicate. Same wrapping, same weight, same shape. Old company paper is easy enough to fake at the margins — ledgers all look alike from outside, columns and bindings and string ties. Inside, we fill it with anything. Shipping manifests. Hymnals. The expedition's actual useless paperwork, God knows we've got enough of it. The real evidence travels separately, split up, on our bodies, in places a search won't flatter itself by checking."

"They'll search the decoy," Mara said. "Vassek will verify. He's not stupid."

"No. But verification has limits. Vassek can confirm weight, dimensions, wrapping, and a convincing glance at period documents. What he can't do is audit eighty-year-old bookkeeping by lamplight in a trading post while his buyers wait. And Okuk—" I paused, hating the next sentence even as I built it. "Okuk won't look closely. That's the sad part. He doesn't need to know what he's selling. He's never needed to know. Knowing would only make it heavier."

The lamp hissed. Outside, the river went on saying the one thing it always says.

"And Okuk?" Hargreave asked. "When he discovers—"

"When he discovers, we'll be gone, and he'll have a choice: admit he was played, or bury the embarrassment and take his cut anyway. Okuk has spent forty years choosing the profitable version of every story. He'll choose it again." I looked around the room. "But understand what we're doing. I'm not conning a stranger. I'm conning the one man who ever gave me work when my name was worth nothing, and I'm going to spend the rest of my life either justifying it or regretting it, possibly both. If anyone has a better idea, now is the hour for it."

Nobody had a better idea. Tomas wouldn't meet my eye, and I understood that too — the boy had learned in the last week that survival and virtue are rarely on the same boat, and he hadn't yet made peace with which one he'd choose at gunpoint. Give him time. The river teaches it eventually, though the tuition is terrible.

Mara caught my arm as the others drifted back to their mats. In the dark behind the rice sacks her face was close and unreadable, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because some conversations happen better in the pause before words.

"You're sure," she said. Not a question about the plan. A question about me.

"No," I said. "I'm sure about the odds and unsure about everything else. That's been my entire career."

"It doesn't bother you? Using him?"

"It bothers me the way a broken bone bothers you. Constantly, and usefully." I hesitated. "There's a version of me that takes the deal, Mara. Hands over the case, walks north, disappears again under a new name, lets the dead stay filed. He'd live longer. He'd sleep worse. I met that version of myself years ago, on a trestle you've heard about, and I've been running from him ever since. Okuk's offer isn't really about ledgers. It's asking me which man I'm going to be when the price is finally posted."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she reached into her shirt and pulled out the film rolls, knotted in her hair as always, and pressed them briefly into my hand — warm from her skin, canisters ticking faintly like small impatient clocks.

"Then we build a very convincing hymnal," she said.

* * *

We worked past midnight by the hurricane lamp, and it was strange, holy work. Hargreaves turned out to have a gift for forgery I chose not to ask about — his hands remembered how to age paper, how to fox a margin, how to crack a binding spine so it sighed authentically when opened. Mara supplied real period documents salvaged from the gauge station's ruins: waybills, ration chits, a maintenance log, genuine consortium paper to sandwich around the filler. Tomas sewed the oilskin wrap with sail twine, copying the original stitching stitch for stitch, tongue between his teeth, and when he finished he held the twin cases side by side and said, quietly, "One of these is my grandfather," and put the false one down as if it were hot.

At ten minutes to midnight I carried the false case out to the veranda, where Okuk sat smoking in the dark, and set it on the boards between us with a sound like a verdict.

"Done," I said. "Passage for eight. Your word on our faces and our route."

Okuk looked at the case for a long moment. Then he looked at me, and I watched the fixer and the friend fight behind his eyes, and I understood that he suspected — not certainly, not provably, but the way an old fisherman suspects weather — and that he was going to take the case anyway, because hope is a currency and he was long on it.

"My word," he said, and picked it up, and it was heavier than paper should be, and lighter than it needed to be, and we both pretended not to notice.

He was halfway to the door when he stopped, his back to me, the case balanced on one palm like a scale waiting for its final weight.

"Casper," he said. "Your father used to say there are two kinds of men on this river. The ones who float and the ones who swim." He turned his head just enough that I caught the edge of his smile in the lamplight. "I never told you which kind I am. Sleep well. We leave at first light."

And he carried my grandfather's ghost — or Tomas's, or no one's, or everyone's — inside into the dark, and somewhere beyond the black line of the far bank, a phone began to ring.
