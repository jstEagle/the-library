## 26. Double-Cross at the Post

Okuk's smile had always been his best forgery. I'd watched him use it on customs officers, on missionaries, on a Belgian diamond buyer who left Wewak with an empty briefcase and a full heart, and it never once cracked under inspection. So when he smiled at me across the trading post table and said, "Brother, I will get you to the airfield for the price of a case," I knew exactly what kind of case he meant.

Not the aluminum instrument cases stacked against the wall. Not the medical kit. He meant the steel document case that had been riding between my boots since Angoram, the one with the ledgers inside — the ledgers that named names, counted bodies as ballast, and could hang Anselm Roos from the tallest tree in Port Moresby if they ever reached daylight.

"Two days' walk through kunai grass," Okuk said, tapping the table with one finger, "or half a day in my cousin's truck, if the road hasn't eaten itself since the wet started. Your choice, Casper. But you look like a man whose legs have opinions."

"My legs are the least of my problems."

"I know." He said it gently, which was worse. "That is why we are friends."

We were friends in the way that two men who have each saved the other's life once, and each lied to the other a hundred times since, can be friends. Okuk had found me in Wewak when I was nobody, under a borrowed name, sleeping in a boat shed. He'd fed me, employed me, never asked questions I couldn't afford to answer. And now he was selling me — or offering to save me, depending entirely on what was in the case and who was paying.

I'd spent the walk down from the ridge working out which.

* * *

The trading post sat where the old survey road met the river again, a corrugated cathedral of rust and trade goods run by a Chinese family for three generations and now, informally, by Okuk's network of cousins and debts. There was kerosene, tinned mackerel, bolt cloth, a shortwave radio behind the counter that the owner pretended not to know how to work. There were three buildings: the store, a storeroom, and a lean-to where Okuk's cousin kept a Toyota that had more rust than metal but, according to Okuk, "a heart that still beats."

Mara, Tomas, Ferro, Hargreave, and I arrived at dusk, smelling of leeches and three days of jungle. Ferro was walking again, barely, propped between Mara and me, fever down but not out. Hargreave hadn't spoken much since the portage — since his confession on the ridge, delivered in a voice like a man reading his own obituary. He carried himself differently now. Lighter, in the way a man is lighter after setting down something heavy, even if what he set down was his own good name.

Tomas's arm was stitched and splinted. He'd stopped asking about his grandfather somewhere on the second day, not because the grief was gone but because he'd learned to carry it the way the villagers carried their loads — balanced, silent, without complaint.

Okuk met us on the porch with beer that was warm and welcome anyway, and I watched him do the arithmetic. Five foreigners, one steel case, one district where Roos's money had bought every checkpoint and most of the constables. His eyes went to the case twice. The third time, they stayed a half-second too long.

That was all I needed.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Okuk, and about buyers, and about how deals die in places like this:

A man like Okuk doesn't betray you out of malice. He betrays you out of structure. Someone — Roos, or Roos's people, or the middlemen Roos's people used — had already made an offer, probably weeks ago, probably before we ever reached the station. Sell me the evidence when it surfaces, and your cousin's truck gets fuel, your debts get cleared, your name gets clean. Okuk would have said yes the way men say yes to rain: not because they love it, but because it's coming either way and you might as well be standing in the right field.

And here is what I knew about myself: I had spent fifteen years being the man nobody looked at twice. The engineer who signed reports. The drifter under borrowed names. Invisible isn't a condition; it's a skill set. And the first rule of the skill set is this — if you can't avoid the trap, make sure the trap catches something worth less than you.

So while Okuk poured beer and talked about road conditions and the price of diesel, I excused myself to check the boats tied below the post — a fiction, since we'd come overland — and instead had a long, quiet conversation with the storeroom.

The storeroom was dark, smelled of kerosene and dried fish, and contained everything a double-cross requires: an identical steel case (trade goods came in them; half the Sepik shipped its valuables that way), a padlock, and forty minutes of privacy, if the owner kept to his habit of drinking with the cousins until full dark, which he did.

Into the false case I put the things a buyer expects to find and a buyer's expert will spend an hour confirming: company letterheads — real ones, lifted from the station office before Roos's men burned it, which I had done quietly during the escape while everyone else was watching the flames — folded around blank ledger paper aged with river water and mud. A handful of photographs from the adit, duplicates Mara had printed at the mission, real enough but stripped of the location data that made them dangerous. And, because a fake needs weight to convince the hands, I added a brick wrapped in oilcloth and, for sound, a layer of gravel.

Then I did the important part. I made the real case boring.

The ledgers, the execution photographs, Ferro's notebook, the sediment cores' documentation — all of it went into a fish crate. Tinned mackerel on top, sealed with the same wire seals the post used, stenciled with the owner's mark. Forty kilos of the least interesting cargo in Papua New Guinea. Nobody raids the mackerel. Nobody has ever raided the mackerel.

When I came back to the porch, Mara was watching me with that look she had — the cartographer's look, the one that measured drift. She'd noticed the mud on my knees and the kerosene on my hands and said nothing, which is how I knew she'd already worked out most of it herself.

Later, by lamplight in the lean-to, while the others slept, she came and sat beside me and asked, quietly, "How long have you known?"

"Since his eyes went to the case the third time."

"And you didn't say anything."

"If I'd said anything, he'd have known I knew, and then the plan dies and we're back to trusting a man who's already sold us. Silence was the only honest option available." I turned the lamp down. "You'll carry the fish crate tomorrow. You, not me."

"Why me?"

"Because Okuk expects the evidence to travel with me. I'm the escort. It's my job description. Everything valuable rides with the escort — that's the story he'll tell his buyers, because it's the story that makes sense. You're the cartographer. In his ledger you're luggage with opinions." I looked at her. "Also, because you're the only person I trust to lie with a straight face if someone opens the crate and asks why the mackerel smells like 1943."

Mara was quiet for a moment. Then she laughed — low, tired, real. "Casper Iversen. Fifteen years of being nobody, and it turns out you were in training the whole time."

"Somebody had to be."

* * *

Morning came gray and dripping. Okuk's cousin's truck coughed itself awake, and we loaded it under Okuk's cheerful supervision — the false steel case into the cab bed, lashed where a careful man would lash something precious, visible enough to be seen, secured enough to be believed. The fish crate went into the canvas-covered rear with the medical kit and Ferro, who rode lying down among the supplies, playing dead so well it required no acting.

The plan, as I explained it to Okuk with a straight face, was simple: the truck would take the survey road as far as the washout, then we'd transfer to the track that climbed toward the airfield. Two vehicles' worth of noise was too much, so Okuk would stay at the post — "someone has to hold our rear," I told him, and watched him accept the lie like a man accepting change for a note he knows is counterfeit.

"Casper." He gripped my hand at the tailgate, both hands, the way you hold a friend or a hostage. "This thing you're carrying. Whatever it is. Some cargoes are heavier after you deliver them than before."

"I know," I said. "I've delivered a few."

His smile held. Mine held. Between the two of them, the truth suffocated quietly, and the truck pulled out.

* * *

The road was worse than advertised, which for roads in this country is a competitive field. Twice we winched the Toyota out of soup that passed for track, and once we forded a creek that had opinions about our axle clearance. Hargreave rode in silence, holding the grab rail like a penitent holds a rail in church. Tomas navigated off Mara's hand-drawn strip map, calling turns before they appeared, and every time he got one right, something in his face unclenched a little — the boy discovering that competence is a rope you can hold onto when everything else lets go.

At the washout — a stretch where the wet season had taken thirty meters of road and given back a swamp — we stopped to scout the bypass on foot. That was when we heard it.

Engines. More than one. Coming up the survey road from the direction of the river, moving fast, not bothering with stealth.

Roos's security detail had been hunting us since the station burned. They'd lost us at the sudd, picked up rumor at the trading posts — Okuk's posts, I'd have bet the ledgers themselves — and now they were closing on the one place we'd been seen. Which meant they weren't following us.

They were going to Okuk.

I stood in the wet grass at the edge of the washout and felt the plan assemble itself in my chest the way a bridge assembles in a mind — not built, but revealed, load paths finding their bearings. Okuk had promised his buyers the case. The buyers were coming for the case. The case was still sitting in the truck bed, ten meters away, lashed and waiting.

They were going to raid a cache that was currently attached to us.

"No," I said aloud, and Mara looked over.

"No what?"

"We don't run. Running puts the case in play and puts us in the open. We give them the case." I was already unbuckling the lashings. "On our terms, at a place of our choosing, with everyone looking at the wrong thing."

* * *

The place I chose was a bend in the survey road two kilometers back the way we'd come, where an old culvert had collapsed and left the roadbed pinched between a cut bank and a drop into secondary growth. One vehicle wide. Good fields of fire for anyone above the road, terrible ones for anyone on it. I'd noted it on the way in the way I note everything — old habit, closed lines, the geography of retreat — and now it paid its rent.

We staged the truck broadside across the pinch point, engine off, the false case sitting on the tailgate in plain view like bait on a hook that didn't know it was bait. Then we took positions in the cut bank's brush: me with the shotgun we'd salvaged from the station, Tomas with the rifle, Mara with Ferro dragged back into the treeline where the Toyota waited, loaded, pointed down the overgrown spur toward the airfield track. Hargreave I stationed with the truck, hands visible, the designated surrender object — the man who wanted, God help him, to negotiate with somebody, anybody, and here at last was his chance.

"They'll hail first," I told him. "Let them. Be frightened. Be honest. Tell them the others ran into the bush and you'll trade the case for your life. Every word of that will be true except the last part."

"What's untrue about the last part?"

"You keep breathing either way. That's the trade. The case just changes owners."

He looked at me for a long moment, and there was something new in his face — not courage, exactly, but the shape courage leaves behind when it moves through. "You've done this before," he said. "Not this. Something like this."

"Once," I said. "I was on the other side of the culvert."

The engines arrived four minutes later. Two vehicles — a white Land Cruiser running government plates that belonged to no government, and a flatbed with six men in the back wearing the particular neutrality of hired guns the world over: black shirts, no insignia, weapons slung casual and ready. They fanned out at the sight of the blocked road with a professionalism that confirmed everything I'd suspected about Roos's budget.

A voice called out, amplified and bored: "Occupants of the vehicle. Step out slowly. This is a commercial dispute. Cooperate and no one is hurt."

Hargreave stepped out with his hands up and his voice cracking beautifully. "Please — please, I'm just the scientist, they took everything, the others ran — the case is there, take it, take it and let me go—"

It was a fine performance. What made it fine was that none of it was acting.

Six men converged on the tailgate. The leader — a heavyset Fijian, I guessed, or a Solomon Islander with Fijian training — set his rifle against the truck bed and snapped the padlock with a pair of bolt cutters, and I watched him lift the lid and see the letterheads, and I watched the letterheads do their work. His posture changed. His men crowded in. For ninety seconds, every eye on that road was on the contents of a box full of gravel and history.

"Now," I said.

Tomas fired first — high, deliberate, into the cut bank above them, rock spalling down onto the flatbed's cab. I put two shells into the Land Cruiser's front tires, and the vehicle sat down hard with a sound like a dropped piano. The world went loud. Muzzle flash from the road, return fire chewing the truck Hargreave dove behind, and through it all the beautiful, stupid fact that held: they had come for a case, and a case was what they intended to leave with, and men who intend to leave with something do not shoot at it.

The leader figured it out faster than I'd credited — maybe eight seconds instead of twenty — and screamed "It's a diversion, burn them—" but eight seconds is a geological age when you're the invisible man and you've already moved. I was rolling down the cut bank's far side while his sentence was still forming, and Tomas was sliding after me, and behind us Hargreave performed his final aria — screaming, fleeing into the bush in the direction opposite the Toyota, drawing two pursuers who found nothing but kunai grass and their own echo.

Mara had the Toyota moving before we reached it. Ferro, fever-bright, hauled the door open with both hands and pulled us in like a man hauling in fish, and then we were crashing down the overgrown spur with branches raking the roof and gunfire cracking somewhere behind us, aimed at nothing, aimed at the space where clever men had been.

* * *

We drove an hour before anyone spoke. The spur degraded into a track, the track into a suggestion, and finally into a grassy seam that only Mara's charts insisted existed. She navigated; I drove; the rest held on.

It was Tomas who broke the silence, from the back seat, in a voice scraped raw. "The case. They got the case."

"They got a brick and some very convincing paperwork," I said.

"The letterheads were real."

"The letterheads were real. The ledgers were mackerel." I glanced at the mirror. "Everything that matters is in the fish crate under your feet, son. It has been since last night."

He looked down at the crate he'd been resting his boots on for an hour, and then at me, and then he did something I didn't expect: he laughed. It came out broken and disbelieving and it turned into something else halfway through, and Mara reached back and took his good hand and let him finish whatever it was.

Ferro, from the floor of the truck bed, feverish and grinning: "The mackerel. He hid the fall of a corporate dynasty under tinned mackerel."

"It's the last place anyone looks," I said. "I checked."

* * *

We camped that night in a grove of tulip trees off the seam of a track, cold food, no fire, watches staggered. Somewhere far behind us, Okuk was learning what his buyers had paid for, and I found I couldn't make myself feel triumphant about it. He'd have heard the gunfire. He'd have done the arithmetic by midnight: the case empty, the foreigners gone, his own position suddenly very exposed between angry mercenaries and a tribunal that might someday ask him questions. Okuk survived by being useful to whoever was winning. By tomorrow he'd be calculating which side that was.

"He'll sell us again," Mara said quietly, beside me on watch. "Given the chance."

"Probably."

"And you're not angry?"

I thought about it honestly. "I'm sad. He fed me when I was nobody. That buys a man one betrayal free, in my book. He's spent it. If there's a next time, it's just business."

Mara leaned her shoulder against mine, there in the dark with the frogs going and the trees dripping. "One betrayal free," she repeated. "You keep accounts like a railroad man."

"Railroads keep the best accounts. Everything has to balance or the whole thing falls over." I looked north, toward where the airfield waited under its grass, three days out if the weather held and Roos's floatplane didn't find us first. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we climb."

She squeezed my arm and went, and I sat my watch alone with the stars and the frogs and the weight of what we carried — forty kilos of dead men's testimony riding in a fish crate, headed for an airfield that might already have visitors.

Because that was the thought I couldn't shake, the one that sat in my chest like a stone in a culvert: the gunfire at the post had been heard by more people than Okuk. Sound carries up valleys. And somewhere ahead of us, in the direction of the only plane capable of taking us out, someone else might be doing the same arithmetic we were.

At dawn, we climbed — and by midmorning, from a ridgeline clearing, Tomas spotted it first and went rigid.

Smoke. Thin, straight, deliberate. Rising from the direction of the airfield in the grass.
