## 27. The Airfield in the Grass

The old survey road gave up on us at midday of the third day of the portage, the way a tired man gives up an argument — not all at once, but in stages, first softening at the shoulders, then lying down entirely. After that it was Mara's compass and my memory of a map drawn by men who'd been dead for forty years, and the two of them disagreed more often than either would admit.

We came out of the treeline onto the strip just as the afternoon light went long and gold, and I'll be honest with you: for about ten seconds I thought we'd walked three days over jungle ridges to die in a hayfield.

Kunai grass stood chest-high across the whole length of it, silver-green and hissing in the wind, running maybe eight hundred meters to a wall of forest that had spent decades trying to reclaim what the war had borrowed. At the far end, half-swallowed by the grass itself, sat a shape I recognized the way you recognize a face in an old photograph — angular, familiar, wrong in all the details.

"A plane," Tomas said, from under the bundle he was carrying. He said it the way a drowning man says *land*.

"A plane-shaped object," I corrected him. "Let's not get ahead of ourselves."

Hargreave lowered himself onto a log with the care of a man whose body had filed a formal complaint. He hadn't spoken much since the station burned behind us. When he did speak, it came out flat and small, like a radio with dying batteries. "Is it flyable?"

"Everything's flyable," I said. "The question is whether it's landable afterward."

Mara dropped her pack and stood with her hands on her hips, surveying the grass like she was arguing with it already. She'd been arguing with rivers since Wewak; the grass didn't stand a chance, but it would take time. Time was the one thing we had in quantities even smaller than food.

"Three days," I heard myself say, and then wondered where the number had come from. It had come from somewhere honest, though. Somewhere before the trestle, before the report, back when I still certified aircraft ferries out of Lae and knew a Pratt & Whitney the way a priest knows his psalms. Three days, if the engine wasn't seized. Four, if it was. Never, if the spar was cracked, but I kept that last figure to myself, because a crew walking on hope walks faster than a crew walking on arithmetic.

Ferro, feverish and ninety pounds lighter than the man who'd gone missing from the gauge station, lay in the shade of the treeline with Mara's shirt soaked and draped over him. His eyes tracked us with the desperate attention of a man who'd spent weeks alone listening to the jungle decide whether to eat him. "You can fix it?" he rasped.

"I can find out why I can't," I said. "That's usually step one."

* * *

The plane was a Noorduyn Norseman.

I want you to understand what that meant to me. A Norseman is not elegant. It is a flying barge with delusions of usefulness — high-winged, tail-dragging, built in the forties to land on things that were not, strictly speaking, landing surfaces. Bush pilots loved them the way mules love other mules: with respect, familiarity, and no illusions whatsoever. Somebody had ferried this one in during the war or shortly after, hauled freight along the very rail spur we'd just walked, and then — for reasons lost to whatever paperwork survived the consortium's tidy habits — simply walked away from it.

Which meant it had been sitting here through perhaps thirty wet seasons.

I waded into the kunai with a machete we'd taken off a burned checkpoint and started cutting toward the fuselage. The grass fought back. It always does. Kunai has edges like a razor's opinion and hides mud, holes, snakes, and personal embarrassment with equal enthusiasm. Ten meters in I found the port wheel, sunk to the axle in black soil, and five meters past that the propeller, one blade bent forward like a finger beckoning.

"Well," I said, to nobody. "There's our first bill."

Mara appeared beside me, machete swinging in a rhythm she'd learned fast, the way she learned everything. "Bad?"

"Bent prop means the engine stopped hard. Means something happened — ground loop, gear collapse, maybe just a pilot who got bored of waiting out the rain and landed heavy." I put my hand flat against the cowling. The metal was cool, which was good. Cool metal meant no fire. "Or it means nothing. Engines have surprised me before."

"You're smiling," she said.

"I'm remembering." I looked at her, and the truth came out easier than I expected, the way truths do when you've been carrying them uphill for three days. "I used to certify these. Ferry flights, out of Lae. Some outfit would buy a war surplus airframe cheap, and some fool — usually me — would sign the paper saying it was safe to deliver. I was twenty-four and I thought a signature was just ink."

"And now?"

"Now I know a signature is a promise you make to strangers." I turned back to the cowling and started unfastening it. "So I don't sign anything anymore unless I mean it."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "You're going to sign this one."

"If it deserves it."

"That's not what I asked, Casper."

I didn't answer, mostly because the answer was yes and yes was a door I'd kept locked for eleven years. Instead I pulled the cowling panel free and looked at an engine that had been dreaming since before Tomas was born.

* * *

Here is what three days of repair actually looks like, in case you're imagining something cinematic:

Day one is archaeology. You open every panel and every inspection cover and you catalog the ways a machine can disappoint you. The Norseman's R-1340 radial — nine cylinders in a ring, a design older than most nations — was full of water where water should never be, which meant at least one cylinder was likely scored. The fuel tanks held rainwater and a dead rat, in that order of volume. The tires had rotted to the consistency of overripe fruit. The fabric on the aft fuselage had been eaten by sun and insects until you could see the ribs, like a fish skeleton wearing a coat.

But the spar — the spine of the wing, the thing that decides whether you live or die — was dry. And the control cables, miracle of miracles, had been greased by somebody competent before the plane was abandoned, and grease keeps. I ran my hand down each one like a man checking a pulse.

"The depot," I told Tomas that evening. We'd made camp at the treeline, and down the strip, half a kilometer through the grass, stood the remains of the rail depot that had served this airfield — a corrugated roof on stilts, a loading ramp, and, if the closed-line maps had taught me anything about how these places were built, a maintenance shed with tools nobody had bothered to steal because nobody remembered the place existed.

Tomas had become my shadow since the tunnel. He carried things without being asked. He watched my hands when I worked, memorizing. His grandfather had been listed in a company ledger as *expended*, two words that contained an entire life, and I think the boy had decided that if the world could produce men who wrote such ledgers, it could also produce men who fixed airplanes, and he intended to be standing next to the second kind.

"What are we looking for?"

"Spanners. Pliers. Wire. Any oil that isn't soup. And a battery, though I'm not stupid enough to hope." I paused. "And a hacksaw blade. For the prop."

"We're going to cut the propeller?"

"One blade. Then file the other two back into balance." I saw his face and laughed for the first time in days. "It sounds mad. It is mad. But a Norseman will fly on a hand-filed prop if the man filing it is patient and slightly afraid, and I am both."

* * *

The depot gave like a generous uncle. In the shed, under a roof that had held better than anyone deserved, we found a tool chest with the lid rusted shut — Tomas opened it with a crowbar and a grunt and stepped back like he'd uncovered treasure, which, in fairness, he had. British spanners, Australian pliers, a set of feeler gauges still in their envelope, a tin of engine oil gone to varnish at the top but liquid underneath, and — I actually sat down when I saw it — a six-volt battery, dry-stored on a shelf, its terminals still bright.

Beside the battery, someone had left a pair of work gloves folded neatly, as if they'd be needed tomorrow.

Tomorrow had been thirty years ago. I put the gloves on anyway. They fit badly and I wore them for the rest of the job, and if you want to know what kind of man I am, that's probably as good a detail as any.

* * *

Day two was surgery.

We drained the engine — nine cylinders' worth of milky water-oil cocktail that smelled like a swamp's regrets. Then came the part I'd been dreading: turning it over by hand to feel for damage. With the plugs out, you bar the prop through compression, cylinder by cylinder, and you listen with your hands. A healthy engine resists evenly, springy and smooth. A damaged one catches, grinds, or gives you a silence where metal should meet metal.

One through four: fine. Five: fine. Six —

Six scraped.

Not seized. Not ground to death. Scraped, the way a sleeve might scrape if a ring land had let go, or the way a bore might drag if a cylinder had rusted pitted where water pooled above the piston. I knelt there with my arm inside the engine's throat for a long time, feeling that small wrongness repeat itself, and Tomas watched my face the way you watch weather.

"Can you fix it?"

"No." I sat back on my heels. "But I might be able to persuade it. Rust in a bore can sometimes be broken down with penetrating oil and patience, and the ring may reseat once it runs. Or it may smoke like a signal fire and lose power on takeoff. There's exactly one way to find out, and it involves being airborne when we do."

He considered this with the gravity of a young man who'd recently learned that ledgers get written about people like him. "Then we find out in the air," he said, "where it matters less."

"That," I said, "is either very brave or very foolish."

"My grandfather crossed an ocean to dig in a hole for men who wrote numbers next to his name." He picked up the oil can. "I think I can handle a smoking engine."

I didn't have anything to say to that, so I handed him the feeler gauges and taught him to gap spark plugs, and we worked until the light went, and somewhere in there I realized I'd stopped counting the hours, which is what happens when your hands finally believe in what they're doing.

Mara came out at dusk with rice and tinned fish and sat on the wheel strut watching us. She'd spent her day doing what she always did — mapping. She'd walked the strip end to end, pacing it, noting the soft ground near the midpoint where a creek had undermined the surface, marking the wind sock pole — bare, of course, thirty years bare — and drawing the whole thing in her notebook with those quick certain strokes she used on channels.

"Eight hundred and ten meters usable," she reported. "Maybe seven-fifty if we avoid the wet section. Grass adds drag but the surface underneath is laterite. Firm."

"You paced it?"

"I argued with it," she said. "It conceded."

That night she sat beside me while I cleaned tools by lamplight, and after a while she said, quietly, "You're different here."

"Different how?"

"In Wewak you moved like a man practicing being invisible. On the river too, right up until the crocodile bank. But today you were loud. You were swearing at an engine in front of everyone." She tilted her head. "I liked it."

"I swear beautifully. It's one of my few marketable skills."

"Casper."

"Mara." I set down the spanner. The lamp threw our shadows huge against the shed wall, two shapes leaning together like they'd been drawn that way on purpose. "Eleven years ago I signed a paper I didn't mean, and a bridge fell, and three men died, and I spent every day since making sure no one ever had reason to ask me to sign anything again. This week I've been asked to sign everything. Testimony. Maps. Names. And now an airworthiness certificate for a thirty-year-old wreck carrying people I—" I stopped. Recalibrated. Started again, lower. "People I refuse to lose."

"So sign it," she said. "Mean it this time."

"That's the plan. That's the whole plan. Sign it, load it, fly it, and put the evidence somewhere Roos can't burn it."

"And after?"

"After," I said, "there's a tribunal, and a trial, and a version of me with a name attached to it that anyone can look up. No more borrowed names. No more Okuk handing me work under aliases. The invisible man becomes permanent record." I turned the glove over on my hand, the dead man's neat stitching. "I keep finding that I mind less than I expected."

She reached over and took the rag out of my lap, folded it, set it aside — small domestic gestures, the grammar of people building something. "Good," she said. "Because I charted the river route back to Angoram while you were playing mechanic, and I intend to need a co-pilot who argues with me properly."

"Properly," I repeated.

"It's a term of art."

* * *

Day three was resurrection.

We gapped and installed the plugs. We serviced what we could of the brakes — one worked, which on a taildragger is a coin flip you make with your heels. We wired the battery into a starter circuit I rebuilt from scrap wire and prayer, and at noon, with Ferro propped against a log where he could see, with Hargreave standing apart the way guilty men stand apart, with the whole ragged company of us gathered at the edge of the grass, I swung the prop.

Nothing.

I primed again, checked the fuel flow — gravity feed from the header tank, thank God for simple airplanes — checked the mags, and swung again.

On the third pull, engine number six coughed up thirty years of rust and history and caught, and the R-1340 lit cylinder by cylinder around the ring like a slow round of applause, and then settled into a rhythm that was rough, smoky, uneven — and alive. Blue smoke rolled back over the grass. The whole strip seemed to lean toward the sound.

Tomas was shouting. Ferro was laughing, which turned into coughing, which turned into laughing again. Even Hargreave took a step forward, then another, like a man approaching a fire he wasn't sure he was allowed to warm himself at.

I shut it down after ninety seconds, ears ringing, hands shaking in a way I decided to attribute to adrenaline rather than anything else. Number six was smoking like a wet leaf pile and losing compression on every stroke. But it was holding. Barely. The way a rope holds when you're careful how much weight you trust it with.

"She'll fly," I announced, and immediately hated how much I sounded like the twenty-four-year-old in Lae. So I amended it, loudly, so everyone heard: "She'll fly *carefully*. Full tanks, light load, no showboating, and if she starts losing power over the ridge we turn back while turning back is still a thing we can do. I'm certifying her for one flight. One."

Mara appeared at my elbow. "One flight to where? The missionary strip at Yabim is two hundred kilometers. That's beyond your fuel margin with six cylinders instead of nine."

"Yabim's the only strip long enough and friendly enough." I wiped my hands. "So we carry maximum fuel, minimum people, and the evidence goes in the cabin where I can see it. First flight takes me and Tomas and the ledgers and the photographs. If I make it, I send help back for the rest of you within the day."

"Absolutely not," she said. "You're not flying off alone with the evidence while we sit in the grass waiting for Roos to find us."

"Mara—"

"He's already looking," she said, and her voice went quiet in the way that meant she'd been sitting on this all day. "Tomas heard it first — a drone, high, northeast, an hour before dusk. Too high to identify. Could be a patrol plane. Could be a crop duster. Could be nothing." She met my eyes. "But you taught me that dread arrives before danger, and I've been dreading since lunch."

The laughter died out of the clearing. The engine ticked as it cooled, ticking away the seconds.

I looked down the strip — eight hundred meters of gold grass, one battered Norseman breathing blue smoke, and somewhere over the northeastern ridgeline, possibly, a set of eyes.

"Then we don't wait for morning," I said. "Fuel her to the tabs. Load the evidence now. We go at first light — no, earlier. We go in the dark, on the lights I rig from the truck batteries in the depot, and we're airborne before that drone comes back to count us."

"And if the engine won't take the load in cold, dense night air?" Hargreave asked. His voice was flat, but I noticed he'd moved closer to the group. Guilt makes strange bedfellows of pride.

"Then it won't," I said, "and we'll know quickly." I picked up the gloves — the dead man's gloves, my gloves now — and pulled them on. "Somebody go wake Tomas. He's going to want to see this part."

Above the treeline, the first stars were coming out, and the kunai grass hissed all down the strip like a crowd settling in to watch.
