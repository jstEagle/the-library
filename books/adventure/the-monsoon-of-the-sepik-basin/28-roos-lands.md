## 28. Roos Lands

The floatplane came out of the east at half past four in the afternoon, which told me two things before I ever heard the engine. First, that Roos liked to arrive with the sun behind him so that anyone watching from the ground squinted into the glare while his men didn't. Second, that he'd been circling long enough to time it. Men who circle are men who already know what they'll find.

I heard it first as a change in the air over the kunai grass — a pressure shift, like the field itself flinching — and then the drone resolved into a twin-engine amphibian, white with a blue stripe, dropping down through the heat haze toward the river bend half a kilometer south of the airfield. It touched down with the casual authority of something that had landed on worse water in worse places, taxied to the bank, and began disgorging men.

I counted from the treeline with Mara's binoculars. Eight on the ground plus the pilot, which meant nine, which meant Roos had come heavy. The floatplane could carry ten with fuel for this range if nobody brought luggage, and these men traveled light — carbines slung across their chests, webbing, no packs. Professionals don't pack. Professionals assume they'll be going home by the same door they came in.

"Eight," I said, passing the glasses back to Mara.

"Nine," she said. "There's one holding at the plane."

Of course there was. There was always one holding at the plane. In my railroad days we called him the man who keeps the engine warm, and every operation worth its salt had one, because the difference between an escape plan and a wish was always whether somebody had thought to keep the engine warm.

* * *

We'd reached the strip the evening before, eleven of us moving like ghosts through grass tall enough to hide a standing man, and I will not pretend it had gone well. The portage had taken everything out of Hargreave — not his legs, which were still working, but whatever lived behind his eyes. He walked the last ridge like a man following his own funeral procession, and when the derelict hangar finally rose out of the kunai he sat down against its wall and stared at his hands as though they belonged to someone whose biography he'd rather not discuss.

The plane was a wreck. That's the honest word for it. A 1960s-era utility taildragger that some mission or mining outfit had abandoned when it stopped being cheaper to fix than to replace, sitting where the grass had grown up around its wheels like the jungle was slowly digesting it. Three days of work, I'd told them, and I'd meant it — fabric patches, a fuel system full of varnish, tires that were more rumor than rubber. But it was a wreck with wings, and wings beat walking.

Now the wings had competition.

Roos's men fanned out along the riverbank and started up toward the strip in a loose skirmish line, unhurried, weapons low. They weren't hunting. They knew exactly where we were — or believed they did — and their pace said they considered us cargo, not combatants. That was fine by me. Arrogance is a gift, and I've spent my whole life accepting gifts from careless men.

"Casper." Hargreave's voice behind me, raw from three days of silence. "How many?"

"Enough."

"I want to talk to them."

Mara and I turned together. Hargreave had gotten to his feet, and something had happened to his posture — he stood straighter than he had since the station burned, straighter than he had on the portage, straighter maybe than he had in years. It was the posture of a man who has finished doing arithmetic and doesn't like the total but refuses to round down.

"No," I said.

"You don't get to decide that."

"I'm the one keeping you all breathing, Doctor, so today I do."

He looked at me, and then past me, at the treeline, at the distant figures in white and blue moving up through the grass, and he said, quietly, in the voice of a man stating a fact about weather: "He won't stop, Casper. You know that now. Not for the ledgers, not for the witnesses, not for anything. He burned the station. He'll burn the village. He'll burn the tribunal's credibility and every one of us with it, because that's cheaper than the alternative, and everything Anselm Roos does is priced." He swallowed. "But he'll trade. He trades. It's the only language he speaks fluently. And I am — I am the most expensive thing on this field."

"Hargreave—"

"He wants the ledgers and he wants the problem to go away. I can be the problem. Me, walking out there, hands empty, offering myself and every document we have in exchange for the crew walking away." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Redemption priced honestly. I've been carrying that phrase around since the trestle, Casper, since you told me what you signed and why. I thought it was poetry. It turns out it's just an invoice."

"It's a lie," Mara said. Her voice was flat and hard. "You walk out there and he takes you and shoots us anyway. That's how men like Roos close accounts. They don't honor invoices."

"Maybe," Hargreave said. "But maybe they do, and right now 'maybe' is the only currency I have left." He picked up his pack — the one with Ferro's notebook, the photographs, the ledger pages we'd carried over the ridges — and held it against his chest like a man hugging a life vest. "I knew. From the start. I knew what Kessler was funding and I told myself it was heritage recovery, I told myself the ends would justify, I told myself all the things clever men tell themselves while they're being paid. I'm done telling myself things." He looked at each of us in turn — Tomas with his bandaged shoulder, Ferro still gray with fever under a tarp, the villagers huddled in the hangar's shadow, Mara, me. "Let an old coward spend himself properly. It's the only thing left I'm qualified to do."

And God help me, I understood him. That was the worst part. I understood the arithmetic completely, because I'd run the same numbers once outside a collapsed trestle with a report in my hand and a company's survival on the other side of the scale, and I'd chosen wrong, and I'd been running from the choice for fifteen years. Hargreave was choosing right, late, and late-right is still right. But choosing right and surviving it are different transactions, and I had watched enough men die to know that Roos accepted sacrifices the way a furnace accepts coal — gratefully, and without any obligation afterward.

"Give me an hour," I said.

"For what?"

"For the thing I'm good at."

* * *

Here is what I did with the hour. I did what I had done at the gauge station, what I had done on the river, what I had been doing my whole disreputable life: I made myself small and useful and invisible, and I moved pieces.

Tomas, shoulder strapped but hands good, went into the hangar with me and we dragged the derelict's spare fuel drum — mostly rust, quarter full of ancient avgas — to the north end of the strip, where the treeline crowded closest. A rag soaked in that gas, a spark from the truck battery Tomas had been using to test the magneto, and we had ourselves a fire that would burn hot and black and look, from four hundred meters away through heat shimmer, exactly like an aircraft being torched. Roos's men would see smoke and think: evidence destroyed, job half done, push faster. Men accelerate when they think the prize is burning. Accelerating men stop checking their corners.

Ferro, feverish but lucid in the way that fever sometimes sharpens rather than blunts, gave me the coordinates of the false distress frequency he'd used at the station, and I keyed our salvaged handheld and put out a call in Tok Pisin, rough-voiced, panicked-sounding, from the far side of the strip: police post, ambush, wounded, need boat at the lower landing. It wouldn't fool anyone for long. It didn't need to. It needed to make two of the eight hesitate about which direction the threat lay, and hesitation is a currency too.

And Mara — Mara took the real ledgers, the real photographs, Ferro's real notebook, and wrapped them in oilcloth inside her chart tube, the one she'd carried through the sudd and the portage and the ambush at the viaduct, and she slung it across her back and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to, because reading it would have cost me the next hour and I didn't have the hour.

"If he walks out there," she said, "and Roos takes him—"

"Then we take the plane."

"With eight men between us and it."

"Seven, after the fire pulls two. Six, if the radio call works. And one of those six is going to be watching the plane instead of us, because I'm going to make sure of it." I checked the pistol I'd taken off a mercenary at the trestle and found I disliked its weight, which was correct; familiarity with a weapon is a comfort, and comfort is how men die stupidly. "Mara. If it goes wrong—"

"Don't say it."

"Somebody has to fly it. If it's not me—"

"I said don't." She gripped my forearm, hard, once, and let go, and that single grip contained more negotiation, argument, apology, and promise than most marriages manage in a decade, and then she turned and went to stand with the villagers, and I went to find Hargreave.

He was waiting at the edge of the kunai, pack hugged to his chest, watching the white-and-blue figures come on through the grass. Up close, in the last light, he looked older than he had that first morning at the staging camp, when he'd radiated confidence like a stove radiates heat — wastefully, and to everyone's eventual discomfort.

"They're forty minutes out," I said. "When they're two hundred meters, walk. Hands visible. Say your piece fast. Don't give him time to think, because thinking is where he lives and you don't."

"And you'll be—"

"Nowhere. Being nowhere is my profession."

He almost laughed. "Casper. The ledgers. When this is done — whatever happens to me — they go to the tribunal. All of them. Promise me you won't trade them for anything, including me. Especially me."

"That's your price, not mine."

"It's the whole point of a price," he said. "It has to hurt, or it isn't one." And he stepped out of the shadow of the treeline into the open grass, and began to walk toward the men coming to kill us, and I lost sight of him in the kunai and hated every second of it.

* * *

They met at the old windsock pole, a hundred and fifty meters out, where the strip's faded centerline used to start. I watched from the hangar's broken window through the binoculars, and Mara watched beside me, and neither of us spoke.

Roos came forward alone. I'd never seen him in person — only heard his name spoken the way people speak the names of weather systems — and he was smaller than I'd expected, neat, silver-templed, dressed for a board meeting that had gone regretfully outdoors. He stopped six feet from Hargreave and listened with his head tilted, courteous as a banker, while Hargreave talked. We couldn't hear the words. We didn't need to. We'd written them together in the hour, and I knew their shape: the ledgers, the photographs, the witnesses released, the crew walks, I stay, I sign whatever you want, I am yours, buy me.

Roos let him finish. Then he said something short.

Hargreave's shoulders dropped — not much, a centimeter, but I saw it, and Mara's breath caught beside me, and we both knew what a centimeter of dropped shoulders means in a negotiation. It means the answer was no.

Roos extended his hand. Hargreave hesitated, then took it, and I thought — for one stupid, hopeful second — that maybe the deal was done, that mercy had cleared customs after all. Then two of the mercenaries stepped forward, and one of them took the pack gently off Hargreave's chest, and the other took Hargreave's arm, also gently, also firmly, and Roos turned and began walking back toward the river, and Hargreave walked with him, unresisting, looking — this is the part I can't shake — looking relieved.

"He took the deal," Ferro said from the doorway, fever-bright. "Didn't he? He took it?"

"No," Mara said quietly. "He took the man. Not the mercy."

Because that was the thing about Anselm Roos, the thing the whole Sepik Basin was about to learn: he didn't honor invoices. He collected assets. Hargreave had offered himself as payment, and Roos had simply accepted delivery — the expedition lead, the scientist whose testimony could anchor a tribunal case, the man who could confirm provenance, chain of custody, intent. Dead, Hargreave was a problem. Alive and owned, he was a witness they controlled. Somewhere downstream, in a room with good lighting, a broken man would sign statements saying whatever the Kessler Development Group required him to say, and his signature would carry weight precisely because of how honestly he'd once tried to spend himself.

Or they'd use him up and discard him. With men like Roos both outcomes were budgeted for.

The fire at the north end chose that moment to catch properly, black smoke boiling up into the orange sky, and the skirmish line broke — two men sprinting toward it, shouting, exactly as designed — and Roos paused, glanced at the smoke, and made a small gesture with two fingers that I couldn't interpret and didn't need to, because the pilot at the floatplane spun up his engines, and the message was clear: whatever burns, burns; we have what we came for.

Except they didn't. The pack they'd taken from Hargreave held the decoy ledgers — photocopies, aged with tea, wrapped around blank survey forms — because I had learned my lesson at Okuk's trading post and never again let the real thing travel in the obvious container. The real evidence rode in Mara's chart tube, on the back of a woman standing thirty meters from me in a broken hangar, and Anselm Roos was walking away from it toward a plane, believing the transaction closed.

Hargreave turned once, at the water's edge, and looked back at the strip — at the smoke, at the hangar, at the grass where he knew we were — and raised his free hand. Not waving. Just raised, palm out, the way you'd signal a train from a platform: I see you. Go on.

Then the floatplane lifted off the brown water, climbed into the last of the light, and turned south, and the sound of it faded until the river and the insects took the world back.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The fire crackled itself out at the north end. Somewhere in the kunai, a bird made an idiotic, beautiful noise, indifferent to history.

"We go after them," Tomas said finally. His voice cracked on *them*. "The plane — we fix ours, we follow—"

"With what, a slingshot?" Ferro said. But gently.

"We follow the *plan*," I said. "The pass opens in five days. The witnesses move tomorrow night. The evidence goes with them. And Hargreave—" My throat closed on it, and I made myself finish. "Hargreave bought us the five days. Whatever else that costs him, we don't waste the purchase."

Mara was staring south, at the empty sky, her jaw set in a way I'd learned to fear and admire in equal measure. "He looked relieved," she said. "Did you see it? At the end. He looked *relieved*."

"Yeah," I said. "I saw it."

Because I had. And because I knew — the way you know things about strangers you recognize — that somewhere over the mountains, in a seat between two mercenaries, a man who had spent twenty years telling himself stories was finally, terribly, at peace with how his ended. And that peace was the most expensive thing anyone had purchased on this river in eighty years, and Anselm Roos hadn't paid a cent for it.

Five days. The glacier season waited, and the pass would open like a door, and on the other side of it were a tribunal, a storm of lawyers, and a dead man's sworn statement that we hadn't even recorded yet — because Hargreave had died, in every way that mattered, before he'd finished speaking.

Which meant the rest of us would have to finish it for him.

I turned back to the hangar, to the wreck with wings, and rolled up my sleeves.

Three days of work. We had five. For the first time in my life, the math felt generous.
