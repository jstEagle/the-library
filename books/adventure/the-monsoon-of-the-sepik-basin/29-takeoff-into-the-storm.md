## 29. Takeoff Into the Storm

Three days of scavenging, swearing, and bleeding knuckles had produced a machine that was, by any honest engineering standard, an insult to aviation. She was a derelict de Havilland Canada Beaver that hadn't flown since before the war that mattered, with a carburetor I'd rebuilt twice from parts of two other dead engines and a fuel system held together by faith and hose clamps. Her fabric skin was patched in six places with kunai grass woven into adhesive tape. Her compass swung like a drunk looking for the door.

But her crankcase compression tested true on all seven cylinders, and her propeller was straight, and in this part of the world those were the only two opinions that counted.

"She'll fly," I told Mara, for the fourth time, because saying it out loud made it more likely.

"You said that yesterday."

"Yesterday she wouldn't have. Today she will. That's progress. Engineering is just failure with a schedule."

Mara didn't laugh. None of us had laughed much since Hargreave walked out onto the strip two mornings ago with his hands open and his conscience finally loaded, and Roos took the man and not the mercy. We'd watched from the treeline as the floatplane lifted off the river with Hargreave standing between two mercenaries, upright, almost dignified, a man paying his debts at last — and we'd all understood that what Roos bought wasn't Hargreave's cooperation. It was his silence. And silence, once purchased, tends to be disposed of quickly.

So there was no more time for careful engine break-ins or proper magneto timing. There was a squall line building over the western ridges, purple-black and organized, the kind of weather that either grounds you for a week or gives you one ugly window, and there were nine of us — me, Mara, Tomas on a stretcher of lashed poles with his thigh wrapped in a field dressing that had soaked through twice, Ferro still feverish but lucid enough to insist he could walk, four witnesses from the villages who had chosen, God help them, to put their names on paper — and somewhere behind us, Roos's men were coming up the old survey road at the speed of money.

I did my final walk-around in the grey half-light, touching each thing I'd fixed the way you touch a wound to see if it's still yours. Fuel: full tanks, sump drained, no water. Oil: pressure would tell us within thirty seconds whether the three days meant anything. Controls: full and free, though the elevator cables ran over pulleys so corroded they sang when they moved, a thin whining note like something small being hurt.

Tomas lay across the cargo bay floor, propped against the ledgers' transit case, grey-faced but grinning at me through the cabin door.

"Casper. If this thing crashes—"

"It won't crash."

"—if it *does*, I want you to know the engine work was beautiful. The rest of it was a crime scene, but the engine work—"

"Shut up, Tomas."

"Yes, sir." He grinned wider. Fever and blood loss had made him looser than I'd ever seen him, a boy unbuttoned by nearly dying. "My grandfather rode rails worse than this. He'd have called it luxury."

That landed in my chest and stayed there. Everything about Tomas landed in my chest now. Somewhere between the adit and the trestle ambush, between finding his grandfather's name on a company ledger under the word *expended* and holding the crossing under fire while the rest of us lived, the kid had stopped being cargo I was responsible for and become the reason I was doing any of it.

Mara climbed into the right seat and buckled in with the efficiency of someone who had decided fear was a scheduling problem. She had the evidence case strapped across her lap — ledgers, photographs, Ferro's notebook, the whole buried century of it — and her hand rested on it the way other people rest a hand on a child's head.

"Runway," she said.

"Grass," I corrected. "The runway is theoretical. What we have is a corridor of shorter grass pointing vaguely west."

"I've charted it. Four hundred and ten meters usable, downslope, wind quartering from the southwest at—" she glanced at the bent grass along the strip, at the way the kunai leaned and shivered, "—ten knots, gusting. Building."

"Building fast."

"Building fast," she agreed. "Which means if we don't go now, we're here when Roos lands, and I've read enough of your closed-line maps to know how you feel about waiting."

I got in. The cockpit smelled of avgas and mildew and the particular sourness of my own three-day sweat. I primed the engine, cracked the throttle, and hit the starter.

The inertia starter whined up through its gears with a sound like a prayer being wound too tight. I let it spin, counted two, and meshed it.

Nothing. A cough. A chuff of blue smoke.

Behind us, far down the valley, a bird went up off the treeline in a panic — and then another, and then a whole ragged line of them, flushing west ahead of something moving east.

"Mara."

"I saw the birds." Her voice was flat and very calm, which is how I knew she was terrified. "Casper. Start the engine."

Second mesh. The engine caught on the third cylinder, stumbled, caught on all seven, and roared up to full static RPM with a vibration that rattled my teeth and shook loose a year of dust from every seam in the airframe. The oil pressure needle climbed — forty pounds, fifty-five, sixty — and steadied where it should. Three days of my life, holding.

I turned in my seat. Nine faces looked back at me down the cabin: Tomas white and grinning, Ferro fever-bright and gripping the stretcher ropes, the four villagers — old Manu, his daughter Sera, the two brothers whose names I'd learned on the portage and would carry the rest of my life — and every one of them watching me with the terrible, total trust that people give you when you're the only machine between them and the men coming through the trees.

I had spent eleven years making sure nobody trusted me with anything heavier than a theodolite. It's remarkable how quickly you can get used to it anyway.

"Everyone strap in!" I shouted over the engine. "When we stop rolling forward, we're flying. Nobody touches anything. Mara — call the wind."

"Quartering tail-left, gusting fifteen and building!"

Tailwind on takeoff, downhill runway, overloaded by roughly the weight of our survival, into a squall line. In my certification days I'd have written this up as a no-go and gone home to a drink and a clean bed. But home was a word I'd only recently learned to spell, and the alternative was Anselm Roos.

I pushed the throttle to the gate.

* * *

The Beaver accelerated like a barge leaving mud. Tailwind is a thief; it steals your airspeed while the ground speed lies to you, and for the first hundred meters we were doing neither one thing nor the other, the tail wheel light, the nose heavy, the grass whipping under us in silver waves. Fifty meters. A hundred. The engine was pulling — God bless its rotten, resurrected heart, it was *pulling* — but the airspeed needle sat stubbornly below rotate, trembling, apologetic.

Two hundred meters. The trees at the far end grew taller in the windshield with the patience of things that have waited decades to kill someone.

Then the wind shifted.

It came around behind us fully, the leading edge of the squall arriving early, shoving the tail — and the airspeed needle jumped ten knots in a heartbeat, and I hauled back on the yoke, and the Beaver left the earth in a mushy, wallowing, graceless surge that was nonetheless, by every definition I cared about, flight.

We cleared the treeline with perhaps four meters to spare. Sera made a sound in the cabin that was half sob and half laughter, and Ferro said something in Italian that I chose not to translate even internally.

And then the sky tried to kill us.

The squall line didn't look like weather from inside it. It looked like geography — a new country, vertical, built of boiling grey towers with green-black bases and rain falling out of them in solid curtains you could see individual miles away. The turbulence hit us before the rain did: a drop of two hundred feet that pressed my stomach into my spine, then a slam upward that unloaded the wings and left us hanging weightless for a second that lasted a year. Loose items in the cabin floated. Tomas swore with impressive creativity for a man with a hole in his leg.

"Rain!" Mara shouted, and then it was on us, hammering the airframe like gravel, visibility dropping to nothing, the whole world a grey roar. I flew on instruments I didn't entirely trust, in an aircraft I'd rebuilt with borrowed tools, through weather I'd have refused to taxi a Cessna in, and I flew it well, because there was nothing else in the universe to do.

Lightning strobed. In one flash I saw the altimeter — climbing, we were being sucked up into the cell — and in the next I wrestled the nose down and shoved in power and dove back toward the warmer dark beneath the storm's floor.

"We can't outrun it!" Mara yelled. "Where are we going?"

"Marienhof!" I yelled back. "Missionary strip! Northeast — ninety miles!"

"That's *through* it!"

"The edge is twenty miles south! We don't have twenty miles of fuel margin to go around!"

She looked at me — really looked, the way she looks at rivers, measuring, arguing, deciding — and then she nodded once and pulled the chart onto her knees. "Then give me a heading and pray your compass isn't lying."

"My compass is a liar," I said. "But it's our liar."

* * *

For forty minutes we flew inside the throat of the storm, and I will not pretend I remember it clearly. Memory doesn't record terror properly; it records fragments, the way a broken radio records static with voices in it. I remember the hail — small, then not small, drumming the wing surfaces hard enough that I watched the leading edges for damage I couldn't do anything about. I remember ice beginning to glaze the struts and my hand moving the mixture and carb heat with the mechanical calm of a man who has decided that panic is a luxury item. I remember Mara's voice calling headings off the chart, steady as a metronome, and once — only once — her hand finding my forearm in the dark of the cockpit and squeezing, hard, without a word, and letting go.

I remember thinking: *this is what it costs to stop running. This is the invoice.*

The lightning came closer. You can hear it in the headset first, a crackle riding the static, and then the flashes stopped being distant weather and started being local events, close enough to smell — ozone and hot metal and the wet-earth smell of a storm that means business. I was threading between cells by dead reckoning and luck, and luck, I knew, was a currency Roos also carried.

The first bullet came without warning, because bullets always do.

It hit the fuselage behind my headrest with a sound like a giant knuckle rapping a door, and for one stupid second I thought it was hail — until the second round came through the window frame beside Mara's shoulder and punched a neat hole in the windshield glass, crazing it white, and the third tore through the tail section with a bang I felt through the airframe's bones.

"Guns!" Mara screamed. "Casper, *guns!*"

I rolled the aircraft left and dove, blind, into the worst air I've ever been in, because a Beaver that flies straight and level is a target and a Beaver that flies like a madman is a rumor. Through the side window, glimpsed in the strobing light, I saw it: Roos's floatplane, black against the storm's lit underside, sliding out of a cloud bank a thousand feet above and behind us, a shape in the doorway with a long gun braced against the slipstream.

They'd found us. Of course they'd found us. A plane in this weather flies a predictable line between two known points, and Roos had known both points longer than we had. We weren't escaping the storm. We'd been herded into it.

Another burst. Rounds walked across the wing — I heard them, felt each impact as a shudder — and then a longer burst hammered the tail, and something in the back of the aircraft changed its mind about being attached.

The Beaver yawed hard right. The rudder pedals went soft under my boots, then dead. The whole tail began to buffet, shaking, and through the cabin I heard metal tearing, flapping — the elevator cables, or the fabric skin, or both, shredded, streaming.

"He's shot the tail cone apart!" Mara shouted. "Casper, the tail—"

"I know!" I hauled the trim wheel with both hands, fighting the yaw, feeding in opposite rudder that answered like a drunk answering a phone. The aircraft wanted to spiral. I wouldn't let it. We descended in a flat, skidding, screaming sideslip through the rain, the storm roaring, the damaged tail booming like a drum, and above and behind us the floatplane circled once — checking its work — and then, low on fuel or low on nerve or simply satisfied that nothing with a destroyed tail cone crosses ninety miles of mountain, it turned away into the cloud and was gone.

Gone. Not defeated. Gone to report, or refuel, or wait at Marienhof.

I leveled us out at eight hundred feet, below the worst of it, and took inventory with my hands and my ears. Elevator: partial, maybe half travel, connected by whatever strands of cable hadn't been cut. Rudder: mostly decorative. The aircraft flew like a truck with a broken steering box — controllable, barely, in smooth air, and this was not smooth air.

"How bad?" Mara asked. She'd seen my face. Pilots' faces are instruments too.

"We land at the first flat thing we see, or we land on the last flat thing we see. Those are the options."

"Marienhof?"

"If God is a missionary."

* * *

We came out of the squall's eastern edge twenty minutes later into a world washed silver and dripping, ridges steaming, valleys full of cloud like bowls of milk. My hands were shaking now that they were allowed to. Behind me, in the cabin, someone was praying quietly in Tok Pisin, and someone else — Ferro, I think — was laughing in weak, feverish gasps, and Tomas was talking to the villagers, translating, explaining, keeping them tethered to the moment. Keeping them alive in the ways that aren't mechanical.

Mara found the mission strip with the chart and one glimpse of a river bend, because of course she did. A gash of brown-green grass on a ridge saddle, marked by a white cross beside a tin-roofed church, the whole settlement no bigger than a handprint on the landscape.

"Four hundred meters if it's an inch," she said. "Sloped. Wet."

"And I have maybe half my elevator." I lined us up long, because a damaged tail wants extra speed to stay honest, and extra speed wants extra runway, and the runway had neither. "Tell everyone to brace. Tell Tomas—"

"I'll tell him myself." She turned in her seat. "Tomas! Hold on to something that loves you!"

His laugh came back through the din, cracked and bright. "That's you two! Hold on to each other!"

The strip rose to meet us. I flared with everything the ruined tail would give — which was a lurch, a stagger, a drop of the last five feet — and the main wheels hit hard, bounced once, slammed again, and we were rolling, rolling, the tail wheel dragging and skipping, the aircraft swaying as the rudder gave its useless opinions, grass and water spraying past the windows, the treeline at the far end growing with horrible patience.

Brakes. The brakes were, generously, suggestions. I stood on them and pumped them and stood again, and we slowed the way a landslide slows, gradually and then all at once, and stopped with the propeller nine meters from the first tree and the engine ticking over in the sudden enormous quiet like a heart slowing after a nightmare.

Nobody moved. Then Sera began to cry, and then Manu began to sing — low, rough, a few notes of some hymn or harvest song — and then everyone was talking at once, and Mara reached over and shut the mixture lever, killing the engine, killing the noise, and in the silence that followed she put her forehead against my shoulder and stayed there for exactly three seconds, which from Mara Ellingsen was a sonnet.

"Alive," she said.

"Narrowly, repeatedly, and loudly," I said. "But yes."

* * *

The missionaries were two Australian sisters who had run the station for thirty years and received emergency arrivals with the unhurried competence of women who had buried too many pilots to be impressed by one. They had penicillin, a real bed for Tomas, quinine for Ferro, and a shortwave radio behind the chapel that the younger sister, Sister Edith, guarded like a relic.

While the others were carried in and settled, I walked the length of the aircraft in the drizzle with a torch, counting damage. The tail cone was gone — torn open like a tin can, ribs exposed, fabric shredded to bare frame. Both elevators holed. Rudder control cables cut on the starboard side, hanging in frayed whiskers. Eleven holes in the wings and fuselage that I could find, probably more that I couldn't. The starboard fuel tank had taken a round and sealed itself, by some miracle of self-tapping lead, at three-quarters capacity.

I stood in the rain looking at it for a long time.

She could be repaired. Given tools, given fabric, given dope and time, she could fly again — I'd brought a dead plane back once already this month. But the parts weren't here. The nearest supply of aircraft cloth and cement was Wewak, or Mount Hagen, or anywhere that wasn't a ridge-top mission at the end of ninety miles of nothing, and Roos's floatplane knew exactly where we were, because we had led it here ourselves like children following breadcrumbs.

Mara came out and stood beside me under the wing, out of the drip line. Neither of us spoke for a while. The rain ticked on the fabric patches. Down the hill, lamplight showed in the mission house windows, warm and small and temporary.

"They'll come here," she said finally. Not a question.

"Sooner or later. They know the only strips within range. They'll check them all." I wiped rain off my face. "Which means we have what we always have. A little time, and no legal way home."

"There's the radio. Sister Edith's set. We could raise Wewak — raise anyone."

"And say what? 'Nine witnesses and a crate of evidence, please advise'? Every frequency in this district has ears on it, Mara. Roos bought the river police with a phone call. He'll have bought the wireless operators too, or he'll be listening himself." I looked at her. "There's one person I can call. One voice I trust. And if I raise her, I'm not invisible anymore. Not to her, not to whoever else hears the traffic, not ever again."

Mara was quiet a moment. Rain ran off the trailing edge between us like a curtain.

"You were going to have to stop being invisible eventually," she said. "You mailed the evidence, Casper. You signed your name to a tribunal package in a town where Okuk knows your face. Invisible was never on the manifest."

Inside the mission house, faintly, Tomas was laughing at something — really laughing, a boy with a bandaged leg laughing in a safe room — and the sound came out to us through the rain and did something to my chest that I decided not to examine until morning.

"Sister Edith says the set works best after dark," Mara said. "Conditions. She used to relay messages during the war." She touched my arm. "Tonight, then. Call your investigator. Tell her where we are. And Casper — tell her everything. All of it. Even the part you've never told anyone. Especially that part."

I looked at the wrecked tail, at the holes in the wings, at the strip too short and the sky too full of Roos, and I understood that she was right, and that being right was going to cost me the last thing I had left.

"After dark," I said.

The rain thickened. Somewhere down the valley, thunder muttered — or maybe it wasn't thunder. Maybe it was already the sound of the next thing coming for us, still far off, getting closer, patient as water.
