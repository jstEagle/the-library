## 30. The Missionary's Radio

The mission at Ambunti-side had a church with a roof that leaked in the shape of a question mark, a generator that ran two hours a day if Father Anselm—no relation to Roos, thank God, though I checked twice—felt charitable toward diesel, and a radio room that smelled of solder, mildew, and forty years of prayers sent out over shortwave.

It also had a chair. After nine days of kunai grass, leeches, and sleeping on a rail bed that had been abandoned for reasons I was beginning to understand all too well, a chair felt like civilization itself.

"You'll want the antenna up before the storm front," Father Anselm said, handing me a mug of tea that was mostly condensed milk and optimism. "The skip's good in the evening, but when the squall line comes through from the highlands, you'll get nothing but static and your own regrets."

"I'm familiar with both," I said.

He laughed. Missionaries in the Sepik learn to laugh at things that would make other men weep, or drink, or both. He was a big Dutchman gone soft at the edges, with forearms like dock rope and reading glasses pushed up into white hair. He'd been on this bend of the river since before independence, which meant he'd been here long enough to know what everyone else had agreed to forget.

That was why we'd come to him. That, and the fact that his radio was the only one within eighty kilometers that Kessler Development Group didn't own, lease, fund, or have photographed from three angles.

Mara was outside with Ferro, who was still feverish but lucid enough to complain about the heat, which I took as a good sign. Tomas lay on a cot in the vestry, his leg wrapped where the machete slash had opened it during the portage, sleeping the dreamless sleep of the young and the wrecked. Hargreave was in the plane seat he refused to leave, staring at nothing, composing and recomposing whatever confession he intended to make and unmake. The ledgers, the photographs, Ferro's notebook—all of it—sat in an ammunition box under my bunk, wrapped in oilcloth, weighing approximately as much as a cathedral.

I set down the tea and looked at the radio. A Yaesu transceiver, older than Tomas, patched with what appeared to be bicycle spokes and devotion. Beside it, a logbook of frequencies, call signs, and notes in handwriting that ranged from careful to desperate.

"Who do you need?" Father Anselm asked.

"A woman in Port Moresby," I said. "Investigator. Tribunal staff, back when the tribunal was young. She'll be harder to find now."

"Name?"

"Vashti Rokouk." I spelled it. "She may not use that name anymore. She may not want to hear mine."

Father Anselm looked at me over his glasses with the particular patience of a man who has heard a great many confessions and found most of them boring. "And what name would that be?"

"Casper Iversen."

He turned back to his logbook without reaction, which either meant the name meant nothing to him or that he'd made a professional discipline of meaning nothing. In the Sepik, both are survival skills.

* * *

The skip came up around seven, as promised, and by eight I'd worked through a relay chain that read like a history of everyone who'd ever passed through this country with something to say: a coconut cooperative, a fishing fleet, a medical patrol, a mission in Vanimo, a ham operator in Cairns who keyed in every night at the same time whether anyone was listening or not, which struck me as its own kind of faith.

Vashti Rokouk took some finding. She wasn't on the tribunal frequency anymore—the commission had been wound down, folded into a justice department office with a smaller budget and a longer title. But Father Anselm knew a lawyer in Wewak who owed him for a funeral, and the lawyer knew a clerk, and the clerk, after the third night of trying, came back with a number and a warning: *She doesn't take calls about old cases. She especially doesn't take calls about that case.*

*That case.* The trestle. The report I signed. The eleven names on the casualty list and the twelfth name nobody ever counted, because he was only the engineer who'd put his signature where they told him to put it.

On the fourth evening, with the storm front building purple over the highlands and the generator coughing its last hour into the wires, she answered.

Static first. Then a voice, low and level, with the particular flatness of someone who has learned not to sound interested until interest is safe.

"Go ahead, calling station."

I keyed the mic and then, for a moment, didn't speak. It's a strange thing, rehearsing a sentence for twenty years and finding you've forgotten it.

"This is a call I don't expect you to welcome," I said finally. "But I'm going to say my name anyway, because you taught me that the first thing evidence needs is a name attached to it."

Silence. Long enough that I checked the meter needle. Then:

"Say it."

"Casper Iversen."

Another silence, different in texture. When she spoke again, the flatness was gone, replaced by something colder and more precise. "The engineer. The trestle report."

"The same."

"You testified for my inquiry and then you recanted half of it under pressure from your employer's lawyers, and the half you kept cost you your license, and the half you gave back got buried so deep the commission couldn't reach it." No anger in it. Just inventory. She'd had years to file me, and her filing system was merciless. "Why are you on my frequency?"

"Because I'm holding the other half now," I said. "More than the other half. Company ledgers from a wartime mine in the Sepik headwaters. Execution photographs. Payroll records classifying dead laborers as *expended ballast*. Correspondence linking it to the consortium that became the Kessler Development Group. And witnesses—descendants of the men on those pages—who are willing to stand up in Port Moresby and say their grandfathers' names out loud."

The static breathed. Somewhere in Port Moresby, a woman who had spent her career losing fights like this one went very quiet.

"How much of it is authenticated?" she said at last.

"That depends on what you mean by authenticated."

"I mean exactly what I said, Mr. Iversen, and I'm going to say it once, so listen, because the storm's coming up my way too and I may lose you." Her voice took on the cadence of a lecture she'd given a hundred times to people who hadn't listened a hundred times. "Documents are not evidence. Documents are paper with opinions printed on them. Evidence is documents plus provenance plus custody plus corroboration, and if you're missing any leg of that table, Kessler's lawyers will walk in, tip it over, and be having drinks by four o'clock while your witnesses are being driven home in hired cars wondering what just happened."

"I know how it works," I said. "I was on the wrong end of it once."

"Then you know better than most." A pause. "Tell me about custody. Start from the tunnel. Who touched the material, in what order, and can each of them prove it?"

I could. I'd been keeping the list in my head since the adit, almost without deciding to—old habit, the surveyor's compulsion to record everything. "Ferro found the chamber first. His notebook documents the discovery, dated, in his hand—we have the notebook. Mara Ellingsen photographed the ledgers in situ, with location data embedded in the files, before anything was moved. Tomas Okane witnessed the opening of the chamber; his grandfather is named in the payroll ledger, which makes him both witness and next of kin. Hargreave signed the expedition manifest that authorized the dig—he'll give a sworn statement, and he has reason to tell the truth now, unless I miss my guess. And I've kept continuous physical possession of the originals since we left the station. One box, one custodian, no gaps."

"No gaps," she repeated. "You slept with it?"

"Under my bunk. Within arm's reach. Every night."

"Good. Because the moment there's a gap—a night you can't account for, a transfer you can't document—that's the seam they'll pull. Kessler doesn't fight evidence, Mr. Iversen. They *unmake* it. They find the weak link in the chain and they pull until the whole thing comes apart in your hands, and then they sue you for defamation on the way out." Papers rustled on her end. "You said photographs. Originals or copies?"

"Originals. Wet-printed, wartime stock, with company stamps on the reverse."

"And the ledgers themselves—condition?"

"Water-stained but legible. Rat damage on two volumes. The payroll ledger is intact."

Another pause, and when she spoke again something had shifted underneath the precision—not warmth, exactly, but the sound of a door that had been locked for twenty years testing its hinges. "There's a session convening in Port Moresby. Public inquiry into development-sector historical liabilities. It wasn't convened for you—it was convened for land disputes—but jurisdiction covers documentary evidence of corporate succession, and if what you're describing is real, it fits inside it like a bullet in a wound." She said it clinically, but I heard the pulse under it. "The problem is timing and the problem is transport, and they are the same problem."

"Glacier season opens the high pass in—" I did the arithmetic against Mara's chart, pinned above the radio desk. "Nine days. Maybe less if the calving runs early."

"Then you have nine days to move documents, witnesses, and a dying man across a hundred and forty kilometers of country that a very wealthy organization is actively searching, and deliver them into a hearing room where three judges will spend the first hour deciding whether to believe you exist." She let that sit. "I can do my part. I can get the material entered properly, get your witnesses called, get protection orders filed before Kessler's lawyers finish their coffee. What I cannot do is manufacture credibility. If the chain breaks anywhere between that river and my desk, everything you're carrying becomes a story told by a disgraced engineer with a grudge. And they will make sure the world hears it that way."

"I've heard it that way before," I said. "In my own voice, as it happens."

"Yes," she said. "You have."

The storm front hit the antenna then—a crackle, a surge, the needle jumping. I gripped the edge of the desk and waited it out, and when the signal steadied she was still there, which felt like its own small verdict.

"One more thing," she said. "This stays off the record until the material is in the room. No press, no leaks, no righteous speeches to village councils. The moment Kessler learns the evidence is moving toward a tribunal instead of toward destruction, they stop buying police and start buying worse. Do you understand me? Silence isn't cowardice here. Silence is the container. You break it early, you spill everything."

"I understand," I said. And I did—which was the problem, because there was someone on the other side of the mission wall who did not.

* * *

Mara was waiting on the veranda when I came out, arms crossed against the rising wind, watching the first fat drops hit the dust. Behind her, through the open door, Ferro slept, and beyond him the lamp over Hargreave's corner burned the way it burned every night now, late and stubborn.

"Well?" she said.

"She'll take it. Chain of custody intact, witnesses called, protection filed. We deliver the box and the people to Port Moresby and it goes on the record."

Something loosened in her shoulders—I watched it happen, the tension of nine days letting go half an inch—and then, because she was Mara, it came right back. "Conditions?"

"One. Total silence until the box is in the hearing room. No word to anyone downstream, no messages, no warnings to the families along the corridor. Not even the ones walking with us." I heard myself say it and hated the taste. "If Roos gets wind that this is headed for a courtroom instead of a furnace, he stops playing river police and starts playing something else. Vashti was clear. The silence is the container."

Mara turned and looked at me fully, rain starting to bead in her hair, and I braced for the argument, because we'd had this argument at the gauge station and at the sudd and halfway up the portage, and she had never once yielded the point that the truth belonged to the living as much as the dead.

But what she said was: "You already told them. Didn't you. The families on the rail corridor. You told them what we're carrying."

I didn't answer, which was an answer.

"Casper." She stepped closer, voice dropping under the rain. "The elders at Yamil walked their people onto that spur because we promised them their grandfathers' names would be spoken in a court of law. Not eventually. At the hearing. You think I can go back to those women and tell them to be silent *again*? That we need one more secret, one more season, one more promise deferred?" Her jaw tightened. "Their whole lives have been one more season. That's how it survives, Casper. That's how a massacre stays buried for sixty years—everyone agreeing to wait."

"And if we speak now, everyone agrees to die," I said. "Roos has money, guns, and a floatplane. We have a box, a bad leg, a fever, and a plane held together with bicycle spokes and prayer. Vashti's right. The container holds or we all spill."

"It's not your decision alone."

"No," I said. "It's yours too. That's rather the point of us."

We stood there in the rain, close enough that the argument had nowhere to go but through us, and I saw her doing the arithmetic she hated—weighing the living against the dead, the promise against the grave, and finding no column that came out clean. Nine days. One pass. One chance.

"The elders decide," she said finally. "Not you, not me, not Vashti's procedure. If the people whose dead these are choose to wait in silence for nine more days so the names can be spoken in a room with power in it—then we hold the container. But they choose, Casper. Out loud. Tomorrow. And if they say speak now, we speak now, and God help whoever stands in the way."

"Agreed," I said, and meant it, and understood even as I shook on it that whichever way the elders chose, someone we loved was going to pay for it.

Inside, the lamp over Hargreave's corner flickered as the generator died for the night, and in the sudden dark I heard his voice, thin and steady, speaking to no one—or to everyone:

"There's something you should both hear. Before the elders decide. About what I knew, and when."

* * *

He was sitting upright when we came in, hands folded on his knees like a schoolboy called before a board of inquiry, which in a sense he had been all his life and never once faced until now. The fever-light from the storm lantern moved on his face and made him look older than his years, which were already considerable.

"When I signed the contract with Kessler," he began, "the heritage-recovery brief, the one that brought us upriver—they gave me a background file. Standard practice. Due diligence." He smiled without humor. "I read it. All of it. There was a section on the consortium's wartime operations. Labor recruitment figures. Casualty figures. A footnote about a 'channel adjustment project' above the gauge station, 1943, costs exceeded, personnel losses attributed to disease." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "I knew the numbers didn't add up before we ever launched a boat. Eleven hundred recruited. Two hundred and six returned. I sat in a conference room in Singapore with a man from their legal department and I asked him where the other nine hundred went, and he looked at me with great kindness and said, *Dr. Hargreave, the consortium's archives are incomplete.*"

The rain filled the pause.

"And I signed anyway," he said. "Because the funding was unprecedented, and the science was real, and I told myself the rest was archaeology's problem, not mine. I told myself I'd excavate the site, publish the hydrology, and let others draw the moral conclusions." He looked up at us, and there was no performance in it anymore, no negotiation, no cleverness—just a tired man setting down something heavy. "I have known since Singapore. I have known every day on this river. And every day I chose the expedition over the dead, because choosing otherwise would have cost me the work, and the work was all I had."

Mara sat down slowly on the cot beside Ferro, who had woken at some point and was listening with his eyes open, saying nothing.

"So when you ask whether I'll testify," Hargreave said, "understand what you're getting. Not a repentant man. A complicit one, testifying against himself, with documentation of his own knowing. They will use it to discredit everything—the expedition, the evidence, the two of you, all of it. My statement is a weapon pointed in both directions." He drew a breath. "But it is also the only sworn testimony from inside their employ that the chain of custody has. Without me, your documents are found objects. With me, they are *known* objects—known to the successor company, known to its counsel, known to the man who signed the dig authorization. That's worth something. I intend to find out how much."

Nobody spoke. Outside, the storm worked the roof, and somewhere in the dark the river ran on over ground that remembered, patient as a ledger.

It was Ferro, hoarse and feverish, who broke the silence. "Then we'd best make sure you live long enough to sign it," he said. "Because if Roos learns what you just told us, Dr. Hargreave, you stop being an asset he can buy and become a liability he can't afford."

And as if the words had been a summons, Father Anselm appeared in the doorway with the storm dripping off him, holding a scrap of paper torn from his logbook, and his face was the face of a man who has heard a great many confessions and just received one he didn't want.

"Radio traffic tonight," he said quietly. "Relay from the coast. A charter floatplane out of Jayapura, filed flight plan for the Sepik headwaters. Passenger listed as a corporate consultant." He handed me the paper. "Name of Roos. Arrival window—six days."

Six days. Three days before the pass opened. Three days before we could move.

I looked at the paper, then at Mara, then at the ammunition box under my bunk, and felt the narrow door Vashti had described begin to swing shut on its hinge of nine days.

"He knows," I said. "Somehow, he already knows."
