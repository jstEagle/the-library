## 31. What Casper Knows About Falling

The missionary station at Ambunti had a generator that ran two hours a night, a radio that ran on prayer, and a pastor who ran on both. He'd given us the back room, the one with the map of the world on the wall and a pinhole of daylight through the tin roof that moved across the floor like a slow hand. I lay under that hand of light most of that first day, listening to Ferro's fever break in the next room, listening to the tribunal investigator's voice still ringing in my ears from the radio call.

*Chain of custody, Mr. Iversen. Or Kessler buries it again.*

I knew. That was the thing. I knew exactly how it would go, because I'd held the shovel once myself.

Mara came in at dusk and sat on the edge of the cot without asking, which by then was how we did things. She had ink on three fingers and the ledger photographs spread across her knees like a hand of cards she was losing.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"I'm resting my eyes."

"Your eyes are open."

"Then I'm resting the rest of me." I sat up. The room smelled of kerosene and mildew and, faintly, of the ledgers themselves — old paper and iron gall ink and something under it like a cellar that had been sealed a long time. "You want to know how a man like Kessler makes seventy years of bones disappear. Not the first time. The second time. The time that matters, now, with a tribunal coming."

She waited. She was good at waiting. It was one of the first things I'd noticed about her, back at the staging camp, watching her argue with a river chart like the river had personally insulted her. She didn't fill silence. She made it work for her.

"I signed a report once," I said.

* * *

It was eleven years ago and four thousand kilometers away, and it was raining there too, because it's always raining somewhere on the lines I've worked.

The Kowai Gorge trestle. Forty-one meters of steel and creosote over a river the color of strong tea. I was thirty-one, a structural engineer with a company stamp and a reputation for being the man who found problems, which is a reputation that gets you promoted right up until it gets you hated. I'd flagged the Kowai trestle twice. Fatigue cracking in the lower chord members, corrosion at the pins where nobody had ever repacked them because repacking cost money and the line carried freight, not passengers, and freight doesn't sue.

The third inspection was scheduled for the spring. The freight contract was worth more than the trestle.

I remember the morning of the collapse better than I remember most weddings. I was in the district office, and the call came in, and the room went the particular kind of quiet that rooms go when the number involved is going to be a number of people. A maintenance train. Four men. The trestle didn't fall all at once — that's what nobody understands about collapses. They're polite. They give you a groan, a shudder, a sag, half a second of the world telling you the truth. The driver had half a second. He put the locomotive into emergency and it wasn't enough, and the whole works went into the gorge with a sound I have heard every night since, in the space between waking and sleep, where the honest things live.

Four men. I knew two of them. One had shown me how to read a bridge's vibration through the soles of my boots, an old trackwalker named Eri, who could tell a cracked member by feel the way a doctor reads a pulse.

The inquiry came after. Inquiries always come after; that's their whole job. And the company — this was Meridian Rail Infrastructure, but the name hardly matters, the name was a coat any body could wear — the company needed the report to say one thing: unforeseeable. Act of God, act of fatigue, act of anything except the two inspection reports that sat in the district file with my signature at the bottom, warning, in engineer's language, that this structure was living on borrowed time.

The district manager was a man called Voss. Not a monster. That's what I want you to understand, because it took me years to understand it myself. Voss was a tired man with a mortgage and a daughter at university and a boss above him who had a boss above him, and somewhere up that chain was a man who had never seen the trestle and never would, who had signed a paper that said *defer maintenance, Q3* because Q3 was a bad quarter. Voss came to my hotel room with a bottle of something decent and sat down like a man visiting a sick friend.

"Casper," he said. "The company survives this or it doesn't. If it doesn't, four thousand people lose their jobs. If it does, we rebuild every trestle on the district — I've got that in writing, I made them put it in writing — and no family ever stands under one of our spans again wondering."

"And the report?"

"Will note that the fatigue indicators were ambiguous." He turned his glass. "Which, between us, they were. You wrote 'suggests' and 'indicates,' not 'will fail.' Any competent counsel will make a jury out of that."

"They were ambiguous because I was being careful. I was being careful because I was sure."

"I know." And he did know, and that was the worst of it. He wasn't lying to me. He was asking me to lie to everyone else, and he was asking it in the voice of a man who hated the asking, and the bottle sat between us like a bribe that had learned manners.

I signed it.

I want to be precise about what that means. I didn't forge anything. I didn't destroy anything. I wrote a report in which every individual sentence was technically defensible, and the architecture of those sentences — the thing I built them into, the thing that was mine, the thing no one could point to and call a lie — that architecture said *unforeseeable*, and four men stayed dead in a gorge, and the company survived, and they did rebuild every trestle on the district, and Voss retired two years later with a plaque, and I got a settlement and a quiet reference and the knowledge that my signature was the last thing standing between the truth and the daylight, and I had folded it like a map.

They called it a career-ending accident. It was. Just not the way they meant.

* * *

Mara hadn't moved while I told it. The pinhole of light had gone off the floor and up the wall and died somewhere near the map of the world.

"Eri," she said. "The trackwalker."

"His daughter got the settlement. The company called it an ex gratia payment. She called it what it was, to my face, at the inquiry, and the inquiry called her hysterical." I rubbed my eyes. "I sat there and said nothing. That's the part I've never managed to explain to myself. Not the signing. The silence after. The signing I can almost dress up as weakness. The silence was a choice I made every day for years."

"And now you think Kessler's people are making the same report about the Sepik."

"I know they are. Somewhere in Port Moresby there's a Voss with a bottle and a speech about jobs and heritage and how the ledgers are *ambiguous*. And somewhere above him is a man who's never seen the adit and never will, signing a paper that says *defer justice, Q3*." I looked at her. "Here's what I know about how it works. They won't deny the bones. Denial makes news. They'll *contextualize* them. Wartime conditions. Labor practices consistent with the era. Isolated incident, regrettable, under review. They'll fund a memorial — a tasteful one, no names, a shape of stone that means nothing and photographs well. They'll commission their own historians who will spend a decade producing four-hundred-page reports in which the massacre is a footnote to a footnote. And the ledgers — the ledgers will be *genuinely disputed*. Provenance questioned. Custody questioned. Did the surveyor handle them properly? Was the tunnel sealed between discovery and examination? Who had access? A tribunal doesn't need to find the evidence false, Mara. It only needs to find it *tainted*, and tainted is a much easier thing to manufacture, because taint doesn't have to be true. It just has to be argued."

"Then we get it authenticated. Now. Before it's argued about."

"With what? I'm a dead man's name on a warrant list. You're a cartographer with a grudge and a camera. The chain of custody starts with the first person who touches the evidence and ends with the tribunal table, and every link has to be a person with a name that means something. Ferro could be a link — he's a doctor of something, when he's not a doctor of fever. Hargreave could have been a link." I stopped, because Hargreave was dying in a room down the corridor, coughing the way men cough when the jungle has gotten into the architecture of them, and his sworn statement was the most valuable piece of paper on this river and the most perishable. "Hargreave could have been."

"He still can be."

"He's running out of hours to be anything."

Mara gathered the photographs and squared their edges, over and over, the way she did when she was thinking with her hands. "You signed their report once," she said. "So you know the grammar."

"I know the grammar."

"Then write the counter-report. Your survey records — the ones from the closed lines. You told me you kept them. The spurs, the gauge stations, the freight schedules for the mine. You've been carrying the company's own skeleton in your map case for eleven years."

I had. Of course I had. A man throws away a lot of things after a disgrace — the plaque photographs, the references, the phone numbers of men who used to return his calls — but he keeps the thing that ruined him, because the thing that ruined him is the last proof he was ever any good. My survey books from the basin. The closed lines, drawn in my own hand, dated, stamped, cross-referenced to the colonial mining consortium's freight manifests, which I'd copied out of an archive in Lae one winter out of pure, stupid professional curiosity: *ore tonnage out, labor tonnage in*, and the numbers had never once matched, and I had noticed, and I had done what I always did with things I noticed.

I had put them in a drawer.

"Chain of custody," I said slowly, "starts with provenance. My surveys are provenance. Dated, signed, contemporaneous. They place the spur at the adit, they place the adit on consortium land, they place the freight schedules on consortium paper. They're the frame that makes the ledgers a picture instead of a pile of old paper."

"And you'd have to sign them. In public. Under your own name." She said it gently, but she said it, because she was Mara, and she had never once in my hearing let me off a hook I'd baited myself. "The name you've spent eleven years not being."

The generator coughed and died somewhere down the hall, and the station went to its nighttime dark, one lamp in the pastor's study and the river sound coming up through the floor, big and patient, the sound of something that had been moved once by men who counted bodies as ballast and had never once forgotten where it used to run.

"The investigator," I said. "On the radio. She said evidence needs a chain or Kessler buries it again. What she didn't say — what she couldn't say, on an open frequency — is that the chain needs a first link. Somebody whose name is already on the consortium's paper, whose survey already sits in an archive, whose signature predates all of this. Somebody they can't discredit without discrediting their own records."

"You."

"Somebody they ruined," I said, "which is the one credential they gave me for free. A disgraced engineer has no price left to protect. Roos figured that out on the river — it's why he offered me money first and a clean name second. A clean name. To a man whose name is the whole problem." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way my laugh had gone for eleven years, a sound with a hole in it. "He was offering me back the thing I traded away. Four men in a gorge for a signature on a report. That was the exchange rate. And I've been living on the proceeds ever since."

Mara put her hand over mine on the cot's edge. Her fingers were still cold from the evening river, and I held on like a man holding a rail in wind.

"You understand," she said, "that once you sign, there's no drawer. No borrowed names. Okuk can't sell you, Roos can't buy you, and no tribunal on earth will ever let you be invisible again."

"I know."

"You've spent your whole life being no one, Casper. It's kept you alive. It's the only thing that's kept you alive."

"It's also the thing that let four men stay in a gorge," I said, "and seventy more stay in a hill, and it will keep letting it happen as long as I'm any good at it. Voss wasn't a monster. I'm not a monster. That's the whole machinery of it, Mara — it doesn't run on monsters, it runs on tired, decent men signing things in hotel rooms, one at a time, each one sure the paper doesn't really matter, each one sure the bones are somebody else's department." I turned my hand over and held hers properly. "Roos isn't a stranger to me. That's what I've been trying to say all night. He's the man I would have become if the company had kept me. He's the Voss with a better tailor. And the only thing I know that he doesn't — the only thing I've got that he can't buy, can't context, can't taint — is that I've already fallen once. I know the sound it makes. I know what the half-second feels like, the groan and the shudder and the sag. And I am telling you, as a man who has inspected a great many failing structures, including the one I used to be—"

I stopped. Down the corridor, a door opened, and the pastor's footsteps came quick and uneven, and then his voice, low and urgent, calling my name — not the borrowed name, the real one, because Ferro was delirious and talking, and what Ferro was saying was a date, and a name, and the word *manifest*, and the pastor thought I should hear it now.

Mara and I looked at each other in the dark.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we start building the chain."

And down the hall, Ferro was calling for a pen.
