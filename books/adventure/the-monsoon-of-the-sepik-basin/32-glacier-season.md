## 32. Glacier Season

The radio at the mission station was older than the country's independence and twice as temperamental. Father Anselm — no relation to Roos, thank God, though I made the joke once and Mara didn't laugh, so I retired it — kept it running with baling wire, prayer, and a battery bank he charged off a water wheel that sounded like a man gargling gravel.

It was through this contraption that we learned the mountains had opened a door.

I want to be precise about what "word comes" means in a place like this, because in Wewak it would have meant a phone call, maybe an email, something with a timestamp and a sender you could sue later. Here it meant: the priest's cousin's son walked two days down from a patrol post with a message written on the back of a school exercise book page, folded into eight, and carried against his skin so the rain wouldn't take it. The paper was soft as cloth by the time it arrived. The handwriting had survived anyway, which is more than I can say for most official correspondence I've dealt with.

Mara read it first. She read it standing at the window of the mission's back room, where Ferro slept off his fever under a mosquito net that hung like a bridal veil over a man who looked like he'd been dug up rather than rescued. She read it twice. Then she held it out to me without turning around, which was its own kind of message.

"Nine days," she said.

I took the page. The patrol officer up at the high country wrote in the careful hand of a man educated by missionaries and employed by a government that had stopped paying him on time sometime last year. *Glacier calving early this season. Pass route open approximately nine days from receipt. After that, ice bridge unstable until next year. Advise movement of any persons or cargo requiring transit.*

"Advise movement," I said. "That's one way to put it."

"It's the only way," Mara said. "There isn't another route out. Not with witnesses. Not with this much weight." She tapped the oilskin packet on the table — Ferro's notebook, the photographs Tomas had copied, the ledger pages we'd photographed twice and hidden three times, because redundancy is the only religion I've ever practiced faithfully. "Roos controls the river. He controls the checkpoints. He might as well control the weather, the way money works here. But he doesn't control the glacier."

"No," I said. "The glacier answers to nobody. It's the last honest institution left."

She almost smiled. Then she didn't, because we both did the same arithmetic at the same moment, and neither of us liked the sum.

If the pass opened for nine days, then Roos knew it too. He'd bought the river police with a phone call; buying a weather report would be beneath his dignity. Which meant somewhere downstream or upcoast, a man who wore good boots in places where good boots were a liability was already planning how to spend those same nine days. And he only needed to stop us once. We needed to succeed every single day for nine of them.

That's the thing about a clock. It doesn't have to beat you. It just has to run while you're standing still.

* * *

We called a meeting that evening in the mission's common room, which doubled as a classroom, a clinic, and — judging by the chalk marks still ghosting the blackboard — a venue for at least one memorable dispute about fractions. The benches filled up slowly. Hargreave came leaning on Tomas's arm, moving like a man whose body had finally filed the paperwork on everything he'd done to it. Ferro stayed abed but insisted on being briefed, so we briefed him loudly, which felt right; the man had spent weeks hiding upstream eating grubs, and he'd earned the right to hear plans shouted.

The villagers came too. That was the part that still caught in my throat when I let myself notice it. Old Yawan, who'd first told us about the adit with a silence so heavy it qualified as testimony. His daughter-in-law Ruth, who ran the trade store and remembered every debt in the valley to the toea. A dozen others who had no formal reason to trust a disgraced engineer, a Norwegian cartographer, and a hydrologist with a broken conscience — and who had decided to trust us anyway, because the bones in the mountain belonged to their grandfathers, and bones don't file paperwork either.

I laid out the map. My map — the closed lines, the old survey road, the spur that climbed toward the pass through country so steep the colonial engineers had abandoned it rather than finish the cuts. Forty years of jungle had grown over it, but rail beds are stubborn things. Gradients don't rot. Culverts silt but hold. A road that was built to carry locomotives can carry men, if the men are willing to suffer, and I've never met anyone willing to suffer more than people walking toward justice.

"Nine days to the pass," I said. "It's six days' walk to the ice line if nothing goes wrong. That leaves us three days of margin, total, for everything that will go wrong." I looked around the room. "Something will go wrong."

"You're very inspiring," Mara murmured.

"I'm honest. Inspiration is for funerals."

Hargreave raised a hand like a schoolboy, then seemed embarrassed by it. "The ice bridge," he said. "How reliable is the patrol officer's estimate? Glacial timing varies. If we arrive and the crossing hasn't opened—"

"Then we wait at altitude with no shelter and food for exactly as long as our food lasts," I said. "Which is why we leave with extra. Which is why we leave heavy. Which is why we need porters, and pack straps, and every able body in this valley who'll come."

"And if Roos reaches the pass before us?" Ruth asked. Direct woman. I liked her immediately and feared her slightly, which is my favorite combination in a person.

"Then he holds the far side, and we've walked six days into a trap with our families along," I said. Nobody thanked me for saying it out loud, but they all nodded, because everyone in that room had already thought it and pretending otherwise would have been an insult.

Old Yawan spoke from the back. He rarely spoke, and when he did, rooms adjusted themselves around his voice the way water adjusts around a stone. "The mountain opened for the river once," he said. "Men moved it. Now the ice moves for us. My father said the ground keeps accounts." He looked at me. "You keep accounts too, I think."

"I used to keep them badly," I said.

"Then keep them well now."

There was no vote after that. There didn't need to be. In places where government arrives as a rumor and law arrives as a price list, decisions get made the old way: by looking at each other until everyone knows.

* * *

The next morning I stood on the mission porch with a cup of terrible coffee and watched Mara teach Ruth's boys to read contour lines off her anomaly map, using a termite mound as a demonstration hill. She had a way of teaching that involved no condescension whatsoever, which is rarer than you'd think among people with doctorates. She'd point at the mound and say, "This is a ridge. Where would water go?" and the boys would argue, and she'd argue back, and eventually the ground itself would settle the dispute when the afternoon rain came and they all watched where it ran.

I'd fallen for her somewhere between the braided channels and the sudd, and I'd been pretending otherwise with the discipline of a man who'd once signed a report he knew was false. But pretense gets expensive, and I was nearly broke.

"Nine days," she said, coming up beside me without looking away from the boys. "You've said it eleven times since yesterday. You count things when you're scared."

"I count things when I'm thinking. Fear is just thinking with worse posture."

"What happens at the pass, Casper? Honestly. Not the version you gave the room."

I drank the coffee. It deserved better than the truth and got it anyway. "Honestly? Roos has a floatplane, satellite phones, and mercenaries who shoot straight. We have feet, a rail bed, and a glacier that may or may not cooperate. If he decides the pass matters enough, he puts ten men at the ice bridge and we're finished. Our only advantage is that he thinks like a buyer. Buyers minimize cost. Walking six days uphill through leech country costs. He'll try to intercept us low, near the trestle, where his boats and radios work. So we go high, fast, and ugly, and we pray his accountants win the argument in his head."

"And if they don't?"

"Then I collapse a span on him and we find out what kind of man I am when the math is mine."

She turned and looked at me then, and there was something in her face that wasn't fear and wasn't admiration and was somehow both wearing each other's clothes. "You've been waiting your whole life to do the math right," she said. "Haven't you."

"Trestles don't forgive," I said. "Neither do I. Not anymore."

* * *

Day two, we prepared. Preparation in the Sepik means accepting that half of what you prepare will fail, and preparing it anyway, because the alternative is arriving at the pass with nothing and calling that a plan.

Tomas and I stripped the mission's store room and the gauge station salvage for anything that could carry weight or mend a foot. We cut pack frames from green hardwood, lashed with bark cord, and Tomas — who'd spent the whole expedition befriending me over engine repairs, bless him, as if friendship were a maintenance schedule — worked beside me in a silence that had changed quality since the night he found his grandfather listed as ballast. Grief had burned off the boyishness and left something harder and kinder underneath. He checked every lash knot twice. He'd started checking everything twice.

"For the ledgers," he said at one point, not looking up. "How many copies?"

"Three sets. One travels with the witnesses. One with Mara, separate group, separate pace. One stays here with Father Anselm, buried under the chapel floor, addressed and stamped, to be mailed if we don't come back." I tightened a lashing. "Roos can't burn what he can't find, and he can't find all of it, because even I won't know where all of it is until we're moving."

"That's paranoid."

"That's engineering. Redundancy is just paranoia with load calculations."

He grinned — briefly, the old grin surfacing like a crocodile's eyes — and went back to work.

Ferro, propped up and furious about it, contributed the most valuable thing anyone gave us that week: memory. The man had spent weeks alone in the bush above the station, and he'd mapped the high country from ridgelines while feverish, out of the particular madness of scientists who cannot stop observing even while dying. He described a col below the ice line where the old survey road crested, a sheltered bench with water, big enough to stage twenty people for a day. "If the bridge isn't open when you arrive," he rasped, "you stage there. Don't bunch up on the ice. Ice punishes crowds."

"You mapped it while hallucinating," Mara said.

"I mapped it while *motivated*. Hallucination was incidental."

* * *

On the third evening, the radio spoke, and the news was bad in the specific flavor I'd been expecting.

Father Anselm summoned me with the particular gravity of a man who hates being the messenger. The set crackled through a relay — a coastal contact of his, a trader with a shortwave and no loyalties beyond the tide. The trader reported traffic: a charter floatplane had landed at Angoram twice in two days. Men with new rifles and satellite phones were paying cash for information about a party of foreigners moving overland. And — this was the detail that made my hands go cold around the coffee mug, which by then was my third — someone had asked specifically about the old survey road. About *my* closed lines.

They knew. Not everything, maybe. But they knew the shape of our escape, which meant someone had talked, or sold, or both. Okuk's name sat in my mouth like a stone. I hadn't seen him since the double-cross at the trading post, and I'd told myself the fake ledger case had settled our account. But Okuk was a man who treated betrayal as a subscription service, not a transaction.

"They'll move on the trestle," I told the room that night. "The viaduct across the gorge — it's the one place the old road can't be bypassed. If I were Roos, I'd put men there by day five and wait for us to walk into the open span."

"So we go around?" Hargreave asked.

"There is no around. That's why the engineers built the viaduct. The gorge is two hundred meters of vertical argument." I spread the map again, and everyone leaned in, and I felt the strange weight of it — these people, these witnesses, these children and grandmothers, all of their futures resting on lines I'd drawn from a dead company's survey notes. "So we don't avoid the trestle. We control it. We get there first, we watch the approaches, and when Roos's men come, they find the span already dropped behind us and nothing ahead but jungle and a head start."

"You're going to blow a bridge," Ruth said. Not a question. An audit.

"I'm going to drop one span of a bridge that's been dead for forty years, using charges I know how to place because placing them wrong is how I ruined my life." I met her eyes. "Yes. I'm going to blow a bridge."

Nobody objected. That frightened me more than objections would have. When good people stop arguing with a plan, it's usually because they've run out of better ones — and I could see it in every face in that room that we had all arrived, together, at the bottom of the barrel of options, and found it solid enough to stand on.

* * *

The night before we marched, I couldn't sleep, so I went out to the porch and found Mara already there, sitting with her knees drawn up, watching lightning walk along the horizon without any rain attached. Dry storms. The mountains clearing their throat.

"Say it," she said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on for three days. You think I haven't noticed? You narrate your fears in numbers, Casper. Nine days. Six days' march. Three days' margin. Two hundred meters of gorge. You haven't said the number that actually scares you."

I sat down beside her. The boards were warm from the day and smelled of decades of sermons.

"One," I said. "One mistake. That's the number. Everything else has margin. One mistake doesn't. A misread channel, a bad knot, a charge placed a meter off — and the witnesses pay for it. Not me. Me, I've been paying my whole life; I'm current on that account. Them—" I stopped, because the sentence had gotten heavier than I'd planned. "They stayed silent for forty years because staying silent was survivable. They're walking now because we asked them to believe it isn't anymore. If I'm wrong about that—"

"You're not wrong."

"You don't know that."

"No," she agreed. "But I know the alternative. Silence kept them alive and kept the dead buried. That's not safety, Casper. That's just a slower massacre." She rested her head briefly against my shoulder, the way you'd lean on a wall you trusted to hold. "Nine days. Let's spend them well."

Lightning walked the horizon again, closer this time, and this time the thunder followed like a bill collector.

* * *

We marched at first light on the fourth day — twenty-three witnesses, four of us from the expedition, Hargreave insisting on coming despite legs that shook on the mission steps, Ferro staying behind with Father Anselm because a man who'd eaten grubs for a month had earned the right to guard a buried archive instead of a mountain trail.

The old rail bed took us north out of the valley, climbing through kunai grass that slashed at bare arms like it held a grudge, into country where the jungle closed overhead and the light went green and submarine. The culverts held. The gradients held. Forty years of abandonment, and the dead engineers' work still carried us — which felt less like irony than like instruction.

Behind us, far downstream, I imagined I could hear the river running full over ground that remembered. Ahead, past the ridgelines, the glaciers waited with their own calendar, indifferent to ours, opening their brief and terrible door.

And somewhere between us and the ice — at a ruined viaduct I knew better than I knew my own signature — men with new rifles were already counting the same nine days, backward.

The clock had become a character in this story. I only hoped that when the hour struck, it struck for us.
