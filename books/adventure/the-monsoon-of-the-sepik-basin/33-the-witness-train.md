## 33. The Witness Train

The trouble with asking people to do an impossible thing, I've learned, is that they'll say yes before you've finished explaining why it can't be done.

I stood in the longhouse at Yamil village on a morning so humid the air felt like wet wool, looking at perhaps forty faces — old men with knees like knotted vine, young women with babies slung in bilum bags, a scattering of teenagers who watched me with the flat, patient attention of people who had been lied to by outsiders their whole lives and had learned to wait for the lie to finish before deciding anything.

I had come to ask them to walk.

Not walk to the garden, not walk to the river. Walk ninety kilometres along a dead railway through jungle and kunai and leech country, carrying their grandparents' names and their own bodies as evidence, into the teeth of a man who had already burned a research station and bought a police force with a phone call, all so a tribunal in Port Moresby could hear them say out loud what their families had kept folded away for eighty years.

Dr. Ferro sat behind me on a bench, still thin from his fever but upright, his notebook open on his knees like a Bible at a funeral. Mara stood at the edge of the lamplight with her arms crossed, letting me talk. Hargreave was not there. Hargreave was dying slowly in a back room, coughing up the jungle, and had asked me to say his piece for him, which was the first honest work he'd done in years.

I told them about the ledgers. I told them about the photographs — the ones I wished I could unsee, rows of men in company numbers, and the column in the ledger beside each photograph that said, in a clerk's tidy hand, *expended*, as if they were fuse wire. I told them about the adit behind the gauge station, the rusted rails, the collapse that had been no collapse at all but a door closed on purpose, with men behind it.

And then I told them the part I had not wanted to say, because it was mine.

"Eighteen years ago I signed a report about a bridge," I said. "A trestle on a line in another country. I knew the timbers were rotten. My company knew. The report I signed said the bridge was sound, because the man above me asked me to sign it, and I was twenty-six and wanted to keep my job. Eleven men went into the river when it fell." I looked at my hands, which had not gotten any cleaner with age. "I have spent eighteen years being a man with no address, moving before anyone could ask me questions I already knew the answers to. So when I tell you what the Kessler company does — how it buys men, how it buries things, how it makes the paperwork say whatever keeps the money moving — I'm not guessing. I used to be the paperwork."

Silence. A baby fussed and was quieted. Outside, the Sepik ran brown and patient, the way it had run for ten thousand years, over ground that remembered everything and told nothing.

An old man in the front row — I knew him by then as Kambui, the luluai's grandson, keeper of the village's grudges the way other men keep seed yams — leaned forward on his walking stick.

"The railway," he said. "You say it is your map. Your closed lines."

"It is."

"My grandfather helped cut that bed. Before the war. He carried rails on his shoulder where no machine could go. The company paid him in rice and took the rice back at the company store." He said it without heat, the way you'd recite a genealogy. "When the massacre came, after — the men who ran did not run along the river. The river had eyes. They ran along the railway bed, at night, because the company's own guards were told to watch the water. The bed was the one thing the company never thought anyone would use against them. Why would they? It went nowhere. It was dead."

"It's not dead," I said. "It's sleeping. And I know how to wake it."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about closed railway lines: they don't disappear. The jungle takes the rails, yes — pulls them crooked, buries them in vine — but the bed itself endures, because a bed is only a decision made permanent. Somebody decided where the ground would be flat and straight and drained, and the ground, once argued with by enough labour, mostly agrees to stay argued with. Culverts still run. Cuttings still cut. The formation holds its line through country that swallows roads in a single wet season, because eighty years ago men died making it hold.

I had spent three nights with my map spread on the longhouse floor and Mara's charts beside it, cross-hatching the old survey notes against what the river had done since, and the route was there, faint as a vein under skin: from Yamil, northeast along the formation through the Sera cut, over the saddle at kilometre forty-one where the viaduct still stood on its concrete legs, down the old incline to the depot clearing at the disused airfield strip. Ninety kilometres. Six days at the pace of the slowest walker, and the slowest walker was eighty-one years old and intended to come.

"The window," Mara said the last night, tapping the glacier report Ferro had decoded from the mission radio traffic. "Calving season opens the high pass in nine days. After that, the route closes and Roos has all the time in the world to find us. We move the witnesses and the ledgers together or we've risked everyone for nothing."

"Roos knows the window too," I said. "Which means his mercenaries know it. Which means the river police will be watching every boat and every track between here and the pass."

"Which is why," Mara said, and there was the thing in her face I had come to live for, that stubborn lit certainty, "we don't use the river, and we don't use the tracks. We use *your* tracks. The ones only you believe exist."

I wanted to tell her I loved her then. There's a time for that, and a bamboo floor at midnight with a war coming was not it, so instead I said, "The bed's overgrown in the Sera cut. We'll need forty men with bush knives ahead of the column."

"You'll need forty men with bush knives," she corrected, "and a woman with a compass who doesn't trust your map."

"Especially a woman with a compass."

* * *

The village answered the next morning, and the answer was not a speech.

Kambui stood first. Then the old women, one after another, in no particular hurry, the way they did everything. Then a young man named Wari — Tomas had been teaching him engine repair before the station burned, and the two of them had a friendship conducted mostly in gestures and laughter — who said, "My great-grandfather is in the ledger. Number forty-one. I have seen the photograph." He said it the way you'd claim luggage. "I will walk."

By noon there were sixty-one of them, and by evening seventy-four, and people had begun arriving from the downriver settlements in dugouts, having heard the news travel the way news travels out here, faster than radio and more accurately. The community that had kept its silence for eighty years — silence bought with rice and terror and the very reasonable knowledge of what happened to villages that talked — had looked at the ledger photographs Ferro laid out on a tarpaulin, found their own faces in the faces of the numbered men, and decided, collectively, without a single vote being called, that the silence had been the company's all along, and they were done renting it.

I have seen courage that looks like Tomas holding a crossing under fire. I have seen the other kind, quieter, which is a grandmother in her seventies sewing extra pockets into a bilum bag so she can carry her granddaughter and a copy of a ledger page, and telling you, when you ask if she's sure, that she has been sure since 1944, and that being sure for eighty years is not patience, it is a debt, and debts want collecting.

* * *

We left at first light on the fourth morning, and I will tell you what a witness train looks like, because there is no other phrase for it and I have stopped being embarrassed by the railway in my head.

It looked like this: seventy-four villagers and nine of us expedition survivors strung out along a dead rail bed in single file, a kilometre and a half long, moving through morning mist over a formation of crushed stone that no foot had troubled since the war. Ahead of the column, Wari's bush knife team — thirty men I'd organized into three relays — cut the Sera open, and the sound of their work carried back through the trees like slow applause. Behind them the old people walked on the ballast line itself, because the ballast drains and the jungle floor doesn't, and the grandmothers knew a thing about staying dry that no surveyor ever taught me. Mara walked the line with her compass and her charts, calling corrections — "bed bears left here, follow the cut, not the ridge" — and I walked up and down the column like a stationmaster with no station, counting heads, checking water, listening to the country.

Because the country was listening back. I could feel it. Roos's men were out there somewhere — the river police bought and blind, the mercenaries not — and a column a kilometre and a half long makes a sound like a village breathing, and villages breathing are findable.

So we moved the way the old men remembered their grandfathers moving: no fires after dawn, cooking done cold, no voice above a murmur between nine and four, the column dissolving into the treeline whenever Mara's forward scouts — she'd recruited two teenage boys with eyes like rangefinders — signalled movement on the flanks. Twice we went flat in the kunai for an hour while something with an engine worked along the river valley below us, and both times the old people lay down in the wet grass without being told, without complaint, with the practiced stillness of people for whom hiding had once been the whole of survival, and I understood that I had not taught these people anything. I had only reminded them of what they already knew, and they had known it longer and better.

On the second day, at a cutting where the formation ran through solid rock, Kambui stopped the column and showed me something.

The cutting walls were carved. Not decorated — carved, in the way of men who had been told to dig and had decided the digging would say something whether the company liked it or not. Names, in Tok Pisin and village scripts. Dates. Small crude figures of men carrying rails on their shoulders, marching in a line into the rock itself. And in one place, low, where the moss had half-taken it, a list — eleven names, I counted them twice — and beneath the list, a single word in a hand that had pressed hard enough to outlast the moss:

*lukim mi* — see me.

"They cut it at night," Kambui said. "The company foreman beat a man for it, once. So they cut the next ones deeper." He put his palm flat against the stone, over the eleven names, and left it there a moment, the way you'd rest a hand on a shoulder. "The company thought the railway was theirs. It was never theirs. It was a letter, written slow, waiting for somebody to come and read it."

I am a man who has spent his life reading grades and gauges and load tables, and I stood in that cutting and read a letter eighty years in the delivery, and my eyes were not dry, and I did not care who saw.

"We're the postmen," I said, which is not what a novelist would say, but I have never been a novelist.

* * *

The third night it rained, a proper monsoon downpour that turned the cuttings into creeks, and we sheltered in the ruins of a wayside hut — company-built, walls of rotting planks stamped with the old consortium's mark, the same mark that was on the ledgers, the same mark that was on Roos's floatplane. Ferro, wrapped in a tarp and still coughing, read the ledger pages aloud by torchlight while the descendants of the numbered men listened, and I watched an old woman named Yawa hear her father's name — number sixty-three, *expended, cause: dysentery, remains: not recovered* — and watched her nod once, slowly, like a woman confirming an account she had audited her whole life without the paperwork.

"They wrote it down," she said to me, in the tone of a person closing a very long transaction. "All these years, we were told no one wrote it down. That it was only in our heads, and heads are not proof." She tapped the ledger page with one finger. "Now it is proof. Now their own paper says what we always said. You cannot argue with a company's own handwriting. That is the one thing they taught us."

Mara found me afterward at the hut's broken doorway, watching the rain hammer the old ballast.

"Tomorrow we cross the saddle," she said. "The viaduct."

"I know."

"You've been quiet since the cutting."

"I've been quiet since 1987," I said. "It's a long habit. It's breaking."

She leaned her shoulder against mine, there in the doorway, rain roaring past us like a river standing on end. "Do you know what I keep thinking? Everyone always asks who the hero of a story is — the man with the map, the woman with the compass, whoever. But look at them." She nodded back into the hut, at the torchlit huddle of old people and children sleeping in a dead company building, holding their dead company's paper. "The hero is seventy-four people who decided, all at once, after eighty years, that walking is a form of speech."

"Then I'm just the timetable," I said.

"You're the man who found the line," she said. "Somebody had to believe the closed lines went somewhere."

* * *

We crossed the saddle at kilometre forty-one on the fifth morning, under a sky the colour of gunmetal, and the viaduct came up out of the mist the way it always does in my memory of that morning: suddenly, and too solid to be true. Nine concrete paces marching down a gorge that the jungle had spent eighty years trying to close, the spans still standing, the rails still pinned across them though the timber deck had long since rotted to lace. The column stopped at the near abutment, and for a moment nobody spoke, and then Kambui began to sing — low, wordless, a working song, the kind of song men sing when they are carrying something heavier than they are — and one by one the others took it up, seventy-four voices on a dead bridge over a live gorge, and the sound went out into the mist like something being sent.

I stood at the head of the crossing with my map in my hand and did the arithmetic I had been refusing to do for two days. The viaduct was the only crossing of the gorge for thirty kilometres. It was also, I had to admit, looking at its legs, the one place on the whole line where a small number of determined men could stop a large number of walking ones. Or where a column could stop pursuit — if it was willing to burn the bridge behind it, and the bridge was the last thing left of the men whose names were carved in the cutting, and burning it would feel like burning the letter itself.

Roos would know that too. Roos had the same map access I did, the same survey archives, and a helicopter.

The song finished. The column began to cross, old people first, hand over hand along the rail, testing every tie. Wari came and stood beside me at the abutment, looking down the line ahead, into the mist on the far side.

"Casper," he said quietly. "The scouts came back. There's smoke down the valley. At the depot clearing." He paused. "Somebody got there first."

I looked at the mist, at the far end of the bridge where my ninety kilometres of closed line went on toward an airfield that might already be an ambush, and I heard, very faint under the gorge wind, the first far-off thrum of rotor blades.
