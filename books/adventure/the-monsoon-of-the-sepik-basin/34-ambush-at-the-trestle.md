## 34. Ambush at the Trestle

There is a particular sound a man makes when he decides to stop running and turn around. It isn't a shout. It's quieter than that — a kind of settling, like a house changing its mind about a foundation. I heard it in my own chest somewhere past the second ridge of the old survey road, and I didn't like the acoustics one bit.

We had been walking the rail bed for two days. The closed lines of my map had become a corridor of witnesses: forty-one villagers descended from men listed as *expended* in ledgers that were wrapped in oilcloth against my back, moving single file along ballast that hadn't felt a boot's weight since before the war. Old women who remembered grandmothers' stories. Boys carrying their fathers' machetes. A schoolteacher named Yawane who had appointed herself marshal of the column by the simple expedient of being right about everything.

Mara walked beside me with her charts in a waterproof tube slung across her chest like a bandolier. She'd stopped arguing with me somewhere around the leeches, which I took as either love or exhaustion, and I'd decided not to ask which.

"The viaduct," she said, nodding ahead to where the trees broke apart.

I'd been dreading it since dawn. The map called it Bridge 7 — Kilometer Post 118, span of ninety meters over a gorge the Sepik's ancestor river had cut and then abandoned, leaving a green slot of shadow and the sound of water far below. Three steel spans on concrete piers, two of them still standing out of what I could only assume was spite. The middle span had come down decades ago — you could see the twisted rails hanging into the gorge like the ribs of something that had died mid-stride.

"We go around," I said. "There's a game trail down the north bank, crosses the stream bed, comes up—"

"It adds six hours."

"Six hours we have."

"Casper." Mara stopped and looked at me the way she looked at rivers that lied on maps. "Roos knows the pass opens in seven days. He doesn't need to catch us. He needs to slow us down until the window closes. Every hour we spend being careful is an hour he wins without firing a shot."

"I know how arithmetic works."

"You know how guilt works. That's different."

Yawane came up the line to see why the column had stopped, took one look at the viaduct, and said something in Tok Pisin to the elder behind her. The word I caught was *bikpela bris*, and the tone was the tone people use for graves.

The villagers wouldn't cross it either way, it turned out. Not because of the drop. Because their grandparents had been marched across this bridge toward the mine, and some of them had been marched back across it the other way, and the ones who came back had never spoken of it, and the silence had soaked into the steel like rust. Yawane told me this without drama, the way you'd report a weather condition.

"So we go around," I said.

"No," she said. "We go across. But we go across knowing what it is."

I have spent most of my adult life avoiding exactly that sentence.

* * *

We crossed in relay groups of eight, testing each tie before trusting it, the whole column strung out along the standing spans while the gorge breathed cold air up at us. Tomas carried the rear — his choice, not mine. He'd been different since the adit, since he found his grandfather's name in a ledger column headed with a number instead of a name. Quieter, but not hollowed out. More like forged. He'd taken to walking with his rifle slung forward, and when I'd offered to take the rear position myself he'd shaken his head and said, "You're the engineer. Engineers belong at the front of things. Someone has to be the last man across; let it be a grandson."

I didn't argue. There are arguments you lose on purpose because losing them is the respectful thing to do.

I was forty meters onto the far span, helping an old man named Kepas whose knees had opinions about railroad ties, when the first shot came.

It didn't come from ahead. It came from below and behind — from the north bank, the game trail, the route I'd wanted us to take. Which meant they'd scouted both options and chosen to let us commit to the bridge before showing themselves, which meant professionals, which meant Roos had spent real money, which meant everything I'd feared about the man's patience expiring was now a matter of public record written in rifle fire.

The sound rolled up the gorge and came back doubled. Somewhere in the column a woman began singing — low, steady, a line of some song I didn't know — and other voices joined it, and I understood later that it wasn't a song of fear. It was a work song. Rail-laying rhythm. They sang because their grandfathers had sung laying track for the men who would kill them, and they were going to cross this bridge singing if it killed them, and God help me, it organized them better than any order I could have shouted.

"Tomas!" Mara's voice, cracking across the water. "Rear span!"

I turned in time to see him do the thing I will remember until I die, and probably after, if there's any justice in the accounting.

He didn't run. Eight men with rifles were coming up the bank trail toward the near abutment, and Tomas — twenty-four years old, geologist, grandfather expended as ballast — stepped to the center of the rear span, knelt behind the rusted remains of a signal post, and started shooting back.

Not well. Not like a soldier. Like a man who had read about shooting and believed in it completely. But he was calm, and he was loud — shouting the count of shooters to Mara, calling positions, keeping the mercenaries' heads down while the column moved. And here is the thing about courage measured in blood: it doesn't look like the movies. It looks like a young man kneeling in birdlime and rust, reloading with shaking hands, saying "six, maybe seven, left side of the trail" in a voice that only shook on the numbers.

"Mara, get them off the bridge!" I shouted.

"They're crossing! You get them off!"

She was already doing it — moving down the line, physically turning people around, pointing them to the far abutment and the cut beyond it where the roadbed dove into jungle. Yawane had reorganized the column into a withdrawal so fast I genuinely couldn't tell if she'd planned it or simply refused to acknowledge that panic was an available option.

Bullets began finding the bridge. Steel sings when it's hit — a high, wrong note, a bell with a grudge. Ties splintered. One round clipped the rail a meter from my head and the ricochet went off into the gorge whistling like a dropped kettle.

And then Tomas made his mistake, which was also his finest hour.

A mercenary worked his way along the near abutment — flanking, patient, good. Tomas saw him, shifted to cover the angle, and stood up to get a clear field of fire across the broken gap where the middle span had fallen. For three seconds he owned that entire bridge. He drove the flanker back under cover. He bought us another minute, maybe two.

On the fourth second, a round from the trail caught him high in the chest, and he went backward off the signal post and onto the deck of the span like a coat falling off a hook.

* * *

I want to say I thought clearly. I didn't. What I did was move, and sometimes moving is all clarity ever amounts to.

I ran the span. Forty meters of rotten ties with bullets stitching the rails beside me, the gorge breathing up cold, the singing behind me faltering and then — this is true — starting again, lower, faster, the rhythm of men driving spikes. I reached Tomas where he lay half against the parapet stub, and there was a lot of blood and it was coming from higher than I liked and he was trying to say something.

"Don't talk," I said.

"Left side," he managed. "Trail. Six. Maybe five now."

"You counted under fire?"

"Habit." His teeth were red. "Field notes."

Mara arrived at a dead run, dropped to her knees, and became a different person. I'd seen her argue with rivers, seen her chart sudd mazes no official map admitted existed, seen her photograph evidence with hands steady enough to shame a surgeon. This was that, weaponized. She stripped his shirt, found the entry wound, found the exit, and started working with the field dressing from her kit while telling him exactly what she was doing in a flat, calm voice, because she knew — everyone who has been around blood long enough knows — that the wounded stay alive better inside a competent narration than inside a silence.

"Bullethole through the shoulder muscle," she said. "Missed the lung. Missed the artery. Tomas, look at me. You are going to be furious about this scar for the rest of your life, and the rest of your life is going to be long."

"Liar," Tomas said fondly.

"Cartographer," she said. "I don't draw things wrong."

Getting him up was the hard part. He weighed what a strong young man weighs and contributed maybe a tenth of it. We got an arm over each of our shoulders and started for the far abutment, and the shooting changed character — longer intervals, shifting angles. They were repositioning. Coming onto the near span.

Which brought me to the problem I'd been carrying in my back pocket since the moment the first shot rang out, the way you carry a stone you picked up on a beach because some part of you knew the day would want it.

I am a railroad engineer. Disgraced, yes. Blamed for a trestle collapse I did not cause but did sign a report about, yes. But before all of that, before the fall, I spent eleven years learning everything there was to know about why bridges stand and — this is the part that matters today — how much encouragement they need to stop.

Bridge 7's far span was dying. I'd known it since we arrived. The concrete pier on the far side had a shear crack running hip-high, old and wet and full of root, and the span itself sat on it the way a tired man sits on a stool. The middle span had already gone down sometime in the forties or fifties, taking its pier's crown with it. The near span, where the mercenaries now were, was actually the healthiest of the three. Nature's little joke.

If the far span went, the near span became an island. A very well-armed island with a long swim home.

"Mara." We'd reached solid roadbed, the cut swallowing us into green dark. Behind us, boots on steel, the mercenaries advancing across the near span, unhurried now, masters of their ground. "How many are across the cut? All of them?"

"Six on the trail, two on the bridge. The boat crew stayed downstream."

"And the villagers?"

"Gone. Yawane took them into the cut the moment Tomas fell. They're ten minutes ahead and moving like the wind owes them money."

Tomas, slung between us, half-conscious, said: "Casper. Whatever you're doing. Do it fast."

I set him down against a fig tree root, kissed the top of Mara's head — no time to make a ceremony of it, though I noted for the record that I'd have liked one — and went back out onto the roadbed alone, toward the edge of the cut, toward the dying pier, carrying nothing but a surveyor's eye and thirty years of professional knowledge I had sworn never to use this way.

Here is what nobody tells you about demolition: it's mostly bookkeeping. Every structure keeps accounts. Loads in, loads out, margins of error, fatigue accumulating in steel and concrete like interest on a bad loan. Bridge 7 had been insolvent for seventy years. It had kept standing the way failing companies keep operating — through habit, through nobody asking hard questions, through the sheer embarrassment of collapse.

I asked the hard questions.

The shear crack was the asset. Everything else was arrangement. Two hundred meters back along the roadbed, the old rail embankment had been built up on cribbing — timber cribs, war-era, black with rot but stacked with the dumb honesty of colonial engineering. I'd clocked them on the walk in and filed them away the way I file everything, because a man who owns no address learns to keep his possessions in his head.

I kicked out the bottom crib logs. Four of them. It took perhaps ninety seconds and every gram of strength I had, and the whole time the gunfire behind me was getting closer, more confident, the mercenaries crossing the middle gap on the fallen span's twisted spine because they had no reason on earth to hurry.

Then I ran. Fifty meters, sixty, the embankment sloping down toward the abutment, my lungs filing formal complaints. Behind me I heard a shout — someone had spotted the old man kicking timbers, someone had done the same arithmetic I had, someone started shooting in earnest.

I threw myself off the roadbed into the fern brake as the far pier finished its bankruptcy.

It didn't explode. Nothing so honest. It sighed — a deep structural exhalation, concrete giving up its grip, steel finding angles it was never drawn at — and the whole far span folded sideways into the gorge with a sound like the earth clearing its throat, and the near span, suddenly holding a hundred tons of advancing mercenaries and no counterweight, followed it with a shriek of tearing rivets that I felt in my teeth through the ground.

Dust boiled up out of the cut. When it thinned, Bridge 7 was two stumps and a memory, and the middle of the gorge belonged to the river's dead ancestor, and six armed men stood on the near rim looking across eighty meters of empty air at an old man lying in the ferns.

One of them raised his rifle. I looked at him — really looked, the way you look at weather — and waited.

He didn't fire. Too far for certainty, too much dust, and professionals don't spend rounds on maybes when their employer bills by the bullet. He lowered it and said something to the others, and they began the long, furious walk back down the trail to find another crossing they no longer had time to use.

I lay in the ferns and laughed until it hurt, which, given my recent exertions, was not far.

* * *

They'd carried Tomas another kilometer into the cut before I caught up, Mara refusing all help with the stretcher the villagers had improvised from a shelter-half and two rifle slings. His color was bad but his pulse was stubborn, and when I knelt beside him he opened his eyes and said:

"The bridge?"

"Gone."

"Our side or theirs?"

"Both. Neither. The middle belongs to the birds now."

He smiled, and the smile cost him, and he paid it anyway. "My grandfather helped build these lines. Did you know that? Probably this bridge." He looked up through the canopy, at nothing, at everything. "First thing our family's built on it that lasted."

Mara's hand tightened on his wrist, checking a pulse that didn't need checking, just needing to hold on to something. Above us, Yawane stood on the roadbed with the column ranked behind her, forty-one witnesses who had crossed a haunted bridge singing and were now standing in the smoke of its funeral, and she caught my eye and gave me a single nod — the nod of one marshal acknowledging another's field promotion, or perhaps just acknowledging that the day was not yet done with any of us.

Because it wasn't. Far downstream, faint but unmistakable, came the sound I'd been listening for under everything else all afternoon: an engine. Not a boat engine. An aircraft, laboring, climbing — a floatplane fighting up the river valley against the grain of the terrain, and there was only one man in this basin with the money and the spite to fly it.

Roos had stopped buying checkpoints. He was coming to collect in person.

Mara met my eyes over Tomas's stretcher, and neither of us said anything, because there was nothing left to negotiate. Seven days to the pass. One wounded man on a sling. Forty-one witnesses and a bag of ledgers that had already cost a bridge.

I picked up my end of the stretcher, and we walked.
