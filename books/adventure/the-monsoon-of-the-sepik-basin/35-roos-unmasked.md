## 35. Roos Unmasked

There is a particular quality to light in the high country just before a storm decides whether to commit. The clouds gather their argument, the air goes dense and yellow, and everything below—the kunai, the mud, the blood on a man's shirt—gets rendered in a kind of terrible clarity, as if the world were a document about to be signed and someone had turned up the lamp to check the fine print.

That was the light we stood in, the afternoon after the trestle, when Anselm Roos ran out of men.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was the whole point of what happened next. He didn't run out of courage. Courage was never in his inventory. He ran out of men—two dead in the collapse I'd set, one limping back down the trail with a shoulder wound and a grudge, and the rest scattered into the treeline like cards thrown at a ceiling that refused to hold them. Roos himself stood at the edge of the ruined viaduct with a pistol he no longer had a good reason to draw, his floatplane idling on the river below like a patient animal that had already smelled the ending.

Behind me: twenty-three witnesses, a satchel of ledgers, a stretcher with Tomas on it, and a dying man propped against a rail bed spike who kept insisting he was fine in the voice men use when they are not fine at all.

In front of me: Anselm Roos, third-generation heir to the Kessler Development Group, offering me a future.

"You're Iversen," he said. It wasn't a question. It was an audit.

"Last I checked."

"The railroad engineer. The trestle at Kukena." He smiled the way lawyers smile at a typo in an opposing brief—delighted, because delighted is cheaper than worried. "I read your file. All of it. The inquiry, the report, the license revocation. You signed a certification you knew was false, and eleven people went into the gorge, and you walked away with nothing but a name nobody will hire." He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; you could hear the money in it. "I can fix that."

The wind came up the valley and moved the kunai in long silver strokes. Somewhere behind us, one of the village women was singing very quietly to Tomas—not a song so much as a rhythm, the kind you keep time to when you're keeping someone on the far side of the dark. Mara stood at my left shoulder with the satchel strap across her chest and her hand resting on it the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping child's back. Not guarding it. Just confirming it was breathing.

"Fix it how?" I asked, because I wanted to hear him say it. I've always believed a man should be made to narrate his own bribe. It's the only confession most of them will ever give.

"Two million, wired anywhere you like. Reinstatement—the Kessler Group has friends on every licensing board between here and Brisbane; your certification would be restored inside a year, with back pay and a letter of apology from the inquiry, if we felt like producing one. A clean name, Iversen. The one thing you've been walking around without for eleven years." He gestured at the satchel with the pistol, which was a mistake of staging but an honest one. "All you have to do is what you did at Kukena. Sign something that isn't true, and let the past go into the gorge. You've done it before. You were good at it."

I'll admit something here, since this whole account is a ledger of its own: for about four seconds, I looked at that man and saw a door. Not a door out of the valley—a door out of myself. Two million and a clean name is not a bribe to a man like me. It's an amnesty. It's the universe walking up to you on a Tuesday and saying, *actually, we've reviewed the file, and we've decided the worst thing you ever did didn't happen.* There is no drug on earth that works faster than that.

Four seconds. Then I heard the singing behind me, and the door closed.

"You know what's funny about my file," I said. "Everyone reads it the same way. The company wrote it that way on purpose. *Engineer falsifies certification, eleven dead, license revoked.* Clean little sentence. Cause and effect. One bad man, one bad signature." I took a step toward him, and he didn't step back, which I'll give him credit for. "But here's what the sentence leaves out. I didn't falsify that certification because I was greedy. I falsified it because my regional director called me into his office three days before the inspection and showed me photographs of my sister's house, her street, the school my nephew went to, and asked me—in the politest voice you ever heard—whether I understood how much of my family's life was exposed to weather."

Roos's smile stayed where it was, but something behind it shifted, the way ice shifts on a river before it goes.

"The company didn't just need my signature," I said. "They needed my *complicity*, because a man who's signed one lie will sign anything after, and a man who's signed anything after will never testify. That was the design. That was the whole design, from Kukena to this valley, from my director to your grandfather to you. You don't buy silence, Roos. You *manufacture* it. You make it out of people."

"You're giving a speech," Roos said. "Speeches are free. Names cost money."

"I'm not giving a speech." I held out my hand, palm up, and waited until he understood what I was asking for. He didn't want to. He looked at my open hand like it might be a trick, and then, because he was a man who had never in his life been offered anything for free and couldn't model the possibility, he put the pistol in it.

I turned and set it on the rusted rail beside me. Then I turned back.

"I'm giving *testimony*."

* * *

Here is what I mean, and why it mattered, and why the next three minutes did more damage to the Kessler Development Group than the ledgers, the photographs, the bones, and the tribunal combined.

A bought man's testimony is worth what he was bought for, and no more. The moment you pay a witness, you've built him a price tag, and every lawyer in the hemisphere will hang the case on it. Kessler's people had known that for eighty years. It was their whole method. They didn't need to win tribunals; they needed every man who could hurt them to be, by the time he stood up, a man who had already sold something.

So I stood up unsold.

"You can't buy me," I told Roos, "because I'm about to tell the tribunal something you can't afford for me to tell them, and the fact that you offered me two million dollars to stop me is going to make it *worse* for you, not better. I'm going to tell them about Kukena. Not the file version. The real one. I'm going to tell them how a company manufactures complicity, step by step, because I was one of the steps, and I still have the survey notes, the original stress calculations, the letter I was made to sign, and the memory of my director's voice saying *weather* the way you just said *fix*. I'm going to hand them the method, Roos. The ledgers tell them what your company did to these people. My testimony tells them *how your company does it to everyone*. And the difference between those two things is the difference between a fine and an execution."

The kunai hissed. Down on the river, the floatplane's engines turned over, patient, obedient, waiting for a man who hadn't moved.

"You'd burn yourself," Roos said quietly. It wasn't contempt anymore. It was genuine confusion, the confusion of a man who has never once seen someone reach for the thing that costs him. "You'd stand up in front of the tribunal and confess to killing eleven people just to—" He stopped. Recalculated. "What do you want? Everyone wants something. What is it?"

And there it was. The whole man, in one question. Not *how can this be true*—but *what's the price*. He had a calculator where other men kept a soul, and it was returning an error, and the error was making him sweat.

"Nothing," I said. "That's what you can't model. I'm not doing this to get something. I'm doing it because eleven people went into a gorge so a company could survive a quarter, and I helped, and the only currency that ever pays that debt back is the kind you spend on yourself. You can't buy it, Roos. You can't buy it because it isn't for sale. It's for *giving*. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire moral history of the world."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then, to his credit—and I have thought about this a great deal since; there is something almost pitiable in a man whose only remaining move is the honest one—Anselm Roos stopped selling.

"My grandfather," he said, "kept a second set of books. Did you know that? Not for the tribunal. For himself. He wanted to remember what it cost. He used to say an heir should know what he's inheriting." He looked down at the ruined viaduct, at the span I'd dropped into the gorge with forty kilos of cutting charge and eleven years of engineering grudge. "I never read them. The ledgers you're carrying. I never read them. I just knew they existed, and that they couldn't exist."

"That's the inheritance," I said. "You inherited the not-reading."

Behind us, Hargreave laughed. It was a terrible sound, wet and thin, the laugh of a man spending his last reserves, and it stopped Roos cold.

"Dr. Hargreave," Roos said.

"Mr. Roos." Hargreave's voice came out gravel and wire. "I signed a confidentiality clause once. Did you know that? Eighteen years ago, a predecessor of yours showed me photographs in a hotel room in Lae, and I signed, and I told myself for eighteen years that I'd done it to protect a research program." He coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it ended he was grayer. "I'm dying, Mr. Roos. The doctor in the mission gave me weeks, maybe. So let me tell you what I've learned about clauses. A clause is a debt with your name spelled wrong. You sign it, and then for the rest of your life you're paying interest to men who own the spelling." He lifted his chin at the satchel. "My statement's in there. Sworn, witnessed, dated. Everything. The funding, the dig, the photographs in Lae, all of it. I wrote it out by hand because I didn't trust myself to dictate it without editing."

Roos looked at him for a long time. "You could have had anything," he said. "You could have had the program. The funding was never going to stop."

"Yes," Hargreave said. "That was the trap, wasn't it. The funding was never going to stop." He closed his eyes. "Go home, Mr. Roos. Take your plane and your remaining men and go home, and when the summons comes—and it will come—answer it. It's the only door left that opens."

* * *

He almost did it. I want to record that, because the record should be fair even to men like him. Anselm Roos stood at the edge of that viaduct for the better part of a minute, and I watched something move across his face that in a better man you would have called conscience and in him you could only call arithmetic finally balancing wrong. His hand twitched toward the pistol on the rail—Mara's hand went into the satchel, and Tomas, half-conscious on the stretcher, put his own hand flat on the planks and pushed himself up two inches, which was the bravest useless thing I saw in that whole valley—and then Roos looked at the three of us, at the witnesses coming out of the treeline behind us with their faces set like weather, at the twenty-three people whose grandparents his company had listed as *expended*, and he understood, finally and completely, that the gun was not the problem. The gun had never been the problem. The problem was standing behind the gun, and it was multiplying.

"Two million," he said one more time, to nobody, to the air, to the arithmetic.

"Testimony," I said.

He turned and walked down to the floatplane. His men came out of the treeline one by one, folding toward the river like a hand unclenching, and the plane took off into the yellow light and banked south, and the sound of it faded until the valley was just the valley again—wind, water, kunai, the creak of the broken viaduct settling into its new geometry.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then the singing behind us started up again, the same low rhythm, and I went back and knelt by Tomas and put my hand on his forehead, and he opened his eyes and looked at me with the enormous gravity of the very young and the very hurt.

"Did he offer you a lot?" Tomas asked.

"Enough," I said.

"And you said no."

"I said something better than no."

He considered that with the seriousness he brought to everything, even engine repairs, even bleeding. "My grandfather's in the ledgers," he said. "Number one-fourteen." His hand found my wrist and held it, not for support—for emphasis. "When you testify. Say the number. They gave them numbers instead of names. Say the number out loud, so it gets into the record, so it's a name again."

"Number one-fourteen," I said. "I'll say it first."

Mara sat down beside me on the rail bed, the satchel between us, and for a while neither of us said anything, because there is a kind of silence that only works between people who have just watched each other refuse the same door.

"You know what happens now," she said at last. It wasn't a question either. She'd learned that from me, or I'd learned it from her; by then I'd lost track.

"He'll go to ground," I said. "Lawyers, injunctions, a wall of paper between himself and the summons. And the pass opens in—" I looked at the sky, at the clouds still holding their argument, at the glacier country to the north where the ice had been groaning all week like a door swelling in its frame. "Four days. Maybe five. He knows the window as well as we do. If he can't buy the ledgers, he'll try to buy the weather. The checkpoints, the river police, the airfield. He'll spend everything he has to make sure that pass stays closed to us."

"Then we'd better be ready when it opens."

I looked north. The high country had gone the color of hammered tin, and somewhere up in it, under a hundred meters of ice, a river the size of a nation was waiting for the calving to begin.

"Get them moving," I said. "All of them. We walk tonight."

Mara stood, shouldered the satchel, and put out her hand—not for the pistol this time, just for mine, briefly, hard, the whole conversation in a grip. Then she turned and raised her voice in three languages at once, and the column began to form, twenty-three witnesses and two stretchers and a satchel of numbers waiting to become names again, moving north along the old rail bed into the last of the light.

Above the pass, the ice groaned once, long and low, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

It was beginning to wake.
