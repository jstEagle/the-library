## 36. The Pass Opens

The ice spoke all night.

Not the polite creak of a river freezing or a hull working in a swell — I've heard both, and both are honest sounds. This was different. This was a sound with weight behind it, a groan that came up through the ground and into the soles of your boots and told you, in the language of things under strain, exactly how much mass was waiting above you and how little it cared about your plans.

We camped at the tree line because we had no choice. The kunai grass ended where the old survey road began to climb, and beyond that the rail bed became a scar of ballast and rusted fishplates climbing toward the pass like a stitch holding two halves of a country together. Somewhere up there, past the last switchback, was the saddle the glacier had sealed for most of a year. Somewhere up there was the other side.

Nine days, the radio man at the mission had said. Maybe eight now. The calving season would crack the high route open, and when it did, it would stay open about as long as a door held by a tired man's foot.

I lay on my back on a bed of cut fern and listened to the glacier turn over, and I thought about doors.

* * *

We started before dawn, because Mara insisted and because Mara was right, which was becoming a habit neither of us discussed.

"Ice moves in the warm part of the day," she said, cinching the straps on Ferro's stretcher. "Midmorning to midafternoon, that's when it sheds. We cross in the cold. We're on the ice before the sun hits the face and we're down the far side before it warms enough to let go."

"You've done this before," Tomas said. His arm was splinted and strapped across his chest, and he'd refused the second stretcher with a stubbornness I was starting to recognize as hereditary. His grandfather had apparently been a stubborn man too. It ran in families, along with everything else.

"I've charted this glacier," Mara said. "From photographs, from old surveys, from a German expedition report from 1938 that described the terminus as 'impassable and morally suspect.' Nobody's crossed here since the war. But ice is ice. It has rules."

"Everything has rules," I said. "It's the exceptions that kill you."

Mara looked at me over the stretcher pole. "That's very grim, Casper."

"That's very railroad," I said. "Every rule on a railway exists because somebody died learning it. The rules aren't the danger. The danger is the day the rule turns out to have an exception, and you're standing on it."

She didn't argue. She just put her hand flat against the satchel — the ledgers, the photographs, Ferro's notebook, the whole buried history of a massacre riding on one shoulder — and nodded once, and turned to get the column moving.

Twenty-three witnesses. Two stretchers — Ferro, still feverish but lucid enough to apologize for his own weight every time we lifted him, and old Yawan, the elder's brother, whose heart had decided somewhere on the third day of walking that it had given enough. Hargreave walked. That was the thing nobody said out loud: Hargreave, who should have been on a stretcher beside Ferro, walked at the front of the column with a stick he'd cut himself, gray as ash and steady as a metronome, because he had said he would carry his statement across the pass on his own feet or not at all, and dying men get to be unreasonable.

He'd written the statement at the mission, three nights ago, by lamplight, while the investigator's voice crackled on the radio explaining chain of custody for the fourth time. He'd signed it. He'd had it witnessed. And then he'd folded it into an oilskin packet and handed it to me, not to Mara, and said, "You keep it. You're the one who knows how to make sure things arrive."

I hadn't asked what he meant. Some sentences you don't open in company.

* * *

The climb took four hours and cost us more than I wanted to count.

The old rail bed was a lesson in what money can abandon. Ties rotted to black lace. A rusted handcar sitting in a cutting like the skeleton of some insect that had died mid-journey. Culverts plugged with sixty years of slide debris, so that water had found its own routes down the mountain, cutting channels across our path that we had to jump or wade. The witnesses climbed the way their grandparents must have built this line — steadily, without complaint, carrying more than any reasonable person would ask. Women carried children who were too big to be carried. Teenagers carried the stretchers' spare poles. A grandmother named Kapiak carried a chicken in a woven basket and refused, absolutely refused, every offer to take it from her, and when I finally understood why — the chicken was for the far side, a gift for a family that had lost theirs — I stopped offering.

Tomas walked beside me for the steepest hour, breathing hard, refusing help.

"My grandfather's name is in those ledgers," he said, somewhere around the fifth switchback. "Number 114. They wrote it as a number, Casper. He dug this tunnel and they wrote him down like tonnage." He stopped, leaned on a rusted rail post, looked back down the valley we'd climbed out of. "I used to think I came up here to study rocks. Sediment layers, flow rates. Good clean science." He laughed, and it wasn't a happy sound. "Turns out I was following him the whole time. The river he helped move. The mountain he died inside of."

"He didn't die inside of it," I said. "He got out. You're the proof."

Tomas looked at me for a long moment. Then he straightened off the post and kept climbing, and I understood that I'd said the only useful thing I owned.

Above the tree line the air went thin and bright and cold enough to bite. And there it was.

The glacier came down out of the high country like a frozen river with somewhere to be — a tongue of blue-white ice filling the valley between two rock walls, crevassed and ridged and groaning, its lower face calved into a wall of shattered blocks. Above it, the saddle. The pass. The notch in the ridge where the old line had once crossed on trestles long since fallen, and where, for a few weeks each year, the ice pulled back just far enough to let a body through.

"It's open," Mara said. She'd gone ahead with two of the younger men, and she stood on a knob of rock with the wind pulling at her, and she was smiling, actually smiling, in a way I hadn't seen since Wewak. "Casper. Look at it. It's open."

It was. A corridor of gravel and old ice, maybe forty meters wide, running up the glacier's spine between seracs the size of houses. Through it, the sky on the far side.

Nature keeping a promise. For nine days. Maybe eight.

"All right," I said. "Rules. Nobody stops on the ice. Nobody wanders. Single file on my marks, spacing of ten paces, and if anyone shouts *ice*, you drop everything except the person next to you and you move toward the near wall. We cross in the cold hours. We are down the other side before noon or we camp on rock, never on ice. Everybody understand?"

Kapiak's chicken made a noise that I chose to interpret as agreement.

* * *

Crossing a glacier is like crossing a sleeping animal. You do it quietly, quickly, and with total respect, and you never forget for one step what it could do if it woke.

The first hour was good. Hard going — the gravel gave way to bare ice polished by meltwater, and we roped nothing because we had no rope worth the name, so instead we picked our line carefully along the medial moraine, that ribbon of stone the glacier carries down its middle like a spine. The witnesses moved well. Better than well. These were people whose grandparents had carried rails up mountains, and something of it lived in their knees. Old Yawan's stretcher-bearers kept pace without being asked twice.

Hargreave walked at the front, beside me, his stick clicking on the ice.

"You know," he said, conversationally, as though we were discussing weather, "I reviewed the hydrology data for this region in ninety-four. Published paper. Cited the sediment anomalies myself — noted them as 'inconsistent with natural channel evolution.' Recommended further study." He clicked along. "I knew, Casper. Not everything. But I knew the shape of it. You can't look at that river and not know something was moved by hands."

"I know," I said.

"And still I took Kessler's money." He said it without drama, the way a man reports a diagnosis. "Because the money was real and the knowing was abstract. That's how it works. That's how it always works. The horror stays abstract until it's lying on a stretcher in front of you with a fever." He glanced back at Ferro's bearers. "Ferro found the adit because I sent him. I told myself it was science. It was a commission. I sold a dig site and called it research."

"Why are you telling me this now? On the ice?"

"Because," he said, "there's no future tense left in my vocabulary, and it's remarkably freeing. You should try it sometime." He smiled — a small, dry, ruined smile. "Besides. If the glacier takes me, I'd rather it took me mid-confession. Better acoustics."

The groan came then — a long, deep exhalation from somewhere up-valley, rolling through the ice beneath our feet like a train passing under a bridge. Everyone froze. The column went silent, twenty-three people and a chicken standing on a living thing's backbone, listening to it breathe.

Then it passed, and we moved.

* * *

The trouble came at the icefall, an hour short of the saddle.

The route narrowed where two seracs leaned together, leaving a gap barely wide enough for the stretchers, floored with blue ice glazed by melt. Mara scouted it, came back, and shook her head at the stretcher parties.

"We lighten them. Everything off the stretchers except the men themselves. Packs go over one at a time, on the rope we don't have — so on shoulders, in relay. Then the stretchers, tilted, eased through. Slowly."

It took forty minutes. Forty minutes standing on a glacier in the coldest morning of my life, watching human beings pass human beings through a gap in a wall of compressing ice, and I will tell you honestly: I have seen bridges built with less care than these people spent on each other.

Ferro went through delirious, murmuring about gauge readings. Yawan went through awake, and insisted on looking up at the seracs as he passed between them, and said afterward, quite clearly, "They are older than the company. Let them look at me too." I have thought about that sentence every day since.

Hargreave went through last of the walkers. Halfway, his stick slipped. He caught himself on the ice wall with one hand and stayed there a moment, head down, breathing, and I moved to take his arm and he waved me off with the other hand — *not yet, not yet* — and finished the passage on his own feet, which was, I understood, the entire point.

And then we were through, and the saddle opened ahead of us, and the far side fell away into green.

* * *

We were twenty minutes down the descent when the glacier settled its account.

It started as a sound like the world clearing its throat — a crack, enormous, from the upper face — and then the sound I'll hear for the rest of my life, which is the sound of several thousand tons of ice deciding simultaneously to become water's slower cousin. The calving face let go. Not above us — thank whatever keeps books on men like me — but off to the east, a whole flank of the terminus sloughing away in a slow-motion avalanche that filled the lower valley with white thunder and sent a blast of displaced air down the glacier like the breath of something exhaling.

Everyone ran. There was no dignity in it. Twenty-three people and a chicken sprinting down a moraine while the mountain behind them rearranged itself. The pressure wave hit us as dust and cold wind and a rain of fine ice crystals that glittered in the sun like the whole sky had been ground to sugar.

When it settled, we stood in the green of the far slope, gasping, and looked back.

The route was gone. The corridor we'd come through was buried under fresh collapse, a hundred meters of new ice and rubble sealing the pass like a door slammed shut. Whatever window the calving season had opened, it had just closed early — days ahead of schedule, without apology.

Mara found my hand in the crowd without looking. Her fingers were freezing and shaking slightly, and she gripped mine the way she had at the mission, the whole conversation in a grip.

"Eight days," she said. "We made it with five to spare."

"We made it because you counted wrong," I said. "In our favor. Don't ever learn to count better."

Hargreave lowered himself onto a boulder, breathing hard, his face the color of the dust still settling around us. He looked back at the sealed pass for a long time.

"I've spent thirty years," he said, "standing in front of closed doors, telling myself they'd open if I paid enough." He tapped his stick on the stone. "That one didn't even wait for an offer."

Behind us, safe on the green side of the divide, the column regrouped. Kapiak's chicken announced its survival at volume. Somebody started laughing, then several somebodies, the ragged hysterical laughter of people who have walked across a sleeping animal and heard it roll over, and I let it run, because they'd earned it, and because laughter carries further than fear and I wanted Roos — if any of Roos's ears were within ten miles — to hear exactly how many of us there were and exactly how unburied.

Then I opened the oilskin packet, checked Hargreave's statement — dry, intact, signed, witnessed — and put it away again.

Five days to the tribunal's reach. One dying man's confession, one satchel of numbers waiting to become names, twenty-three living witnesses, and a route down the far side that existed on no map anyone had paid to erase.

I looked at Mara. She was already unfolding her charts.

"Which way?" I asked.

She smiled, and pointed north, and the column began to move.
