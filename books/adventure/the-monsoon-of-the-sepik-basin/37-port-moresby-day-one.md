## 37. Port Moresby, Day One

The tribunal building in Port Moresby had been a colonial customs house once, and it still smelled like it — mildew and old varnish and the ghost of men signing things they shouldn't have. They'd hung a new sign over the door in three languages, but the building itself hadn't been fooled. Buildings remember what was carried through them.

I stood outside it at seven in the morning with my hands in my pockets, doing the thing I do best, which is nothing. Across the street a man in a blue shirt read a newspaper he wasn't reading. I'd clocked him the night before outside the guesthouse too, same shirt, different newspaper. Kessler's lawyers traveled with security the way other men traveled with toothbrushes — automatically, and without admitting how much they needed it.

"You're going to wear a hole in the pavement," Mara said, coming down the steps with two cups of terrible coffee. She handed me one. "Drink this. It's punishment for something."

"For everything, probably." I drank it. It tasted like hot cardboard with ambitions. "How's Ferro?"

"Sleeping. The fever broke around four. He wants to testify today and Dr. Okoro says absolutely not, so he'll be testifying from a chair in the hallway by lunchtime, mark me."

"And Tomas?"

"Walked himself to the courthouse an hour ago. He said he wanted to see the room before he had to be brave in it." She looked up at the facade, at the carved wreaths and the Latin nobody could translate anymore. "It's a big room, Casper."

"I know. I've testified in rooms like it before."

She went quiet in the particular way she did when she was deciding whether to poke something. Mara poked most things — rivers, official maps, me — but she'd learned which of my silences were load-bearing. This one wasn't. Not today.

"Tell me about it," she said anyway. "The last time. While we've got twenty minutes to stand here being terrified."

So I told her again, the short version, the version without the flashback machinery: a trestle over the Wabash gorge, eleven years ago, a report I signed because a regional director put his hand on my shoulder and said *Casper, companies survive, engineers don't have to.* Fourteen months later the span came down under a freight load it should never have carried. Nobody died — that was the miracle everyone used as an excuse — but my signature was on the certification, and when the inquiry came, the company produced the report, and the report produced me, and I did what I'd been trained to do. I became very hard to find.

"You signed under pressure," she said. "You were coerced."

"I signed. That's the whole verb. There's no asterisk on a signature." I turned the cup in my hands. "Which is why I know exactly what their lawyers are going to do to us today. I used to be the asterisk."

* * *

The chamber itself was smaller than the building promised — a converted hearing room with a bench raised on the old customs dais, three commissioners behind microphones, and gallery seating that filled fast once the doors opened. Word had gotten out. Some of it had even gotten out accurately.

Mara sat at the evidence table with the ledgers arranged in front of her like organ pipes: seven company volumes from the adit, water-stained, rat-nibbled, each one wrapped now in archival plastic that some tribunal clerk had insisted on and that Mara had grumbled about for two days because, she said, plastic traps moisture and moisture eats paper and paper is the only witness that can't be intimidated. Ferro's notebook lay beside them in its own case. The photographs — execution photos, forty-one of them, catalogued with the bureaucratic tenderness of men who numbered everything — were in sealed sleeves. And next to all of it, the sediment cores, the channel charts, her own quiet map of anomalies, the whole argument the river had been making for sixty years while nobody listened.

I sat behind her with the witnesses. Tomas in his borrowed shirt with his arm still strapped. Two elders from the river villages who had walked out of the Sepik Basin on rail beds I'd mapped from memory, women among them who had never been more than a day's walk from home in their lives and who now sat in an air-conditioned room in the capital watching ceiling fans turn like slow propellers. Hargreave's sworn statement was there too, read into the record the week before; he'd died waiting for sentencing, and the tribunal president had opened this morning's session by acknowledging it in a voice that didn't quite manage neutrality.

Kessler's delegation occupied the right side of the floor: six lawyers, two consultants, and Anselm Roos's replacement, a smooth grey man named Voss who smiled at everyone including the microphones. Roos himself was in custody pending his own hearing — indicted, frozen out, his development projects suspended like birds shot mid-flight. But corporations are hydra-work. You indict one head, the body grows another with better tailoring.

The morning went the way mornings go in tribunals: slowly, then all at once.

Mara presented first. She was magnificent, and she would hate hearing that, so I'd say it here where she can't reach me. She walked the commissioners through the hydrology the way she argued with rivers — patiently, precisely, with the underlying assumption that the truth was simply a current and you only had to chart it honestly. The rerouted channel above the gauge station. The sediment layers impossible for natural flow. The spoil dumps aligned with the old mine adit. Then the ledgers themselves: labor rolls from 1943 through 1946, columns of names, and in the margin column, in a clerk's neat hand, the word that had made Tomas sit down hard on a jungle log — *expended.* Not deceased. Not lost. Expended, the way you'd write off fuel or blasting powder.

"The company recorded these men as consumables," Mara said, and her voice didn't shake, though I watched her hand on the ledger edge go white. "The photographs confirm what the ledgers imply. The river confirms what the photographs couldn't show — that after the massacre at the lower camp, the company moved the entire channel ninety meters east to bury the site under six meters of new gravel. They didn't just kill these men. They re-plumbed a watershed to hide the arithmetic."

One of the commissioners, an older woman with reading glasses on a chain, asked how long the diversion had held.

"Sixty years," Mara said. "Rivers forget slower than people do. That's rather the point of my profession."

There was a small sound from the gallery — not laughter, exactly. Recognition.

Then the witnesses. The elders spoke through interpreters, and the interpreters' voices kept breaking in the same places theirs didn't. One old woman, Nali, whose grandfather appeared in Ledger Four between a shipment of rails and a consignment of rice, told the story her grandmother had told her: how the men went upriver to work and the company boat came back empty, and how the village was told the men had been taken to Rabaul, and how her grandmother had set a place at every meal for nine years until she died. "We knew," Nali said. "Not the facts. We knew the shape of it. A silence has a shape. You live inside it your whole life and you know its walls."

Tomas testified last before the break. He identified his grandfather's name in the roll — written there in the same clerk's hand, expended, dated three days after a listed "labor dispute" — and then he did the bravest thing I saw all week, which was stop talking. He stood at the microphone with his mouth closed for a full ten seconds, letting the name sit in the air of that old customs house, and nobody rushed him, and when he finally said "that's all," the word came out level.

* * *

The trouble started at half past two, as trouble does.

Voss rose for cross-examination, and I will grant him this: he was good. He didn't bluster. He didn't deny the ledgers were real — denial was a dead letter now, with Roos indicted and the photographs in the record. Instead he did what I would have done, back when I was the kind of engineer companies keep on retainer. He attacked the chain.

"Dr. Ellingsen. These documents were recovered from an unsecured site, in a conflict situation, by persons acting without legal authority. Is that correct?"

"They were recovered from a mass grave someone was actively trying to strip," Mara said. "Correct."

"So no warrant. No custody documentation. No professional archivist present. Forgive me — who handled these volumes between recovery and this room?"

"I did. Casper Iversen packed them. They traveled with us continuously—"

"Continuously." Voss let the word hang like a hook. "Through a military-style escape. Through a jungle portage. Through an armed ambush at a viaduct, during which — if the record is accurate — gunfire struck the aircraft carrying them. Dr. Ellingsen, how does this tribunal know that page four hundred twelve of Ledger Three is the same page four hundred twelve that left that tunnel? How do we know pages weren't added, altered, removed? How do we know any of this wasn't assembled — curated — by interested parties to serve a narrative?"

And there it was. The asterisk move. I felt it in my chest like an old fracture changing weather. Because he was right, in the narrow lawyerly sense — the chain was ugly, hand-to-hand, bloodstained. Evidence that travels through gunfire doesn't arrive with clean receipts. And a tribunal that accepted it on faith would hand Kessler's real lawyers — the appellate ones, the ones not in the room — the knife they needed later. Bury it again, my investigator contact had warned me. Chain of custody, or it all comes apart on appeal and the families watch the verdict dissolve in a courtroom they'll never enter.

Voss worked the seam for forty minutes. He questioned the packing. The storage. The trip. He asked Mara, gently, whether her relationship with Mr. Iversen might reasonably be described as close, and whether close relationships were known to affect judgment, and Mara answered with a flatness I intend to treasure forever: "My relationship with Mr. Iversen affects many things. My measurements are not among them."

But the commissioners were writing notes, and the notes had a worried look.

* * *

They called a recess. In the corridor, Ferro — propped in a wheelchair he'd escaped from twice already — grabbed my sleeve with a feverish grip. "He's winning," he whispered. "Not today. Today they'll accept it. But he's building the appeal. He's building the grave."

"I know."

"Then do the thing you've been not-doing all week. I've watched you carry that tube case like it's made of glass and guilt. Whatever's in it — it's time."

Mara found me at the window at the end of the corridor, looking down at the courtyard where the man in the blue shirt had been joined by a second man in a blue shirt, which is one more than coincidence allows.

"Casper."

"I heard him," I said. "He's right. That's the miserable part. Everything we carried is true and none of it is provable, not the way law wants proof. Hand-to-hand evidence. My hands, mostly." I turned to face her. "There's one set of documents in existence that has a clean chain. Sealed, stamped, professionally archived, signed and dated by a licensed surveyor. They've been sitting in a records office in Lae for eleven years because nobody ever connected them to anything worth burying."

Her eyes went wide, then narrowed, working it ahead of me the way she worked channels. "Your survey records. From before. The closed lines."

"The spur surveys. Nineteen thirty-eight original alignment charts, and my own resurvey — eleven years ago, commissioned by a land title firm, filed and forgotten. I walked that corridor with a theodolite before I ever walked it with witnesses. Every benchmark I logged matches the benchmarks in the ledgers' margin notes. The adit coordinates. The spoil dump elevations. The old gauge station datum." I took a breath. "And the filing stamp is dated, witnessed, and countersigned by a government archivist who has no idea what he's holding. Clean chain. Cold storage. Eleven years of nobody touching it."

"You've had this since Lae," she said. Not accusing. Worse — understanding. "Since before the pass. Before the trestle. Why didn't you—"

"Because entering them means entering myself. Name, license number, signature, the works. Permanent record, Mara. The one thing I've spent eleven years making sure I never appear in." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "You know what the funny part is? Voss is going to make the argument for me. He's going to tell those commissioners that documents without provenance are worthless. And the moment he does, the only question in the room becomes: does provenance exist? And I'm the answer."

She studied me for a long moment. Outside, the rain that had been threatening all day finally committed, hammering the courtyard flags.

"You understand," she said quietly, "that once you stand up in that room, there's no map with closed lines for you anymore. Every registry, every database, every future employer, every man in a blue shirt. You'll be findable for the rest of your life."

"I've been thinking about that," I said. "On the porch, in the rain, at night, when I should be sleeping. And here's what I keep coming back to. Invisible was supposed to be safe. It wasn't. The trestle happened while I was visible and the running happened after, and neither one kept anything buried that deserved burying. Maybe the problem was never being seen. Maybe the problem was what I let be seen instead." I shrugged. "Besides. You chart rivers for a living. You of all people should appreciate a man finally deciding where he flows."

Something crossed her face that I'll be working out for years — half fear, half the expression she gets when a channel finally shows her its true course. She reached out and straightened my collar, an unnecessary act of cartography.

"Then stand up straight," she said. "Records should be accurate."

* * *

Afternoon session. Voss was finishing his peroration, summing up the custody failures with the relish of a man laying foundation stones for an appeal, when I stood.

The tribunal president looked over her glasses. "Mr.—?"

"Casper Iversen. Licensed surveyor, registration number 4471-C, Commonwealth of Australia reciprocated under PNG statute. I have documents to enter."

Voss's smile stayed exactly where it was, which is how I knew I'd hit him somewhere the smile wasn't guarding.

The clerk brought the tube case forward. I unwrapped it myself, because if this was going to be done, it would be done with my own hands, in front of everyone, no gloves, no intermediaries — the opposite of everything I'd practiced for a decade. Inside: the 1938 alignment charts, brittle and brown, showing the spur running to the mine adit, drawn by a colonial surveyor whose company letterhead matched the letterhead in Ledger One. And beneath them, my own sheets. Elevations, benchmarks, coordinates, each one tied to the national grid, each one stamped and countersigned by the Lae archives office, dated eleven years back.

"These surveys were commissioned for a land title assessment," I told the chamber. "Filed, indexed, and untouched since. The chain of custody is continuous, documented, and involves no one connected to either the expedition or the Kessler Group. I'd draw the tribunal's attention to benchmark S-14." I laid my sheet beside the open ledger, and the clerk projected both onto the screen. "Benchmark S-14 appears in my resurvey at elevation 212.4 meters. It appears in the company's own margin notes in Ledger Three, dated 1945, at the same elevation, described as the datum for the 'channel adjustment works.' The tunnel, the diversion, the spoil — the company wrote its crime into the landscape, and the landscape kept the receipt, and I measured the receipt before I knew what it was a receipt for."

Silence. The rain filled it, drumming on the old roof.

"Who commissioned your resurvey?" the president asked.

"A title firm. Which was retained — I learned this later — by a subsidiary of the consortium's successor interests. They wanted to know what the old corridor was worth." I let that sit. "They paid me to measure their own burial ground. I didn't know it at the time. I know it now, and I'm telling the tribunal that my knowledge is recent, my data is old, and my signature—" I touched the corner of the sheet, the ink gone soft with years, "—is mine. It was given freely then, for money, to the wrong side. It's given freely now, for nothing, to the right one."

I looked at Voss. He was writing something, fast, and for the first time all day his handwriting looked like a man's and not a machine's.

"One more thing," I said, and the president inclined her head. "Eleven years ago I certified a rail bridge I knew was compromised, because a man told me companies survive and engineers don't have to. I've been hiding from that signature ever since, and hiding hasn't helped anyone, least of all the people who trusted the bridge. So let the record show what kind of witness stands before you: a compromised one, a confessed one, and a permanent one. I'm done being findable only when it's convenient to other people."

I sat down. Mara's hand found my knee under the table and stayed there.

The senior commissioner took off her glasses, polished them slowly, and put them back on. "The panel will recess until tomorrow morning," she said. "The newly entered exhibits will be verified against the Lae archive tonight, under seal." A pause, and then, dry as the Sepik in October: "Mr. Iversen. The tribunal thanks you for making yourself so difficult to ignore."

* * *

That evening the rain kept on, and the guesthouse porch dripped, and Ferro sat wrapped in a blanket drinking the ginger tea Nali had brewed from roots she'd somehow produced from her luggage like a conjurer. Tomas was on the step below me, his strapped arm resting on his knee, watching the water run down the street in bright braids.

"They verified the sheets yet?" he asked.

"Courier went at six. We'll know by morning."

"And if Voss's people get to the archive first?"

"Then I burn down the concept of archives," I said, "which I'm told is frowned upon."

He almost smiled. Down the street, the man in the blue shirt had been relieved by a new man in a blue shirt, and I noticed — because noticing is the last skill I'll lose — that the new man was talking on a phone, and that he stopped talking when he caught me looking.

Inside, the guesthouse radio murmured the late news: tribunal proceedings, the Kessler freeze, and then a item that made Mara come out to the porch with her tea forgotten in her hand. The Lae records office, the announcer said, had reported an attempted after-hours entry this evening. Attempted. A night watchman, an alarm, two men detained.

Two men. In custody. And the archive sealed under tribunal order since sundown — meaning whatever they'd been after, they'd tried for it *after* the verification courier arrived, which meant they knew the sheets were there, which meant—

"Mara," I said slowly. "How did Voss know what I was going to enter before I entered it?"

She stared at me, and the rain came down harder, and somewhere across town a phone was ringing in a house where a grey man named Voss was about to learn that the chain of custody ran both ways — that evidence proves its custodians too, and that somebody in our convoy had been keeping a ledger of their own.
