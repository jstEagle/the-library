## 39. The Map of Open Lines

The first drawing I ever put my name to was a report that ended my career. The second was an apology I never sent. The third — and I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only religion I've ever kept — was a title block, bottom right corner, in a surveyor's hand that had gone soft from disuse: *C. Iversen, P.E. — Alignment & Structures.* Under it, the date. Under that, nothing. No firm. No client. Just my name, sitting on a government drawing like a man who had finally been invited to sit down.

Mara found me staring at it.

"You've been looking at that for ten minutes," she said.

"I'm checking it for errors."

"You are the error-checking stage, Casper."

"Then I'm checking myself." I tapped the name. "It feels illegal. Forty years of engineering and this is the first time signing something hasn't felt like signing a confession."

She took the pencil out of my hand, wrote her own initials beside mine — *M.E., Hydrology* — and handed it back. "There. Now if it's illegal, you have an accomplice."

That was in March, if you can call anything in the Basin a month. The tribunal in Port Moresby had been over since the wet season's first rains, and the world had done what the world does after verdicts: it moved on with the speed of a river after a flood, leaving the silt of the story settled in the news for a week and then gone. Roos indicted. Kessler's projects frozen mid-pour, concrete trucks parked like abandoned animals. Hargreave dead in a cell he'd asked to be kept in, his sworn statement read aloud in a courtroom he never saw. Okuk testifying — Okuk, of all people, in a pressed shirt and a tie the color of a warning sign, explaining to the tribunal exactly which buyers had come to him and what they'd offered, with the weary fluency of a man reading his own obituary and finding it accurate.

And the spur — the closed line, my closed line, the one I'd carried on a folded map through crocodile water and gun smoke because it was the only honest thing I owned — was going to be rebuilt.

Not by Kessler. That was the point. The tribunal's reparations order had set aside funds, and the Sepik provincial government, flush with the particular righteousness of a government that has just watched someone else be punished, had commissioned a memorial railway: the old survey spur from the lowland junction to the site of the adit, restored not for freight but for memory. A line that would carry the descendants of the laborers — the men listed in the company ledgers as *expended*, a word that makes a human being sound like a battery — to the place where their grandfathers' bones had been counted as ballast.

And someone in Wewak, in an office that smelled of diesel and carbon paper, had asked a question that still made me laugh at odd moments: *Does anyone actually know where this line ran?*

The answer, it turned out, was a disgraced engineer with a map of closed lines and, for the first time in his life, no reason to hide.

* * *

We worked out of a shed at the lowland junction that the crews had christened the Cathedral, on account of the corrugated roof that turned every rainstorm into a sermon. Inside: a drafting table Mara had salvaged from the gauge station — scrubbed, repainted, its surface still bearing one old compass scratch that she refused to sand out, calling it provenance — and a second table for me, and between them a floor buried in drawings, sediment charts, and the particular debris of two people who had stopped pretending they worked separately.

The river came first, because the river always comes first. The diversion the consortium cut in the forties to hide the adit and drown the old channel had never been undone; the water had simply forgotten its first argument. Mara's restoration charts — the ones she'd begun quietly at the gauge station, the map of anomalies that had curdled from scientific curiosity into evidence — were now being reworked as engineering. Where the river had been forced, it would be allowed back. Not all at once. Rivers don't forgive all at once. But channel by channel, the old path would reopen, and the memorial railway would follow the ground beside it, the way railways should, the way this one had before men who counted bodies as ballast decided the ground was inconvenient.

"Here." Mara laid a fresh sheet over the table, weighted the corners with engine parts because we were still too broke for proper weights. The channel lines ran in blue, the rail alignment in red, and where they converged near the old adit she'd drawn a small, precise symbol — a station platform, a shelter roof, a name.

I read the name and had to look at the roof beams for a while.

*Ellingsen-Iversen Halt.* No. That wasn't it. She'd never have done that; she argued with rivers, not with modesty. I looked again.

*Ferrolanding.* For Dr. Ferro, who had hidden in the bush with a fever and a notebook and come out of it alive enough to testify, though he still started at loud engines. And below it, in smaller type, the name the elder by the river had finally given us, months after the tribunal, after the village had decided we were worth the risk of memory: the laborers' name for the place, the one that had never appeared on any colonial chart.

"They voted on it," Mara said. "The descendants. Ferro came to the meeting himself. He stood up and said he'd spent his career measuring rivers and had never once measured what they cost." She smoothed the sheet. "Tomas translated. Halfway through, Tomas had to stop and let his mother finish."

Tomas. Who had held the crossing at the ruined viaduct with a borrowed rifle and a courage nobody had drafted into him, and who now walked with a cane and a limp that the Wewak surgeons called *good as it's going to get*, and who had appointed himself, without anyone appointing him, the memorial project's liaison to the villages. He arrived most mornings on the early truck, cane first, grin second, and stood over my drawings telling me the alignment was wrong because his grandfather's generation had walked a different path to the water.

"The path isn't the grade, Tomas," I'd told him the first week. "The grade has to respect the river."

"So did they," he said. "And the river didn't respect them back. Maybe the railway can."

I had put the alignment where he suggested. It cost us four hundred meters of extra fill and one difficult culvert. It was the best four hundred meters I ever spent.

* * *

The trouble with building something honest, I discovered, is that you have to keep being honest while you build it. There was no one left to bribe and no report to sign under pressure, and I kept catching myself reaching for the old reflexes anyway — the instinct, when a cost came in high, to find the cheapest truth available and dress it as the whole one. The trestle collapse lived in me the way a bullet lives near a spine: not in the way, but always there, reminding me which way I could still fall.

In May we found soft ground at the second crossing — alluvial silt over old channel fill, exactly the kind of bed that had eaten the trestle I'd once certified. The young surveyor on the crew, a local man named Wari who had taught me more Tok Pisin than I deserved, brought me the borings and waited for my call.

I looked at the numbers for a long time. Longer than they needed.

"You want to know if I'm going to do what the last engineer did," I said finally.

Wari, who had heard the story from Tomas, who had heard it from everyone, shrugged the shrug of a man too young for diplomacy. "Everyone wants to know that."

"Pile it deeper. Six meters past spec. It'll cost three weeks."

"It'll cost my crew three weeks of mosquitoes."

"It'll cost them nothing if it holds, and everything if it doesn't. Six meters. And put my name on the boring log."

He wrote it down. Then he grinned. "You know, Mr. Iversen, the old men say a man's name on paper is like a crocodile's eyes on the water. Everything else can hide. That part can't."

"Your old men," I said, "should write survey manuals."

* * *

Mara and I had not, in any formal sense, discussed what we were. We had discussed channel hydraulics, culvert sizing, the tribunal's appeal timeline, whether the cook's dog was entitled to a share of the tinned beef. What we were had assembled itself the way her charts did: one accurate observation at a time, no line drawn until the evidence was overwhelming, and then drawn in ink because pencil would have been cowardice.

The evidence had become overwhelming somewhere around the second month, in a manner I could date precisely, because I dated things now. It was the evening I caught myself leaving the Cathedral not by the door nearest my bunk but by the door nearest hers, a detour of no engineering merit whatsoever, and understood that I had been taking it for six weeks and calling it habit.

That night she was re-inking the river's old channel, the one that would come back, and she said, without looking up, "I've been offered a chair. Copenhagen. Coastal dynamics. Starting in the autumn."

The pencil in my hand did not stop moving, because I had spent a lifetime making sure my hands never testified. "Good chair?"

"Good chair."

"Good city?"

"Good city. Rain, though. Nothing like this rain." She set down her pen. "I haven't answered."

I kept drafting. The culvert on sheet fourteen was getting very well detailed.

"Casper."

"Hm."

"Ask me the question you're not asking."

I set the pencil down. Precision is the only religion I've kept, and there are moments when even a religion requires you to stop working. "I'm not asking anything," I said, "because I spent thirty years learning that everything I love gets taken away by something I signed, and I've only just stopped signing things I don't believe in, and I don't want to start the other way around — asking someone to stay before I've proven I'm worth staying for. The railway opens in November. Ask me then. If I'm still here."

"You'll be here."

"You don't know that."

"I charted you," Mara said, going back to her inking, "from the first morning at the staging camp. You read water like a man apologizing to it. Men like that don't leave rivers they've finally been allowed to fix." She drew a line, sure and blue. "But I'll ask you in November anyway. I like watching you try to answer."

* * *

The dry season came, and with it the first rails.

They were secondhand — reclaimed from a plantation line in Madang, rusted but true, and the crews handled them like relics, which in a sense they were. The first length went down on a morning so clear the mountains stood up out of the haze like witnesses, and the whole valley seemed to attend. Wari drove the first spike. Then an old woman named Kapiak, whose father was ledger entry forty-one, *expended*, drove the second, and the sound of it went through me like the sound of the trestle going down, except inverted — a collapse run backward, a fall undone.

Tomas stood beside me with his cane in the ballast and said nothing for a long time. Then: "My grandfather walked this ground carrying stone. He didn't know he was building a railway."

"He wasn't," I said. "He was building a grave they didn't mark. We're just finishing the paperwork."

"That's a terrible thing to say."

"It's the only true thing I know how to say about railroads."

He considered that. "Then say it at the opening ceremony. The minister will hate it."

"I'm counting on it."

* * *

In September, a letter arrived from Port Moresby, tribunal letterhead, and I read it standing in the Cathedral with the rain starting its sermon on the roof. The reparations trust, it said, wished to record its formal acknowledgment of the survey records entered into evidence — my records, authenticated, permanent, the first honest signature of my life now part of the public archive — and further wished to inquire whether the engineer responsible would accept a standing appointment: chief engineer of record for the memorial corridor, river and rail, in perpetuity, or as close to perpetuity as government funding allowed.

In perpetuity. The words of a man who owns an address.

I carried the letter to Mara's table and put it down on her charts, on the blue lines of the river going home.

"They want an answer by October," I said.

"It's September."

"I know how calendars work."

"You're doing the thing where you check the document for errors so you don't have to check yourself."

"The document has no errors. That's the problem." I sat down across from her. "Every address I've ever had, I left before it could be taken. Every name I used, I used so it could be discarded. And now they're offering me the one thing I spent my whole life avoiding — permanence, on paper, in a place where everyone knows exactly who I am and what I did and what I failed to do — and the terrifying thing, Mara, the genuinely terrifying thing, is that my only hesitation is that it took them this long to ask."

She looked at me for a moment the way she looked at a channel she was about to re-chart — measuring, unimpressed by the surface, reading the flow underneath.

"Say yes," she said.

"I'm going to say yes."

"I know. I'm telling you to say it out loud, to me, now, so that in November when I ask my question, I already have the answer to mine."

The rain thickened on the roof. Somewhere down the line, Wari's crew was singing over the spike mauls, the work song of men building something that would outlast them, which is the only kind of building worth the mosquitoes.

"Yes," I said. "I'm staying. I'm staying here, with the river and the line and the dog's share of the tinned beef, and if you ask me in November I will say it again with witnesses."

Mara picked her pen back up, and on the title block of the channel restoration chart — the master drawing, the one that would hang in the provincial archive — she wrote, beneath *M.E., Hydrology* and beneath *C. Iversen, P.E.*, a third line in her small sure hand: *Restoration to continue, in perpetuity, per agreement of the engineers of record.*

She dated it September. She did not date it November. Some evidence, she'd taught me, you enter early, before anyone can contest it.

* * *

The last week of the dry season, we walked the finished grade together, end to end, twenty-two kilometers of open line where my map had shown only closed ones. The rails ran clean through the cut at the second crossing, six meters of pile holding honest ground. At Ferrolanding, the platform was framed and the name board painted, and Ferro himself was there — he'd come up on the first inspection train, gripping the handrail the whole way, and stood on the platform of a station bearing his name and wept without embarrassment, which is the hardest engineering feat I've ever witnessed.

Beyond the platform, the ground fell away to the river — the old channel, the true channel, and Mara stood at the edge with her chart case under her arm, watching the water that would, next wet season, begin coming home.

"It'll take three floods," she said. "Maybe four. The river has to relearn the way."

"Rivers are slow learners."

"Rivers are *honest* learners. They only remember what's real." She glanced at me. "That's why I stopped arguing with them, eventually. You can't out-argue something that's only telling the truth."

Downstream, the water ran full and quiet over ground that remembered everything. And I stood on my own name's worth of solid ground beside the woman who had charted me, on a line that was open, going nowhere except back — back to the adit, back to the ledgers, back to the forty-one names and the four hundred more — which is to say, going forward, the only direction a memorial can travel.

The wet season was six weeks out. The first flood of the restoration would come with it, and Mara wanted to be on the water for every hour of it, gauges and current meters and that look she got when the river did something the official maps said was impossible.

I had already booked the boat. Both of us on it. In ink.

It was, I reflected, walking back along the rails as the evening train — the first one, a single inspection car with the descendants of the expended riding in it like royalty — sounded its whistle behind us, the strangest thing that had ever happened to me: for the first time in my life, everything ahead of me was scheduled.

And then, at the platform, Wari came running up the grade with a telegram flapping in his hand and a look on his face that I recognized from a lifetime of reading trouble at a distance, and I learned that the past, too, keeps a timetable.
