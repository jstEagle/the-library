## 40. Monsoon

The first rain of the season came at four in the morning, and I woke before it landed.

I don't know how to explain that to anyone who hasn't spent years listening to water for a living — the way the air changes its mind an hour before the sky does, the pressure dropping like a held breath finally let go, the frogs going quiet in that particular way that means they know something you don't yet. I lay on my back under the mosquito net and listened to the wind come down off the high ground through the kunai grass, and I thought: here it is.

Then it hit the tin roof like applause.

I got up and made coffee on the wood stove because the generator was off and I liked it that way, and I carried the cup out onto the porch I had built with my own hands — badly, at first, then better after Mara redrew my measurements the way she redraws everything, patient and merciless — and I sat down to watch the monsoon arrive over the Sepik Basin like a debt coming due.

The river was already up. It had been climbing for two weeks, brown and muscular, carrying whole islands of floating vegetation downstream past the memorial railway's new trestle. By noon it would be over the lower survey road. By evening, if the rain held, it would reach the old gauge station foundations and run full over the ground where we'd found Ferro's grave marker, where Tomas had knelt in the mud, where Hargreave had stood in the smoke of his own burning camp and finally told the truth about what he knew and when he'd known it.

The water would cover all of it. The water always covers it.

That used to be the problem. Now I'm not so sure.

* * *

People ask me sometimes — usually engineers passing through on the rehabilitation project, young men and women with clean boots and government letterhead — why I stayed. They mean it politely. Wewak is a place people pass through; the Sepik is a place people are posted to; nobody is supposed to choose either one voluntarily, least of all a man who once had a name that opened doors on three continents and traded it away for a porch in the wet season.

I tell them different things depending on how much truth they can carry. To the ones who just want a story for the mess hall, I say the fishing is good and the beer is cold and a man gets tired of airports. That's true as far as it goes, which is about as far as most of them want it to go.

To the rare one who asks twice — who looks at me the way Mara looked at me the first week, like a map with a fold in it that doesn't match the terrain — I say this:

I kept moving for eleven years because stopping cost more than moving. Every town I stopped in, somebody eventually ran my name, or something close enough to my name, and the trestle came up, and the report I signed came up, and the seventeen people on the morning train came up, and I had to become someone else again by nightfall. Moving was cheap. Staying was ruin. So I stayed nowhere, and I told myself that was freedom, the way a man tells himself a cell with the door open is a room he chose.

And then one year I stopped anyway. Not because the cost went down. Because I finally found something that cost more than staying.

You can't put that on a plaque. But it's true, and the rain doesn't care whether it's tidy.

* * *

Mara came out around six, wrapped in a blanket, hair still damp from washing in the rain barrel because she says roof water tastes better than tank water and I've stopped arguing with her about anything that involves her senses being right. She's almost always right. It's one of the things I love about her and one of the things that made our first year together feel like living inside a peer review.

She sat down next to me without speaking, which is her preferred form of greeting, and we watched the light come up gray-green through the rain.

"River's at nine-two," she said. "Gauge read it at five."

"You checked the gauge at five?"

"I checked the gauge at five."

"Of course you did." I handed her my coffee, which is also part of the liturgy. She drank half and gave it back, and we watched the water.

The channel charts she drew — the ones the official maps still get wrong, the ones the tribunal used to reconstruct exactly where the company had cut the diversion and buried the adit approach — hang framed above her drafting table now, three of them, annotated in her small fierce handwriting. There's a fourth frame, empty, waiting. She says it's for the restored route, when the diversion is finally reversed and the river is allowed to go home to its old bed. The engineering study is funded. The hearings start after the wet season. Whether the water actually returns is a question even she won't bet on, and she'll bet on anything involving flow rates.

"The Kessler appeal ruling comes down Thursday," she said, after a while.

"I know."

"You haven't said what you think it'll be."

"I think lawyers are like rivers," I said. "They find the lowest ground and they stay there until something bigger moves them."

"And you're not worried."

It wasn't quite a question, so I didn't answer it right away. The rain thickened, drumming the tin, running off the eaves in silver ropes. Down at the landing, our boat — the same hull we came out of the sudd in, patched twice and repainted once, named *Ferro* against the doctor's strenuous objections — strained at its lines against the current.

"I'm not worried," I said finally. "Roos is indicted, the projects are frozen, the ledgers are in the archive with a chain of custody the investigators would defend with their lives because I made them build it that way. Whatever the appeal does, it can't un-ring that bell. And if it could—" I shrugged. "There are forty-one witnesses now. Villagers who walked the old rail bed when walking it could have gotten them killed. You don't bury that many voices twice. Even money can't afford it anymore."

"That's almost optimistic."

"Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation."

She laughed, low, and leaned into my shoulder, and for a while we just sat there being rained on adjacently, which is most of what marriage is, in my experience.

* * *

Tomas came by mid-morning, poling a dugout through the flooded track because the road was gone again, grinning like the water was a personal gift to him. He's twenty-four now and runs the memorial railway's community liaison office, which means he spends his days collecting names — the names of the laborers listed as *expended* in the company ledgers, matched against village memory, mission baptismal records, anything that will turn a column of dead men back into grandfathers. He has recovered three hundred and six names so far. He keeps the list in a waterproof case and sleeps with it within arm's reach, and he has never once, in two years, complained about the work, though I have seen him cry over it twice and both times pretended I hadn't.

He climbed onto the porch dripping, accepted coffee, and delivered his news with the gravity of a man announcing weather: the elders from the villages upstream were coming down after the flood peak for the dedication. All of them. Even old Yawan, who is ninety-one and swore after the tribunal that he was done with boats.

"They want to see the trestle," Tomas said. "The new one. With the names on it."

"It's just a bridge," I said, because someone has to say that kind of thing out loud so the rest of us remember it's allowed to be ordinary.

"No," Tomas said, patiently, the way he explains things to engineers, "it's the first thing anyone ever built there that wasn't built to take something out. Everything else — the mine, the rail line, the diversion — it was all built to take something out and hide where it went. This is the first thing built to bring something back." He looked out at the brown water. "My grandfather helped build the old spur. Under guard. Nobody ever wrote down that he was good at it. The new drawings have his name in the corner. Surveyor's assistant, grade alignment. Mara put him there."

"He earned the corner," Mara said from her chair, not looking up from the chart she was correcting.

Tomas smiled and finished his coffee and poled back out into the flood, and I sat there thinking about corners of drawings, which is not where I expected to end up at fifty-eight, and finding I had no complaints.

* * *

In the afternoon the rain eased to a steady silver fall and I walked the property, because a man should inspect what he owns, even if what he owns is a house, a shed full of survey instruments, a boat, and eleven hectares of ground that remembers things.

The instruments are the part that would have made my younger self laugh hardest. I own a theodolite again. A real one, serial-numbered, registered to Casper Iversen, purchased legally, insured. For eleven years I did survey work under borrowed names with borrowed equipment, good enough work that nobody asked questions, and the whole time I treated every instrument like contraband because in a sense it was — evidence of me, existing somewhere I couldn't control. Now there's a cabinet in the shed with my name stenciled on it and I leave the door unlocked, and some mornings I stand there a beat too long looking at the stencil, the way a man looks at a scar that finally stopped hurting.

The ground remembers too. I've learned to read it the way Mara reads channels. There's a slight depression behind the house where the old portage trail ran — the impossible route, the closed rail spur we carried witnesses along while Roos's men burned the station behind us. In the dry season you can still find rail spikes in the soil if you walk it slowly. Kids from the village collect them. There's a jar of them on Mara's shelf, maybe sixty spikes, and she refuses to let anyone melt them down or sell them to the scrap buyers who come through asking. "Evidence," she says. Of what, exactly, she declines to specify, and I've decided that's between her and the spikes.

At the fence line I stopped where I always stop, at the post we set the day after the tribunal verdict, facing the river. There's no plaque on it. There was talk of a plaque — there is always talk of plaques — but the elders decided the river itself was the marker, and the post is just a post, a place to stand and face the water and do whatever a person needs to do facing water. I've seen villagers stand there. I've seen Tomas stand there. Once, in the first year, I saw Dr. Ferro — recovered, gaunt, permanently feverish-eyed — stand there for an hour in the rain, and then walk back up to the house and eat an entire pot of rice without saying a word, and that was the closest we ever came to discussing what he'd hidden from in the fuel drums while the radios were smashed and the buyer's floatplane circled.

I stood there a while myself. The river ran full and brown and indifferent, carrying the season downstream, covering the old bed, uncovering new banks, doing what rivers do, which is everything and apologize for none of it.

Somewhere under that water, thirty kilometers up, is the sealed adit. The tunnel entrance was concrete-sealed by court order after the evidence recovery — the ledgers and photographs and remains brought out with chain-of-custody tags on every item, my authenticated survey records cross-referencing the wartime rail spurs to the tunnel approach, the whole horror catalogued and archived and entered into the permanent record under my actual name. The bones went home to the villages. The ledgers went to the tribunal and copies to the families. The tunnel itself stays shut, a wound kept closed until the doctors decide how to treat it.

Some nights I dream about it. Not the chamber — I only went in once, and the bureaucratic horror of it, the neat columns and the execution photographs filed like invoices, is more than enough for one lifetime. What I dream about is the rails. Rusted rails running back into the dark, matching my closed-line map line for line, the map I carried for eleven years like a talisman of everything that had been erased, never knowing that the erasure and the map were the same document. The men who paid to close those lines thought closing them was forgetting. It wasn't. It was filing. And files can be reopened.

I'm the one who reopened them. Some mornings that feels like redemption. Most mornings it just feels like work, which is probably healthier.

* * *

Evening. The rain settled into its long overnight rhythm, and Mara cooked, and we ate on the porch because the kitchen floods ankle-deep in monsoon season and we've stopped pretending otherwise. Rice, fish from the flooded grasslands, chili enough to make her happy. She talked about the reversal project — the diversion gates, the modeling runs, the argument she's having with a hydrologist in Canberra via letters that take three weeks each way and get increasingly personal. I listened, mostly, and watched the lightning walk up the basin in long silent strokes, counting the seconds to thunder out of habit.

After dark she spread her charts on the dry half of the table and worked by lamplight, and I read, and the rain played the roof like it had all season to practice, which it does.

"Casper," she said, near ten o'clock, not looking up.

"Mm."

"The empty frame. On the wall."

"What about it?"

"When the river comes back to the old bed — if it comes back — I want to survey the restored channel myself. First transit, end to end. Our boat, your navigation, my charts." She finally looked up. "It'll take a month. Maybe six weeks, if the sudd regrows in the old reach, which it will."

I set down my book. Outside, the rain kept on, patient as arithmetic.

"You're asking if I'll stay put long enough to finish it," I said.

"I'm asking if you'll come."

"Same question, wearing different clothes."

She smiled. "Everything worth asking is."

I looked out at the water running black and full past the landing, over the drowned track, toward the ground that remembers. Eleven years I measured my life in departures — how fast, how clean, how little left behind. It took a collapsed trestle, a massacre, a monsoon, and a woman who argues with rivers to teach me the other unit of measurement: what a man is willing to be responsible for. A porch. A name on drawings. A jar of rail spikes. An empty frame waiting for a river's homecoming.

"Yeah," I said. "I'll come."

"Good." She went back to her chart. Then, a minute later, in the tone she saves for corrections she knows I won't like: "We leave the day the flood drops. Which means the hearing about the diversion gates—"

"—is the week we're on the water. I know. I read the schedule."

"You hate public hearings."

"I hate them slightly less than I love you. It was close, some years."

She laughed, and the lamp flickered as the generator coughed, and the rain came down harder, and I sat on the porch I owned in the house I owned under the name I'd been born with, listening to the monsoon fill the basin the way it has filled it since before there were maps, since before there were men who counted bodies as ballast, since before there was anything to remember except water finding its way downhill and keeping at it.

The river runs full tonight over ground that remembers. Come morning it will run fuller. And I will be here to watch it — here, of all the places on earth, in the one spot my old life would have called a trap and my new one calls an address.

Stillness, it turns out, isn't the absence of movement. It's movement you've chosen, in a direction you believe in, sustained long enough to grow roots. The river taught me that. Or maybe the woman charting it did. Either way, I finally understand what all that moving was for: it was the long way around to this porch, this rain, this life.

The thunder moved off upstream. The rain kept falling. And somewhere out in the dark, the water rose another inch over the old bed, patient, persistent, going home — and for the first time in as long as I can remember, so was I.
