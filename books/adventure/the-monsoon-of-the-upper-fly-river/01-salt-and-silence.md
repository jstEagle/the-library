## 1. Salt and Silence

The salt flats don't forgive mistakes, which is why I like them.

Out here a person is exactly as good as their water discipline, their boot leather, and their ability to read ground that wants to be read by no one. No politics. No ledgers. No consortium men in pressed linen asking me to remember what I owe them. Just the white glare to the horizon and the small honest arithmetic of survival: three days of water left, two if the wind stays up, one if I'm stupid.

I try not to be stupid. It's the only vow I've ever kept without breaking.

The thief had crossed here two days ahead of me — maybe three. I'd picked up the trail at the last well before the flats, where a woman matching Wren Calloway's description had paid for water with consortium scrip, which was either arrogance or a joke, and with thieves it's usually both. From there: boot prints in the alkali crust at the flats' edge, a campsite with the fire pit buried so carefully that most trackers would have walked past it. Most trackers aren't paid by the mile to notice things.

She was good. I'll say that much now, at the start, because by the end of this story I'll be saying worse things about her and better ones too, and it's only fair to establish early that competence was the first thing I noticed about Wren Calloway, before anything else got in the way.

The prints told a story I didn't like. She traveled light but not desperate — no scattered gear, no panic-stride, no doubling back except once, and that once was deliberate, a false trail laid across a pan of crusted salt where she knew her tracks would show. She'd walked a half-mile east, then circled wide on bare stone and gone north. That's not flight. That's a person who knows someone is behind them and has opinions about how that someone should spend their time.

I stood on the false trail for a long moment, sweating through my shirt in air hot enough to bake bread, and felt something I hadn't felt on a hunt in years.

Respect. And under it, faint as water under salt, doubt.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about me, since we're going to be traveling together for nine more chapters and lying to each other gets exhausting:

My name is Astrid Yarrow. I hold a ranger's commission from the Port Meridian Mining Consortium, which sounds impressive until you understand what it means. It means I'm a dog. A well-trained, well-equipped dog, sent out after anyone who runs off with something the consortium has decided belongs to it. Water rights. Survey maps. Deeds signed by people who couldn't read them. Last year I tracked down a boy of nineteen who'd stolen a cistern's worth of drinking water for his mother's village, and I brought him back in chains, and the consortium fined his village into ruin for the crime of being thirsty.

I did it because I have to. Not want to — have to. There's a difference, and the difference has a name, and the name is my brother.

Elias Yarrow took fever when he was twenty-two and I was twenty-five, and the fever settled into his lungs and stayed there like a tenant who pays nothing and breaks everything. The doctors in Port Meridian could keep him alive. Keeping him alive costs more per month than a ranger earns in a year. The consortium covered it — every treatment, every night in the sanatorium with its clean sheets and its oxygen pipes — and in exchange, I signed a debt-bond that makes me theirs until the ledger says otherwise. Which, at current rates, is never.

People call it loyalty when they see me ride out. They're being polite. It's a leash, and I've worn it so long I sometimes mistake the pull of it for my own direction.

Elias doesn't know the terms. He thinks I'm out here because I love the work, and God help me, some mornings, alone on the flats with the sun coming up like a wound reopening, I almost do. The desert doesn't lie to you. It just kills you honestly, which is more than I can say for Port Meridian.

* * *

By late afternoon I'd gained on her. The salt gave way to broken ground at the flats' northern edge — shattered shale and dry washes running down toward the green line of the Fly River country — and her trail turned from careful to hurried. Something had spooked her. Or something ahead mattered more than whoever was behind.

I found her second camp in the shadow of an overhang, and this time she hadn't bothered hiding the fire. That bothered me more than the first camp's cleverness. A tracker reads fires the way priests read scripture, and hers said: *I was here, I ate, I slept four hours, I left before dawn moving fast.* No fear in it. Purpose. She wasn't running from Port Meridian anymore. She was running toward something.

And there was this: tucked under a stone where any sensible fugitive would have left nothing at all, a scrap of paper, weighted against the wind.

I should have burned it unread. A ranger collects evidence; a ranger doesn't read other people's mail on the trail. But I'm telling you the truth of this story, and the truth is that I crouched there with the wind pulling at my collar and I unfolded it, and it wasn't mail. It was a page torn from a document — consortium letterhead, survey division, the kind of paper that isn't supposed to leave the archive room in Meridian's basement — and the visible text read:

*...resettlement of indigenous settlements within the Upper Fly catchment to proceed upon completion of hydrological works. Relocation sites designated. Compensation not applicable.*

Compensation not applicable. Six words, in the flat bureaucratic voice the consortium uses when it's saying something monstrous. Somewhere upstream of me, past the gorges, past the country the maps mark with warnings instead of names, people were going to be moved off land they'd held longer than the consortium had existed, and the company had decided they weren't even worth the price of a bribe.

I sat back on my heels. The wind came off the flats behind me, carrying that smell salt gets at dusk, like old tears dried into stone.

Wren Calloway hadn't stolen money. Thieves steal money. She'd stolen proof — and proof of what? That the source valley, the forbidden one, the one the consortium's own charts showed as blank white space with a red border and the words *NO ENTRY — SURVEY PENDING*, wasn't empty at all. That it was full of people whose existence was inconvenient enough to erase from paper before erasing them from the earth.

Every instinct I had said: this changes nothing. Your job is the job. Find her, take the documents, bring her in, collect whatever credit knocks a few months off the bond. Elias needs his oxygen. Sentiment doesn't pump air into lungs.

But I looked at that scrap of paper a long time, and then I did something I'd never done on a hunt before.

I put it in my pocket instead of my evidence satchel.

* * *

I caught sight of her at dusk, and I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last thing I had left that night.

She was perhaps half a mile ahead, a slim figure moving along the lip of a dry wash where the ground began its long descent toward the river country, and the light was doing that thing it does at the end of a day on the salt — turning everything gold and then rose and then the color of a bruise healing. She moved well. Economical, no wasted motion, the walk of someone who learned distance young.

I had her. Wind at my back, terrain favoring me, a full canteen and ten years of practice. In another hour I'd close the gap. In another hour after that, it would be done, and I'd ride back to Meridian with my prisoner and my recovered property and Captain Hale would clap me on the shoulder and the ledger would shrink by some fraction of a percent, and Elias would breathe another month of clean air bought with a girl's freedom.

I drew rein — I'd traded my truck for mules at the last station; trucks die on the shale — and I watched her, and I hesitated.

Not because of the paper. Well. Not only because of the paper.

Because of the direction she was going. North and east, upriver, straight toward the forbidden country. Nobody flees *into* the place the consortium fears. Fugitives run away from power, toward borders, toward towns with loose law. Running toward the source meant the documents weren't cargo to her. They were a delivery. Someone up there needed to see them — the people in the resettlement memorandum, the ones marked *compensation not applicable*. She was carrying a warning.

And Tomas Reyes's name surfaced in my head unbidden, the way it had been surfacing for two days, ever since the well-keeper mentioned a prospector heading upcountry ahead of the girl, an old man everyone assumed was chasing one last vein. Old Tomas, who'd spent forty years poking at the Upper Fly and come down every season with less to show and more to drink. If he was up there, and if she was going to him—

No. That was speculation, and speculation is just hope wearing a uniform.

What I knew was this: I had a trail, a target, and a duty, and all three pointed the same direction, and I sat on my mule in the failing light and could not make my hands pick up the reins.

The debt made me a dog. But dogs hesitate too, sometimes, at the end of a chain, when the scent goes somewhere the master didn't point.

I followed her. Of course I followed her. Elias was breathing in Meridian and the ledger doesn't take hesitation as payment. But I followed slowly, and I told myself it was tactics — let her lead me through the hard country, let her draw the snakes and the crocodiles and whatever else the river kept — and I knew, even as I thought it, that I was lying, and that the lie was the first crack in a wall I'd been building for six years.

That night I camped cold, no fire, watching the dark line of the river country breathe on the horizon. Storm clouds were stacking up over the far ranges, fat and blue-black, weeks early. The monsoon was coming, and everyone in the lowlands pretended it wasn't.

In my pocket, the torn page rustled against my ribs like something alive.

Two days later, at a crossing choked with reeds, I found out what the thief had been carrying besides paper — and why an old prospector's dying words would send us both upriver together, whether we liked it or not.

But that's getting ahead of the story. Out on the salt, that first night, there was only the wind, the waiting clouds, and a woman's footprints pointing north toward the one place on earth the consortium had drawn a red line around — and me, crossing it, alone, with my badge in my pocket and my conscience starting, very quietly, to speak.
