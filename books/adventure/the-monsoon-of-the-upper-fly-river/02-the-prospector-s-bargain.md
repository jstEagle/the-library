## 2. The Prospector's Bargain

The camp had a name once—Copper Latch, according to the sign that hung by one nail from the last standing post of what had been a gate. The other three posts were gone, and the sign had given up pretending. COPPER LA read clearly enough. The rest of it had curled into the sun like a dead leaf.

I'd been following the thief's trail for six days, and Copper Latch was where the trail got strange.

Strange, in my trade, usually means one of two things: either you're being played, or someone ahead of you is dying. I've learned to bet on death. It's more honest than people.

I came in from the east with the wind at my back, which is the only way to come into a camp full of old mining spoil if you want to smell anything besides rust and regret. The place was a scatter of collapsed tents, a headframe leaning like a drunk against its own shadow, and a slag heap that some optimist had terraced years ago and then abandoned to the saltbush. No smoke. No dogs. But there was a mule tethered in the shade of the headframe, ribs showing like a washboard, and a mule means a person, and a person in a place like this means trouble or a story.

Usually both.

I circled wide, the way you do, and found the story before I found the person. There was blood on the stones outside the largest tent—not a lot, but old enough to have gone brown, and dragged, as though someone had tried to crawl and thought better of it. A rifle lay nearby with its action open and empty. Somebody had fired everything they had and missed, or hit, depending on which side of the arithmetic you stood on.

I drew my knife instead of my gun. Guns are for people who plan on talking afterward.

"Tomas Reyes," I said to the tent flap, because I'd seen the name stenciled on a crate by the door, and because men who live alone out here like to be reminded they exist.

Nothing. Then, faintly, a sound like wet gravel turning over.

He was inside, propped against a bedroll that had been dragged halfway across the tent floor, one hand pressed to his belly, the other hand gripping a canvas-wrapped bundle like it was the last plank of a sinking ship. He was sixty if he was a day, sun-cured to the color of old rope, and his eyes had the particular shine of a man whose body has started making its final arrangements without consulting him.

"Gut-shot," he said, before I could say anything. "Three days. Or four. Lost count."

"Who did it?"

"Does it matter?" He managed something that was almost a smile. "You're not the law."

"I'm worse. I'm a ranger." I knelt beside him, keeping my hands visible, an old habit with strangers and a newer habit with dying ones. "Astrid Yarrow. Port Meridian charter."

"Yarrow." His eyes moved over me, cataloguing—boots, badge, the dust of six days' hard riding. "They said a ranger was hunting upriver. Chasing a thief."

"News travels."

"Not news. Rumor. There's a difference." He coughed, and the cough cost him something I could see he couldn't afford. "Rumors travel faster. And further."

Water first. I gave him water from my flask, and he drank like a man rationing even now, small measured sips, and I understood this was a person who had spent his whole life making things last. Then I looked at the wound properly, because looking is part of the job whether you can fix anything or not. It was bad. It was the kind of bad where the kindest thing you can do is stop pretending there's a kind thing to do.

"You need a surgeon," I said. "Nearest one's Port Meridian. Four days hard ride."

He laughed, and it turned into the cough again. "Four days. Listen to her." He patted the bundle in his lap. "I have something better than a surgeon."

"Everyone says that right up until they don't."

"This one's true." He worked at the canvas wrapping with fingers that shook, and I confess I helped him, mostly so he wouldn't die mid-gesture with whatever it was half-revealed, which struck me as an undignified way to go. Inside the canvas was oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth was paper—a sheet of heavy stock, soft at the edges from handling, covered edge to edge in ink. Lines. Elevations. Contours drawn by a steady hand, annotated in a cramped, patient script.

It took me a moment to understand what I was looking at, and when I did, I stopped breathing for a second, which is not a professional reaction, and I noted it as such.

It was a map of the Upper Fly River. Not the survey maps the consortium sells to investors—the ones that end, conveniently, at the third gorge with a decorative flourish labeled TERRA INCOGNITA, as if the river simply lost interest beyond that point. This map went past the gorges. Past the falls. All the way up to the source itself, drawn in detail no survey party had ever come back to confirm, because no survey party had ever come back.

"How," I said.

"I drew it." He said it simply, the way you'd admit to a scar. "Forty-one years ago. I was twenty-three and stupid and the consortium paid well for stupid." His hand drifted toward the map's upper edge, to a basin sketched there with a care that bordered on tenderness. "There's a spring-fed valley up there. Green in the middle of all that rock. People living in it who never asked to be on anyone's map."

"People."

"Kessari folk. Have been since before Port Meridian had a name." He looked at me then, and the shine in his eyes wasn't delirium. It was intent. "I sold them, girl. Drew their valley for men with dynamite and ledgers, and I told myself it was just ink. Forty-one years I've been telling myself that."

"And now?"

"Now the consortium's going back." His grip found my wrist, and for a gut-shot man he had a prospector's strength, the kind that comes from hauling rock your whole life. "New survey. New ledgers. Same dynamite. They mean to take the source, and they mean for nobody to know there was ever anyone there to take it from."

"That's not my warrant," I said. "My warrant is a thief. Stolen documents. Water rights."

"Is it?" Something flickered across his face—amusement, maybe, or pain, or the two wearing each other's clothes. "Then why are you still sitting here?"

Because the thief's trail ran through this camp. Because whoever had shot Tomas Reyes had come through within the last day, maybe less, heading the same direction I'd been heading for six days. Upstream. Toward the gorges. Toward everything this map described. I didn't say any of that. Dying men collect information the way magpies collect bottle glass, and I wasn't sure yet what he'd do with mine.

"What do you want from me?" I asked.

"The obvious." He pushed the map into my hands. It was heavier than paper should be, the way guilt is heavier than ink. "Monsoon comes early this year—I can feel it in my knees, and my knees have called every monsoon for forty years. When it breaks, the passes seal. Nobody goes up, nobody comes down, until it's over, and by then—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Carry it upstream. To the source. Before the rain."

"To whom?"

"Anyone who's there. Anyone who'll listen." His breath was getting shorter between words, each one costing more than the last. "The Kessari have a right to know what's coming for them. That's all. That's the whole of it. A warning, delivered. Forty-one years late."

"I'm not a courier. I'm a ranger under charter to the consortium you're asking me to warn against."

"Yes," he said. "That's rather the point, isn't it?"

I should have refused. Let me be clear about that, since I'm the one telling this story: I knew exactly what I should have done. Take the map as evidence, log the location, send word downriver, let the machinery of law grind along its usual slow and purchased way. That was the procedure. Procedure has kept me alive through things that should have killed me, and I trust it the way other people trust God.

But Tomas Reyes was looking at me with forty-one years of self-deception burning off him like morning fog, and underneath it was something plainer. A man who wanted, once, before the end, to have done one thing with clean hands.

"Say it," he whispered. "Say you'll carry it. Out loud. Words matter. I learned that too late."

And here is the thing about promises: they're light as air right up until they're not. I opened my mouth intending to say something careful, something procedural, something with an exit built into it—and what came out was:

"I'll carry it upstream. Before the monsoon seals the passes. On my word."

His whole body changed. The tension went out of him like water finding its level, and he sagged back against the bedroll, and he smiled—a real one this time, crooked and relieved, the smile of a man setting down a load he'd carried longer than I'd been alive.

"Good," he said. "Good. Now get out of here. Whoever shot me will come back for the map, and they won't knock."

"Who shot you?"

He waved the question away with two fingers, a gesture that cost him visibly. "Names are just debts with faces. I'm done collecting." His eyes were drifting now, going soft at the edges. "Deliver the map. Forget my name. That's the bargain entire."

"It's a terrible bargain. You're getting nothing."

"I'm getting everything." He closed his eyes. "I'm getting out."

* * *

I stayed long enough to make him comfortable, which is a phrase that means exactly nothing and everything. Water within reach. The rifle unloaded and broken, so no one could use it against him or him against himself. His mule turned loose with enough feed to keep it honest for a day or two—it would wander down to the lower camps eventually; mules always know the way to people.

He was still breathing when I stepped out of the tent. The light had gone long and copper-colored, the way it does before weather, and I stood in the ruins of Copper Latch and looked north, up the line of the river, where the first gorge cut a black seam into the hills.

The thief's trail came through here. Fresh boot prints over Reyes's older bloodstains, heading upstream, unhurried. Whoever they were, they hadn't done the shooting—they'd arrived after, same as me, and stood over the same blood, and read the same story. And then they'd kept going. Upstream. Toward the source. Toward the passes that would seal with the first hard rain.

Toward the same destination written in faded ink on the paper now folded against my heart.

I checked my gear, topped off my waterskins from the camp cistern, and gave the tent one last look. From inside came the sound of breathing—slow, spaced far apart, the tide going out.

"Nobody," I said quietly, to no one, to him. "You died naming no names. Fine. I'll find them myself."

I put Copper Latch behind me and walked north into the copper light, carrying a dead man's promise and a thief's trail, both pointing the same direction, and understood—with the clarity that only arrives when it's least convenient—that I had just made a promise I might have to break, to a company I already owed, on behalf of a stranger who trusted me more than I trusted myself.

Behind me, very faint, the wind changed.

It smelled, for the first time in months, like rain.
