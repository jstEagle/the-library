## 3. Thief in the Reeds

The river announced itself before I saw it—a green smell, a change in the birds, the ground going soft under my boots like it had opinions about my weight. After four days on the salt flats, where the only sound was my own blood ticking in my ears, the Upper Fly's tributaries came as a shock. Water everywhere, all of it moving with somewhere to be.

I'd picked up Wren Calloway's trail at dawn and lost it twice by noon. The thief was good. Better than good—there were stretches where I tracked them mostly by faith and spite, reading bent grasses and a footprint that seemed to apologize for existing even as it mocked me. Small feet. Light stride. A habit of stepping on stone whenever stone was offered, which told me two things: they knew someone might follow, and they thought that someone would be me.

They weren't wrong.

I crouched at the edge of the reed bank and studied the crossing. The river ran wide here, sluggish and brown-green, thick with silt from the hills upstream. A raft waited on the far side—or rather, half a raft, lashed from bamboo and oil drums, tied to a stunted fig tree with a knot I could read from thirty meters. Someone had built it in a hurry and meant to come back for it. On the near bank: fresh mud prints, a crushed reed bed, and the faint chemical sweetness of lamp oil.

And there, hip-deep in the shallows, pulling the raft across hand over hand on the rope—a figure in a patched canvas coat, hood up, moving with the economy of someone who had done this before and did not intend to die doing it again.

I should have called out. That's procedure. Announce yourself, identify your office, demand compliance. But procedure is for rangers who expect to be obeyed, and I had spent enough years in Port Meridian's pay to know exactly how much obedience my badge bought east of the last surveyor's post. Out here, I was just a woman with a rifle and a warrant nobody had asked for.

So instead of announcing myself, I did what the flats had taught me. I went quiet, and I waited, and I let the thief come to me.

* * *

The rope brought the raft across in slow jerks, drums groaning, water peeling off the bamboo in sheets. The figure hauled it into the shallows, checked the lashings with two sharp tugs, and then—this was the part I remember most clearly—paused. Stood perfectly still in the reeds with their head cocked, listening, the way a heron listens. For a long moment neither of us moved. The river muttered. A kingfisher went past like a thrown blue knife.

Then the hood turned toward my hiding place, and a voice said, conversationally:

"You breathe loud for a professional."

I stood up out of the reeds with the rifle level and felt, absurdly, like I'd been caught doing something wrong. "Ranger Yarrow," I said. "Port Meridian authority. You're wanted for theft."

"I know what I'm wanted for." The hood came back.

The face underneath was younger than I expected—young enough to make me feel old, which I resented immediately. Sharp jaw, sunburnt cheekbones, dark eyes set close together and absolutely unimpressed by my rifle. A woman, mid-twenties maybe, with a scar through one eyebrow that looked self-inflicted by impatience. She kept her hands visible, loose at her sides, and she looked at me the way you look at weather. Something to be endured, possibly exploited.

"Wren Calloway," I said.

"Ranger Yarrow." She said my name like she'd been carrying it around for days and was tired of the weight. "You've been behind me since the salt. I want you to know I admired the persistence right up until this exact moment."

"Hands where I can see them."

"They are where you can see them." She spread her fingers, wiggled them. "Look. Ten fingers, no weapons, standing in a swamp talking politely to the person sent to drag me back to Meridian in chains. If I were you, I'd be asking myself why."

"If I were you," I said, "I'd be asking myself why I'm not running. The river's right there."

"Because the river has teeth, and because you'd shoot me in the back, and because—" she tilted her head, reading me the way I'd been reading her trail for four days, "—you hesitated. Back on the flats. I doubled back at the mirage line and watched you stand over my campfire for ten minutes. You had a clean shot at my trail and you just... stood there. Like a man at a door he isn't sure he wants to open."

My finger tightened fractionally on the guard. She saw it and smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile, but it wasn't a frightened one either.

"What did you steal?" I asked. It wasn't the question I'd planned. The planned question was something authoritative about surrendering documents and coming along peacefully.

"A box," she said. "Paper inside. Consortium seal on it, which is how I know it matters, because nothing with that seal is ever printed for the benefit of anyone downstream of a dividend."

"Theft of consortium property is a flogging offense."

"So is being born in the wrong valley, but nobody's written that statute down yet." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "Ask me what's in the papers, Ranger. You've been dying to since the salt flats. I could see it in the way you followed—not like a dog on a scent. Like a person reading a letter over someone's shoulder."

The river filled the silence between us. And I'll be honest with whoever finds this account, if anyone does: I wanted to know. Four days I'd told myself the job was the job, that Tomas Reyes's map in my coat and Calloway's trail ahead of me were two separate errands that happened to point the same direction, and that coincidence was just the territory being efficient. But I'd stood over that cold campfire on the flats and felt something I hadn't let myself name. Recognition, maybe. Or its ugly cousin, doubt.

"Show me," I said.

She laughed—one short, disbelieving note. "Show you. The ranger. So you can arrest me properly, with evidence."

"So I can decide," I said, "what I'm arresting you for."

That stopped her. For the first time her hands came up—not in surrender, but to push back the hood entirely, and I watched her make some fast internal calculation, the kind I recognized because I made it constantly: trust a stranger or trust the water. She chose me. God help us both.

She waded to the raft, knelt, and pulled an oilskin packet from beneath the deck boards. Inside were documents—real ones, consortium letterhead, survey stamps, signatures I knew by reputation. I read standing in the mud while she watched me with her arms crossed.

The first pages were routine. Hydrological surveys of the Upper Fly's source basin, flow measurements, seasonal projections. It was the third document that put a cold wire down my spine: a census. Not of settlers, not of licensed claims. Of villages. Names I'd never heard—Kessari, Ilan-Moro, a dozen smaller ones—each with population counts, each annotated in a clerk's neat hand with terms like *relocation viable*, *resistance negligible*, *compensation unnecessary given absence of formal title*. And beneath it all, a memorandum recommending that the source valley's inhabitants be moved before the diversion works began, quietly, and before the monsoon sealed the passes and made the valley impossible to reach for six months.

Inhabited. The forbidden source—the one every prospector was warned away from, the one the consortium maps marked with a blank space and the word *unproductive*—was full of people who were about to become blank spaces themselves.

"You knew," Wren said. Not a question. "Or you suspected. That's why you hesitated on the flats. That's why you're standing there looking like someone handed you a live coal." She stepped closer, mud sucking at her boots, close enough that I could have counted her eyelashes. "So here's my offer, Ranger. You can take me back to Meridian and collect whatever bounty they've stapled to my head, and these papers go in a drawer, and next monsoon a dozen valleys empty out and nobody writes it down because the people who lived there couldn't write. Or you can help me get these upstream, past the gorges, before the rains—and spend the rest of your career finding out what the consortium does to rangers who read things."

"That's not an offer," I said. "That's a threat with better manners."

"I'm told I have excellent manners." She held out her hand for the packet.

I didn't give it back. Not yet. "There's a third option," I said. "You tell me everything. Who sent you. What village. Why a thief and not a lawyer."

"Because lawyers cost money and thieves cost only nerve." Her chin came up. "Kessari. My grandmother's village, two days below the source. And nobody sent me—I sent myself, which is why I'm better at it than anyone they could have hired."

It was, I would learn later, almost true. But standing in the reeds with the census in my hands, I believed her completely, and belief is a dangerous thing to hand a woman whose whole life is built on not believing anything until it's signed in triplicate.

That's when the water moved.

* * *

I saw it first—a swell in the flat brown surface, moving against the current, deliberate as a patrol boat. Twenty years in the territory teaches you what that swell means. "Croc," I said. "Off your left. Big one."

Wren went still in a way that told me she'd grown up around these rivers and respected them properly. "How big?"

"Big enough that we discuss it from the raft."

We got there in three strides each, and I'll grant her this: she didn't panic, didn't splash, didn't do any of the hundred things that turn a crocodile's curiosity into its dinner. She pushed the raft off with the pole while I shoved from the bank, and we both came aboard as the wake reached the shallows—a long V of displaced water, and beneath it, briefly visible, a snout the length of my forearm and a body that went on longer than the raft itself.

The estuarine croc surfaced once, twenty meters out. Just its eyes and nostrils, riding high, watching us with the patience of something that has never once needed to hurry. Then it began to follow.

"Pole," Wren said, holding it out. "You're heavier. You pole, I'll paddle, and we don't stop for arguments."

"I'm not heavy."

"You're a ranger who lives on salt beef and stubbornness. Pole."

I poled. She paddled. The croc paced us for a quarter mile, close enough that I could see the ridged armor of its back sliding under the surface like a submerged wall, and then, mercifully, lost interest in bamboo and oil drums and slid off toward a herd of wallabies drinking at the far bank. Neither of us spoke until it was gone.

"Well," Wren said finally, wringing out her sleeve. "That's the first time a crocodile has improved my day."

"It forced you onto a raft with the woman hunting you."

"Exactly. Now you can't arrest me without drowning us both, and I'm an excellent swimmer." She grinned, and up close, with the fear draining out of her face, the grin rearranged everything—it made her look young, and reckless, and like someone I might have liked in another life where I'd been allowed to have friends. "Truce? Until the far bank?"

"Until the far bank," I agreed, and then, because the current was strong and the lie was obvious even to me, "and probably a bit past it."

She laughed at that—a real one, surprised out of her—and something in my chest shifted its weight, uncomfortable as a stone in a boot.

We made the far bank as the light went copper and the first clouds came over the gorge walls upstream, stacked and bruise-dark, weeks early. Wren saw them at the same moment I did, and her grin faded.

"Monsoon's coming," she said. "Early. We need to move tonight, Ranger. Because whatever you've decided about me—" she nodded at the oilskin packet, now tucked inside my coat next to a dead man's map, "—those papers don't survive getting soaked, and neither do we if we're caught in the gorges when the river stands up."

Above us, thunder cleared its throat in the high country, and the river below us began, very quietly, to rise.
