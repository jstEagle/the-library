## 4. What the River Keeps

Here is what nobody tells you about stealing from a consortium: the paperwork is worse than the crime.

I'd spent three days in the Port Meridian records annex pretending to be a clerk's niece with a talent for filing, and what I learned was that men who move rivers keep better ledgers than men who save souls. Every diversion survey, every "resettlement schedule," every euphemism for eviction was copied in triplicate, initialed, and filed under a code so dull no one would ever look twice. *K-7: Hydrological Adjustment, Upper Catchment.* As if you could adjust a river the way you adjust a hat.

I took the folder that mattered and left the copies, because copies can be reprinted and I only had two hands and one grandmother downstream of everything the consortium intended to drown.

That was six weeks ago. Since then I'd been shot at twice, bitten by something in the reeds that I preferred not to identify, and followed — I was nearly sure — by a desert ranger with a face like a closed door and eyes that counted everything.

Which is how I came to be sitting on a raft made of lashed bamboo and bad decisions, sharing it with said ranger, while crocodiles the length of canoes circled us with the patience of creditors.

"You're staring," Astrid Yarrow said. They were poling, because of course they were; they'd taken the pole before I could reach it, the way people take things from you when they've decided you're cargo.

"I'm assessing," I said. "There's a difference."

"Assessing what?"

"Whether you're going to sell me to your masters at the first landing, or whether I'm going to have to push you into the water and let the local wildlife settle the question."

Astrid didn't look back at me. The river ran wide here, brown-green and muscular, sliding between walls of reed and rot-softened timber, and the first gorge opened ahead of us like a throat. Storm light was gathering in the west — not rain yet, just the promise of it, stacked up purple and patient behind the ridgeline. The monsoon was coming early this year. Everyone on the river said so. Everyone on the river had been saying so for a month, the way people say things they're afraid of.

"If I wanted to sell you," Astrid said, "I'd have done it at the crossing, when Hale's patrol boat was still in signaling range."

"You didn't know it was Hale's."

"I knew it was a consortium hull by the wake. Meridian boats ride heavy in the stern. Too many armed men drinking the water ration." A pause. "You noticed too. You went for the reeds before I did."

I had gone for the reeds. I'd been proud of it, right up until the moment the ranger had appeared out of those same reeds ahead of me, which should have been impossible, and put a knife to my collar with a hand steadier than mine.

"I notice things," I said. "It's a family trait. We also hold grudges, but that's not relevant today."

"What's in the folder?"

And there it was. Not a day and a half of uneasy truce, not one shared fire, not even a full night of me deciding whether this person was a jailer or a liability, and already the question. I suppose I couldn't blame them. If I'd spent years chasing a thief across salt flats only to catch her and then get dragged upstream by her, I'd want to know what all of it was for, too.

But wanting to know and being told are different animals, and I'd been raised by a woman who said trust was like a dugout canoe: fine once you're in it, foolish to test in shallow water.

"Paper," I said.

"Paper that cost you a knife fight."

"That wasn't a knife fight. That was an introduction."

The pole dipped, bit, held. The gorge swallowed us. It was narrower than it had looked from the crossing — twenty meters of green water between walls of black stone furred with moss, the canopy closing overhead until the daylight came down in coins. Somewhere above us a bird screamed twice and quit. The air changed. Cooler. Older. Rivers in gorges always feel older to me, like the water remembers being ice.

"The documents," Astrid said again, quieter now, the way people ask things they actually want answered rather than things they want to win. "The ones you stole. I read enough of them, in your pack, when I tied your wrists. Resettlement schedules. Compensation ledgers. Survey coordinates for the Upper Catchment." Another stroke. "Compensation implies there are people to compensate. The consortium's maps say the upper valley is uninhabited."

"The consortium's maps say a lot of things."

"Such as?"

"Such as that Kessari doesn't exist."

I watched their shoulders. People lie with their mouths; shoulders are honest. Astrid Yarrow's shoulders didn't move, but the pole slowed half a beat, and half a beat on a river is a confession.

"Never heard of it," they said.

"Liar," I said, almost fondly. "It's in the compensation ledger you read. Village of Kessari, forty-one households, listed under *affected parties, category C: minimal disruption.* Minimal disruption means they move us somewhere worse and call it generosity. Forty-one households, ranger. My grandmother's among them. She was born on that bend of the river. So was her mother, and her mother's mother, back past anyone's counting. And some man in a Meridian office drew a line through it with a pencil and wrote *minimal* next to it."

The gorge carried my voice away and gave nothing back. Good. I hadn't meant to say all of it. Grandmother says I leak when I'm angry, like a bad seam.

Astrid poled us around a deadfall, smooth as breathing. When they spoke again their voice had that flat quality I was learning to distrust and, against my will, respect.

"Tomas Reyes," they said. "The prospector. Dying camp, three days east of here. Gut-shot. He gave you something too, didn't he? Or told you something. Because you weren't following the documents upstream. Documents go downstream, to tribunals, to lawyers. You were following his map."

My turn to go quiet. The raft slid. Water talked against the bamboo, a small continuous gossip.

"He was from Kessari," I said finally. "Or he was once. He left before I was born. Sold survey work to the consortium when I was a girl — the valley's first betrayal, my grandmother calls it. She won't say his name. She spits when the wind comes from the east." I turned the folder's oilskin wrapping over in my hands, though she couldn't see it. "He came back to die, I think. And dying, he remembered whose river it was. His map goes where the surveys stop. Past the diversion sites. To the source itself."

"And you need me because—"

"Because the source country is beyond anything I know, and you've ranged the whole watershed. Because you can read water the way I can read ledgers. And because—" I hated this part, hated it the way you hate admitting a debt, "—because whoever shot Tomas Reyes is probably still upstream, and I'd rather have a ranger between me and them than my own good intentions."

* * *

We made camp that night on a shelf of rock above a side channel, and I learned three things about Astrid Yarrow.

One: they build a fire like a surgeon closes a wound — small, precise, invisible from ten paces. Desert habits. You don't grow up on salt flats without learning that smoke is a signature.

Two: they eat facing the dark. Always. I set myself opposite and watched them watch the trees, and after a while I stopped taking it personally. It wasn't rudeness. It was armor, worn so long the wearer had probably forgotten it was armor and thought it was skin.

Three: they could be tested.

I waited until the fire had burned down to its red arithmetic and the storm-light in the west had gone from purple to bruise. Then I said, casually, the way you drop a line into deep water:

"Hale. Your old captain. The patrol boat signaled you, at the crossing. I saw the lamp pattern. Three long, two short — that's *report status*, isn't it? Consortium signal code."

Astrid's spoon stopped over the tin cup.

"You know signal code."

"My uncle ran barges for Meridian before they ruined him. I grew up on that river talking in lamps." I fed a twig to the fire. "You answered. One short. *Status nominal.* Which means either you reported honestly that you'd found your thief sitting right beside you, or you lied to your own captain. So which is it, ranger? Do you serve them, or do you serve whatever's left over when the badge comes off?"

The silence went on long enough that the river got loud again. I let it. Grandmother taught me that too: never fill a silence you've earned.

"They hold a debt over me," Astrid said at last. Not looking at me. Looking at the coals as if the answer were written there in a language they resented. "My brother took fever, four years ago. Meridian medicine saved him. The price was seven years of service. I have four left."

"So you're bought."

"I'm *bound*. There's a difference." Their jaw worked. "Bought men stay bought. Bound men count the days."

"And do you? Count?"

"Every one." A breath. "Hale offered to clear the debt. At the crossing, before — well. You know what happened at the crossing." The crocodiles. Neither of us mentioned the crocodiles; we had agreed, without agreeing, that the crocodiles were a shared embarrassment. "The map and the thief, in exchange for four years of my life returned. It's a fair price. Any sensible person would take it."

"But?"

"But I stood on that bank holding a knife to your throat," Astrid said, "and you looked at me like I was the one drowning. And I thought — this person stole paper. Paper about villages. I've hunted cattle rustlers and water poisoners and a man who burned a waystation with three families inside. In eleven years, the worst thing this thief has done is file documents under the wrong name." They finally looked at me, and their eyes in the firelight were less like a closed door than I'd been telling myself. More like a door someone had locked from the inside, a long time ago, and forgotten the key. "So I lied to Hale. One short lamp. Status nominal. First lie I've ever told the badge."

I felt something shift in my chest that I refused, for professional reasons, to name.

"Why?" I asked. Softly, now. "Why lie?"

"Because Tomas Reyes died holding my hand and asking me to carry his map home," Astrid said. "And I don't break promises to dying men. Even ones I made too easily." They stirred the coals. "Ask me tomorrow whether I serve the consortium. Ask me after the gorge. I'll give you a different answer each time, probably. But tonight—" a shrug, awkward, almost boyish on a person built like a fence post, "—tonight I serve the map."

"Well," I said, and lay back on my bedroll with my hands behind my head, studying the first fat drops beginning to stipple the canopy leaves overhead. "Tonight that's good enough."

It began to rain sometime after midnight — not the monsoon proper, just its advance scouts, feeling out the valley. I woke once and found Astrid awake, sitting up, watching the water rise along the rocks with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of someone doing arithmetic they don't like.

"The gorge floods," they said quietly, not knowing I was listening. "When the monsoon hits the highlands, this whole channel becomes a chute. We'll lose a day to the banks if we're smart. Two if we're stupid."

"We're not stupid," I murmured, mostly asleep.

"No," Astrid said, to the rain more than to me. "But we might be something worse."

I asked what, and got no answer, and was too tired to press. It was only in the grey of morning, hauling the raft up onto the rising bank while the river climbed hungrily behind us, that I understood what they'd meant.

Because that was when the lamp flashed from the ridge above the gorge. Three long. Two short.

Then a fourth, longer than any signal I'd ever learned — a lamp burning steady, deliberate, aimed straight down at us like an eye.

*Report status,* said the code. *And this time, ranger, I'm watching.*
