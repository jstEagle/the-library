## 5. Port Meridian Sends Its Regards

I smelled the horses before I saw them—or rather, before Wren did. That's the advantage of a desert upbringing: you learn to read the air the way priests read scripture, and the air that morning said *dust, iron, and too many men who don't like each other but ride together anyway*.

We'd made camp in the lee of a collapsed watchtower, half a day above the second gorge. The river ran loud and green below us, and the clouds stacking up over the western ranges had gone from white to bruise-colored overnight. Wren was asleep with her boots on, one hand curled around the stolen documents even unconscious, which told me everything about how she'd learned to sleep.

I stood up, and the air said *trouble*.

"Get up," I said. "Company."

She came awake the way animals do—all at once, no groggy middle—and had a knife out before her eyes finished opening. "Consortium?"

"Probably." I scanned the ridgeline. Dust plumes, three of them, moving with the unhurried discipline of people who knew exactly where they were going. Nobody hunts that confidently unless they've been given a map of their own. "Six riders, maybe eight. They'll reach this bend within the hour."

"You could run," she said. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse—it was an offer, extended carefully, like she was handing me back my own freedom and watching to see if I'd take it.

"I could," I agreed. And then, because lying to her had apparently become a habit I couldn't break: "I won't."

* * *

They came down the switchback trail in good order, which is more than I could say for most consortium enforcers, who generally rode like men who'd bought their horsemanship rather than earned it. These were different. These were Hale's.

Captain Odile Hale had taught me everything I knew about tracking, and half of what I knew about surviving, and all of what I knew about reading a man's intentions from the way he sat his saddle. She was sixty now, gray at the temples, built like the anchor chain of a river barge, and she still wore the old ranger's badge on her chest even though the consortium had absorbed the ranger corps a decade ago and turned badges into invoices. She reined in ten paces from our camp and looked at me for a long moment with eyes the color of dried mud.

"Astrid Yarrow," she said. "Still alive. The desert's getting soft."

"Hale." My voice came out steadier than my stomach felt. "You're a long way from Meridian."

"The Fly's a long way from everywhere. That's its charm." Her gaze slid past me to Wren, who stood by the cold fire with her knife held low and casual, fooling nobody. "And this would be the thief."

"This is a colleague," I said.

"She stole forty pages of sealed survey documents from the Meridian records office, knocked out two clerks, and set fire to a filing cabinet on her way out the window." Hale dismounted, slow, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "Some colleague."

"Sounds enterprising."

"Astrid." Just my name, in the tone she used when I was being stupid on purpose, which she knew better than anyone because she'd watched me learn it. She walked past me to the fire ring and crouched, reading it the way I'd read the salt flats—ash color, ash depth, what had burned and what hadn't. Old habit between us. We'd spent six years reading the same ground together, back when I was nineteen and furious at the world and she was the only person patient enough to teach me instead of shooting me.

"You're carrying something," she said, without turning around. "Reyes gave you a map before he died. Don't insult me by denying it; the man was followed out of Kessari territory for three days, and his killers report to the same office I do."

The bottom dropped out of my stomach, quiet as a stone into deep water. So they knew about Tomas. They'd known before I found him. Which meant the prospector hadn't just been gut-shot by random violence—he'd been *handled*, badly, and left to die in the dark where no one would find the map.

Except someone had.

"We should talk privately," I said.

"We should." Hale straightened, knees popping, and finally turned to face me. Behind her, her enforcers had spread along the ridge in a loose crescent—professional spacing, rifles unslung but not raised. A courtesy. For now. "Walk with me."

* * *

We walked down to the water's edge, far enough that the river noise covered whatever the others might strain to hear. She stopped on a flat shelf of rock and looked upstream, toward the gorges, toward the source neither of us could see but both of us were thinking about.

"Twenty-two years ago," she said, "I pulled a half-drowned kid out of a flash flood in the Chalk Hills. Kid had lost her brother to fever the year before and decided she had nothing left to lose, which is the most dangerous thing a person can decide." She glanced at me. "You remember."

"I remember."

"I got you your first contract. Taught you to read sign. Vouched for you when the consortium wanted to ship you to the coastal patrols because you punched a surveyor." A dry almost-smile. "You punched him because he was cheating a widow out of her water rights. I told them you were young. I told them you'd grow out of it."

"I did punch him," I confirmed. "He deserved it."

"He did. That's not the point." She turned to face me fully, and there it was—the thing I'd been dreading, laid out plain. "Here's the offer, Astrid, and I'll only make it once. Bring me the map. Give me the girl. Your debt to the consortium—one hundred and forty thousand marks, incurred saving your brother's life—is voided. Clean slate. Badge reinstated, full rank. You walk away from this river and never look back."

The number landed like a blow. One hundred and forty thousand marks. Eleven years of payments, of contracts I took because they paid and refused ones that didn't, of a life measured out in installments against the night I'd signed my name to save Dario from the lung-rot that would have killed him inside a season. Every mile I'd ever walked had been a payment on that debt. Every compromise. Every time I'd looked the other way at a surveyor's stakes driven into someone's grazing land.

And she was offering to erase it. All of it. For a map and a girl.

"And if I refuse?" I asked.

Hale's expression didn't change, which was itself an answer. "Then I take both of you at the source, and the debt stands, and I write in my report that Ranger Yarrow died of exposure somewhere in the third gorge. Nobody will question it. People die out here all the time." She paused. "I'd rather not write that report. You were the best student I ever had. That's not sentiment—that's inventory."

"There it is," I said. "Always knew where I stood with you, Captain."

That earned me a flicker of genuine amusement. "Give me an answer by dawn. We camp above the bend." She started back up the trail, then stopped, her back to me. "Astrid. Whatever Reyes showed you—whatever you think is up that river—I've seen the orders. There are things worth more than a clean conscience, and things worth less than a hundred and forty thousand marks. Make sure you know which is which before you choose."

She climbed away from me, sure-footed as ever, and I stood alone at the water's edge listening to the river run, and understood that I was already choosing. Had probably chosen the moment I'd promised a dying man I'd carry his map home. Maybe earlier. Maybe the moment I'd caught Wren's trail in the salt flats and hesitated instead of closing the trap.

Duty, I thought, was a lot like debt. You could pay it forever and never own anything.

* * *

Wren was waiting where I'd left her, arms crossed, watching the enforcers on the ridge with the fixed attention of someone counting guns. She didn't ask what Hale wanted. She'd heard enough, or guessed enough, or simply understood that whatever it was, it had taken eleven years off my life in twenty minutes.

"Well?" she said.

"They want the map and you. In exchange, they erase my debt."

Her jaw tightened, but her voice stayed level. "How much?"

"One hundred and forty thousand marks."

Something moved behind her eyes—not fear, exactly. Recalculation. "That's a lot of marks."

"It's eleven years," I said. "It's my brother alive. It's—" I stopped, because the sentence was going somewhere I didn't want it to go. *It's everything I am.* "It's a lot of marks."

"So take it." She said it fast, like ripping off a bandage. "Hand me over. I've been running from them since Meridian; I always figured it ended one of two ways. At least if it's you, I'll get a fair hearing before they—"

"Wren."

"—and the village is downstream anyway, maybe they'll only relocate them, maybe—"

"*Wren.*" I stepped closer, close enough that the enforcers on the ridge became very interested silhouettes, and lowered my voice to almost nothing. "Listen to me, because I'm only saying this once, and then we're both going to pretend I'm a better liar than I am. I'm not giving them the map. I'm not giving them you. When they come at dawn, we're already gone, and we're taking the high route through the scree so their trackers lose us at the shale line."

She stared at me. The knife-hand had gone slack. "Why?"

Because a dying man had trusted me with his way home. Because her grandmother's village had a name and faces and didn't exist in any ledger the consortium kept. Because Hale had called me *inventory*, and I'd finally heard it for what it was.

"Because the debt's theirs," I said, "but the promise is mine. And I keep those."

For a long moment she just looked at me, and I had the uncomfortable sensation of being read more thoroughly than any trail I'd ever tracked. Then, quietly: "You're a terrible liar, Yarrow. You lied to Hale for five minutes and sweated through your shirt."

"I noticed."

"If we're doing this—if we're really doing this—you follow my lead through the gorges. I grew up on this river. You didn't."

"Noted."

"And Astrid?" She was already kicking dirt over the fire ashes, erasing us, efficient as water. "When this is over, you tell me what it cost. All of it. I won't owe you a lie."

"It wasn't a lie," I said, but she was right, and we both knew it, and the honesty of it sat strange and warm in my chest.

Above the bend, someone struck a light—a pipe, a lantern, small and orange against the gathering dark. Dawn was seven hours away. The monsoon clouds had swallowed the last of the stars.

I picked up my pack, checked the map one final time where it rode against my ribs, and started climbing.
