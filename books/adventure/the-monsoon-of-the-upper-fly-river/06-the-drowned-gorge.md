## 6. The Drowned Gorge

The monsoon was supposed to be three days out. I knew this because I'd spent my whole life reading the sky over the Fly's headwaters, and because I'd told Astrid Yarrow so, twice, with the particular confidence of a person who has never once been wrong about weather in front of a stranger they want to impress.

The sky, it turned out, had been listening, and took it personally.

We heard it before we saw it—a sound like the gorge itself clearing its throat, deep in the rock above us. Then the light changed. Not darkened exactly; *greened*, the way water looks from underneath. The birds went silent all at once, which is the thing that should have frightened me most and did, right up until the first wall of rain came down the canyon like something with intent.

"Up," Astrid said, and for once I didn't argue about who was giving orders.

We scrambled for the ledge they'd spotted an hour earlier—barely a shelf, maybe four feet of slick stone above the old flood line—and hauled ourselves onto it as the river below stopped being a river and became an argument between the gorge walls. I have seen floods. I grew up on Kessari's banks; I know what moving water can do to a village, to a field, to a person. But I had never seen the Upper Fly *angry*. It rose ten feet in the time it took me to say a bad word about the weather, and it kept going.

Our raft went first. It hit a submerged snag and opened like a struck melon, and everything we'd lashed to it—food, rope, the oilskin wrapping half my documents—went into the brown churn and did not come back.

"The papers," Astrid said. Not *the food*. Not *we're going to die*. The papers.

"I've got them." I patted my chest, where they rode against my skin in a waxed pouch my grandmother had sewn, the kind of sewing that outlasts its maker. "They were never on the raft."

Astrid looked at me for a long moment. Rain ran off their jaw. "You could have said."

"You could have asked."

Something happened to their mouth then that I suspect, on other people, would be called a smile.

* * *

The ledge held. That was the day's one mercy. We crouched on it through the worst of it, shoulder to shoulder because there wasn't room not to, while the gorge filled and roared and tried, with the patience of all water everywhere, to unmake the stone that contained it. Twice the spray reached us anyway, cold as a slap. My hands went numb around my knees. At some point Astrid moved my hands into their armpits without asking, the way you'd handle someone else's frostbitten fingers, brisk and impersonal, and I let them, and hated how much I didn't hate it.

When the water finally dropped—not far, but enough—it left the gorge rearranged. Our route along the eastern bank was gone, simply deleted, replaced by a sheer new face of wet rock. The western bank was reachable only by swimming a channel that was still moving fast enough to fold a person in half.

"We go through it," Astrid said, chin at the channel.

"Through the water."

"That's generally what a channel means."

"I'm pointing out that you're insane."

"And I'm pointing out," they said, already unbuckling their pack to make it lighter, "that the alternative is standing here until the next surge, and I've read the sky too. There's more coming tonight."

I looked at the water. I looked at them. Neither option improved on inspection.

Astrid went first, which I understood later was not bravery but arithmetic: if the current took them, I'd see where, and wouldn't follow. They made it two-thirds across before the river decided otherwise. I saw it happen—the exact moment the current found the gap under their arm and *took*, rolling them under, their pack strap snagging, their head coming up once with an expression I will remember until I die, which was not fear so much as furious surprise, a ranger betrayed by arithmetic.

I went in without deciding to. That's the truth of it. No plan, no rope, just my body doing the thing bodies do when someone is drowning in front of you. The water hit me like a door. I got a fist into their collar and the current got both of us, and for a long gray stretch I genuinely believed we were both dead and simply hadn't finished the paperwork. Then my foot found stone—an old root wad, a miracle, whatever you want to call it—and I shoved, and we came out on the western gravel bar coughing and crawling and alive, which felt, frankly, presumptuous of us.

Astrid vomited half the Fly River onto the rocks, rolled onto their back, and stared at the sky.

"You saved me," they said eventually, in the tone of someone reporting a theft.

"Don't flatter yourself. You're collateral. If you drowned, I'd have had to explain it to your consortium."

"They don't ask questions. They just bill you."

I laughed. It hurt my ribs, which meant it was real laughter, and I lay down next to them on the wet gravel and laughed at the green sky until we were both doing it, two drowned animals wheezing at the clouds.

* * *

We found dry wood under an overhang a quarter mile on, and Astrid built a fire with the competence of someone who has built fires in worse places than this, which I chose not to think about too hard. We stripped to our last dry layers and hung the rest steaming on sticks, and sat close to the flames because the air had gone cold and strange after the storm, and because sitting close was easier to explain than wanting to.

"So," I said, when the shivering stopped. "Tell me about the debt."

Astrid fed a stick to the fire. I watched them decide whether to lie. I'd gotten good at watching people decide that.

"My brother," they said. "Teo. He was nineteen. He signed on with a Meridian survey crew because they pay desperate boys triple and tell them nothing. There was a collapse at a cut site—shoring they'd skipped to save money. Four men buried. The company sealed the shaft inside a day and wrote it up as geological."

"But Teo lived."

"Teo lived because I pulled him out, and pulling him out cost the consortium a season's work and a foreman's pride. Debt doesn't work like a number here. It works like a hook. They own me until they decide they don't. Ranger badge, ranger wages, ranger silence—all of it rent on my brother's lungs."

"And if you stop paying?"

"They take the badge. And then they take the parts of me that are useful without a badge."

I turned my stick in the fire. "My turn, then. Kessari isn't just my grandmother's village. It's mine. I was born there. Left at twelve when my mother took work in Meridian, and I swore I'd never go back to a place that small, and then I read those documents in a consortium office and every name in the displacement schedule was somebody who taught me to fish." I shrugged, or meant to; it came out more like a flinch. "You spend years building a life somewhere else and it turns out the whole thing was scaffolding. One page of figures and I was thirteen again, and furious, and I stole the file because nobody else was going to."

"Tomas Reyes," Astrid said. "The map. Tell me why a dying man gives it to a stranger."

So I told them about finding him—that part was theirs to know now, the promise I'd made with his hand crushing mine, *carry it upstream, carry it home*—and about the name he kept saying at the end, too quiet to catch, and how I'd assumed it was a prayer. Astrid listened the way they did everything: completely, without interruption, eyes on the fire but present in a way most people never manage.

"He was from Kessari," I said. "Or somewhere like it. Nobody sells a map of his own heart unless he's trying to get back to it."

"Then we're not delivering a map," Astrid said slowly. "We're finishing something he started fifty years ago."

The fire popped. Downstream, the river ran high and loud, carrying our raft and our food and the last easy version of this journey off toward the delta. I became aware, all at once, of how near Astrid was sitting—near enough that the heat off their shoulder competed with the fire's—and that neither of us had moved away, and that the space between us had acquired a specific gravity I didn't have a word for yet.

"You lied to Hale," I said. "Back on the trail. Badly, by the way—you're a terrible liar."

"Noted."

"You warned me instead of turning me in. Why?"

Astrid was quiet long enough that I thought they wouldn't answer. Then: "Because when I watched you fight at the crossing, you fought like someone protecting something. Rangers fight like someone enforcing something. I've been enforcing things for nine years, Wren. I wanted to know what the other one felt like."

"It feels like drowning," I said. "And then laughing about it."

Their eyes came up to mine. Firelight does things to faces it shouldn't be allowed to do, and I looked away first, which I will also deny forever.

"Sleep," Astrid said, gruff, taking first watch as though the gesture canceled out everything else. "We move at first light."

I lay down with my back to the warmth and did not sleep, because I was busy cataloguing the fact that somewhere between the gorge and the fire, the thief and the ranger had become *we*, and neither of us had signed anything.

It was near dawn when Astrid's hand closed on my shoulder—gently, which should have warned me.

"Wren. Wake up. Look downstream."

I crawled to the lip of the overhang. Below us, on the far bank, torchlight moved in single file. A dozen men, maybe more, working their way upriver against the flood, unhurried, organized, sure of their welcome.

At their head walked Captain Hale, and he was not searching for us anymore.

He was herding us—up the gorge, toward the source—like dogs driving game.
