## 7. Kessari

The river changed its mind about us somewhere below the second gorge.

For three days it had been all teeth—white water chewing at the raft, spray in my eyes, Wren shouting instructions I pretended not to need. Then the gorge walls fell away like shoulders relaxing, and the Fly spread itself wide and brown and slow, braiding through a floodplain so green it looked wet even where it wasn't. Birds I didn't have names for argued in the canopy. The air went thick enough to chew.

"We're close," Wren said, and something in her voice had come loose from the wire it had been wound around since the reeds.

I said nothing. I'd learned on this trip that when Wren talked about home, she talked with her whole chest, and there was no room in me for that kind of weather. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

The village announced itself before we saw it: woodsmoke, then drumming—not war drums, or not only—and then canoes, three of them, cutting across the current toward us with the casual violence of people who owned the water. The paddlers were young, bare-chested, painted in patterns that meant nothing to me and everything to them. One of them stood up in a moving canoe, which struck me as showing off until I realized it was just how they stood.

"Wren!" A boy's voice, cracking with disbelief. "Wren Calloway, you absolute ghost!"

"Kofi," she called back, "you still paddle like a wounded duck."

They came alongside and hauled us in without ceremony, and I noticed nobody asked who I was. Nobody asked anything. They took the raft line, took our soaked packs, took Wren into an embrace that lifted her off her feet, and took me—after a moment of collective assessment, the way you'd appraise a stray dog that might bite—as a matter of course. A woman pressed a coconut into my hands. That was the whole interrogation.

I'd spent eleven years as a ranger for Port Meridian, and in all that time I'd never once been handed anything without a ledger entry following it.

* * *

The village sat on a bluff above the river, stilt houses with ironwood legs, gardens climbing the slope behind them in terraces old enough to have their own geology. Children swarmed us immediately, more interested in my boots than in Wren's homecoming, which seemed fair—I was the novelty. One small girl traced the ranger's badge stitched into my collar with a finger gone sticky with mango and asked if it meant I could arrest the river.

"Sometimes," I said, "it feels like the other way around."

She nodded gravely, as though I'd confirmed a suspicion, and ran off.

Wren found her grandmother at the loom under the big house—a small woman, spine straight as a mast, hands moving through the warp without looking at it. She looked up when Wren's shadow crossed her work, and her face did something complicated: joy arriving late, after grief had already settled in and made itself comfortable.

"You were gone four months," the old woman said.

"The passes were closed. Then the documents—" Wren stopped herself, glancing at me. "Grandmother. This is Astrid Yarrow. A ranger. But also—" she fumbled, and I watched her fail to find a category for me, which I confess I enjoyed. "Also the person who got me here alive."

The grandmother looked at me the way the river had looked at our raft: measuring what I'd cost and what I might yet be worth. Then she said, "Sit. You're both thin," and that was that. I was fed within ten minutes of arrival. Fish, taro, some green thing that burned going down and warmed going further down. Nobody asked my business. It began to feel less like hospitality and more like a trap I hadn't identified yet.

It was Wren's grandmother—her name was Sefina—who broke the pattern. She did it by taking the map.

Wren had kept Tomas Reyes's hand-drawn chart wrapped in oilcloth against her skin since the drowned gorge, and now, fed and safe, she finally unrolled it on the loom bench, smoothing it flat with both palms. "We found it with a dying man upriver. He made me promise to carry it home. I thought he meant Port Meridian. Then I thought—" she tapped the upper reaches, the source basin drawn in careful ink, "—I thought maybe he meant here."

Sefina went very still.

Not surprised-still. Grieving-still. I've seen that stillness before, usually over bodies. Her hands left the loom and hovered above the map without touching it, the way you'd hover over a sleeping child you weren't sure was yours anymore.

"Where did you say you found this?" she asked.

"A mining camp, two days below the salt flats. A prospector named Tomas Reyes. Gut-shot. He died before dawn."

"Tomas." She said the name the way you'd set down something heavy. "Tomas Reyes." And then, to me, though her eyes never left the map: "He was born in this village. In the house behind the breadfruit tree, third one from the water. My mother's cousin's son. I held him when he was smaller than a loaf of bread."

I looked at the map again. The fine, patient linework. The annotations in two scripts—the colonial hand and another, older, looping. The care of it. I'd assumed it was the work of a surveyor. It was the work of someone drawing his way back to a place he'd been taught to forget.

"He sold its secrets," Sefina said. "That's the story every child here learns. When the consortium first came looking for the source, forty years ago, Tomas guided their men upriver. Drew them maps. Told them where the narrows were, where the crocodiles slept, where the valley opened." Her voice stayed level; her hands did not. "He was paid in coin and a house in Meridian, and the village paid in everything else. We exiled him in name. He exiled himself in fact. Either way, he never came back."

"And the map?" Wren asked quietly.

"I don't know. Perhaps guilt keeps better than coin. Perhaps an old man looks at the end of his life and finds he has been living in the wrong direction the whole time." Sefina finally touched the paper, one fingertip resting on the source basin. "This is not a thief's map, girl. Look at the margins. These are songs. He wrote the songs down. You do not write down the songs of a place unless you are asking its forgiveness."

I thought of Tomas Reyes delirious in the dark, gripping my wrist hard enough to bruise, making me swear on nothing but my own word because he had nothing else to offer. *Carry it upstream.* I'd taken the promise the way you take a stranger's parcel at a door—lightly, because it cost nothing yet.

It was costing plenty now.

* * *

They gave us a guest house on stilts at the edge of the bluff, and evening came down over the valley like a lid being placed gently on a jar. Frogs started up in the reeds. Somewhere a baby cried and was sung to. Cooking smoke rose from thirty fires and mingled into one smell that I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe to anyone who hasn't smelled it.

Wren sat on the steps beside me, knees to her chest, watching the river go bronze.

"My grandmother used to tell me about him," she said. "Tomas. As a cautionary tale. *Don't be a Tomas.* Don't trade what can't be bought." She laughed, but it came out wrong. "And then I stole those documents, and I remember thinking, halfway down the river with Hale's hounds behind me, *well. Here I am. A Tomas.*"

"You're not," I said. "He sold the valley. You're trying to save it."

"That's the difference between theft and treason, is it? Which direction you're running?"

"Something like that." I turned the coconut shell cup in my hands. "For what it's worth, I think he figured out the direction late. But he figured it out. That map isn't a betrayal. It's an apology forty years long, delivered by courier."

Wren looked at me sideways. "You're getting soft, ranger."

"I'm getting tired. It presents similarly."

She bumped her shoulder against mine and left it there, and I let it stay, and neither of us made a joke about it, which between the two of us was practically a marriage vow.

Here is what I didn't say: that all day, the village had been doing something to me I had no defenses against. That when the small girl with the mango hands waved at me from the garden path—waved at *me*, specifically, having decided I belonged to the day's inventory—I had felt something crack open in my chest that eleven years of salt flats and solo patrols had kept sealed. That I had grown up in a barracks in Meridian where meals were counted and affection was rationed by the quartermaster, and I had told myself for so long that this was freedom—no ties, no debts beyond the one I already carried—that I'd forgotten to check whether the armor had fused to the skin.

Nobody in Kessari had asked what I was owed. They'd just handed me a coconut.

It was the most dangerous thing that had happened to me all trip, and I'd recently fought crocodiles.

* * *

I woke past midnight to the sound I'd been listening for without admitting it: a lantern signal, far upriver. Three flashes, pause, two. Consortium field code. I knew it the way you know your mother tongue—shamefully, permanently.

I was on my feet and out the door before my boots finished deciding, and found Wren already awake, already dressed, standing at the rail with her eyes fixed on the dark bend of the river where the light had blinked.

"How many?" she whispered.

"Can't tell. Three-and-two is a scout marker. Could be two men. Could be twenty behind them."

"Hale."

"Hale doesn't travel at the front of his own column. But his scouts do." I watched the bend. Nothing moved now. The river muttered to itself in the dark. "They'll report the village by dawn. After that, it depends what Hale wants. If he thinks the documents are here, he'll want them quiet. If he thinks *we're* here—"

"He'll want us quieter."

Behind us, a floorboard creaked. Sefina stood in the doorway of the main house, wrapped in a shawl, holding a lamp that lit her face from below and made her look carved rather than old.

"You'll want the back trail," she said, as calmly as if offering more fish. "Through the sago swamp. The scouts won't find it in the dark, and the leeches will discourage the ones who try." She set the lamp down and looked from Wren to me, and then at the map, still lying open on the loom bench where we'd left it, Tomas Reyes's apology glowing pale in the lamplight.

"But you're not leaving," she said. It was not a question. "You're going up. To the source."

Wren and I looked at each other. Downriver, faint as a heartbeat, the lantern flashed again—three, pause, two—and Sefina's grandson Kofi appeared out of the shadows at the foot of the stairs, breathing hard, paint still on his face from the welcome drums.

"They're past the narrows," he said. "A dozen boats, maybe more. And grandmother—they're flying the company flag."
