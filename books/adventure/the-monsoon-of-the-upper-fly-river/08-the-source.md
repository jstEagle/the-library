## 8. The Source

The map's last leg was drawn in a hand that shook, and I don't mean from age.

Tomas Reyes had inked the lower river with the confidence of a man who'd grown up drinking from it—every bend labeled in a language I only half remembered, my grandmother's language, the one she'd been beaten for speaking at the mission school and had taught me anyway, in whispers, like contraband. But the final climb was different. The lines went ragged. The annotations crowded each other, as if he'd run out of room or nerve: *the basin lies above the second falls — do not trust the water's color — they will tell you it is empty.*

*They will tell you it is empty.* He'd written that twice, underlined once.

"Your dead prospector had a flair for the dramatic," Astrid said behind me. They were breathing hard on the switchback, which gave me an unreasonable amount of pleasure. Rangers were supposed to be made of goat and granite. Instead here was Astrid Yarrow, badge and all, sweating through their shirt because I'd set a pace and refused to apologize for it.

"He wasn't being dramatic," I said. "He was being specific."

"Specific about what?"

I didn't answer, because the answer was the smell. We'd been climbing through cloud forest for two days—moss to the knee, birdsong so thick you could lean on it—and sometime past dawn the birdsong had thinned. By midmorning it stopped altogether. And underneath the green rot-smell of the forest there was something else now, faint but wrong, like copper left out in the rain. Like a wound.

Astrid smelled it too. I watched them catch it, watched the ranger go still in that particular way of theirs, head tilting, nostrils flaring, the whole body becoming an instrument. It should have frightened me, how good they were at reading country. It did frighten me, a little. But mostly it was a relief, because it meant I hadn't imagined it.

"That's not mineral," Astrid said quietly. "That's chemical."

"I know what it is." My grandmother had described it once, when I was small—a taste she said would come back to the valley someday, brought by men who measured everything and loved nothing. She'd called it *the water forgetting itself*. I'd thought it was one of her metaphors. Grandmother Kessari had a hundred of them, and most were just ways of saying *be careful*. This one, apparently, was a way of saying *run*.

We climbed faster after that. Neither of us suggested turning around. There are conversations you have without speaking, and that was ours: whatever was up there, we were going to walk into the middle of it, because Tomas Reyes had died face-down in the dirt of a mining camp rather than let this map stay buried, and because my grandmother's village drank from this river, and because—though I'd have bitten off my own tongue before saying it aloud—I had started trusting the person behind me more than I trusted my own legs.

Which was either love or stupidity, and I've never been able to reliably tell them apart.

* * *

We came out of the treeline at midafternoon, and the world simply ended.

I want to describe the source properly, because everything that came after—the fury, the fear, the things we found and the things we did—grew out of this first sight of it, and I don't ever want to remember it wrong. The basin sat in a bowl of black stone maybe half a mile across, ringed by cliffs, and it was full of light. Not sunlight exactly, though the sun was on it. The water itself seemed lit from below, spring-fed from a hundred vents in the rock floor, each one rising in a silver thread, and where the threads met they braided into pools so clear you could count the stones forty feet down. The Fly began here—not as a river, nothing so grand, just water welling up out of the earth's own heart, patient and endless, gathering itself before its long fall to the sea and to us.

My grandmother used to say the source was where the river dreamed. Standing on the rim of that basin, I finally understood it wasn't a metaphor. It was a description. You could feel the place thinking.

And then you could feel something else, because the dream was being cut open while we watched.

On the far side of the basin, where the water gathered to spill over the lip of the falls, someone had built a wall. Not finished—a skeleton of it, timber and poured concrete footings, scaffolding crawling over it like white ants. A channel had been cut into the eastern rim, dry for now, waiting. And the pool below the unfinished weir—

Below the weir, the water was the wrong color. Not poisoned-looking, not yet; just *dimmed*, the way a lamp dims when someone is drawing off its current. And downstream of it, threading away along the cliff path we'd climbed, ran a pipe. Iron, new, gleaming wet. It went down and down toward the valley, toward Kessari, carrying away the river's first breath before the river even knew it had taken one.

"They're not surveying," Astrid said. Their voice had gone flat, which I'd learned meant the opposite of calm. Flat was Astrid's fury, banked and burning. "Survey crews don't pour concrete."

"No," I said. "They don't."

We lay flat on the rim in the fern-shadow and counted. Eleven men visible, maybe more under the tarps of the work camp pitched against the north wall—tents, a cook shelter, crates stacked in rows. And beside the crates, unmistakable even at distance, stenciled markings I recognized from the consortium freight that passed through Port Meridian: *EXPLOSIVES — HANDLE WITH CARE — PROPERTY OF THE MERIDIAN CONSORTIUM.*

Dynamite. Enough of it, by the look of the stacks, to crack the basin floor wide open.

"They told the tribunal it was a hydrological survey," I whispered. "Grandmother showed me the notice. *Preliminary assessment of upper catchment resources.* They swore on paper there'd be no construction until the review board sat next season."

"The review board sits next season," Astrid said, "and finds a finished diversion works and a done deal. Classic Hale. Present the tribunal with a fait accompli and a bill for damages if they object." A pause. "I helped write those procedures. God help me, I thought they were protections."

"You thought your cage was a house," I said. "I know. So did I, once, about Port Meridian."

Astrid turned their head and looked at me then—looked a long time, in that way they had, like they were reading a document for forgery and finding none. Then they looked back at the basin, and I saw the moment the ranger died a little more. It happened in the shoulders first. Everything Astrid felt lived in their shoulders.

"There's enough proof down there to hang the whole consortium," they murmured. "Concrete is dated. Those crates have manifest numbers. If we can get photographs—or better, paperwork from the camp office—and get it downriver before the monsoon seals the passes—"

"If," I said. "There's always an if with you."

"That's what makes me fun at parties."

* * *

We went down the eastern rim in the last hour of light, when the work crews knocked off and the camp noise changed from industry to supper. Astrid led, because moving unseen through hostile ground was the one thing they were unquestionably the best in the territory at doing, and I followed in their footsteps like a shadow learning to be a person. We came at the camp from the drainage side, past a latrine trench and a heap of broken drill bits, and slipped between the cook shelter and the first row of crates while a man sang badly inside about a girl from the coast.

The crates were real. That was the thing that made my hands shake—how ordinary it all was. Stenciled numbers, shipping dates, the consortium's little crowned-M stamped on every board like a signature on a confession. Astrid pried one lid with a knife and looked at the waxed-paper sticks nested inside, and closed it again very gently, the way you'd close a coffin.

"Enough to drop the east rim into the vents," they breathed. "They wouldn't even need the weir. One blast and the source becomes a quarry seep. The valley gets a trickle and a silt load." They straightened. "Camp office. Now. While they eat."

The office was a shed with a desk, a strongbox, and a filing cabinet that some clerk had thoughtfully left unlocked, because who robs a secret site at the top of the world? Astrid did, apparently, with the efficiency of long practice, and I stood watch at the door with my heart going like a drum and watched them lift out survey reports, requisition orders, a construction schedule annotated in a hand I recognized—blocky, confident, impatient.

Captain Hale's hand. I'd seen it on the wanted notices for me.

Then Astrid went still over one page, and I knew before they spoke that it was bad.

"Wren." Their voice had dropped into that terrible level register. "This schedule. The diversion gates close tomorrow night. Full flow through the pipe by the following morning." They looked up at me, and the lamplight caught their face, and I saw what I'd been refusing to see since the rim: we were already too late for anything easy. "The poisoning isn't coming. It's *here*. It started weeks ago. Every day the gates stay open, another piece of the valley dies."

Outside, somewhere down the line of tents, a man laughed at something. The sound of ordinary evening, of men who'd go home rich and sleep fine.

"And the monsoon?" I managed.

Astrid glanced at the sky through the shed's high window—at the bruise-colored clouds stacking up over the western peaks, the ones we'd been outrunning for three days, the ones that would seal every pass and drown every trail and make this basin unreachable until the project was a fact no tribunal could undo.

"We have until it breaks," they said. "Maybe less."

I took the papers from them and wrapped them in oilcloth against the rain that was coming, and for a moment neither of us moved. Below us, faint through the shed wall, I could almost hear it—the source, dreaming on, unaware that men with dynamite and schedules had come to end its dream.

"We burn the pipe," I said. "Tonight. Before the gates—"

The door banged open.

Not a guard. Worse. A runner, a boy with a message slip, who froze at the sight of us with his mouth open—and then, behind him, across the camp, a bugle note, sharp and rising, and voices, many voices, and boots.

Astrid blew out the lamp.

"Window," they whispered. "Now."
