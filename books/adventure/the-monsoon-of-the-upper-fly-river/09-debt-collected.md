## 9. Debt Collected

The source of the Upper Fly River was not what I expected, and I had spent three weeks expecting everything.

I'd imagined a trickle seeping from rock, something apologetic. Instead we came over the last ridge at dawn and found a basin the size of a cathedral, ringed in ferns taller than Wren, fed by a spring that rose out of the earth itself—clear, cold, and so blue it looked like a wound in the green. The water gathered there before it committed to anything, pooling in perfect stillness, and for one long moment neither of us spoke. Even Wren, who had opinions about everything and shared them freely, went quiet.

"Reyes drew this," she said finally, unrolling the map with hands that shook—not from fear, I realized, but from something closer to grief. "He remembered it right down to the ferns."

"He was coming home," I said. "The long way around."

Then I saw the survey stakes.

They marched across the basin's far edge in neat rows, orange flags snapping in the morning wind, and beyond them—half-hidden behind a screen of canvas and corrugated tin—the diversion works. A cofferdam of timber and stone already choked the spring's eastern outlet. Pipes lay stacked like felled trees. And against the cliff face, under tarpaulin, the dull amber gleam of dynamite crates. Enough to crack the basin open and send every drop of it down a channel toward the consortium's new holdings, leaving Kessari and a dozen villages like it to drink dust.

Awe curdled in my chest and came out the other side as fury. Twenty years a ranger. Twenty years enforcing water law written by men who had never once been thirsty.

"They're already here," Wren whispered.

"No," I said, because the camp was too quiet, the cook-fire cold. "They were here. They'll be back."

They were back within the hour.

We heard the mules first—bells on their harnesses, cheerful as church chimes—and then Captain Hale came around the bend of the trail with eight armed men strung out behind him like beads on a wire, and I understood that my stalling in the gorge had bought us nothing but time enough to arrive.

Hale hadn't changed. Grey at the temples now, but the same flat grey eyes, the same unhurried way of moving, as if the world would always wait for him to finish his coffee. He'd taught me to read sign, to shoot, to keep my word when keeping it cost. He'd also taught me, without ever meaning to, that a debt is just a leash someone else holds.

"Astrid." He said my name like a verdict. His men spread out along the rim of the basin with the easy competence of people who had done this before, rifles slung but hands near. "You look terrible. Gorges will do that."

"Captain."

"Don't 'Captain' me. I offered you a clean trade at the crossing. Map, the girl, your slate wiped. You lied to my face—badly, incidentally; you always did lie badly—and led me on a merry chase through country that nearly killed two of my men." He nodded at the basin, the stakes, the pipes. "None of which matters now, because you've delivered yourself. Both of you. That's economy. I appreciate economy."

Wren's hand found mine. Her fingers were cold. Mine weren't much better.

"The documents are copies," she said, chin up, voice steady. "The originals are already downstream. In Kessari. In Port Meridian. Killing us changes nothing."

"It changes whether there's anyone left alive who knows where the rest are buried," Hale said mildly. "But yes. Continue."

I stepped forward, putting myself between her and the nearest rifle, and did the arithmetic I'd been doing since the gorge—the same arithmetic, always arriving at the same sum. My brother, fevered and drowning in his own blood in a Port Meridian charity ward. The consortium's surgeon. The bill. Ten years of service against it, six paid, four remaining, and the leash running through my badge like a stitch through cloth.

"I'm invoking the debt," I said. "Me for her. Take me in, take the map, let her walk. That settles it. That's the terms."

Hale studied me for a long moment, and something almost gentle moved behind his eyes—the mentor, surfacing briefly through the company man like a fish through dark water.

"You'd do that," he said. Not a question. "Of course you would. It's the finest thing about you, Astrid, and it's why you were never any good at this work. Duty without judgment." He turned to his men. "Cuff the ranger. The girl comes with—"

"No."

It came out of me before I knew I was going to say it, and once it was out, the rest came easy, the way floodwater comes through a cracked dam.

"No. I take it back. All of it. The debt, the terms, the whole rotten ledger." My voice carried across the basin, and I made sure of it—I wanted every man on that rim to hear. "Ten years ago your consortium bought my brother's life and called it generosity. Six years I've paid on a bill that was never mine, doing your dirty work in country you've never seen, dragging people off land you intend to steal. Well, I've seen it now. I've seen *this*." I swept an arm at the spring, the stakes, the dynamite. "There are villages downriver, Captain. Children. And you're going to drain their river to fill your shareholders' cups and call it development. So here's my counteroffer: the debt is void. Voided, publicly, by the party who owed it. You can't collect on a promise I no longer acknowledge. Write that in your ledger."

The silence rang. One of Hale's men shifted his weight. Somewhere above, a bird screamed and fell silent, as if it too understood what had just happened.

Hale's face didn't change, which was how I knew how badly I'd cut him.

"You understand what that makes you," he said quietly. "Not a debtor. A thief. A traitor. There's no tribunal on this coast that will hear anything else."

"Then it's fortunate," said Wren, stepping up beside me, "that we're not planning to be heard by any tribunal on this coast."

And she ran for the cofferdam.

* * *

Everything after that happened very fast, the way disasters do, and I remember it in pieces.

I remember Wren tearing the tarpaulin off the sluice-gate assembly while Hale shouted orders and his men scrambled down the slope, rifles coming up, and the morning splitting open around us with gunfire that echoed off the basin walls into a sound like the end of the world.

I remember diving behind the timber of the cofferdam as bullets chewed splinters past my ear, and hauling myself up the scaffold to where the main gate-wheel stood rusted and stubborn, and putting my shoulder to it beside Wren while rounds sparked off the iron an arm's length away.

I remember Hale climbing the scaffold after us—of course he did; he never sent a man to do what he could do himself—and the wheel giving way all at once, the gate groaning up, and the spring finding its old path with a roar like a released animal.

I remember the water hitting the diversion works.

It took the timber flume apart in seconds. Took the pipe stacks, swept them downstream like matchsticks. Took the survey stakes, the flags, the cook-fire's ashes, the whole tidy geometry of theft, and rolled it all into the gorge in a brown thundering wall. Two of Hale's men dropped their rifles and simply ran, which struck me even then as the sanest act of the entire morning.

I remember Hale reaching the top of the scaffold, soaked to the waist, drawing his sidearm, and looking at me across four feet of roaring spray—and I saw it in his face, the exact moment the fish stopped surfacing, the moment the company man won whatever argument had been going on inside him for twenty years.

"Astrid," he said, over the water. Almost sadly. "You could have been—"

The scaffold went.

Whether the flood took the footings or the footings had been shoddy from the start, I couldn't tell you. The platform lurched, tilted, and Hale went down with it—one hand scrabbling at wet timber, finding nothing, his eyes finding mine for half a heartbeat with something in them that wasn't anger. It might have been relief. It might have been accusation. I lunged; my fingers closed on spray and rain and nothing; and the river took him the way it takes everything, without malice and without pause, folding him under and carrying him toward the country he'd come to strip.

I stood on the high timber with the water screaming past and felt something tear loose in my chest—not grief exactly, or not only grief. Something structural. A beam pulled out of a load-bearing wall.

Wren's hand closed on my arm. "Astrid. *Astrid.* We have to go. Now. The whole hillside's going."

She was right. The flood had undercut the slope below the basin, and the ground was moving in slow, syrupy slides, trees tilting, mud sighing. Behind us the spring poured free and glorious through the wreckage of the works, but between us and dry ground lay forty yards of terrain actively deciding to become lakebed.

We went anyway. There was no version of staying that ended well.

I remember the run in flashes: Wren ahead of me, light as a wader bird, picking the stable line through chaos; the ground liquefying under my left boot and her hand shooting out to haul me onto rock; a rifle floating past, butt-first, spinning gently, owned by no one now; the two of us sliding down a chute of red mud on our backsides, laughing or sobbing or both, into the drowned wreck of the lower works where the water had already gone quiet and deep and green.

By the time we reached the gorge mouth, the monsoon had arrived in earnest—a solid grey curtain marching up the valley behind us, erasing the basin, the ridge, the evidence, everything. But not everything. Wren had the documents strapped against her body beneath her oilskin, dry as scripture, and Tomas Reyes's map was wrapped in waxed canvas under my shirt, against my heart, where promises are kept.

We stood in the shallows where the gorge opened out, gasping, plastered in mud, alive. Downstream, faint through the rain, I could see the smoke of Kessari's cooking fires. Upstream, the valley we had just unmade was already dissolving into cloud.

"Hale's men will report it," I said. "Some will tell the truth. Some will tell the version that keeps them employed. Either way, the consortium knows the works are destroyed and two witnesses are loose with proof of what they were building." I looked at her. "They'll come harder next time. This isn't over."

"No," Wren agreed. She pushed wet hair out of her eyes and grinned at me, wild and mud-streaked and utterly unafraid. "But they can't divert a river they can't find, and they can't find it until the monsoon ends, and by then—" she patted the oilskin over her heart, where the documents rode— "the tribunal in Port Meridian will have read every page. My grandmother says the colonial courts are slow and corrupt and vain. She also says they cannot abide being *embarrassed*. So we embarrass them."

"Downstream, then," I said. "Through flooded country, in monsoon season, carrying stolen documents and a dead man's map, with the consortium's enforcers somewhere behind us and my badge forfeit and my name worth more dead than alive."

Wren considered this inventory of ruin. "So," she said, "a normal Tuesday."

I laughed—actually laughed, out loud, for the first time in longer than I could reckon—and the sound startled birds out of the reeds, and the rain came down warm as forgiveness, and we turned our backs on the source and started for the water's beginning-to-end, toward Kessari, toward Port Meridian, toward whatever was left of a life I hadn't finished choosing yet.

Behind us, the river ran free.
