## 10. Where the Water Begins

The tribunal building in Port Meridian smelled of mildew, ink, and the particular rot of institutions that had never once been surprised by their own corruption. I stood in the corridor outside the hearing chamber for the better part of an hour, documents wrapped in oilcloth under my arm, watching clerks scurry past with the studied indifference of people paid not to notice anything.

My badge was gone. I'd left it on Hale's desk three days before, or what remained of his desk—the consortium had already sent men to clear out his office, as though a man could be erased by tidying. The brass eagle pin sat in my palm for a long moment before I set it down. Twenty-two years. It weighed less than Tomas Reyes's map had, and it had cost me considerably more.

"You're expected," said a clerk at last, without looking up from his ledger.

"I'm expected," I agreed, "by whom?"

He didn't answer. Nobody ever did, in this town. Port Meridian ran on questions nobody answered, debts nobody named, and water everybody counted.

* * *

The tribunal chamber was smaller than I'd imagined—smaller than the weight of what I carried deserved. Three magistrates behind a scarred mahogany bench, a consortium lawyer in a suit worth more than Kessari's entire village, and a gallery half-full of company men pretending they weren't company men.

I laid the documents out one by one. Survey reports dated two years back. Diversion schematics for the source basin. Requisitions for dynamite signed by men whose names appeared nowhere in any public filing. And beneath all of it, Tomas Reyes's hand-drawn map, its ink faded to the color of old tea, marking the spring-fed heart of the Upper Fly with a small careful star and, in a script gone shaky at the edges, a single word: *home*.

The consortium lawyer objected to everything. Authenticity. Provenance. The mental state of a dead prospector. He objected so thoroughly and so tediously that even the senior magistrate began checking her pocket watch, which I took as a good sign. Boredom is the first symptom of conscience in colonial administration.

"Ranger Yarrow," the senior magistrate said finally, when the lawyer paused for breath. "You understand that by your own testimony you have voided a debt owed to this consortium. That you acted against direct orders. That Captain Hale's death occurred during an operation you initiated."

"I understand," I said, "that the valley below the source has been drinking poison for eight months while these gentlemen filed paperwork."

A silence. Somewhere in the gallery a fan creaked on its pivot.

"The project is suspended pending investigation," she said, in the tone of a woman nailing shut a coffin she hoped no one would open again. "Your ranger's commission is revoked. You are fortunate the tribunal does not pursue charges, given—" she glanced at the map, and something flickered across her face, too quick to name, "—given the circumstances."

"I am," I said, "enormously fortunate."

It came out dry enough that two of the magistrates almost smiled. The third wrote something down. They always do.

Outside, the monsoon light lay over the harbor like hammered tin. Wren was waiting by the customs house, arms crossed, watching me come down the steps with the expression she wore when she was deciding whether to be impressed or insufferable. Usually both.

"Well?" she said.

"Suspended. Revoked. Fortunate."

"They let you keep the hat?"

"They did not let me keep the hat."

She clicked her tongue. "Tragic. You looked almost respectable in it."

We walked down toward the water without deciding to. That was the thing about Wren Calloway—I never decided anything around her; things simply happened, the way weather happens, the way rivers find the sea without asking permission of anyone.

* * *

Kessari had buried Tomas Reyes's memory the week we arrived—or rather, had unburied it. His grandmother's grave, they told me, had been left a dishonor for forty years, and now the village women had cleaned the stone and planted frangipani beside it, because a man who drew his way home, however late, had at least tried to come back. Wren's grandmother had pressed the map's corner to her forehead before we surrendered it to the tribunal's copyists, murmuring something in a language the consortium had spent decades trying to tax out of existence.

"He sold us once," she'd told me, in the smoky quiet of her house, while Wren pretended to sleep in the corner. "But a man who spends his last breath carrying the way home upstream—he bought himself back. And you, ranger. You carried him the last mile." She'd looked at me the way you look at weather coming over a ridge. "Debts die. Promises don't. Remember which one you kept."

I thought about that now, standing at the river's mouth where the Fly spread itself gray-green and patient into the sea. The rain had stopped an hour before dawn—the first clean stop in eleven days—and the whole delta steamed like a beast cooling after a long run. Somewhere upstream, past the drowned gorge and the poisoned shallows slowly flushing themselves clear, the source ran unguarded for the first time in years. Men were pulling dynamite stores out by barge. Survey stakes were coming up like rotten teeth. The consortium's lawyers were already drafting the story they'd tell: regrettable misunderstanding, rogue operation, isolated incident. Institutions forgive themselves faster than any god ever has.

"Do you regret it?" Wren asked. She'd come up beside me quiet as reeds. She always did. "The badge. The hat. The whole—life."

I turned a stone over with my boot. Underneath, the sand was dark and wet and full of small busy lives.

"I spent twenty-two years believing the debt made me somebody," I said. "Owed. Bound. Purposeful. Turns out it just made me owned." I watched an egret stalk the shallows with the patience of a creature that had never once doubted itself. "Hale used to say duty was a rope. Hold on tight enough and it'll haul you anywhere. He never asked who was holding the other end."

"And now?"

"Now I'm holding my own end." I looked at her. Rain-washed morning light, salt wind, that sharp clever face that had gone from enemy to ally to—whatever this was, this thing we hadn't named yet because neither of us trusted names not to break things. "Turns out there's nothing on the other end of it. Just me. And apparently you."

"Apparently me," she agreed. Her mouth twitched. "You could go back east. Take tribunal work. They love a repentant instrument of empire."

"I could."

"You won't."

"I won't."

She nodded slowly, looking out at the river, and for a long moment neither of us said anything, because some silences are conversations and this was one of them. The monsoon clouds were breaking apart overhead into great white continents, and the light came through in shafts, and the whole valley downstream of us—the gorges, the reed beds, Kessari's gardens going green and greedy after the floods—lay shining like something newly forgiven.

"Grandmother says you're to come to the planting festival," Wren said at last, in the tone of someone delivering orders on behalf of a higher power. "She says you eat too little and talk too much and need feeding."

"She said that about me?"

"She said that about every stray she's ever taken in. It's how you know you're taken in." Wren bumped her shoulder against mine, deliberate, warm. "There's a room above her house. Small. Smells like smoke. Faces the river."

"A room."

"A room, Astrid. Don't make it strange."

I laughed—actually laughed, rough and rusty, a sound I barely recognized as mine. Twenty-two years of tracking thieves across salt flats and here I was, badgeless, hatless, unemployed, standing at the mouth of a river I'd nearly died in twice, being offered a smoky room by a woman I'd once held at knifepoint over stolen paper.

Tomas Reyes had traded a map for a promise. I'd kept it. The promise had cost me everything I'd been and handed me, somehow, everything I hadn't known I wanted.

"Tell your grandmother," I said, "I accept."

Wren grinned and started back up the bank, and I followed, and behind us the river went on doing what rivers do—carrying everything downhill, forgetting nothing, beginning again.

At the tree line she stopped, squinting upriver, and her grin faded into something sharper.

"Astrid," she said. "Who's that coming down the water road?"

Far off, dust rising, a rider. Then another. Moving fast, and flying no colors at all—which, in my long experience, was the only flag worth worrying about.
