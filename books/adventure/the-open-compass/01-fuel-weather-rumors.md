## 1. Fuel, Weather, Rumors

The trick to staying alive in the northern interior was simple, and Yara Pemberton had learned it the hard way, at a cost she never talked about: land, unload, leave. In that order. Before anyone learned your name.

Seven years of practice had worn the rule smooth as a river stone. She could be on the ground in Kestrel Bay for eleven minutes if the wind cooperated — twelve if old Marta Okafor was slow counting insulin vials, and Marta was always slow counting, because Marta believed conversation was a form of medicine too, and Yara had never found a polite way to tell her that conversation was a form of dying.

She came in low over the bay at 9:40 in the morning, the Cessna 208 Caravan heavy with freight and light on everything else. The water below was the color of gunmetal and old rope, and the town sat at its edge like something the tide had coughed up and couldn't quite take back again: a fuel dock, a general store, a gravel airstrip that flooded twice a year and froze solid the rest of it, forty houses leaning into the wind like men waiting out a storm under one awning.

"Kestrel Bay traffic, Caravan Seven-Three-Bravo, inbound from the south, landing runway two-seven," she said into the radio, though there was nobody in Kestrel Bay to hear it except the fuel man, who was probably in the store buying cigarettes, and Marta, who didn't own a radio she trusted.

Nobody answered. Nobody ever answered. That was part of the arrangement.

She flared onto the gravel, taxied to the fuel pump, and shut down. The engine ticked as it cooled, ticking away the seconds of her eleven minutes, and Yara climbed out into air so clean it felt like a debt she'd have to pay later.

Marta was already at the strip's edge, which meant Yara was already behind schedule. Marta moved through the world at the speed of certainty, and certainty took time.

"You're late," Marta said, not unkindly. "You're always late."

"I'm early. Everyone else is slow."

"Same thing." Marta handed over a clipboard without looking at it. "Insulin. Amoxicillin. The good tape, not the cheap stuff. And a letter for you."

"I don't get letters."

"You do now." Marta tapped the clipboard twice, a gesture Yara had never been able to decode and had stopped trying to. "Man came in on the mail boat yesterday. Asked after flights north. Said he needed a ride up the coast, past the headlands."

"No."

"He hasn't asked yet."

"He will. The answer's no."

Marta smiled the way old women smile when they've already won an argument the other person doesn't know they're having. "He's standing by my counter right now, drinking coffee I can't charge him enough for. Tall fellow. Talks like a textbook that fell down the stairs. Says he's a geologist."

"They're all geologists until they want something."

"And what do you want, Yara Pemberton?"

Nothing. That was the whole point. Wanting things was how you ended up staying, and staying was how you ended up responsible, and being responsible was how you ended up standing in the ashes of a settlement that wasn't there anymore because you'd made the mistake of belonging to it.

"Fuel," she said. "I want fuel, and then I want sky."

* * *

The trouble with rules was that they only worked until they met a person who didn't know them.

Yara crossed the gravel toward the store with the clipboard under her arm, rehearsing the refusal. She'd give him thirty seconds. Politeness was expensive but she could afford a little; rudeness got remembered, and remembered people got looked for.

He was exactly where Marta said he'd be, folded onto a stool built for smaller men, a paper cup of Marta's coffee held in both hands like something rescued from a fire. He was tall in the way that tall men in small rooms are tall — apologetically, constantly, knees knocking the underside of the counter. His coat was new and already wrong for the country, some catalog version of cold-weather gear that would soak through in the first real rain. His eyes were the problem. They were quick, hungry, the eyes of a man reading everything, and Yara had spent seven years making sure there was nothing about her worth reading.

"Pemberton Air," he said, standing too fast, the stool scraping. "You're the supply run. Marta said—"

"Marta says a lot of things."

"Teodor Vance." He put out his hand. She looked at it. He put it away. "Geologist. Structural, mostly — coastal erosion, sediment cores, that sort of thing. I need to get up the coast. Past the headlands. There's a stretch of shoreline about ninety miles north where—"

"No."

"—where I need survey photographs. Aerial. It's three hours of your time, maybe four. I'll pay charter rates. Double charter rates."

"Double charter rates don't buy back time when the weather turns, and the weather's going to turn. Barometer's been sliding since Tuesday. You can read it yourself on the wall there."

He did read it himself on the wall there, which annoyed her more than the asking. Most passengers were ignorant and knew it. This one looked at the barometer the way she looked at fuel gauges, with a kind of professional grief.

"It's dropping," he agreed. "But it's not dropping fast. I've watched this coast's weather for eleven years from tide tables and ship logs. It has patterns. This is a pattern."

"Eleven years." Something in the number snagged on her, some burr she couldn't name yet. "You've never been here."

"Not here. Here—" he gestured at the coast generally, the gray water, the headlands stacked north like cards about to fall, "—here I've been everywhere and nowhere. Paper. Archives. My mentor worked this coast. Elias Marchetti. Eleven years ago he ran an expedition up this shoreline, six people, two boats, and none of them came back. No wreckage. No distress call. Nothing."

The store was quiet around them. Even Marta had stopped pretending to count vials.

"The sea took them," Yara said. It wasn't a question. It was the answer she was offering so he'd stop talking.

"The sea doesn't take six people and two boats and leave no oil slick. I've read the incident report nine times. It reads like someone wrote it fast and wanted it over." He turned his coffee cup, and Yara noticed his hands — long, careful hands, hands that belonged to a man who handled delicate things. "There's an anomaly in the tide charts. Forty years of records, and along one twelve-mile stretch of coast the tides run wrong. Not much wrong. Minutes wrong. But consistent. Every year. Like something under the cliffs is breathing."

Yara felt the words go past her and keep going, all the way down to some floor inside her she'd boarded over years ago. *Like something under the cliffs is breathing.* She hated him a little for saying it out loud. She hated herself more for listening.

"There are caves along that shore," he went on, quieter now, the way men talk when they've stopped performing and started confessing. "Local legends say one of them charges admission. That's what the old trappers called it. Charges admission. Took a boat once, they say. Took gear. Never found the gear. I think the tide anomaly and the cave and my mentor's expedition are the same story wearing three coats, and I think if I can see that coastline from the air — just see it, one pass, photographs, nothing more — I'll know whether it's worth the rest of my life."

"One pass," Yara said. "That's what everyone says before the pass becomes two."

"I'm not everyone."

"Everyone isn't."

They stood there while the engine finished cooling outside, ticking away her margin, and Yara understood with sudden clarity that she was losing an argument she hadn't agreed to have. Not because he was winning it. Because some part of her — the part she kept locked below the waterline, the part that had once loved a place enough to stay — had heard *eleven years* and *no wreckage* and *breathing cliffs*, and had leaned forward.

She put her hand flat on the counter, the way she did when she needed the world to hold still.

"Here's what I do," she said. "I fly medicine. Insulin, antibiotics, vaccines, blood. I fly mail when the mail pays. I land, I unload, I leave, and nobody learns my name, and everybody lives. That's the whole business model. What I don't do is carry curious men toward coastlines that eat expeditions. Because when the curious man goes missing, the pilot goes looking, and when the pilot goes looking, the pilot stops coming home."

"That's not a business model," Teodor Vance said. "That's a eulogy."

"It's a rule."

"Rules written that carefully usually have blood in them."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the wind had come around to the northwest, dragging a low gray ceiling with it, and the barometer needle had slid another notch, and somewhere in her chest a needle she didn't keep on any instrument had also moved.

"Write down your coordinates," she said finally. "On paper. If I'm ever flying that way with time to kill and altitude to spare, I might look. Might. From the air. Once."

He wrote them. He wrote them fast, like a man afraid the offer would dissolve, and he pressed the paper across the counter with both hands, formal as a treaty.

Then he asked the question she should have seen coming from orbit.

"When does the next mail flight go north?"

"Two weeks, if the weather holds. It won't hold."

"So I wait here."

"You'll freeze here."

"I've frozen in worse places for worse reasons." He picked up his pack, which was heavier than a geologist's pack had any right to be, and settled in by the window like a man prepared to outlast a siege. "Eleven years, Ms. Pemberton. I can give this coast two more weeks."

* * *

She was wheels-up by 10:14, which meant she'd blown her eleven minutes by twenty-three, and she felt every wasted second like money spent.

The Caravan climbed out over the bay and turned north, and Kestrel Bay shrank beneath her to its true size — a scatter of tin roofs, a scar of gravel, a thread of smoke from Marta's stove pipe. Small enough to forget. That was the design.

She leveled off at nine thousand feet with the ceiling scraping her belly, and the coast unrolled ahead of her, gray water, black rock, white scars where avalanches had combed the slopes clean. Somewhere back there, a tall man with hungry eyes was drinking bad coffee and waiting for weather that wouldn't hold, and Yara told herself she'd already forgotten him.

She hadn't. That was the trouble.

Around noon, crossing the headlands with the fuel gauge honest and the sky still holding, she found herself doing arithmetic she had no business doing. Ninety miles north of Kestrel Bay. Twelve miles of wrong tides. She unfolded the paper he'd given her — she'd sworn she wouldn't, and then she had, somewhere over the first ridge — and the coordinates sat there in his cramped, careful handwriting like a confession.

She didn't divert. Diverting was a rule, and rules were load-bearing, and she'd learned what happened when you pulled one out.

But she banked two degrees north. Just two degrees. Just enough that the coastline she wasn't interested in slid into the edge of her windshield, far off, hazy, a dark seam between the water and the sky. Just enough to look.

From this height, at this distance, she couldn't see caves. Couldn't see anomalies. Couldn't see anything except the ordinary violence of a northern shore doing what northern shores do.

And still she looked longer than a pilot looks at coastline she doesn't care about. Still she held those two degrees until the headlands slid past abeam, and only then did she ease back on course, telling herself the detour had been wind correction. Telling herself the paper would go in the trash at the next fuel stop.

It didn't go in the trash. It went in the map case, under the sectional, where pilots keep the things they're not ready to admit they're keeping.

By the time she set down at the mining strip at Two Rivers, the barometer had fallen another ten points and the radio was full of stations reporting snow where snow had no business being in September. She fueled, signed the sheet, and stood a moment in the prop wash, watching the front build itself over the western hills like something with intent.

Land, unload, leave. She'd done all three. Her name was still hers. Her nose was still clean.

So why, she wondered, taxiing out into the rising wind, did it feel like she'd left something behind in Kestrel Bay — some small, dangerous seed of wanting, planted by a man who talked too much about breathing cliffs?

Why did she catch herself, climbing through the murk on the way home, thinking not about the weather or the freight or the rule, but about a single line from the incident report she'd never read, described by a voice she'd heard exactly once:

*No wreckage. No distress call. Nothing.*

And why — this was the thought that stayed with her all the way down through the clouds, the thought she'd still be carrying when the storm finally broke — why did the number eleven keep circling back? Eleven years since the expedition vanished. Eleven years of wrong tides. Eleven years of a legend that charged admission.

Somewhere on that coast, something had been counting too.
