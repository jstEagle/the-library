## 2. The Passenger Who Wouldn't Land

Teodor Vance had been in Kestrel Bay for six days before he understood that the town had a shape, and the shape was a funnel. Everything came down the hill from the airstrip — freight, mail, fuel drums, gossip — and pooled at the harbor, where it either dissolved or fermented. He'd spent those six days at the lodge window with his mentor's field notes spread across a card table, watching the same blue-and-white bush plane come and go like a metronome.

The pilot never came into the lodge. That was the first thing he learned about her. Other pilots drank coffee in the galley and argued about weather and left their gloves on the radiator to dry. She landed, shut down, stood by her wing while the freight was swapped out, signed nothing anyone could read from a distance, and was gone before the dust settled. Once — only once — she came as far as the porch, took a thermos of coffee from the cook without speaking, and went back.

Her name, when he finally pried it out of the harbormaster, was Yara Pemberton. The harbormaster said it the way you'd say the name of a weather system. *Pemberton's doing the run this week.* Nothing else. When Teodor asked where she kept her hangar, the man looked at him with something close to pity.

"She doesn't keep anything," he said. "That's the point of Pemberton."

Teodor had come three thousand kilometers on the strength of eleven-year-old paper. His mentor, Dr. Elias Vance — no relation, though Teodor had let people assume otherwise for years because it saved explaining — had led a four-person survey along the northern coast in the spring of 2013 and stopped existing somewhere between one tide chart and the next. No wreckage. No distress call. Nothing. The official report ran nine pages and concluded, in language so carefully bland it read like an apology, *environmental causes presumed.*

Elias had been more than a mentor. He'd been the man who taught Teodor that a rock told the truth even when nobody wanted to hear it, and that the wanting was exactly why you listened. When the news came, Teodor had been mid-thesis, and grief had done what grief does to young men: it had made him efficient. He finished the doctorate. He got the lectureship. He published. And every year, on the anniversary, he opened the box of Elias's last letters and read the one line that had never made sense:

*The coast is lying to us, T. Beautifully, but it's lying.*

Eleven years later, Teodor had finally found the lie. It was in the tide tables.

He'd found it the way most things are found — by accident, at two in the morning, cross-referencing old survey data against historical tidal records for a paper he wasn't even writing anymore. The coastal station nearest the expedition's last known position showed anomalies. Not dramatic ones. A few centimeters here, a timing offset there, a pattern of residuals that any reasonable analyst would file under instrument drift. But Teodor had spent a decade teaching undergraduates to distrust instrument drift as an explanation, because instrument drift is where geology hides its secrets. The residuals weren't random. They repeated. They repeated on a cycle, and the cycle matched nothing oceanographic he knew — but it matched, almost perfectly, the dates of the fresh-supply drops the local legend had supposedly inspired. Someone, some*thing*, was breathing through that coastline on a schedule, and the tide gauge had been recording its breath for forty years while everyone called it noise.

He needed to see the coast. Not from a boat — the charter skippers all refused that stretch anyway, muttering about the cave that charged admission, and Teodor had learned in six days that Kestrel Bay superstition was load-bearing architecture. From the air. One overflight. Two hours of fuel. A camera and a pair of eyes that had spent fifteen years reading landscapes for a living.

Which meant he needed Yara Pemberton, and Yara Pemberton, it was becoming clear, could not be hired.

*

He tried the direct approach first, because he was a scientist and believed in falsifiable hypotheses. He waited by the strip until she taxied in on a gray Thursday, walked up while she was still running through shutdown, and introduced himself with his university affiliation and a laminated map.

She looked at the map. She looked at him. She did not take the map.

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I heard." She pulled her headset off and hung it on the propeller blade, which he would learn later was a courtesy, a sign she intended to speak in full sentences. "You want a sightseeing flight over a stretch of coast that no boat will go near, in shoulder season, based on coordinates connected to an expedition that disappeared. I fly medicine and freight. I don't fly mysteries."

"It's not a mystery. It's a survey anomaly. There's a tide-gauge record—"

"Mr. Vance." She said his name like she was checking it against a list. "I know who you are. You've been asking questions at the harbor for six days. Do you know what happens to people who ask questions about that coast?"

"They get answers?"

"They get left alone," she said, "which is better than they deserve," and she turned back to her preflight.

Most men would have taken the hint. Teodor had once spent four months proving a colleague's celebrated dataset was fabricated, and the experience had burned out whatever circuit in his brain registers social warning signs. He came back the next day with the tide charts. She was loading insulin coolers and didn't stop loading them. He came back the day after with Elias's letters, and this time she paused — one beat, her hand flat on a crate, her eyes flicking to the envelope in his hand — and then finished the load and flew away.

It was the pause that kept him going. A woman truly indifferent doesn't pause.

*

On the seventh day, the storm came in from the west, and everything in Kestrel Bay stopped moving except the rain. Teodor sat in the lodge galley with the cook, a vast silent woman named Odile who fed him stew and tolerated his presence the way a cliff tolerates lichen, and he laid out his evidence on the table because there was no one else to lay it out for.

Odile wiped down the counter. "Pemberton won't take you."

"I've noticed."

"Then hear an old woman." She set down the cloth. "That girl has flown out of here seven years and never once stayed for supper. Never told anyone where she sleeps. Never let a soul buy her a drink twice. You think that's coldness. Maybe it is. But around here we figure a person who works that hard at not being known is holding something at arm's length, and whatever it is, it's heavy. You want to hire her to fly toward the thing she's avoiding? People don't thank you for that."

"What is she avoiding?"

Odile shrugged, magnificent and unhelpful. "Ask her. See how far it gets you."

But that night, going through Elias's letters again, Teodor found something he'd missed — or rather, something he'd read a hundred times without hearing. The last letter, dated four days before the disappearance, contained a single sentence about the local guide they'd hired: *Our man Sif says the tide's wrong this year. Says the sea is pulling back further than it has in living memory, and that when it does, the cave opens its mouth, and we should not be here when it does.*

Sif. The harbormaster had mentioned the name without being asked — *old Sif Halvorsen still keeps the trapline up past the point, if you're fool enough to walk out there* — and Teodor had filed it. Now he pulled it back out. A guide who warned the expedition away. A guide who was still alive, still on the coast, still keeping something. Eleven years of warnings, eleven years of wrong tides, and one survivor-shaped hole in the story.

He wrote the coordinates on a card that night. Not the survey grid references — those meant nothing to a pilot — but a plain latitude and longitude, the mouth of the cove where the expedition's last camp had been photographed from offshore, plus the tide table for the coming week with the anomalous lows circled. He stared at the card for a long time. Then he added, beneath it, in handwriting he hadn't used since graduate school, the one sentence he actually believed:

*My mentor died out there and no one ever looked. I'm asking you to look.*

*

She wouldn't take the card either. He left it wedged under the windscreen of her plane on the morning of the ninth day, having learned that ambushes worked better than conversations, and watched from the lodge window as she found it during her walkaround. She read it standing very still. Then she crumpled it in her fist, and Teodor felt the whole enterprise die in his chest —

— except she didn't throw it away. She put it in her jacket pocket. He saw that clearly, even at distance, even through rain: the small deliberate motion of a pocket flap being closed over something kept.

He gave her three days. On the third day she flew north and didn't come back until dark, and when she passed the lodge porch on her way to wherever she slept, he was sitting on the steps with two cups of coffee, because Odile had told him that offering a thing costs nothing and refusing a thing costs the refuser, and he wanted to watch her pay.

She stopped. Looked at the cups. Looked at him.

"You're still here."

"I'm still here."

"The answer's still no." But she took the coffee. She held it without drinking, warming her hands, staring out at the black water of the bay. For a moment — one unguarded moment, gone almost before it arrived — she looked less like a locked door and more like someone standing behind one. "Eleven years," she said, not to him exactly. "You've been carrying this eleven years and you can't set it down long enough to leave."

"Can you?" he asked, and immediately wished he hadn't, because her face closed like a valve.

"Everyone leaves eventually," she said. "The smart ones choose when." She set the untouched coffee on the rail and walked into the rain.

*

He should have booked the ferry south after that. Every rational instinct said so. He had a lectureship waiting, a department that already muttered about his obsession, a life assembled with such care that it functioned whether or not anyone lived inside it. Instead he rented a room by the week and started walking the shoreline south of town, reading the rocks, because if no one would fly him to the coast he would do it the slow way, the way Elias would have — boot leather and notebook, one outcrop at a time.

What he found within two days changed the texture of everything.

The coastal formation was basalt — old, layered, ordinary. Except it wasn't. At three separate points along the shore, the layering broke pattern in ways that made him stand with his hand pressed flat against cold stone for minutes at a time. Veins of something pale and fibrous ran through the basalt like marrow through bone, and where the veins surfaced, the rock around them was altered, bleached, chemically hungry. He sampled two of them, wrapped the specimens in socks, and sat on a boulder that evening with his heart going too fast for a man who was supposed to be objective.

He knew what mineralization like that could indicate. Everyone in his field knew, and everyone in his field knew what it meant when a formation like this sat on land with no claims staked, no permits filed, no survey data in any public database — a geological blank spot in one of the most thoroughly mapped countries on earth. Blank spots that size don't happen naturally. They're maintained.

*The coast is lying to us, T. Beautifully, but it's lying.*

Elias had seen it. Eleven years ago, with four people and a season's funding, Elias had seen enough of it to matter — and then Elias had stopped, permanently, and the blank spot had stayed blank, and the legend of the cave that charged admission had kept boats away, and the tide gauge had breathed its strange breath for another decade while the world looked elsewhere.

Someone had gotten there first. Someone had decided what the world would know. And the question that had been academic for eleven years — *what happened to the expedition?* — quietly reorganized itself into a much worse question: *what happened to the expedition, and who benefited from the answer?*

*

On the twelfth day, the storm broke and the flying resumed, and Teodor went back to the lodge window and his metronome plane. He had stopped trying to convince her. Convincing was pressure, and pressure made her seal. Instead he did the only thing he had left: he made himself legible. He worked on the porch where she'd have to pass him. He left his sample bags open where Odile could peer into them. He talked to the harbormaster about tides within earshot of the fuel dock. He became, in the small economy of Kestrel Bay, a known quantity — the fool who walks the shore — and fools, he was betting, are less threatening than petitioners.

Whether it worked, he couldn't tell. She flew her runs. She took her coffee from Odile on the porch twice, standing, saying perhaps forty words total. The card stayed in her pocket; he knew because he'd written a second one, with updated tide predictions, and left it under the windscreen, and the second card vanished too, and neither was ever in the trash.

Then, on the seventeenth day, she was gone longer than usual. North at dawn, and the light failed, and the bay filled with rain, and she still wasn't back. Odile stood at the window with her arms folded and said nothing for two hours, which from Odile was screaming. The harbormaster put a kettle on the radio channel and called twice. No answer. At the third hour, Teodor caught himself standing in the rain at the end of the strip, staring north into murk, understanding with unpleasant clarity that in seventeen days he had somehow acquired something to lose.

She came back at full dark, dead-stick quiet, landing on instruments and nerve. He watched her climb out, sway, steady herself against the strut. And then she did something she had never done in seventeen days of observation: she walked toward the lodge lights instead of away from them.

She stopped in front of him, soaked, exhausted, eyes shadowed. From her jacket she took both cards — his handwriting, softened by rain, the circled tide numbers still legible — and held them out.

"I flew over it today," she said. "Don't ask me why. I diverted. First time in seven years I've diverted." Her voice was flat with fatigue, but underneath the flatness something moved, something that sounded almost like anger at herself. "There's fog bank sitting on that coast that isn't on any forecast. It sits in one place, Mr. Vance. Fog doesn't sit in one place."

"Did you see the cove? The archway?"

"I saw fog." She pushed the cards into his hands and closed his fingers over them. "And I saw that you're going to keep standing on my strip until one of us breaks. So here's what I'll give you, and it's more than I should: the lowest tide in forty years hits that coast in ten days. Your numbers say so and the almanac says so, and old-timers here say the cave only shows its face on tides like that. If your mentor's expedition found anything, it found it at low water, and if there's anything left to find—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening, as if the sentence had gone further than she'd authorized. "Ten days. Lowest tide in forty years. That's all I'm telling you."

"And will you fly me?"

"No." She said it instantly, and it had the sound of a wall, not a decision. "I told you what I know because you won't use it. Boats won't go. Hikers can't reach it before the tide turns. You'll wait out the window in town like everyone else, and then you'll go home, and that coast will keep its own counsel. That's the best ending available. Take it."

She walked past him into the lodge. Through the window he watched Odile put a bowl of stew in front of her without being asked, and watched her, after a long moment, sit down and eat it — the first time he had ever seen her stop moving indoors.

*

That night Teodor sat with the cards spread on his table and understood that he had been given something stranger than a yes. She had refused the job and handed him the intelligence anyway. She had told him the tide window — the exact operational detail that made an independent attempt possible — while insisting he couldn't use it. Either she was managing him, feeding him just enough to keep him from doing something reckless, or—

Or she wanted him to make the attempt, and couldn't afford to say so, even to herself.

He thought about Odile's warning: *a person who works that hard at not being known is holding something at arm's length.* He thought about the pause when she'd seen Elias's letters. He thought about a woman who diverted a seven-year-straight course to look at a fog bank that shouldn't exist, then flew home and built a wall out of tide tables.

Curiosity, his mother used to say, is the only debt that grows when you ignore it.

He looked at the circled numbers. Ten days. Lowest tide in forty years. He had no boat, no pilot, no permission, and a growing suspicion that the blank spot on the map was blank because someone was paying to keep it that way — which meant that going alone wasn't brave, it was stupid, and going with her wasn't safe either, but at least it was stupid in company.

He didn't sleep. Near dawn he wrote a third card, and this one had only a date and a time on it, and the words: *Lowest tide in forty years. If you happen to divert again, I'll be on the shelf at first light. No charge for the company.*

He didn't deliver it. He told himself he'd decide tomorrow.

But the next morning, walking the shore with his samples knocking against his hip, he caught himself reading the waterline the way she must read weather — looking for the pull of the sea, the rhythm underneath, the count. And far up the beach, half-buried in kelp, he found a crate. Old wood, salt-scoured, stamped with a supply-company mark from Kestrel Bay. The wood was gray and ancient, but the nails were bright.

Fresh supplies, delivered to a coast no one visited, in a crate that had been opened recently and resealed.

Someone was still counting.
