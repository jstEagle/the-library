## 3. Lowest Tide in Forty Years

The rule was simple, and rules were the only thing keeping her alive: land, unload, leave.

Yara Pemberton had written it on a card and taped it above her instrument panel seven years ago, after the fire, after the funerals she'd watched from the back of a crowd that had every right to hate her. The ink had faded to a ghost of itself now, but she didn't need to read it. She could recite it the way other pilots recited emergency checklists. *Land, unload, leave before anyone learns your name.* It wasn't loneliness. She'd stopped calling it that around year three. It was professionalism. A pilot who belonged nowhere owed nothing to anybody, and owing nothing meant nobody got hurt when she left — and she always left.

So it was a bad sign, three days after dropping Teodor Vance at Kestrel Bay, that she caught herself doing arithmetic over the Bering Sea at six thousand feet with a hold full of insulin and no reason at all to be thinking about tides.

The cargo run was supposed to be simple. Two stops up the coast, then home to her hangar at Teller Flats where nobody knew her name beyond *the pilot* and nobody wanted more than that. But the wind out of the southwest was running hard, pushing her forty minutes ahead of schedule, and ahead of schedule was dangerous. Ahead of schedule meant idle time, and idle time meant thinking, and lately all her thinking kept circling back to a geologist with salt-and-pepper stubble who talked about tide charts the way other men talked about lost loves.

*"Forty-year low,"* he'd said, leaning across the table at the Bay's one café, his coffee going cold because he couldn't stop gesturing long enough to drink it. *"Syzygy plus the storm surge deficit plus the lunar perigee. The water's going to pull back farther than it has since 1984. And when it does—"*

*"When it does, you'll get mudflats and dead clams,"* she'd said. *"Not my problem."*

*"There's an archway in the cliffs eleven miles north of Cape Meridian. My mentor called it the Door. He flew over it twice in '12 and both times the sea had it sealed. If the legends are even half true—"*

*"Legends are how people die in this country."*

He'd smiled at that, like she'd said something charming instead of something true. *"Kestrel Bay kids grow up on it. The cave that charges admission. You pay the sea, and the sea lets you look."*

She'd told him no. She'd told him no twice more when he came to the strip with a charter offer that would have paid a month of hangar rent. She'd watched his chartered boat chug out of the harbor two mornings ago with him aboard and four crew he'd hired off the dock, and she'd felt the specific relief of a door closing on a draft.

That had been two days ago. Now here she was, holding altitude over open water, doing the math anyway.

Eleven miles north of Cape Meridian. Her route to the second stop bent within thirty miles of it. Thirty miles was nothing. Thirty miles was a five-minute dogleg, invisible from any shore, explainable as a weather detour if anyone ever asked — and no one would ask, because no one tracked her routes except the freight manifest, and the freight manifest didn't care where she looked.

*Land, unload, leave.*

"I'm not landing," she said aloud, to the cargo, to the card above the panel. "I'm looking. Looking isn't landing."

The Cessna Caravan's nose swung north, and the compass needle — the real one, the brass one bolted to the panel that she trusted over every digital readout — swung with her, steady and honest, pointing exactly where she told it to. That was the thing about compasses nobody appreciated. They didn't judge. They just went where the heading said, same as her.

* * *

The coast came up gray and enormous, basalt cliffs stacked in broken columns like the ribs of something that had died standing. Winter light lay flat on the water, silver-white, sourceless. She dropped to eight hundred feet and ran the shoreline, one eye on the fuel gauge, one on the cliff face sliding past her window.

For twenty minutes there was nothing but rock and the white scribble of waterfalls too thin to reach the sea. Then the cliffs stepped down into a series of terraces, and the terraces opened into a bay she'd flown past a hundred times without ever once looking at, because looking was the first step of every mistake she'd ever made.

The bay was empty of water.

Not drained — *withdrawn*. The whole floor of it lay exposed, a mile of wet black stone and tidal pools steaming faintly in the cold, kelp laid out in ropes and braids like the sea had gotten up mid-hairdo and walked away. Sandbars stood in ridges where no sandbar should be. A wreck showed its spine near the northern point, some old seiner, ribs green with forty years of weed, and she thought: *forty years. Nobody living has seen this bay empty.*

And there, at the head of the bay, where the cliffs folded together into a dark V —

The archway.

It was bigger than she'd imagined. That was the first dishonest thing her mind did, shrink it down to something manageable, and the eye corrected it ruthlessly as she banked for a closer pass. Forty feet of black basalt opening, framed in stone so smooth it looked cut rather than worn, and behind it a throat of darkness that swallowed the flat winter light without giving anything back. Water still trickled out of its base in a thin silver sheet, the last of the tide paying out its admission.

Teodor's voice in her memory, insistent, delighted: *"Charges admission. You pay the sea, and the sea lets you look."*

She circled once. Twice. On the third pass she noticed what she should have noticed on the first: the shelf. A broad apron of wave-cut rock extended from the archway's mouth, flat enough to land on, dry enough — just barely — at this tide. It was the kind of feature a bush pilot catalogued automatically, the way a gambler counted cards without meaning to. Landable. Marginal. One wheel-width of margin on either side, and the drop-off at the shelf's edge fell away into pools deep enough to swallow a strut.

She should have climbed out, set course for the second stop, and logged the whole thing under *curiosity, resisted.*

Instead she found herself checking the wind — offshore, ten knots, quartering across the shelf, doable — and the tide tables she'd pulled up on the tablet without remembering deciding to. Low water at 13:47. It was 13:20 now. Which gave her, by the coldest possible arithmetic, less than half an hour of exposed shelf, and every minute after that the sea would start buying its ground back.

"Looking," she reminded herself, on final. "Just looking."

The Caravan's tires touched the shelf with a slap and a shudder, stone through the gear, and she rode the brakes gently along the wet rock with her heart going like a drill motor, and when she finally stood on the yokes and brought it to a stop facing back the way she'd come — nose into the wind, always nose into the wind — the silence that came up through the floorboards was unlike any silence she knew. No engine wash, no surf. Just the drip of the bay draining, somewhere out past the wreck, patient as a clock.

She sat for a full minute with her hands on the wheel, waiting for the good sense to arrive.

It didn't. So she unbuckled, pulled on her parka, and stepped down onto stone no plane had ever touched.

* * *

The air smelled wrong for a coastline — mineral, cold-iron, like the inside of a bell. Her boots rang on the shelf. Out past the wreck, tide pools lay scattered across the black rock like coins spilled from a pocket, each one holding its own perfect inverted sky, and she walked between them carefully, absurdly careful, as though the sky might break.

The archway loomed. Up close it wasn't smooth at all; the apparent polish resolved into tool marks, deliberate, ancient or recent she couldn't say, channels cut into the basalt in patterns that repeated — spirals, notches, lines of short vertical strokes like a tally. Somebody had spent serious time on this rock, and somebody else had let the sea keep it hidden for longer than anyone in Kestrel Bay had been alive.

At the archway's base, just above the last trickle of retreating water, something glinted.

She crouched. A ring of iron, rusted to a nub, bolted into the stone — a mooring point, old style, the kind sealing crews used before the war. And beside it, half-buried in gravel, a length of chain fused into a single red-orange mass. Someone had tied boats here. Someone had worked here. The legend hadn't come from nothing; legends never did. They were stories people told to keep other people away from things that needed keeping away from.

Her hand was on the cold stone of the archway before she decided to touch it. The basalt was bitter-cold and faintly humming — no, not humming. Vibrating. Faintly, rhythmically, from somewhere deep inside the cliff, a pulse slow as breathing.

Behind her, out on the shelf, her radio crackled inside the plane. She heard it dimly through the open door — a voice, garbled, then clearer as the wind shifted:

*"—any aircraft on the shelf, any aircraft, this is the vessel Corvus inbound your position, we see you parked on our landing zone—"*

Yara turned.

The boat came around the southern point at speed, throwing a bow wave across the drowned-and-drained bay, white water chasing across stone that hadn't seen a wake in decades. A chartered hull, dock-worker sturdy, and standing at the rail with one hand raised — whether in greeting or warning she couldn't tell — a figure in a canvas coat with salt-and-pepper stubble catching the flat light.

Teodor Vance. Who was supposed to be in Kestrel Bay. Whose coordinates she had refused, whose job she had turned down three times, whose boat she had watched leave without her.

And who was now, unmistakably, coming straight toward the only dry landing spot on this coast, which she was currently occupying with twelve hundred pounds of other people's insulin.

"Oh, you have got to be kidding me," she said.

She jogged back across the shelf, boots hammering, the pulse in the stone keeping time beneath her. By the time she reached the Caravan the boat was close enough to shout across, and Teodor's voice carried over the water with the particular carrying power of a man accustomed to lecture halls:

*"Dr. Pemberton! I don't suppose you'd shift your aircraft sixty meters south!"*

*"I don't suppose you'd turn your boat around!"* she shouted back, climbing onto the wing strut for height. *"You're dragging a keel through a bay that's about to fill back up!"*

*"We've done the math! We have until roughly fourteen-thirty!"* He sounded delighted. He sounded like a man who had done the math and found, at the bottom of it, her. *"You're standing on the discovery of the century and complaining about parking!"*

She looked past him — at the archway, at the tally marks, at the iron ring bolted by hands long dead, at the faint tremor rising through the soles of her boots like something below the stone turning over in its sleep — and felt the rule card flutter somewhere behind her sternum, ink-faded, ignored.

*Land, unload, leave.* She'd landed. She hadn't unloaded. And leaving was starting to look like the one thing the sea wasn't going to permit.

The Corvus swung wide, throttling down, hunting for water deep enough to hold it. Teodor waved both arms now, urgent, pointing at the archway, at the dying light, at her.

"Sixty meters," Yara muttered, hauling the chocks loose. "One hour. And if the tide eats my airplane, Vance, you're swimming to Teller Flats to explain it."

She fired up the engine and taxied south along the shelf, toward the archway, toward whatever the sea had decided she'd paid enough to see — while behind her, over the drone of the prop, she could hear the Corvus's anchor chain rattling out across stone, loud as a countdown.
