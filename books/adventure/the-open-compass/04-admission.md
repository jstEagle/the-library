## 4. Admission

The archway swallowed sound before it swallowed light.

Yara felt it the moment she stepped under the black stone — the ocean's long breathing cut off behind her, replaced by a hush so complete she could hear her own pulse ticking in her ears. Her headlamp beam reached maybe thirty feet into a passage that kept going, walls slick and ribbed like the inside of some enormous throat.

"Forty years," Teodor said behind her. "That's what the tide tables say. Lowest water since 1984." His voice had gone strange — reverent, almost frightened. "Nobody's seen this entrance in four decades."

"Somebody has," Yara said.

She didn't know why she said it until her light found the first marker.

It was carved into the wall at shoulder height, just past where daylight died: three parallel grooves, each about a hand-span long, cut clean and deliberate into rock that otherwise wore its edges rough as torn flesh. Below them, smaller marks — tally strokes, dozens of them, worn soft by salt air but unmistakably made by a knife or chisel held by someone patient.

Teodor crouched beside them. He didn't touch. His gloved fingers hovered an inch off the stone, tracing without contact, the way you'd approach something sleeping.

"These aren't survey marks," he said. "Survey crews use paint, tags, GPS pins now. This is—" He stopped. Started again. "This is somebody keeping count."

"Counting what?"

"Days," he said quietly. "Or tides. Or trips in and out." He looked up at her, and even in the flat white glare of their headlamps she could see the color had left his face. "Yara, this is my mentor's handwriting."

"You said the expedition vanished eleven years ago."

"Vanished. Not never existed." He stood slowly, joints cracking, a big man trying to make himself small inside his own skin. "Elias Voss. My doctoral supervisor. He ran a coastal geology survey out of Kestrel Bay the summer of 2013. Four people. They flew up the coast on the twenty-third of June and were reported overdue on the ninth of July. The search covered two hundred miles of shoreline." His laugh came out wrong, hollow. "Two hundred miles. And they were standing inside a cave that only opens once every forty years."

"The legend says the cave charges admission," Yara said. "That's why nobody comes. That's why the old-timers keep the story alive."

"Admission," Teodor repeated. He was staring at the tally marks. "Maybe the admission isn't money."

* * *

They went deeper because neither of them could make themselves go back.

Yara told herself it was professional judgment — assess the passage, check the ceiling, confirm the exit window before committing. But the truth was simpler and worse: eleven years of questions were lying on the floor ahead of them, and Teodor Vance walked toward them like a man walking toward his own grave, and she couldn't let him do it alone. She'd spent seven years perfecting the art of leaving people alone. Apparently she'd forgotten how.

The passage widened after fifty meters. The floor leveled into pale sand scattered with shell fragments, and the walls opened into a chamber tall enough that their lamps couldn't find the roof. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with a rhythm like slow clockwork.

Then the gear.

A rusted carabiner, fused to the stone by orange corrosion. A coil of rope gone to gray mush, holding the shape of its last knot. A camp stove, its tank bloomed with rust bubbles, sitting upright as if someone had set it down meaning to come back. Everything arranged not scattered — placed against the walls, stacked, ordered. Someone had tidied this place.

"Elias was fastidious," Teodor murmured. "Drove his students insane. Every tool back in its loop, every sample logged before sundown." He lifted the stove, turned it over. Stenciled on the base: *Voss Coastal Survey — UVic*. "This is his. This is all his."

"How does equipment sit for eleven years and look like this?" Yara asked. "Rust, sure. But it's *arranged*. Salt air eats nylon in five. This rope—"

"I know." Teodor's voice had dropped to almost nothing. "I know what it looks like. It looks like someone maintained it."

Neither of them finished the thought. There were thoughts you didn't finish in dark places.

Twenty feet further, half-buried in the sand at the chamber's center, sat a field case.

Aluminum, olive drab, the kind universities bought by the dozen in the nineties. Sand had drifted against it in a smooth apron, but the latches were clear, and stenciled across the top in fading white letters: *E.V.* — and beneath that, smaller: *DO NOT OPEN WITHOUT CHAIN OF CUSTODY*.

Teodor went down on his knees in front of it like a man at an altar.

"That's his initials," he said. "That's his case. I carried that case for him for two field seasons. I packed it. I know the weight of it empty." His hands hovered over the latches. "Yara. It's sealed. The seals are intact. Nobody opened it."

"Eleven years ago somebody put it here," Yara said. "And then walked away from it. Or didn't walk anywhere at all." She swept her lamp across the chamber's far reaches, past the case, into shadows the light couldn't finish. "Teddy. Where are the bodies?"

The question landed in the silence like a dropped tool.

"We don't know there are bodies," Teodor said.

"Four people come into a cave that opens once every forty years. Three tons of gear. One sealed case. No bones, no boots, no—"

Her lamp caught it then, at the edge of the beam: a shape against the far wall, low and rectangular. She crossed the sand in six strides and stopped.

A sleeping platform. Raised on driftwood stilts above the damp floor, the way coastal trappers built them. A groundsheet, brittle but whole. On top of it, folded with military precision, a wool blanket gone stiff with salt.

And beside the platform, mounted on a wooden post driven into the sand, a shelf. On the shelf: a tin of matches, rusted shut. A candle stub. A plastic jug, clouded, half full of something. And a notebook wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord.

Fresh cord.

Yara knew rope. She spliced her own tie-downs, inspected every line on her plane twice a season. The cord binding the oilcloth was new — bright, unfrayed, the knots dressed tight and recent. Whatever else in this chamber belonged to 2013, that package had been touched within the year.

"Teodor," she said. "Look at the cord."

He came over. Looked. Went very still.

"Someone's been here," he said. "Recently."

"Someone's been coming here," Yara corrected, and heard her own voice go flat and cold, the pilot's voice, the voice she used when the weather turned. "For years. Maintaining the gear. Replacing the cord. Keeping the legend alive so nobody comes looking." She turned a slow circle, lamp sweeping the chamber. "This isn't a grave, Teddy. This is a *storage unit*. Somebody is using it."

* * *

The tide turned early.

Yara felt it before she understood it — a change in the air pressure, a faint new sound beneath the drip-clock rhythm, a whisper of moving water somewhere back along the passage they'd entered through. She checked her watch. Checked it again.

"We've been down here ninety minutes," she said. "Low slack should hold another hour at least."

"Currents in caves don't read the tables," Teodor said. "A surge from the open coast can stack water into a bottleneck. If the entrance channel narrows inland of us—"

"Then the water comes back faster than it left." She was already moving. "Go. Now. Move."

They ran. The chamber fell away behind them, the field case and the sleeping platform and the notebook dropping back into darkness, and Yara hated every step of the retreat with a hatred that surprised her — eleven years of answers abandoned on a shelf because the sea had changed its mind early. The passage narrowed. Their lamps threw jumping shadows. Ahead, the black archway of the entrance showed a thin line of gray daylight — and below it, water. Ankle-deep already, sheeting across the sand in long silent tongues, rising as they watched.

"It wasn't doing this a minute ago," Teodor gasped.

"Tide turned. Move *faster*."

They hit the water at the archway. Cold climbed Yara's shins, her knees — shockingly cold, glacier-fed cold, and moving with a shove that wasn't gentle. She grabbed the carved wall, the one with the tally marks, and pulled herself past it, and Teodor's hand closed on her pack strap and hauled her forward, and then they were both through the archway and onto the outer shelf —

— onto rock, dry rock, under open gray sky, with the sea still forty yards out and receding no longer but turning, the water's edge beginning its long crawl back toward them across the exposed boulders.

Yara lay on her back on the shelf and breathed.

Above them the cliff rose two hundred feet, streaked black and rust. Behind them the archway gaped, draining, the water inside it running backward out onto the shelf in thin silver ropes. In twenty minutes, maybe thirty, the crawl space under the arch would flood. In an hour the entrance itself would be ten feet under. She'd known that when she landed. She'd planned around it. The plan had assumed the tables were honest.

"The tide turned ninety minutes early," she said to the sky.

"Or we lost time in there," Teodor offered, but he didn't believe it either. She could hear it in his voice. Time doesn't bend; pressure systems do; something had pushed the water.

She sat up. And that was when she heard the sound from inside the cliff.

Not water. Deeper than water. A grinding, a shifting, a deep structural complaint, like a house settling except the house was a mountain. Small stones pattered down the passage behind them. Then larger ones. Then a boom rolled out of the rock and the gray daylight under the archway flickered — choked — dimmed to nothing.

The dust came next, pushed out of the tunnel mouth in a slow gray exhalation, smelling of crushed stone and ancient salt.

Teodor got to his feet very slowly. He walked to the archway. Put his hand into the dark where the light had been. Pulled back a palm coated fine and white.

"Rockfall," he said. "Inner passage. Behind us." He turned, and his face in the flat northern light looked ten years older than it had at breakfast. "The way we came in is gone."

Yara stood. Made herself think like a pilot, like a problem-solver, like the woman who had survived seven years by always knowing the exits. Outer shelf: dry for now, drowning within the hour, and beyond it open sea with a storm building on the horizon she did not want to name yet. Inner passage: sealed. The chamber with the case and the platform and the fresh-cut cord: unreachable, or reachable another way, if caves had other ways, which they sometimes did, which was how people got killed.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying it made it a plan instead of a fear. "Options. One: the shelf stays dry long enough for your boat crew to see us and come in. Two: there's a second vent — caves on drowned coastlines breathe through multiple openings, it's basic karst. Three—"

"Three," Teodor said, "is that whoever's been leaving fresh cord in there just heard several tons of rock come down, and knows exactly where we are."

The sea crawled closer across the boulders. Somewhere behind the cliff, in the dark, the dust was still settling on everything they hadn't had time to carry out — on the sealed case with Elias Voss's initials, on the notebook wrapped in new cord, on whatever truth four people had carried into this rock eleven years ago and however many of them had never carried it out.

Yara looked at the water coming, and at the man beside her, and understood with total clarity that the legend had been right all along.

The cave charged admission.

They had paid it the moment they stepped inside. Now it intended to keep them.
