## 5. What the Dead Were Carrying

The rockfall had come down like a held breath finally released.

Teodor stood with his back against the inner wall, headlamp beam cutting a cone through the dust, and listened to the last of it settle — a long gravel whisper, then nothing. The passage they'd come through was gone. Not blocked. Gone. Where there had been a corridor of black stone wide enough for two people to walk abreast, there was now a slope of shattered rock that rose to meet the ceiling, packed tight as mortar.

"Tell me you see another way out," Yara said behind him.

He swept the light left. Right. The beam found water — a sheet of it sliding across the floor where the flooded exit channel was backing up, already ankle-deep and rising in slow, patient pulses. Each pulse came a little further inland than the last.

"No," he said. "Not this way."

"Then we find another way." Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a woman who had decided panic was a luxury item she didn't stock. "Tide turned early. That means we have hours, not days, before this whole lower gallery goes under. So we go up or we go deeper, and we do it now."

She was already moving, wading through the shallows toward the far wall, testing the rock with her hands like a woman reading braille. Teodor followed, and it struck him — absurdly, inappropriately — that eleven years of imagining this place had never once included the sound of another person breathing in it.

He had imagined the cave since he was twenty-four years old. He had drawn it from memory of his mentor's last radio transmission, sketched it in the margins of notebooks, built it in his head out of grief and guesswork. In all those versions, he had been alone with the mystery. He had never accounted for Yara Pemberton, who moved through the dark like she'd been born owed a favor by it.

"Here," she said. "Crack in the wall. Air's moving."

He put his hand into the gap. Cool air threaded against his fingers, faint but constant, the way a cave breathes when it has somewhere else to be. Above the crack, the wall sloped back into shadow.

"That's our chimney," he said. "But not yet. Look."

His light had caught on something across the gallery — a shape too regular to be geology. A rectangle of pale fabric, half-buried under rubble near the base of the far wall.

Yara saw it at the same moment. He watched her whole body change, the pilot's economy dropping away, replaced by something warier.

"What is that?"

"Tarp," he said. His mouth had gone dry. "Or a tent fly. It's—"

He didn't finish. He was already crossing the gallery, boots splashing, the water sucking at each step like the cave wanted him to move slower. When he reached the shape he knelt and brushed away the grit with the side of his hand.

It was a tent fly, olive drab, rotted along one fold but intact enough to read the stencil on its corner: **BARENTS GEOSURVEY — EXP 2013-04**.

Eleven years. Eleven years of petitions, denied permits, closed files, a company that had dissolved itself like sugar in water rather than answer one question from one family. And here was the answer, stenciled in fading paint, lying in the dark forty meters inside a cliff that legend said charged admission.

"Teodor." Yara's voice, low. "There's more."

* * *

The camp was intact.

That was the first thing he understood, kneeling at the edge of it with his heart going like a piston: whoever had lived here had not died suddenly. The camp had been *kept*. Three dome tents in a row against the wall, pitched with care, guy lines still taut where the cold had preserved the nylon. A cooking area ringed with stones. Crates stacked and lashed. A surveyor's table made from a plank across two ammo boxes, and on it, weighted down with a carabiner against a draft that no longer existed, a field notebook with an elastic band still holding it shut.

And four sleeping bags.

Three were occupied.

Teodor's light found them one at a time, because some part of him insisted on doing this properly, in order, the way Elias Vance would have done it — method first, feeling later. The nearest bag held a man curled on his side, mummified by cold and dry air, his features smoothed into something almost peaceful. The second held a woman, arms crossed over her chest in a way that looked deliberate, arranged. The third held a younger man, maybe twenty-five, a Barents Geosurvey patch still legible on his jacket shoulder.

Names. He needed their names. He knew them; he had known them for eleven years, had recited them at every hearing where nobody listened. Henrik Ossler, team lead. Marta Reyes-Iqaluk, geophysicist. Denny Foss, field tech, youngest, the one whose mother had written to Teodor every Christmas for six years until the letters stopped.

"Denny," he said aloud. It came out cracked. He cleared his throat. "Denny Foss. That's — I met his mother. Twice."

Yara said nothing. She had taken off one glove and stood with her bare hand pressed flat against the rock wall, as if steadying herself, or the mountain. Her face in the lamplight was unreadable, but her eyes were moving — not over the bodies, he noticed. Over the walls. The ceiling. Counting exits, reading the rock, doing whatever pilots did instead of grieving.

"The fourth bag," she said quietly.

He turned. The empty sleeping bag lay apart from the others, nearer the inner wall, unzipped and folded back neatly. Beside it, a pair of boots, placed together, toes pointing outward. Whoever had left had left in socks, or in spare footwear, and had taken the time to fold the bag.

"You don't fold your bag if you're running," Yara said. "You don't fold it if you're dying. You fold it if—" She stopped.

"If you're coming back," Teodor finished. "Or if you're leaving a message."

He crouched by the surveyor's table and lifted the elastic band off the notebook with two fingers, the way you'd handle something consecrated. The cover was soft with damp. Inside, Elias Vance's handwriting marched across the pages in tight, slanted columns — depths, strike angles, assay notes, all of it annotated in that impatient shorthand Teodor had spent his own career imitating without ever quite matching.

The last entry was dated 14 March 2013. Nine days after the expedition's final scheduled check-in.

*E.V.: Core series C confirms. Extending grid north. H. wants to radio the company tonight. Told him wait — want full picture first. If this holds at depth, nothing else on the continent matters.*

Below that, in different ink, different hand — Henrik Ossler's blocky capitals:

*Company says stop sampling. Says seal the site. Says no further transmissions. E. refuses. Argument 0300.*

And below that, in pencil, pressed so hard the paper had nearly torn:

*They knew before we did.*

Teodor sat back on his heels. The cold of the stone came up through his knees and he let it, let it hold him steady.

"Yara," he said. "I need to show you something, and I need you to tell me what your instincts say, because mine are compromised."

* * *

What he showed her was the core boxes.

They were racked along the inner wall, wooden crates stenciled with the same expedition code, each slot holding a split steel sleeve with a cylinder of rock inside. Teodor pulled the sleeves one at a time and laid them on the surveyor's table under both their lights, working from oldest label to newest, and the story assembled itself in the order of the dates.

The early cores were ordinary. Greywacke, slate, veins of quartz — the dull grey skeleton of any coastal range. Then, core after core, the labels climbing through February and into March, the rock changed.

A band of pale greenish stone appeared, then thickened. Shot through it ran threads of something dense and metallic, and where his knife scratched it, the metal shone a flat, greasy silver that didn't tarnish.

"Do you know what you're looking at?" he asked.

"Mineralization," Yara said. "Obviously rich. Obviously wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Wrong latitude." She picked up a sleeve and turned it in the light, and he realized she wasn't just a pilot who happened to be standing here. Someone had taught her rocks. "This assemblage doesn't occur north of sixty. I've hauled drill crews all over this coast. Nobody cores this formation because the textbooks say it isn't here."

"It isn't here," Teodor said. "According to every survey ever published. Which is why Elias came — he'd found anomalies in old gravity data, magnetic readings that didn't match the maps. He thought the maps were wrong." He laid the last core down. "He was right. The maps weren't wrong by accident, Yara. They were wrong by omission. Somebody surveyed this strike decades ago and buried the data so deep even the companies forgot they had it. Or didn't forget."

Yara set the sleeve down carefully, the way you set down something loaded.

"And the rockfall," she said. "The one that sealed us in an hour ago. And the one that ended this expedition."

"Now you're reading my notes."

"I'm reading your face." She turned to face him fully. "Say it plainly. You think this was sabotage. Eleven years ago and an hour ago."

He looked at the three sleeping bags. At Denny Foss, twenty-five forever, whose mother's last letter to Teodor had said only, *I just want to know if he was warm, at the end.* He had flown out to answer her in person and had gotten as far as her driveway and turned around, because the honest answer was *nobody knows*, and he hadn't been able to carry it through her door.

Now he could carry something heavier than not-knowing, and he found, to his surprise, that it weighed less.

"There's no natural rockfall pattern in that collapse we came through," he said. "I've spent my career looking at failure surfaces. Natural falls leave a scalloped scar, a detachment plane. That ceiling came down in a slab, on a cut face. Someone drilled it and shot it. Same for 2013 — look at the rubble composition in the outer gallery, it matches a single bench blast, not progressive erosion." He heard his own voice go quiet and hard. "They found the strike. They told the company. The company told someone. And someone decided the cheapest way to bury a mineral deposit worth billions was to bury the men who found it, and call it weather."

Yara was silent for a long moment. Water pulsed at the edge of the lamplight, a little closer each time.

"The empty bag," she said at last. "Someone walked out. Before the blast, or after."

"After. The notebook says the argument was 0300 on the fourteenth. The blast — the seismic record from the regional station shows a small event on the fifteenth, magnitude one-point-eight, dismissed as ice movement. One of them got out between those two dates. Walked out of a cave everyone else died in, and then—" He gestured vaguely at the black above them, at the cliff, at the coastline beyond it. "Then spent eleven years making sure nobody else came in. The legends. The 'cave that charges admission.' Fresh supplies at the mouth. That's not folklore, Yara. That's a fence. A survivor built a fence around a grave and a secret, and kept it for a decade."

"And now we've broken through the fence," she said, "and the tide's coming in, and the people who killed three geologists to keep this quiet are presumably still in business."

"Yes."

She nodded slowly, and something in her expression shifted — not softer, exactly. Sharpened. Like a tool being reground to a finer edge.

"Then here's what happens next," she said. "We don't die in here. We get out, and we don't come back empty-handed, and we don't hand this to anyone with a company badge and a smile. Whatever's in that notebook and those cores goes to people who can't be bought." She glanced at the bodies, and for the first time her voice lost its edge, just slightly. "They waited eleven years. They can wait out a tide."

Teodor gathered the notebook, the most diagnostic cores, wrapped them in the rotted tent fly and lashed the bundle with cord from the camp stores. His hands were shaking and he let them shake. Grief, he thought, was just fuel that hadn't found its engine yet. Eleven years it had sat in him, inert, mislabeled as failure. Now it had a name and a direction, and it burned clean.

"Air's still moving in that crack," Yara said, already climbing toward it, testing holds. "Chimney goes up. If it breaches the surface, we're out. If it doesn't, we're deeper, and I'll want you to remember I told you the tide math didn't work."

"Yara."

She paused, half-turned in the light.

"The fourth sleeping bag," he said. "Whoever folded it. If they're alive — if they're the one keeping the legend — then when we get out of here, they're going to know we were inside. And they're going to come looking for us."

Yara looked back at the camp, at the three shapes the dark had kept, at the neat folded bag of the one who hadn't stayed.

"Good," she said. "Because I have questions too. Move."

Above them, unseen, the crack breathed its thin cold breath, and the water took another step across the floor.
