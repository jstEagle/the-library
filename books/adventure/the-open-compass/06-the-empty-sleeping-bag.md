## 6. The Empty Sleeping Bag

The headlamp beam found the sleeping bag first, and for a long moment that was all I could look at.

It was zipped shut.

Teodor saw it at the same time I did. I heard his breath change — a small catch, like a man stepping off a ledge he hadn't known was there. We stood in the chamber the rockfall had given us, two hundred feet inside a cliff that no longer had an exit we knew of, and we looked at a sleeping bag that someone had closed from the outside.

"Maybe it fell," he said. "Maybe — wind, water, something shifted it."

"Zippers don't shift themselves." My voice came out flat, pilot-flat, the voice I used on radios when the weather turned. "And nobody packs up camp and zips an empty bag unless they're trying to say something. Either to whoever comes next, or to themselves."

He didn't answer. He crouched beside it instead, careful as a man handling evidence, which I suppose he was. His fingers hovered over the fabric without touching. "Synthetic fill. This model stopped being made in — I'd have to check. Early two-thousands, maybe. It's been down here eleven years and it doesn't smell right for eleven years."

"What does it smell right for?"

He looked up at me, and the headlamp threw his shadow huge against the far wall, wavering with every breath he took. "Nothing," he said. "That's the point. It smells like nothing. Like it's been sealed in plastic until recently."

I turned my light away from him because I didn't want him reading my face. Eleven years is a long time for anything organic to keep its silence, but I'd flown enough remote strips to know what abandoned camps smell like when you crack them open — fuel and mildew and mouse droppings and the sweet-rot of things left behind. This chamber smelled of cold stone and old wax and, faintly, of paraffin. Someone had been burning candles down here. Recently enough that the air still carried it.

"Teo." I kept my voice low, though there was no one to hear us but the dead. "How many bodies did you count?"

"Three." He said it carefully. "In the main camp. Two in bags, one under a groundsheet. And the logbook stops mid-sentence in October 2013."

"And how many people were on the expedition?"

His jaw worked. "Five."

I did the arithmetic out loud, because saying numbers makes them real. "Three dead. One bag empty. That's four accounted for. Where's the fifth?"

"Gone." He stood up slowly, knees cracking — a man who'd spent too many years crouching over core samples. "Walked out. Or was carried. Or—"

"Or never left." I swept my light across the chamber again, slower this time, forcing myself to see it as a crime scene instead of a tomb. "Somebody's been leaving supplies at the mouth of this cave, Teo. Fresh rope. A case of matches that wasn't rain-swollen. That tin of biscuits by the entrance wasn't eleven years old — the label wasn't even sun-faded, and nothing down here sees sun, so if it were old it would be mold-furred. Somebody stocked this place. Recently. Repeatedly."

"For the survivor," he said. "If they lived nearby. If they come back."

"Nobody comes back to the cave their friends died in for eleven years." I said it harder than I meant to. "You don't leave biscuits for a ghost. You leave them for somebody who's *using* them. And if the survivor's alive and using them, then either they're living in here—" I let that sit, ugly, in the lamplight "—or they're coming and going through a way in that isn't the front door. Which means there's another way in. Which means the rockfall might not be the wall we think it is."

Teodor went very still. I watched the idea land on him the way weather lands on a windshield — all at once, blotting everything else out.

"The legends," he said quietly. "Kestrel Bay has a dozen stories about this cave. The tide takes what it's owed. The cave charges admission. Old Sif Halvorsen tells the version where you hear knocking inside the rock and you mustn't knock back." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "I always took them for sailor's nonsense. Every coastline has its haunted cave."

"Every coastline has its haunted cave," I agreed. "But most of them don't have fresh matches."

* * *

We worked while we talked, because working keeps panic at arm's length, and I've gotten very good at keeping things at arm's length. Teodor photographed everything with a phone that had no signal and wouldn't for the rest of its natural life, and I went through the camp stores with hands that wanted to shake and didn't let them.

The three dead had been dead a long time. I'll say only what needs saying: the cold and the dry air had kept them, and I made myself look at each one because it seemed like a debt owed, and because looking was the only way to learn what the scene wanted to tell me. The nearest body lay with its boots on. The second had a wristwatch still running — no, not running; stopped, but wound, the crown screwed down tight. Somebody had wound that watch after the others died. You don't wind a dead man's watch unless you're keeping time yourself.

The third was the one that undid me a little. She was curled near the expedition's radio, and the radio's battery compartment was empty. Not corroded-empty, not broken-empty. Removed-empty, cleanly, the contacts bright. Somebody had taken the batteries out and taken them away. And beside her, laid parallel to her arm like a tool set out for morning, was a pry bar.

"You don't leave a pry bar," I told Teodor, when he came to stand beside me. "Not in a survival situation. Not unless you've got somewhere better to put it, or you expect to need it again soon."

"Yara." His voice had gone strange. "Come look at the core boxes."

They were stacked against the north wall, aluminum, expedition-stamped, and most of them were empty. Not looted-empty — organized-empty, lids seated, foam inserts intact, arranged in a stack with the precision of a quartermaster. Only three boxes still held samples, and those three had been placed apart from the others, wrapped in wax paper, labeled in a hand that wasn't shaking.

Eleven years ago, someone had stood in this dark with dying friends or already-dead friends, and they had inventoried. They had packed. They had separated the important samples from the rest and hidden them, and they had left the empties neat, and they had walked out through whatever passage the sea had opened for them, and they had never told anyone. Because if they'd told anyone, we'd have known. There would have been a rescue, an inquiry, names in a newspaper. Instead there was a legend about a cave that charges admission, and five men on a manifest, and three bodies in the dark, and a country full of people who believed the story because believing stories is easier than digging.

"Somebody decided the truth was worth more than the search," Teodor said. His voice cracked on *truth*, and I understood — this was his mentor's handwriting on those labels. Elias Vance, initials E.V., the field case we'd found sealed in the inner passage before the rockfall cut us off from it. The man had died down here, or been left to die down here, and his student was standing over the evidence eleven years later with a headlamp and a hole in his chest where the grief had gone septic.

"Or somebody decided the truth was dangerous," I said, "and the survivor agreed to keep quiet, and then couldn't quite bring himself to let the samples rot. Sentiment. Or insurance. Hard to tell the difference sometimes."

"Which do you think?"

I thought about the wound watch. About the pry bar laid straight. About the biscuits at the mouth with their unfaded labels.

"I think," I said, "that whoever walked out of here has been back. More than once. And I think they weren't checking on the dead."

* * *

We found the second entrance an hour later, and I want it on the record that I found it, because Teodor spent that hour photographing labels and I spent it doing the thing I'm actually good at, which is reading terrain.

Air moves. That's the whole secret. A sealed chamber with two people burning breath and candle wax develops a draft, and a draft means a throat, and a throat means a way through. I killed both our lamps and we stood in absolute black — the kind of black that presses on your eyes like thumbs — and I held a strip of torn chart paper from my jacket pocket and watched which way the flame-less flutter leaned. It leaned inland. Away from the flooded entrance. Toward the back wall.

"There," I said, and my voice sounded enormous in the dark, and something about speaking into that blackness made the hair stand up on my arms, because it occurred to me that other people had stood in exactly this spot, in exactly this dark, holding exactly such a flame, and had known precisely what I now knew.

The back wall wasn't a wall. It was a collapse — old, healed over with flowstone, but healed around a gap at floor level, a crawl maybe eighteen inches high, breathing cold clean air against my face. Air that smelled of salt and kelp and open sky.

"That goes somewhere," Teodor said behind me.

"That goes *out*." I pressed my palm flat to the gap and felt the tide-cool draft steady as a heartbeat. "Low, wet, probably sumps partway. But it moves air from the sea side, which means it surfaces below the cliff line somewhere, which means—"

"Which means the survivor didn't walk out the front door at low tide like a tourist." He'd come up beside me, and I could feel the heat of him in the cold. "They crawled. They knew this route existed. Eleven years ago, in the dark, with whatever injuries they had, they found a crawlway that took us an hour and a lamp and a piece of chart paper."

"Because they'd been in before," I said. "Before the expedition. Before the deaths. Teo — this wasn't a discovery that went wrong. This was a return visit. Somebody brought an expedition back to a place they already knew, and then the place killed four of them, and the fifth crawled out the back and spent eleven years making sure nobody else came in."

The words came out of me in a rush and then hung there, and I heard how they sounded, and I didn't like it. Because there were only two kinds of people who spend eleven years fencing off a secret. The kind protecting something. And the kind hiding something.

"Yara." Teodor's lamp came up, and in the sudden light his face was gray. "The supplies at the mouth. If the survivor's been maintaining them — if they've been *coming back* —"

"Then they may have come back recently." I finished it for him, because finishing sentences is what you do when the alternative is letting them finish themselves. "And the tide turned early today. And the rockfall came down within an hour of us entering. And I don't believe in coincidence stacking three deep."

"You think someone knows we're in here."

"I think someone knew we were *coming* in here. I think they watched us land on the shelf. I think they may have been watching this cave for eleven years the way a farmer watches a fence line." I turned my light toward the crawlway, toward the thread of sea-smelling air, and made myself say the rest of it plainly, because plain speech is the last luxury paranoia leaves you. "And I think if there's a person at the end of that crawl, we'd rather meet them crawling than have them find us asleep."

* * *

Here is what I will admit, in the privacy of my own head, since the page is the closest thing I have to privacy: I have run from places my whole adult life, and I know the exact flavor of fear that comes from being *found*. It isn't the fear of danger. Danger is simple; danger is weather; you fly around it or through it and log the hours. The fear of being found is different. It's the fear that the name you buried will surface, that the settlement fire will follow you across a thousand miles of tundra like a dog that won't stop tracking, that some stranger will look at you and see not a competent bush pilot with a clean record and a short list of clients, but the woman who flew away while the smoke was still rising.

Standing in that chamber, with the empty sleeping bag zipped shut behind me and a breathing wall in front of me, I felt that fear come home. Because whoever had maintained this legend — whoever had left the matches and the biscuits and the fresh rope coiled just inside the arch — that person had done the thing I do. They had built a fence out of story. They had made a place so unpleasant to think about that the world would leave it alone. The difference between us was that they'd stayed to guard their fence, and I'd flown from mine, and neither strategy had actually kept anyone safe.

"Talk to me," Teodor said. "You've gone somewhere."

"I've gone nowhere useful." I checked my watch. Four hours to the next low tide, if the anomaly in his mentor's charts held, and the anomaly had held well enough to strand us. "Options. One: we dig at the rockfall and hope it's shallow. Two: we take the crawl and pray it doesn't sump. Three: we sit here with the dead and wait for the sea to open the front door, assuming the sea is feeling generous and nobody's waiting outside with opinions."

"Three sounds worst."

"Three sounds worst to me too, and yet it's the one I trust." I crouched at the crawlway and played my light along it: wet stone, a squeeze, then darkness that curved away. "But three assumes the person who's been stocking this cave wants us dead. And I keep coming back to the supplies, Teo. You don't leave matches for people you mean to kill. Matches are for keeping people *alive*. Whoever's been tending this place has spent eleven years keeping something alive in here — and I don't think it was the memory."

"Then what?"

I looked back once at the camp. At the three still shapes. At the watch with its wound crown. At the empty bag, zipped with terrible neatness, like a sentence left deliberately unfinished.

"A witness," I said. "A witness they're keeping fed. Or—" and the thought arrived whole and horrible, the way the worst ones do, "—a prisoner."

Teodor stared at me. Somewhere deeper in the rock, faint as a pulse, came a sound I chose not to interpret.

Knocking.

* * *

We gave the crawl forty minutes and it gave us back a verdict: it narrowed past the squeeze into a slot that swallowed my shoulders and laughed at Teodor's, and beyond the slot the sound of moving water said *sump* in the oldest language there is. So it was the rockfall, then, or the wait, and I chose the rockfall, because waiting in a grave with a possible third party moving around in the dark behind a wall is not patience, it's inventory — ours.

We hauled rubble for two hours by lamplight, passing stones hand to hand in a rhythm that kept us from thinking, and I will say this for terror: it organizes. We cleared perhaps a ton. Behind it, more rock, packed tighter, the work of gravity and not of men — I checked the faces of the debris for tool marks and found none, and hated myself a little for being relieved, and hated myself more for checking.

It was during the fourth hour, wedged in the gap with my arms full of stone, that I understood the thing I'd been circling since the sleeping bag. I set the rock down. I sat back on my heels in the grit and the cold, and I said it out loud, because Teodor needed to hear it and because saying it would make me act on it.

"The legends are a fence," I said. "Somebody built them. Somebody maintains them. Somebody has been feeding this cave for eleven years like you'd feed a dog you didn't want wandering. And every year the fence holds, the truth stays buried, and everybody who paid for that burial stays unpunished. We didn't stumble into a haunted cave, Teo. We broke into a vault. And vaults have owners."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Sif Halvorsen tells the best version of the legend. Everyone says so. She's been telling it for decades."

"She's eighty-some years old and lives alone at the end of the Kestrel Bay road," I said, "and she's the one person on this coast who never asks a bush pilot her business, and I have always liked her enormously for it, and right now that fact is sitting in my stomach like ballast." I picked up another stone. "When we get out — and we're going to get out — the first thing I'm doing is landing at her strip. And the second thing I'm doing is asking her a question I should have asked years ago."

"Which is?"

I passed him the rock and met his eyes over it, and the lamp-light made us both look like the dead men we were cataloguing, and I said:

"*Who taught you the legend?*"
