## 7. Breach

The water reached the 2013 camp at 3:14 in the morning.

I know because I was watching my watch, counting the seconds between waves, as if arithmetic could hold back the sea. The first surge came up the channel we'd mapped in the dark — a black tongue licking over the rusted frame of a cook stove, retreating, coming back higher. By 3:20 the sleeping bags were floating. By 3:30 the three of them — the ones who never walked out — had begun to lift, gently, like the cave was trying to give them back.

"We can't let it take them," Yara said.

Her voice was flat. That was the thing I'd learned about Yara Pemberton in the thirty hours since the rockfall sealed us in: she flattened everything. Fear, grief, the fact that we'd found her mentor's initials stenciled on a field case that had been sitting in the dark for eleven years. She flattened it all into something she could carry.

"They've been down here a decade," I said. "The water's been taking care of them longer than we have."

"That's not what I mean." She turned her headlamp on me. "Reeve's people will come. You said it yourself — someone's been leaving supplies at the mouth. Whoever they are, when they find this camp disturbed, when they find the case gone—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The sea does a cleaner job than any company man."

I understood then. She wasn't talking about dignity. She was talking about evidence.

* * *

We'd spent the first day mapping our prison, because that's what I do — I map. It's the geologist's answer to panic: when the world becomes intolerable, measure it. The inner passage where the rockfall had come down was choked with maybe forty tons of collapsed limestone, slabs the size of truck hoods stacked in a jumble that went back further than our lights could reach. Digging was out. The main entrance was a drowned throat now, the tide pouring through it twice a day like the cave swallowing on a schedule.

But caves breathe. That's the first thing any caver knows. There's always a second way air moves, and where air moves, stone is thin.

I found it on the second night, lying flat on a shelf of rock with my cheek against the stone, feeling the draft. Cold air, moving out. Steady. It came from a fissure near the ceiling of a side chamber, a crack no wider than my hand, breathing the smell of salt and open sky.

"There," I said, and Yara climbed up beside me and put her palm against the crack the way you'd check a horse's nostrils.

"Thin?" she asked.

"Thin enough." I didn't say the rest: thin enough if we had explosives, which we didn't, and time, which the tide was spending for us.

Yara was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Flare compound."

I looked at her.

"Marine flares. The expedition's kit is still in the camp — I saw the case. Old ones, but the compound doesn't spoil the way the ignition does. Potassium perchlorate, strontium nitrate, a binder. It's not dynamite. It's not even close to dynamite. But packed into a fissure that thin—" She shrugged. "It's a lever, not a sledgehammer. You'd know better than me whether the rock wants to move."

I put my hand back on the draft and thought about the strata I'd been reading since we got trapped, because reading strata is the only prayer I know. The limestone here was interbedded with shale — I'd seen it in the fallen slabs, dark partings between the gray. Shale fails easily. A shock along the bedding plane, and the fissure would either open like a book or bring the ceiling down on our heads.

"It wants to move," I said. "The question is whether it moves the way we want."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we'll have bigger problems than the tide."

Yara almost smiled. Almost. "I've had bigger problems than the tide my whole life. Fine. Show me where to pack it."

* * *

We worked through what remained of the night, and I'll record honestly that most of the engineering was hers. I know rock. She knew the flares — she'd carried them on every flight for seven years, she told me, for downed aircraft and polar bears and once, she said, for a wedding that went wrong in ways she declined to describe. She cracked the flare casings with a rock hammer, tapped out the gray-red compound onto a groundsheet from the dead men's camp, and mixed it with wax she shaved from a candle stub, because the binder in eleven-year-old flares goes crumbly and crumbly compound is a bomb you can't aim.

"Loose and dense," she muttered, working. "Packed, not compressed. You compress it and it detonates instead of burns, and detonation in a crack that thin brings the whole chamber down."

"You've done this before."

"I've done stupid things before. This is a subset."

I carried the compound up in the expedition's mess tins, three loads, and tamped it into the fissure with a tent stake, working it as deep as my arm would reach. The draft pushed cold against my wrist the whole time, like the cave breathing on me, like something deciding whether to let us go.

The fuse was the hard part. We had no fuse. Yara solved it with a length of surveyor's tape soaked in stove alcohol, which burns slow and uneven and is, by any professional standard, an insane choice. She fed it into the compound, and then we retreated — down, across the chamber, behind a fin of rock she'd selected with a surveyor's eye for blast shadow.

"Ten seconds of burn, maybe eight," she said. "When it goes, get flat and open your mouth."

"Why open my—"

"Pressure wave. It'll try to pop your eardrums. Open your mouth and it equalizes." She lay down beside me, close enough that I could feel her breathing, fast and shallow, the only unflat thing about her. "Teodor. If this brings the roof down—"

"It won't."

"You don't know that."

"No," I admitted. "But I know my mentor spent his last year looking for what's in that case, and I know the tide will be back in four hours, and I know I'd rather be crushed looking for the way out than drowned waiting for one."

She was quiet. Then: "That's the first honest thing you've said since the rockfall." And she struck the match.

* * *

I have been in earthquakes. I have stood beside blasting crews in three countries. Nothing prepared me for what a flare compound does in a throat of limestone.

It wasn't a boom. It was a *whump* — a deep, wet concussion I felt in my teeth and sternum before I heard it — and then the chamber filled with red smoke and the sound of stone shifting, a long grinding exhalation, like a glacier calving in slow motion. Dust came down in curtains. Something large struck the floor thirty feet from our fin of rock and I felt it through the ground, through my ribs, through the eleven years of rock between me and my mentor's bones.

Then: silence. And through the silence, threading down through the red haze, the unmistakable sound of the sea.

Yara was up before the dust began to settle, headlamp beam swinging. "Teodor. Teodor, look."

The fissure had opened. Not wide — a gap maybe forty centimeters across, a jagged slot angling up through the rock toward a gray suggestion of light. The blast had done what I'd hoped: found the shale bedding and split it, peeling the stone back along its own weakness. The cave had a mouth again.

It also had a new pile of rubble directly beneath the slot, and a ceiling that groaned once, softly, like an old man settling, and I chose not to think about that.

"Air's coming in," I said, and it was — I could feel the draft reverse, the cave inhaling, the pressure equalizing through our new wound. "That's good. Moving air means it's stable. Mostly."

"Mostly." She was already climbing.

The slot was brutal. Forty centimeters is enough for a body if the body is willing to be a snake, and we were willing. I went first because I was lighter and because she'd done the compound and I'd do the crawling — that was the arithmetic of fairness we'd fallen into without discussing it. I wedged my shoulders in, exhaled everything, and pushed. The rock held me the way rock does: completely, impersonally. At the narrowest point I had to turn my head sideways to breathe, my cheek against wet stone, and for ten seconds I was certain I'd misjudged and I would die in a crack in the earth with red flare smoke in my hair, and then the slot widened, and I was through, hauling myself up a slope of broken shale toward the gray.

The gray got brighter. Then it was light — actual light, overcast, ordinary daylight — and I came out onto the tidal shelf like something being born, and lay there gasping in the wind and the rain and the smell of the open sea.

Yara came out thirty seconds later, and for a moment neither of us said anything. We just lay on the shelf, two people returned to the world, while the rain hit our faces and the wind tried to take our hoods and the tide — I looked at the waterline and my stomach dropped — the tide was already back, a long gray swell rolling in over the shelf, maybe two hundred meters out and closing.

"We have to move," she said. "Now."

* * *

Here is what I did, and I have gone over it a hundred times since, and I would do it again.

The field case was still inside. We'd left it at the edge of the flooded passage, wrapped in the groundsheet, because hauling it through the crawl hadn't been possible and we'd planned — God help us, we'd *planned* — to come back for it at the next low tide. It held the core samples. It held the logs. It held the photographs that showed, in my mentor's own handwriting, an ore-grade mineralization in formations where the textbooks said nothing of the kind could exist. It held the reason three men died and a legend was manufactured and a company man named Aldous Reeve would, I was now certain, do anything to keep it buried.

And the tide was coming in at two hundred meters and closing.

"Wait here," I said, and went back into the slot.

Yara shouted my name. I heard it once, distorted by stone, and then I was in the dark again, crawling backward down my own escape route, because I could not — I physically could not — let that case go into the water. Elias Vance had carried those cores out of a living rock face with his hands. He'd written *confirmed, tell no one* on a sample tag and then he'd died in the dark for it, and I was not going to hand his life's work to the Atlantic because the crawl was tight and the tide was fast.

The camp was under thirty centimeters of water. The case floated when I lifted it — the seal had held, bless Elias's paranoia — and I got it to the base of the slot and realized, in the rising water, in the failing light of my headlamp, that I could not get it up the crawl. It was too wide. It would jam, and I'd jam with it, and the sea would take all three of us.

So I opened it.

The water was at my waist. The tide was pushing through the drowned entrance behind me with an audible hiss. I opened the case and I took out what my hands found first — three core samples in their waxed sleeves, each the length of my forearm, each tagged in Elias's handwriting — and I zipped them inside my jacket, against my chest, and I left the case.

I left the logs. I left the photographs. I left the sealed envelope with his initials that I hadn't been able to bring myself to open, and I left the rest of the cores, and I stood in the flooding camp of the dead and I made the only trade available: their paper for his stone. Then I swam-crawled back up the slot with the samples grinding against my ribs and the sea climbing the walls behind me, and when I came out into the light Yara had the rope ready and she hauled me the last three meters onto the shelf like landing a fish.

The field case went into the water eleven minutes later. I stood on the shelf and watched the surge take it — watched it roll once, catch on a shelf of rock, hang there as if deciding — and then a wave lifted it clean off the shelf and the sea closed over it, and eleven years of evidence became the ocean's problem.

Yara put her hand on my shoulder. She didn't say *I'm sorry*. She didn't say *you did the right thing*. She said, "Can they read what's in your jacket?"

I pulled out a core sample. The waxed sleeve was soaked but the tag inside was dry, protected the way Elias had protected everything — double-bagged, labeled twice, the handwriting small and furious. *E. Vance. Station 9. Confirmed. Tell no one.*

"They can read this," I said.

"Then walk," Yara said. "Because whoever's been leaving supplies at that cave mouth is going to come looking, and the tide just told them we were here."

* * *

We climbed. The shelf gave way to scree, the scree to a goat track Yara read like a chart, and by the time the cave mouth behind us was a black archway swallowing the returning sea, we were four hundred feet up the cliff face with the storm season's first real wind shoving at our backs. My chest ached where the samples rode against it. Three cylinders of stone. Three sentences in a dead man's handwriting. It seemed impossible that the whole weight of it — the strike, the cover-up, the three bodies, the empty sleeping bag, the legend maintained like a fence — could fit against my ribs.

"Teodor." Yara had stopped on the track, looking back down the coast. "Smoke."

I followed her gaze. Far down the shoreline, where the tidal shelf gave way to a shingle beach, a thread of woodsmoke rose from behind a windbreak of spruce. A camp. Someone was camped within sight of the cave, in the worst weather of the year, in a place with no reason to be.

"Supplies," I said. "The legend-keeper."

"Or the people who pay for the legend." Her jaw was set. "We go the other way. Over the top, down the river, and we don't stop until we're in Kestrel Bay."

"And then what? We walk into the bay with these—" I touched my jacket. "Reeve will be waiting. He was waiting before. Now we're carrying the one thing he can't allow to exist."

Yara looked at me for a long moment. Rain ran off her hood. Below us the sea hammered the shelf, and somewhere under that water lay a field case with my mentor's initials, and somewhere behind that spruce windbreak a thread of smoke rose straight up in the wind, patient as a held breath.

"Then we'd better decide whose side we're on before we land," she said, and turned up the track, and I put my hand flat against my chest, against the stone, and followed her into the storm.
