## 8. Debrief Nobody Asked For

The Cessna came out of the overcast at four hundred feet with salt spray beading on the windshield and both of us smelling like the inside of a wet boot. Teodor had his forehead against the window, watching Kestrel Bay assemble itself out of the murk — the breakwater, the cannery roof gone to rust, the fuel tanks with their faded company logo. I watched him watching it. He had a core sample tube wrapped in his spare shirt in his lap and he was holding it the way some men hold a sleeping child.

"Radio's quiet," he said.

"It's always quiet."

"You'd think after two people nearly drown in a sea cave, somebody would—"

"Nobody knows yet." I leveled out for final. "We haven't told anybody."

He turned that over. I could hear him turning it over, the way you can hear a man turn a stone in his hands looking for what's underneath. That was the trouble with geologists. They never accepted a surface.

* * *

Kestrel Bay has one strip, gravel, nine hundred feet if they're feeling generous, and it ends in a swamp that has swallowed better planes than mine. You come in low over the water because the hills will kill you and the water only dents you. I'd made this approach maybe three hundred times. Land, unload, leave before anyone learns your name — that was the rule, my rule, seven years old and worn smooth as a river rock from handling.

Except today the strip wasn't empty.

There was a truck parked at the mid-field marker, dark green, new tires, engine running so the exhaust stood up straight in the cold air. And beside it stood a man in a parka too clean for the coast, hands in his pockets, watching us come in with the patience of someone who already knew we were coming.

My stomach did something old and familiar. Not fear exactly. Recognition. There are men who wait at airstrips the way spiders wait in doorframes, and I have spent seven years learning to tell them from the ones waiting to greet somebody. This one had the stillness wrong. Greeting-men shift their weight. Waiting-men don't.

"Wheels down," I said, mostly to myself. "Unload, refuel, leave."

Teodor looked at the truck, then at me. "You know him?"

"No."

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure I don't want to." I flared her in and the gravel took us with its usual argument, and before the tailwheel had settled, before the prop had even begun to spin down, the man was walking toward us. That was the other thing about waiting-men. When they move, they move like the decision was made hours ago.

I cut the mixture and let the silence come up around us, that ringing quiet after six hours of engine drone. The man stopped ten feet off the wing, which is polite distance, which is trained distance, and smiled like a man who has practiced smiling at people who don't want to see it.

"Ms. Pemberton." His voice carried easily across the strip. "Aldous Reeve. I'd hoped to catch you."

Nobody catches me. That's the point of the whole arrangement. I keep no schedule anyone can read, file flight plans under three different names depending on the district, and land at Kestrel Bay when the fuel gauge tells me to and not before. So either Reeve had been watching the bay for days, or he had a source, or —

Or he'd known where the cave was, and known roughly when the tide would give it back, and done the arithmetic.

That thought went through me cold as seawater through a boot seam.

"Mr. Reeve." I stayed in the seat. There's a reason pilots stay in the seat. It keeps your hands near things that matter. "You have me at a disadvantage."

"I imagine you feel that way often." He said it kindly, which made it worse. "I represent Meridian Resource Group. We hold mineral exploration leases along this stretch of coast. I understand you've had an eventful day."

"We had a rockfall," Teodor said, climbing out onto the strut. He had the sample tube behind his back now, casual as anything, and I wanted to grab his collar and haul him back into the seat. "Bad luck. Nearly didn't walk away from it."

"Yes." Reeve's eyes moved to him, weighed him, dismissed him, came back to me. Dismissal is a technique. I've used it myself. "Unstable cliffs, that whole coastline. The winter storms undermine them. A cave that opens at extreme tide, fills again — honestly, Ms. Pemberton, I'm surprised more people don't get caught in there. The locals' stories keep most folks clear, but stories only work on people who listen to them."

"And we don't listen," I said.

"You struck me as a woman who listens very carefully." He reached into his coat and I felt my hand drift toward the door frame, toward nothing useful, and then his hand came out with an envelope instead of a gun, and somehow that was worse. "Which is why I'll be brief."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hush money: it doesn't look like guilt. It looks like kindness. It arrives with soft words and reasonable explanations and the unmistakable aroma of a man who would very much like to help you avoid trouble. Trouble being defined, always, as whatever happens next.

Reeve laid it out standing on the gravel with the wind pulling at his coat tails: Meridian had survey crews working the headlands; the cliffs were dangerous; the company had liability concerns; if word got around that tourists or treasure hunters could wander into that cave at low tide, someone would die eventually, and the lawsuits would fall on the leaseholder. So the company preferred, naturally, that the incident remain what it plainly was — an accident, weather-caused, survived by luck.

"Naturally," I said.

"The scientific community can be excitable." He glanced at Teodor without much interest. "Dr. Vance, your mentor's expedition was a tragedy. Eleven years ago. Rockfall, same section of coast. The inquest was thorough. I'd hate to see his name dragged back into the papers on the strength of some rusted gear and a story about legends."

I watched Teodor take that. Watched the way his jaw set and his hand tightened on the strut and the color came up his neck. Eleven years ago. Same section of coast. He hadn't told Reeve any of that. Which meant Reeve had told himself, which meant Reeve knew precisely what was in that cave, or had known once, and had just handed us the shape of it while pretending to describe the weather.

"The gear was rusted," Teodor said carefully. "Nothing worth papers."

"Of course not." Reeve smiled again. From the envelope he drew a fold of bills, counted half of them out with his thumb, and held them out to me. "Salvage rights. The equipment in the cave belongs to the leaseholder. You've recovered some of it — the case, I believe, and some tools. Standard compensation for recovery and, frankly, for your trouble. And discretion regarding the location. Fishermen talk. We'd rather they didn't."

The money sat there in the wind between his fingers.

I should tell you I hesitated. I'd like to say the cave was still on my skin, the cold of it, the dark, the three bodies arranged by someone else's hands, the empty sleeping bag folded with terrible neatness. I'd like to say I looked at that envelope and saw the rockfall coming down and heard the sea filling the passage and understood exactly what I was being paid to bury.

But here's the truth, and I've promised myself I'd stop lying about smaller things first: my hand came out almost before I decided anything. Seven years of taking payment for silence — not the dramatic kind, not envelopes at airstrips, but the ordinary kind, the freight I flew for outfits that didn't ask questions and didn't answer mine, the manifests I didn't read closely, the passengers I didn't look at twice — seven years of that builds a groove, and my hand rolled right down into it like a wheel into a rut.

I took the money. Counted it, because counting makes it business. Put it in my breast pocket, zipped the pocket.

"Location stays quiet," I said. "Case and tools are yours. We'll send coordinates of what we recovered."

"Coordinates aren't necessary." Reeve's smile warmed another degree. "We know where our own property is."

And there it was. Clean as a confession, delivered like a courtesy. He knew where the case was because he knew what the cave held, had probably known since before Teodor's mentor ever filed a float plan. The legends weren't keeping people out. They were keeping people from asking why.

"Anything else?" I asked.

"Just gratitude." He stepped back. "And a small caution. Storm season's starting early this year. The passes will close soon. A prudent pilot might consider staying on the ground until spring." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Safe travels, Ms. Pemberton. Dr. Vance."

He walked back to the truck. It pulled away toward the village road, unhurried, exhaust unspooling behind it, and the sound of it lasted long after it was out of sight.

* * *

Teodor didn't speak until the truck was gone. Then: "You took it."

"Yes."

"You took it. After everything in there. After—" He stopped, because there was nowhere to put the sentence that wasn't the cave, and neither of us wanted to go back in there yet. "Yara. He just told us they killed those people. Or caused it. Same cliff, eleven years apart? That's not geology. That's a pattern. And you shook his hand and pocketed his cash like it was a delivery fee."

"I didn't shake his hand."

"That's your objection?"

"My objection is that you stood there holding a stolen core sample behind your back like a teenager hiding cigarettes, and he clocked it, and he let us keep it, which means he doesn't care whether we have it. Think about what that means." I swung out of the plane finally, legs stiff, boots hitting gravel. "It means the samples don't matter to him. It means whatever's in that rock, he's confident enough to let two witnesses walk around holding proof of it. Either it proves nothing — or proving it costs more than we can pay."

"So we do nothing. Is that it? We take his money and fly home and let him—" He gestured at the coast, the gray water, the invisible cave somewhere under all of it. "Three people died in there, Yara. One of them was my teacher. I found his boot. I found his *boot*, and you took an envelope."

The wind came off the bay and pushed between us. Down at the dock a gull screamed at something and got no answer.

"You want to know why I took it?" I started pulling the tie-downs, because hands need work. "Because I've met men like Reeve before. Men who show up before the wheels stop spinning. And every time, the ones who refused the envelope ended up in the ground or in the paper, and the ones who took it got to choose their moment later. That money isn't payment. It's a receipt. It's him writing us down in a ledger as bought, and bought people get watched less than dangerous people. I just bought us time."

"For what?"

"I don't know yet." I yanked the tie-down free. "Time."

"That's not a plan."

"No," I agreed. "It's a survival strategy. I recommend it. It's kept me alive longer than plans have."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide something, the way he'd decided things all day — to charter the boat, to follow me onto the shelf, to crawl into the dark. He was a man who decided fast and paid later, and I recognized it because it used to be how I lived, before I learned that deciding slow was the only luxury the sky gives you.

"There was a fourth name on the expedition manifest," he said quietly. "Halvorsen. Sif Halvorsen. Guide and camp keeper. Her body was never recovered. The inquest assumed the sea took her." He nodded toward the village, toward the road the truck had taken. "The trapper everyone talks about out here. The one who tells the cave stories. The one who 'keeps the legend.' Her name is Sif Halvorsen."

The wind picked up. Somewhere behind the hills, thunder that wasn't thunder — the ice going out on the upper river, or a quarry blast, or nothing.

"She walked out," Teodor said. "Eleven years ago she walked out of that cave, and she's been standing guard over it ever since, and today the lowest tide in forty years opened the gate while we happened to be standing there. Reeve's men didn't find us. She didn't warn us off. Somebody let that tide happen." He shouldered his bag, the sample tube wrapped safe inside his shirt against his chest like a second heartbeat. "I'm going to go find her. You can drop me at the lodge and wash your hands, or you can come with me. But I don't think we were discovered today, Yara. I think we were *invited*."

He walked off toward the village road without waiting for my answer, which was either brave or stupid, and having just watched him argue with a company man on an open airstrip, I was leaning toward stupid.

I stood by the plane with Reeve's money pressing flat and cold against my chest through the zipped pocket, and I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the wheels touched: the man had been waiting for us. Not for the plane — for *us*. He'd known the tide table. He'd known the cave. He'd known, maybe, that a pilot with my habits and a geologist with my passenger would end up in that archway sooner or later, and he'd been ready with an envelope and a story about unstable cliffs, and the story had fit so neatly that I'd taken the money before I even noticed the fit.

Bought people get watched less than dangerous people. I'd told myself that on the strip, and it was even true.

But there's a third category I hadn't mentioned, the one I was standing in now with salt drying on my cheeks and a stranger's blood-money in my pocket: bought people who start asking questions anyway. Reeve would have a file on me by nightfall. And files, in my experience, have a way of growing appendices.

I fueled the plane. Checked the ties. Looked at the sky, which was building something in the west, big and slow and purple-black, storm season arriving early just like the man said.

Then I walked up the road after Teodor Vance, because the alternative was sitting alone in a plane with an envelope, and I have done that, and it turns out the envelope gets heavier every year.

Sif Halvorsen. I turned the name over as I walked. Keeper of the legend. Keeper of supplies at a cave mouth. Empty sleeping bag.

Somebody had been feeding that secret for eleven years, and I had a sudden, certain feeling that whoever it was already knew my name — and had known it a lot longer than Aldous Reeve had.
