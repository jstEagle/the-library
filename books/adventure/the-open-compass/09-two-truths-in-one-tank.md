## 9. Two Truths in One Tank

Teodor found the envelope under the windshield wiper of Yara's Cessna at six in the morning, and by seven he had counted it twice.

Forty thousand dollars. Banded, crisp, smelling faintly of the machine oil someone had handled it with. A business card clipped to the top: ALDOUS REEVE, REGIONAL LIAISON. No company name. No address. Just a phone number written in ballpoint on the back and a single line in the same handwriting: *For your trouble. There'll be more when the paperwork settles.*

He stood in the cold hangar light with the money in his hands and felt something he hadn't felt since the day he'd identified his mentor's remains from a photograph — that specific vertigo of grief discovering it has a price tag.

Yara was already at the fuel pump, running through her pre-flight ritual like a woman saying rosary. He watched her from across the apron: check the caps, drain the sumps, wipe each sample on the white rag she kept folded over her belt. She did everything twice. She trusted nothing, including her own hands.

"Yara."

She didn't turn. "If you're here to talk about tide tables again, I've got a med run to Fort Nelson by eight."

"It's not tide tables." He held up the envelope. "It's forty grand in a windshield wiper."

The sump cup stopped mid-drain. For three full seconds she didn't move, and Teodor understood he was watching a person decide between several versions of herself. Then she finished the drain, screwed the cup home, and turned around with a face as flat as the horizon.

"So you took it."

"I counted it. I didn't take it." He walked toward her across the wet concrete. "You did."

"Someone had to." She said it the way you'd say *someone had to take out the garbage*. "Reeve doesn't make offers twice. You refuse a man like that, he starts wondering what else you'll refuse."

"And accepting makes us what, exactly?"

"Alive." She pulled the rag off her belt and wiped her hands, slow, deliberate, buying time or performing calm — with her it was always both. "That's what it makes us. Alive, funded, and boring. Two more bush pilots who saw some rocks and got paid for their trouble. That's the whole story. That's the story everyone tells, including us."

"The story includes three bodies in a cave."

"The story includes three bodies in a cave that nobody's looking for," she said. "Eleven years, Teo. Nobody came. Not police, not search and rescue, not family. You know why? Because somebody made sure there was nothing left to find. And now we've gone and found it anyway, and the only question on the table is whether we get to keep breathing while we decide what to do about it." She tossed the rag onto the wing strut. "Forty thousand says yes."

"That's not an answer. That's a muzzle with a dollar sign on it."

"Everything's a muzzle. Some of them are just quieter than others."

* * *

They fought in the hangar because the wind had come up overnight and neither of them wanted to shout over it outside, and shouting inside a corrugated steel building is its own kind of weather. It echoed. Every accusation came back at them twice.

"You pocketed core samples out of a grave," Yara said. "Don't lecture me about dirty hands."

"I pocketed evidence. There's a difference."

"Is there? From where I'm standing it looks like two people who both took things that weren't theirs and are now deciding which theft was the moral one."

"The samples go to a lab. The money goes into your tank fund. One of those leads somewhere."

"Both of them lead somewhere. That's the problem." She climbed onto the wing root to check the fuel cap, which she'd already checked, and spoke down at him from the height of it. "You want to know what happens if we walk into a university with those samples? I'll tell you, because I've flown this coast long enough to know how these stories end. First, a professor gets excited. Then a department gets excited. Then a press release. Then a man in a good coat shows up in Kestrel Bay asking polite questions about chain of custody, and somewhere around question four he stops being polite, and somewhere around question nine one of us stops answering them. Permanently. You've read enough expedition reports to know I'm not inventing this."

"I've read enough expedition reports," Teodor said, "to know that's exactly what happened in 2013. Which is the argument for finishing it, not burying it deeper."

"Finishing it." She laughed, and there was no humor in it, just a hard dry sound like gravel going through a hopper. "You think truth is a destination. Fly straight, land safe, hand over the goods. I used to think that too."

"Used to?"

The word landed wrong and he saw it land wrong — saw her shoulders set before she'd decided to let them. But the hangar was cold and the fight was already moving and she answered anyway, maybe because stopping would have cost more.

"There was a settlement up the Liard," she said. "Grayling Bend. Forty-one people, give or take. Schoolhouse, diesel generator, a nurse who flew in twice a month. I was twenty-four and I thought I was their lifeline. Medevacs, mail, fresh insulin when the fridge died. I knew every kid's name. I ate supper at the Kowalskis' every Thursday. Mrs. Kowalski made borscht that could raise the dead." Her hands were very still on the fuel cap. "There was a mining outfit upstream. Exploratory, they said. Low impact, they said. Then the tailings pond failed in spring breakup and the creek went orange, and I'm the one who flew the water samples out because nobody else had a plane. And the results came back bad. Really bad. Arsenic, selenium, the whole ugly alphabet."

"What happened?"

"I told them." Simple words; her voice made them complicated. "I told everybody. I stood up in the schoolhouse with the lab report and I read it out loud, because I believed — God help me, I actually believed — that sunlight was disinfectant. That if people knew, it would get fixed." She looked at him then, and her eyes were the gray of ice before it goes blue. "What got fixed was the lawsuit. The company settled fast and quiet, bought out the holdouts, and the ones who wouldn't sell spent two years losing everything slowly instead of quickly. And the pilot who read the report out loud — well. Nobody wanted to charter the girl who brought the bad news. Can't blame them. Bad news is bad for property values. I lost my base, my contracts, my name on the board at the flying club. So I moved north and I invented a new rule: land, unload, leave. Don't learn names. Names are liabilities."

"Yara—"

"Three weeks after I left Grayling Bend for the last time," she said, over him, relentless now, a woman opening a door she'd kept locked so long the frame came away with it, "the community hall burned down. Arson, probably. Never proven. Eleven people were sleeping in it because their houses had been bought out from under them and it was the only warm building left. Including Tomasz Kowalski. He was nineteen. He fixed my landing gear once for free because I couldn't afford it, and he told me I was the only reason the Bend still felt connected to anything." Her jaw worked. "So don't stand there and tell me truth is a destination. I drove that road. Truth is a road with bodies on the shoulder, and I put some of them there by refusing to shut up when shutting up might have saved them."

Teodor stood very still in the echoing hangar. Somewhere outside, the wind dragged a loose strap along a tie-down ring, a small metallic ticking like a clock.

"You don't know the fire was because of you."

"No," she agreed. "I don't know. That's the worst part. I've had seven years of not knowing, and not knowing is heavier than guilt. Guilt you can carry. Not knowing you have to keep picking up to see if it's gotten heavier." She jumped down off the wing, landed light, and started coiling the fuel hose with sharp efficient motions. "Reeve's offer isn't a bribe, Teo. It's a mercy. It's the universe finally offering me the version where I keep my mouth shut and nobody burns."

"And the version where the next cave full of people stays buried?"

She flinched. Actually flinched, small, like a bird taking shot.

"There's always a next cave," he pressed, hating himself a little and doing it anyway. "You know that. Silence doesn't end things. Silence just moves them downstream to whoever can afford the current. Reeve's employer buried eleven men and women once already. You're not buying peace with that money. You're buying a subscription."

"Maybe peace on a subscription is all anybody gets." She hung the hose on its reel and faced him fully, arms crossed, the whole fortress of her assembled at last. "Here's what I know, Vance. I know that I am alive because I learned to stop telling people things. I know that you are alive right now, standing in this hangar arguing with me, because I took an envelope off a windshield. And I know that if we do it your way — fly the samples south, blow the whistle, be heroes — then either we die, or we live and something like Grayling Bend happens again with our fingerprints on it. Those are the options. Pick one."

"Third option," Teodor said quietly. "We do it carefully. Witnesses first. Sif Halvorsen knows more than she's told us — you said it yourself, the supplies at the cave mouth were too regular to be accidental. Somebody's been maintaining that legend for a decade. Find out who and why, and we're not two lunatics with a rock, we're a case. Cases survive. Heroes don't, but cases do."

For a moment something moved behind her eyes — the thing he'd seen in the cave, that hunger for the black archway, curiosity reaching up through the permafrost of her caution like green shoots through snow. Then she killed it. He watched her kill it. It was almost a physical act, a door closing on a lit room.

"Sif's an old woman with a rifle and a grudge against visitors," Yara said. "And I've got a med run to Fort Nelson."

"Yara."

"Don't." She swung up into the cockpit and the door banged shut, and through the glass he watched her run the start checklist with hands that were not quite steady. The engine caught on the second try. Propwash flattened the puddles on the apron and shoved him back a step, and she taxied out without looking at him, without waving, the way she taxied past every settlement on the coast — land, unload, leave, don't learn names.

Except she'd told him hers. Or the shape of it, anyway — the wound underneath, the fire, the boy named Tomasz who never got old. She'd handed him the coordinates of herself and then taken off to get away from having done it.

Teodor stood in the emptying hangar with the envelope in his jacket pocket, forty thousand dollars pressing against his ribs like a second heart, and understood that he'd gotten the argument wrong. Not the facts — the facts were on his side and always would be. He'd gotten the *person* wrong. You couldn't argue a woman out of a fortress built from a burned hall and a dead boy. You could only wait for her to open the gate herself, and pray the thing that made her open it arrived before Reeve's people did.

He walked back to the lodge, sat down at the little table under the window, and spread Sif Halvorsen's cryptic note on the wood beside the tide tables. *Come alone when the water's low. Bring nothing you can't burn.* He'd assumed it was about the cave.

Now, replaying the old woman's voice in his head — the way she'd said *bring nothing*, the weight she'd put on it — he began to suspect it wasn't about the cave at all.

Outside, Yara's plane droned away to the northeast, shrinking into the gray, carrying two truths in one tank and burning through both.

He picked up the phone in the lobby and asked the operator for the Halvorsen trapline frequency, and waited, listening to the static, for someone to pick up.
