## 10. The Third Survivor

The summons came the way weather comes to the interior — not announced so much as *felt*, a pressure change in the ordinary air of Kestrel Bay.

Yara was elbow-deep in the Otter's cowling, third day of chasing a fuel-line vapor lock that came and went like a bad mood, when the kid appeared at the hangar door. Eleven, maybe twelve, parka two sizes too big, standing with the particular stillness of someone who'd been told exactly what to say and was afraid of getting it wrong.

"You're the pilot," the kid said. Not a question. Everyone in Kestrel Bay knew the plane; nobody knew her name, and she'd worked hard for eleven days to keep it that way since the cave.

"I'm the pilot."

"Sif Halvorsen wants you. Up the river trail. Before dark." The kid shifted. "She said you'd know what it's about." A pause, then, quieter, like an apology: "She said to tell you she knows about the sleeping bag."

Yara's wrench stopped mid-turn.

The kid watched her the way kids watch animals — without pretending not to. Then he turned and jogged off down the boardwalk, boots slapping, done with his errand and visibly glad of it.

Yara stood there a long moment with her hand still on the engine. The vapor lock could wait. It had been waiting three days; it would keep.

*She knows about the sleeping bag.*

Nobody knew about the sleeping bag. She and Teodor had agreed — shaken hands on it at the airstrip like two conspirators in a bad play — that what they'd found stayed between them until they decided otherwise. Reeve had gotten his money and his silence. The field case was somewhere under forty feet of cold North Pacific water. And the only person alive who'd seen the empty sleeping bag besides Teodor was Yara herself.

Unless.

Unless the sleeping bag hadn't been empty when its owner left it.

* * *

The river trail climbed away from the bay through alder and black spruce, mud frozen into ridges that turned your ankles if you hurried. Yara didn't hurry. She wanted the walk. Wanted the time to decide whether she was walking toward something or being walked into something, and by the time the trees opened onto the bench above the river, she still hadn't decided.

The cabin sat where every trapper's cabin sits: close enough to water, far enough from neighbors, built by someone who expected to die there and had made peace with it. Sod roof over rough logs, smoke rising thin and straight from a stovepipe — straight smoke, Yara noted automatically, meaning high pressure holding, meaning good flying weather tomorrow, because some part of her brain never stopped doing that math even when the rest of her wished it would shut up.

An old woman stood in the yard splitting kindling. She didn't look up when Yara stopped at the edge of the clearing. She finished the split, set the axe against the block with care, and only then raised her head.

Sif Halvorsen was seventy if she was a day, wide through the shoulders the way old lifeworkers get, with a face like driftwood — grayed, checked, but dense as iron underneath. Her eyes were pale blue and completely unbothered, and they went through Yara the way a lamp goes through fog.

"You're taller than I figured," Sif said. "Come in before the light goes. Tea's on."

"I didn't say who I—"

"You're the one who flew the geologist out to the coast. You're the one whose plane came back heavy with questions. And you're the one," Sif said, picking up her kindling basket, "who looked at my cabin door twice on your way past last week and pretended you weren't looking. People who pretend not to look are always the ones worth talking to. Come."

Inside, the cabin smelled of spruce smoke, dried fish, and paraffin. One room, immaculate. A bed, a stove, a table scarred by sixty years of knives and mugs. On the wall above the table hung a framed chart of the coast — hand-annotated, decades of corrections in different inks — and Yara's eye snagged on it the way Teodor's eye must have snagged on tide tables: professionally, helplessly.

One mark on the chart was circled in red ink gone brown with age. The archway. The cave.

"Sit," Sif said. "You'll want to hear this sitting down, or you won't want to hear it at all."

Yara sat. Sif poured tea from a dented pot, two cups, no ceremony, and lowered herself into the chair opposite with the economy of someone whose joints had been negotiating with her for years and had reached a settlement both sides resented.

"My name is Sif Halvorsen," she said. "I have lived on this coast fifty-one years. And eleven years ago I crawled out of that cave with a broken arm, a dead husband, and a story nobody would believe."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside a raven said something rude and moved on.

"There were four of us in 2013," Sif continued. "My husband Anders, who ran traplines and knew stone better than any man alive. Two men from the south — Dr. Ellery Vance's people. Core drillers. And me, because somebody had to cook and somebody had to keep the radio working, and Anders trusted me with machines more than most men trust their own hands."

Yara's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "Vance."

"Teodor's father? No." Sif shook her head slowly. "His mentor. The man who taught him everything, I'd guess, from how the boy talked about him when he came asking questions around the bay. Ellery Vance led the expedition. He wasn't in the cave when it came down. He was at the boat camp. Which is why he spent the rest of his life trying to prove what happened in there, and why his student is now finishing the job with worse equipment and better stubbornness."

"The rockfall," Yara said. Her voice came out flat. "You were inside."

"We were inside when the mountain came down." Sif said it the way you'd report a tide table. Factual. Finished. "Not a natural fall. I've known that coast my whole life — frost-heave, spring melt, I can read a cliff like you read sky. That rock came down because somebody put charges in the fault line above the entrance and closed the door behind us. Three days after we radioed in our first assay results."

The tea had gone cold in Yara's hands. She hadn't noticed drinking any of it.

"Anders died in the first hour. The two drillers lasted six days. I lasted nine, on what was in the packs, and then I found the secondary vent — the one you two blasted open, though yours was lower down; mine was a chimney near the ceiling, tight as a coffin and half as comfortable. I broke my arm getting through it and walked eleven miles down the coast with it hanging wrong, and when I finally reached a settlement and told them what happened, they told me the expedition had already been reported lost. All hands. Weather event. Very sad."

"They reported you dead while you were walking."

"They reported us all dead the day after the rockfall." Sif's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it — it never rose. "Somebody wanted the story closed before anyone thought to check the closing. And the assay results — the ones we radioed in — those were buried deeper than we were."

Yara set the cup down. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. "The supplies. At the cave mouth. Fresh rope, rations — we saw the wrappings. Years of them."

"Mine." Sif said it simply. "Every spring, low water, I row down and leave stores at the shelf. Rope, food, a signal mirror, matches in wax. In case whoever came out of that cave wasn't finished coming out." She looked at Yara with those unbothered eyes. "In case it was one of my drillers, gone strange from nine days in the dark, living wild in the hills. Or in case someone else found the way in and needed a way out. I kept the legend alive for the same reason. 'Charges admission,' the story goes — the cave takes a toll, stay away. Fear keeps people honest. Fear kept that place empty for eleven years except for me and my bundles."

"And then we happened."

"And then you happened." Something moved at the corner of Sif's mouth — not quite a smile. "Two fools in a hole in the ground during a turning tide. I watched your plane land on the shelf from the ridge, and I aged ten years watching the water come back in. When you flew out, I sent the boy. I'd have come myself, but my knees have opinions now."

"Why?" Yara asked. The question came out harder than she intended. "Why tell me? Why bring me into this? You kept the secret eleven years. Keep it. I took Reeve's money. I'm not a hero, Mrs. Halvorsen. I fly cargo and I mind my business, and minding my business has kept me alive longer than heroism keeps most people."

Sif studied her for a long moment. Then she got up, slow, joints complaining audibly, and went to the bed. From beneath it she dragged a wooden box — oilcloth-wrapped, latched, heavy-looking — and set it on the table between them without opening it.

"Because you took the money," Sif said, "and it's eating you. I could see it across a room. I've watched guilty money do that to people for fifty years." She laid one weathered hand flat on the box lid. "And because in nine days in the dark, I learned one thing worth learning: secrets don't keep people safe. They just decide who gets to be afraid alone."

Yara said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a confession, and she wasn't ready to confess — not the fire, not the settlement she'd flown away from, not the seven years of land-unload-leave. But Sif watched her like a woman reading a chart, marking positions, and Yara had the distinct feeling she'd just been plotted.

"Reeve works for Meridian Consolidated," Sif said. "Same company, same name at the top of the letterhead, eleven years running. They bought the assay data's suppression along with everything else. The strike under that coast is worth more than every town from here to the border, and it sits under ground that will never survive being mined — which means it can never see daylight legally, which means it has to stay buried, and everyone who knows it exists has to stay buried with it. That's the arithmetic. It hasn't changed since 2013. It's just gotten more expensive."

"You think they'll come after us."

"I think they already started. Hush money is step one. Step two is making sure the hush holds." Sif unlatched the box — and stopped, hands resting on the lid, looking at Yara with sudden directness. "Before I show you what's in here, I need to know something. Not what you'll say. What you are. Because I'm seventy-three years old, girl, and I don't have time to be wrong about people."

"What do you need to know?"

"When the moment comes — and it will come — are you the one who flies away?" Sif's voice was quiet. "Or are you the one who stays?"

The stove ticked. The raven outside said something else. Yara thought of a fire seven years ago, and a takeoff roll in smoke, and seven years of wheels-up before anyone learned her name, and heard herself say:

"I don't know."

It was the first honest thing she'd said aloud in months, maybe years, and it landed in the cabin like a dropped tool.

Sif nodded slowly, as if that too had been plotted on her chart. "Honest," she said. "Good. Liars answer fast." She lifted the lid.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a ledger — a real one, cloth-bound, its spine cracked white — and beside it a bundle of papers yellowed at the edges: radio logs, carbon copies of assay reports, a hand-drawn map of the cave system marked in a firm draftsman's hand. *E. Vance,* said the map's corner. Eleven years old and preserved like scripture.

"This is the proof," Sif said. "Payments. Deliveries. Dates. Names — not all of them, but enough. Eleven years of Meridian's money moving through accounts that shouldn't exist, buying silence, buying the legend, buying me." Her jaw tightened. "They paid me too, at the start. A pension for a dead woman. I took it the same as you took Reeve's envelope. I used it to buy rope and rations for a cave full of ghosts. We're not so different, you and I. We just chose different things to be guilty about."

Yara looked at the ledger and felt the weight of it the way she felt ice on the wings — not yet dangerous, but present, and growing.

"Why give it to me?"

"I'm not giving it to you yet." Sif closed the box and latched it. "I'm showing it to you. There's a difference. Giving means you carry it, and carrying it means they come for you the way they came for Anders and the drillers — quietly, with paperwork and rockfalls." She pushed the box an inch toward Yara, then drew it back. "But storm season opens soon. The passes will close. And when they close, Meridian thinks everything up here freezes along with the ground — including witnesses. That's when they move. That's always when they move."

"So what do you want from me?"

"Nothing tonight." Sif began stacking the cups, done with revelations for the evening, moving back into the small domestic motions of a woman who had survived by keeping her hands busy. "Go home. Sleep. Look at your plane and ask yourself what she's for. And when the weather turns — and it will turn early this year, my knees say so — you come back up this trail and we'll talk about what a seventy-three-year-old woman's word is worth against a billion-dollar company."

At the door, pulling her collar up against the river cold, Yara paused.

"The drillers' families," she said. "Do they know?"

Sif's hands went still on the cups.

"Eleven years," she said, "I've told myself the truth would find them eventually. That's the lie old women tell themselves so they can sleep." She looked up, and for the first time her eyes weren't unbothered at all. "The truth doesn't find anyone, girl. It waits for someone to carry it. And I am running out of road to carry it on."

Yara walked back down the river trail in the blue dark, the vapor lock forgotten, the ledger's weight ghosting against her ribs though the box had stayed behind. Above the tree line the sky was doing something she didn't like — a hard clarity in the west, a bruised band building in the north, pressure falling the way Sif's knees had promised.

Storm season, opening early.

By the time she reached the hangar, she'd already made the mistake of checking the long-range forecast. By the time she'd made it, she understood that Sif hadn't summoned her to recruit her.

She'd summoned her to start a clock.
