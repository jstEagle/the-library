## 11. Storm Season Opens Early

The barometer at Sif Halvorsen's cabin had been falling for two days, and by the third morning it had fallen so far that Teodor Vance stopped trusting it and started trusting his knees instead. His left knee had been set badly after a fall on a glacier in 2016, and it ached before weather the way other people's teeth ached before cold. It was aching now like a sermon.

"Storm season," Sif said, standing in her doorway with a tin mug of something that smelled like spruce tar and ambition. She was seventy-something, built like driftwood, and she looked at the sky the way accountants look at ledgers — with total, unromantic comprehension. "Opens early this year. You feel it?"

"My knee does."

"Your knee is smarter than you." She handed him the mug. He drank it because refusing Sif Halvorsen was not a thing the north permitted. "You want to move those rocks of yours, you move them today or you don't move them till spring."

Teodor looked out past the treeline to where Yara Pemberton's plane sat on the frozen mud strip she insisted on calling an airstrip, tied down against a wind that hadn't decided yet whether it wanted to be weather or just a bad mood. The sample case was inside the plane, wedged behind the pilot's seat, wrapped in a parka that still smelled faintly of smoke from the night Sif burned her own cabin down.

Three days ago he'd watched a woman torch eleven years of her life into ash and ash-smell, then turn around and hand them a ledger as casually as if she were passing the salt. Payments, dates, initials. A company name that appeared in none of the registries but appeared everywhere in the margins. And at the bottom, in handwriting gone brown with age: *They didn't fall. They were dropped.*

He'd read it four times. Each time it got heavier.

"We could wait," he said, and heard how thin it sounded even as he said it.

Sif snorted. "Wait for what? For Reeve's people to get organized? Boy, they've been organized since 2013. You're the ones who are late." She took the mug back from him and drank from it herself, which he understood was either an insult or a blessing, possibly both. "The passes close tomorrow. Maybe tonight. After that, nothing flies over the Divide until breakup."

"And Reeve's people?"

"Reeve's people have helicopters." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd say *the river has fish*. "Helicopters don't care about passes. What they can't do is be everywhere. Your girl can be somewhere first, if she's willing to fly stupid."

"She's not my girl."

"No," Sif agreed. "She isn't anybody's. That's the problem with her, and someday it'll be the solution." She went inside without saying goodbye, which Teodor was learning was simply how conversations with Sif ended — mid-sentence, door shut, subject closed.

* * *

Yara was under the plane when he found her, flat on a mechanic's creeper with her boots sticking out into the mud, doing something violent and precise to the port-side fuel line. Her voice came out from beneath the fuselage before he'd finished walking over.

"If you're here to talk about flying, I've already decided no."

"I haven't asked yet."

"You were going to. You've got your asking face on." She rolled out, sat up, wiped her hands on a rag that was filthier than her hands. Up close, in the gray light, she looked like someone running on reserves — sharp-eyed, short-tempered, and watching him the way she watched weather. "Ask anyway. I want to hear how you say it."

"The samples need to reach Meridian University," Teodor said. "Dr. Okonkwo's lab. If they sit here through storm season, Reeve's people will find them. Sif's ledger is proof of payments. The samples are proof of *why*. One without the other is just a story."

"Uh-huh."

"The pass over the Divide closes tonight. Possibly within hours. There's a window — maybe ninety minutes, maybe less — where the front hasn't arrived but the pressure gradient is already pulling. If we leave now, we make the crossing ahead of it. If we leave late, we die in it. If we don't leave—"

"—Reeve wins." Yara stood, slung the rag over her shoulder, and looked at the southern horizon where the clouds had organized themselves into something with intent. "You've been practicing that speech."

"Sif helped."

"Sif would help a wolf plan an ambush." But something moved behind her eyes — not warmth exactly, more like a door opening onto a room she kept locked. "Here's what I know about windows like that. I've flown three of them in seven years. Medicine runs. Kids with burst appendixes, men with crushed legs. Every time, the window was real right up until it wasn't. Weather doesn't negotiate, Teodor. It doesn't care that you did the math."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you're a rock man. Rocks stay put. That's the whole appeal of rocks." She walked to the tail of the plane and put her hand on it, and he realized she wasn't touching the aircraft so much as consulting it. "Last time I flew a window like this, I made it. Landed at Kestrel Bay with forty seconds of fuel and a kid who lived. And you know what I remember about it? Not the landing. I remember thinking, halfway across, that if I went down, nobody would come looking for me for a week. Nobody would even know which direction to look."

She said it without self-pity, which made it worse.

"That's why I keep moving," she said. "Not because I like it. Because a person nobody knows where to look for is a person nobody has to grieve."

"Except now people do know where to look," Teodor said quietly. "Sif knows. I know. Half the coast will know by spring, once the ledger's out."

Yara was silent for a long moment. Wind came off the flats and rocked the plane on its gear, and both of them instinctively checked the tie-downs, and that small synchronized motion — pilot and passenger, both reaching — was the closest thing to an answer she gave.

"Fine," she said finally. "We fly. But not for your truth, and not for your dead mentor, and not because Aldous Reeve needs taking down a peg, though God knows he does." She pulled the tie-down pin free and coiled the rope with quick, angry competence. "I'm flying because a seventy-year-old woman burned down her own house and asked me to carry something. When Sif Halvorsen asks you for something, you do it, or you spend the rest of your life being the kind of person who didn't. I've been that kind of person already. Once was enough."

She climbed into the cockpit and started her preflight, and Teodor understood he'd just been dismissed from a conversation that had never technically included him.

* * *

They were loaded and running up the engine when Sif came down the slope from her temporary quarters — the hunting shed behind the ruined cabin, which she'd declared perfectly adequate with the tone of a woman daring the universe to disagree. She carried a canvas satchel and she thrust it through the window at Teodor without ceremony.

"Radio frequencies," she said. "Every operator on the coast. Fish camps, missions, the weather station at Cape Renner. Names, call signs, times they listen. If you get through with the data, these are the people who'll repeat it until it can't be taken back."

Teodor took the satchel. It was heavier than paper should be. "How long have you had this list?"

"Eleven years." Sif's face didn't change. "Same amount of time I've been waiting for somebody crazy enough to use it."

Yara leaned across from the pilot's seat. "Why us?"

"Because you came out of the cave alive," Sif said, "and because you came back. Everybody else who found that place ran. You two ran *toward* things. That's rare enough to build on." She stepped back from the plane and looked up at Yara, and for a moment the old trapper's weathered face did something complicated — grief and hope fighting for the same ground. "Storm's coming in from the southwest. It'll hit the Divide an hour after it hits you, if your knee-man's math holds. Fly the low route along the river until the last bend, then climb hard. Don't linger. Don't admire the view."

"I never admire the view," Yara said.

"Liar," said Sif, and turned away, and that was the goodbye.

* * *

The first hour was almost gentle, which Teodor learned was the most frightening thing weather could do. They flew the river valley low and fast, the water below them running iron-gray and fast-moving, banks of black spruce sliding past like a film run at the wrong speed. Yara flew the way she seemed to do everything — with a competence so complete it looked like indifference, correcting for gusts before Teodor felt them, reading the air ahead off the behavior of the trees.

He'd spread Sif's frequency list on his knees and was copying it into his notebook when the light changed.

It wasn't dramatic. The shadows just went wrong — too blue, too vertical, coming from underneath the cloud deck instead of through it. Yara saw it at the same moment he did. Her hand moved to the throttle without her face changing at all.

"There it is," she said.

"What?"

"The front. It's early." She keyed the radio, listened to static, tried again. Nothing but the long empty hiss of a sky with no one friendly in it. "Sif said an hour. We've got twenty minutes, maybe. Last bend's ahead. When I say climb, I mean *climb* — no hesitation, no questions. The pass is a slot between two walls and the wind through it is going to be a firehose."

Teodor looked at the sample case wedged behind his seat, then at the satchel of frequencies, then at the woman flying them into a closing gap in the world. Eleven years ago, his mentor Elias Wren had stood in a cave and held evidence worth billions, and men had chosen to bury it with him. Teodor had spent eleven years telling himself that if he'd been there — if he'd been older, braver, anything — it would have gone differently.

Now he was here. Now it was going differently. And the difference was a five-foot-two pilot who claimed she was only doing a favor for an old woman.

"Why did you really say yes?" he asked, over the engine noise.

Yara's jaw tightened. "I told you. Sif asked."

"That's not it."

"Teodor—"

"You told me in Kestrel Bay that silence keeps people alive. You meant it. Something changed between then and this morning, and it wasn't Sif's cabin burning, because you'd already decided before that." He leaned forward. "What changed?"

For a moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then, without looking at him, eyes fixed on the river bending ahead toward the mountains:

"You said something at the fire. Night we fought. You said staying for people is how they die, and I threw it back at you that leaving is how they die too, slowly, alone, and you called me a coward." The river bent. The mountains rose up ahead of them, white-capped and indifferent, the pass visible now as a thread of lighter gray between two dark shoulders. "And I lay awake all night being furious, and around four in the morning I figured out why. Not because you were wrong. Because you were right, and I've known it for seven years, and knowing it and stopping it are different animals."

"So this is you stopping it."

"This is me doing a favor for Sif," Yara said, in a voice that closed the subject like a door. "Hold onto something."

The last bend came up fast, and beyond it the valley floor began to rise, and Yara hauled the nose up into the climb with the engine already screaming, and Teodor pressed back into his seat as the ground fell away and the pass opened in front of them like a mouth.

It was worse than a firehose. The wind through the slot hit them broadside with a sound like tearing canvas, and the plane lurched sideways, and for one lurching second Teodor saw the rock wall on their left close enough to count the strata — and then they were through, punched out the far side into calmer air, the Divide falling away behind them, the front a boiling gray wall chasing its own shadow across the tundra below.

Yara exhaled. It was the only sign she gave.

Then the radio crackled — not static this time, but a voice, thin and distorted, breaking apart in the interference.

*"—aircraft on the river route, unidentified, do you copy—"*

Teodor grabbed the mic before he thought better of it. Yara's hand shot out and clamped his wrist, hard, her eyes wide and warning.

Because the voice had said something else, half-lost in static, but clear enough in one word:

*"—Pemberton—"*

Someone on the coast knew her name. Someone was calling for her by name, in a storm, on a frequency only a handful of people in the north ever monitored.

Yara stared at the radio the way you'd stare at a snake in a sleeping bag.

"That," she said quietly, "is not possible."
