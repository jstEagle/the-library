## 12. Tailwind of Lies

The storm was still four hours south of her when Yara found the tracker.

She found it the way she found most things that mattered — by accident, at the worst possible moment, with no room to do anything graceful about it.

They were forty minutes out of Kestrel Bay, climbing through the pass toward the Divide, the Otter heavy with fuel and the sample case lashed into the second seat where a passenger would sit. Teodor had insisted on riding along until she'd talked him out of it in the pre-dawn dark of the hangar. *One of us should be on the ground if something happens to the other,* she'd said, and he'd looked at her like she'd handed him something fragile and said yes too quickly. Now it was just her, the drone of the two radials, and eleven years of buried geology riding shotgun.

Sif's ledger was in the case too — sixty pages of dates, payments, deliveries, written in a hand that shook but never lied. The samples were wrapped in waxed cloth and nested in a steel ammunition box Sif had kept oiled since before Yara was born. The whole package weighed maybe thirty pounds. It felt like carrying a lit stove.

The turbulence started over the first ridge line, the kind of chop that made loose cargo talk. She heard the case shift against the seat frame — a hard knock, metal on metal — and then a sound she didn't have a name for yet. A thin rattle inside the box, rhythmic, wrong. Not sample vials. Vials didn't tick.

She keyed the intercom out of habit, remembered she was alone, and swore at herself in the empty cockpit.

At the next smooth patch between ridges she pulled the yoke back enough to buy ten seconds of lighter load, reached across with her right hand, and unlatched the ammo box. Inside, beneath the wrapped cores, something she hadn't packed. A flat pouch of grey fabric, sewn flush against the case lining, neat as a surgical implant. She'd have needed to turn the box upside down in good light to ever see it, and she'd never had reason to.

She got one finger under its edge. Stitched. Deliberate. Someone had opened the seam of the case lining and closed it again with small, even stitches — machine work, not field work — and whatever sat inside the pouch was dense and slightly warm from vibration.

A transponder. Battery-backed, probably motion-activated, probably pinging on a schedule she couldn't hear over her own engines. Somewhere behind her, or ahead of her, or both, someone was watching a little moving dot crawl across the interior and taking notes.

Reeve.

Of course Reeve. He'd met her plane before the wheels stopped spinning; there'd been a window of ninety seconds when the case had sat unattended on the dock while he shook her hand and slid the envelope of hush money into her jacket pocket. Ninety seconds and a man with steady hands and a sewing kit. She'd taken the money out of habit, told herself it meant nothing, and in doing so she'd handed him a leash.

Yara flew for a while without deciding anything, which was itself a decision, and a bad one.

* * *

The geography of the problem arranged itself in her head the way weather did — all at once, and worse than forecast.

Option one: turn around. Land at Kestrel Bay, tear the tracker out, confront Reeve with it, and lose every hour of the window Sif's ledger had bought them. The storm front was already chewing the southern passes; by afternoon the Divide would be a wall of cloud and rotor you didn't argue with. Teodor's university contact could hold the lab door open exactly as long as the weather allowed, which was to say barely. Turning around meant the evidence went nowhere today, and today was the only day.

Option two: keep flying, tracker and all. Fly straight to the coast, land at the university strip, let whoever held the other end of this signal follow her right up to the lab steps and watch her hand the future of a billion-dollar mineral strike to a man in a cardigan. That wasn't delivery. That was a guided tour for the people who'd buried three geologists under a rockfall and called it weather.

Option three.

Her eyes kept going back to it even though she tried not to look. On the chart, taped to the glare shield, the direct route ran northwest over the Divide — ninety minutes of high country, one pass, done. But there was another way. An old way. The decoy route, she was already calling it, because naming it made it feel like a plan instead of a suicide note. South along the river valleys, low, below the weather if the weather stayed where it was supposed to stay — except the weather was not staying where it was supposed to stay, because the weather this year had opened storm season three weeks early and hadn't asked anyone's permission. Then west across the flats, then north again up the far side, doubling the trip, adding two hours she did not have fuel for unless she stopped, and the only place to stop was a fuel cache at a hunting camp that might or might not still have drums buried under the snow berm.

Two hours through the worst flying weather in a decade, dragging a beacon that would lead anyone who cared about the samples directly into conditions where a single engine cough, a single downdraft, a single white moment of zero visibility would end everything — her, the plane, the ledger, the truth, all of it, neatly, and Reeve would never even need to dirty his hands.

Or she could dump it. Open the window at altitude over deep country and let eleven years of core samples and Sif's whole burned life tumble away into the spruce, and fly home light and clean and alive, and spend the rest of her days knowing exactly what she'd traded for her skin.

Yara Pemberton had spent seven years being very good at option four, the one nobody put on charts: leave early, land soft, love nothing. The fire at Alder Creek had taught her the arithmetic. Stay, and people die. Go, and only you carry the weight, and weight doesn't kill you, it just sits in your chest like ballast until you die slowly instead.

But Sif Halvorsen had stood in her doorway two nights ago with smoke still in her hair, having burned down her own cabin of fifty years so the truth could travel lighter than she could, and said: *It has to outlive me, girl. Not you. It.*

And Teodor was on the ground now, waiting at a radio in Kestrel Bay, trusting her with the real samples because trust was the only currency either of them had left that hadn't been counterfeited.

Yara looked at the tracker pouch in her palm. Small. Grey. Warm.

"Fine," she said to it. "You're coming."

* * *

She worked it out in the air, because there was nowhere else to work anything out anymore.

If she dumped the tracker here, over the Divide, Reeve's people would see the dot stop moving and vanish, and they would know. They'd know she'd found it, know the route she'd been on, and they'd simply wait at the university strip for a package that would never arrive, then widen the circle. A vanished dot bought her hours, not safety.

But a dot that kept moving — a dot that behaved — bought her something better. A lie with a heartbeat.

So she didn't dump it. She gave it a story.

She turned south.

Not a hard turn, nothing that would look like panic on some watcher's screen. A gentle banking drift off the direct route, the kind of deviation a pilot makes when weather builds over a pass and she chooses the long way around, which was true, because the weather *was* building over the pass, a towering anvil of it, blue-black and lit from inside like something thinking. Any reasonable pilot would go around. She was, nominally, a reasonable pilot. She let the tracker see a reasonable pilot making a reasonable decision.

What the tracker wouldn't see — what no one could see from any distance — was how low she took the river valley after that. Two hundred feet over the frozen river, then lower, skimming the white corridor where the terrain itself would smear her radar return into the ground clutter, where any aircraft following the dot would have to come down into the same canyon and fly the same blind thread, and most pilots wouldn't. Most pilots would look at that valley in this weather and make the reasonable call, and the reasonable call would be to wait at the far end and hope.

Let them wait. Let them stand around a screen in some warm office watching her dot crawl through hell, betting she'd crack. She'd been cracking quietly for seven years. This was different. This was cracking *toward* something.

The first gust hit her abeam of the confluence, a shudder through the airframe like a dog shaking off water, and the snow began — not falling so much as arriving sideways, horizontal, the world going from grey to white in the space of a breath. She clipped the chart to her knee board, killed the glare shield tape, and started flying instruments in conditions that weren't officially instrument conditions because officially nobody flew here at all.

The Otter bucked. The case knocked against the seat frame, tracker ticking its idiot heartbeat, and Yara put her left hand flat on the cold steel lid, the way you'd quiet a nervous animal.

"Hold together," she told it. "Both of us."

* * *

The fuel cache was where the maps said it was and half gone, which was where her luck said it would be. She put down on the lake ice in a lull that lasted maybe six minutes, snow squalls marching across the far shore like a slow invasion, and dug out one serviceable drum and one drum full of water and rust. While the pump gurgled she tore the lining seam wider with her teeth and a multitool and finally saw the thing entire: a commercial unit, marine-grade, the kind trappers used on gear sleds, with a battery pack good for weeks. Whoever installed it wanted long-term coverage, not a one-day tail. Reeve hadn't been improvising on the dock. He'd been prepared. Which meant he'd expected her to take the job before she'd agreed to take the job, which meant somewhere in Kestrel Bay her habits were known better than she'd believed, and that thought sat colder than the wind off the ice.

She left the tracker in the case. She zipped the seam back down with clumsy stitches that would fool nobody up close and everybody at a distance, which was the point.

Then she stood on the lake ice in the screaming wind, fuel smell on her gloves, and made herself say the rest of it out loud, because saying it made it real and real things could be planned around:

"They'll follow the dot. The dot goes where I go. So I don't go anywhere I can't survive, and I don't stop anywhere I can't leave." She looked south, where the storm wall had finished eating the horizon. "And I don't tell Teddy, because he'd want to come, and one of us stays alive to testify."

That last part tasted like the old arithmetic. Leave someone behind so the leaving works. She hated how easily it still fit her mouth.

She got back in the plane.

* * *

The crossing of the flats was the part she'd have paid money to skip.

There was no terrain to hide behind out there, no river corridor, no ridgeline — just two hundred miles of tundra and lake ice under a sky that had stopped pretending to be a sky. Visibility dropped to nothing in bands, cleared to a quarter mile, dropped again. Her hands flew the airplane in small constant corrections while her mind ran the map and the fuel numbers and the image of Teodor standing in the hangar doorway saying *come back*, and underneath all of it, steady as the tracker's tick, the knowledge that somewhere behind her a dot was telling a story, and she was the author, and the genre was lies.

Twice she considered turning north anyway, gambling the pass, gambling the storm gap. Twice she caught herself reaching for the old exit — the fast way, the clean way, the way that ended with her alone and intact and the world unchanged. Seven years of instinct pulling the yoke toward escape like a phantom limb.

She banked south instead, deeper into the weather, and felt something in her chest shift that wasn't fear. Closer to grief. The price tag coming due on a person who'd spent seven years paying in a cheaper currency.

Over the last chain of lakes before the far hills, the Otter took a downdraft that shoved her down five hundred feet in four seconds, and for one long white moment the altimeter and the ground were having a conversation she wasn't invited to. She fed it full power and hauled back and the stall horn sang one clear note — the loneliest sound in aviation — and then the downdraft let go and the wheels missed the ice by a margin she chose not to measure.

After that her hands were shaking, so she made them do work. Fuel check. Case check. Tracker check — still ticking, still faithful, still lying beautifully on somebody's screen: *pilot deviates around weather, continues west, all normal.* The best cover story in the world was a true story with one fact removed, and the removed fact was that she was carrying the only proof in the hemisphere that a company had murdered three men for a rock.

The far hills rose out of the murk at dusk, black and ragged, and beyond them, if the charts and Sif's memory and God were all agreeing today, the river that led down to the coast and the university strip and the lab and the end of the beginning.

The radio crackled. Static, then a voice fighting through it — Teodor, relayed through a coastal operator, his words chopped to bones by distance and interference:

*"—Yara— storm closing Divide— Reeve's plane— heard— refueled— heading—"*

Static ate the rest.

She stared at the radio. Played it back in her head. *Reeve's plane. Refueled. Heading—* Heading where? Heading along the route the dot was drawing for them, is where. Heading into the same white hell she'd chosen precisely because she'd assumed they'd wait it out on the ground like sane men.

They weren't waiting. They were following her in.

Yara looked at the storm wall ahead, then at the fuel gauge, then at the case with its stitched-shut secret, and understood with perfect clarity that the decoy had worked too well. She hadn't led them into a trap. She'd led them onto her own flight path, and now whatever happened in the next two hours would happen to all of them together, in weather that forgave no one, and the only thing she'd decided for certain was that she was not going to let Reeve's dot and hers arrive anywhere at the same time.

She pushed the nose down toward the river valley as the last daylight went out, and the storm came up to meet her like a door opening.
