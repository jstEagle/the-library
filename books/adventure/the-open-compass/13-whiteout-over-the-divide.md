## 13. Whiteout Over the Divide

The tracker came out of the case seam at eleven thousand feet, and Yara's whole body went cold before the air did.

She'd found it with her thumbnail, worrying a lump in the leather binding that shouldn't have been there — a hard grain the size of a lentil, sewn into the stitching like a seed. She'd picked it loose over the Mackenzie lowlands, held it up to the dome light, and understood in one flat, silent instant that Aldous Reeve had been listening to them fly for two days.

She hadn't said anything. She'd smiled at Teodor across the cabin, told him the weather looked fine, and then she'd done the thing seven years of discipline told her to do: she'd changed everything.

The real samples were in the survival kit under her seat, wrapped in a wool sock, riding in a tin that had once held pemmican. The case bolted to the cargo floor — the one with the tracker — held river stones wrapped in Teodor's field notebooks and a note she'd written in block letters on a fuel manifest: *YOU SHOULD HAVE ASKED NICELY.*

The decoy route was the one no sane pilot flew in November. South over the divide, where the ridgelines ran up to meet the jet stream and the weather didn't forecast so much as *happen*. Reeve's people would follow the signal into the teeth of it, and while they were out there burning fuel and nerve chasing stones, she and Teodor would be — somewhere. She hadn't figured out *somewhere* yet. That was the flaw in the plan, and she'd flown anyway.

"You're quiet," Teodor said. He had to raise his voice over the engine. The Otter was old and honest, and it made no pretense of being anything but loud.

"I'm flying."

"You're quiet *for* flying."

She checked the horizon. The horizon was gone. That was the first bad sign — the divide had swallowed it whole, the southern sky a bruised gray wall with a white smear at its base where the snow squalls had already gone to work. The second bad sign was the way the air felt under her hands, thick and wrong, like wading. Mountain wave. The ridgeline was throwing the wind up and over in long, invisible breakers, and they were about to surf them.

"Teodor. Get the survival kit out from under your feet. Sleeping bags, stove, the works. Move it into your lap."

"Why?"

"Because in about four minutes you're going to want it in your lap."

The first downdraft hit like a hand pressing them toward the earth. The altimeter unwound — eight thousand, seventy-five hundred, seven — and Yara fed in full power and the engine roared and the needle slowed, wavered, and finally, grudgingly, began to climb. Then the updraft caught them and shoved them skyward so fast her stomach arrived late, and the airspeed bled off and the stall horn chirped its small, polite warning, and she dropped the nose and let the mountain have them for a moment, falling, falling, until the wing bit air again.

"Is this normal?" Teodor shouted. He was white. He was holding the survival kit like a life vest.

"Nothing about today is normal. Strap in. Tight."

The whiteout came for them the way weather comes in the north — not arriving but *revealing itself*, suddenly everywhere, as though it had been in the cabin all along. One moment there was a world; the next there was white, white in every direction, white above and below, no horizon, no ground, no sky, just the engine's roar and the instruments and Yara's hands moving on them like a pianist's. She'd flown instruments in bad weather before. This was different. This was the divide doing what the divide did, erasing the difference between up and down, and somewhere below them was rock and ice and a frozen lake she'd marked on the flight plan as their one mercy: Caribou Lake, long and narrow, running with the wind, a runway made by God and abandoned by everyone.

She flew the heading. She flew the altitude. She flew the clock, counting seconds, and the whiteout pressed against the windshield like a living thing trying to get in, and Teodor had stopped talking, which frightened her more than the turbulence, because a silent Teodor was a man who had finally understood where he was.

"Caribou Lake should be dead ahead in about ninety seconds," she said. "When I say *look*, you look for anything darker than white. Water shows darker. Ice with snow on it shows lighter. You're my eyes."

"I'm your eyes," he repeated, like a man taking an oath.

"Look."

He leaned against the window, face pressed to the glass, and the seconds peeled away — ten, twenty, thirty — and there was nothing, nothing, nothing, and Yara began running the arithmetic she hated: fuel remaining, distance remaining, the height of the ridgeline to the east that she could not see and could not climb above because climbing meant more whiteout and less margin.

"Darker patch," Teodor shouted. "Ten o'clock. Low. Maybe."

She saw it. Or she saw something — a smudge, a suggestion, the ghost of a shoreline. She banked toward it, gently, because in a whiteout your inner ear lies to you and a gentle bank is the only kind you can trust, and the smudge resolved into a shoreline, and the shoreline resolved into a lake, and the lake was there, long and white and — she understood with a lurch — *blowing*. Snow was streaming off its surface in long gray banners, the wind funnelling down its length like water down a chute.

Landing downwind was how pilots died. Landing crosswind on ice was how pilots died. There was no upwind option; the lake ran the wrong way, and the wind was forty knots and building.

"Yara." Teodor's voice had gone very quiet. "Is this — are we —"

"Hold on," she said. "Hold on, hold on, hold on."

She came in low over the shoreline trees, half-seen, half-imagined, flying on the ghost of a lake she'd marked from a chart, and she flared the Otter over the ice and held it off and held it off, feeling for the surface with the wheels the way you'd feel for a stair in the dark, and then the skis — she'd landed on skis, thank God for skis — touched, and the world became a shriek. The plane slewed sideways. Something cracked. She fought the rudder, fed opposite power, and the Otter ground to a stop a hundred yards from the south shore, canted, alive, its tail still trembling like a struck animal.

The silence afterward was enormous.

Snow hissed against the windows. The wind moaned in the struts. Teodor unbuckled himself with hands that shook and didn't try to hide it, and Yara sat with her own hands on the yoke for a long moment, feeling the airplane's heartbeat through the airframe, before she said, out loud, to no one:

"Okay. Okay. We live."

* * *

They built camp in the cabin, because the cabin was the only structure on the lake and the wind outside could strip skin from bone. Yara lashed the tail to a shore anchor she'd planted through a drilled ice hole — the plane would not fly again until the wind died, and possibly not until spring — and then they dragged the sleeping bags, the stove, the food box, and every blanket the Otter owned into a pile behind the cargo bulkhead, out of the wind's reach, and made a nest that would have embarrassed them in daylight.

The stove hissed. The lantern made a small gold room of the dark. Outside, the storm worked the lake like a man beating a rug, and inside, two people who had been pretending not to be afraid sat in the pretense's wreckage.

Teodor poured melted snow into two cups and added the last of the whiskey from a flask he'd carried, apparently, for eleven years, waiting for an occasion. "To Caribou Lake," he said. "Population: us."

"To bad decisions."

"To your flying," he said, and meant it, and she looked away because she didn't know where to put that.

They sat. The storm howled. And then Teodor said, in the careful voice of a man approaching a tripwire, "You changed the route because of the tracker. You didn't tell me."

"I'm telling you now."

"You told me *midair*. You made the decision alone."

"That's how I make decisions."

"I've noticed." He turned his cup. "Yara. The fire."

She went still.

"Sif told me. Not all of it. Just that there was a settlement, and a fire, and that you flew people out, and that some didn't — " He stopped. "And that you never stayed anywhere again. I've flown with you for weeks. I've nearly died with you twice. I think I've earned the rest of it. But I'm asking, not demanding. You can tell me it's none of my business, and I'll drop it forever."

The lantern hissed. The wind screamed. Yara looked at the ceiling of the airplane, at the frost flowers growing there, and found — to her genuine surprise — that she wanted to talk. That the story had been sitting in her chest for seven years like a swallowed stone, and that this cold dark room with this stubborn, grieving man was somehow the place it had chosen to come out.

"Little Otter Creek," she said. "That's what it was called. Forty-one people. A trading post, a school, a diesel generator, a dock. I was twenty-six. I'd been flying the north for three years, and I was the only plane they had, and I loved them." She said it flatly, the way you'd read a casualty list. "That was my mistake. Not that I cared. That I *let them know* I cared. I stopped being the pilot and started being the neighbor. I had a key to the school. I sat with the elder women and learned to smoke fish. I had a — there was a boy, Danny, eight years old, who used to meet my plane every time, every single time, rain or forty below, standing at the end of the dock. Waiting for me like I was the mail and Christmas at once."

Teodor didn't move.

"In February the generator building caught. Electrical, they think — old wiring, cold-soaked. It went up in nine minutes. Nine minutes, and I was on the ice two miles away, and I got there in four, which was a miracle of flying, and it didn't matter. The fire had the school. Half the town lived at that end. I got eleven people out on the first lift. Eleven. Overloaded past every limit, on a lake that was barely frozen, in smoke you could taste. And I went back." Her voice didn't waver; she'd rehearsed this in the dark too many times for it to waver. "I went back twice more. And on the last trip I took on fourteen people and the plane was heavy and the wind had come up off the lake and I clipped the tree line on the climb-out and I put it down in the trees a mile out. Everyone lived. Everyone in my plane lived. But that was the last lift I could make, because the plane was done, and the fire kept going, and nine people died, and Danny was one of them. He wasn't even in the school. He was at the dock. Waiting for me. He'd seen me come back and he ran for the dock instead of away from the fire, because Danny's whole world was that I came back, and I didn't. Not in time. Not ever again."

The stove hissed. Teodor's eyes were wet and he let them be.

"So here's the arithmetic I've lived by since," Yara said. "Every person who knows my name is a person who might wait at a dock. Every place I stop is a place that can burn. If I don't belong anywhere, nowhere can take anything from me. Seven years. Two hundred settlements. Land, unload, leave. And it worked. It *worked*, Teodor. Nobody waited for me. Nobody ran toward my plane. Nobody died because they trusted me." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "And then you left coordinates on my windshield, and I couldn't stop reading them, and now I'm sitting on a frozen lake in a whiteout with a man who knows my name, carrying evidence that people have killed for, and every single rule I ever made is broken, and the terrible thing — the genuinely terrible thing — is that I don't want them back."

She said it and then sat there, emptied, listening to the storm as though it might rule on her confession.

Teodor set down his cup. He reached over and took her hand — not dramatically, not with a speech, just took it, the way you'd take the hand of someone standing at the edge of ice, and held it.

"Danny ran toward your plane," he said, "because you were the best thing that ever landed there. That's not the tragedy, Yara. That's the testimony. The tragedy is the fire. The tragedy is a town with one generator and no second chance. You've been serving a sentence the fire handed down, and the fire wasn't yours."

"I clipped the trees."

"You overloaded to save fourteen people and everyone in your airplane lived. I'm a scientist. I read data. And the data says you're the finest pilot I've ever seen, and the cruelest judge I've ever met, and they're both you, and only one of them is telling the truth."

Yara looked at their hands on the sleeping bag between them, at the lantern light lying gold on the frost flowers, and something in her chest — the thing that had been clenched for seven years, the fist around the stone — loosened. Not all the way. But enough.

"The samples," she said finally, because she was still herself. "Under your sleeping bag. The real ones. When this wind drops, we're not flying south — that's where Reeve's people are chasing my rocks. We go north, low, under the weather, to Fort Providence. There's a university charter that comes through Thursdays. We make the handoff, and it's out of our hands forever."

"And Reeve?"

"Reeve is going to spend tomorrow digging river stones out of a snowbank and learning what it feels like." She almost smiled. Then the smile faded, because the radio — she'd been keeping one ear on it all evening, scanning the frequencies out of habit — crackled with a burst of static, and a voice came through, thin and warped by distance and atmosphere, but clear enough.

"— all aircraft, all aircraft, be advised, Kestrel Bay strip reported closed, unconfirmed fire on the field, repeat, unconfirmed fire at Kestrel Bay —"

Yara's blood went to ice water.

Kestrel Bay was where the *Beaver* was hangered. Kestrel Bay was where Sif Halvorsen lived, eighty-one years old and alone. Kestrel Bay was the only place on earth that had ever felt, in the last few weeks, like a place she might stop.

She looked at Teodor. He was already looking at her, and she saw her own thought land in his face.

"Wind drops at dawn," she said quietly. "We fly north. And then we keep flying."
