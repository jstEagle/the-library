## 14. What Reeve Knows

The satellite phone took four minutes to connect, which gave Aldous Reeve four minutes to decide how much of the truth he was willing to say out loud.

He sat in the company's Kestrel Bay office — a rented room above the hardware store that smelled of diesel and old coffee, furnished with a desk, a filing cabinet with nothing in it, and a window that looked out on the harbor. From the window he could see the floatplane basin. He could see Yara Pemberton's aircraft tied off at the far berth, its paint sun-bleached, its tires worn to the cords. A working plane. A working woman. The kind of asset that looked harmless right up until it wasn't.

The line clicked open.

"Report." The voice at the other end was the same as it had been for eleven years: dry, unhurried, patient in the way that deep water is patient. Reeve had never seen the man's face. He'd stopped wondering about it around year three.

"Reeve here. Status is changed."

"Go on."

"The pilot and the geologist went into the cave."

A pause. Not a long one. Long enough for Reeve to hear the man set something down — a pen, maybe, or a glass.

"When?"

"Three days ago. Lowest tide in forty years. They were inside roughly six hours before we got them out." Reeve turned his chair toward the window, away from the filing cabinet, as if the empty drawers could hear him. "They found the camp."

Another silence. This one longer.

"All of it?"

"Three sets of remains. The gear. And the core samples." Reeve kept his voice level, the way you keep your voice level when you're walking across river ice. "Vance pocketed at least two cylinders before the case went into the water. I've confirmed that from the debrief transcripts. He thinks he hid it well. He didn't."

"And the pilot?"

"Pemberton's a complication." He said it flatly, because it was true, and because saying it flatly made it sound like an operational assessment rather than what it actually was — which was, if he was honest with himself, a kind of respect. "She's been flying this coast seven years without leaving a footprint. No fixed address. Cash transactions. She doesn't drink with anyone, doesn't owe anyone, doesn't talk about her past. Until now she was the safest pair of hands in the territory precisely because she belonged to no one."

"But now."

"Now she belongs to a story," Reeve said. "And stories want telling."

* * *

He'd read the file eleven years ago, when they first brought him north. Most of it was redacted. What remained was enough: the 2013 survey expedition, five names on the permit, three of which matched the bodies in the cave. The fourth name belonged to a woman who had officially died in a boating accident two hundred miles south, eight months after the rockfall. Sif Halvorsen. Alive, as far as Reeve could tell, in a trapper's cabin up the Serpentine valley, old now, careful, paid on a schedule so regular you could set a clock by it.

The fifth name had no file at all. That absence had always told Reeve more than the redactions did.

"What exactly did they find?" the voice asked. "In 2013. Tell me again, in your own words. I want to know what you believe, not what the summary says."

Reeve appreciated that. It was the closest thing to trust the man ever offered — the request for a firsthand account, unfiltered by the people whose job was filtering. He also understood it for what it was: a test. If Reeve's version drifted too far from the record, or too close to it, either way there would be consequences. So he told it straight.

"They were doing stratigraphic work on the coastal formation. Routine. The mentor — Vance's mentor, a man named Okonkwo — had a theory about hydrothermal alteration along the fault line. Everyone thought it was academic. Then they pulled cores showing a sulfide body under the cliff that runs, conservatively, four kilometers. High-grade. Copper, nickel, platinum group metals, and a rare-earth signature that doesn't occur naturally anywhere else on the continent."

"You've seen the assays."

"I've seen the assays."

"Worth?"

"Billions. Plural. Over the life of the mine, with the rare-earth premium, you're looking at the largest strategic mineral discovery in the hemisphere." Reeve paused. "Which is why it can't exist."

"It exists."

"It exists in a cave that floods twice a day behind forty meters of collapsed rock, on land held under a heritage claim by three families in Kestrel Bay who don't know what's under their feet, documented only in the field notes of three dead men and whatever Vance has in his pocket." Reeve counted it off like inventory. "It exists as long as we say it does. The order in 2013 was to bury it with its finders. The rockfall accomplished half of that. Halvorsen's silence and the legend accomplished the rest. Eleven years of maintenance — small payments, fresh supplies at the cave mouth, a story about admission-charging spirits that keeps the curious at a polite distance. It worked. It worked until a tide chart anomaly put a bored geologist and a pilot with a conscience gap on that shelf at the same time."

"You blame the tide."

"I blame the schedule," Reeve said. "We knew the tide window was coming. We had eleven years of tide tables. Someone decided a forty-year low was a problem for next quarter."

The line went quiet in a way that was not comfortable. Reeve let it be quiet. There was a hierarchy here, and part of surviving inside it was knowing which silences to fill and which to stand inside like weather.

Finally: "What do they have? Concretely."

"Two core samples, possibly three. Field notes, probably water-damaged but legible — Vance photographs everything, it's a habit, I've watched him do it. Photographs of the camp. And the ledger." He hesitated on that last word, then committed. "Halvorsen kept records. Payments received, deliveries made, dates going back to 2013. If they have the ledger, they don't just have evidence of the strike. They have evidence of us."

"Do they have the ledger?"

"Not yet. Halvorsen still has it. But Pemberton visited her cabin yesterday. Two hours on the ground. Whatever passed between them, it wasn't small talk."

* * *

After the call, Reeve walked down to the harbor and bought coffee from the kiosk, because buying coffee was what Aldous Reeve did, publicly, every morning at ten-forty, rain or blow. Predictability was camouflage. In a town of four hundred people, the man who varied his routine was the man who got talked about.

Yara Pemberton was at the fuel dock.

He saw her before she saw him — or she saw him and chose not to show it, which with her amounted to the same thing. She was hand-pumping avgas into the wing tanks, sleeves rolled, hair tied back, moving with the economy of someone who had done ten thousand small mechanical tasks alone and intended to do ten thousand more. She didn't look up until he was close enough to speak at conversational volume.

"Ms. Pemberton."

"Mr. Reeve." She kept pumping. "Come to check on your investment?"

"Our conversation the other day was about safety," he said. "Unstable cliffs. Liability. I recall you found it persuasive."

"I found the money persuasive." She screwed the cap down on the tank. "The cliffs I'll make my own judgment about."

"Of course." He smiled the smile he used with suppliers and council members, the one calibrated to say: I am reasonable, I am local, I am no threat to your way of life. "Weather's turning. Storm season early this year, they're saying. You'll want to get your flying in while the passes are open."

"That's the plan."

"Where will you fly?"

She looked at him then, fully, and Reeve had been in negotiations on four continents, and he recognized what he saw: not hostility, not fear — arithmetic. She was calculating what he knew, what she knew, and what the difference between those two things was worth.

"North," she said. "Medicine run. Same as always."

"Give my regards to the Serpentine valley," Reeve said pleasantly. "Beautiful country this time of year."

Her jaw tightened, a fraction, controlled. She shouldered past him toward the office to settle her fuel account, and he stood at the rail with his coffee and watched her go, and felt the thing he always felt after talking to her, which was the unpleasant sensation of having been measured and found — not wanting, exactly. Found readable.

* * *

The second call came at midnight, from the same number, which never happened unless the matter had escalated above the man's own authority. Reeve sat by the window with the lights off, the harbor black below, one anchor light bobbing.

"You recommended containment," the voice said. "Standard containment. Payment, narrative, monitoring. Explain why that's no longer sufficient."

"Because standard containment assumes the secret stays static." Reeve spoke slowly, choosing each word like footing on scree. "Pemberton isn't going to sit on this. I've spent a week watching her try to be the person who sits on things, and she can't. There's a fracture in her — something from before she came north, some reason a pilot with her hours lives like a ghost — and this cave has found the crack. She took our money and hated herself for it by sundown. Yesterday she flew to Halvorsen. Today she filed a flight plan for the Divide corridor in a storm window nobody sane flies. She's building toward something. Disclosure, most likely. University lab, press, inquiry — I don't know the shape yet, but the shape doesn't matter. What matters is that she's moving and we're reacting, and I don't win reacting."

"Options."

"Option one: recover the samples. I have a man positioned at the lab end. If they fly the Divide, weather may do our work for us; if they make it through, we take possession at the delivery point and the evidence chain breaks. Clean, deniable, survivable."

"And option two."

Reeve looked at the anchor light for a while before answering.

"Option two is Pemberton herself. Permanently."

Say it plainly, he told himself. Don't dress it. Dressing it was how men in his position started believing their own euphemisms, and men who believed their own euphemisms made mistakes.

"She is the load-bearing element," he said. "Vance is grief and scholarship — dangerous, but legible. He wants the truth known; you can predict him. Pemberton wants something she can't name yet, and until she names it, she's unpredictable. She has the flying skill to go anywhere, the anonymity to vanish, and — as of this week — a grievance against us. Every scenario where this ends quietly requires her cooperation. Every scenario where she refuses ends with her testifying. Remove her, and Vance becomes a lone academic shouting into a storm season. Grief keeps. Grievance escalates."

"You understand what you're asking me to authorize."

"I understand that in 2013 the instruction was to bury it with its finders, and the finders are buried, and the only reason we're having this conversation is that one grave was dug shallow." Reeve heard his own voice go quiet. "I'm asking permission to finish the job properly this time. Weather over the Divide kills pilots every year. A fuel miscalculation. An engine that quits over the tundra. In this territory, accidents are a growth industry. I can arrange one that no inquiry will ever question, because no one will look for a question."

The silence stretched long enough that Reeve checked the connection light. Still live.

"There's a condition," the voice said at last.

"Name it."

"Halvorsen. The ledger. If the old woman's records reach the light, none of the rest matters — not the samples, not the pilot, not the narrative. Paper outlives everyone." A pause. "Handle the paper first. Then the pilot. In that order, Mr. Reeve, and not reversed."

"Understood."

"And Reeve." The voice dropped half a register, into the range it used perhaps once a year. "You asked for permanent. Think carefully about what permanent means for the man who performs it. Men who handle problems permanently become problems themselves, eventually, somewhere down the schedule. Do the work cleanly, close the file completely, and come home. Don't build anything north of the Divide that has your fingerprints on it. Nothing you'd miss. Nothing that could testify."

"I've kept clean for eleven years."

"Eleven years ago you were maintaining a legend. Now you're making graves. Those are different trades, and they attract different attention." The line hummed. "Permission granted. Both items. Report when the ledger is destroyed."

The call ended. Reeve sat in the dark with the phone warm in his hand and the harbor black below him, and he noticed that his heart was beating faster than the conversation warranted, and he catalogued that honestly, the way he catalogued everything: not fear, exactly. Proximity. For eleven years he had managed a secret. Tomorrow he would begin killing for it, and the distance between those two professions was shorter than any man likes to admit.

He looked out at the floatplane basin, at the far berth where Pemberton's aircraft sat tied down, pale in the starlight, fueled and ready.

Storm season opens tomorrow, he thought. Fly safe, Ms. Pemberton.

But even as he wished it, some cold practical corner of his mind was already checking the forecast for the Divide — wind speed, ceiling, snow load — the way a man checks a blade.
