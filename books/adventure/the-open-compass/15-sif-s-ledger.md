## 15. Sif's Ledger

Sif Halvorsen's cabin sat eleven miles upriver from Kestrel Bay, at the end of a trapline trail that hadn't seen maintenance since before I started flying. Teodor and I walked it in the gray half-light of an October morning that couldn't decide whether to be autumn or winter, our breath hanging behind us like question marks. I'd left the Cessna on the bay gravel bar under a tarp, and every step of those eleven miles I'd asked myself why we were walking instead of flying.

The answer was simple: because if Reeve's people were watching the sky, I didn't want to lead them here.

Sif had summoned me by radio two nights earlier — three clicks, pause, three clicks, the old trapper's code she'd taught me my first winter in the interior, back when I still thought of this country as temporary. *Come alone*, she'd said, and then, after a silence long enough to make me check the frequency twice: *Bring the geologist. It's time.*

"It" being whatever Sif had been sitting on for eleven years. The empty sleeping bag. The fresh supplies at the cave mouth. The legend she'd maintained like a fence around a grave.

The cabin appeared out of the willows all at once — low-slung, sod-roofed, smoke rising from a stovepipe bent like a finger pointing at nothing. A dog lifted its head from the porch boards, considered us, and put it back down. That was Sif's welcome: acknowledgment without ceremony.

She was waiting in the doorway, small and square as a stump, seventy-some years of weather written into her face in lines deep enough to lose a coin in. Her eyes went past me to Teodor, held there a moment, and something moved through her expression — grief recognizing its own shape in a stranger.

"You have his chin," she said. "Elias Vance. Stubborn jaw. He argued with me about tide tables the day I last saw him alive."

Teodor stopped walking. "You knew him."

"I fed him." She stepped aside from the door. "Come in before the wind changes its mind. Both of you. And wipe your feet — I just swept eleven years ago."

* * *

Inside, the cabin was one room and a loft, dense with the smell of spruce smoke and rendered fat and old paper. Shelves lined every wall, and most of them held not cans or traps but binders — dozens of them, spines labeled in a hand so precise it looked printed. Ledgers. I counted without meaning to. Fourteen visible. There were more in the loft; I could see the edges of boxes through the ladder opening.

Sif poured tea from a dented pot into three tin cups, handed them out, and sat down across from us at a table scarred with knife marks. On the table, centered between us like a card player's ace, lay a single ledger bound in oilcloth.

"Elias Vance came to me in the spring of 2013," she said, without preamble. "Him and two others — Okonkwo and Lindqvist. Geologists, he said, working a private survey. They asked about the coast south of the point. About the cave." She sipped her tea. "I told them what I tell everyone. Bad ground. Tide takes the unwary. Admission is paid in lives."

"But you told them more," Teodor said quietly.

"I told them where the entrance would open at extreme low water. Because Elias had done something no survey crew does — he'd brought me a gift of coffee I actually drink, and he'd listened when I talked about my husband, dead six years by then, and he'd asked real questions instead of pretending to listen while checking his watch." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Old woman alone on a river. You'd be surprised how rare listening is. How expensive."

I thought of myself landing, unloading, leaving before anyone learned my name, and felt the habit of seven years shift slightly, like ice groaning under new weight.

"They went in on the June low," Sif continued. "Came out with rock in their packs that made Elias go quiet in a way quiet men don't go quiet. He came back a week later, alone, and showed me a core sample under lamplight. Asked me what I knew about mineral claims." She turned the ledger over but didn't open it. "I knew enough. My husband staked claims in '79. I know what a strike looks like when a man holds it. Elias was holding the biggest thing this coast has ever produced, and he was frightened of it. Not of the rock. Of who'd sent him."

"The company," Teodor said. His cup was untouched, both hands flat on the table. "Meridian. Or whoever Meridian was fronting for."

"Meridian Resources," Sif confirmed. "Elias worked their northern desk eleven years. Loyal man. Until loyalty met arithmetic." She finally untied the oilcloth. "He figured it out before they did — that once the assay results reached Anchorage, the expedition had become a liability. Four people knew the location. Three were expendable. He was supposed to be the fourth, but he was too useful to bury until the claim was secured. So they let him keep flying samples south while they arranged the rest."

"And the rockfall," I said. My voice came out flatter than I intended.

"Wasn't geology." Sif opened the ledger. "Page one. Read it yourself."

* * *

The handwriting was hers — that same printed precision — and the entries ran in columns: date, party, amount, delivery, witness. But the first pages weren't payments. They were notes, dated June 2013, written in a hand pressed hard enough to emboss the next three sheets.

*June 19. Two men came upriver by jetboat, no registration numbers. Said they were doing cliff surveys for Meridian. Carried explosives permits signed by someone in Whitehorse. I copied the permit numbers. Left before dark.*

*June 24. Blast heard from the point around 0400. Ground shake woke the dog. No seismic activity reported anywhere after. Checked with the band office in Kestrel Bay — nobody surveyed anything.*

*June 26. Radio traffic on the company channel, scrambled but short. One phrase caught clear: "cave sealed, manifest complete." Manifest complete means the men inside were counted as cargo.*

I set the cup down because my hands wanted to shake and I refused to give them the satisfaction. Teodor had gone the color of tundra slate.

"They blew the entrance," he said. "With Eli inside. With Okonkwo and Lindqvist inside."

"With everyone inside except the one who walked out," Sif said, and looked at me. "Which brings us to the part your pilot already guessed, because pilots guess everything and admit nothing."

I didn't rise to it. "You were the empty sleeping bag."

"I was the empty sleeping bag." She said it the way other people recite their birthday. "Elias sent me a radio warning the night before the blast — coded, off-channel, the kind of thing you learn when you've spent forty years listening to other people's traffic. Told me to meet him at the cave at first light, bring the boat, tell no one. I got there as the sun cleared the ridge and heard the charge go." Her voice stayed level, but her thumb pressed white against the table edge. "The inner passage collapsed. The outer arch held. I dug for two days through the rubble at the vent with my hands. Pulled Elias out on the third morning. He lived nine hours."

Teodor made a sound I'll remember the rest of my life — small, involuntary, the sound of a man being unmade quietly in public.

"He gave me the field case," Sif went on, gentler now, the way you'd handle a load-bearing wall. "Told me the samples proved the strike, the strike proved motive, and motive was the only weapon anyone would ever have against Meridian. Then he told me the rest: stay dead to them. Let them believe the cave took everyone. Be the legend." She closed the ledger. "So I was. Every year after, I kept the story alive — charged admission, cursed ground, tide takes the greedy. Kept people away better than any fence. And once a year, on the June low, I went in through the vent Elias and I cut, and I checked the camp, and I left supplies."

"Why?" I asked. "They were dead. Supplies for the dead?"

"For whoever came next." Her eyes were steady on mine. "Because I knew someday someone else would find that cave. Meridian's own people, maybe, coming to verify the seal. Or fools like you two. And if it was fools, I wanted them to live long enough to carry what Elias carried out. The supplies weren't for the dead, girl. They were bait for the truth."

* * *

We sat with that a long time. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind had found its mind and chosen winter; sleet ticked against the single window like static.

It was Teodor who broke first, voice rough: "The ledger. All of it — the permit numbers, the radio intercepts, the payments—"

"Payments start page thirty-one." Sif slid the ledger across the table to him. "Eleven years. Meridian's fixer — a man named Aldous Reeve, though he used four names before that one — has paid me quarterly since 2014. Hush money for the legend. Delivery confirmation for the supplies I pretended were superstition. Signatures, dates, account routing codes, a photograph of each cheque before I cashed it. I am not a clever woman, but I am a thorough one, and thorough beats clever over eleven years every time."

"Why keep taking it?" I asked. "If you had the proof—"

"Because the money proved they believed the secret was safe." She almost smiled. "And because every cheque they wrote was another confession with a stamp on it. You don't stop a man mid-confession."

Teodor was turning pages now, faster, his lips moving over the entries. "This is — Sif, this is everything. This plus the samples ends them. The inquiry, the land claim, criminal referrals—"

"Yes." She stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and went to the shelf. She began pulling down binders. All of them. Stacking them on the table with the unhurried efficiency of a woman who had rehearsed this moment for eleven years and was not going to rush it now. "Fourteen ledgers. Copies of everything Elias gave me, copies of everything I collected, triplicate correspondence, photographs, the works. Eleven years of a dead man's war, fought by an old woman with a pencil."

"What are you doing?" I asked, though I already knew, the way you know weather before it arrives.

She looked at the stack. Then at the stove.

"No," I said.

"Girl." She said it kindly. "Sit down."

* * *

What happened next happened slowly, which is the only way terrible things should happen, and the only way Sif Halvorsen did anything.

She didn't burn the ledgers. I want that understood, because for one lurching moment I thought she'd lost her mind. What she burned was everything else — the cabin's paper life. Correspondence. Maps marked with the trapline. Her husband's claim stakes records. Photographs. Letters from a sister in Tromsø, forty years of them, read and reread until they fell apart. She fed them into the stove page by page while Teodor and I packed the fourteen ledgers into a waterproof dry bag with the reverence of men carrying a bomb.

"The ledgers are copies," she explained, feeding in another handful. "Duplicates of duplicates. The originals went to a safety deposit box in Whitehorse in 2016, under a name that isn't mine, keyed to a death notice that hasn't run yet. When I die — whenever that is — the bank mails them to a lawyer who owes me. So burning these costs the truth nothing." She glanced back at us. "But it costs *me* everything they could trace. Every scrap of paper in this cabin connects to Sif Halvorsen. After today, Sif Halvorsen becomes a woman who died in a fire of unknown cause, and whoever comes asking finds ash and a dog that wandered off."

"You're leaving," I said. "For good."

"I'm eleven years overdue." She wiped down the table where the ledger had lain, thorough even in erasure. "Reeve thinks I'm his tame legend, paid quarterly, contained. The moment you two surfaced from that cave with Elias's initials on a field case, my usefulness ended. Either I was a loose end or a witness, and Reeve doesn't employ either kind. Burning the cabin buys me a week of confusion. A week is enough to walk out along the coast to my sister's people, where no plane lands and no cheque follows." She paused at the stove, firelight moving on her face. "Besides. A fire makes a better headline than an old woman's testimony. Fires get investigated. Old women get ignored."

"That's not—" Teodor started, and stopped, because there was nothing to finish the sentence with. What? *That's not fair*? Nothing about this country had ever been fair, and Sif Halvorsen had never once been invited to expect it.

I stood up. "Then come with us. Fly out. Tonight, before—"

"Fly out." She said the words like she was tasting them. "In your plane. Registered in your name, filed on your flight plan, witnessed by every radio operator on the coast. Delivering the last living keeper of the cave legend straight to Aldous Reeve's front door." She shook her head once, decisive as a trap falling shut. "No. I walk. I've been walking out of things for seventy years. I'm good at it."

"Sif—"

"Yara Pemberton." She crossed the room and took my hands in hers, and her grip was stronger than it had any right to be, callused down to leather. Up close her eyes were pale gray, nearly colorless, and absolutely certain. "Seven years you've flown over this country keeping everyone at arm's length because staying got people killed. I know. Elias told me about you too — the pilot who wouldn't land. He admired you. Said you were the only honest coward on the coast." Something flickered in her face that might have been affection. "Well. Here's what an honest coward needs to hear: I'm eighty-one years old. I buried a husband. I buried Elias Vance with my own hands and lied about it for eleven years. Everything I love is either dead or in that dry bag. There is nothing left in this cabin to save except me, and I am the only one who knows the way out on foot."

She squeezed my hands once, hard, and let go.

"All I ask — the only thing I ask of either of you — is that the truth outlives me. Not the samples. Not the money. The *truth*. That somewhere, someone says out loud that three men were murdered in that cave and an old woman kept the receipts. That's the whole inheritance. That's the estate. Don't lose it, don't trade it, don't let Reeve buy it back with a smile and a wire transfer." She stepped back and looked at us both, and for a moment she seemed taller, though she hadn't moved. "Can you carry that?"

Teodor answered first, hoarse: "Yes."

I took longer. Long enough that the sleet against the window filled the silence like applause for the wrong speech. Because I knew what she was really asking. Not whether we'd publish the ledger — that was easy, that was already decided, that was the whole reason we'd walked eleven miles through freezing rain. She was asking whether we'd *stay with it*. Whether we'd stand in the inquiry and say our own names alongside the guilty ones. Whether we'd pay admission.

"I can carry it," I said finally. "Whether I survive carrying it — that part I'm not promising anybody."

"Good girl." Sif nodded, satisfied, as if that were the correct answer on the exam. "Honest cowards make the best witnesses. You've got something to lose. Men with nothing to lose lie for free."

* * *

We left at dusk, the dry bag split between us, heavy as a body. Behind us the cabin windows glowed orange, and by the time we reached the first bend in the trail the glow had become a pulse, and by the second bend it was a column of sparks standing up against the early dark like a signal flare aimed at heaven.

Sif stood beside the burning cabin, small and square against the firelight, the dog at her heel. She didn't wave. She raised one hand — not goodbye, I realized. *North.* Pointing the way she'd go, on foot, into the willows and the dark and the eleven-year war's final retreat.

Then the trees took her, and she was gone, and the cabin burned like a pyre for everyone she'd ever been.

Teodor watched the flames a long time. "She gave up everything," he said. "Her home, her name, her whole life. For paper."

"Not paper," I said. My breath came out ragged, and not from the cold. "For Elias. For Okonkwo and Lindqvist. For the truth outliving her." I shifted the dry bag higher on my shoulder and felt its weight settle against my spine like a passenger I'd agreed to carry the rest of the way. "That's not sacrifice, Teo. That's inheritance. And we're the heirs."

We walked the last miles to the gravel bar in silence, sleet turning to snow, the fire's glow fading behind us until the dark was total and ordinary again. At the plane, under the tarp, I checked the fuel and the tie-downs and the sky all in the same sweep, seven years of habit running smooth as an engine.

Then I stopped. Because the sky to the southeast — the direction of Kestrel Bay, the direction of Reeve, the direction of everything — was clearing. Fast. A high pressure ridge shoving the storm front inland ahead of schedule, opening a window over the Divide that shouldn't exist until spring, narrow and clean and closing fast.

A window wide enough to fly the samples to the university lab in a single impossible run.

Or wide enough for someone else to fly something toward us.

I looked at Teodor. He was already looking at the same sky, reading it the way I read it, and neither of us said the obvious thing out loud:

The storm season had opened early — and it had opened for us.
