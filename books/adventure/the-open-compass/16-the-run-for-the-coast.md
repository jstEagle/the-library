## 16. The Run for the Coast

The snowmobiles came at dawn, and Teodor heard them before he saw them — a thin mechanical whine threading through the wind off the divide, the sound of engines that had no business being forty miles from the nearest road.

He was breaking camp when Yara came out of the tent with her binoculars already up. She didn't say anything for a long moment. That was how he'd learned to read her: silence meant bad news, longer silence meant worse.

"Three machines," she said finally. "Coming up the river valley. They'll be here in an hour, maybe less if the crust holds."

Teodor's hands went cold inside his mitts — not from the temperature. From recognition. He'd spent eleven years imagining this moment in the abstract, the way you imagine your own funeral: necessary to think about, impossible to actually picture. Now it was arriving on a tide of engine noise, and it looked exactly like what Reeve's people would look like. Practical jackets. No branding. Moving like men who had done this before.

"We run," Yara said. It wasn't a question, but she said it like one anyway, giving him the dignity of agreement.

They ran.

* * *

The tundra in late storm season was a country of illusions. What looked like solid ground was muskeg wearing a skin of ice; what looked like flat going rose into hidden hummocks that could flip a loaded sled. Yara drove the lead machine with the economy of someone who had broken things out here before and hated doing it twice. Teodor rode the second sled, the sample cases lashed behind him — two of them now, identical in every way except one. The real case held Sif Halvorsen's ledger wrapped around the core samples in oilcloth. The decoy held bricks of frozen peat cut from the riverbank at midnight, weighted to match.

Yara had built the decoy herself, by headlamp, without being asked. When he'd asked her why, she'd shrugged and said, "Because whoever's chasing us expects us to protect the truth with our lives. So we let them take something worth our lives instead."

Now, cresting a long swell of frozen ground, Teodor looked back and saw the three machines fanned out behind them like a hand opening. They weren't gaining yet. The crust was holding their weight better than it held the sleds' — heavier machines, heavier men — but the river valley ahead bent south toward the coast, and the bend was where the going got honest or got fatal, depending on which line you picked.

"The airstrip?" he shouted over the engines.

"Coastal strip's forty miles. Fuel cache at the old survey camp at mile twenty-two." Yara's voice carried flat across the wind, stripped of everything but information. "After that, nothing until the bay."

"And them?"

"They know about the fuel cache too. Everybody knows about the fuel cache. That's the problem with secrets in this country — they're only secret until they're useful."

The first shot came twenty minutes later, and it wasn't aimed at them. It cracked off somewhere to their left, high and wild, and Yara didn't even flinch — just leaned harder into the throttle and put a ridge between them and the sound.

"That's a message," she called back. "Telling us they're serious."

"You believe it?"

"I believe a man who wants to talk doesn't fire first." Her eyes stayed forward. "But I also believe they haven't tried to actually hit anything yet, which means they want the cases more than they want us dead. For now."

For now. The phrase hung in the exhaust smoke behind them like something with weight.

* * *

At mile nineteen the crust failed.

It happened the way these things always happened — instantly, completely, and to the person least able to afford it. Yara's sled punched through a lens of ice over a feeder slough and went down hard, the machine burying itself to the handlebars in freezing muck, and Teodor was off his own sled before he'd decided to move, hauling at the rear rack while she worked the throttle. The engine screamed. The mud gave an inch, then another.

Behind them, the pursuers were closing. He could see them clearly now — three dark shapes against the pale ground, kicking up spindrift, spreading into a formation that had nothing casual in it.

"Leave it," Yara said suddenly. "Leave the machine."

"It's your—"

"It's a machine." She was already pulling the sample cases off the dead sled, shoving one into his arms, taking the other herself. "We go on foot until we reach the survey camp. There's a canoe cached there — Sif told me, years ago, in case I ever needed—" She stopped herself. "In case anyone ever needed it."

They ran. The tundra took its toll immediately — the going that had been fast under runners became brutal under boots, each step a negotiation with ground that wanted ankles. Teodor's breath tore in his chest. Behind them the snowmobiles slowed at the slough, circled the hole where Yara's machine sat drowning, and stopped.

One man dismounted. Raised a rifle. And then, visibly, deliberately, lowered it again.

"They won't shoot," Teodor gasped. "Not yet."

"No." Yara's face was set in that expression he'd come to think of as her compass face — the one that pointed away from everything. "Not while there's a chance we're carrying what they want. Dead men drop things, and things sink in sloughs."

* * *

They reached the survey camp at dusk, or what passed for dusk this far north in storm season — a bruised half-light that made the world feel underwater. The camp was three collapsed structures and a fuel drum, exactly as forgotten as Sif's memory of it. But the canoe was there, overturned under a sheet of rotted poly, and beneath the poly, wrapped in wax paper, a coil of fresh rope and a note in handwriting gone brown with age:

*For whoever comes after. The river runs true to the coast. Trust the current, not the map.*

Sif. Eleven years ago, Sif had stocked this cache, and it had waited all this time like a letter never sent.

Yara stood looking at the note for a long time. Then she folded it carefully and put it inside her jacket, over her heart, and said nothing at all.

They ate cold food and watched the valley through gaps in the collapsed wall. The pursuers had made camp two miles back, at the edge of the slough country. A small fire. Three figures. Patient.

"They'll push at first light," Yara said. "Maybe sooner. The river's the only fast way to the coast now, and they know it. They'll try to get ahead of us, run the valley parallel, pick their spot."

"So we don't take the river."

"We take the river." She turned to him, and in the failing light her eyes were very steady. "But not both of us. Not together."

Teodor felt the sentence arrive before he understood it, the way you feel thunder before you count the miles to the lightning.

"Reeve's people have seen two of us since Kestrel Bay," she went on, level, relentless. "Two sleds, two sets of tracks, two silhouettes. If they see two canoes, or two people in one canoe, they know exactly what to chase. But if one of us becomes visible and loud and *obvious* — carrying a case, making noise, lighting a lamp — and the other disappears..." She let it hang.

"No," Teodor said.

"You haven't heard the plan."

"There's no version of this plan where I like it."

"Teodor." She said his name the way she said almost nothing else — directly, without armor. "Eleven years ago, someone walked out of that cave alone and spent a decade keeping a legend alive so the dead wouldn't be forgotten. You understand carrying things alone. I'm asking you to trust me to carry this one."

"And what do you carry?"

"The real samples." She tapped the case at her side. "You carry the decoy. You take the river loud — lamp lit, moving slow enough to be followed, stopping where a careful man would stop. They take you, they take the case, they open it, and by the time they know it's riverbank peat, I'm thirty miles downriver and gone."

"And if they don't open it before they catch you? If they decide you're not worth the trouble?"

Her jaw tightened. He watched her weigh honesty against comfort and choose honesty, because that was what they did now, what they'd been doing since the cave — choosing the heavier thing.

"Then I'm in trouble," she said. "And you'll be at the airstrip with a radio, telling every operator on this coast that a geologist named Teodor Vance is missing, and that his last known position is a lie worth investigating."

"That's your plan for me. Radio calls."

"That's my plan for you. Stay alive. Be loud. Make yourself expensive to kill." A ghost of something crossed her face — not quite a smile. "You've been doing it since the day you hired me."

He looked at her for a long time. At the woman who had spent seven years perfecting the art of leaving, offering to be the one who escaped — no. Offering to be the one who *arrived*. Because that was the shape of it, he realized. She wasn't running anymore. Somewhere between the cave and the slough and Sif's note folded over her heart, Yara Pemberton had stopped pointing away.

"If it goes wrong," he said, "if they take me — you fly out. You don't come back. You don't trade yourself. Promise me."

"I promise I'll make it count."

"That's not—"

"It's the only promise I keep cleanly." She held out her hand. "Trust me, Teodor. Not because I've earned it. Because the alternative is trusting them, and we've seen what they do with trust."

Outside, the light died completely. In the valley below, the enemy fire burned down to embers.

He took her hand. Her grip was cold and certain, and it held for one second longer than a handshake needed, and in that second Teodor understood that this was the act of faith the whole journey had been building toward — not the flying, not the cave, not the evidence. This. Handing the truth to someone else and walking in the opposite direction, loud and slow and bait-shaped, believing she would carry it home.

"For the record," he said, "I still hate this plan."

"Noted for the inquiry," she said, and almost smiled.

* * *

They launched at gray dawn, separately, an hour apart.

Teodor went first, down to the riverbank with the decoy case lashed prominently to the canoe's center thwart, a chemical lamp clipped to the bow where no one could miss it. The river ran high with meltwater, fast and brown and full of the winter's debris, and it took the canoe the way Sif's note promised — true, insistent, trustworthy. He didn't paddle so much as steer, letting the current do the work of a man in a hurry.

An hour later, from a bend in the river, he saw Yara launch. Or rather, he saw nothing at all, which was the point — a shadow slipping into the current downstream of his passage, unlit, silent, gone. He stared at the empty water where she'd been and felt the strange vertigo of faith: no proof, no signal, just the belief that somewhere behind him, in the gray morning, she was moving.

The pursuers found him within the hour.

He heard them before he saw them — engines along the valley rim, paralleling, patient as wolves. Then a rifle shot, close, deliberate, splintering the gunwale six inches from his hand. A voice amplified by a megaphone, flat and official:

*"Dr. Vance. Stop the canoe. Keep your hands visible. This ends easier than you think."*

Teodor raised both hands off the paddles and let the river take him. The current swung the boat toward the bank, toward the waiting machines, toward the three men whose faces he would remember later with terrible clarity — competent, incurious, following orders.

They pulled him from the water on the gravel bar at mile thirty-one. The tall one took the case from the thwart, weighed it in his hands, and frowned slightly — a frown Teodor catalogued and cherished, because it was the first hairline crack in their certainty.

"Where's the pilot?" the tall one asked.

"Gone," Teodor said. "You're chasing the wrong river."

The man studied him for a long moment, then keyed a radio and spoke four words that Teodor would carry into the next chapter of his life like a stone in his boot:

*"We have the doctor."*

And forty miles north, in a canoe moving silent and dark down a river that ran true to the coast, Yara Pemberton heard nothing at all — and kept paddling toward the airstrip, toward the plane, toward whatever came next, carrying the truth the dead had paid for and the living had chosen.
