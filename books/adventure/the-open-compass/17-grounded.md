## 17. Grounded

The decoy case went into the tundra with Teodor, and I flew north with the real samples strapped behind my seat like a second heartbeat.

That was the plan. His plan. He'd said it on the airstrip at dawn with his hands jammed in his pockets and his breath smoking in the cold, and he'd said it like a man proposing a chess move instead of a funeral: *They'll follow the case they can see. You fly low, you fly west, you don't look back.*

"I'm not good at not looking back," I told him.

"You're excellent at it," he said. "Seven years of practice. Just do it one more time."

I should have known then that he was wrong about me — or that I was wrong about myself, which is worse, because at least being wrong about someone else leaves them to blame.

* * *

The first hour was clean. Low over the muskeg, skimming the frozen rivers where the ice ran white and unbroken, the Otter heavy but honest under my hands. Behind me the sample case rode in its padded wrap, and inside it were core samples that proved a mineral strike worth more than anything else in the territory, and eleven years of proof that men had been murdered to keep it quiet. Sif's ledger copies were sewn into the lining. Every mile I flew west carried the weight of three dead geologists and one old woman's burned cabin.

The radio stayed silent. That was part of the plan too — no transmissions, nothing for Reeve's people to triangulate. Silence has always been my native language. For once it felt less like armor and more like a wound.

At the forty-minute mark I broke my own silence, because I couldn't help it. One click of the transmit button, half a second of carrier wave, nothing spoken. Just enough to tell Teodor's receiver — if he still had a receiver, if he was still alive to hear it — that I was flying and free.

He didn't click back.

I told myself the range was wrong. I told myself he wouldn't risk the signal. I told myself a lot of things for the next twenty minutes, and then the river bend came up beneath me, the one where we'd agreed he'd cut south toward the coastal strip with the decoy case, and I looked down, and I stopped telling myself things.

There was a snowmobile track stitched across the white like black thread. It ended at a cluster of figures near the tree line — four of them, maybe five, standing around something small and kneeling. Even from eight hundred feet I knew the shape of what I was seeing. A man on his knees in the snow, hands on his head, and men standing over him with the loose, settled posture of people who have done this before and expect to do it again.

One of them raised a rifle and fired at me. The sound never reached me over the engine; I just saw the muzzle flash, absurdly small, like a struck match.

Every instinct I owned lit up at once, and every instinct said the same thing: *climb, turn, run.* Seven years of instincts. Seven years of leaving. My hands were already doing it — nose up, bank west, throttle forward — before my mind caught up and asked the only question that mattered.

If I run, what happens to him?

I knew the answer. I'd read it in Reeve's face in Kestrel Bay, in the careful way he'd counted out bills like a man who understood exactly what money was for. Men like Aldous Reeve don't kill witnesses when the witnesses are useful. Teodor, caught with a decoy case full of gravel and newspaper, was useful in exactly one way: as bait. Bait for me. Bait for the real samples.

And bait only stays fresh so long.

I leveled off over the ridge line, out of rifle range, and let the plane fly itself for a moment while I did the arithmetic I'd spent seven years refusing to do. The nearest settlement with a working radio was Teller Creek, ninety minutes east. Reeve's men would move Teodor somewhere defensible by nightfall — the old mining camp at Caribou Cut, most likely, because it had walls and a stove and one road in. They'd hold him until they heard from their boss. And their boss would send word: *the woman flies the samples to us, or your geologist stops needing feeding.*

I could be in Fort Nelson by dark. I could be in Whitehorse by tomorrow. I could hand the samples to a university lab through channels Teodor had set up months ago, from a thousand miles away, safe, anonymous, alive — and I could spend the rest of my life knowing exactly what my safety had purchased, and hearing the click of an unanswered radio every time the wind came up.

Sif had burned her own cabin down around sixty years of her life rather than leave her tracks behind for those men. She'd handed me her ledger and asked for one thing: that the truth outlive her.

Teodor had split off into the open tundra carrying a box of lies so that I could carry the truth. He hadn't asked me to come back for him. That was the part that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. He'd trusted me with the evidence and expected nothing — because everyone he'd ever relied on had taught him to expect nothing, and because I'd spent seven years teaching everyone who met me the same lesson.

Well. Lesson over.

I put the Otter into a wide descending turn toward the bay.

* * *

Kestrel Bay in storm season is a gray fist of water punching at a gray jaw of land, and the airstrip sits on the shingle like an afterthought. I came in low over the breakwater with the wind quartering across my tail and put it down hard, the way you land when you're angry, and taxied straight into the lee of Sif Halvorsen's old boathouse, where the plane would be invisible from the water and mostly invisible from the air.

My hands were shaking by the time I shut the engine down. Not fear. Something adjacent to fear but hotter. I sat in the cockpit for a full minute breathing the smell of avgas and cold canvas, and then I took the sample case out from behind the seat, and then I did the thing I should have done over the tundra and hadn't, because grief makes you stupid and I'd been grieving since the river bend.

I opened the case and checked everything.

Samples intact. Ledger pages intact. And there, sewn into the padding along the hinge seam, a small black rectangle the size of a matchbox that had no business existing.

A tracker. Reeve's tracker — the same make I'd found in the first case back in Kestrel Bay, the discovery that had sent us down this whole bloody road. He'd tagged the replacement too. Of course he had. He'd assumed I'd check the decoy and forget the real one, or that panic would make me careless, or simply that I was the kind of person who runs first and thinks later. He'd built his whole strategy on the assumption that I was who I pretended to be.

For once in my life, I was grateful to be underestimated.

I pried the tracker out with a knife blade, wrapped it in a rag, walked two hundred yards up the shingle, and tucked it into the netting of a fish-drying rack at the edge of the village, where it would sit broadcasting its steady little signal from among a hundred racks just like it, in a village where half the population owned vehicles and snow machines and boats that moved around unpredictably. Let Reeve's people chase ghosts through Kestrel Bay. Every hour they spent here was an hour Teodor breathed.

Then I went to find Marta Okonkwo, who ran the co-op and the radio and, unofficially, everything else.

* * *

Marta listened without interrupting, which is rarer than courage. She stood behind her counter with a mug of tea going cold in her hands while I laid it out: the catch, the trade Reeve would offer, the mining camp at Caribou Cut, the timeline. When I finished she set the mug down and looked at me for a long moment.

"You're going back for him," she said. Not a question.

"I'm going to get him out."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

She turned to her shelf of logbooks — decades of them, radio logs, weather logs, movement logs, the collective memory of a coast that official maps ignored — and pulled the current volume, flipping pages with a thumb gone brown from handling. "Caribou Cut," she said. "Reeve's outfit chartered the Lund boat twice this month, fuel and stores, drop point logged as the Cut both times. There's six of them on the ground now, maybe seven. They've got a satellite phone, a generator, and rifles." She looked up. "You've got a fifty-year-old airplane and a grudge."

"And a plan," I said, which was thirty percent true when I said it and needed to be a hundred percent true by morning.

"Yara." She said my name the way almost nobody did anymore — like it belonged to a person, like she'd decided it was worth using. "Whatever you're thinking of doing alone, don't. This bay has been eating Reeve's secrets for eleven years and pretending to taste nothing. There are people here who owe Sif, and people here who owed the men who died in that cave, even if they never knew their names. You're not the only one who's tired of pretending."

I stood in the warm clutter of the co-op with the storm rattling the windows and felt something crack open in me that I'd nailed shut seven years ago. It wasn't comfort. It was heavier than comfort. It was the understanding that I had stopped being a woman passing through sometime in the last few weeks, and that the price of that was no longer being able to leave without taking other people's futures with me.

"Radio operators," I said slowly. "Every station on the coast. How many would take a broadcast from an anonymous source if it came with proof?"

Marta's mouth curved, just slightly. "All of them. It's been a slow winter."

* * *

I worked through the night in the boathouse by lamplight, and the work kept the shaking hands busy. Copies of Sif's ledger pages, photographed and printed at the co-op. Copies of the assay data Teodor had transcribed from the core samples, the numbers that made the whole murder make sense. A cover letter in plain language, naming Reeve, naming the company shell behind him, naming the 2013 expedition and the rockfall that hadn't been an act of God. Enough for any radio operator with a conscience and a transmitter to make the story unkillable. Once it went out, it couldn't be recalled. Neither could I.

That was the design of it, and I won't pretend the design was noble. If I broadcast the truth, Reeve's leverage died. A dead secret can't be traded for a living hostage. But underneath the strategy, honest as I could be with myself in the lamplight, sat a simpler thing: they had Teodor, and I was not leaving him in the snow. Whatever it cost. Whatever it cost was the whole sentence, no fine print.

Seven years ago I had flown away from a burning settlement because staying meant staying, meant being known, meant being responsible, meant that whatever happened next happened partly to me. I'd told myself for seven years that I'd left to survive. Sitting on a crate of aviation oil with a stack of photocopies on my knees, I finally admitted what Sif had probably known the moment she saw me: I hadn't left to survive. I'd left so that surviving wouldn't require anything of me. And it hadn't. It had required nothing, year after year, and it had cost everything.

Dawn came up the color of dishwater. The storm had eased to a hard blow out of the northeast — flyable, barely, if you respected it. I loaded the plane: the evidence case, fuel drums for the long way around, a coil of rope, a pry bar, a first-aid kit sized for optimism. Then I walked up to the co-op and put the sealed broadcast package in Marta's hands.

"Not yet," I said. "Noon tomorrow. If I'm not back by noon tomorrow, or if anyone from Reeve's outfit sets foot in this bay asking questions — send it. Everything. To every station from Teller Creek to the border."

"And if you are back?"

"Send it anyway." I watched her absorb that. "It was never supposed to stay buried. That was the deal. That was always the deal."

Marta held the package against her chest like an infant. "You know what you're asking. Once this goes out, your name goes with it. Your license, your flights, all of it. They'll take your wings for the illegal legs if nothing else."

"I know."

"You've spent seven years making sure nobody knew your name."

"I know that too." I zipped my flight jacket to the chin and found that my hands had finally stopped shaking. "Turns out I want it back."

* * *

Caribou Cut at dusk looks like what it is: a place the world tried once and gave up on. Rusted headframe, collapsed shed, one bunkhouse with a stovepipe breathing smoke into the last gray light. I brought the Otter in on the frozen creek bed a mile downstream, into the wind, with the tires kissing snow so gently the alders barely nodded, and killed the engine, and sat in the sudden enormous silence listening to my own pulse.

Six men, Marta's count. Maybe seven. One hostage. No weapon but a pry bar and the truth, and the truth wouldn't arrive until noon tomorrow, and Teodor might not have until noon tomorrow.

I checked the sample case one last time — empty now except for the false bottom and the ledger copies I'd already distributed — and slung it over my shoulder anyway. Bait recognizes bait. If they wanted the case, I wanted them looking at it instead of the tree line.

Then I started walking up the creek toward the smoke, through the blue snow, toward the men who had taken everything from Sif and the dead and were holding the last piece of it on their knees in a bunkhouse, and I made myself one promise with every step:

This time, nobody gets left behind. Not him. Not me. Not even the ones who don't deserve it.

The stovepipe smoke bent sideways on the wind, pointing away from the camp, away from the coast, away from everything I'd ever run toward.

I walked the other way.
