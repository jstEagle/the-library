## 18. Negotiation at Low Water

**Yara**

The tide chart said the water would pull back at 4:17 in the morning, and it was right. By 3:50 the sea had already begun its long retreat, dragging itself off the shelf in sheets of black glass, and by 4:30 the cave mouth stood exposed for the first time since the equinox — a ragged arch of wet stone, breathing cold air out into the dark like something sleeping and half-awake.

Yara sat on the ridge above it with the engine off, watching the moonlight move on the flats. Below, forty meters down the scree, a man waited with a lantern.

Aldous Reeve had come alone. That was either respect or arrogance, and she'd decided somewhere over the Divide that it didn't matter which. He stood at the base of the arch with his hands in his coat pockets, patient as driftwood, and every few minutes he checked his watch — not because he needed to know the time, but because he wanted her to see him checking it. *We have until the tide turns,* the gesture said. *And then we don't.*

Teodor was behind her, crouched at the tail of the plane where she couldn't see him, doing whatever he'd been doing to the sample case for the last hour. She hadn't asked. Some things you were better off not knowing until after.

"You're sure about the frequency?" she said, without turning around.

"Every operator from Kestrel Bay to Cape Merrow," Teodor said. "Sif's ledger, the assay numbers, the core logs. I read it twice before I sent it. Slow, so they'd copy it clean."

"And Reeve's people?"

"Reeve's people heard it too." A pause. "That was sort of the point."

She nodded, though he couldn't see it. Her hands were steady on the yoke cover, which surprised her. Seven years of leaving had taught her body one reliable trick: it went calm exactly when it should have been afraid, the way a plane goes quiet just before the stall warning sounds. She knew what that quiet meant now. She'd spent most of her life mistaking it for peace.

Below, Reeve raised the lantern once — a slow arc, an invitation.

"Time to go be honest," Yara said, and climbed down into the dark.

* * *

**Teodor**

He watched her descend the scree with the case slung across her back and thought, not for the first time, that she moved like someone who had already survived the worst thing that would ever happen to her. There was a looseness in her shoulders that wasn't relaxation. It was the specific looseness of a person who has stopped protecting herself because she has decided the outcome doesn't depend on her body anymore.

He stayed at the plane, as agreed, with the radio keyed and the second battery warm. His job was simple: if things went wrong, he flew. Not away — that had been the old plan, the plan they'd built separately and never spoken aloud. The new plan, the one made on the frozen lake with their breath freezing between them, was different. If things went wrong, he flew *toward* Kestrel Bay, low over the water, and told everyone everything again, louder, until somebody with a badge had no choice but to listen.

Reeve met her at the base of the arch. Through the binoculars Teodor could see them clearly in the lantern light: two figures on wet stone, the cave mouth behind them like a held breath.

He couldn't hear the words. He didn't need to. He'd rehearsed this scene in his head for eleven years — first as grief, then as research, then as rage, and finally, in the last three weeks, as something he almost didn't recognize as hope. His mentor, Dr. Ilse Marchetti, had stood in some version of this negotiation once. He knew how it ended. Three bodies in a camp, one empty bag, core samples worth billions sealed under a rockfall that no natural process explained.

The lantern swung. Reeve gestured toward the case on Yara's shoulder. She didn't hand it over.

Teodor exhaled slowly and keyed the radio, more to give his hands something to do than because anyone was listening. The frequency was open. The whole coast was listening. That was the whole point.

* * *

**Yara**

"You came," Reeve said. "I'll admit, I gave you even odds on running."

"I considered it." Her voice carried flat across the flats. "Then I remembered I'm bad at it."

"You're excellent at it. Seven years of excellent." He tilted his head at the case. "May I?"

"No."

He smiled — a small, professional smile, the kind that had probably closed a hundred deals in rooms with better lighting. "Yara. May I call you Yara? We're past formalities, I think. You've been paid. Twice now. The money is in your account, the story about unstable cliffs is filed, and my employer considers the matter closed. All you have to do tonight is put that case on the ground, walk back up to your plane, and fly home a wealthy woman who never thinks about any of this again."

"That's the offer? Same as before?"

"The same offer, improved. Because the situation has changed." He glanced at the tide line, still retreating, patient. "You've become difficult, and difficult people require more expensive solutions. So: the case, and in exchange, Mr. Vance walks free. No charges, no accidents, no unfortunate weather events. My employer is generous when the arithmetic works."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the arithmetic changes." He said it without heat, the way a man reads a shipping manifest. "The tide comes back in ninety minutes. This shelf will be underwater. And you'll both be standing here when it does, or you won't be standing anywhere. I'd rather write the generous version of this report, Yara. Truly."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she slid the case off her shoulder and set it on the stone between them, and saw something ease in his face — the fractional release of a man who believed the world ran on transactions, because transactions were all he'd ever been asked to understand.

"It's heavier than you think," she said.

"They usually are."

"Open it."

He crouched, worked the latches, lifted the lid. The lantern light fell inside, and Yara watched his expression do something complicated — relief collapsing into confusion, confusion into a stillness that was worse than either.

The case was full of river stones.

"Where are the samples?" Reeve said quietly.

"Broadcast," Yara said. "Three hours ago. Sif Halvorsen's ledger — eleven years of payments, dates, delivery codes, your name next to most of them. The assay results. The core logs. The location of the strike. Every radio operator from Kestrel Bay to Cape Merrow copied it down by hand before dawn. Half of them are reading it back to each other right now to make sure they got the numbers right. Old Man Ferro in Merrow is probably typing it into his weather report so it goes out on the coastal net at six sharp."

Reeve rose slowly. The lantern threw his shadow long across the wet stone, and for the first time since she'd met him, he looked less like a man conducting business and more like a man doing math that kept refusing to come out.

"That's not possible," he said. "Your aircraft was—"

"Tracked? Yes. Sewn into the case lining. Which is why the case you're holding flew the decoy route through the Divide while the real samples went south with Sif's grandniece on the mail boat, two days ago, in a fish crate, under a load of halibut." She let that land. "You've been listening to a radio beacon attached to a box of rocks, Aldous. Your employer has been paying very close attention to a box of rocks."

The tide sighed against the shelf, further out now, the flats gleaming.

"So the secret's out," she said. "It was out before you landed. Before I landed. There's nothing left to buy, nothing left to bury, nobody left to bury it with. The only thing your employer can still control is how he looks when the inquiry starts asking why a mineral strike worth billions got sealed under a rockfall with three scientists inside it. And I promise you, Aldous — I have read that ledger. I know exactly what your handwriting looks like in it."

For a moment he said nothing. The wind moved off the water, cold and clean, smelling of kelp and iron.

"You understand what you've done," he said at last. "Not morally. Practically. You've taken the one thing keeping you alive — your usefulness — and thrown it into the sea."

"No," Yara said. "I took the one thing keeping me *quiet*, and I gave it to everyone. Those aren't the same thing. I spent seven years learning the difference."

* * *

**Teodor**

Through the glasses he saw Reeve go still — the particular stillness of a man recalculating everything — and then saw him say something short and final, and turn, and start up the scree toward the trailhead where, presumably, a boat or a plane or a very patient subordinate waited.

It was over too easily. Teodor knew that in his bones, the way you know a runway is shorter than it looks. Men like Reeve did not walk away from leverage; they walked away from *spent* leverage, and they did it while planning the next expenditure.

He keyed the radio. "Yara. He's moving. Get clear."

"He's moving," her voice came back, calm as a checklist. "So am I."

But she wasn't. Through the glasses he watched her stand at the cave mouth instead, looking up at the black arch, and he understood a half-second too late what she was doing — the same thing he would have done, the same thing Sif had done for eleven years. Saying something to the dead. Telling Ilse Marchetti and the two whose names Teodor had never learned that it was finished, that the rockfall had failed, that the truth had walked out of their grave and gone out on the airwaves in a fish crate and a weather report.

Then she turned and started up the scree, fast, and Teodor was already pulling the chocks when the first engine coughed.

It coughed again. Caught. Died.

Behind him, out on the black water, a boat he had not seen — riding dark, no lights, engine idling far off the shelf — came suddenly alive. Two wakes converged on the tidal flats from opposite directions, fast, deliberate, closing on the only dry ground left.

"Yara," Teodor said into the radio, watching the tide gauge in his mind begin to run backward. "Change of plans. The arithmetic isn't done."

Above the cave mouth, the moon slid behind cloud, and the water below them began, very quietly, to come back.
