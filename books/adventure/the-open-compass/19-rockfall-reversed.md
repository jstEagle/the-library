## 19. Rockfall, Reversed

The tide was going out when I landed on the shelf, and I remember thinking — with the strange clarity that comes right before everything goes wrong — that the sea had opened the door for us twice now. Once to show us the dead. Once, if we were lucky, to bury the living.

Teodor sat in the back of the Cessna with his wrists zip-tied in front of him, which was Reeve's idea of a compromise. Reeve wanted rope. Teodor had pointed out, reasonably, that a geologist needed his hands free to walk on wet rock, and Reeve — who liked to think of himself as a reasonable man, the way a trap likes to think of itself as a doorway — had allowed it.

"Forty minutes of low water," Reeve said, checking his watch. He stood at the cave mouth with the wind pulling at his coat, looking like a man inspecting property he intended to repossess. "That's what your chart says. So let's not waste it."

"Reeve," I said. "You've already lost. Every radio operator from here to Fort Nelson has Sif's ledger. The sample data went out an hour ago. There's nothing left to trade."

"I know." He said it mildly, almost kindly, and that was the worst part. "This was never about the data, Ms. Pemberton. Data can be discredited. Ledgers can be forged. What can't be survived is precedent." He turned and looked into the black archway of the cave, where the last of the daylight died about ten meters in. "Eleven years ago my employer gave an instruction: no strike, no witnesses, no story. The instruction worked. It will work again."

"You're going to kill us in there," Teodor said from the plane's door. Not a question. He'd been quiet since the airstrip, since two of Reeve's men walked him out of the treeline with a rifle barrel resting against his shoulder blade like a polite suggestion.

"I'm going to *show* you something," Reeve said. "What happens after that is geology."

* * *

Inside, the cave was exactly as the tide had left it three weeks ago, except colder, except darker, except that now I knew every meter of it by heart — the carved markers counting down the passage, the rusted carabiners still hanging where the 2013 expedition had racked them, the place where the inner passage had come down on four people who thought they'd found a miracle.

Reeve's men carried floodlights. They swept them along the walls without reverence, and the light made the rock look wet and alive, like the inside of something swallowing.

"The seam runs through here," Reeve said, crouching at a wall where the headlamps of the dead had once found color. Even under floodlight I could see it — a vein of something that caught the light wrong, green-gold, like the rock had a fever. "Palladium group metals, in concentrations that don't occur in nature. My employer's geologists spent two years confirming it before your mentor's expedition stumbled onto the samples." He glanced back at Teodor. "Your Elias Vance was thorough. Thorough men are dangerous."

"He wasn't my mentor because he was thorough," Teodor said. His voice was steady, but I could hear the crack running through it underneath, the same one I'd heard over the frozen lake when he told me how Elias used to make him memorize tide tables as punishment for daydreaming. "He was my mentor because he believed things belonged to everyone."

"And look where believing got him." Reeve straightened up. "Ms. Pemberton. A word about your situation."

I didn't move. One of his men moved instead, drifting toward the mouth of the cave with the casualness of a man taking up a position he'd rehearsed.

"Here is what happens next," Reeve said. "We proceed to the inner chamber. You will show me where you cached the remaining samples — and don't insult me by saying there are none; I watched you carry that case out of the water three weeks ago, and I've read your flight logs since before you knew my name. Then my colleagues and I will withdraw, and the charge I placed at the throat of this cave yesterday afternoon will do what charges do."

The word *charge* went through me like cold water through a wetsuit.

"You sealed it," I said. "You sealed it while the tide was up."

"Geology," Reeve said again, pleasantly. "Unstable cliffs. The same story we told in 2013. It's a good story. Stories get better with practice."

Behind him, past his shoulder, past the floodlights, I could see the cave mouth — a gray coin of sky and sea, getting smaller in my vision the way it does when your body understands a threat before your mind agrees to. And I understood, finally, completely, what Sif Halvorsen had lived with for eleven years. Not guilt. Arithmetic. She had walked out of this rock carrying the knowledge that staying alive meant staying silent, and she had paid the silence out year by year, supply crate by supply crate, like a debt she could never finish servicing.

I was done servicing debts.

"Okay," I said.

Teodor's head came around. *Yara—*

"Okay," I said again, louder, so both of Reeve's men would hear it. "I'll show you where the samples are. But you should know something first, Mr. Reeve, since you're a man who appreciates accurate information." I took a step toward him, and his man with the rifle shifted, and I kept my hands loose and visible and my voice level. "You said the data can be discredited. That's true. But I didn't send the data."

Something flickered across his face — the first honest thing I'd seen him do.

"I sent the *coordinates*," I said. "And the tide tables. And a description of the seam, in enough detail that any geologist on this coast can walk in here at the next record low and verify it with their own eyes. Which they will. Because I also sent them the date of the next extreme low water." I smiled at him, and it wasn't a nice smile. "It's in nine days, Mr. Reeve. You can't blow this cave fast enough to outrun a tide chart."

For a long moment the only sound was the sea, breathing somewhere behind us, patient as a creditor.

"You're bluffing," Reeve said.

"I'm a bush pilot," I said. "Bluffing is all weather decisions are. Ask me how often I'm wrong."

He looked at his men. He looked at the dark. And I watched him do the arithmetic I'd handed him — nine days, witnesses, a verified strike on protected-land petitions already moving through three offices — and I watched him arrive at the answer I'd been counting on, which was that killing us had just become insufficient.

It was the moment his man with the rifle glanced at him for instruction.

It was the moment Teodor moved.

I'll give him this: eleven years of grief had taught him patience, and patience is just violence with good timing. He came off the wall where Reeve's men had parked him, brought his bound wrists down across the forearm of the second man — the one holding the floodlight rather than the rifle, because Teodor Vance understood that in a dark cave, light is the weapon — and the light went spinning across the floor in a wheel of white. In the strobing chaos I did the only thing I've ever been genuinely world-class at: I ran, low, along the wall I'd memorized, shoulder-checking the rifleman into the rock as I passed. The gun clattered. Somebody shouted. The floodlight rolled to a stop against a carved marker, throwing the dead man's tally marks huge and trembling across the ceiling.

"OUT!" I screamed. "Everybody OUT — the charge, Reeve, your own charge — the shockwave—"

Because that was true, and it was the only card left in either of our hands. Whatever Reeve had set at the throat of the cave, it was sitting in fractured rock above a rising sea, and four panicked people fighting in its blast radius was a risk equation even Aldous Reeve couldn't balance.

He knew it too. I saw it land on him — saw the man who had ordered a rockfall in 2013 stand in a cave of his own making and understand, for one naked instant, exactly what kind of arithmetic he'd been doing all these years.

His men got to the mouth first. Reeve followed. Teodor grabbed my sleeve and we ran together, our boots slipping on wet stone, the gray coin of the exit swinging wider with every stride as the tide fell away beneath it — and then we were out, all five of us, gasping on the shelf under a sky the color of hammered tin, the wind hitting us like a wall of hands.

Nobody had the rifle. It was somewhere in the dark behind us, and the sea was already coming for it.

* * *

Reeve's men stood at the edge of the shelf, breathing hard, looking at their boss for instructions they weren't going to get. Because out here there was no cave to control, no dark to own, no story to tell — just open coast, an anchored boat, a floatplane, and a woman standing between Aldous Reeve and the truth with nothing in her hands at all.

That was the thing I'd figured out on the flight in, the thing I'd bet all our lives on: Reeve couldn't shoot us on an open beach in front of his own employees, because his entire method — eleven years of it — depended on never leaving evidence. Two bodies on a tidal shelf with witnesses was evidence. Two bodies inside a collapsed cave was geology.

So I stood between him and the plane, and I said, quietly, so only he could hear:

"You're going to walk to your boat, Mr. Reeve. You're going to motor out of this bay. And you're going to spend the rest of your career explaining to very careful people why the strike you buried is now public, documented, and scheduled for verification by people who don't work for you." I let him see my face, all of it, no professionalism left on it anywhere. "Or you can stay. The tide comes back in six hours. I hear the admission price is steep."

"This isn't justice," he said. His voice had gone thin. "You understand that. Your ledger, your broadcast — none of it brings anyone back. All you've done is dig up the grave and stand in it."

"No," I said. "Elias Vance dug the grave. You put him in it. I'm just refusing to let it be unmarked." I nodded past him, at the archway, at the dark where three men and an empty sleeping bag had waited eleven years for someone to say their names out loud. "There's a difference between vengeance and testimony, Mr. Reeve. Vengeance wants you dead. Testimony just wants you *present*. At the inquiry. On the record. Under oath." I stepped aside, clearing his path to the water, the way you'd hold a door. "Be present."

He looked at me for a long time. Somewhere in that look, I think, was the understanding that I had beaten him with the only weapon he'd never planned for — not a gun, not leverage, but the sheer unreasonable stubbornness of a woman who had finally found a reason to stop running. He'd built his whole career on people choosing survival over truth. He'd never met anyone who'd learned, the hard way, that they're the same choice.

He walked to the boat. His men followed. The engine started, and the wake spread out across the bay like a sentence being crossed out.

Teodor came and stood beside me, wrists still zip-tied, watching the boat shrink.

"You could have let the cave have him," he said.

"Yeah." I pulled my knife and cut his bindings. "Sif burned her cabin down rather than leave tracks. She asked for one thing — that the truth outlive her. Killing him in there wouldn't have outlived anything. It would've just been another rockfall with better intentions." I looked at the archway, black and patient above the falling tide. "Let the inquiry be the rockfall. Let it fall on him square."

Teodor rubbed his wrists and didn't say thank you, because we were past that, way past it, into the country where you don't thank a person for being your own heartbeat. He just said, "Nine days?"

"Nine days," I confirmed. "Low water. Half the geology departments on the coast are going to be fighting over charter flights."

"And the other half?"

"The other half," I said, turning toward the plane, "are going to be subpoenaed."

Behind us, right on schedule, deep inside the cliff, something settled with a sound like a held breath finally released — Reeve's charge, or the sea, or eleven years of weight coming off the rock. Dust breathed out of the cave mouth and hung in the cold air, glittering.

The entrance held. The tide kept falling. And somewhere south, a man in a boat was rowing — motoring — toward a reckoning he could no longer buy, bribe, or bury.

I climbed into the Cessna and reached for the radio. There were names to say out loud, and for the first time in seven years, I wanted the whole coast listening.
