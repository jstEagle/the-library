## 20. Testimony

The hearing room in Whitehorse smelled like floor wax and old coffee, and it was the first time I'd seen Yara Pemberton sit still for longer than ten minutes since the night we crawled out of the cave.

She sat at the witness table with her hands flat on the wood, flight jacket folded over the back of her chair like a flag she'd already lowered. Her license — the laminated card she'd carried for seven years, the one that let her be nobody anywhere — lay on the table in front of the chairwoman. She'd put it there herself before anyone asked. That detail mattered to me more than anything else that happened that week, and a lot happened that week.

The room held maybe forty people: three commissioners behind a long bench, two lawyers from the Ministry who kept looking at their notes like the notes might bite them, a stenographer whose fingers never stopped moving, and rows of observers — radio operators from the coast, bush pilots in jackets too good for them, Sif Halvorsen's grandniece Ingrid sitting in the second row with Sif's ledger in a plastic sleeve on her lap because Sif herself had refused to come.

"She said her testimony is the cabin ash," Ingrid told me outside, the first morning. "And the ledger. And that she trusts me to hold her seat." Ingrid was nineteen and had Sif's same unblinking way of looking at you, as if you were weather she'd already decided to wait out.

I took the stand first. They wanted chronology, and chronology started with me — the coordinates, the charter, the tide chart anomaly my mentor Elias Brandt had circled in red eleven years before his expedition stopped answering its radio. I talked for two hours. I described the carved markers inside the cave, the rusted climbing gear, the three bodies in the 2013 camp, the core samples in their aluminum sleeves. I described the drill marks on the inner passage where the rockfall had been set rather than suffered — the charge pattern too clean, the scorch too even, the kind of work you don't get from geology.

Commissioner Adeyemi, the chairwoman, was a compact woman with reading glasses on a chain and the patience of someone who'd spent thirty years listening to people lie to her. She let me finish, then asked the only question that mattered.

"Dr. Vance, when did you first understand that the 2013 expedition was killed?"

I'd rehearsed an answer. Something measured, something academic. What came out instead was: "When I found Elias's field case sealed and packed for transport. He wasn't planning to die down there. He was planning to come back out with proof. Somebody decided he shouldn't."

The stenographer's fingers didn't pause. Somewhere in the back row, a man in a company windbreaker — not Reeve; Reeve hadn't been seen in public since the cave, and his absence had its own weight — shifted in his seat.

* * *

Yara testified on the third day.

I'd watched her prepare for it the night before, or rather I'd watched her refuse to prepare. We were in the same boarding house, rooms across the hall, and around midnight I heard her boots in the corridor and found her in the kitchen making coffee she didn't drink. The transcript of her own statement sat untouched on the table.

"You could lawyer this," I said. "Half the pilots in the territory would tell you to. Take the deal, keep the license, testify narrow."

"I know what they'd tell me." She poured a cup anyway, held it, didn't sip. "Teo, do you know what I did with Reeve's money?"

I knew some of it. Eighteen thousand dollars, handed over on the tarmac in Kestrel Bay while the wheels were still hot, taken "out of habit" — her words. I'd learned later she'd never deposited it. It had sat in a manila envelope under the passenger seat of her plane for six weeks, riding every flight like ballast.

"I flew it to Ingrid," she said now. "Every dollar. It's paying for the lodge roof and Ingrid's pilot training, and I wrote on the envelope exactly what it was and who gave it, so when this all came out — and it was going to come out — nobody could say I laundered it. But I took it. That's the part I'm saying tomorrow. Not just the illegal flights. The taking."

"You're going to hand them your license."

"I'm going to hand them the truth and let them decide what it costs." She finally drank the coffee, grimaced at it like it had personally disappointed her. "Seven years ago I flew away from a fire I helped start by staying somewhere I shouldn't have stayed. I've been telling myself ever since that leaving is the safe thing. Leaving is professional. Leaving keeps people alive." She set the cup down. "Reeve counted on that. He looked at me on that tarmac and saw a woman who always leaves. The money was a test, and I passed his test, and I've been sick about it ever since. Tomorrow I fail his test on the record."

I didn't sleep much after that. I don't think she did either.

* * *

The hearing room went quiet in a particular way when Yara walked to the witness table — not respectful quiet, not hostile quiet, but the hush of forty people deciding simultaneously to stop pretending they weren't there for her. Everyone in the territory knew the name Pemberton by then. The ledger had made sure of that. Sif's eleven years of dated entries — payments received, deliveries logged, the initials of the men who carried them, the slow arithmetic of a cover-up maintained one supply run at a time — had been read aloud into the record over two days, and every entry pointed eventually toward the same corporate signature two layers above Aldous Reeve.

Yara raised her right hand. The oath took eight seconds. Then Commissioner Adeyemi said, "Ms. Pemberton, in your own words," and Yara Pemberton, who had spent seven years being no one's witness to anything, began.

She started with Kestrel Bay. The rule she'd built her life on: land, unload, leave before anyone learns your name. She said it plainly, without apology and without self-pity, the way you'd describe a procedure. Then the tide chart anomaly. The diversion she had no legal reason to make. The black archway in the cliff. The rockfall. The dead.

Then she got to the parts that made the lawyers sit up.

"On June fourteenth, Aldous Reeve met my aircraft on the Kestrel Bay strip before I'd shut down the engine. He offered me eighteen thousand dollars and a story about unstable cliffs. I accepted the money." A murmur moved through the room. Adeyemi let it move and die. "I want to be precise about why. Not because I believed his story — I didn't. Because taking the money was the oldest reflex I have. When things go wrong, take the exit. I took the exit for seven years and called it professionalism. Mr. Reeve recognized the reflex because he shares it. The difference between us is that he sells other people's lives to fund his exits, and I only ever sold my own."

"Did you spend the money?" the Ministry counsel asked.

"No. I delivered it, documented, to the Halvorsen estate trust. It's buying a roof and a young woman's flight training. I'll surrender whatever portion the tribunal directs, but I won't pretend accepting it didn't happen."

"And the flights themselves," Adeyemi said. "Walk us through the ones you know were illegal."

Yara walked them through. The decoy route through the storm cell over the Divide — filed as a medicine run, flown as a lie. The unlogged landing on the frozen lake. The low pass over Reeve's survey camp to confirm the tracker sewn into the sample case. Each one she named with date, tail number, weather, and the specific regulation broken. She'd memorized the regulation numbers. I realized, watching her, that she'd done it not to demonstrate competence but so that no one else would have to look them up — so that the cost arrived complete, itemized, impossible to soften.

"Why?" Adeyemi asked at last. "You had the money. You had the exit. One more flight north and you'd have been untouchable. Why turn back?"

The room waited. I waited. I thought I knew the answer — I'd heard it on the frozen lake at two in the morning, wrapped in a survival blanket, her voice steady in the dark while the temperature fell: the story of the settlement fire, the family that took her in, the stove she'd left burning because leaving felt safer than belonging, the smoke column she'd watched from eight thousand feet. I thought she'd tell it again.

She didn't. She said: "Because Sif Halvorsen burned down her own cabin of fifty-one years so that the truth would outlive her, and asked nothing except that somebody carry it the last mile. I was the somebody with the airplane. That's the whole answer. You can weigh it however you like."

Adeyemi weighed it for a long moment over her glasses. "For the record, Ms. Pemberton — the tribunal doesn't like your flying record. Several members find your judgment reckless. But I'll note that this inquiry has heard testimony from eleven witnesses, and not one of them would be alive to give it if you had chosen the exit you're describing." She turned a page. "We'll take the license matter under advisement. Next question."

* * *

They took it under advisement for nine days, which in tribunal time meant they understood exactly what they were doing and wanted the territory to watch them do it slowly.

The verdict, when it came, was read aloud in the same wax-and-coffee room, now standing-room only. The corporate entity two layers above Aldous Reeve — Meridian Extraction Holdings, a name designed to be forgettable — was referred for criminal prosecution on charges including sabotage causing death, conspiracy, and obstruction. Reeve himself, already in custody since the cave, waived extradition and, according to the courthouse gossip that travels faster than any radio broadcast, had begun cooperating against his employers with the enthusiasm of a man who'd read Sif's ledger and understood precisely how thoroughly he'd been set up to be its final entry.

The mineral strike itself — the rare-earth deposit worth, by the government geologists' estimate, more than the entire territorial budget for a decade — was designated protected land pending environmental review, with the review's terms written to make extraction functionally impossible. Commissioner Adeyemi read that clause aloud twice, deliberately, looking at the company lawyers as she did it. Eleven years ago, Elias Brandt's cores had been worth killing for. As of that morning, they were worth nothing to anyone but science, and science could walk right up to the cliff and take all it wanted, above the tide line, in daylight.

Elias got his names in the record. All three of the 2013 dead did, read aloud with their roles — expedition leader, field geologist, camp cook, a twenty-four-year-old cook named Dennis Okafor whom nobody had mentioned until his sister stood up in the gallery and said thank you in a voice that broke the room open. The empty sleeping bag was entered into evidence with a photograph, and beside it, Sif's ledger, catalogued as Exhibit Fourteen, and Ingrid held it on her lap through the whole reading like it was warm.

Then the license.

"Suspension of twelve months," Adeyemi read, "retroactive to the date of voluntary surrender, with mandatory safety review prior to reinstatement. The tribunal further notes for the record that the respondent's voluntary surrender preceded any demand by this body, and that this candor has been weighed." She paused. "Ms. Pemberton, you'll have your wings back in the spring. Try to deserve them slightly less dramatically."

There was laughter in the gallery — real laughter, the exhausted kind — and Yara stood, and nodded once, and I watched her take the suspension paper in both hands like a receipt for something she'd actually bought.

Outside on the courthouse steps, the light was doing that northern autumn thing where it comes in sideways and gilds everything whether it deserves it or not. Ingrid was waiting with the ledger boxed for archive, and a reporter from the coast was asking Yara questions she answered in full sentences, on camera, using her whole name — Yara Pemberton, Kestrel Bay — like a woman reading her own address off an envelope and finding she liked how it sounded.

"So that's it," I said, coming down the steps. "Truth's out. Land's protected. Reeve's singing. You're grounded till spring."

"That's it." She squinted into the low sun. "Cheapest price I've ever paid for anything that mattered."

"It cost you a year of flying."

"It cost me a year of flying *away*." She turned to look at me, and there was something in her face I'd been watching assemble itself since the frozen lake — piece by piece, like a shelter built against weather. "Teo. There's a Cessna sitting on the Kestrel Bay strip that needs an engine overhaul, a lodge that needs a roof finished before snow, and a nineteen-year-old who thinks stall recovery is a suggestion. I've got a year and nowhere I need to be."

"Where will you—"

"Kestrel Bay," she said, like the answer was obvious, like it had been obvious for months and she was only now permitting herself to say it out loud. "I'm stopping there."

Behind us, Ingrid laughed at something on her phone and held it up — a photo from the coast station, already circulating: the cave entrance above the tide line, and someone — the radio operators, of course it was the radio operators — had painted a small white marker beside it, official-looking, with fresh lettering that read, in careful block capitals:

**BRANDT EXPEDITION MEMORIAL SITE — HISTORIC — VISITORS WELCOME**

Visitors welcome. The legend that charged admission for eleven years, retired. Yara looked at the photo for a long moment, and then she smiled — not the armored half-smile I'd catalogued across a thousand miles of bad weather, but the whole thing, unguarded, and said:

"Well. Someone finally paid the admission honestly."

Snow was forecast for the passes by the weekend. None of us cared.
