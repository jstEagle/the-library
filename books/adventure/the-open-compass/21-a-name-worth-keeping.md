## 21. A Name Worth Keeping

The first week of my grounding, I woke at 5:40 every morning out of pure habit, lay in the dark listening for wind I couldn't use, and hated everyone in Kestrel Bay on principle.

By the second week, I'd stopped hating them and started fixing things.

It wasn't a decision so much as a surrender. The lodge Sif had left to her grandniece — and which her grandniece had left, in effect, to me — had a roof that leaked over the kitchen in three places, a woodstove that smoked like a signal fire when the draft was wrong, and a porch step that had been threatening to collapse since sometime during the previous administration. There was no plane to preflight. There was only this. So I climbed up with shingles and roofing tar while the bay lay flat as poured glass below me, and I discovered something I should have learned years ago: work you can see the end of is its own kind of mercy.

Flying had never ended. Every landing was just the pause between departures. But a roof — a roof gets finished. You stand back at the end of the day and look at it, and it stays done.

I didn't know what to do with that feeling. I kept checking it for rot.

* * *

Astrid Halvorsen was fourteen years old and had her great-aunt's eyes — pale grey, unimpressed, set in a face that hadn't yet decided whether to be kind or formidable and was leaning hard toward both. She showed up at the lodge on a Tuesday morning in October with a notebook, a pencil worn down to a stub, and the expression of someone who has been promised something and intends to collect.

"Sif said you'd teach me weather."

"Sif said a lot of things." I was holding a two-by-four and a spirit level. "Sif's not here."

"She knew you'd say that." Astrid dropped her pack on the porch — the new one, the one that no longer threatened anyone's ankles — and sat down on the rail like she owned it, which, technically, she did. "She wrote it in the ledger. Page forty-one. 'When Yara says no, bring the notebook anyway.'"

I looked at the girl for a long moment. Somewhere out over the water, a line of cloud was building against the far hills, flat-based and slow, the kind of formation that meant nothing before noon and everything after.

"Barometer," I said finally. "You check the barometer first. Always. Before you look at the sky, before you listen to your gut, before you do anything else. The sky lies. Glass doesn't."

Astrid already had the notebook open. "Falling or rising?"

"Falling fast means wind. Falling slow and steady means a long blow. Rising after a fall—" I pointed my chin at the southern horizon, where the light had gone that particular bruised yellow I'd spent seven years reading from two thousand feet. "Rising after a fall, with a wind backing from southwest to south, means the front's through and you've got maybe six hours of clean sky. You plan around those hours. Everything else is waiting."

"Six hours," she repeated, writing. Then: "How do you know it's six?"

"Because I've been wrong about it four hundred times and right about it four thousand, and the difference between those numbers is the whole job."

She considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. "Sif could tell by the smell off the water."

"Sif was better than me," I said, and found, to my surprise, that it cost nothing to admit. It might even have been the first true thing I'd said out loud all month. "But she started with the barometer too. Everybody does. That's why we're starting there."

We worked until the front came through, exactly six hours later, and then we sat inside with cocoa while the rain hammered the new shingles and neither of us moved an inch to check them, because we both already knew they'd hold.

That was the day I stopped counting how long my grounding had left.

* * *

Word got around, the way everything got around in Kestrel Bay, which was to say faster than any aircraft I'd ever flown. By November I was the person who fixed things. Not officially — nobody posted anything, nobody made announcements — but the lodge acquired a coffee pot that was always on, and people began appearing in ones and twos with things that needed looking at.

Marta Osei brought a chainsaw that wouldn't start and stayed two hours talking about her son in Whitehorse. Old Pete Kowalczyk came for help reading a letter from his sister down south and stayed for supper, and then came back the next week without a letter at all, just to argue about hockey. The Hendersons' boy broke his wrist sledding behind the co-op and I drove him to the clinic at Forty Mile because their truck was buried to the axles, and when I got back there was a cooler of fish on my porch and no note, because notes weren't how it was done here.

I kept waiting for the itch. That was the honest truth of it — I kept waiting for the old restlessness to come up my throat like floodwater, the need to be gone, wheels up, somewhere else, anywhere else. Seven years I'd built my whole life around outrunning that feeling, or feeding it, depending on the week. And now I was grounded, legally forbidden from leaving, stuck in a town small enough that everyone knew my business, and the feeling didn't come.

What came instead was stranger. People used my name.

Not "the pilot." Not "that woman who keeps to herself." Yara. They said it across the counter at the co-op, said it on the radio band when they checked in for the evening net, said it when Astrid arrived on Saturdays with her notebook and her aunt's stubbornness. Small thing. Should have been a small thing. But I'd spent so long being nobody-in-particular that hearing my own name spoken casually, warmly, like it belonged to someone who lived here — it landed in my chest every time like a fresh coat of paint on an old wound.

Teodor called every Sunday from the university, eight o'clock sharp, his voice arriving slightly compressed and always too loud, the way long-distance voices were.

"The core samples cleared peer review," he told me in December. "Both of them. The strike is real, Yara. It's real, and it's going to be protected land by spring if the ministry does what the inquiry recommended."

"They'll drag their feet."

"They'll drag their feet, and we'll let them, and then it'll happen anyway. That's how institutions work. Slowly, and then all at once, and then slowly again." A pause, the sound of him adjusting something on his desk. "How's the roof?"

"Holds rain, holds snow, holds a fourteen-year-old who thinks she knows more than I do."

"She does know more than you do about some things. She knows more than you did at fourteen, certainly." He was smiling; I could hear it. "Are you sleeping?"

"Most nights."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I'm offering at this price."

He laughed, and I hung up on him gently, and stood in the kitchen afterward with the receiver still warm in my hand, looking out the window at the harbor ice forming in long white feathers along the shore, and thought: most nights. It was true. Most nights I slept. The other nights I dreamed about fire, but the dreams had changed — the settlement burning was still there, still mine to carry, but lately the dream had begun including what came after. The takeoff. The coast sliding away beneath me. And then, instead of the endless grey interior, the dream kept returning here. To this window. This ice. This kitchen where the coffee pot was always on.

Even my unconscious had apparently decided to stop running.

* * *

In February, Astrid found the flight bag.

I'd kept it in the back of the hall closet, packed the way I'd packed it for seven years — fuel receipts, spare charts, a change of clothes rolled tight, the emergency kit, the little tin of things I'd told myself I'd sort out someday. Grounding or no grounding, I'd never unpacked it. Unpacking felt like something you did when you'd decided. I hadn't decided anything. I'd just stopped leaving.

She pulled it out while hunting for the good flashlight, and she didn't open it — she was Sif's blood, after all, and Sif had raised her to respect other people's gear — but she carried it out to the kitchen and set it on the table between us like evidence.

"You haven't touched this since I've known you."

"No."

"When does your license come back?"

"August. Fourteen months total, less if the board shows mercy, which they won't, because I asked them not to."

"Why would you ask them not to?" Genuine bafflement. Fourteen years old, and the idea of volunteering for punishment was foreign country to her.

"Because I broke rules that mattered, and the rules mattered, and if I get my license back early just because I feel bad, then the rules were never real." I turned my mug slowly on the table. "Also because part of me wanted them to take it. Taking it meant I didn't have to choose."

"Choose what?"

Whether to leave again. Whether the next storm season would find me here or gone. Whether belonging was something I could actually survive, given everything I knew about what happened to places I stayed. I didn't say any of that. Instead I unbuckled the flight bag, opened it, and took out the charts — seven years of them, folded soft as cloth, marked in my own hand with the routes I'd flown and the places I'd refused to land.

Astrid spread them across the kitchen table, all of them, edge to edge, and studied the empty spaces the way her great-aunt must once have studied tide tables.

"There's a hole here," she said, tapping the coast north of the cave. "You never flew this stretch."

"I flew it once."

"And then?"

"And then I helped make sure nobody else would have to find what we found." I looked at the blank space on the chart — the space where, someday, someone would print the words PROTECTED AREA, HALVORSEN RIDGE MINERAL SITE, CLOSED TO CLAIM STAKING, and children would learn about it in school without ever knowing what it cost. "There'll be a survey flight in the spring. Official. Mapping the boundary. They'll want a local pilot who knows the weather."

Astrid's head came up. Her eyes were absolutely Sif's.

"You're going to apply."

"I'm going to apply."

"For August. When you get your license back."

"When I get my license back."

She grinned — quick, fierce, gone in a second, replaced by the gravity of a professional — and pulled her notebook toward her. "Then I've got five months to learn everything you know, so that when you're flying surveys, somebody competent is reading the glass here."

"Astrid—"

"Page forty-one," she said. "'When Yara says no, bring the notebook anyway.'"

So we studied. Five months of barometers and cloud bases and the smell off the water, of her learning the six-hour rule and me learning, slowly, that teaching something you love doesn't dilute it — it multiplies it. There was more weather in the world than one pilot could ever fly through. I'd known that abstractly for twenty years. I only understood it standing next to a fourteen-year-old who read the southern horizon with her great-aunt's squint and got it right, got it *right*, calling the break in the March storms a full hour before my instruments agreed.

When the front passed and the sun came out over the bay, she looked at me, and I looked at her, and neither of us said anything, because there was nothing to add. The sky had spoken. We'd both heard it. That was the whole religion, and she was already ordained.

* * *

On the last night of April, Teodor flew in on the mail plane — his first visit since the inquiry, since the hearings, since the world outside had finished chewing us up and spitting out its verdicts. He came up the walk carrying a bottle of terrible wine and a document folder, and he stopped halfway across the yard and just looked at the lodge for a moment. New roof. Repaired porch. Wood stacked to the eaves. Smoke rising straight up in the still evening air.

"You live here," he said. Not a question. An observation, delivered with the wonder of a man confirming a hypothesis eleven years in the making.

"I reside here," I corrected. "Living is still under review."

He laughed and kissed my cheek, and we drank the terrible wine on the porch while the light went gold and then rose-colored over the water, and he talked about the protected land designation moving through committee, and the memorial the university wanted to build at the ridge for the three who died in 2013, and Sif's name going onto the dedication plaque beside her expedition colleagues because — as Teodor put it — history owed her that much and she wasn't alive to refuse it.

At the bottom of the folder, under the official correspondence, was a single sheet on university letterhead. Survey contract. Boundary mapping, Halvorsen Ridge site. Start date: mid-August. Pilot requirements: current license, northern experience, familiarity with coastal weather patterns.

The signature line was blank.

"I told them I knew a pilot," Teodor said. "They said bring references. I gave them the inquiry transcript, the testimony, the whole record — the good and the ugly. They read all of it." He slid the sheet across the table. "They want you, Yara. All of you. The compromises and the corrections. Apparently that's rarer than a clean record."

I held the paper and didn't sign it. Not because I was hesitating — I'd done enough hesitating for one lifetime — but because signing it at this table, on this porch, with the smoke rising straight and the harbor ice long gone and Astrid's notebook sitting on the kitchen counter inside, felt like the kind of act you did slowly, so you'd remember it.

Seven years ago I'd made a rule: land, unload, leave before anyone learns your name. I'd broken it a piece at a time — the supply runs that ran long, the suppers I stayed for, the name spoken across counters until it wore smooth as river stone. And the last people I'd stayed for had died, and I'd believed for seven years that the lesson was *don't stay*. That staying was the danger. That the compass needle pointed away for a reason.

But the fire hadn't happened because I stayed. It happened because I left — left before the worst of it, left while there was still something left to save, and told myself for years that distance was the same thing as safety. Sif had stayed her whole life. Sif had stayed through the cover-up, through the payments, through eleven years of keeping a legend alive like tending a fence around a grave. Staying hadn't killed her. Staying was the only reason any of us had come out the other side with the truth in our hands.

I picked up the pen.

"What are you going to write on the reference line?" Teodor asked. "They'll want a contact in town. Someone local. Someone who can vouch that you'll actually stay."

I thought about it. Marta would do it. Pete would do it with excessive commentary. Any of a dozen people who now said my name like it belonged here.

But there was really only one answer, and she was due on Saturday with her notebook and her stub of pencil and her great-aunt's unimpressible eyes.

"Astrid Halvorsen," I said, and signed my name underneath — full, unhurried, legible — on the line that waited for it.

Outside, the evening settled over the bay, and the compass on the shelf above the stove — the one I'd finally taken out of the flight bag in March and set where I could see it every day — sat pointing north, toward the ridge, toward the cave mouth above the tide line that men were coming in summer to mark honestly at last.

It wasn't pointing away anymore.

Neither was I.
