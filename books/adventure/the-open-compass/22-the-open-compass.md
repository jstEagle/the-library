## 22. The Open Compass

The morning her license came back, Yara Pemberton did the thing she'd sworn for seven years she'd never do.

She slept in.

Not long — old habits died hard, and the mail plane didn't care about her feelings — but she lay in the bunk above the Kestrel Bay hangar office and watched the light come up over the water, and she didn't move. Somewhere below, the lodge's generator coughed awake. Somewhere down the shore, Sif's grandniece was already up, because the girl was always already up, hauling crab pots with the grim competence of someone twice her age. Somewhere out on the strip, the Otter sat fueled and waiting, and for the first time in eleven months, Yara was legally allowed to fly it.

The letter had come Tuesday. Transport Canada, formal letterhead, language so dry it could start fires: *Following the completion of the inquiry and consideration of mitigating circumstances, the Airline Transport Pilot Licence of Y. Pemberton is hereby reinstated, subject to standard recency requirements.* Eleven months of grounding, of testifying, of sitting in a rented room in Whitehorse reading her own compromises back to herself in deposition transcripts. Eleven months of being known.

She'd thought it would feel like a cage opening. It felt more like a door she'd forgotten she'd nailed shut, swinging loose on its hinges, creaking.

She got up. She made coffee in the dented pot. She drank it standing at the window, watching the tide go out.

* * *

The medicine run was the same as it had always been, which was the whole point of it. Insulin for Halvorsen Point, antibiotics for the mission at Two Rivers, a crate of dental supplies that had been promised to the Koyukuk station since spring. She flew the route she'd flown a hundred times, the one she'd once flown like a thief crossing her own tracks — land, unload, wheels up before anyone could learn her name.

At Halvorsen Point, she landed.

And she stayed.

It wasn't a decision so much as a failure to leave. Edie Marsh flagged her down before she could climb back in, holding a pot of caribou stew like a warrant. "You'll eat," Edie said, in the tone of a woman who had buried two husbands and did not intend to bury a pilot. "You're too thin. You fly like a person who's too thin."

"I fly like a person with a schedule."

"Your schedule can rot." Edie set the pot down on the table in the station house and pointed at a chair, and Yara, who had once stared down a whiteout over the Divide and a man named Aldous Reeve at the mouth of a flooding cave, sat down in the chair.

The stew was good. The talk was better — Edie's granddaughter's school play, the new diesel tank, the slow scandal of someone's divorce in Fort McPherson. Small things. The kind of things Yara had spent seven years flying over, around, away from, because the last time she'd stayed somewhere, staying had killed people. She'd told the whole story once, on a frozen lake in the dark, to a geologist who didn't flinch, and she'd learned something in the telling: the story didn't get lighter, but she got stronger under it.

"You'll stay for the play?" Edie asked, and it wasn't really a question, and Yara heard herself say, "I'll stay for the play."

The play was forty minutes long and featured a cardboard whale. It was, without competition, the best forty minutes of theater Yara had ever seen. She clapped until her hands hurt.

* * *

Two Rivers at dusk. The mission priest, Father Anselm, met her on the beach with a lantern and a complaint about the fuel invoice, and then, grudgingly, an invitation to supper, and Yara — who would once have invented a weather cell over the Mackenzie rather than accept — said yes before the excuse could form.

They ate dried fish and bannock and canned peaches that Anselm saved for occasions he never defined. He was a quiet man, mostly, but he'd read about the inquiry in the southern papers — *PILOT GROUNDED, MINERAL GIANT EXPOSED* — and he asked about it the way he asked about everything, gently, sideways.

"You gave up your wings for a year," he said. "For the truth."

"I gave up my wings because I broke rules. The truth was just what was lying around when they fell."

He smiled at that. "And now you have them back. What will you do with them?"

Yara looked out the window at the dark water. "Fly," she said. "But differently, I think."

* * *

The Koyukuk station was the last stop, and by then it was full dark, and the strip lights — two trucks parked nose to nose with their high beams on, the way it had always been done — guided her in. And there, standing beside the trucks with his collar up against the wind and a thermos in each hand, was Teodor Vance.

"You're late," he called over the props.

"You're supposed to be in Whitehorse."

"Whitehorse is where I file reports. Reports are a form of prayer. I prefer to pray in person." He handed her a thermos. Coffee, laced with the sugar she pretended not to take. "Also, I heard your license came through. Figured you'd be flying. Figured I'd be standing somewhere you'd land."

"That's a lot of figuring for a geologist."

"We're trained in it." He looked at her across the idling engine, and there was something careful in his face, the same care he'd had in the cave, holding his mentor's field case while the water came in. "There's something I want to show you. If you've got fuel and daylight to spare."

"I've got both," Yara said, and was surprised at how easy it was to say.

* * *

They flew south along the coast at first light, low, the way she liked it, the Otter's shadow running along the water below them. The cliffs came up the way they always did — gray, indifferent, wearing their eleven thousand years like a coat they hadn't asked for.

And there, above the tide line, where the black archway had once opened only at the sea's lowest, most secret moods, there was now something else.

A marker.

Teodor had arranged it through the university, through the land-protection office, through eleven months of paperwork that he described as "the least interesting heroic act of my career." A bronze plaque, anchored into the rock above any tide the sea would reach, set where the old legend used to stand guard. Yara set the Otter down on the shelf — the shelf that was hers now, in the way a place can be yours if you've nearly died on it and then chosen to come back — and they climbed up to it together.

The plaque was plain. No drama. Just the facts, which was the entire point:

*In memory of the members of the 2013 Halvorsen Expedition, who found the truth and paid for it. And for Sif Halvorsen, who kept faith with the dead until the truth could keep faith with them. This land is protected. The cave below is marked honestly at last.*

Below the inscription, the entrance to the cave stood open to the air — no longer a secret, no longer a legend charging admission, but a site, catalogued and guarded and *named*. The university had a team coming in the summer. The mineral strike was protected land now, locked against every Reeve and every company man who'd ever looked at the north and seen only what it could be emptied of.

Yara stood there a long time.

She thought about Sif, who had burned her own cabin to the ground and handed two strangers a ledger and asked for nothing but that the truth outlive her. She thought about the empty sleeping bag, and the fresh supplies left at the cave mouth, year after year, a fence built out of kindness instead of fear. She thought about the fire she'd flown away from, and the seven years of arm's length, and the moment in the flooding dark when she'd realized that the thing she'd been running from wasn't death.

It was belonging. Belonging was the thing that could kill you. Belonging was the thing worth dying for.

"You okay?" Teodor asked.

"No," Yara said, and found she was smiling. "Yes. Both. I don't know." She put her hand flat on the bronze, cold under her palm, solid. "I used to have a rule. Land, unload, leave. Never let anyone learn your name."

"And now?"

"Now I'm teaching a twelve-year-old to read weather. And I'm fixing up a lodge. And I stayed for a cardboard whale last week, and I cried, Teo, I cried at a cardboard whale." She laughed, and it came out rough. "Now I land. And I stay. And I eat the stew. And somehow nobody's dead, and I'm not dead, and the compass—" She stopped, and shook her head at herself.

"Say it," Teodor said quietly.

"The needle doesn't only point away anymore." She looked out at the water, at the light coming up gold along the cliff face, at the cave mouth standing open and honest above the tide. "That's all. That's the whole thing. It took me thirty-eight years and a flooded cave and a burned-down cabin and the worst winter of my life, and the whole discovery is that the needle doesn't only point away."

Teodor didn't say anything for a moment. Then he reached into his coat and took out a small object, worn smooth, and pressed it into her hand. An old brass compass, its face scratched, its needle settling north with a faint, patient tremble. Sif's. Found in the ruins of the cabin, in a tin box, with a note in Sif's handwriting: *For the pilot who stayed.*

"She knew," Yara said.

"She knew," Teodor agreed. "Trappers usually do."

Yara closed her hand around it. The metal was cold and then it wasn't. The needle swung once, twice, and settled — not pointing away, not pointing anywhere she had to run to or from, but steady, at home in its housing, the way a thing is steady when it has finally stopped fighting where it is.

They stood there until the sun cleared the cliffs. Then they climbed down to the plane, and Yara ran up the engine, and the Otter lifted off the shelf and turned north, toward Kestrel Bay, toward the lodge with its loose shutter and the girl waiting to read the evening sky, toward supper she had every intention of being late for because she'd stop along the way, because stopping was allowed now, because stopping was the whole point.

Below her, the sea came in over the shelf, gentle, covering and uncovering the place where the cave breathed, keeping its own honest time.

The compass in her pocket pointed north, and home, and it was the same direction.
