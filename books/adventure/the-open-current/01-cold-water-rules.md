## 1. Cold Water Rules

The fog came off the water at dawn like a held breath, and Hazel Almeida sat in the wheelhouse of the *Marta B* watching it swallow the harbor buoy by buoy until there was nothing left of the world but his own reflection and the smell of diesel and old coffee. Forty-one minutes past six. The navy's patrol boat would make its first pass of the morning at seven, exactly as it had every morning for eleven months, and Hazel would be on station by then, riding his anchor over the wreck site with all the innocence of a man who had never wanted anything in his life.

He poured coffee into a tin cup and drank it standing up, because sitting down invited thinking, and thinking was a luxury he'd stopped affording himself around the time the salvage license came through with a red stamp on it that nobody would explain. *Conditional. Restricted waters. No dive operations without escort.* Which was the navy's way of saying: we know what's down there, and you're allowed to look at it only as long as you never find anything.

The wreck was the *Corvane*, a coastal freighter that had gone down in forty fathoms nine years ago with a cargo manifest listing machine parts, timber, and general freight, and a casualty count the newspapers had rounded down to "several." Hazel had been diving her for three years now, working the edges the patrol schedule left open—dawn passes, dusk passes, the long blind stretch between two and four in the morning when even the navy slept. He took copper pipe, brass fittings, whatever hadn't rotted. Enough to keep the *Marta B* fueled and the harbormaster's palm greased and himself fed on a diet that ran heavy on rice and light on questions.

That was the rule. His rule. Cold water rules, his old instructor used to call them: the sea doesn't care what you think, so stop thinking. Go down, do the work, come up, get paid, don't look too long at anything that glitters wrong. A man who survived by not asking lived a long time in this business. A man who asked ended up as a paragraph in an incident report, or worse, as a name on a list of people whose boats caught fire at anchor for no reason anyone could prove.

Hazel had gotten very good at not asking. It was, if he was honest, the only skill he'd ever mastered completely.

He killed the engine noise to a low idle and went forward to check the gear. Two tanks staged on the transom, regulator breathing tested against his palm, the lift bag folded neat as a folded flag. The water below was black-green and flat, holding the fog on its surface like a lid. Somewhere under that lid lay the *Corvane*, lying on her port side in the silt, her starboard rail pointing up at him like a row of broken teeth. He knew her the way he knew his own hands. He'd swum her keel to rail maybe sixty times. He knew where the copper was. He knew where the brass was. He knew which compartments had been cut open by the navy's own salvage teams in the first month after she sank—clean torch lines, methodical, everything catalogued and hauled away—and he knew which ones they'd left shut.

He'd never gone into the shut ones. That was part of the rules too. The navy sealed what it sealed for reasons that were above his pay grade by about four zeros, and a diver who pried at a sealed hatch on a restricted wreck didn't lose his license. He lost more than that. There had been another salvager working the site the first year, a quiet Dutchman named Verhoeven who'd asked one question too many at the harbormaster's office, and Verhoeven's boat had burned at anchor on a night with no wind, and Verhoeven had been listed as missing, presumed drowned, though everyone knew he hadn't been anywhere near the water.

Hazel had attended no funeral, because there wasn't one. He'd just moved his mooring fifty meters east and stopped asking.

He spat into the water, checked his watch, and began to dress. The fog was thinning from the top down; soon the patrol boat would come through it, gray and patient, and he wanted to be down before then, invisible, a man doing nothing but honest work in dishonest water.

* * *

The descent was cold enough to hurt, the way it always was this time of year, the thermocline hitting at ten meters like a door closing behind him. Below that the water went from green to slate to a darkness that his lamp carved into a small moving circle. He followed the shot line down hand over hand, equalizing, watching his gauges, feeling the familiar pressure settle onto his chest like a second set of ribs.

The *Corvane* materialized out of the dark the way she always did—not all at once but in pieces, a shadow becoming a shape becoming a ship. Her hull was furred with weed and soft coral now, nine years of the sea quietly eating her, and the silt around her was tracked with the marks of his own previous dives, a record written in mud of every place he'd been and everything he'd taken. He hung at the starboard rail for a moment, letting his breathing settle, letting the wreck become ordinary again. Just work. Just metal. Just a job.

He dropped through the cargo hatch into hold two, where the copper pipe runs lay half-buried in silt like the ribs of some drowned animal. Routine. He worked fast, gloved hands finding the cuts he'd made last week, freeing a length of pipe, bagging it. The work filled his head the way good work did, leaving no room for anything else. This was the other thing the cold water taught you: down here, there was only the next breath and the next task, and everything ashore—the rent, the license, the red stamp, the loneliness that waited for him in the evenings like a dog he hadn't asked to own—all of it stayed up there, on the far side of the surface, weightless and irrelevant.

He'd cleared four lengths of pipe and was working loose a fifth when his lamp swept across the aft bulkhead and stopped.

There was a seam in the plating that shouldn't have been there.

He knew this bulkhead. He'd looked at it sixty times. It was supposed to run unbroken from the deck stringer down into the silt, riveted plate, weed-furred, boring. But the silt had shifted—heavy seas two weeks back, the kind of swell that reached down and rearranged things—and what the movement had uncovered was a vertical line in the steel, dead straight, running floor to overhead, with a flange edge showing at the top where a plate met a frame. Not damage. Not corrosion. A joint. A manufactured, deliberate, welded-and-bolted joint, the kind of line you got when someone had built a compartment inside a compartment.

Hazel hung in the water and told himself it was nothing. A doubler plate. A repair. Ships got patched; the *Corvane* had been twenty years old when she went down; old ships carried scars.

But his lamp kept coming back to it, the way a tongue goes back to a cracked tooth, and after a while he gave up arguing and swam closer.

Up close, the seam resolved into something worse than a repair. The plate inside the joint was newer than the hull around it—different mill scale, different weathering—and bolted along its edge at intervals were fittings he recognized from a different context entirely: pressure-equalization ports. He'd seen them on decompression chambers. On submarine ballast systems. Hardware designed to let a sealed space hold air, or hold out water, or hold something in. And stamped faintly into the flange, half-eaten by corrosion but still legible under the touch of his glove, was a stencil:

**C-7 — NO ENTRY — AUTH PERS ONLY**

C-7. Compartment seven. He ran the official manifest in his head, the copy he'd paid a clerk too much money to let him photograph. Holds one through five. Forepeak. Aftpeak. Engine room. There was no compartment seven. There was no compartment six. The *Corvane*, according to every document the government had ever released about her, did not have a sealed internal compartment with pressure-rated hardware stenciled NO ENTRY.

His breathing sounded enormous in his ears. The cold had gotten into him somewhere along the way, or something else had, a chill that had nothing to do with depth. He put his glove flat against the plate. Solid. Newer welds underneath older paint that someone had matched carefully to the hull color—which meant someone had repainted this compartment *after* the ship went down, or the paint had been applied knowing what was coming. Either answer was worse than the question.

He should have turned around then. Sixty dives of discipline said turn around, bag your pipe, go up, be a man who survives by not asking. And he actually started to—had turned his body, had kicked once toward the hatch—when his lamp caught the bottom edge of the plate, where the silt had scoured out a shallow trench along the deck, and showed him the gap.

Two centimeters of gap, at the plate's lower corner, where a bolt was missing. And through the gap, faint but unmistakable in the lamp beam, came a gleam. Not rust. Not weed. Something smooth and pale and deliberate, packed inside the dark of the compartment, stacked with a precision that nature never managed.

He got his lamp close to the gap and angled it. What he saw was the corner of a case. Hard-shell transport case, military pattern, pale gray, latched, stenciled along its visible edge with characters he couldn't read from this angle and didn't need to. Because he already understood what he was looking at. He was looking at cargo that had never been on any manifest, installed inside a compartment that had never existed, in a ship that had been sunk—or had sunk—with several people aboard whom the newspapers had rounded down.

The navy hadn't cut open holds one through five because they were recovering cargo. They'd cut them open because they were searching. And they'd left C-7 sealed because they'd either never found it—

Or because they had, and decided the best place to hide a thing was inside a wreck everyone thought was empty, guarded by a red stamp and a burned boat.

Hazel's air gauge read a third remaining. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Down here the body did what it was trained to do regardless of what the mind was doing, and his body said: breathe, check your gauge, ascend slowly, stop at fifteen feet, do everything correctly, because a dead diver asks no questions and answers none either.

He backed away from the plate. Deliberately. Making himself do it. One kick, two, three, until the seam and the gap and the pale corner of the case slid back into the dark, and it was just a bulkhead again, just a wreck, just work.

He ascended slow, watching his gauge, doing his safety stop in the cold bright band near the surface while the fog thinned overhead into a colorless morning. He came up alongside the *Marta B* and climbed the ladder with the bag of copper pipe in his hand, four lengths, a day's pay, and stood dripping on the deck while the gulls screamed at him about something.

He didn't say anything. He didn't write anything in the log beyond *dive 61, 38 min, 4x Cu pipe recovered.* He stowed the gear, washed the salt off his hands, and ate rice and tinned fish at the little table in the wheelhouse while the gray shape of the patrol boat came through the last of the fog right on schedule, seven o'clock, its wake rolling the *Marta B* gently, a man on its bridge raising a hand to him in the flat courtesy of watchers everywhere.

Hazel raised his hand back. Smile optional. He'd long since stopped bothering.

* * *

The trouble with questions, he thought that evening, is that they don't drown. You can sink them forty fathoms deep, weighted with routine and fear and the memory of a Dutchman's burning boat, and you can surface yourself and eat your dinner and drink your beer and tell yourself the day is closed—but the questions come up on their own schedule, on their own shot line, breaking the surface of you at two in the morning when the harbor is quiet and the tide is turning.

He sat in the stern of the *Marta B* with a beer going warm in his hand and watched the lights of the naval yard across the water, white and orderly and awake at hours when honest installations slept. Somewhere in that yard, he supposed, were files. Manifests. The real list of what the *Corvane* carried, and why, and what the words *several* had been rounding down. Somewhere in that yard was a person who could tell him what C-7 meant, and Hazel knew with a certainty that sat in his stomach like swallowed lead that he would never ask them, because asking was how men named Verhoeven stopped existing.

So instead he did what he always did. He finished the beer. He checked the mooring lines. He wrote tomorrow's plan in his head—dive 62, work the forepeak, stay away from the aft bulkhead, stay away, stay away—and he told himself the story he'd been telling himself for three years, the one where he was just a salvager, just a man scraping a living off the bones of other people's disasters, just a survivor.

It was a good story. It had kept him alive. It had also, he realized, looking at his own reflection wavering in the black water, left him alone in a way that had started to feel less like peace and more like a sentence being served.

The harbor clock struck eleven. And across the water, at the mouth of the fuel dock, a small boat was coming toward him through the dark—a skiff with a single running light, moving with the unhurried certainty of a vessel that knew exactly where it was going. Toward him. Toward the *Marta B*.

Hazel set down the bottle and watched it come, and felt the cold-water rules shifting under him like silt in a swell, and understood, in the wordless way divers understand things, that the day he'd spent three years not asking about had just come looking for him anyway.
