## 2. The Dying Man's Ledger

The Anchor Line smelled of diesel, old varnish, and the particular despair of men who had bet their lives on water and lost the wager slowly. Hazel Almeida took his usual stool at the far end of the bar, the one with a wobble he'd learned to compensate for with his left heel, and ordered what he always ordered: whatever was cheapest and wouldn't make him blind.

Three days since the dive. Three days since he'd surfaced from the navy-sealed wreck with an empty catch bag and a head full of questions that paid nothing. The salvage broker had looked at him like he'd come back from the dead without bringing souvenirs, which was more or less true, and the settlement check—such as it was—had covered fuel, food, and exactly one night of pretending he was a man who could afford to sit in a bar instead of standing outside one.

He was on that night now. Spending it in slow sips, watching the door out of habit. A diver who worked sealed wrecks learned to watch doors. You never knew what came through them, and you never wanted to be surprised sitting down.

The man who came through at half past nine was not surprised-looking. He was dying-looking. That was different, and Hazel's eyes snagged on him the way they'd snag on anything wrong underwater—a shape where a shape shouldn't be, a shadow moving against current.

Old, but not elderly-old. Weathered-old. The kind of old that came from decades of sun and hard labor rather than years alone. He wore a canvas coat gone soft as cloth at the elbows, and he moved like a man carrying something heavy inside himself, some weight no one else could see. His face was brown as saddle leather, seamed deep, and his eyes went around the bar once—quick, professional, cataloguing exits the way Hazel catalogued depth gauges—and then fixed on Hazel with a directness that made the hair on his forearms rise.

The old man crossed the room. Didn't hesitate, didn't scan for an empty seat. Came straight down the bar like a torpedo running a bearing, and sat on the stool beside Hazel's crooked one, and said:

"You're Almeida."

Not a question. Hazel turned back to his drink. "People call me that."

"People say you're the best wreck diver on this coast." The voice was gravel dragged over gravel, and underneath it, faintly, a wet rattle that Hazel recognized because he'd heard it in his father's chest the last winter of his father's life. "People also say you ask no questions."

"Depends who's saying it."

"The harbor master. Two deckhands off the *Marianne*. A chandler who sold you lift bags last spring." The old man smiled, and it was a terrible smile, all erosion and iron. "I've been asking about you for six weeks, son. I don't do anything fast anymore."

Hazel looked at him properly then. Up close the man was worse than wrong-shaped; he was wrong-colored, a gray under the tan that no amount of sun could put there. His hands were enormous, knuckles swollen like river stones, and they rested on the bar with the stillness of hands that had learned to stop trembling through sheer discipline.

"You've got me confused with someone worth following around for six weeks."

"I don't think I have." The old man signaled the bartender with two fingers. "Whiskey. The kind you keep under the counter for people who tip." Then, to Hazel: "Elias Vann."

"Should that mean something?"

"It should mean nothing. That's the whole point of me." The whiskey came, amber in a glass that had seen better decades. Vann lifted it, didn't drink, held it like a prop in a play he'd rehearsed too many times. "I prospected. Forty years. Rivers mostly, high country, places where the maps go thin. Made money twice, lost it three times, and found one thing near the end that wasn't money at all."

Hazel drank. "And now you want to sell it."

"Now I want to give it away," Vann said, "which is worse, and harder, and why I've been drinking cheap whiskey in dockside bars looking for a man with your particular reputation." He finally drank, a small controlled swallow, and the wet rattle in his chest answered it. He set the glass down with exaggerated care, the way men did when their hands were lying to them about being steady. "You work the sealed wreck off Cape Ferren."

It wasn't a question either, and that was the second thing tonight that made Hazel's skin prickle. The navy's wreck. The one that appeared on no public manifest, that the patrol boats circled like sharks around a kill, that Hazel dove at his own risk for salvage scraps the navy pretended not to see him take.

"Everybody works that wreck."

"Nobody works that wreck. Everybody steals from it, and the navy lets them, because what's down there is worth less than the trouble of stopping petty theft." Vann turned on his stool, slow as a dredge swing, until he faced Hazel fully. "But you saw something. Three days ago. Something the manifests say isn't there."

The bar noise seemed to recede, the way sound did at forty meters, going distant and woolly. Hazel kept his face still. It was a skill older than his diving, older than his grief: show nothing, know everything, survive by not asking.

"I don't talk about work."

"I know. That's why you're the only man on this coast I can talk to." Vann leaned in, and up close his breath was sick-sweet, the smell of a body eating itself. "A compartment. Sealed. Welded shut from the inside, if your report to yourself was honest. Not on any cargo list ever filed. You touched it and you came up and you didn't tell a soul, and you've spent three days telling yourself it doesn't matter."

Hazel set his glass down. Carefully. The way Vann had set down his.

"What do you want, Vann?"

"I want to hire you. And then I want you to break your only rule." The old man's eyes were bright now, fever-bright, lit from behind by something that was either conviction or the end stage of a long illness. "One job. One expedition. One promise. And in exchange—" he reached inside the canvas coat, movements careful, deliberate, a man handling his own ribs like they might crack, "—this."

He laid it on the bar between them.

Cloth first, oilskin wrapped around something flat and stiff. His huge fingers worked the wrapping open with a tenderness that told Hazel more than any words could. Inside was a sheet of drafting linen, old, stained at the corners, hand-inked. A map. But not a chart—not water depths and hazards and the cold arithmetic of navigation. This was drawn by someone who loved what they were drawing: contour lines fine as hair, a river valley rendered in elevation shading, notes in a cramped engineer's hand along the margins. And across the middle of the valley, a single blue wash of ink, deliberate, covering everything—the whole gorge drowned under a stroke of blue that some cartographer had applied knowing exactly what it meant.

*Here is where we put the water. Here is where we put it on purpose.*

"A river valley," Hazel said. His voice came out lower than he intended.

"A drowned one. Sixty miles upstream of nowhere, behind a dam that appears on no public record, holding back a reservoir that appears on no public record, over a town that appears on no record at all." Vann tapped the blue wash with one stone-knuckled finger. "I walked into that valley when I was twenty-two years old, before the water. I stood in streets. I bought bread. And then I watched the government move every soul out of that town in eleven weeks, seal the dam, and erase the place so thoroughly that my own survey maps got confiscated at the county office." His jaw worked. "They missed one thing. They missed me."

The bartender drifted past; Vann covered the map with his palm, casual as a man resting his hand, and the moment passed.

"And what's down there now," Hazel said slowly, "under sixty years of water."

"That's the job." Vann's fever-brightness sharpened. "That's the promise I need from you."

"Promise."

"Three parts." The old man raised three swollen fingers, and Hazel noticed for the first time the tremor he'd been suppressing, visible now in the lamplight, fine as a compass needle hunting north. "One: you take an expedition there. Real one. Boat, crew, equipment—I'll fund it, there's money enough, more than your salvage pays in five years. Two: you bring everyone back alive. Every soul you take down that river comes home. That part is not negotiable and it is not metaphorical, Almeida. People die around this secret. They have before. Three—" and here his voice dropped, dropped so far that Hazel had to lean into the wet-rattle breathing to hear it, "—you tell no one what you find. No one. Not the papers, not the navy, not a woman in a bar, not your own reflection if it asks twice. What's in that valley belongs to the people it was taken from, and it stays down there or it comes up quiet, and that decision is yours after you've seen it. But the silence—that's mine to demand, and yours to swear."

Hazel sat with it. The whiskey had gone warm in his glass. Around them the bar clinked and laughed and lied to itself, and Hazel felt the peculiar vertigo of a man whose entire architecture—one rule, *ask nothing*, built over thirty years of surviving by not asking—being asked to bear weight it had never been designed for.

Because here was the thing, the thing he couldn't say aloud: he wanted it. Not the money—though the money was real, and he was tired down to the marrow of counting pennies against tide charts. He wanted the *shape* of it. A purpose with walls around it. A job that wasn't stripping copper from a grave the government pretended wasn't a grave. And underneath that, deeper than he liked to look, the sealed compartment on the navy wreck had cracked something open in him three days ago, and here was this dying stranger holding a crowbar.

"Why me?" he asked. "You've had six weeks. There are other divers."

"There are better divers," Vann agreed placidly. "There are divers with more equipment, more crew, more credentials. None of them can keep a secret, and the ones who can would sell it inside a year, because keeping a secret for money is just selling it slowly." He picked up his whiskey, and this time the tremor won, and he steadied the glass with both hands like a man praying. "You steal from the navy and feel bad about it. You saw a sealed door and your first instinct was to leave it shut. You're a man who survives by not asking questions, Almeida, and I'm offering you the one question worth asking, wrapped in enough obligation that you can't run from the answer." He drank, grimaced, set the glass down. "Also, I'm dying, and dying men get to be choosy."

"How long?"

"Doctor gave me till spring. Doctor's an optimist. I'd say eight weeks, and I intend to spend four of them watching you decide."

Hazel looked at the map. The blue wash. The fine-haired contours of a valley that officially did not exist, drawn by a hand that had loved it, drowned by a government that had feared it. And he thought about the welded compartment, the metal under his gloves, the way his own heartbeat had sounded in his ears like something knocking to be let out.

"If I say yes," Hazel said, "and we find nothing down there but mud and rust—"

"Then you bring your crew home and you keep the silence anyway, and you'll have been paid to learn that the world is sometimes exactly as boring as it looks." Vann smiled again, that terrible eroded smile. "But you and I both know it isn't. You've known it since Tuesday."

The name of the day landed like a depth charge. Hazel hadn't told anyone the date of his last dive. Hadn't logged it anywhere a civilian could read.

Vann saw it land. Nodded, unsurprised. "Six weeks, son. I told you I don't do anything fast."

He began rewrapping the map, and something in Hazel's chest clenched—an actual physical sensation, absurd, the feeling of watching a door close that he hadn't decided whether to walk through.

"Wait." The word was out before he could weigh it, and he hated how much it revealed, and Vann's eyebrows rose with the patience of a man who had baited a hook and felt the line go taut. "Leave it. Tonight. If I'm going to spend four weeks deciding, I'll decide faster with it in front of me."

For a long moment Elias Vann said nothing. The rattle in his chest rose and fell. Behind the bar, someone fed coins into a jukebox and the room filled with a song about a woman and a train, sentimental and stupid, and the old prospector looked at Hazel Almeida with an expression that was almost gentle.

"No," he said. "You don't get the map yet. You get the promise first. That's the order things happen in, and it's the only protection either of us has." He slid off the stool, slow, joints cracking like rigging in a swell, and tucked the oilskin bundle away inside the canvas coat. "Tomorrow night. Same stool. Bring me your answer, and if it's yes, bring me the names of every person you'd trust on that boat. If it's no—" he paused, hand on the bar, and for a second the fever-light guttered and Hazel saw the plain exhausted animal underneath, a man at the end of his strength leaning on furniture to stay upright, "—if it's no, I'll shake your hand and bother you never again, and one of us will die wondering."

He pushed off from the bar and made it three steps toward the door before the cough took him. It bent him double. It went on too long. When he straightened, he wiped his mouth with the back of one huge hand, and even in the dim light Hazel saw the color on it, dark as bilge water, and understood exactly what kind of dying this was and how little spring had to do with it.

Vann didn't look back. The door swung shut behind him, and the jukebox played on, and Hazel sat alone at his crooked stool with a warm drink and a heart going like a pump at the end of a long hose line.

Temptation, he thought, was supposed to feel good. This felt like a harness going on—leather and buckles and a weight already attached, and the strange thing, the thing he'd examine later at two a.m. staring at his ceiling, was that some part of him had been waiting years for someone to buckle it.

He finished the whiskey. He left money for two more he didn't drink, because the stool beside his suddenly felt like it should stay occupied.

Outside, the harbor lay black and oily under the sodium lights, and somewhere out past the breakwater the navy wreck sat on its shelf of cold sand, keeping its sealed compartment, its secret, its silence. Hazel walked home along the pilings with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, and by the time he reached his room above the chandlery he knew two things with diver's certainty, the kind of certainty you don't argue with at depth.

He was going to say yes.

And Elias Vann was going to be dead before the boat ever cleared the harbor—which meant that whatever promise Hazel swore tomorrow night, he'd be swearing it to a ghost, and ghosts, unlike clients, never renegotiated terms.

He slept badly. In the morning there would be lists to make: crew names, equipment, the cold arithmetic of what a drowned valley cost to visit. But tonight there was only the map he hadn't been given, drawn in his mind's eye now, permanent as ink: the fine contours, the blue wash, the valley full of water over a town full of secrets, and a dying man's three fingers raised in the lamplight like a man taking an oath.

*Bring them back. Tell no one.*

Hazel lay in the dark and practiced the words silently, testing their weight, and found—to his considerable alarm—that they fit his mouth like they'd been measured for it.

* * *

He was still awake at dawn when the knock came.

Two soft raps at the door of his room above the chandlery, apologetic, unhurried. Hazel was out of bed and silent, knife off the shelf, before the third could land. Nobody knocked softly at his door. Debt collectors hammered. Brokers rang. Friends didn't exist in numbers large enough to knock.

"Almeida." Vann's voice, gravel through the wood, thinner than last night, worn down to wire. "I know you're awake. Divers sleep like cats. Open the door."

Hazel opened it with the knife low against his thigh.

Elias Vann stood in the gray morning light looking like a man who had rowed across a river overnight—gray-faced, breath steaming, one hand braced on the doorframe, the other holding the oilskin bundle pressed to his chest. Behind him the street was empty, gull-haunted, washed pale with fog.

"Changed the schedule," Vann said. "Dying men are allowed." He pushed past Hazel, gently, without really pushing, the way water finds its way through a gap, and lowered himself onto the room's single chair as though the journey had cost him something he wouldn't get back. He set the bundle on his knees. "Sit down, son. We're doing this now, while I'm still me. Last night I watched you decide. Today I'm not giving you four weeks to un-decide."

Hazel stood with the knife and the fog-gray light and the old man filling his chair like a monument to stubbornness, and felt the last dry footing of his old life crumble under his boots.

"You cough blood," Hazel said. "You followed me for six weeks. You know things about me nobody should know. And you want a promise from a stranger." He sheathed the knife, slowly. "Give me one reason to believe a word of it."

Vann smiled—that eroded, iron smile—and unwrapped the map one final time, laying it across his knees in the early light, the blue wash gleaming like actual water.

"Come here," he said, "and look at what they drowned. Then tell me you need a reason."

Hazel stepped forward. Outside, the fog horn sounded, long and low, like something vast clearing its throat beneath the harbor—and Elias Vann began, in a voice fraying at every edge, to tell him about the valley.
