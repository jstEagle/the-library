## 4. Assembling the Impossible

Elias Vann had been in the ground four days when Hazel started making phone calls, and the map had been in his possession long enough to feel less like a gift than a diagnosis. He'd kept it folded inside the dive log, inside a waterproof chart tube, inside a locked drawer in the wheelhouse of the Kestrel, and still he caught himself thinking about it at odd hours — hauling lines, eating standing up over the galley sink, lying awake listening to the harbor slap against the pilings. A drowned river valley. Ninety days. A promise made to a dead man who had no right to expect it kept.

He told himself he was being methodical. He told himself a lot of things at four in the morning.

The truth was simpler: he couldn't do it alone. The valley was two hundred miles upcoast through water nobody charted honestly, and the dive at the end of it — whatever the sonar shadows on Elias's sketches meant — would be deep, cold, and long. He needed an engineer who could keep a dying boat alive and build things that didn't exist yet. He needed hands on deck that didn't shake. And he needed someone who could read the map the way Elias had drawn it, which was to say like a man who'd learned cartography from rivers instead of schools.

He needed, in other words, people. Which was the one thing Hazel Almeida had spent forty-one years learning to do without.

* * *

Tomas Reyes was living above a machine shop in Serrano Flats when Hazel found him, in a room the size of a lifeboat, with a cot, a hotplate, and a workbench buried under the carcasses of outboard motors. He was thirty-four and looked fifty, all elbows and hollows, with burn scars laddering his left forearm and a way of talking that suggested he'd rehearsed his defenses in front of a mirror and then decided against using them.

"I know who you are," he said, before Hazel finished the first sentence. He didn't get up from the workbench. He was holding a piston ring up to the bare bulb, turning it, hunting some flaw only he could see. "You're the guy who works the Corvina wreck. The one the navy pretends isn't there."

"The navy doesn't pretend. It just doesn't confirm."

"Same thing, costs less." Tomas set the ring down and finally looked at him. His eyes were the color of weak coffee and just as hard to read. "You're not here about a motor."

"No."

"You're here because somebody told you I'm the best hydraulics man on this coast, and they were right, and you think that means I'm cheap."

"It means you're available," Hazel said. "Cheap is a separate negotiation."

Something moved at the corner of Tomas's mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, the way a fish ghosts under a boat — you see the shadow and know better than to name it. "Who died?"

Hazel hadn't expected the question to land so close. He kept his face still, the way he kept his face still at forty meters with a bad regulator. "A prospector. Old man. Left me a job instead of money."

"What kind of job?"

"The kind where I tell you the details after you say yes, and you can walk away after I tell you, but not after we sail."

Tomas set down the piston ring with exaggerated care, like it was a bomb he was defusing out of politeness. "That's a terrible way to hire somebody."

"It's the only way I know."

For a long moment the only sound was the machine shop below them — someone running a lathe, the whine climbing and falling like a siren with a sore throat. Tomas rubbed his scarred forearm, a habit, Hazel guessed, not a symptom. Or maybe both.

"Three years ago I built a decompression rig for a commercial outfit in the straits," Tomas said. "Worked fine. Saved two lives, as far as I know. Then a chamber flooded during a storm transit because some genius mounted the intake below the waterline, and the company needed a name to give the insurers. Mine was the one that wasn't related to anybody." He shrugged, a gesture that cost him something. "I've got a license nobody honors and a reputation that fits in a matchbox. You want to know what I can do, look at the bench. Everything on it is broken, and everything on it will run again by Friday."

"I don't need Friday. I need ninety days, and I need the Kestrel's hydraulics, compressors, and winch to survive a dive I can't fully describe yet."

"Deep?"

"Deeper than I like."

Tomas nodded slowly, and Hazel watched him do the arithmetic every broken man does — not the money, not the risk, but the question of whether this was the job that finished him or the one that started him back. Hazel knew the arithmetic. He'd been carrying his own totals for years, in columns he never showed anyone.

"I want it in writing that I'm not the fall guy this time," Tomas said. "Whatever goes wrong down there, it goes wrong on all of us. Equal shares of the blame."

"Equal shares," Hazel agreed. "Equal shares of whatever we find, too."

"Which is what?"

"Ask me again when we're past the headlands."

Tomas exhaled through his nose. "You know, most people who hire me at least lie to me first. It's traditional." He stuck out his hand. It was small and hard as a cleat. "Ninety days. And Almeida — if this boat sinks because of something I built, I'll be the last one off. That's not heroics. That's just the order of operations."

Hazel shook the hand and felt, faintly and against his will, something that might have been the beginning of trust. He distrusted it immediately. It didn't help.

* * *

June Okafor found him, which was how Hazel would remember it afterward — not that he'd sought her out, but that she'd appeared at the Kestrel's gangway at seven in the morning, duffel over one shoulder, a paper cup of something steaming in her free hand, looking at his boat the way a woman looks at a horse she's been warned about.

"You're hiring deck crew," she said. Not a question.

"Who told you that?"

"Harbor runs on two things: diesel and gossip, and the gossip's cheaper." She came up the gangway without being invited, which Hazel noted and, against his better judgment, respected. She was maybe thirty, compact and quick, with close-cropped hair and hands that had clearly done every kind of work hands could do. "June Okafor. I ran deck on the Marisol for two seasons, before she went aground, which was not my fault, and on the Tern before that, which also was not my fault."

"Boats you've worked keep having accidents."

"Boats I've worked keep having owners," she said. "Different problem."

He should have sent her away. That was the honest accounting, and Hazel had always prided himself on honest accounting, at least the kind he did in his own head at night. A crew was a liability chain, every link a way to lose the boat or the secret or his skin, and this woman came with a story that had too many endings that weren't her fault. But she'd come up the gangway like she already belonged to the deck, and there was a steadiness in her eyes that he recognized the way you recognize weather — not the kind you want, the kind you can work with.

"References?" he said.

"I can give you three names. Two will vouch for me. The third will say I stole a toolbox, which I did, but it was my toolbox, and I was stealing it back from a man who owed me four months' wages."

"And the money he owed you?"

"Gone. Like my wages, my savings, and most of my optimism." She drank from her paper cup and made a face at it. "I'm not going to lie to you, Almeida, because you'll find out anyway and then we'll both have wasted a morning. I owe money to people who are not banks. Banks have paperwork. These people have other methods. It's not a large amount, by the standards of large amounts. It is a large amount by the standards of me."

Hazel looked at her for a long moment. The harbor worked at its morning noises behind them — gulls, halyards, the far cough of a trawler heading out. "How much is not large?"

"Enough that I'd rather be two hundred miles away working than here explaining it." She set down the cup. "You want a deckhand who tells you the truth in the first five minutes, or one who tells you in the fifth week, when it's expensive?"

He wanted neither. He wanted a crew that didn't exist, a boat that didn't leak, and a dead man's map that didn't weigh what it weighed. He hired her because the alternative was sailing shorthanded, and because when she said *explaining it*, she hadn't looked away, and because some part of him — the part he kept meaning to drown and never quite managed — believed that people who told the truth early were the ones you could trust with it late.

"Wages start today," he said. "One rule. If your creditors come looking, they come looking for you, not for this boat. You keep them off my deck."

"They won't find me on your deck," June said. "That's the whole point of two hundred miles."

Later — much later, in the fifth week, when it was expensive — Hazel would remember that he hadn't asked her the obvious question: *how much, exactly, and to whom?* He'd tell himself he'd been in a hurry. He'd know better.

* * *

Marguerite Chen was the hard one, and Hazel knew it before he walked into her office above the maritime supply house on Cordell Street, because everything he knew about her came from Elias's dive log, where the old man had written, in a hand gone shaky near the end: *For the charts, find M. Chen. She owes the water a debt. She'll know the valley.*

He didn't know what that meant. He'd hoped it would become clear. It had not.

Her office was small and ruthlessly ordered — flat files like a wall of shallow drawers, a drafting table under a north-facing window, a single framed chart on the wall that Hazel recognized, with a small cold shock, as the Corvina wreck site, annotated in pencil with corrections the official surveys had never made. She was at the drafting table when he came in, and she didn't stand. She was younger than he'd expected, mid-thirties maybe, with ink-stained fingers and the particular stillness of people who work alone with precision instruments.

"Hazel Almeida," she said. "You called about a survey commission."

"I did."

"You didn't say what for."

"I'm saying now." He laid Elias's map on her drafting table, unfolded, weighted at the corners with two of her own ink bottles and a brass letter opener she watched him handle like it was a loaded gun.

Marguerite bent over the map. Hazel had looked at it a dozen times and seen what he always saw: Elias's cramped notations, depth soundings in fathoms, a river's ghost-course traced in fading ink. What Marguerite saw, he couldn't say. But he watched her go still — not the stillness of study, the stillness of recognition — and he watched her right hand drift, almost unconsciously, toward the top corner of the sheet, where the old surveyor's grid reference sat in Elias's careful numerals.

Her fingers stopped an inch from the paper.

"Where did you get this?" she asked. Her voice had changed. Flatter. Careful.

"The man who drew it."

"His name?"

"Elias Vann. He's dead."

She looked up then, and whatever she'd been about to say — whatever had risen in her face like a fish rising to a fly — she swallowed it. Hazel watched her do it. He'd spent a career reading faces through water and through lies, and he knew the mechanics of a swallowed sentence when he saw one. She'd recognized the coordinates. Not the region — anyone who knew the coast might know the region. The coordinates. The way you recognize a face across a crowded room, the way you recognize a name you'd hoped never to hear again.

"Vann," she repeated. "I don't know it."

"His map says you will."

"My map says a lot of things." She straightened, and now she was all business, brisk and smooth, the recognition folded away somewhere behind her eyes like a document returned to a flat file. "It's an old projection. Pre-war datum, if I'm reading the grid. The river course here—" she touched the fading ink line, lightly, the way you touch something asleep "—doesn't match any modern chart, which means either the survey's wrong or the river's been changed. Rivers don't change on their own at this scale. Somebody moved it, or buried it, or drowned it."

"Can you chart it?"

"I can chart anything," Marguerite said. "The question is what you're paying and what you're not telling me."

"Same answer to both. Enough, and you'll find out aboard."

"That's twice now someone's tried to hire me that way." She said it lightly, but Hazel caught the weight under it — *someone* — and filed it where he filed things he didn't understand yet, in the growing drawer of things about this valley that didn't add up. "I'll do it. Full survey, approach routes, bottom contours where the old soundings let me project them. But I work my own hours, I touch nothing of yours without asking, and when we're done, I keep a copy of everything I draw. Those are my terms. Take them or draw your own maps."

"Done."

She shook his hand, and her grip was dry and firm, and if it held anything else — reluctance, fear, the residue of that swallowed sentence — she kept it out of her hand the way she'd kept it out of her voice. Professional. Complete. A closed drawer.

Hazel gathered the map, and as he folded it, Marguerite said, "Almeida. The grid reference on this — where Vann learned it, whoever taught him that datum — it's not a surveyor's notation. It's an institutional one. Government. Pre-war hydrographic office, the kind of numbering they used before they stopped publishing half their charts." She paused, and the pause had a hairline crack in it. "You should know what you're carrying around."

"I know what I'm carrying," Hazel said, which was a lie, and she knew it was a lie, and they both let it stand, the way you let a mooring line stand when you're not ready to cast off.

* * *

He stood on the Kestrel's aft deck that night and watched the three of them, in his mind's eye, already at their stations. Tomas at the workbench below, rebuilding the winch hydraulics with the focused fury of a man proving something to a company that would never hear it. June forward, splicing line in the floodlamp's cone, her movements quick and certain, her debts two hundred miles away and closing. Marguerite in the wheelhouse corner with the map and a sheet of drafting film, drawing a river that hadn't existed on any chart for sixty years, her pencil pausing — he noticed, he noticed everything, it was the curse of his life — every time her work brought her near those grid numbers.

Four days ago he'd been one man with a secret. Now he was four, and the math was worse, not better. Four people who could talk. Four people who could be bought, or frightened, or found. He thought of Elias coughing blood into a bar rag, trading a map for a promise, and understood, finally, what the old man had actually purchased with his last coin: not Hazel's silence. Hazel's *witness*. Someone had to go down there, and someone had to come back, and one man alone could be erased as easily as a chart notation. It took a crew to make a thing undeniable.

It was a cold kind of comfort. He took it anyway.

June finished her splice and bit the line clean and looked up, catching him watching. "We're really going?" she called across the deck.

"Day after tomorrow. Tide's right."

"And we're not saying where."

"We're not saying where."

She grinned — quick, crooked, gone — and went back to her work, and Hazel turned to the rail and looked down the harbor toward the navy yard's lights, where the Corvina lay in her sealed berth and the sealed compartment waited with whatever it waited with, and thought: *ninety days*. Eighty-six now. Enough time to get there, get down, and get back — if the weather held, if the boat held, if the four of them held.

Behind him, in the wheelhouse, Marguerite's pencil stopped again over the same coordinates, and this time she didn't start it moving for a long while.

And out on the dark water, past the breakwater, a boat with no running lights held position a half mile out, engines idling, watching the Kestrel's deck lamps burn.
