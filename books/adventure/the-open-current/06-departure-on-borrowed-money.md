## 6. Departure on Borrowed Money

The money came apart faster than June had promised herself it would.

She sat in the back office of Ferro's Marine Supply with the ledger open in front of her and a cup of coffee gone cold at her elbow, doing arithmetic she already knew the answer to. The Kestrel needed fuel—diesel, not the cheap blend, because Tomas Reyes had looked at her engine room for exactly four minutes and then said, in the gentle voice of a man delivering a diagnosis, *this boat will get you there once, if you feed her right*. She needed tanks, regulators, a compressor that didn't sound like a dying animal. She needed decompression tables printed and laminated because Marguerite Chen had produced them from somewhere and refused to say where. And she needed all of it by Thursday, because Hazel Almeida had said Thursday like a man who did not repeat himself.

Ferro slid a clipboard across the desk without being asked. He was seventy, built like a mooring bollard, and he had known June's mother before June existed.

"Sign there," he said. "And there. That second one's the one that matters."

June read both pages anyway. Her mother had taught her that much before the rest of her lessons went sideways: read the paper that matters twice. It was a lease-to-purchase on equipment, interest calculated weekly, secured against the Kestrel herself—which meant Ferro would own the boat's bones if June missed two payments. She thought about the man in the gray suit who had found her outside the labor hall three months ago, the one with the soft voice and the ledger of her debts, who had told her, smiling, that money was just another kind of rope, and everyone held some of someone's.

"Problem?" Ferro asked.

"No," she said, and signed. "You'll have your first payment inside thirty days."

"Thirty-five," Ferro said. "I'm giving you five days of grace because I liked your mother. Don't tell anyone I did that."

* * *

Loading started at dawn on Wednesday, and the harbor fog was thick enough to drink.

June worked the dock while Hazel directed from the Kestrel's deck, and she watched him the way she'd learned to watch weather—peripheral, constant, looking for the change. He moved like a man conserving himself, hands sure, voice low, correcting Tomas's rigging knots without comment and letting Marguerite's crates be stowed exactly where she'd chalked their outlines on the deck plates. He hadn't slept, she'd have bet the boat on it. There was a particular stillness to him that wasn't calm; it was the stillness of a man holding his breath below a hull, listening for what was above.

Tomas arrived with a borrowed welding rig strapped to a handcart and an apology he didn't finish saying out loud. "It leaks a little at the fitting," he said instead, patting the machine. "I fixed it. Mostly. I'll fix it again if it needs fixing."

"You fixed my bilge pump too?" June asked.

"I improved your bilge pump." He grinned, and for a moment he looked twenty years old instead of however old he actually was under the exhaustion. "Your bilge pump was a crime against fluid dynamics. I want that noted somewhere official."

"Noted," Marguerite said, coming up the gangway with a hard case under each arm and a canvas map tube slung across her back like a rifle. She set the cases down with the care of a woman handling other people's teeth. "Also noted: we are departing with no filed float plan, no destination declared, and a fuel purchase pattern that any competent harbormaster could read like a headline. I've done what I can with the paperwork. What I can't do is make us invisible."

"We're not trying to be invisible," Hazel said. "We're trying to be boring."

"There's a difference?"

"Invisible gets noticed. Boring gets ignored." He looked at each of them in turn, and June felt the look land on her like a hand on the shoulder—not heavy, but present. "That's most of what I need to say. But I'll say the rest anyway, so nobody can claim they weren't told."

He waited until they were all aboard, until the gangway was up and the fog pressed close around the hull, and then he laid it out flat, no drama in it, which somehow made it worse.

"Rule one: the radio stays quiet about where we're going. Weather reports only, plain language, no coordinates, no place names, nothing that sounds like anything. If someone hails us, we're doing a salvage survey on the coastal wrecks, standard concession work, and we're happy to sell them our spare coffee. Rule two: nobody promises anybody anything beyond coming home. Not to each other, not to yourselves. We go, we look, we come back. Whatever else happens is extra, and extras kill people who planned for them." He paused. "Rule three: if anyone wants off this boat, today is free. No questions, no debt between us. After we clear the headland, leaving costs something."

Silence. The foghorn out at the point groaned like something waking up.

"That's it?" Tomas said. "No speech? No *we sail at dawn for glory*?"

"The glory's underwater," Hazel said. "Glory doesn't keep well. Come home first, then talk."

June laughed before she could stop herself—a short, surprised bark—and Hazel almost smiled, which from him was practically a parade. But then his eyes went past her shoulder, out toward the harbor mouth, and stayed there, and the smile finished dying before it was born.

She turned. A navy patrol boat was coming up the channel at an easy, unhurried pace, white hull clean as a tooth, wake spreading wide behind it. It wasn't heading for them. It wasn't even slowing. It simply passed along the breakwater the way patrol boats passed every day, except that its bridge windows caught the morning light and threw it directly across the Kestrel's deck, across all four of them standing there, a slow bar of light like a searchlight doing its rounds.

Nobody spoke until it was gone.

"Boring," Marguerite said finally, into the quiet. "Right."

* * *

They took on fuel at the municipal pier because it was cheaper, and that was where the day began costing more than June had budgeted.

The attendant was a kid with sunburned ears who filled the tanks slow and talked slower, and June stood on the deck counting hose-gallons in her head, converting each one into weeks of Ferro's grace period, when a launch came around the breakwater flying no flag at all. It was a good launch—too good, twin engines, polished rails—and it idled down alongside with the smooth confidence of money that has never been refused.

Two men. One stayed at the wheel. The other stepped onto the pier, and June knew him before he smiled: gray suit, soft voice, the ledger-keeper from outside the labor hall. Mr. Ansel, he'd called himself, though she'd have bet the boat that name was rented.

"Miss Okafor." He tipped his head at the Kestrel like a man appraising livestock. "So this is where the payments went. I confess I pictured something smaller."

"My payments are current," June said.

"They are. Impeccably." He examined his fingernails. "Current is such a fragile word, though. Current means you haven't missed one yet. It says nothing about next month, or the month after, or what happens to this charming vessel if you do." He looked up, and his eyes were friendly the way a hook is friendly to a worm. "I'm not here to collect, Miss Okafor. I'm here to invest. People I represent find maritime ventures interesting. Very interesting. They'd love to see this one succeed."

"We don't take investors."

"Of course not. You take loans, though. Loans are honest work." He reached into his jacket, and June's whole body tightened—but he only produced a card, cream-colored, blank except for a phone number. "No obligations. Just a number. If the venture meets difficulties—weather, machinery, creditors—you call, and the difficulties become negotiable. That's all. A lifeline is only a rope until you need it. Then it's a gift."

He set the card on the deck rail under a washer so the wind couldn't take it, wished her fair seas in a voice like warm oil, and the launch pulled away, twin wakes braiding together out toward the channel.

June stood looking at the card for a long time. Then she picked it up, walked to the stern, and dropped it over the side, and watched the water take it. It floated longer than she expected. Everything dangerous floats longer than you expect.

When she turned around, Hazel was at the wheelhouse door, watching her. He didn't ask. That was the thing about him she was starting to trust: he never asked for what people weren't ready to give.

"Fuel's topped," she said. "We can cast off whenever you're ready."

"Ready now," he said, and rang the engine order bell himself, one soft clang, like a church deciding something.

* * *

The Kestrel cleared the breakwater at 0640, and the sea opened out ahead of them gray-green and long-breathed, swells rolling in from nowhere in particular, the coast beginning its slow slide astern.

June had the first watch. She took the wheel while the others got the deck squared away, and she let herself feel it—the fragile, ridiculous thrill of it, the boat alive under her hands, borrowed and patched and mortgaged to a man who liked her dead mother, carrying three strangers and a secret none of them would name aloud. The compass needle held steady on the heading Hazel had given her, written on a scrap of paper and burned in the galley stove afterward, and she steered by numbers alone, numbers that meant nothing to her and everything to somebody.

Tomas came up after an hour with coffee that tasted like engine coolant and stood beside her at the wheel, hip against the housing, watching the water.

"She's running clean," he said. "Better than she deserves."

"Everything on this boat runs better than it deserves."

"That's called hope," Tomas said. "It's load-bearing. Don't remove it."

Marguerite appeared at noon with charts she wouldn't open on deck, and Hazel came off watch at fourteen hundred and stood at the rail a while, looking back the way they'd come. June watched him do it. The coastline was a thin blue pencil line now, and somewhere behind it were the wrecks he'd worked alone for years, and whatever compartment he'd found in one of them that he never talked about, and the bar where a dying man had bought him a drink and handed him a river valley folded in wax paper.

"You think he knew?" she asked, coming to stand beside him. "Vann. What he was handing you."

Hazel considered the horizon. "He knew it was heavy. I don't think he knew how heavy. Heavy's something you learn by carrying."

"And if it's heavier than us?"

"Then we put it down before it breaks us," he said. "That's rule two. Nobody promised anybody anything but the way home."

The wind freshened through the afternoon, quartering from the southeast, and by evening the sky over the water had gone the color of a bruise healing wrong—yellow-green at the horizon, bruised purple above, the light going strange and metallic. Tomas stuck his head out of the engine room, sniffed the air like a horse, and said, "Barometer's falling like my last paycheck. This isn't weather. This is a message."

Hazel studied the sky for a long moment, then the chart, then the sky again.

"There's an anchorage behind Serrano Point," he said. "Forty miles. We run for it now and we're tied up before the worst of it, or we stay out here and find out what the Kestrel thinks of heavy weather with a full load."

June looked at the sky, then at the card-shaped absence on her stern rail where a cream-colored rectangle had floated away that morning, and felt the first cold drop hit the deck beside her shoe.

"Run for it," she said.

Hazel rang the bell. The Kestrel leaned into the darkening water, and forty miles ahead, behind a point of land none of them had ever seen, something was waiting that none of them had planned for either.
