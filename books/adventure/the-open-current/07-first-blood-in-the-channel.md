## 7. First Blood in the Channel

Tomas Reyes had rebuilt engines that ran on prayer and spite, and he trusted both more than he trusted the Kestrel's weather deck. The barometer had been falling since noon—slow at first, the way a man descends stairs when he knows what's waiting at the bottom—and by three o'clock it was dropping like something pushed.

He stood in the engine room with his palm flat against the main housing, feeling the vibration come up through the steel and into the bones of his hand. Every engine talked if you listened long enough. This one was saying: *I am forty years old and I have been run hard by men who did not love me.* Fair enough. Tomas had been that man once, back when he'd had an engineering firm instead of a duffel bag and a reputation for leaving jobs half-finished because the money dried up. He knew how machines felt about the people who used them.

The knock on the engine room hatch was June's—two raps, pause, one more, the rhythm she'd invented on day two because she said shouting down a ladder made her feel like a foreman.

"Barometer's in freefall," she called down. "Hazel wants to know if we can outrun it."

"Outrun it to where?" Tomas shouted back, already reaching for the rag to wipe his hands. That was the thing nobody told you about running from weather at sea: there was no *away*. There was only *toward*, and you hoped the toward was better than the here.

* * *

On the aft deck, the sky had gone the color of old pewter, bruised purple along the western horizon where the front was stacking itself up like a wall being built by something with a grudge. Hazel stood at the wheel with the chart spread on the box beside him, weighted down with a wrench and a coffee mug, and Marguerite was bent over it with her finger tracing coastlines.

"There." She tapped a notch in the land. "Serrano Anchorage. It's a working harbor—fuel barge, a cannery, breakwater all the way around. Ugly, but it'll hold."

"How far?"

"Four hours at our best speed. The front makes landfall in five, maybe less."

Hazel looked up at the sky the way Tomas had seen him look into black water—measuring, weighing, deciding what he could live with. "Tomas. Can she take four hours of building sea?"

"She can take four hours of anything," Tomas said. "It's the fifth hour I'd worry about."

"Then we make it in four." Hazel spun the wheel and the Kestrel leaned into her turn with the reluctant grace of a large animal changing its mind. "June, secure everything that isn't welded down. Marguerite, keep watching that front. If it speeds up, tell me immediately and not politely."

June was already moving, lashing crates, double-clamping the dive gear lockers, tying off the spare tanks with the quick, economical knots of someone who'd grown up around boats that sank when you got lazy. Tomas went below and gave the engine what it actually wanted, which wasn't admiration but clean fuel and cool oil, and by the time the first real gusts came offloading out of the west, he had her turning over as sweet as she was going to get.

The channel roughened in stages, the way bad things usually do. First just a chop that made the coffee walk out of cups. Then swells with shoulders, lifting the bow and dropping it into troughs that slammed like doors. By the second hour the rain arrived—not falling so much as arriving horizontally, driven so hard it sounded like gravel thrown against the wheelhouse windows.

Tomas stayed below through most of it, one hand on the housing, listening. At some point June came down the ladder, soaked through, hair plastered to her skull, grinning like this was the best day of her life.

"You look terrible," Tomas said.

"You should see the other guys," she said. "Hazel says ninety minutes to the anchorage. He also says—" she paused, and the grin flickered into something more careful, "—he says there's a light showing off the starboard bow that isn't on any chart Marguerite has."

"What kind of light?"

"The kind that moves. The kind that's keeping pace with us."

Tomas wiped his hands again, though they were already clean. It was a habit from another life, from boardrooms and site inspections, from a version of himself that had believed problems could be delegated. "Radar?"

"Hazel doesn't trust the radar. Says it's painting ghosts. Or maybe not ghosts."

They looked at each other in the engine room's orange light, and Tomas understood that they were both thinking the same thought and neither wanted to say it aloud: *nobody follows a salvage boat through a storm unless they want to know where it goes.*

* * *

Serrano Anchorage received them the way harbors receive anything in weather—with indifference. The breakwater took the worst of the sea and turned it into confused, steep chop inside the mooring field, and Hazel brought the Kestrel in on instruments and instinct, threading between fishing boats riding their anchors with their noses into the wind, and dropped the hook in eight meters of gray-green water with a single smooth motion that made even June exhale.

Then the wind arrived for real.

It came off the land in screaming gusts, bending the rain flat, and the whole harbor became a drumming, whistling world where you had to shout two feet from someone's face. They anchored watch in shifts. Tomas took first, huddled in the wheelhouse with the heater that worked only when it felt like it, watching the anchor light of the nearest fishing boat swing and steady, swing and steady, and telling himself the knot in his stomach was just the storm.

It wasn't the storm. It was the light. Because at some point in the third hour of his watch, squinting through salt-streaked glass at the harbor mouth, he saw it again—a white running light, low on the water, moving without any visible boat behind it. Then the rain shifted and he saw the boat: a rigid-hull inflatable, navy gray, riding the chop like it had been designed by someone who hated the ocean personally and wanted revenge. It held position near the breakwater gap. Not approaching. Waiting.

Tomas woke Hazel without ceremony. Hazel came up the ladder already dressed, which meant he hadn't really been sleeping either, and stood beside Tomas at the window for a long time without saying anything.

"Patrol?" Tomas asked.

"At anchor? In this?" Hazel's voice was quiet. "Patrol boats don't loiter. They're not loitering. They're making sure we don't leave before someone talks to us."

"And if we leave anyway?"

Hazel almost smiled. "In this sea, in the dark, with a forty-year-old hull? We'd be doing their work for them." He rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands. "Wake the others. If they've come all this way to be polite, the least we can do is be polite back."

* * *

They came aboard at dawn, or what passed for dawn under that sky—a thin gray brightening, the wind down to a hard sustained blow, the rain gone to drizzle. The rigid-hull put six men alongside the Kestrel's boarding gate, and the man who climbed up first did it with the unhurried competence of someone who had done it a hundred times and expected to do it a hundred more.

He was maybe fifty, square-built, with a face weathered down to its essential functions, and he wore a patrol officer's jacket with the insignia squared away to parade standard, which in itself told Tomas something: men who expected routine didn't dress like they were being photographed.

"Good morning," the officer said, and his voice carried the particular flat courtesy of officialdom everywhere. "Captain Almeida?"

"Hazel is fine."

"Hazel, then. Lieutenant Commander Duarte, Coastal Patrol. I'm afraid I'll need to board and inspect your vessel. Routine traffic verification—the storm brought a lot of unregistered movement through the channel last night, and we're checking everyone." He smiled, and it reached every part of his face except his eyes. "You understand. Paperwork keeps us all honest."

"Of course," Hazel said. "Come aboard. Mind the coaming, she leaks a little."

Duarte's men spread through the Kestrel with a thoroughness that had nothing casual in it. One went forward to the chain locker. One went below, and Tomas heard his own engine room receive the stranger's footsteps—the pause at the tool bench, the opening of the spares locker, the long silence in front of the decompression chamber frame they'd bolted to the bulkhead in port, the piece of equipment that said, louder than any manifest, *these people plan to go deep.*

Tomas stationed himself at the foot of the ladder with a wrench in his hand and an expression he hoped read as *simple man guarding simple machinery.*

"Nice setup," the inspector said, coming back down. He was younger than Duarte, with the kind of neat, closed face that made you think of filing cabinets. He looked at the chamber frame, then at Tomas, then at the chamber frame again. "Salvage work?"

"Mostly. Whatever pays."

"Mixed-gas capability." The inspector tapped the frame with one knuckle. "That's serious equipment for whatever pays."

"We bought it cheap from a defunct operation. Half the valves don't hold." Which was true, and therefore the best lie available, and Tomas watched the inspector file it away in whatever cabinet he kept such things.

Up top, Duarte sat at the wheelhouse table across from Hazel with a clipboard, and Marguerite stood by the chart table with her arms folded, wearing the expression of a woman performing patience. June leaned in the doorway of the galley, close enough to hear everything, far enough to look bored.

"Vessel registration, crew list, cargo manifest, last port, next port, purpose of voyage," Duarte recited, ticking items. Hazel fed him answers one at a time, each one true and each one incomplete: registered salvage vessel, crew of five including himself, general salvage and survey contract, departed from Meridian Harbor, destination undetermined pending survey work. All of it accurate. None of it useful.

Duarte wrote it all down in a small, precise hand. Then he turned to the final page of his folder, and Tomas, watching from the companionway, saw the moment happen—the small hitch, the fractional pause, the way Duarte's pen stopped moving for exactly one second too long.

Because the boarding authorization form had a section near the bottom. *Referred by / Coordinating authority.* And typed neatly beneath it, in letters that everyone in the wheelhouse could pretend not to read and none of them could avoid reading, was a name:

**Cdr. E. Locke, Office of Naval Intelligence — Special Inquiries.**

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere below, the engine ticked as it cooled. Marguerite's arms unfolded slowly, deliberately, the way a person sets down something heavy.

"Special Inquiries," Hazel said at last, mild as milk. "That sounds important."

"It sounds like paperwork," Duarte said, closing the folder. But his ears had gone slightly red, and Tomas understood: the lieutenant commander hadn't known whose name would be on his own forms until he'd opened the folder in front of witnesses, and he liked it no better than they did. Somebody upstairs had wired this inspection before the storm ever formed. "Routine boards get routed through various offices. Nothing for you to concern yourselves with."

"No," Hazel agreed. "Nothing at all."

Duarte stood, buttoned his jacket, and became once again entirely courteous. "Your equipment is in order. Your papers are in order. You're free to weigh anchor when the weather lifts—which won't be today, if the forecast holds, and it will." He paused at the boarding gate, one hand on the rail, and looked back at Hazel with something that might, in a different life, have been sympathy. "One piece of advice, captain, free of charge. The channel south of here closes for naval exercises tomorrow. Forty-eight hours, minimum. Charts haven't been updated; they rarely are, for that stretch. You'd save yourselves a long detour by going north when you sail."

"North," Hazel repeated.

"North," Duarte confirmed. "Lovely country up there this time of year. Very quiet." He went down the ladder, and the rigid-hull pulled away, and the six gray shapes of it shrank into the drizzle until the harbor was just a harbor again.

Nobody spoke for a full minute.

"He told us to go north," June said finally. "Why would he tell us that?"

"Because he thinks we're going south," Marguerite said quietly. Her voice had a strange, tight quality, like wire under load. "And he wants us to know they know."

Tomas looked at her. In twenty years of working around smart people, he'd developed a sense for the moment when someone's private knowledge got too heavy to carry alone, and Marguerite Chen was standing at the chart table holding a weight that had just gotten heavier, her knuckles white against the table edge, staring at the spot on the chart where the valley waited under sixty meters of drowned river.

"They're not following us," she said. "They're ahead of us. They've been ahead of us since before we left the dock." She looked up, and her eyes were frightened and furious in equal measure. "Locke didn't send a patrol boat after a rumor. He sent one after *us*."

Hazel picked up the coffee mug, found it cold, and set it down again. When he spoke, his voice was calm in the way that Tomas had learned to recognize as the calm of deep water—still on the surface, everything moving underneath.

"Then we have a decision to make," he said. "We can go north, like the nice lieutenant suggested, and spend the rest of the concession dodging exercise zones until the permit expires and Elias's map dies in a drawer. Or—" he tapped the chart, south of the closed channel, where the coastline bent inland toward a gorge that appeared on no modern survey, "—we go tonight. Before the closure starts. Through weather that no sane patrol boat will follow us into."

"That's insane," June said.

"It is," Hazel agreed. "Tomas. Can she make the passage tonight?"

Tomas looked at the chart, at the narrow water between the headlands where the tide ran like a freight train even in good conditions, and thought about the engine talking to him through the housing, saying *I am old, I am tired, I have been run hard by men who did not love me.*

"Yeah," he said. "She can make it. It's the fifth hour I'd worry about."

Hazel nodded once, and began rolling up the chart, and outside the wheelhouse windows the rain came down on Serrano Anchorage like the sea trying to wash something away—or hide it.
