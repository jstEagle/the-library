## 8. The Valley That Isn't on Charts

The river found us before we found it.

We'd been running up the coast for two days on a heading that existed nowhere but in Elias Vann's hand-drawn map and Marguerite Chen's careful silence, and on the morning of the third day the water changed color ahead of us—green going to brown going to a kind of tea-dark amber that meant silt, which meant a river mouth, which meant we were close enough that my hands started sweating inside my gloves.

"Should be a gorge," Marguerite said, standing at the rail with the map flattened against her thigh so the wind couldn't take it. She'd been holding it like that for two days, half-sheltering it, half-hiding it, as if the paper itself were something that could be stolen out of the air. "Vann drew it from memory. He said the walls came down close enough to touch from either side of the boat."

I looked at the chart plotter, then at the paper charts Tomas had spread and weighted with coffee mugs in the wheelhouse, then at the water.

There was no gorge on any of them.

There was no river, either—not past the first three miles inland. The official charts showed the coastline, showed the old fishing village of Puerto Sombra with its crumbling jetty, and then showed nothing. Blank space where the interior should have been. Not uncharted in the honest way, the way deep water is uncharted, with soundings trailing off into white nothing. This was blank in the deliberate way. The cartographers who made these charts had drawn the coast, drawn the roads that ran along it, and then simply stopped, as though the land behind the hills were none of anyone's business.

"Fifty years," Tomas said, tapping the blank space with one blunt finger. "Fifty years of surveys, satellite passes, everything. Nobody maps a whole valley by accident."

"Nobody floods one by accident either," June said from the doorway.

Nobody answered that. We'd all been thinking versions of it since the channel, since the boarding party with Locke's name on the papers, since the patrol boat had peeled away with that courteous menace that sat in my chest like swallowed ballast. But saying it out loud made it real, and there's an old rule aboard any working vessel: you don't name the thing you're afraid of until you have to, because named things grow teeth.

The Kestrel pushed on up the brown water. She was old and slow and ugly, our ship—a salvage tug somebody had once loved and nobody had loved since, with a rust scar down her port side like an old surgery—and she handled the current with the grudging competence of a mule. The banks narrowed. The jungle came down to the water in green walls hung with vines, and the air went thick and hot and full of insects, and somewhere back in those trees birds were making sounds I didn't have names for.

Elias Vann had drawn this stretch of river in pencil, in a hand that shook. I'd studied the map until I could see it with my eyes closed: the gorge, the bend, the waterfall he'd marked with two wavy lines and the word *loud* written beside it in his cramped script. A man who spent his life alone writes notes to himself like that. *Loud.* As if he needed reminding what the world could do when it wanted to.

The bend came up on schedule. The walls began to rise on both sides—limestone gone black with rain-stain, dripping, hung with ferns—and I brought us around it slow, engine at idle, and every soul aboard came out onto the deck without being called.

Then the walls closed in, exactly as Vann had drawn them, close enough to touch—

And stopped.

Not the rock. The rock kept going, rising sheer on either side, a canyon cut by ten thousand years of hard water. What stopped was the river. Or rather, what stopped was the *riverbed*. Because where Vann's map promised rushing water over white stone, where his pencil had marked the loudness of a waterfall, there was instead a lake. Still, dark, green-black, lying between the canyon walls like something poured in and left to set. No current. No sound but the drip off the limestone and the insects and our own idle thumping through the hull.

The waterfall was under us. Somewhere down there, drowned, still falling maybe, in the dark.

"Dam," Tomas said quietly. He was already at the stern rail, looking back the way we'd come, and I followed his eyes downstream to where the canyon pinched together maybe four hundred meters back and the light changed—the particular flat gray-green shimmer of water backed up against concrete. "See the color? See how the surface goes glassy right there? There's a wall across this gorge."

"There's no dam on the charts," Marguerite said. Her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was working a problem she didn't want to say out loud yet. "There's no dam in the engineering registries. I checked. Before we left, I checked every public record for hydroelectric projects, irrigation schemes, flood control—" She stopped herself. We all heard her stop herself, heard the sentence she wasn't finishing: *and I checked because I already knew something about this valley, and I lied about why.*

I let it go. Not because I wasn't curious. Because there's a time for pulling threads and a time for holding the needle steady, and we were forty miles from anywhere with a navy that had already boarded us once.

"June," I said. "Soundings."

She was already moving. We'd rigged the old fathometer back in port with parts Tomas had scrounged and soldered at three in the morning, and now June stood at the transom reading depth off a dial older than she was, calling numbers in the flat voice of someone doing arithmetic they don't like.

"Forty feet." Pause. "Sixty." The bottom dropping away as the drowned riverbed fell toward its old channel. "Ninety. Hundred and ten—" Then the needle swung, steadied, and June frowned at it. "Hundred and thirty. And it's flattening out. Bottom contour's wrong, Hazel. It's not a channel anymore. It's—" She looked up. "It's level. Like a floor."

*Like a floor.* I felt the words go through me cold, the way cold water goes through a wetsuit—slow at first, then all at once.

Because rivers don't have floors. Rivers have beds, uneven, scoured, littered with boulders and snags and the debris of a hundred floods. But a town has streets. Streets get graded. Streets get leveled before anybody builds on them.

"Tomas," I said. "Get the sonar up."

* * *

We anchored in the lee of the north wall, in water too deep for comfort, and while June held station Tomas assembled the sidescan rig on the aft deck with the focused fury of a man who'd been told his whole life he was wasting his talents and had finally found a use for them. The towfish went over the side on a hundred meters of cable, and the Kestrel crept back and forth across the drowned valley at two knots, painting the bottom in sound.

I stood at the monitor with Marguerite and watched the picture build.

Sonar images are always a little like dreams—you see shapes before you understand them, your mind reaching for names. First pass, we got the obvious: the old river channel, the rocky spine of the original gorge floor, a slide of talus off the eastern wall. Ordinary geology. My shoulders had just started to come down when the second pass crossed the center of the basin and the screen filled with straight lines.

Straight lines don't happen underwater. Not naturally. Water rounds things, buries things, knocks things down. Straight lines mean hands.

"There," Marguerite said, and her finger touched the screen, and I watched her discipline hold—the cartographer keeping her voice level even as whatever she knew pressed up against the back of her teeth.

A grid. Faint, silt-softened, but unmistakable: streets crossing at right angles, blocks between them, the long regular shadows of structures lining each one. And at the grid's heart, a larger footprint—a building bigger than the rest, walled, with an open court inside it. South of that, a circular shape with a tower shadow beside it. I'd spent twenty years swimming over wrecks, and wrecks are chaos, collapsed and scattered, iron bones strewn by whatever killed them. This wasn't chaos. This was a town, sitting intact under a hundred and thirty feet of green water, streets and all, preserved like a fly in amber.

"It never flooded," I heard myself say. "Tomas—the sediments. Look at the streets. They're clean. If the river had risen gradual, you'd have scour, you'd have debris fields, you'd have—" I gestured at the screen. "This looks like someone turned the water on slowly and evenly, like filling a tank."

"Or like the water was already coming and everyone had time to leave," Tomas said. "No cars in the streets. No boats at the docks. Look—" he traced a line, "—there should be a bridge here across the channel. There's nothing. Dismantled, or taken down before the water came."

"They knew," June said softly, from the doorway. "Whoever lived here knew it was coming."

Marguerite hadn't spoken in ten minutes. When she finally did, her voice was strange, stripped down, like a woman reading coordinates off her own death certificate. "The records office," she said, pointing at the big central building. "That's a civic structure. Municipal scale. Every planned relocation starts with the archives—they move the paperwork first, or they drown it. Either way, that building is the story."

"What story?" I asked.

She looked at me then, and I saw her make a decision, saw it cost her something, saw her decide to pay only part of the price. "Hazel, I recognized these coordinates the night you showed me the map. I've seen this valley before—in a survey index from before the war, a hydrological study that was withdrawn from circulation. Every copy pulled. I told you I didn't know why the charts were blank." Her jaw tightened. "That part was true. I don't know why. But I know that governments don't withdraw studies of places that don't matter."

The sonar pinged on, patient, indifferent, painting the drowned streets stroke by stroke while the three of us stood there in the dim wheelhouse with the truth settling over us like the silt over the rooftops below.

* * *

I went up on deck alone after that. Some things a man needs to work through with salt air instead of company.

The evening came down the canyon in stages—the light leaving the rimrock first, then the ferns, then the water, until the lake lay black and perfect between the walls and the first stars came out overhead in their hard tropical abundance. Insects droned. From the jungle, something called, long and low, and something else answered.

I thought about Elias Vann. About the way he'd gripped my wrist with that blood-flecked hand in the dockside bar and made me promise—*bring them home, tell no one*—and how I'd thought, at the time, that the promise was about treasure. Salvage men think everything is about treasure; it's how we keep from thinking about anything else. A drowned valley, a dying prospector, a ninety-day permit: I'd built myself a comfortable story out of those parts. Gold in the ruins. Relics worth selling. A last big score to buy a quiet old age.

But there was no gold on the sonar. There were streets. There was a church with its bell still hanging in its tower—I could see the shadow of it, a thin bright line inside the round dark mouth of the belfry, and something about that line hurt to look at. Bells are for calling people. Somebody had cast that bell to gather a congregation, to mark weddings, to toll funerals, and it hung down there now in the cold and the dark, calling no one, into water.

You don't flood a town by accident. You can flood a house by accident, a field, a road—a river will take anything it's given. But a town is a decision. Every street on that sonar image had been surveyed, platted, approved, taxed. Someone had signed documents making that place real, and then someone else had signed different documents making it disappear, and the second set of signatures had won, because the second set had the navy behind it, and the navy had been sniffing at our wake since the channel.

I stood at the rail a long time, doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the bar.

Elias Vann hadn't sent me after treasure. He'd sent me after evidence. He'd known exactly what was down there—that's why the map, why the promise, why *tell no one what it finds*. He'd spent his life in these hills, walking ridgelines above a valley that officially didn't exist, watching the water rise over rooftops that officially had never been built, and he'd carried it alone until his lungs gave out, and then he'd walked into a dockside bar and bet everything on a stranger who was good at not asking questions.

Well. I was asking them now.

Behind me the wheelhouse light burned yellow, and inside it I could see them—Tomas hunched over the sonar logging positions, June checking fuel numbers twice because checking once scared her, Marguerite drawing the drowned streets freehand from the screen, fast and precise, her pen moving like she was afraid the image would be taken away before she finished. My crew. Three people who'd climbed aboard a borrowed-money expedition for their own desperate reasons, following a dead man's pencil marks into a hole the government had spent fifty years pretending wasn't there.

Bring them home, Elias had said. That half of the promise I could keep. That half was mine to keep, come hell, high water, or Commander Locke.

But the other half—*tell no one*—had been made by a man who thought he was handing me a secret. He hadn't known he was handing me a witness statement.

The church bell hung silent in the dark below our keel, and above it the stars turned, indifferent, and I understood with a diver's cold clarity that the easy part of this expedition was over. Whatever we pulled out of that water next, we would not be able to put it back.

In the wheelhouse, Marguerite suddenly went still over her drawing. Then she said, loudly enough to carry across the deck, "Hazel. Come look at this."

I went. On the sonar screen, at the northern edge of the town grid, past the last row of houses where the streets gave up and the old riverbed resumed, the towfish had painted a new shape—one we hadn't seen on the earlier passes, because the earlier passes hadn't reached this far upstream.

It was long, and low, and rectangular, and it lay across the drowned channel like a lid on a box.

And stamped faintly in its echo, where the silt had been scoured away by some lingering thread of current, was a marking. Letters, cut deep enough to survive fifty years underwater, arranged in a single line that even the coarse resolution of a salvage sonar could read:

**PROPERTY OF THE DEPARTMENT OF WATER AND POWER — AUTHORIZED ACCESS ONLY.**

"That's not a dam face," Tomas said quietly. "Dams curve. Arches hold the load. That's flat. That's a plug." He looked up at me, and in the green glow of the sonar screen his face looked ten years older than it had at sunrise. "Hazel, that's not a structure built to hold water back. That's a structure built to seal something shut."

Outside, the canyon had gone fully dark, and the lake lay over the town like a held breath, and somewhere far downstream—too far to see, close enough to feel—a government that had already boarded us once was waiting to learn what we'd found.
