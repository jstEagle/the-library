## 9. Descent to the Rooftops

The morning of the dive came in flat and gray, the kind of light that made the valley look like a photograph of itself. No wind. The water below the Kestrel lay so still it held the reflection of the cliffs upside down, a second gorge hanging in the air.

Hazel stood at the rail with coffee he wasn't drinking and watched the sonar screen Tomas had rigged to the laptop on the deck table. The image refreshed every four seconds, and every four seconds the drowned town assembled itself again out of green static: street grids, rooflines, a long rectangular footprint that could only be a church.

"Forty-one meters at the bell tower," Tomas said. He'd been up since five, checking his rig three times, and it showed in the careful way he moved, like a man carrying something full to the brim. "Forty-six at the deepest point we'll cross, if we go down Main Street like you want."

"Fifty-two," Hazel said.

Tomas looked up. "You've been down there already?"

"I dropped a weighted line off the stern last night." Hazel hadn't told anyone. It was an old habit from salvage work—never trust a chart, not even your own. "Fifty-two meters where the river channel cuts through. The town's built on the shelf above it."

"That's past recreational limits by a country mile. That's pushing commercial limits with a single tank and no chamber within two hundred kilometers."

"I know what it is."

"Do you?" Tomas set down the stylus. "Because I ran the numbers twice, and I keep getting the same answer, which is that this dive is a bad idea we're going to do anyway, and I'd like to hear you say it out loud so I know we're both lying to ourselves on purpose."

Hazel almost smiled. In six days aboard the Kestrel he had learned that this was how Tomas Reyes handled fear: he turned it into arithmetic and made you sign for it. "We go down the church side, not the channel. Forty-one, maybe forty-four if the streets slope. Twenty minutes bottom time, hard stop. Slow ascent with a hang at fifteen and another at nine. If either of us feels wrong, we're done, no argument, no pride."

"And the document tube?"

"If we find one, we grab it and go. We're not archaeologists. We're thieves with better manners."

Tomas exhaled through his nose, which was as close to laughter as he got before a dive. "Marguerite wants us to bring back anything with writing on it. June wants us to bring back our lungs inflated. I told her that was the plan."

They suited up on the deck while June idled the Kestrel into position over the town square, using the sonar overlay Marguerite had printed and laminated overnight. Marguerite herself crouched at the transom with the map spread beside her knees, checking their drop point against Elias Vann's hand-inked lines for the fourth time. She hadn't slept much either. Hazel had heard her moving around the cabin at two in the morning, the scratch of her pencil audible through the bulkhead.

"Vann's soundings match the sonar to within two meters," she said. "For a man drawing from memory, sixty years old, that's—" She stopped herself, pressed her lips together. "That's someone who loved this place."

"You knew about it before the map," Hazel said. Not accusing. Just setting it on the table between them, where it had been sitting since the day she'd recognized the coordinates and lied about why.

Marguerite's jaw tightened. "After," she said. "I'll tell you after. When there's something worth telling."

He let it go. There was a time for pulling threads and a time for diving, and the water didn't care which one you chose.

* * *

The surface closed over Hazel's head at 0740, and the world went quiet in the particular way only deep cold water can manage—not silent, but hushed, like a library after closing. Sixteen degrees at the thermocline, then twelve, then nine. The visibility, which should have been terrible in a drowned river valley choked with sixty years of silt, opened up instead as they descended past thirty feet, forty, sixty. Cold water settling into stillness. Nobody swimming here but them.

A rope of sunlight followed them down, bending, thinning, going from gold to green to a deep underwater blue that Hazel had seen perhaps ten times in his life and never gotten used to. Beside him Tomas kicked in slow, even strokes, his torch beam sweeping ahead of them like a lighthouse learning humility.

Then the rooftops came up out of the dark.

Hazel had prepared himself for structures. Foundations, walls, maybe a standing chimney—the usual bones of a drowned place. What he had not prepared himself for was a *town*. Whole houses, intact, sitting in their rows along streets that were still streets, the asphalt gone soft and furred with algae but the painted center line still faintly visible in places, a ghost's spine running downhill toward the submerged main road. Terracotta tiles on some roofs, corrugated steel on others, all of it furred white and green with sixty years of growth, so the whole settlement looked like it had been dipped in frost and forgotten.

Tomas stopped kicking. Even through the regulator, Hazel could see the shape of the word he wasn't saying.

They hung there at forty meters, two divers suspended above a neighborhood, and Hazel felt the thing he always felt in places like this—the good places, the true places—except scaled up past anything he'd known. Salvage wrecks gave you cargo and corpses and the impersonal violence of weather. This gave you *laundry lines*. A bicycle, fused to rust, leaning against a fence post that was really a fence post. A child's tricycle on a porch, its handlebar tassels rotted to strings. Someone had lived here. Hundreds of someones. And the water hadn't crashed in and taken it; the water had risen slowly, politely, house by house, until the whole valley simply agreed to be a lake.

Nobody had ever mentioned a town here. Not on charts, not in the regional histories Marguerite had loaded onto the Kestrel's laptop, not in Elias Vann's careful, coded notes. A settlement of this size didn't vanish from the record. It was *removed* from it.

Hazel pushed the thought down where he kept all the others, in the compartment below his ribs where questions went to wait, and checked his gauge: 41 meters, seven minutes elapsed. He tapped Tomas's shoulder and pointed down Main Street.

They swam between the rooftops.

It was like flying low over a sleeping village at night, if night were blue-green and pressed against you from all sides. Their torch beams carved tunnels through the murk, and things bloomed in and out of them: a mailbox on its post, number 14 stenciled in flaking paint. A pickup truck settled into the silt of a driveway, doors open, empty—evacuated, not abandoned. A school? The building on the corner had a flagpole in front, bare, and small desks visible through windows furred opaque. Hazel made himself look away from the desks.

The church announced itself before they saw it, because the bell tower was the tallest thing left standing and the current—or whatever slow circulation moved through the lake—had scoured the silt away around its base, leaving it rising clean out of the valley floor like a monument. They came around its corner and Hazel's breath caught hard enough that his computer chirped a warning at his ascent rate.

The bell was still in the tower.

Not fallen, not salvaged, not looted in sixty years of a lake that fishermen must have crossed a thousand times. It hung in its oak headstock, tilted slightly, bronze gone the color of old honey under a skin of pale sediment. And cast into its shoulder, legible even through the growth, was a date.

Tomas swam close and swept his glove across the metal. Silt billowed, hung, drifted aside. He brushed again, gentler, the way you'd clear a gravestone.

**FUNDIDA EN SAN SEBASTIÁN DEL RÍO — 1911**

Below it, smaller: the founder's mark, and a municipal seal—a river, a bridge, a star.

Hazel knew the region's history. Everyone who worked these waters did, because knowing history was how you knew which wrecks were legal and which would get you boarded. San Sebastián del Río did not exist. Not in the census rolls Marguerite had pulled, not in the diocesan records, not on a single survey chart back to the eighteen-forties. A town with a foundry-grade bell, a municipal seal, paved streets, and a school had been erased so thoroughly that even its name survived nowhere except in bronze, underwater, waiting sixty years for two men with torches.

Tomas looked at him across the bell. Behind his mask his eyes were wide, and Hazel understood that the engineer was doing his arithmetic again, and this time the numbers weren't coming out safe.

Hazel reached out and touched the bell himself. One finger, nothing more—you don't ring bells in drowned towns, some instinct older than training insisted—but he touched it, and the bronze was cold in a way that felt less like temperature and more like a held breath.

His computer read eleven minutes bottom time. Fourteen minutes of margin, and the archive might be anywhere in a town of forty buildings.

* * *

They found the municipal building by its flagstone plaza, half a block off Main Street, a squat stone structure with columns that some civic optimist had shipped upriver decades before the dam. Its front doors had rotted away, and the interior beyond the threshold was a cathedral of suspended particulate, their lights reduced to short glowing cones.

Hazel went in first. The floor plan was simple: a public hall, offices behind. Filing cabinets stood in rows like a choir, steel rusted to lace, drawers swollen shut or hanging open and empty. Whatever paper the town's bureaucracy had produced, most of it had either been removed before the flooding or had dissolved into the silt that now lay ankle-deep across everything, gray and fine as flour.

But in the back office—the corner room, the one with the best view of the river, because that was where the man in charge would have sat—one cabinet had fallen against the wall in a way that had pinned a steel document case beneath it, and the case had done what steel cases do, which is refuse, stubbornly, to be nothing.

Tomas helped him lever the cabinet aside. The case's latch was fused. Hazel worked it with his dive knife, patiently, the way he'd opened a hundred sealed compartments on a hundred dead ships, and felt the old calm settle over him, the diver's trance where the world narrows to hands and task and breath. The latch gave. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that someone had thoughtfully—and ominously—packed around them, were three document tubes. Galvanized steel, screw-capped, the kind surveyors used for maps. Two had corroded through; when Hazel lifted them, brown water trickled from the seams and the paper inside came apart like autumn leaves.

The third tube was heavier. Thicker walls. Military surplus, maybe, or just a better brand. The cap fought him, squealed a stream of silver bubbles, and then broke free.

Inside: a roll of heavy paper, wax-sealed at the center, the wax intact. Water had gotten into the tube but not yet to the heart of the roll. Whatever was written there was still written.

Hazel's first instinct—the salvage instinct, the survival instinct—was to hand it to Tomas, share the weight, make it the crew's object instead of his own. His second instinct arrived a half-second later and overrode it: *some things you carry yourself.* He didn't examine the feeling. He rolled the paper gently back, reseated the cap, and slid the tube inside his buoyancy vest, against his chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat.

Tomas watched him do it and said nothing. But Hazel saw him see it, and understood that the silence between them had changed texture. On the descent they had been two divers on a job. On the ascent they would be two men who knew something.

His computer read nineteen minutes. Time's up. He tapped his gauge, pointed up, and got Tomas's nod.

* * *

The ascent was a lesson in patience performed at the edge of fear. Hang at fifteen meters for three minutes, watching the rooftops fade below them into the blue-gray dark, the bell tower sinking away like a hand being withdrawn. Hang at nine. Watch the surface ripple overhead, hammered silver. Hazel kept one hand on the tube in his vest the whole way up, not gripping it, just confirming it, the way you check a wound.

They broke the surface into gray daylight and the smell of diesel and June's voice cracking over the water: "Eleven minutes over profile! Both of you, on the ladder, now—oxygen's rigged on deck, don't argue with me."

"We're fine," Tomas started.

"You're *fine* when I say you're fine. Ladder."

June Okafor, twenty-six years old and in debt to people whose names she wouldn't say, stood at the transom in a life jacket she'd put on for no operational reason whatsoever, and Hazel realized she'd spent the entire dive counting their bubbles and imagining the moment they didn't come back. He climbed the ladder and let her shove oxygen under his nose without protest.

Marguerite met them on deck, and her eyes went straight to the bulge in his vest. She didn't ask. She stood there with her arms folded against the cold, vibrating slightly, a woman holding a question the size of the valley behind her teeth.

Hazel took out the tube. Held it a moment longer than he needed to—long enough that Marguerite's eyebrows rose, long enough that June stopped fussing over Tomas to watch—then handed it to her.

"Careful," he said. "It's the only one that made it."

She carried it to the deck table like it was a sleeping animal. Cut the wax seal with her cartographer's knife. Unrolled the paper with tweezers and a breath held so long Hazel felt his own chest ache in sympathy.

The document was a proclamation, typed, with a typewritten date fifty-nine years ago and a signature block bearing an official title Hazel didn't recognize—an authority that existed in no government index Marguerite had ever searched, and she said so out loud, her voice climbing as she read. An order of removal. Relocation effective in fourteen days. Compensation to be determined. Compliance mandatory.

And stamped diagonally across the bottom third of the page, in ink that had faded to the color of dried blood:

**BY ORDER OF THE COMMISSION — THIS RECORD NOT FOR RELEASE.**

Marguerite looked up. Her face had gone the color of the morning fog.

"Hazel," she said quietly. "This isn't a wreck. This is a crime scene, and we're standing in it."

Behind her, Tomas sat with oxygen still hissing under his nose, watching the tube. June had gone very still at the rail. And down in the blue-green dark, forty meters under the keel, a bell hung silent in a tower in a town that had never officially existed, keeping the only honest record anyone had bothered to write.

Hazel looked at the stamped words for a long time. Then he looked west, toward the open water and the long road home, and felt the secret settle onto his shoulders with its full weight—lighter than a tank, heavier than a ship.

"Ninety days," he said. "We've got eighty-three."
