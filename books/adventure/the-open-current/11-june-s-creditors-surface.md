## 11. June's Creditors Surface

June Okafor had learned to read engines the way other people read faces, and the Kestrel was telling her something ugly.

The port generator had been running rough since dawn—a hitch in its rhythm like a man clearing his throat before bad news. She knelt in the engine room with a stethoscope pressed to the housing, listening past the noise to the noise underneath it. Bearing wear. Nothing fatal yet, but nothing she could fix with what they had aboard either. They were nine days into the valley and already burning through spares faster than Tomas's manifest had promised, and every hour the generator ran hot was an hour borrowed against their air compressors, their lights, their lives.

She wiped her hands on a rag that had stopped being blue sometime last week and climbed the ladder into daylight.

The gorge swallowed sound here. The drowned valley rose on both sides of them in walls of wet granite streaked green to the waterline, and above that the skeletal arms of trees that had died standing when the water came—Hazel called them the congregation, a hundred gray worshippers frozen mid-prayer along the ridgelines. The Kestrel sat at anchor in the middle of it all, small as a toy boat in a bathtub, and the silence between engine noises was so complete June could hear her own pulse.

Which is why she heard the launch before anyone else.

It came around the southern bend at a civilian putter, white hull, no markings, no flag. Her stomach dropped before her brain caught up, because there was no reason for anything civilian to be this deep in a valley that wasn't supposed to exist. Supply runs came from the coast depot on a schedule, and the schedule said three days out. This was not the schedule.

"Hazel," she called up to the wheelhouse. "Company."

He came out shading his eyes, and she watched him do the same math she'd just done—the wrong day, the wrong direction, the wrong everything—and watched his face go carefully blank, which was how Hazel Almeida said *oh no* without saying it.

"Everyone inside except you and me," he said quietly. "Tomas keeps his hands off the radio. Marguerite stays with the documents."

"You think it's Locke?"

"I don't know what it is yet." He paused at the rail, looking down at her. "June. Whatever this is—we handle it together or not at all. Nobody negotiates alone."

*Too late,* she thought, and hated herself for thinking it, because she'd recognized the launch now. Not the boat itself—there were ten thousand white boats on this coast—but the way it handled, the lazy drift of a helmsman who wasn't in any hurry because he already knew where he was going and knew he'd be welcome when he arrived.

She knew that helmsman's employer. She owed him eleven thousand four hundred dollars, and interest that compounded weekly, and the knowledge that men who missed payments on that kind of money didn't always get second chances.

* * *

The man who stepped off the launch onto the Kestrel's deck was not what the word *collector* prepared you for. No muscle, no scar tissue, no gold tooth. He wore linen trousers and a shirt with the sleeves rolled just so, and he carried a leather portfolio under one arm like a professor arriving for office hours. He was maybe fifty, silver at the temples, with the soft hands of someone who had never hauled a line in his life.

"Miss Okafor." He smiled like they were old friends at a wedding. "Mr. Okonkwo sends his regards."

Every hair on her arms stood up. "I don't know any Mr. Okonkwo."

"No?" He looked around the deck with mild, appreciative curiosity—at the crane, the compressor racks, the dive flag snapping in the breeze off the water. "Forgive me. In my line of work we say *the office*. Your office is in Port Meridian. You've been making arrangements through a gentleman named Deshi. Ringing any bells?"

Deshi. The name landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. Deshi, who had sat across from her eight months ago in a back room smelling of fried plantain and cigarette smoke, sliding a contract toward her while her mother's hospital bills sat unpaid in a folder between them. Deshi, who had been almost kind about it. *Everyone needs help sometimes, Miss Okafor. That's all credit is. Help.*

"That rings a bell," she said.

"I thought it might." He set the portfolio on the chart table, unhurried, a man who owned time itself. "My name is Corver. I'm what you might call a specialist in unusual recoveries. And I'll be direct with you, because I suspect you're a direct woman: I'm not here about your balance."

Behind her, Hazel shifted his weight. She felt it more than saw it, the way you feel weather change.

"Then why are you here?"

Corver opened the portfolio and turned it around. Inside was a photograph—recent, sharp, taken from elevation. The Kestrel at anchor in the valley, small and unmistakable, her crane arm folded, her dive flag flying. Taken days ago. Maybe from the ridge. Maybe from the air.

"The people I represent have an interest in this expedition," Corver said pleasantly. "Not a hostile interest. A commercial one. They understand you've found something down here, and they'd very much like to know what it is before certain other parties do. All we require is information. What you find. Where you find it. Who asks you about it afterward." He tapped the photograph once with one soft finger. "In exchange, your debt disappears. Eleven thousand four hundred dollars, gone. Interest, gone. Deshi's file on you, closed and shredded. You walk away clean, Miss Okafor. Clean for the first time in years, I'd guess."

The silence after he finished speaking was the loudest thing June had ever heard.

She thought about her mother's face on the video calls, thinner each month, pretending to be better than she was. She thought about the number—eleven thousand four hundred, compounding—which lived in her head the way other people kept phone numbers, always present, always accruing. She thought about how simple it would be. A few reports over a radio channel. A few photographs. Words, really. Just words, passed from one hand to another, and the weight she'd been carrying since Port Meridian would simply... stop.

And then she thought about Tomas coming up from the flooded town with his eyes shining, talking too fast about a church bell with a date on it that shouldn't exist. About Marguerite sitting up all night with recovered documents, going grayer by the hour, saying *this changes everything, this changes whose fault it all was.* About Hazel standing his watch at 0300 because he never trusted anyone else to stand it right.

About the promise none of them had spoken aloud but all of them had made anyway, somewhere between the storm and the narrows: *we get everyone home.*

"We're not selling anything," she said.

Corver smiled again, patient as tide. "Of course not. Today." He closed the portfolio. "But consider the arithmetic, Miss Okafor, because I know you're good at arithmetic—you keep books on this vessel, don't you? Fuel, food, spare parts. How many weeks can you stay? Two? Three? And what happens when the money runs out and the debt is still growing and the offer is still open? Offers like mine don't expire. They ripen." He glanced at Hazel, gave him a courteous nod. "Captain. Lovely boat. Terrible generator bearing, by the way—I could hear it from two hundred meters. Might want to see to that."

He walked back to the launch rail, then paused, half-turned, and delivered the part June would carry in her ears for days:

"One more thing, and then I'll leave you in peace. The office knows about the young lady with the maps. We know where her office is. We know who else has been asking about her lately—people with uniforms, people with budgets. When they come for her, and they will, it might be useful to have friends who aren't wearing uniforms. Think about who actually protects your crew, Captain. Think about who's offering, and who's only taking."

The launch pulled away around the bend, unhurried, its wake slapping the granite walls and coming back doubled, tripled, the valley repeating the sound long after the boat was gone.

* * *

They held the meeting in the mess because it was the only room with a door that locked, though June suspected locks meant nothing to a man like Corver.

She told them everything. All of it—the debt, the real number, Deshi, the back room in Port Meridian, her mother, the whole rotten architecture of the last two years. She'd decided on the ladder coming down from the wheelhouse that if she was going to survive this she was going to survive it honest, and honest meant letting these people see the ugliest ledger she owned.

"So you owe eleven grand to criminals," Tomas said, when she finished. "And they sent a guy in linen pants to buy you."

"They didn't send him to buy me." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "They sent him to price me. There's a difference. Buying means I say yes. Pricing means they find out what I'm worth, what I'm afraid of, and whether I crack. He got everything he came for, and I didn't sell a thing."

Marguerite hadn't spoken since June mentioned the map. Now she did, quietly, not looking up from her hands. "He said they know about me."

"He said they know where your office is. And that someone official is asking about you."

"That would be Commander Locke." Marguerite's jaw tightened. "Locke visited me in Port Meridian before we sailed. Pointed questions about survey requests near these coordinates. I deflected. Apparently the deflection worked well enough that now someone else is asking the same questions from the other direction." She laughed, short and bitter. "We're being squeezed like a pip, and both thumbs belong to different people."

"Or the same thumb wearing different gloves," Hazel said.

That possibility sat in the room with them for a while. June watched it land on each face in turn—Tomas frowning, Marguerite going still, Hazel's eyes narrowing at some private calculation. If Okonkwo's people and Locke's navy were the same animal, then Corver's offer wasn't a side deal at all. It was Locke's seizure order wearing linen instead of uniform, and refusing it had bought them nothing but time.

"It doesn't matter whose thumb it is," June said finally. "What matters is what I told him. We're not selling."

"You told him *we*," Tomas said, and grinned tiredly. "You could've thrown me under the launch, Okafor. Saved yourself. 'Take the engineer, he talks too much anyway.'"

"The crew doesn't get sold," she said. "That's the rule. Hazel laid it down before we left the dock—no promises beyond return. Everyone comes home. Everyone comes home *whole*, or the whole thing's worthless." She looked at Hazel. "You want to tell them the rest? About what Corver said on his way out?"

Hazel told them. The threat dressed as a courtesy: *when they come for her, and they will.* The suggestion that the office might be friendlier protection than the state. The way he'd named Marguerite like a card he was holding up to show he held the whole deck.

"He's playing a longer game than a debt collection," Marguerite said slowly. "Eleven thousand dollars is nothing to whoever funded this visit. The launch alone cost more than my annual salary. So either they're buying cheap insurance on a big payout—or they're building leverage, piece by piece, person by person, until one of us cracks and hands them the archive wrapped in ribbon." She looked around the table. "They don't need all of us. They need one."

The words hung there. June felt them physically, like cold water finding a seam in a drysuit.

"So what do we do?" Tomas asked. "Run? We've got nine days on the concession and a dying generator and a fuel budget that assumes we left yesterday."

"We finish the job," Hazel said. It wasn't loud, but it ended the discussion the way a keel ends wobble. "We came here to document a claim, and we're going to document it, and then we're going home with everyone aboard. Corver's people want information through June? Fine. Then June carries nothing, transmits nothing, and from tonight nobody works alone—not on deck, not on the radio, not on the documents. We rotate watches in pairs. If anyone gets approached again, on shore, by launch, by letter—they say nothing and report it within the hour. To all of us. Not to one of us." His eyes moved around the table and settled, briefly, on June. "Especially not to one of us."

She understood. He wasn't accusing her. He was protecting her—from the suspicion that was already germinating in the room, planted by Corver himself, watered by the photograph and the portfolio and the terrible plausibility of the offer. A collector's oldest trick: make the accusation inevitable, then let the crew do the interrogating for free.

"I'll sleep on deck," she said. "By the radio. If anything transmits that isn't scheduled, I'll hear it first, and I'll wake all of you, not one of you."

"Nobody sleeps on deck," Hazel said. "Radio stays locked in the chart locker, key split between me and Marguerite. That's not punishment, June. That's armor. For you most of all."

*For you most of all.* She nodded and looked down at her hands, calloused and grease-stained and, for the first time in two years, not shaking.

* * *

At dusk she went topside alone—allowed, now that the rules were set, as long as she stayed in sight of the wheelhouse—and stood at the stern rail doing the survival math she couldn't do in front of the others.

Here was the truth she hadn't said at the table: for about four seconds, standing in front of Corver with the photograph lying open between them, she had wanted to say yes. Not because she was weak. Because eleven thousand four hundred dollars was not an abstract number; it was her mother's oxygen concentrator, her brother's school fees, the difference between a family that survived and one that got sold off in pieces. Four seconds of wanting, and then the wanting passed, the way a cramp passes, and what remained underneath it was something harder and cleaner.

She had spent two years believing her debts made her the weakest link on any chain she hung from. Corver had come all this way to confirm it. But the crew had heard the whole number, the whole story, and nobody had flinched, and Hazel had built armor around her instead of a cage, and Marguerite—Marguerite, who had her own secrets, her own debts of a different currency—had said *they need one of us* and then looked at June like she was not, in fact, the one.

Maybe loyalty wasn't the absence of temptation. Maybe it was the four seconds. Maybe it was what you did inside them.

The light was failing over the congregation of dead trees, and the water below the stern had gone the color of hammered pewter. June was about to go below and start the generator check she'd promised Hazel when she saw it: far downstream, at the edge of dark, a single point of light where no light should be. Not a navigation mark. Not a campfire. Steady, elevated, patient—a lamp burning on the ridge, exactly where a camera would want to be.

Watching.

She went below without hurrying, without looking back, and found Hazel in the passageway, and told him in six words: "Light on the ridge. South wall."

He was moving before she finished. By full dark the Kestrel had weighed anchor and shifted two hundred meters into the shadow of the north cliff, engines barely turning over, and the watch schedule was rewritten in pencil, and nobody slept well.

And in the morning, when the supply launch *did* arrive on schedule with their fuel drums and fresh water, June stood on the deck counting barrels and found herself counting something else too—days. Nine left on the concession. Nine days to finish, to document, to get out of a valley that now had eyes on its walls and hooks in its crew.

Nine days, and somewhere behind them, a file with her name on it that Corver had promised to shred—and hadn't.
