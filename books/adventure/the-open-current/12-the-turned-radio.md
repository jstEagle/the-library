## 12. The Turned Radio

Tomas Reyes found the fault the way he found most faults: by refusing to believe the machine was lying to him.

The Kestrel's radio log was a strip of paper as long as his forearm, and at 0347—dead center of the silence window Hazel had decreed on their first night out of port—the transmitter had drawn forty watts and spoken for eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. Not enough to say much. Enough to say a callsign, a grid reference, maybe a single word. The log didn't record what it said. It only recorded that the radio had opened its mouth.

He sat in the radio shack with a cup of coffee gone cold and gray, reading the strip for the fourth time, listening to the generator throb below him like a heart that wouldn't slow down. Outside the porthole the valley's walls rose black against a sky just beginning to bruise gray, and somewhere down there under two hundred feet of green water lay the rooftops of a town that had never existed.

Eleven seconds at 0347.

Tomas was an engineer, and engineers have a religion: nothing happens without a cause. A transmitter doesn't key itself. Either someone keyed it, or something failed in a way that made the circuit close—and he could live with a failure. Failures were honest. He'd already torn the set half apart looking for one: a stuck relay, a corroded contact, moisture bridging a terminal. He'd found nothing. The transmit relay tested clean. The keying line tested clean. The set was, by every measurement he could take, healthy.

Which meant the cause wasn't in the machine.

He thought about June asleep in the forward compartment, curled around her debt like a second skeleton. He thought about Marguerite up on the flying bridge at odd hours with her charts, her pencil moving in small precise strokes, correcting maps that were already correct. He thought about Hazel, who slept like a man lying next to an exit.

And he thought about himself, because honesty was the only tool he owned that he couldn't afford to lose. He'd been awake at 0347. He'd been awake every night since the deep dive, running decompression tables in his head for a man who wasn't even bent anymore, checking and rechecking the numbers on Hazel's bottom time like a penance. He could have walked past the shack. He could have touched the set. He couldn't remember doing it, but memory was a compressor that failed under pressure, and they were all deep inside the pressure now.

* * *

He told Hazel first, alone, in the engine room where the noise would keep them honest.

"Eleven seconds," Tomas said over the thrum. "During the window. Someone transmitted."

Hazel was elbow-deep in the seawater pump, rebuilding a seal with hands that moved like they'd done it ten thousand times, which they had. He didn't stop working. That was how Hazel listened—with his body busy so his face could stay empty.

"You're sure it wasn't the set?"

"I tore it down. It's clean. If it were a fault I'd have found it, and if I hadn't found it, it would've happened again." He paused. "It hasn't. One transmission. One."

Hazel pulled his hands out of the pump housing and wiped them slowly on a rag that had stopped being gray sometime last year. When he finally looked up, his eyes had the flat calm Tomas had learned to distrust—the calm of a man standing very still on ice and listening to it talk.

"Who else knows?"

"Just you."

"Keep it that way. For tonight."

"Hazel." Tomas heard his own voice come out harder than he intended. "I've been running this boat's systems since we left. If someone aboard is talking to Locke—or to whoever's paying them—I need to know before I trust my life to a regulator they might have touched."

Hazel folded the rag with unnecessary care, corner to corner, the way men fold things when they're buying time. "You think I don't know that? You think I haven't been counting the silence windows?" He looked at the bulkhead as if he could see through it, through the hull, into the drowned dark. "I promised Elias Vann I'd bring everyone home. Everyone. Including whoever keyed that radio. So before I start treating my crew like suspects, I want to know what I'm treating them from."

"And if you guess wrong?"

"Then I guess wrong," Hazel said quietly, "and somebody pays for it. Same as everything else on this boat."

* * *

They held dinner at nineteen hundred, as always, in the cramped mess where the table was bolted down and everything else moved. Rice and tinned sausage and Marguerite's hoarded hot sauce, deployed in drops like medicine. The overhead light swung gently with the swell coming down the gorge, and everyone's shadow leaned and recovered, leaned and recovered, like the boat itself was breathing wrong.

Tomas watched them eat and hated what he was doing—cataloguing, weighing, the engineer's disease applied to people. June ate fast and looked at no one, which was normal. Marguerite ate slowly and precisely, which was normal. Hazel ate like fueling a machine, which was normal. Everything was normal, and normal had never felt so much like theater.

It was Marguerite who broke it, because Marguerite had spent three days with the archive documents and had apparently decided that secrets were heavier than she'd budgeted for.

"The municipal seal," she said, to no one in particular, turning her fork over. "The one from the church. I ran the date against the regional land registry abstracts I brought. There's no incorporation record. No tax rolls. Nothing. A town of—" she gestured vaguely downward, at two hundred feet of water and several hundred souls, "—that size, and it's not in a single ledger anywhere. It was erased before it was ever written."

"That's why we're here," Hazel said.

"That's why we're *here*, yes." She set the fork down. "But erasure takes paperwork. Somebody signed orders. Somebody paid movers. Somebody kept the receipts, and those receipts are sitting in a records office under two hundred feet of water, and we are going to go get them in"—she checked some internal calendar—"eleven days, before the concession dies. And I find myself wondering who else knows they're there."

The light swung. Shadows leaned.

"Why," Hazel said, in a voice with nothing in it, "do you wonder that tonight?"

Marguerite looked at him across the table, and Tomas saw her decide something—saw the decision move through her face like weather. "Because I was asked about these coordinates six weeks ago, in my own office, by a man wearing a uniform, and I lied to him, and I've been carrying that lie like a stone ever since. Because if Locke knows where we are, then either he guessed, or he followed, or—" she let it hang, "—someone told him."

June's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "Someone told him."

"It's arithmetic," Marguerite said, not unkindly, which somehow made it worse. "I'm sorry. But it is."

"Arithmetic," June repeated. She put the cup down hard enough that coffee jumped the rim. "You want to do arithmetic? Fine. Who's got debts? I do—who's been open about it, loudly, since day one, because I figured hiding it was worse than the thing itself. Who's got a naval officer who knows her name and her office? Marguerite does. Who's got a brother-in-law who works harbor security back home and a habit of writing letters he shouldn't? Ask Tomas. Ask all of us. Everybody on this boat has a thread somebody could pull."

"My letters are none of your business," Tomas said, stung, though she was right—he did write to Rafael, and Rafael did work the harbor, and he had mentioned, once, in a moment of pride he now wanted to vomit up, that he was off on a salvage contract to coordinates he probably shouldn't have mentioned.

"See?" June spread her hands. "That's the point. We're all pullable. So maybe instead of sitting here doing each other's arithmetic, somebody should check whether the radio's been—"

She stopped.

The silence came down like a dropped hatch. Tomas watched her face change—watched the color leave it in real time—and understood, with the sick clarity of a man watching a gauge cross into red, that she knew something. Or suspected it. Or had just realized, out loud, in front of everyone, exactly the question she should not have known to ask.

"What about the radio, June?" Hazel asked softly.

"Nothing." Too fast. "Nothing. Forget it. Bad example."

"June." Hazel's voice stayed soft, which was how you knew. "What about the radio?"

She stood up so fast her chair skidded. "I said forget it. I'm going to check the anchor watch."

She was gone up the companionway before anyone could answer, and the three of them sat in the swinging light with their cooling food, and nobody ate another bite.

* * *

Tomas went up an hour later. The night had gone glassy, stars doubled in the still water of the drowned valley, the gorge walls cutting the sky down to a river of them. June was at the bow rail with her arms crossed, and when he came alongside her she didn't tell him to go away, which from June was practically an invitation.

"You knew," he said.

"I guessed." She stared at the water. "There's a difference."

"How?"

She was quiet long enough that he counted her breaths. Four. Five. Then: "A launch came alongside nine days ago. Supply run. Fuel drums and flour." Her voice was flat, reciting, the voice of someone reading charges already read to her. "One of the men on it was Ray Mendoza. Works collections for Aurelio Serrano. Back home, Serrano owns a piece of what I owe, and Mendoza doesn't make house calls to boats in the middle of nowhere unless he's been invited."

Tomas felt the deck tilt under words that hadn't moved. "What did he want?"

"To offer me a miracle. Full forgiveness. Every cent." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all. "All I had to do was report our findings. Positions. What we lifted. What we saw. To interested parties. He gave me a frequency and a schedule and said, no pressure, think it over, we'll be in touch." She turned and looked at Tomas, and her eyes in the starlight were furious and terrified in equal measure. "I told him no. I told him no before I even finished thinking it, and I've been telling myself every night since that saying it fast meant it was true."

"And the radio?"

"I don't know anything about the radio, Tomas. I swear it on whatever you want. But when Marguerite started doing her arithmetic, all I could hear was Mendoza's voice going, *somebody on this boat will say yes, they always do.* And I couldn't sit there and listen to you all count each other off when I'm the one holding the number."

Below them, impossibly far down, the drowned town held its silence. Somewhere in it, a bell hung in a flooded steeple, waiting for a wind that would never reach it.

"You should tell Hazel," Tomas said.

"He'll throw me off at the next port."

"No," Tomas said, and surprised himself with how certain it came out. "No, he won't. He's been carrying a promise to a dead man about bringing everybody home, and I don't think he knows yet what that promise costs, but he's going to pay it. Tell him. Tonight. Before he hears it some other way—because Hazel always hears it some other way, eventually. It's his only superpower."

June looked at him for a long moment. "Why are you being decent to me? Half this crew thinks I'm the leak."

"Because I know what the radio log says, and I know what I was doing at 0347, and I know that none of us is clean enough to throw the first stone. Also—" he shrugged, "—you checked the anchor watch for me twice this week when my head was still full of nitrogen. Debts matter. Even small ones."

* * *

But it was Hazel who found the truth about the radio, because Hazel always heard it some other way.

Tomas was in the shack at midnight, running the log strip through his fingers again like a rosary, when Hazel filled the doorway without knocking. He had the ship's chronometer log in one hand—a maintenance record, battery checks, the boring liturgy of a working boat—and he laid it on the desk beside the radio log and aligned the two strips of paper with a diver's patience.

"Walk me through the power draw," he said. "Forty watts. Eleven seconds. What draws forty watts besides the transmitter?"

Tomas frowned. "Depth sounder spikes. Bilge alarm test. The autopilot clutch, briefly, if it loses heading lock and reacquires. Why?"

"Because the chronometer log shows a voltage sag at 0347. Small. Half a second. And the bilge alarm in the aft lazarette tripped its test cycle at 0346 and fifty seconds." Hazel tapped the paper. "Somebody was in the aft compartment at 0347. Somebody opened the electrical panel—there's a shared bus between the panel lights and the keying line, I checked the schematics today after you told me. They didn't use the radio's key. They jumped the transmit relay directly off the panel. Eleven seconds is about how long it takes to hold two wires together while somebody reads you a message off a card."

Tomas stared at him. "Prearranged. A scheduled burst. Whoever it was didn't improvise—they had instructions."

"They had instructions," Hazel agreed. His face in the shack's red standby light was carved out of old wood. "Which means this didn't start on this boat. It started before we ever left the dock. Somebody planned it. Somebody recruited." He straightened up, and for a moment he looked older than the sea had any right to make him. "Get everyone in the mess. Now. All of them."

"Hazel, wait—June came to me tonight. She told me everything. Mendoza, the offer, the frequency. She refused it. She's been dying to tell you for days."

Something moved behind Hazel's eyes—not relief, not quite. Recalculation. "Did she give you the frequency?"

"She remembered part of it."

"Then she can help us narrow it down." He turned for the companionway, then stopped, one hand on the frame, and said, without turning around: "The transmission was eleven seconds, Tomas. A callsign and a position fix fits in eleven seconds. So does one word." He paused. "*Confirmed* fits in eleven seconds."

The generator throbbed. The stars wheeled slowly over the drowned valley. And down the companionway, Hazel's boots rang on the steel as he went to gather his crew—the family he'd promised a dead man he'd bring home alive, one of whom, at 0347 on a moonless morning, had held two wires together in the dark and let the boat speak.

Unless, Tomas thought, following him down into the lamplit belly of the Kestrel, unless they'd been made to speak. Unless the wires had been jumped days ago and the sag was something else entirely, and the person he was about to sit across from at a bolted-down table was innocent, and the guilty one was watching them all count each other off, learning, patient, already knowing exactly what each of them would do next.

At 0400, while the mess light burned and five voices argued themselves raw, the radio log quietly drew forty watts for eleven seconds.

Nobody noticed until morning.
