## 13. Locke Comes Upstream

The helicopter came up the valley at midday, riding the thermals off the drowned river like a hawk that had learned to fly in straight lines.

Hazel heard it first as a tremor in the water—a pressure change against the hull of the *Kestrel*, the way a big engine announces itself through steel before sound ever arrives. He was on deck coiling line, and he felt it in his palms before his ears caught the thump-thump-thump rolling down the gorge. He stood, shaded his eyes, and watched it come around the bend above the narrows: gray fuselage, naval roundel, skids fitted with floats for landing on open water.

It did not slow at the dam. It did not circle to survey. It came directly for them, the way weather comes for a man standing on a ridge with nowhere to hide.

"June," he called, not raising his voice. "Company."

She appeared from the galley hatch wiping her hands on a rag, took one look skyward, and said, "That's not a supply launch."

"No."

"Tomas is down."

"I know." Tomas and Marguerite were at forty meters, working the municipal building's foundation where the sonar had shown a second structure—something older than the town itself, maybe, or something built into it. Hazel had been due to relieve them in an hour. The hour had just been taken away.

The helicopter flared over the anchorage, kicked up a ring of spray, and settled onto the flat water fifty yards off their beam with the casual authority of something that owned everything it touched. The downdraft flattened the little chop, pressed the *Kestrel* down onto her lines, and sent June's coffee cup skittering across the deck. A door slid open. Two figures climbed out onto the skids—one in flight gear, one in a uniform so dark and unadorned it might have been designed specifically to avoid appearing on any organizational chart.

Hazel set down the coil of line. Folded it once more, unnecessarily, because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

"Get the tender ready," he said. "And get ready to bring my divers up fast if I signal."

"What are you going to do?"

"Find out what I'm worth."

* * *

Commander Locke came across in the inflatable himself, alone, leaving the pilot and the second figure aboard the aircraft. That was a message in itself. Men who send lieutenants want deniability. Men who come themselves want something they can't trust to anyone else.

He was younger than Hazel had imagined—not young, but younger than the weight he carried suggested. Mid-forties, maybe. Close-cropped hair going silver at the temples like frost creeping across a window. He wore no ribbons, no name tape beyond a small stitched tag reading LOCKE, and he stepped aboard the *Kestrel* with the easy balance of a man who'd spent time on small boats and hadn't forgotten what they cost.

"Captain Almeida." He extended a hand. His grip was dry, firm, calibrated. "Thank you for receiving me."

"You didn't ask."

"Would you have said yes?"

Hazel looked at him. "No."

"Then we understand each other." Locke smiled without warmth—the smile of a man acknowledging a shared rule rather than expressing pleasure. "May I?"

He gestured at the deck. Hazel nodded toward the cockpit shelter, the only place aboard with two chairs and anything resembling privacy. Locke moved through the vessel with an eye that catalogued everything—the borrowed compressor, the patched dive suits drying on the rail, the chart table with its corners weighted by coffee mugs—and Hazel watched him catalogue it and understood that every detail was being filed somewhere behind those pale eyes.

In the shelter, Locke sat, declined coffee, and placed a leather folder on the chart table between them. He did not open it.

"You've been busy," he said.

"We hold a concession permit. Legal salvage survey, ninety days, properly filed."

"I've read the permit." Locke turned it over in his hands without opening the folder. "Filed under Vann, Elias. Deceased. Transferred to you as named custodian. All very tidy. Elias Vann was thorough that way—he'd been preparing this handoff for years, actually. Longer than you knew him, I'd guess."

The words landed harder than Locke probably intended—or exactly as hard as he intended; Hazel couldn't tell yet which kind of man he was dealing with, and suspected Locke preferred it that way. *Longer than you knew him.* As though the navy had been watching Elias file his maps and draft his permits while his lungs filled with the dust of whatever mine had killed him, and had decided an old prospector dying slowly in dockside bars was beneath surveillance until the moment he passed his secret to a stranger with strong hands and no history.

"How long have you known about us?" Hazel asked.

"Officially? Since your boarding in the channel. Unofficially—" Locke glanced out the shelter's smudged perspex at the water, at the drowned valley beyond it, at the rooftops sleeping under sixty feet of green silence. "Unofficially, Commander, I've known about this valley since I was a lieutenant. There's a file. It has a number instead of a name. I inherited custody of it the way you inherited custody of that map. Neither of us asked for the job."

"And here I thought you were going to pretend you didn't know why we're here."

"I could." Locke folded his hands. "I could cite your permit's environmental clauses. I could impound your compressor for improper import documentation. I could make your lives administratively miserable for six months without ever mentioning the word *archive*. But I'm not going to do that, Captain, because I don't have time, and neither do you. So let me be direct, and you can decide how much of my directness to believe."

He opened the folder.

Inside lay two documents. The first was printed on heavy stock with a seal Hazel recognized from the boarding papers in the channel—the same eagle-and-anchor device, the same dense legalese. SEIZURE ORDER, it began. MATERIALS PERTAINING TO NATIONAL SECURITY. The second document was typed on plainer paper, single-spaced, and looked almost naked next to its official sibling.

Locke tapped the seizure order. "This gives me the authority to take everything. Documents, film, data cards, the dive log, your charts, your sonar records, and the physical archive you've recovered from the records office. You'll receive a receipt listing items seized. You will never see them again. Your permit will be voided effective today—you're inside international-adjacent waters under a lapsed regional authority, and I can bury the paperwork question deep enough that no lawyer alive would dig it out. You go home. No charges. No blacklisting. In eighteen months, if you keep quiet, you can apply for coastal survey work again."

He tapped the second page. "Or."

* * *

Hazel had spent thirty years underwater, and he had learned that the most dangerous moments were never the ones where things went wrong. They were the ones where things went *quietly right*—where the wreck opened a doorway it had no business opening, where the current eased just enough to let you through, where the sea offered you something beautiful at precisely the depth where saying yes meant you might not surface. Every dead diver he had ever known had died accepting an offer. The sea was generous that way. It only asked that you stay.

He looked at the second page and recognized the generosity immediately.

"Salvage rights," he read aloud. "Exclusive. Five-year term. The *Kestrel*, titled free and clear—"

"The navy has three vessels in mothballs that owe us nothing and cost us money every quarter. One of them becomes yours. Papers clean, hull surveyed, engines rebuilt last year."

"—and a consulting retainer." The number made him stop. It was more money than Hazel Almeida had earned in the last four years combined. It was the number that paid off June Okafor's creditors with a phone call. It was the number that bought Tomas Reyes a workshop instead of a workbench. It was the number that let Marguerite Chen stop taking chart jobs from men she despised.

And it was the number that meant he would never again have to wonder whether the compartment on the navy wreck existed, because he would be inside the institution that kept such compartments sealed.

"Quiet money," Hazel said. "That's what this is."

"That's what this is." Locke leaned back. For the first time, something human moved behind his eyes—not quite sympathy, but the memory of it, worn smooth like a coin handled too long. "You think I enjoy this. I can see it in your face. So let me tell you what I actually am, Captain. I'm the man who keeps the file. When I retire—and that's eleven years out, if my heart holds—the file passes to someone else, and that someone else will be younger than me and less tired and considerably less careful about how many people drown holding it. What I'm offering you isn't charity. It's arithmetic. Right now, the people who know what's in that flooded records office can be counted on one hand, and I know all their names. Every day that list grows, the odds grow that someday, somewhere, one of them talks, or sells, or dies badly, and the whole thing comes apart in a way nobody controls. I'm offering to close the list. You're offering to open it. Only one of us gets to be right, and I'm asking you to consider which outcome leaves your crew breathing."

"My crew," Hazel said quietly, "is currently at forty meters on your drowned crime scene."

"Yes." Locke didn't flinch. "Which is why I came myself instead of sending a seizure team. Because I've read your file too, Captain Almeida. Not the navy's file on you—the real one. The one written in what you do when you think no one's watching. You pulled a stranger's body out of the channel two winters ago and drove four hours to find his sister rather than let the harbor office handle it. You've reported three wrecks you found illegally because leaving them unmarked would kill somebody eventually. You are, by every measure I have available, a man who keeps promises." He paused. "Elias Vann thought so. It's why he chose you. And it's why I'm sitting in your cockpit drinking bad coffee instead of stripping your boat with a warrant."

The shelter was very quiet. Through the perspex, Hazel could see the green water, the dark shapes of the gorge walls, the thin white thread of the falls feeding the reservoir far upstream. Somewhere under all of it lay a town with a church bell and a municipal seal and filing cabinets full of names—people who had been told to leave, told to forget, told that the water rising over their streets was progress. People whose grandchildren still believed their grandparents had sold willingly and moved happily and lost everything to bad luck.

Marguerite had shown him the documents two nights ago. He had read them by lamplight in the galley while the others slept, and he had sat afterward in the dark listening to the water lap the hull, and he had understood with total clarity that Elias Vann had handed him a live grenade with the pin already pulled. *Tell no one what it finds,* the old man had said. *Bring them home.* Half a promise each way, and both halves pulling against each other like opposing tides.

"You said you've known since you were a lieutenant," Hazel said. "Thirty years, give or take. Did you ever dive it?"

Locke was silent for a long moment. "Once," he said. "Nineteen eighty-nine. Survey assessment, two-man team, classified. We swam the main street." His voice changed—flattened, the way voices flatten when a man is handling something carefully so it won't cut him. "There was a schoolhouse. The playground equipment was still standing. A swing frame, rusted red, with the chains hanging. My partner put his light on it and then turned it off again, and we didn't speak for the rest of the decompression stop, and we never spoke about it again after. He died five years ago. Heart attack, they said. He was fifty-one." Locke looked at Hazel. "I am telling you this so you understand that I know exactly what's down there. I have carried it for thirty years. And I am telling you that carrying it nearly killed me anyway, and that the offer on this table is the only version of survival I know how to give you."

"And the people in the town?"

"The people in the town are dead, Captain."

"Their names aren't."

* * *

Locke closed the folder. Slid it across the chart table until it rested under Hazel's hands, the way a card player slides the deck to let you cut.

"You have until I lift off," he said. "If the answer is yes, you'll hand me the recovered documents personally, I'll void the seizure order in front of you, and we'll drink to Elias Vann—who, I'll note, tried to sell this same map to the navy in nineteen seventy-four and was turned away, which is a fact I probably shouldn't have mentioned and will deny mentioning. If the answer is no, I lift off, and within the hour a cutter arrives from the coast station, and my people will be considerably less courteous than I am. Either way, Captain, you should know: I gave you the choice. Whatever happens after, you'll remember that I gave you the choice."

"That's supposed to make it easier?"

"That's supposed to make it *yours*." Locke stood. At the shelter's opening he paused, half in shadow, and for a moment the commander's armor slipped and Hazel saw the lieutenant from 1989, hanging in green water above a rusted swing set, turning off his light because some things are too heavy to look at twice. "For what it's worth—I hope you say yes. Not for my sake. Because you strike me as a man who'd rather carry a debt than a grave."

Then he was gone, crossing the gap between vessels in the inflatable, climbing the helicopter's skid, and the cabin door slid shut behind him with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

Hazel sat alone in the shelter with the folder under his hands. Below, at forty meters, his people were swimming through the evidence of a crime the state had committed against its own citizens, and above, the rotor wash was beginning to turn, and the clock on all of it was ticking in units he couldn't see.

He thought about Elias Vann, coughing blood into a bar towel, pressing a grease-stained map across the table with fingers like tree roots. *Bring them home. Tell no one what it finds.* The old man had known, Hazel realized now—had known even then, dying, that the promise was impossible. Had handed it over anyway, whole, and let Hazel discover for himself which half he could keep.

The radio crackled. Marguerite's voice, thin with depth and distance: *"Kestrel, Kestrel, this is dive team. We found something. You need to see this. Bring the camera housing."*

Hazel picked up the handset. Set it down again. Looked once more at the folder—at the salvage rights, the clean title, the number that would buy his family out of every debt they owed—and then out the perspex at the helicopter squatting on the water like a patient predator, waiting for its answer.

He keyed the radio. "Copy, Marguerite. On my way. Tell Tomas to start his ascent clock—we may need to move fast."

Outside, the helicopter's rotors began to slow.

Locke was waiting.

So, apparently, was whatever waited at the bottom of the records office—and Hazel was suddenly, coldly certain that the two of them, the commander and the archive, had been waiting for each other for thirty years, and that he had walked into the middle of it carrying a dead man's map and a promise he was about to learn the true price of keeping.

He pulled on his boots and went to tell his crew that the navy had found them—and that the countdown had just gotten shorter.
