## 14. Ninety Days Becomes Nine

Marguerite Chen had spent eleven years learning to read paper the way other people read weather. A survey plat told her where a river wanted to go. A deed told her where greed had already been. And a legal document, she had learned early, was never really about what it said—it was about what it was built to prevent you from noticing.

So it was that on the eighty-first night of the concession, wrapped in two blankets in the Kestrel's cramped chart room with a kerosene lamp turned low, she found the sentence.

She hadn't been looking for it. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the detail that made her stomach drop and then harden into something cold and useful. She'd been looking for something else entirely—a precedent, an old case citation from the territorial water courts, anything she could use to argue against the seizure order Commander Locke had left hanging over them like weather. The permit Elias Vann had traded for Hazel's promise lay flat under her palm, its corners soft as cloth from decades of handling. She'd read it before. Twice. Once for content, once for structure.

Tonight she read it a third time, slowly, the way you read a chart before entering water you don't trust.

The document was old-fashioned in its drafting, all long clauses and nested conditions, the prose of a lawyer who'd been paid by the word or by the deception. Most of it concerned boundaries: metes and bounds described in chains and rods, coordinates transcribed in Elias's own hand in the margins, corrections initialed in fading brown ink. The valley itself was described not as a place but as a set of relationships—thence north thirty-one degrees west to the submerged weir, thence along the drowned channel—and Marguerite had traced those relationships on her charts until they matched, until the map and the permit agreed with each other like two witnesses telling the same story.

It was clause fourteen that broke the story open.

*All claims herein granted shall remain contingent upon physical documentation of unexplored portions of the claim area, such documentation to be executed upon the site itself and filed prior to expiry of the concession term. Claims or portions thereof remaining undocumented at expiry shall be deemed abandoned, void ab initio, and shall revert without compensation.*

She read it again. Then a third time, her lips moving, because some part of her animal brain refused to accept arithmetic that simple and that cruel.

Void *ab initio*. Void from the beginning. Not voided at expiry—void retroactively, as if the whole ninety days had never existed, as if every dive, every risk, every hour Tomas had spent elbow-deep in the compressor and June had spent talking money out of men who collected fingers, had all been performed on behalf of a claim that would simply evaporate.

And "physically documented upon the site." Not surveyed from a ship. Not sketched from memory in a hotel room. Executed *on site*, in the water, at the structures themselves, with whatever formalities the original grant required—which meant, when she dug through the schedule attached at the back, sealed witness statements, monument placements, and a full plat of the submerged township drawn to a specified scale.

They had documented perhaps a fifth of the claim area.

Marguerite sat very still in the lamplight and did the math she didn't want to do. Eighty-one days gone. Nine remaining. The archive office—the deep one, the one past safe depth limits, the one that had already put Tomas on oxygen and left his hands shaking for two days—was still undocumented. The eastern terraces were undocumented. The weir complex, the most legally significant structure in the entire grant, the anchor point from which every boundary radiated, was undocumented.

Nine days. To finish what should have taken a season.

Her first feeling wasn't panic. It was a strange, vertiginous admiration—the same feeling she got looking at certain old maps, the ones drawn by surveyors who knew exactly which marsh they were steering buyers into. Clause fourteen wasn't an oversight. Nobody drafted a clause like that by accident. Somebody, sometime, had wanted this concession to fail quietly—to expire, to revert, to vanish back into the ledger of things the government held but denied holding. The permit was designed to die. Elias Vann had handed them a document with a trapdoor built into its floor, and either he'd never known, or—

Or he'd known exactly, and trusted Hazel to be the kind of man who moved fast when the water rose.

Marguerite closed her eyes. She thought about Elias only in fragments now: the cough she'd heard through the dockside bar's open door the one time she'd seen him, the way his map had been drawn from memory rather than instrument, every contour line carrying the tremor of a dying hand. He had given them a clock disguised as a gift. Ninety days had sounded generous back at the dock, back when ninety days was an abstraction. Now it was nine, and the abstraction had teeth.

She got up, steadied herself against the bulkhead as the Kestrel rolled, and went to find Hazel.

* * *

He was on deck, as he usually was after midnight, standing at the rail with a mug of coffee he never seemed to drink. The gorge rose black on either side of them, and above its rim the stars came down close enough to feel like company. Somewhere below the keel lay the drowned town, its rooftops furred with silt, its bell waiting in the church tower like a question nobody upstairs wanted answered.

"Hazel."

He turned. In the dark he was mostly silhouette, but she could read him anyway—eleven days aboard will teach you a man's posture the way a year ashore won't. He heard it in her voice before she finished the first sentence. She saw him set the mug down on the rail, carefully, the way you put down a tool before handling something dangerous.

"Tell me."

She told him. All of it. Clause fourteen, the reversion, the on-site requirement, the schedule of formalities, the nine days. She watched him take it the way he took everything—without visible movement, without a word, his stillness so complete it was almost louder than shouting. When she finished, the only sound was the water slapping the hull and, far off, the compressor shed's loose tarp snapping in the wind.

"The weir," Hazel said finally. "That's the anchor. Without the weir documented, the whole boundary description fails."

"And the weir is at depth. Past the terrace shelf, down where the water starts acting like a solid." She paused. "Where we almost lost Tomas."

"I know where we almost lost Tomas." His voice was level, but there was iron under it, and something else—she recognized it from her father, a harbor pilot who had run typhoon seasons his whole life. It was the sound of a man converting fear into schedule. "How many monuments does the schedule require?"

"Seven. We've placed three. Four remain, and each has to be witnessed and sealed on site—there are witness blanks in the back of the permit, Elias pre-signed two of them as grantor. The others need our own documentation, plats, the works." She pulled the folded schedule from her pocket, the paper soft with her handling. "Hazel, I can draft a plat of the whole township in four days if I have soundings and observations. But the observations have to come from the water. From *us*. There's no way around it. Clause fourteen doesn't care how good my draftsman's hand is if the underlying record says we never stood on the claim."

"Locke knows," Hazel said.

It wasn't a question, and it landed in her chest like a dropped weight, because she had arrived at the same thought somewhere around clause fourteen's third reading and had been refusing to look at it directly. Of course Locke knew. A man doesn't fly a helicopter into a gorge carrying a seizure order unless he understands the legal machinery underneath the water better than the people he's seizing from. Maybe that was why the navy had waited. Why the patrol boats sat at the mouth of the valley like patient dogs. They didn't need to board anyone. They only needed to let the calendar work.

"They're not going to stop us," Marguerite said slowly, testing the idea as she said it. "They're going to *allow* us. Right up until day ninety. Then the claim dies on its own, the documents become worthless, and they take the archive off a crew that no longer has any legal standing to hold it. We've been racing a clock we couldn't see."

For a long moment Hazel said nothing. Below them, faint through the hull, she could hear Tomas moving in the aft cabin—he slept badly since the deep dive, surfacing every hour with a cough he tried to hide. June's hammock creaked in the forepeak; even asleep, June slept like a woman listening for footsteps.

Then Hazel smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was narrow and bright and it reminded her, absurdly, of ice forming.

"Then we stop racing their clock," he said, "and start using ours. Nine days. What's the minimum that satisfies clause fourteen?"

Marguerite had already worked it out, twice, in the chart room, her pencil worn to a stub. "Four monuments. Full plat of the township including the archive office. Witness seals on site. Filed before sunset on day ninety." She hesitated. "There's a filing provision. Documents can be lodged with any licensed registrar within the concession district—but the nearest registrar is in Port Sable, two days' run downstream, and the narrows between here and open water are where the patrol boats like to sit."

"So we finish the work in six, run the narrows on day eight, file on day nine with hours to spare." He said it flatly, like a dive plan, depths and bottoms and margins, no romance in it anywhere. "Tomas dives?"

"Tomas shouldn't dive. Tomas needs to dive. The terrace monuments are beyond snorkel range and the bottom times are ugly." She heard her own voice tighten. "Hazel, I've watched him shake for two days. I'm the one who reads the fine print, but you're the one who decides who goes down. I need you to decide it knowing what I know."

"What you know," Hazel repeated, and looked at her—not through her, the way he sometimes looked at the horizon, but *at* her, and she realized how rarely he did that, and what it cost him. "Which is more than you've been telling us since the dock at Port Sable. You knew these coordinates, Marguerite. First night aboard, you looked at that map like you'd seen a ghost wearing your own face. I didn't ask then because asking felt like a luxury. It stopped being a luxury tonight."

The wind picked up, worrying the tarp again. Marguerite wrapped her arms around herself and considered, for the hundredth time, the shape of the lie she'd been carrying—the survey request she'd filed eighteen months ago, the naval officer who'd come to her office afterward with questions dressed as courtesy, the reason she'd said yes to a stranger's expedition on a stranger's dying promise. She had told herself she'd explain when explanation wouldn't break the crew's fragile new trust. She had told herself a lot of things.

But Hazel had just handed her the truth like a man handing over a tool, handle first, and lying to him now would be like falsifying a plat: technically possible, professionally fatal.

"I requested the valley's coordinates for a private client," she said. "Eighteen months ago. A historical foundation—or something wearing a foundation's clothes. They wanted a pre-war settlement confirmed beneath the reservoir. I found references in a flooded parish registry, fragments. Before I could complete the survey, the request was flagged—naval jurisdiction, restricted waters. My client vanished. My professional license came under review. And a Commander Locke appeared in my office asking, very politely, whether I'd shared those coordinates with anyone else." She exhaled. "I told him no. It was even mostly true. Then Elias Vann's map crossed my desk with the same coordinates, and I understood that if I stayed ashore, the next person to plot them would be working for whoever Locke works for. I came aboard to make sure that didn't happen. That's the whole of it. That's the lie and the reason for the lie."

Hazel absorbed it the way the gorge absorbed sound—completely, without echo. Then he nodded, once.

"You should have told me at the dock."

"Yes."

"But you told me tonight, when it mattered, when the fine print made us partners whether we liked it or not." He picked the coffee mug back up and, to her surprise, drank from it—cold, bitter, half a night old. "That'll hold. Now get your pencils. We plan at dawn, all hands, and we plan like the last nine days of our lives, because legally speaking, that's what they are."

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, the gorge filled with a light like skimmed milk. Marguerite spread the permit, the schedule, and her working charts across the mess table and weighted the corners with mugs while the others crowded in—June still damp-haired from a bucket wash, Tomas gray-faced but upright, moving like a man who had argued himself out of his own bed.

Hazel laid it out without drama. Nine days. Six to finish: two monuments on the eastern terraces, one at the weir, one at the northern boundary stone, plus the observation runs for the full plat. Day seven for Marguerite to draft and cross-check. Day eight to run the narrows. Day nine to file in Port Sable before the registrar's office closed at four o'clock.

"Port Sable," June said, and her voice had a flatness Marguerite had learned to hear as fear doing bookkeeping. "That's where my people are. That's where the collector drinks."

"Then we file and we're gone inside an hour," Hazel said. "Nobody stays overnight."

Tomas cleared his throat. "The weir dive. Bottom time at that depth gives me maybe twelve minutes of useful work. Twelve minutes to place a monument, take observations, and seal a witness statement in a waterproof sleeve." He flexed his right hand on the table, and everyone pretended not to watch it shake. "I can do it. I want to say that plainly, so nobody treats me like glass. I can do it. But I'll need Hazel on the line beside me, and I'll need June running the winch like her life depends on mine, because it does."

"It does," June said simply.

Marguerite watched them arrange themselves around the plan—watched Tomas volunteer his damaged body, watched June volunteer her dangerous shore leave, watched Hazel assign himself to every dive without appearing to notice he'd done it—and felt the thing she'd been afraid of since Port Sable finally settle into place in her chest, not as dread anymore but as ballast. These people were going to finish this. Not because the law required it, though the law did; not because the money was good, because it wasn't; but because clause fourteen had done what clauses like it always secretly intend, which is to find out what a crew is made of when the easy exits close.

Desperation, she wrote later that evening in the margin of her field book, is just focus that hasn't found its scale yet. Ours is nine days. Draw accordingly.

She worked until the lamp burned low, drafting the plat skeleton, leaving blank spaces shaped exactly like the observations they didn't yet have. Around two in the morning, June stuck her head into the chart room, said nothing, and set down a mug of coffee—hot, this time, and sweetened, which June claimed was for emergencies only. Marguerite understood it as a treaty ratification.

At three, she heard the engine room hatch open softly and caught the smell of machine oil: Tomas, unable to sleep, checking the compressor for the fourth time that day. At four, Hazel passed the chart room door on one of his silent circuits of the deck, and paused, and looked in at her bent over the tables.

"Sleep," he said.

"After you."

He almost smiled. "Day one of nine starts in three hours. Get it while it's free."

She slept in the chart chair, the permit under her hand like a talisman, and dreamed of clause fourteen rendered as a physical thing: a great iron door at the bottom of the valley, closing by degrees, and nine days' worth of light coming through the narrowing gap.

She woke to the winch already turning, and to a sound that did not belong to their plan at all—an engine, upstream, throttling down.

Through the porthole, rounding the bend above the drowned town, came a launch flying no colors at all.
