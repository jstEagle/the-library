## 15. The Deep Archive Dive

**HAZEL**

The records office sat at the bottom of the map like a coin dropped into a well.

Marguerite had traced it from the municipal seal on the church bell and the street grid Tomas had plotted over three nights of sonar passes: a stone building set back from what had been the town square, two stories, basement level opening onto the old river road. The river road was the key. Everything else in the drowned town had settled into silt and collapse, but the road had been cut into bedrock, and bedrock didn't rot. It ran downslope from the square like a spine, and at its end, if the map Elias Vann had drawn from a dead man's memory was worth anything at all, the archive waited behind a door built to keep water out for exactly as long as it took the government to stop caring.

Ninety days had become nine. The math left no room for caution, and caution was the only currency Hazel had ever traded in.

"Say it again," he said, crouched beside the compressor on the Kestrel's aft deck, checking the manifold for the fourth time. "The whole profile."

Tomas Reyes looked up from his slate, where he'd been scribbling numbers that made Hazel's chest tighten just to read them. "Forty-one meters to the road. Sixty-two to the door if the building sits where I think it does. Bottom time twenty minutes at depth, then staged ascent, thirty-minute hang at nine meters, another fifteen at six." He tapped the slate. "That's the *generous* version. No margin. None. We're past the recreational tables, past the working tables, into the country where the numbers are suggestions and the nitrogen makes up its own mind."

"I know."

"You asked me to say it again."

"I wanted to hear whether you'd flinch saying it."

Tomas almost smiled. He was thirty-one years old and had the kind of face that aged in increments of worry rather than years, and right now it looked fifty. "I don't flinch. I calculate. And the calculation says one of us goes down and comes back stupid, or neither of us goes down and we lose everything Marguerite found, everything June bled for, everything—" He stopped himself. Looked out at the valley.

It was evening light, the good hour, when the drowned gorge turned copper and the ridgelines threw long shadows across water so still it looked solid. Somewhere under that stillness was a town with a name that appeared in no registry, flooded by no project, erased by no order anyone would admit to signing. Somewhere under that stillness were papers that said who had signed anyway.

"Nitrogen narcosis," Hazel said. "At sixty-two meters you'll be drunk. Both of us will. You know how it works on me—I get calm. Too calm. If I tell you we're going deeper when we shouldn't, you argue. That's your job down there. Not the documents. Arguing with me."

"And if you're the one who goes stupid?"

"Then June pulls the line and hauls us both up like fish, and we deal with the bends together."

Tomas wiped his palms on his thighs. His hands were shaking—not fear, Hazel knew, but the particular tremor of a man who has run the numbers too many times and found them wanting every time. "June thinks this is a bad idea."

"June thinks most of my ideas are bad ideas. She's usually right about half of them."

"She thinks this is the other half."

Hazel stood, rolled his shoulders, felt the old familiar click of vertebrae settling into work. Below his feet, through forty feet of steel hull and then open water, the valley floor waited in the dark. He thought of Elias Vann coughing blood into a bar rag, pressing a grease-stained map across the table with hands that shook worse than Tomas's. *Bring them home,* the old man had said. *Tell no one.* Hazel had already decided which half of that promise he could keep. But tonight, looking at Tomas's frightened, brilliant face, he understood there was a third clause neither of them had spoken aloud: *don't let the promise cost more than it buys.*

"One dive," Hazel said. "We find the door. We go in if the door is open. We come back with whatever our hands can carry. And if anything goes wrong—anything—we drop it all. Weight belt rules. You drop the bag before I have to ask twice."

"Weight belt rules," Tomas agreed.

They shook on it, which divers don't do, because handshakes are for men who expect to see each other after. Hazel did it anyway.

* * *

**TOMAS**

The cold came first, as it always did, a fist closing around his chest the moment he broke the surface tension and sank into the valley's throat. Then the dark, and then the light—their lights, two blades cutting a corridor through water the color of old tea. Forty meters. Fifty. The gauge faces glowed green in his mask and the numbers swam slightly, the way numbers do when the pressure starts rewriting the chemistry of your blood.

*Sixty-two meters. You are drunk. Know that you are drunk.*

He repeated it like a prayer. Narcosis management was mostly narration—you told yourself what was happening to you, over and over, because the alternative was believing whatever the nitrogen whispered instead. And the nitrogen was whispering tonight. It whispered that the water was warm. It whispered that the shapes rising out of the gloom below him were friendly.

They weren't friendly. They were the rooftops.

The town opened beneath them like a photograph developing: rooflines first, then chimneys, then the black rectangles of windows that had been staring at nothing for forty years. A streetlamp leaned against a wall at an angle that suggested someone had knocked it down on their way out. Tomas swept his light along a lane barely wider than the Kestrel and saw a child's bicycle fused to the cobblestones with rust and time.

*Don't look. Looking costs bottom time.*

But he looked. Everyone looked. That was the trap of this place—it didn't let you treat it as a job site. It insisted on being a graveyard.

Hazel's light swung ahead, down the slope where the old river road ran. The man moved through water like he'd been decanted into it at birth—all economy, no waste, fins beating in that slow metronome that covered distance without spending energy. They'd been diving together for two months now and Tomas still hadn't seen him hurry. Even now, with nine days on the clock and sixty-two meters pressing on their lungs like a thumb on a grape, Hazel descended the way other men walked to the mailbox.

The road narrowed. Walls rose on either side—retaining walls, cut stone, and Tomas's narcosis-soaked brain registered them with a surveyor's pleasure even as the rest of him registered the wrongness. These walls had been built to hold back a river. Which meant the town's founders had known the water would come someday. Had planned for it, even. Built their archive at the end of a road designed to flood, behind a door designed to hold.

*They always knew,* he thought, and the thought arrived with a strange clarity that cut through the drunkenness like a knife through fog. *They built the lie into the architecture.*

There. At the road's end, half-swallowed by silt: a facade. Two stories of dressed granite, cornice intact, and centered at the base—a door. Steel, not wood. Riveted. With a wheel-lock like a submarine's, crusted white with mineral scale but, God help them, *ajar*. Opened decades ago and never fully closed again, hanging on its hinges like a jaw gone slack.

Hazel reached it first. He put his glove against the steel and pushed, and the door swung inward with a groan of protest that traveled up through the water and into Tomas's bones.

Beyond it: darkness that their lights fell into and didn't come back from.

Hazel turned. Through the glass of his mask his eyes were calm, and that calm scared Tomas more than the depth did, because he knew now that it wasn't courage. It was the nitrogen. It was the gas telling Hazel that everything was fine, and Hazel being too far under to argue with himself.

*Your job is arguing with him.*

Tomas caught Hazel's arm and held up his slate, on which he'd written in block letters: **20 MIN. WE GO IN 10 MAX. THEN OUT NO MATTER WHAT.**

Hazel read it. Nodded. And for one suspended second, through the distortion of two masks and sixty-two meters of murdered town, the two of them hung in the doorway of the evidence, and Tomas felt something that had no business feeling true: that they were brothers. Not by blood. By shared air. By the oldest contract there was—*I watch your gauges, you watch mine, and neither of us dies alone in the dark.*

They went in.

* * *

**HAZEL**

Inside, the water was different. Stiller. Colder by degrees he felt in his teeth. Their bubbles rose along a ceiling of shelved concrete and flattened there, spreading outward like smoke trapped under glass, and Hazel understood they were in an air pocket's ghost—the space where air had been, once, before the water finished its patient work.

Shelves lined the walls. Rows of them, steel, sagging under boxes that had mostly dissolved into pulp. Paper everywhere, drifting in suspension like gray snow, turning slowly in the push of their fins. Forty years of documents reduced to porridge. His heart went down like a stone.

Then his light found the cabinets.

Along the back wall, set into the foundation itself: flat-file drawers. Steel. Sealed with rubber gaskets that some clerk in some office had specified because the drawings stored inside were irreplaceable even in 1953, especially in 1953. The gaskets had done their job. The drawers were heavy with preserved air and dry paper, and when Hazel worked the first one open, the sound it made—metal on metal, a small clean shriek—was the loudest thing in the drowned world.

Drawings. Surveys. Correspondence in careful government type. He couldn't read it all, couldn't read any of it properly through the distortion and the dark and the drunken hum in his skull, but he could read letterheads, and the letterheads were the ones Marguerite had dreamed about, the ones that connected the dam to the ministry to the navy to the names that history books had politely skipped.

He filled the bag. Filled it until the weight dragged at his harness, and then he kept filling it, because the nitrogen had him now, whispering that there was room for one more drawer, one more folder, one more piece of proof, and the whisper sounded so reasonable.

A hand closed on his shoulder. Hard.

Tomas. Slate raised: **14 MIN. GO NOW.**

Hazel looked at the number and felt a distant, floating surprise, as though the figure belonged to someone else's dive. Fourteen minutes. It had been four minutes. It had been—

No. It had been fourteen. Time ran sideways down here; everyone knew that; he knew that better than anyone alive. He clipped the bag shut. Gave Tomas a thumbs-up.

And that was when the ceiling came down.

Not all of it. A section—shelving that had spent four decades holding a load it was never rated for, finally surrendering to the vibration of a steel door swinging on rusted hinges. It came down ahead of them, across the doorway, tonnage of shelf and concrete and silt collapsing into the road beyond in a slow-motion avalanche of gray. The shockwave hit them like a physical blow, tumbling Hazel against the flat-files, driving the breath regulator hard against his teeth.

When the water cleared, the door was gone. Buried. The way out was a wall of rubble breathing fine sediment into the dark.

*Weight belt rules,* Hazel thought, with the terrible lucidity of a man watching his own stupidity from a distance. *Drop the bag.*

His hands were already moving toward the clips when Tomas grabbed the bag's strap and pointed—upward, and to the left, where their lights had found something in the fresh rubble. A gap. A jagged seam where the collapsed stairwell's landing had punched through into the floor above, leaving a crawlspace the size of a coffin lid.

The second floor. The stairs. The original plan's backup, the route Marguerite had marked on her copy of the map with a question mark and the words *probably blocked.*

Probably. It was going to be a night for probably.

Tomas went first, dragging the bag, because Tomas was the kind of man who did the math and then volunteered for the worst term in the equation. Hazel followed him into the gap, shoulders scraping concrete, the whole ruined building groaning around them like a ship in weather, and pushed off into the dark of the upper floor with his brother-in-air three meters ahead and the nitrogen singing in his blood.

* * *

**TOMAS**

The stairwell was collapsing while they climbed it.

That was the sentence his mind kept returning to, absurdly, grammatically, as he hauled himself up steps that disintegrated under his fins—one step, two, the third crumbling into powder and rebar that snagged his tank valve with a screech he felt in his molars. Above him Hazel's fins churned, kicking loose debris that rained down past Tomas's mask. Below him, through the ragged hole they'd come through, the archive room was still exhaling sediment, a gray plume blooming outward like the building's last breath.

His computer was screaming. He couldn't hear it—he could never hear it—but he could feel it buzzing against his wrist like an angry insect, and he knew what it was saying, because he'd programmed the alarm thresholds himself: *deco obligation exceeded. Deco obligation exceeded.* Every minute in this building was adding minutes to the hang, minutes their gas schedule didn't have, minutes that converted directly into nitrogen bubbles seeding themselves in his joints and spine like frost forming in a wall.

He didn't care. That was the frightening part. He noted that he didn't care, filed it away with clinical detachment—*narcosis, decision impairment, note for the report I may live to write*—and kept climbing.

Daylight. Not daylight—surface-light, the pale green glow of sixty feet of water thinning overhead, coming down through a shattered skylight in what had been the building's attic. Hazel reached it first and paused, silhouetted, and turned back with his light to check on him. The gesture was so ordinary, so routine—*you watch my gauges, I watch yours*—that Tomas felt his throat close around his regulator.

Then his left leg stopped working.

Not pain. Worse than pain: absence. The leg simply announced that it was no longer part of the expedition, that it had seceded, that signals from headquarters would no longer be honored. He kicked with it and got a flutter, a suggestion of motion, and understood with a clarity that had nothing to do with the nitrogen and everything to do with forty years of engineering training: decompression hit. Type II, probably, spinal or cerebral, taking root right now in the tissue the screaming computer had warned him about.

He grabbed the stairwell rail and held on, and the rail came away in his hand, corroded through, and he began to sink.

* * *

**HAZEL**

Later, Hazel would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. Memory underwater was like memory in a fever—fragmentary, lit by strobes, weighted strangely. What remained, always, in every reconstruction:

Tomas sinking, one fin trailing uselessly, his light spinning wild arcs across the attic ceiling.

Hazel letting go of the document bag.

He didn't remember deciding. He remembered the strap sliding off his shoulder and the bag dropping away into the dark below, forty years of proof falling back into the building they'd bled to reach, and he remembered feeling—nothing. No grief. The nitrogen had burned that circuit out. There was only the math of a body in water and a body in trouble, and his body knew the math without him.

He caught Tomas around the chest from behind, locked his grip across the man's harness, and blew his own BC to arrest their fall. For a moment they hung there, tangled, two hundred pounds of diver and steel and failing flesh suspended in the attic of a murdered town, and Hazel put his mask against the back of Tomas's hood and shouted into it, uselessly, sound carrying nothing at that range—*stay with me, stay with me*—words that were less instruction than prayer.

Then he swam.

Up through the skylight, dragging a grown man and his gear, kicking with a ferocity that violated every rule of ascent he'd ever taught, because the rules assumed you had time, and the clock in his arms was dying. Sixty feet. Fifty. He watched Tomas's eyes through the mask—wide, unfocused, tracking something that wasn't there—and he watched his own gauge needle climbing through depths that owed them hours of decompression they no longer had gas to pay.

*Nine meters. Hang. You have to hang.*

He found the descent line June had rigged, caught it, wrapped Tomas's grip around it and held it there. Hung in the green water with his partner convulsing gently against him like a man shivering in his sleep, and did the arithmetic of damnation: they'd surfaced too fast from too deep, and the only treatment was depth itself, and the only chamber within reach was the jury-rigged decompression station they'd welded together from salvage tanks and prayer on the Kestrel's deck.

Thirty meters above, June Okafor was watching their bubbles. He'd told her: *if the bubbles stop for more than five minutes, or if they come up fast, send the line.*

The line came down out of the glare like an answer to a question he hadn't dared ask.

* * *

**TOMAS**

Pain arrived on the surface, all at once, like a debt collector.

He was on the deck of the Kestrel, or he was near it—there were hands, many hands, June's voice cracking orders with a fluency that suggested hidden histories, Hazel's face above him gray with exhaustion and masked in something worse than exhaustion. The makeshift chamber, the steel tube they'd joked about and never expected to need, swallowed him in a hiss of compressed air, and the pressure closed over him like a second ocean.

Through the tiny port, distorted, he watched Hazel press one palm flat against the steel. Just that. Just a hand, held there, while the gauge needles climbed and the tube took him down to depth and the pain in his spine slowly, slowly loosened its grip.

*You watch my gauges,* Tomas thought, drifting now, the oxygen rich and sweet, the terror receding like a tide. *I watch yours. Neither of us dies alone in the dark.*

He slept. When he woke, hours later, the port was dark and the chamber hummed and someone had taped a message to the outside of the glass, written backward so he could read it, in Hazel's blunt carpenter's handwriting:

**BAG DIDN'T MAKE IT. BUT I REMEMBERED EVERY LETTERHEAD. GET BETTER. WE DIVE AGAIN IN SIX HOURS.**

And beneath it, smaller, in a different hand—June's, sharp and slanted:

**MARGUERITE FOUND SOMETHING IN THE SONAR. YOU NEED TO SEE THIS WHEN HE LETS YOU OUT. THERE'S A SECOND ROOM UNDER THE ARCHIVE. AND SOMETHING'S COMING UP THE VALLEY ROAD.**
