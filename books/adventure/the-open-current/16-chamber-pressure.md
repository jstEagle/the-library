## 16. Chamber Pressure

The rig looked like something a junkyard dreamed up during a fever. Tomas hung inside it in the flooded hold of the Kestrel, half-submerged in a steel tank they'd welded from two fuel drums and a salvaged pressure cylinder, breathing oxygen through a regulator rigged to a bottle that should have been condemned years ago. His face was gray above the waterline, his lips the color of old pennies, and every few minutes his whole body would shudder like a man walking through a door he expected to be locked.

June stood watch at the tank's edge with a stopwatch in one hand and a log sheet in the other, writing down numbers she didn't fully understand because Tomas had written the schedule himself before they put him under, in handwriting that had already started to slur.

*O₂ at 30 feet equivalent. Fifteen on, five off. If I convulse, hold my head clear of the metal. Don't let me crack my skull on the rim. — T.R.*

That was the whole manual. That was modern medicine, Kestrel-style: a dying engineer's instructions scrawled on the back of a sonar printout, and a deckhand with a stopwatch praying to a god she'd stopped believing in around the time her mother's house went to the bank.

"Air's at nine hundred PSI," Marguerite said from the companionway, her voice carefully level in the way June had learned meant it wasn't level at all. "The reserve is at four. We have maybe six hours of breathing gas total if we're careful, and we are not going to be careful."

"Then we make more careful," June said, and hated how thin it sounded.

Hazel came down the ladder a minute later, carrying the last case of compressed air from the forward locker, and June watched him take in the scene—the tank, the shuddering man inside it, the arithmetic written on Marguerite's face—and she watched him do what Hazel always did, which was to say nothing, set the case down, and start working the problem with his hands.

They'd been running the rig for eleven hours. The dive itself had lasted forty minutes past every limit Tomas had ever taught anyone. Forty minutes at depth, dragging Hazel's gear and Hazel's body through a collapsing stairwell while the records office gave up its secrets and its ceiling at the same time. When they'd finally broken the surface, Tomas had been able to name all three of them—Hazel, June, Marguerite—but not the year, and by the time they got him into the tank his right arm had gone numb to the elbow.

Now the arm was back. That was the good news. The good news was small and the bad news was everything else.

Supplies were failing the way supplies fail—not all at once but in a long diminishing chord. Food for four days if they stretched it. Water for three. Fuel enough to run the generator for the compressor, which was the only thing keeping their reserve bottles filled, which was the only thing keeping Tomas alive, which meant the generator ate first and everyone else fought over scraps. And outside the hull, the valley held them in its green dark water like a hand closed loosely around a stolen thing, waiting for them to try to leave.

June wrote *14:20, O₂ cycle complete, subject responsive* in the log, and then she stood there holding the pen and not writing the next thing, because the next thing had been sitting in her chest for two days now, growing heavier with every hour, and there was only so much longer she could carry it without it cracking her ribs open.

The collector's name was Deverell. He'd come up the valley on a supply launch wearing a linen jacket too clean for the river, smiling like a man who'd already counted the money, and he'd made her an offer in front of the fuel drums where no one else could hear: forgive everything. Every debt, every marker, every threat against her sister in Port Anselm. All of it, gone, wiped clean as new paint. In exchange, she reported the expedition's findings to interested parties. A radio schedule. A code word. Simple.

And she'd stood there doing the survival math she'd been doing her whole life—the math that said a body in motion stays alive, the math that said loyalty is a luxury people can afford only when they're not drowning—and the math had come out different this time. She'd stared at Deverell's outstretched hand with the pen in it and felt something in her go quiet and hard, like water freezing, and she'd heard herself say no.

Not nobly. Not with a speech. She'd said it the way you refuse a drink you know will put you under: flatly, already grieving the version of herself that would have said yes.

Deverell's smile hadn't changed. That was the worst part. He'd packed up his pen and his smile and gone back down the valley, and June had understood that refusal wasn't the end of anything—it was just the moment the interest rate changed.

She hadn't told anyone. Two days. Two days of watching Hazel check the radio for tampering, of watching suspicion move around the dinner table after the transmission nobody could explain, of watching Marguerite look at each of them in turn with those cartographer's eyes that measured everything, and knowing—if not the answer, then at least a question that fit the shape of the hole.

Because here was the thing she'd figured out on the second night, lying awake in her bunk listening to the compressor cycle: the radio had transmitted during the silence window, and she hadn't done it, and she'd nearly confessed anyway, because guilt and innocence feel identical when you're the one carrying a secret. That was how she'd learned what secrets cost. They didn't weigh anything until you tried to put them down.

* * *

Tomas convulsed at 16:05.

It was small, as convulsions went—a stiffening, a rattle in the tank, his head tipping toward the steel rim—and June was across the hold before her mind caught up with her body, both hands cradling his skull away from the metal, holding him steady while his jaw clenched and his eyes rolled white. Eleven seconds. It felt like a season. Then he sagged against her arms, breathing hard, conscious, embarrassed.

"Chamber's too shallow," he rasped. "Need deeper table. Can't—can't build one. Out of steel, out of time." He laughed, a dry cracked sound. "Tell Hazel I'm fine. Tell him I'm fine even though I'm not, because if he comes down here and looks at me with that face he makes, I'll start believing I'm dying, and I don't have time to die. I have tables to recalculate."

June held his head against her shoulder until his breathing steadied, and something about the weight of him—all that brilliance reduced to something she could carry—broke the last seal on her.

She found Hazel on deck at dusk, coiling line in the dark by touch, because Hazel worked with his hands when his mind was chewing something it couldn't swallow. The valley stood black against a sky going the color of a bruise healing. Somewhere downstream, a patrol boat's running lights moved with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world.

"Hazel."

He didn't stop coiling. "Yeah."

"I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you say anything, because if you interrupt me I won't finish, and if I don't finish I'll carry it until it kills me, and I've seen what carrying things does to you. You're the proof it works. You're also the proof it doesn't."

The coiling stopped.

So she told him. All of it. Deverell and the linen jacket and the offer, the debts and the threats against Nia, the pen held out like communion, and then the part that mattered—the part she'd rehearsed twenty times in her bunk and still couldn't say smoothly: *"I told him no. I want you to understand that I'm not confessing because I did it. I'm telling you because I almost did, and because someone on this boat signaled the radio and it wasn't me, and I need you to know that I refused, and I need you to know that refusing cost me everything I've spent six years building, which was nothing, which was less than nothing, and somehow it was still the hardest money I never spent."*

Her voice cracked on the last of it. She stood in the dark with her hands empty, waiting for the verdict.

Hazel was quiet for a long time. Long enough for her to catalog every way this could end: thrown off the boat at the narrows, tied in the hold, the cold polite distance that was worse than anger, the crew learning to eat meals without her the way they'd already learned to eat meals without trust.

"You refused," he said finally.

"I refused."

"With everything he was offering. With Nia in it. You looked at a clean slate and your sister's safety and you said no."

"Yes."

He turned then, and in the low light from the hatch his face wasn't angry or soft. It was something she'd never seen on it before—something like recognition, like a man finding a door in a wall he'd thought was solid.

"Do you know what I was doing when Elias Vann handed me that map?" he said. "I was stealing cargo from a wreck the navy pretends isn't there. My whole life is a series of deals like yours. Smaller. Dirtier. Every one of them a yes I shouldn't have said." He shook his head slowly. "You had the chance to buy your way out free and clear, and you paid instead. Nobody pays, June. People talk about paying, they mean to pay, and then the pen's in their hand and their hand moves. Yours didn't."

"The radio—"

"Wasn't you. I believe you. Which means either Locke has equipment we haven't seen, or—" and here his voice dropped into something grim and thoughtful, "—or there's a fifth party on this river we haven't met yet. But that's tomorrow's problem. Tonight's problem is that you stood in front of a man who owned you and you didn't sign, and then you carried it alone for two days because you thought it would poison us." He stepped closer. "It would have. Not the deal. The carrying."

June felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since Port Anselm, since before Port Anselm, maybe since the bank took the house—some knot she'd assumed was structural, load-bearing, permanent. It didn't vanish. Shame didn't work that way. But it changed shape, the way ore changes shape when you finally smelt it, and what was left was lighter and harder and hers.

"Nia," she said. "If Deverell moves on her because I refused—"

"Then we get her out. After. That's a promise I can keep, because unlike some promises, mine don't come with fine print." He picked the coil of line back up, and the conversation was over in the way Hazel ended things—completely, without ceremony, the door closed and bolted. "Get some food. Real food, you've been skipping your share to feed the rest of us. And June—"

"Yeah?"

"When we're out of this valley, remind me to tell you what I promised a dying man. Fair's fair."

She almost smiled. "Deal."

* * *

She was halfway down the ladder with two mugs of the reconstituted stew they'd all stopped complaining about when Marguerite's voice came down from the wheelhouse, sharp enough to cut:

"June. Hazel. Get up here. Now."

June took the ladder fast. In the wheelhouse, Marguerite stood over the chart table with the concession permit flattened under her palms, and her face had gone the particular white of someone who has just read fine print that reads back.

Hazel arrived behind June, took one look, and said, "How bad?"

"We have nine days," Marguerite said. "Maybe fewer. The permit voids unexplored claims at expiry unless they're physically documented on site—surveys, seals, original instruments. Everything we've recovered so far? Legally worthless the moment that clock runs out. And the clock—" she turned the permit toward them, her finger on a clause near the bottom, "—runs on *filing*, not on our return. The concession office counts days from issuance, not from discovery. We burned eighty-one getting here."

Outside, far down the black valley, the patrol boat's lights swung deliberately, patiently, toward the Kestrel.

"And there's more," Marguerite added quietly. "Those lights aren't coming to board us. They're coming to make sure we don't leave. Someone just converted our nine days into a siege."

Down in the hold, faint through the deck plates, they could hear Tomas's voice, weak and stubborn, recalculating tables for a dive none of them had agreed to yet.
