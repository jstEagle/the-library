## 17. The Trap in the Narrows

The fog came off the water at first light like a held breath finally released, and Hazel Almeida stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* and did not trust it.

Fog was cover, yes. Fog was also the thing that let patrol boats get close enough to touch you before you saw their running lights. He'd learned that years ago, working wrecks inside the exclusion zone, when the navy's launches would materialize out of nothing with their engines idling and their deck guns manned by boys too young to shave. The fog gave, and the fog took. You never got to choose which.

"Six miles to the narrows," June said from the chart table, her voice carefully flat. She'd been flat for two days now—ever since she'd told him about the collector, ever since she'd refused to sign. Hazel had decided that her flatness was the most honest sound on the boat. Everyone else had found something to do with their hands.

Tomas was below, still in the makeshift decompression rig they'd welded out of a fish hold and two surplus oxygen bottles, his ears popping as Marguerite adjusted the pressure in slow, careful increments. He'd taken the hit at forty fathoms, in the records office, and he'd come up the last thirty feet of stairwell half-conscious while Hazel hauled him by the collar of his suit through collapsing silt. He'd live. The rig would see to that, if the sea stayed quiet long enough to let it work.

The sea was not going to stay quiet.

"Contact," June said. She wasn't looking at the radar anymore; she was looking south, where the fog was thinning into rags. "Two of them. Coming up the channel line."

Hazel took the glasses. They were exactly what he'd expected and exactly what he'd dreaded: twin hulls, gray-green, low silhouettes, running without lights at a speed that said they weren't patrolling—they were converging. One held the channel center. The other was swinging wide to the east, patient as a closing hand.

"They've been waiting for us to leave," Hazel said. "Locke didn't need to chase us. He just needed to know which way we'd run."

Marguerite came up the companionway with Tomas's pressure log in one hand and her own fear written plainly across her face, because Marguerite had never learned to hide it and Hazel had stopped wishing she would. "How long?"

"Twenty minutes before they're alongside. Less if they've got a helicopter warm somewhere." He lowered the glasses. "They'll board us. They'll cite something. And Tomas comes off that rig the moment they put boots on our deck, because no inspector in the world lets a pressurized fish hold go uninspected."

Silence, except for the engines and the sea and, faintly, the hiss of the regulator feeding Tomas his air.

"So we don't let them alongside," June said.

"There's nowhere to go." Marguerite spread the chart with hands that wanted to shake and didn't. "The valley's behind us. Open water ahead is theirs. North is—" She stopped. Her finger had stopped too, on the place every chartmaker feared and every local pilot whispered about: the narrows where the drowned river met the sea, a quarter mile of rock and current where the tide poured through twice a day like water through a broken dam.

"No," June said quietly.

"Flood tide starts in forty minutes," Hazel said. "It runs nine knots through there. Maybe ten with yesterday's swell backing it up."

"It runs boats onto the Teeth," June said. "That's what the fishermen call them. The rocks. Nobody runs the narrows on flood. Nobody."

"Nobody who has another choice." Hazel turned the wheel a few degrees, feeling the old girl answer, feeling the sixty years of welded steel under his hands like a living thing. "Locke knows this coast. He knows we know it. He's expecting us to hug the shoreline, run for neutral water, maybe make a break for the fishing fleet and hide among honest boats. So that's what he's positioned for. What he isn't positioned for is a salvage tub doing something that should kill everyone aboard."

"You don't know that it won't," Marguerite said. It wasn't an accusation. It was arithmetic, and she was giving it to him straight because that was the only way she knew how to love people.

"I know the narrows have a channel down the west side. Elias marked it." He tapped the dying man's map, folded soft as cloth from handling, riding in his breast pocket where it had ridden since a coughing stranger bought his promise with his last night alive. "Elias ran timber out of this river before they drowned it. He knew these rocks before the water covered half of them. If anyone ever drew me a true map, it was a man drawing the river that killed him slowly."

"And if he drew it wrong?" June asked.

"Then we die fast instead of slow, and Locke gets his archive anyway, and none of it matters." Hazel looked at each of them in turn—the engineer's wife-widow steadiness of June, the cartographer's terrible clarity, the rig hissing below decks holding the man who'd followed him past safe depth because Hazel had asked. "I'm not going to dress this up. I can't order anyone. This isn't a navy. But I'm going to run the narrows, and I'd rather do it with all of you lashed down and hating me than with any of you on that patrol boat's deck answering questions about what we found."

* * *

They spent fifteen minutes preparing to almost die, and it was the calmest fifteen minutes of Hazel's life, because there is a particular peace in a decision already made.

June went below and lashed Tomas into the rig frame with cargo straps, double-checked the regulator feed, zip-tied every loose bottle, and wedged padding around his head so the impacts wouldn't crack his skull against the steel. Tomas, drifting near the surface of consciousness, looked up at her and said, "Is it bad?"

"It's fine," June said.

"Liar," Tomas said fondly, and closed his eyes.

Marguerite stripped the archive to its waterproof core—three sealed tubes, the originals, the things three governments would eventually want—and strapped them to her own body under her foul-weather gear, because she had announced, in the tone of a woman settling a mathematical proof, that documents survived better when attached to the person most motivated to keep them dry. Then she went forward and secured everything that could become a projectile, which on a boat taking whitewater meant nearly everything.

Hazel stood alone at the wheel and studied the water.

The fog was burning off in long banners. Through the gaps he could see the eastern patrol boat settling into its blocking station, a mile off, contemptuous in its patience. The western one held the channel mouth. Between them and the narrows lay four miles of open gray water that the *Kestrel* would have to cross in full view, engines hammering, committing to the run long before she reached it. There would be no pretending. There would be no feint. Every soul on both those patrol boats would watch them do it.

He thought about Elias Vann, then. Not the dying man in the bar, but the diver whose logbook Hazel had read by lamplight across three sleepless nights—a man who had run this same river with a load of cedar, who had written in a careful schoolteacher's hand: *The narrows forgive no one, but they favor the committed. Hesitation is what puts you on the rocks. Pick your line and love it.*

Pick your line and love it.

Hazel picked his.

* * *

He brought the *Kestrel* around at 0940, directly across the western patrol boat's bow, close enough that he could see an officer's binoculars flash in the morning sun, and he rang for full ahead.

For ninety seconds nothing happened. The gray hull simply sat there, and Hazel understood with cold clarity that they genuinely did not believe it. No one ran the narrows on flood tide. The maneuver existed in accident reports and funeral sermons, not in flight plans. He watched the patrol boat hesitate—he actually watched it, saw its wake curve as its captain second-guessed—and in that hesitation was the whole margin of their lives.

Then the siren started, and the hull came alive, and the race was real.

The narrows opened ahead like a wound in the coastline: black rock walls rising sheer on either side, the gap between them boiling white even at this distance, the flood tide pouring seaward against the morning swell and stacking the water into standing waves that Hazel could hear before he could see—a deep, rolling thunder, like the sea clearing its throat.

"Elias's line," Marguerite called from the bow, map open against the wind, one arm hooked through a lifeline. "West wall. There's a submerged shelf—he calls it the Step—forty yards in. We pass north of it or we lose our keel."

"North of the Step," Hazel repeated, and put the words into the wheel like nails.

The first wave hit them abeam at the mouth of the gap and threw the *Kestrel* sideways six feet. June, lashed to the chart table, heard the hull groan along its whole length—an old ship's voice, complaining, not breaking. Water came over the rail in a single green sheet and found every seam they hadn't sealed. Behind him, below him, he heard the decompression rig's frame take the shock and hold, and he loved Tomas Reyes in that moment with the pure, uncomplicated love of a man for someone else's luck.

Then the walls were on either side of them and the world became noise.

It was not navigation anymore. It was not seamanship, not really. It was a conversation between Hazel and a river that had been drowned for forty years and remembered everything. The water through the narrows moved in coils—you could see them, great rotating ropes of current, standing waves marching upstream against the flood—and between the coils lay seams of smoother water, invisible from shore, visible only to someone reading the surface the way Elias had taught him to read a wreck: not looking at it, but through it.

"Rock! Rock dead ahead!" June's voice, cracked and certain.

"I see it." He didn't, quite—but he saw the water over it, the way the current stood up and combed back in a feather, and he put the bow three feet to port of the feather and felt the *Kestrel* slide past whatever waited beneath with less than a boat-length to spare. Marguerite made a sound that was not a word.

The Step came next. Forty yards of submerged shelf, Elias's pencil lines showed, dropping away on its north side into deep green water. Hazel ran the edge of it so close that the turbulence off the shelf grabbed the stern and swung them, and for one full second the *Kestrel* was broadside to the current in the worst water on the coast, and every person aboard understood simultaneously and silently that this was the moment the sea would decide.

The sea decided in their favor. The stern caught the seam, the bow swung true, and they were through—through the Step, through the standing waves, through the thunder—and the narrows spat them out the far side into calm water with the gentleness of a door closing behind them.

Hazel realized he had been holding his breath since the mouth of the gap. He let it out in a long shudder and discovered his hands were welded to the wheel and would not immediately release it.

Behind them, framed in the gap, the lead patrol boat had stopped dead at the entrance. He could see it plainly: sitting nose-on to the current, wallowing, its captain staring at whitewater that his vessel was built to police, not survive. The second boat was coming around wide, but wide meant time, and time meant the *Kestrel* was already a rumor.

June came up on deck, soaked, shaking, laughing in a way that had tears in it. Marguerite sat down hard on the hatch coaming with the map still clutched in her fist, and said, to no one, "He drew it right. The mad old bastard drew it right."

Below, faintly, through the deck plates, they heard Tomas's voice, groggy and indignant: "Did we win?"

* * *

They ran north for two hours before Hazel allowed himself to believe it, and even then he kept the engine at full tilt until the fuel gauge made the argument for mercy. They anchored at dusk in a cove no chart named, ringed by pines, deep enough to hide a hull, quiet enough to hear the rig breathing below.

Hazel stood in the cockpit with a mug of coffee he wasn't drinking and watched the last light go off the water.

June climbed out and joined him, leaning on the rail beside him, shoulder almost touching his. For a long time neither of them spoke. That was the language they'd settled into, the two of them—silence with weight in it.

"You ran us at a rock formation based on a dead man's pencil," she said finally.

"Yes."

"And you didn't tell us the odds."

"The odds were the same whether you knew them or not. Knowing them would only have changed how you spent the last twenty minutes."

She considered that. "Next time," she said, "I want the odds. Even if they're terrible. Especially if they're terrible." She looked at him, and her eyes were fierce and wet in the dark. "We're not cargo, Hazel. You don't protect us by deciding for us. You protect us by trusting us with the truth. That's the whole job. That's what makes us a crew instead of passengers."

Hazel looked out at the black water and felt the truth of it land somewhere under his ribs, where promises lived. He thought of Elias again—*bring them home, tell no one*—and understood, with sudden and uncomfortable clarity, that he had been keeping the second half of the promise very well and the first half only by luck, and that luck was not a promise kept.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we talk. All of us. About what we owe the dead, and what we owe each other, and what we do with what's strapped to Marguerite's ribs. No more deciding alone. Any of us."

June nodded once, satisfied, and went below.

Hazel stayed at the rail a while longer. Far to the south, beyond the headland, a light blinked—once, twice, deliberate. Not a fishing boat. Fishing boats didn't signal in patterns.

Someone knew which cove they'd chosen after all.

He finished the cold coffee, and went down to wake the others.
