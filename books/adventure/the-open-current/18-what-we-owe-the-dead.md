## 18. What We Oke the Dead

The cove had no name on any chart Marguerite owned, which was precisely why Hazel had chosen it. The Kestrel sat at anchor in a bowl of green water ringed by black basalt walls, rain coming down in ropes, the kind of storm that made even the sea seem to want them hidden. Wind screamed through the gap where they'd entered and flattened the pines on the ridgeline until they looked like a crowd bowing.

Marguerite stood at the chart table with the archive spread out before her—three days of drying, of pressing flat between boards, of Tomas's careful hands lifting each page with tweezers—and felt the weight of it differently than she had underwater. Down there, among the drowned rooftops, it had been history. Up here, dry and legible, it was a weapon.

The municipal ledger lay open to the final entry. She'd read it eleven times. *Relocation complete. Valley sealed per directive 44-C. No further correspondence to be retained.* A bureaucrat's tidy hand closing over four hundred lives like a lid.

Tomas slept in the forward berth, still shallow-breathed from the hit he'd taken at depth, his skin mottled where the nitrogen had bloomed under it like bruised fruit. June sat across from her, arms folded, watching the rain. Hazel stood at the aft window with his back to all of them, watching the entrance to the cove as if the water itself might report their position.

"We can't stay here past tomorrow," June said. "Supplies won't hold, and if that storm shifts north, we're pinned against the wall."

"Then tonight," Marguerite said. "We decide tonight."

Hazel turned. He didn't ask what they were deciding. None of them needed it said aloud. It had been hanging in the cabin since the narrows, since they'd outrun the patrol boats and come here to lick their wounds: what do we do with it?

Marguerite laid her palm flat on the ledger. "Three options. We sell it to Locke. We sink it somewhere no one will ever look. Or we put it into the world."

"And the fourth option," June said quietly, "is we burn it and pretend we never came."

"That's not an option," Hazel said.

"It's the only option where we all live," June shot back. "You ran the narrows at flood tide, Hazel. You nearly killed us doing it. That was to get away from one man with one helicopter. What happens when this goes public? When it isn't Locke anymore but the whole institution behind him?"

"June—" Marguerite started.

"No." June unfolded herself and stood, and for once she didn't look like someone doing arithmetic about survival. She looked furious. "I've been quiet long enough. I owe money to people who break fingers as a courtesy call. I signed onto this job because it paid and because Hazel seemed like a man who kept his head down. Now I'm sitting in a cove with documents that could end careers, end *programs*, maybe end us—and everyone keeps talking like courage is free."

Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it; Marguerite could see her hating it.

"I'm not saying bury it because I'm a coward," June went on, softer. "I'm saying bury it because I've done the math. Sell it, and Locke owns us forever. Publish, and we're dead or famous, and famous is just dead with better lighting. Bury it, and we walk away whole. Tired, broke, hunted a little longer—but whole."

"Tomas almost died for those pages," Marguerite said.

"Tomas almost died because we pushed past safe depth on a clock Elias Vann set ticking ninety days ago," June said. "That's not a reason. That's a cost."

* * *

They woke Tomas for it, because it was his archive too—he'd spent two years rebuilding engines with salvaged parts and dreaming of work that mattered, and Marguerite wasn't going to let him sleep through the argument that decided whether any of it had meant anything.

He came aft wrapped in a blanket, moving carefully, his body still negotiating with the pressure it had carried. He lowered himself onto the bench beside June and looked at the ledger for a long moment without touching it.

"I read the last entry while you were sleeping," he said. "Directive 44-C." He laughed, a small broken sound. "I grew up forty miles downstream of that valley. My grandfather fished the reservoir. He used to say the water tasted wrong, like something underneath it. Everyone said he was crazy." He looked up. "There are probably people alive today who remember being loaded onto trucks. Who were told the dam was progress. And the government knew—knew—the ground under that town was failing, knew their own survey flagged it, and instead of admitting the mistake they drowned the evidence and called it planning."

"So you publish," June said.

"So I don't get to choose," Tomas said. "That's the thing none of us wants to say. Whatever we decide, we decide together, or it doesn't hold. One person leaks, one person sells, one person burns it, and the rest are just along for whatever comes." He glanced at Hazel. "You're the one who took the promise. So talk."

* * *

Hazel had been silent so long Marguerite had begun to think he wouldn't speak at all. Now he crossed the cabin, pulled the bench out from under the chart table, and sat down heavily, like a man setting down cargo he'd been carrying too far.

"Elias Vann," he said. "You've heard me say the name twice. Once when I took this contract, once when Locke asked me whose permit we were working under. You've never asked me more than that, and I've never offered. That ends tonight, because you deserve to know what you're carrying."

He told it plainly, without ornament, the way divers tell things. An old prospector coughing blood into a bar rag in a dockside room that smelled of diesel and rain. A hand-drawn map of a river valley that official charts insisted was open farmland. A concession permit expiring in ninety days. And a bargain: take an expedition there, bring them home, tell no one what they find.

"He was dying," Hazel said. "Cancer, lungs, the kind you earn. He'd found the valley thirty years ago, dove it alone, saw enough to understand what it was. And he spent thirty years deciding what to do about it. He told me he'd been waiting for the right kind of coward—a man careful enough to bring a crew back alive and stubborn enough not to ask questions until it was time." His mouth twisted. "His words. He said the world was full of brave men who got everyone killed and honest men who sold everything. He wanted something rarer."

"What did he want?" Marguerite asked, though she already knew.

"He wanted it brought back. All of it. Documented, dated, witnessed—so it couldn't be dismissed as one dead prospector's fantasy. And then he wanted it buried again. Sealed. Because he'd watched what happened the last time someone tried to surface it. There was a journalist, decades back. Elias never gave me the name. He said the man published three paragraphs and then stopped publishing anything, ever, and the story died and the valley stayed drowned and Elias decided some truths cost more than they pay."

June exhaled slowly. "So the promise is silence."

"The promise was silence," Hazel agreed. "Bring them home. Tell no one." He looked around the table—at June with her debts, at Tomas with his ruined grandfather's reservoir, at Marguerite with whatever she was still hiding behind her cartographer's calm. "And I've been sitting with that promise for three months, and I have to tell you the truth, because you've earned the truth even if it costs me everything else."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere above them, wind tore a limb off one of the pines and threw it into the water.

"I intend to keep half of it," Hazel said. "I'll bring every one of you home. That part is iron. But I'm not going to sit on this. Not sell it—not trade it to Locke for salvage rights and quiet money, though God knows I thought about it the first night after he made his offer. And not bury it, either. I'm going to give it away. Anonymously. Copies to people who know how to make institutions bleed. No names, no claim, no credit. We lose the permit, the salvage rights, the Kestrel if it comes to that. We lose my invisibility, which was the only thing I ever really owned." He spread his hands. "But the town gets its name back. Tomas's grandfather gets to be right. And nobody has to die for it, because there's nothing left to buy and no one left to blame."

Silence. Marguerite realized her hands were shaking against the ledger and pressed them flat.

"You said copies," Tomas said carefully. "Which means originals."

"Originals go somewhere no order will ever reach," Hazel said. "Split. Three countries. Even I won't know all three locations by the end."

"That's why you asked me about archival deposit protocols last week," Marguerite said. "In the galley. You thought I didn't notice."

"I hoped you didn't."

She almost laughed. Almost. Instead she heard herself say, "Then I should tell you what I've been hiding, since apparently tonight is confessions."

* * *

They all turned to her, and Marguerite understood that this was the moment she'd been dreading since the dock, since the day she'd recognized the coordinates on Elias Vann's map and lied about why.

"My father worked for the water authority," she said. "Regional surveys. In nineteen sixty-one he was assigned to a project he wasn't cleared to fully understand—bathymetric mapping of a reservoir that shouldn't have existed yet. He came home one week with a folder he was supposed to destroy and didn't. Hand-drawn soundings of structures under the water. A street grid. A church footprint. He showed me when I was nineteen, before he died, and told me he'd spent his whole career knowing and saying nothing, and that it had eaten him alive from the inside. He made me promise never to go looking." Her voice steadied. "When Hazel showed me those coordinates, I recognized them from my father's soundings before I'd finished reading the latitude. And I said yes to this expedition because I needed to see it. And I've been lying to all of you about that ever since, because I told myself it didn't matter why I came."

"It matters," June said—but not harshly. Almost gently.

"It matters," Marguerite agreed. "Because I came for my father's ghost, and I stayed for you. And because my father's folder is in a bank box in another country, and it corroborates the archive—survey data the government claimed never existed. If we publish, it publishes. I've already photographed it. I did it the night after the deep dive, while you were all asleep, because I'd already decided." She met Hazel's eyes. "I was going to tell you at the handover. I'm telling you now, because if we're choosing each other over safety, then we do it with everything on the table."

Hazel held her gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded, slow, like a man accepting a depth he'd already committed to descending.

"Anyone else?" he asked. "Last chance. Anything anyone's carrying that touches this?"

June looked down at her hands. "The collector," she said. "Back at the supply stop. He offered to clear everything—all of it, every debt—if I reported our findings to his people. I told him no. I told Hazel the next morning. But I never told you two, and I need you to hear it from me: I refused. Not because I'm noble. Because when he said 'the findings,' I thought about Tomas on the deck turning gray, and I thought about that ledger, and I couldn't be the person who priced it." She swallowed. "He knows roughly where we are. That's part of why I wanted to bury everything. Not fear of Locke. Fear of what comes out of the woodwork when there's money in it."

"There's always money in it," Hazel said. "That's why we give it away. You can't ransom what's already free."

* * *

The vote, when it finally came, wasn't a vote at all. Hazel refused to call it one. "Nobody raises hands," he said. "Everybody just says whether they're in. Out loud. And if anyone says no, we bury it, and I keep my whole promise, and none of you ever have to know how close it came."

Tomas spoke first. "My grandfather fished poisoned water his whole life and died thinking he was crazy. I'm in."

June stared at the rain for a long time. Marguerite watched her do the math one more time—the survival math that had kept her alive through debt and worse—and watched her set it down.

"If we do this," June said, "we do it clean. No half-measures, no keeping a copy back to sell later, no insurance policies. All of it out, or none of it. And Hazel—" she pointed at him, finger steady for the first time all night, "—you bring us home. That's the deal. That's the iron. Everything else is weather."

"That's the deal," Hazel said.

"Then I'm in."

They all looked at Marguerite. She thought of her father's hands on that forbidden folder, of the way he'd whispered *say nothing* like a prayer he'd already broken. She thought of the town under the green water, its bell still hanging in the church tower where Tomas had touched it, patient as stone.

"My father waited his whole life for someone to finish what he started," she said. "I'm in."

Hazel nodded once. Then he reached across the table, gathered the ledger, the directives, the coerced-relocation manifests, the engineering failure reports the government had blamed on a fault line and a flood season—and began, methodically, to divide them into three stacks.

"One thing I haven't told you," he said, not looking up. "Locke expects an answer. I told him I'd meet him. Day after tomorrow, neutral harbor, one diver, one case. He thinks he's buying the archive."

June went still. "You're not giving it to him."

"I'm giving him exactly what he paid for," Hazel said, and there was something in his face Marguerite had never seen there before—not the careful blankness of a man surviving, but the cold bright edge of a man who had finally chosen what he was willing to lose. "Scans. Beautiful ones. Every page, every seal, every signature—altered just enough that only someone who already knows the truth would catch it. Let him lock them in a vault and feel safe. By the time anyone opens that vault, the real archive will be speaking in three languages on three continents."

"And if he checks them before he pays?" Tomas asked.

"Then we run the narrows again," Hazel said. "At flood tide."

Outside, the storm began, very slowly, to turn.
