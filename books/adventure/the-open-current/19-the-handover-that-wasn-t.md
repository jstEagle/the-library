## 19. The Handover That Wasn't

The meeting place was Locke's choice, which meant it was a place where Hazel would be alone and uncomfortable: the naval auxiliary pier at Port Callas, a slab of grey concrete jutting into the harbor like a broken tooth, ringed by chain-link and floodlights that burned even in daylight. A seaplane sat moored at the end of it, white and patient as a heron.

Hazel came in on the Kestrel's tender, alone, as agreed. He wore his good jacket—the one without salt stains—and under it, against his ribs, a waterproof case holding two terabytes of scans. The real ones. Or what Locke would believe were the real ones.

In the wheelhouse locker back aboard the Kestrel, wrapped in oilcloth inside a tool case Tomas had stripped to bare metal, sat the originals. And in a compartment forty fathoms down on a wreck the navy pretended did not exist, in a sealed space no manifest acknowledged, waited the copies that mattered most—deposited three nights ago by a man who knew that wreck's bones better than anyone alive.

That was the beauty of it. That was the joke only Hazel was in on. Commander Locke wanted the archive hidden where no one could ever find it? Fine. Hazel had hidden it exactly there.

* * *

Locke was waiting at a folding table someone had set up under the floodlight gantry, out of the wind. No uniform jacket—just a khaki shirt with the sleeves rolled, a man pretending to be informal the way other men pretended to be armed. Two aides stood well back near the seaplane, close enough to see, too far to hear. On the table: a laptop, a document wallet, a thermos, and two enamel mugs. Coffee, offered like a courtesy. Everything about the setup said *this is not an arrest*. Everything about the location said *it could become one*.

"Hazel Almeida." Locke didn't stand. "You look better than your boat does."

"The Kestrel's older than both of us. She's earned her scars."

"Sit."

Hazel sat. The wind came off the harbor carrying diesel and brine, and somewhere behind the fence a crane groaned through its arc, loading something nobody was supposed to ask about onto a grey-hulled ship. He'd spent thirty years around institutions like this one. He knew their grammar. This table was a sentence being constructed, and every word of it was conditional.

"You have something for me," Locke said.

"I have copies. As discussed."

"And I have something for you." Locke slid the document wallet across the table but kept two fingers on it, the way a card player holds a hand he hasn't shown. "Inside: formal withdrawal of the customs violations against your engineer. Discharge of the harbor authority complaint against your deckhand. Both stamped this morning. Plus—" he tapped the wallet once— "a salvage concession extension. Twelve months, the valley grid, exclusive. Legitimate paper. You could work that water for a decade and retire rich."

Hazel looked at the wallet and did not touch it. "And all you want is the archive."

"I want the risk off the water. Those documents describe infrastructure failures in a decommissioned project. They're engineering records with security classifications still attached. In the wrong hands they're a hazard. In mine they're filed correctly." Locke poured coffee into both mugs, unhurried. "You're not a political man, Almeida. I've read your file. Twenty-two years of diving, zero arrests, zero politics. You salvage things. You don't know what they mean and you don't care. That's not an insult. It's why we're sitting here instead of you sitting in a cell."

"The charges were never real anyway," Hazel said. "Tomas's customs paperwork was clean. June's debt is civil, not criminal. You manufactured both to get my crew's names on levers."

Something flickered behind Locke's eyes—not guilt, nothing so human. Recalculation. "Manufactured or not, they exist. Existence is what matters. Ask any lawyer worth his fee." He pushed the wallet forward another inch. "You give me the drive, I give you this, and everyone walks away richer and quieter. That's the whole transaction. I'm not asking you to understand anything. I'm asking you to stop carrying it."

There it was. Not *give me the truth*—Locke didn't care about truth; truth was the one cargo he was paid to sink. *Stop carrying it.* Set down the weight. Take the money and the paper and go back to being invisible.

Hazel thought about Elias Vann, coughing blood into a bar rag, pressing a grease-stained map across sticky wood. *Bring them home. Tell no one what it finds.* Half a promise kept so far. The other half wasn't Locke's to buy, because it had never been Hazel's to sell.

He reached out and took the coffee instead of the wallet. Drank it. It was terrible—naval coffee, boiled to tar—and he let the taste sit in his mouth while he thought about breathing.

* * *

Here is the diver's oldest skill, and it isn't courage. Courage is for people who can afford drama. The oldest skill is breath control: knowing how long you can hold a lie the way you hold air, feeling the burn start, reading your own body's tells before they reach your face, and surfacing calm when everything underneath is screaming for release. Every con man knows it. Every poker player. But divers learn it in a harder school, because underwater the lie is to yourself—you tell your body there's enough air, there's time enough, the surface is close enough—and the body believes you right up until it doesn't, and then you're dead. So you learn precision. You learn exactly how much falsehood a system can carry.

Hazel held his breath now, easy, shallow, economical.

"Before I hand anything over," he said, "I want to see the discharge orders. Not the folder. The actual signatures."

Locke turned the wallet open. Typed letters, official crests, a signature block for the customs directorate, another for the harbor authority. Hazel read slowly, the way he read gauges—looking for the number that didn't match. The dates were yesterday. The stamps were fresh. It was either genuine or an excellent forgery, and after twenty-two years of navy-adjacent salvage he genuinely could not tell the difference, which told him everything about the institution and nothing about the paper.

"They're real," Locke said, watching him read. "I don't forge what I can simply order."

"That's almost comforting."

"It's meant to be." Locke leaned back. "The drive."

Hazel set the waterproof case on the table between them. Grey polymer, dive-rated, scuffed from a hundred descents. He did not slide it over. He rested his hand flat on top of it, and Locke's eyes went to the hand, and for a moment neither man moved, and the crane behind the fence groaned again like something ancient turning over in its sleep.

"Conditions," Hazel said.

"You're not in a position—"

"Everyone's always telling me what position I'm in. Indulge me." His voice stayed level, conversational, the voice of a man discussing tide tables. "One: these orders execute today, before I leave this pier. I watch the confirmations go out. Two: the Kestrel and everyone aboard are named nowhere in any file, current or future. Not flagged, not watched, not 'monitored.' If I hear her name in a briefing room again, whatever you received today turns out to be incomplete. Three: you never come upstream again."

Locke studied him for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the fence a gull screamed and got no answer. "You're negotiating like a man with leverage."

"I'm negotiating like a man who's already decided what happens if you say no. There's no version of this where you get everything and I get nothing. You know that. It's why there's coffee instead of handcuffs."

"And if I refuse your conditions?"

"Then I stand up, I walk back to my tender, and you spend the next six months finding out what I did with the rest of it. Because this—" he lifted his hand off the case— "is not all of it. You know that too. Your sonar people mapped our anchorage. They know we spent nine days in that valley. Nine days, Commander. Nobody needs nine days to copy a hard drive."

It was a gamble and he made it sound like arithmetic. In truth the bluff had a hairline crack in it—if Locke's people had any sense of the timeline, they might guess the archive had been moved, and moved where? But Hazel had spent three nights making the answer to that question true. The originals' twins—the full, unaltered mirror set—sat in the sealed compartment of the unacknowledged wreck, forty fathoms down, in the one place on the coast the navy would never search because searching it meant admitting it existed. Elias's map hadn't just given Hazel a valley. It had taught him the principle: the best hiding place for a thing is inside the institution's own blind spot. The navy had sunk a town to bury its shame. The navy had sealed a wreck to bury its manifest. Very well. Their secrets would keep company with theirs.

Locke picked up the case. Weighed it in his hand like a man judging a fish by feel. Plugged a reader into his laptop, and the screen filled with directory trees—thousands of scanned pages, the municipal seal, the relocation orders, the engineering failure reports, Marguerite's translations, the photographs of the flooded church with its bell still hanging. All of it genuine. All of it real.

All of it subtly wrong.

Marguerite had done the surgery, and she'd done it the way she did everything—with a cartographer's patience. Page numbering offset by a fraction. Three critical relocation orders re-dated, shifted eleven days, enough to break the causal chain between the failure and the evacuation order if anyone tried to reconstruct events from these files alone. The signature on the key directive altered by a single letter—a transcription artifact, she'd called it, the kind of corruption scanning introduces. Nothing missing. Nothing obviously false. Just a record that, if it ever surfaced independently, would quietly fail to convict anyone—while the originals, scattered across three countries in Marguerite's careful dead-drop architecture, remained whole.

If Locke verified deeply, he'd find the flaws. But verification took forensic historians, and forensic historians asked questions, and questions were the one thing Commander Locke could not afford. He needed this transaction closed more than Hazel needed it. That was the actual leverage, and Hazel had understood it only when Marguerite said it aloud in the cove, storm hammering the canvas overhead: *"He's not buying the documents. He's buying the end of the story. Give him an ending."*

The transfer bar crawled. Ninety percent. Hazel drank his terrible coffee and breathed.

"Confirmation of the discharges," Locke said, when the drive was copied and pulled. He typed, sent, and turned the screen so Hazel could watch two receipts land. "Done. Your engineer and your deckhand are clean as of fourteen-twelve today."

"And the monitoring?"

"The Kestrel is a rusting salvage tug with a bad paint job. She'll be lucky if anyone remembers her name." Locke closed the laptop. For just a moment—half a second, less—the professional mask thinned, and Hazel saw the thing underneath, which was not anger and not defeat but a kind of weary respect, the look of one professional acknowledging another's craft. "You know I'll figure it out eventually. Whatever you did. Men like you always leave a seam."

"Men like you always assume the seam is where you'd look." Hazel stood. "Enjoy the filing, Commander."

"One question. Off the record." Locke didn't rise. "Vann. Why do it for a dying stranger? What did he have on you?"

Hazel paused at the edge of the floodlight's circle. The honest answer was *nothing*, and the truer answer was *everything*, and neither would fit in Locke's grammar.

"He trusted me," Hazel said. "Nobody had done that in years. Turns out it's load-bearing."

He walked to the tender without looking back. Behind him he heard the seaplane's engines spin up, heard the aides moving, heard Locke's voice giving some quiet instruction he was not meant to parse and did not try to. He cast off, ran the tender out past the breakwater, and only when the pier was a grey smudge did he let the breath go—all of it, the long-held lie leaving him in a shudder that bent him over the tiller.

* * *

The Kestrel lay at anchor in the outer roads, riding a lazy swell, gulls quarreling over her rail. By the time Hazel climbed aboard, the confirmations had already reached Tomas's phone, and June was standing at the wheelhouse door with her own screen in her hand, and her face was doing something complicated—relief and grief and disbelief fighting for the same square inch.

"It's gone," she said. "Both complaints. Withdrawn. It actually worked."

"It worked." Hazel stowed the empty case in the locker where the full one had lived, and felt the absence of its weight like a phantom limb. "Marguerite?"

"In her cabin. She hasn't slept. She keeps checking the drop schedules like they're a patient on monitors." June hesitated. "Hazel. The scans he got. They'll hold?"

"They'll hold long enough."

"Long enough for what?"

"For the story to be standing on its own feet before he notices it's wearing borrowed shoes." Hazel looked north, toward the coast, toward the drowned valley hidden behind its wall of official silence, and beyond it, further than any chart, toward the wreck on the bottom with its sealed compartment and its impossible cargo—his insurance, his inheritance, his half-kept promise sleeping in the dark. "Three days. Maybe four. Then it won't matter what he finds, because it'll be everywhere."

June searched his face. "And if he finds out sooner?"

Hazel thought of the narrows at flood tide, of running whitewater that should kill you because the alternative was certain death taken politely. He thought of Elias's hands on the bar, steadying the map.

"Then we run again," he said. "But this time, we run loud."

Below decks, faintly, he heard Marguerite's radio crackle to life—a scheduled check-in window, a coded acknowledgment from a journalist three time zones away. The first syllable of the ending Locke had bought and not received. Hazel put his hand flat against the wheelhouse bulkhead, feeling the old boat breathe beneath it, and waited for the current to turn.
