## 20. Expiry

**MARGUERITE**

The last day of the concession began, as all the worst days of Marguerite Chen's life had begun, with arithmetic.

She sat in the Kestrel's cramped chart room with the permit spread flat under a coffee mug, a grease pencil, and Tomas's cracked dive watch, and she did the math one more time. The permit expired at midnight, local time, at the registry office in Puerto Sombra. The Kestrel was forty-one hours of steaming from Puerto Sombra and had no intention of going anywhere near it.

Which was fine. Which was, in fact, the entire point.

International waters began at the two-hundred-mile line, and they'd crossed it at 0300 with every light on the ship burning like a carnival, because Hazel had wanted the navy's satellites to see exactly where they were and exactly how little they cared. Now they rode a long gray swell two hundred and six miles offshore, engines idling, and Marguerite opened the waterproof case she had carried against her body for nine days — through decompression stops and patrol-boat radar sweeps and one memorable argument about whether salted crackers constituted dinner — and took out the archive.

Not the originals. Those were already gone, divided three ways into oilskin and vacuum-sealed aluminum, sleeping in places only four people alive knew about. What remained was the documentation: eleven hundred photographs, the sonar overlays, the survey grids, the chemical assays of the concrete in the dam face, the sworn statements transcribed from June's dictation while Tomas breathed off a jury-rigged oxygen bottle and Hazel held his hand steady enough to write. Every page stamped with coordinates, dates, and the concession number that made it all, legally, real.

Unexplored claims void at expiry unless physically documented on site. Locke's lawyers had written that clause thinking it protected them. It also meant that if the documentation existed — complete, timestamped, filed — then the claim didn't void. It *vested*. And a vested claim on a drowned town full of government secrets was a lever you could pull from anywhere on earth.

They just had to file it before midnight.

Marguerite lifted the satellite phone, checked the charge for the fourth time, and dialed a number she had not dialed in six years.

It rang twice. "Chen." The voice was dry as old paper and just as flammable. "I told you never to call me again."

"You did, Devi."

"And yet."

"And yet." Marguerite looked out the porthole at the empty water, at nothing but sea and sky and the whole honest width of the world. "I have something you'll want. And I need it filed by midnight, your time, which gives you—"

"I know what time it is," Devi Raghunathan said. "I've been awake since your name surfaced in a maritime inquiry three weeks ago. I assumed you were dead or stupid."

"Both, probably."

"What is it?"

Marguerite closed her eyes. This was the moment. The point past which there was no surfacing, no anonymity, no quiet life cataloging charts for shipping firms and pretending the valley didn't live behind her eyelids every night. She thought of Elias Vann coughing blood into his hand in a dockside bar, trading a dead man's map for a living man's promise. She thought of Tomas's face under the nitrogen haze, asking her if he was dying, and her lying to him because the truth would have made him panic and panic kills divers. She thought of June standing in the rain refusing to sign.

"Devi," she said, "do you remember what you told me, the last time we spoke? That I was sitting on the biggest story of the decade and too much of a coward to open the box?"

A pause. Then, carefully: "I remember everything I say to people who disappoint me."

"Well." Marguerite smiled, and it hurt her face, because she hadn't used those muscles in years. "Open the box."

**JUNE**

June Okafor had spent her whole life learning what fear felt like when it came due, so she recognized this one immediately: the specific flavor of terror that arrives wearing excitement's clothes.

Her job was the upload. Devi couldn't come to them — a journalist flying to an unnamed vessel in international waters would be a confession in itself — so the files had to go out over the satlink in compressed packets, disguised inside routine weather telemetry, and June was the only one aboard who could keep the Kestrel's ancient uplink from choking on its own tongue. Tomas had built the masking protocol. June had to make it run.

She sat in the radio shack with the door dogged shut and the fan rattling and eleven hundred documents queued in a folder named STORMLOG_0447, and she pressed transmit at 0914 hours, and nothing happened.

The progress bar crawled. Stalled. Crawled.

"Don't," she whispered to the machine. "Don't you dare."

Every packet was a heartbeat. Weather data, weather data, weather data, and buried inside each one, a photograph of a church bell furred with sixty years of silt. A municipal seal struck before any record admitted the town existed. An engineering report, initialed by three men whose names now decorated a naval academy scholarship fund, concluding that the dam was safe, safe, safe, while the appendix — the appendix Hazel had found fused to the inside of a document tube — listed the hairline fractures, the seepage rates, the warnings ignored, and the memo deciding that relocation was cheaper than repair.

At 0931 the bar hit fifty percent and the link dropped.

June stared at the dead console and heard her own pulse. Somewhere out there, she knew, a patrol boat was probably reading their traffic, watching the burst transmissions, doing its own arithmetic. Locke had lost them in the narrows, but losing wasn't the same as forgiving, and men like Locke didn't forgive; they waited.

She re-established the handshake. Her hands were shaking so badly she missed the keys twice, and she laughed — actually laughed, alone in the shack, high and unhinged — because this was the same feeling as the night she'd signed for the first loan, the same vertigo, the same knowledge that she was betting money she didn't have on a version of herself she hadn't met yet.

Except this time the stake wasn't money. This time the stake was a town full of people who had been loaded onto trucks in the rain and told it was temporary.

The link caught. The bar climbed. At 1047 hours, the final packet cleared, and Devi's confirmation came back in flat text:

RECEIVED IN FULL. CHECKSUM VERIFIED. YOU ABSOLUTE LUNATIC. FILED WITH THREE SEPARATE REGISTRIES AND ONE VERY PATIENT EDITOR. MIDNIGHT DEADLINE MET WITH SIX HOURS TO SPARE. NOW TELL ME WHO HAZEL ALMEIDA IS.

June typed back: NOBODY. THAT'S THE POINT.

Then she went up on deck, into the wind, and told the others, and watched Hazel close his eyes like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it had edges.

**TOMAS**

Tomas Reyes spent the afternoon of the last day taking apart the decompression rig.

It seemed wrong, somehow, to leave it assembled. The thing had saved his life — a cargo hold converted into a pressure chamber, a compressor running backwards, himself sealed inside it for fourteen hours while June read him shipping forecasts through the hull and Hazel sat outside the viewport the entire time, not moving, not eating, just *there*, the way nobody had ever been there for Tomas in his life. But leaving it bolted down felt like admitting they might need it again, and Tomas had decided, somewhere in the nitrogen dreams, that they were done needing things like this.

He worked with a wrench and a bad shoulder, and he let himself think about the aluminum canisters stowed below.

Three countries. That had been Hazel's plan, laid out over instant coffee the night after the storm-bound cove, when they'd all voted — actually voted, hands raised in a rolling cabin, the most democratic act of treason in maritime history — to publish. The originals would never travel together again. Marguerite's half went into a diplomatic courier pouch belonging to a friend of a friend of a friend, bound for a consulate in Lisbon. June's half went into the ballast of a fishing boat crewed by women who owed June nothing and asked nothing, headed for a reef off West Africa where a dive shop owner named Adaeze would find them "lost cargo" and know exactly what to do. And Tomas's half —

Tomas's half was currently sewn into the lining of his own duffel bag, and tomorrow morning he would walk into the chaos of a container port in Cartagena carrying the truest documents in the hemisphere inside a bag with a broken zipper, and he was terrified, and he had never in his life been so certain he was doing the right thing.

That was the strange part. He kept waiting for the doubt. He was an engineer; doubt was his native language; he could worry the structural integrity of anything, including himself, down to the rivets. But there was no doubt. There was only the memory of the records office at depth, the filing cabinets standing in rows in the green dark like a congregation, and the way his light had swept across a child's drawing pinned to a wall — a house, a river, stick figures holding hands — laminated by someone who had believed, right up until the water came, that they were coming home.

You don't leave evidence like that to men like Locke. You scatter it like seed.

He torqued the last bolt free and the rig came apart into pieces that were just pieces, metal and hose and gauge, and he sat among them in the failing light and felt the exhaustion land on him all at once, the good kind, the kind that follows work that mattered.

From the deck above, June's voice came down the hatch: "Reyes. Get up here. Sunset. We're doing something stupid."

"We're always doing something stupid."

"This one's ceremonial."

**HAZEL**

Hazel Almeida stood at the bow as the sun went down on the ninetieth day, and he did not look at the water.

That was new. His whole life he had looked at the water — reading it, fearing it, bargaining with it, making his living out of what it swallowed and his silence out of why. Tonight he looked east instead, toward the coast he couldn't see, toward a registry office closing its drawers on a file that would sit there gathering dust until Devi's story detonated and some clerk went looking and found it, timestamped, vested, undeniable.

"Speech," June said. "Come on. Marguerite brought out the real rum. The bottle with the wax seal. This is a ceremony whether you like it or not."

"I don't do speeches."

"He doesn't do speeches," Tomas confirmed solemnly, accepting the rum.

So Hazel said the only thing there was to say. He took the bottle, drank, and passed it, and then he reached into his jacket and brought out the last thing left aboard from the valley: Elias Vann's map, soft as cloth now from handling, the drowned river drawn in a dead man's careful hand.

He held it over the rail.

Nobody spoke. They understood. Some things you keep and some things you release, and this — this belonged to the water, to the town, to the ninety days and the man who'd started them.

"Half my promise," Hazel said quietly. "Everyone came home. The rest—" He looked at each of them: June with her debts dissolved into something better than money, Tomas upright and scarred and grinning, Marguerite with her box finally open. "The rest belongs to everyone now."

He let go. The map turned once in the wind, white against the darkening water, and then the current took it, and the open current carried it wherever currents carry the things we finally stop gripping.

Marguerite touched his arm. "Devi's going to publish within the week. You understand that. After that, there's no anonymous. There's no invisible. Someone will connect the concession, the Kestrel, the four of us."

"I know."

"And you gave up the salvage claim. The wreck. Everything you were scraping out of that hull for six years."

"The navy's opening it themselves now," Hazel said. "Investigation site. Fences and floodlights and men in suits counting rivets." He almost smiled. "Besides — I already got everything out of that wreck that mattered. Took me twenty-two years to notice it wasn't cargo."

The rum came around again. The stars came out hard and clean, the way they only do far from any shore, and the Kestrel rocked on the long swell like something resting between jobs.

It was June who broke the quiet, because it was always June. She had her phone cupped in both hands, screen lighting her face, and her expression was doing something complicated.

"Hazel," she said. "You need to see this."

He took the phone. The news feed was already scrolling, already screaming — a wire dispatch, minutes old, datelined from a capital that had nothing to do with any of them:

**NAVY DENIES EXISTENCE OF FLOODED SETTLEMENT AS ARCHIVE DOCUMENTS SURFACE; COMMANDER LOCKE NAMED IN LEAKED SEIZURE ORDER; PARLIAMENTARY HEARINGS ANNOUNCED.**

And beneath it, updating live, a second headline, fresher than fresh:

**SALVAGE DIVER SOUGHT FOR QUESTIONING — AUTHORITIES IDENTIFY VESSEL KESTREL IN INTERNATIONAL WATERS.**

Hazel handed the phone back. Out in the dark, somewhere beyond the horizon, the world was waking up to a crime sixty years old, and the world was also, apparently, waking up to him.

"Well," he said, and this time he did smile, tired and unhidden, the first unhidden thing he'd felt in twenty-two years. "There goes the other half of the promise."

June looked at him. "What do we do?"

Hazel turned his face into the wind, toward the coast, toward whatever came next.

"We run with the tide," he said. "All the way home."
