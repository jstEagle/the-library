## 22. Open Current

The buoy said it all before Hazel's fins ever touched water.

It was navy surplus, repainted a municipal yellow, with a placard bolted to its collar: *Site 44-C — Active Investigation. Authorized Personnel Only.* The letters were fresh, the paint still sharp at the edges. Six months ago that wreck had been a rumor the harbor office denied existed. Now it had a name, a number, and a government sign.

Hazel hung on the mooring line at fifteen feet and let the current comb through him, reading the water the way other men read weather. Visibility ran eight meters, better than he'd ever known it here—the salvage boats were gone, the silt left undisturbed for a season. Below him the hull of the *Meridian* lay on her starboard side exactly as she'd lain for eleven years, but she wore new marks now: survey tags fluttering on lines, numbered stakes driven into the sand around her like a fence of white teeth.

He'd come at dawn on purpose. The investigation divers wouldn't start until mid-morning; their tender was tied up at the naval pier, visible across the bay as a smudge of gray. He had maybe two hours of being nobody in his own water.

He descended slowly, hands loose at his sides, not working. That was the difference, he realized somewhere around thirty feet. Every dive of his life had been work—cargo, salvage, survival, or a promise dragging him down by the wrist. This one was nothing. He was a man visiting an old argument.

The wreck opened beneath him, her plates furred with weed, the gash in her flank where the compartment used to be sealed now with a steel plate and a government lock. They'd found what he'd left there. Of course they had. He'd made sure they would—had left the originals arranged with a diver's neatness, wrapped in wax cloth, tagged with coordinates Elias Vann would have admired. A gift with a return address.

Hazel drifted along the deck, past the winch housing where he'd once cracked a knuckle to the bone, past the companionway where he'd surfaced with questions instead of cargo all those years ago. The questions had names now. They had hearings scheduled, subpoenaed logs, a commander in a witness room explaining why a river valley appeared on no chart and why forty-one families appeared in no census. Commander Locke had been suspended pending inquiry. Hazel had read the notice in a newspaper he'd bought with cash, standing in a bus shelter three towns over, feeling nothing he could have predicted—not triumph, not fear. Just a strange lightness, like surfacing too fast without the bends to show for it.

He found the spot where the manifest had lied to him. The hull plate was bare now, scraped clean by investigators' brushes, the ghost-welds exposed. He put his glove against the cold steel and held it there. Eleven years ago a younger Hazel had felt this same metal and decided, as he always did, that surviving meant not asking. He'd built a whole life out of that decision—a life of half-loads and unlogged dives and a barstool at the Salt Line where nobody knew his last name.

Elias Vann had walked into that bar dying and handed him a map, and the not-asking had ended forever.

"I kept half," Hazel said aloud. His voice came out as a flat burr in the regulator, swallowed by the water. "You knew I would."

The fish didn't care. A school of silver bait scattered off the rail and reformed downstream. Somewhere below, near the keel, something shifted in the current with the patient weight of a wreck settling into its final shape.

He swam the length of her one more time. Not searching. There was nothing left to search for—he'd checked the manifests against the investigation bulletins, and every crate, every sealed compartment, every lie had been catalogued by people whose job it now was to tell the truth. The *Meridian* was empty of secrets for the first time since she'd gone down. She was just a ship again. Dead metal, home to eels.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, Hazel thought, hovering above her bridge wing while the light strengthened overhead. Getting what you wanted. He'd spent a decade wanting to be invisible, and invisibility had turned out to be a room with no doors. Then he'd spent ninety days wanting to keep four strangers alive and a dead man's promise, and that want had cost him his claim, his anonymity, his careful small life—and bought him something he hadn't known how to price.

He'd retrieved nothing today. He needed nothing. The realization sat in his chest like ballast trimmed perfectly level.

At the surface, hanging on the line while he purged and breathed and watched the sun break apart on the chop, he let himself say the rest of it, the part he'd only ever said once, storm-bound in a cove with four exhausted faces turned toward him:

"Everyone came home."

The current carried it away. That was fine. Some words you say for the water, because the water keeps them better than you can.

* * *

The Kestrel looked smaller than he remembered, which was impossible, because ships don't shrink. But six months ashore had recalibrated him somehow; when he stepped off the ferry onto the working pier at Carrow Inlet, the old salvage vessel sat low and rust-streaked at her berth, and his first thought was *she's too small for what we did*, and his second thought was *she was always too small, and we went anyway.*

Tomas Reyes spotted him from the wheelhouse and didn't wave. He just leaned out the window and shouted, "You're late. June bet me twenty you'd stand on the pier for ten minutes staring at the water first."

"It's been nine minutes," Hazel called back.

"Marguerite says seven." Tomas disappeared inside, and Hazel heard him relay the count, and heard June's laugh carry across the water—that big, unguarded laugh she'd never once let out during the expedition, back when laughing cost oxygen.

They came down the gangway together. June Okafor first, sun-darkened and heavier than he remembered in the shoulders, her hair cropped short for a season of engine work. She stopped one step above him so they were eye level, studied him for a long moment, then pulled him into a hug that lifted his heels off the planks.

"You look terrible," she said into his ear.

"Good to see you too."

"No, I mean it. You look like a man who's been sleeping." She held him at arm's length. "It's unsettling."

Tomas shook his hand like they were closing a business deal, then ruined the formality by gripping hard and holding on a beat too long. His right hand still trembled slightly in cold weather—the decompression hit had left its signature—but he'd stopped hiding it, which was its own kind of recovery. "The joint's rebuilt," he said. "New compressor, rewired everything, and I fixed that list you never admitted she had."

"She doesn't have a list."

"She has a list. Ask Marguerite."

Marguerite Chen came down last, carrying a document tube under one arm out of pure habit. She'd cut her hair too, and there were lines around her eyes that hadn't been there a year ago, earned lines, the kind cartographers get from drawing coastlines that governments would rather not exist. She handed him the tube without preamble.

"What's this?"

"The contract. We've been sitting on it for three weeks waiting for you to stop brooding in whatever cave you're living in." Her mouth twitched. "Sign it or don't, but decide today, because the tide window for the survey grounds closes Thursday."

Hazel worked the cap off the tube and slid out a single sheet. He read it twice.

Museum contract. The Coastal Heritage Trust—private, funded, squeaky-clean—wanted a full archaeological survey of a wreck site sixty miles north. Published coordinates. Open permit. A liaison who answered letters. Every term printed plainly, nothing buried in fine print, nothing expiring in ninety days, nothing requiring anyone to promise silence about anything.

"They know who we are?" Hazel asked.

"That's why they hired us," Marguerite said. "We're the crew that told the truth. Apparently that's worth money now."

"It's worth trouble, more likely."

"It's both," June said. "Trouble pays better than it used to."

Hazel looked at the sheet again. *Survey of the SS Aldermoor, lost 1931, passenger manifest available upon request.* Available upon request. He almost laughed. After a year of manifests that lied, a document that offered itself up freely felt like a foreign language he was only beginning to learn.

He took the pen Tomas offered and signed at the bottom, his signature quick and ugly, the same scrawl he'd used on Elias's promise. Then he handed the contract back to Marguerite, who countersigned as expedition cartographer, and passed it to June, who signed with a flourish and immediately claimed the captain's cabin as her office, and finally to Tomas, who read every clause twice before signing, because some habits deserved to survive.

"Well," Hazel said. "Chosen this time."

"Chosen," Marguerite agreed.

"Paid, even," said June.

"Boring, even," said Tomas, and grinned. "I'm looking forward to boring."

* * *

They got underway on the morning tide with the kind of departure Hazel had once believed only happened to other people. No borrowed equipment. No creditors watching from the fuel dock. No radio silence, no false destination logged with the harbormaster, no rules laid down over a chart table at midnight. June filed their true course openly, cheerfully, reading the coordinates aloud just to hear how ordinary they sounded. The harbormaster wished them luck. A gull stole a sandwich off the pier.

By noon they were clear of the headlands and running north in a long swell, the Kestrel rolling easy, and Hazel stood at the helm while the others fell into the rhythm of a crew that had done this before and survived doing it. Tomas had the engine hatch open and was arguing lovingly with the port injector. June ran a maintenance checklist out loud, striking items with a grease pencil. Marguerite sat in the wheelhouse corner with the Aldermoor charts spread across her knees, annotating, already three moves ahead of the water.

Around two o'clock she looked up.

"There's something else in the tube," she said. "Under the contract. I forgot to mention."

Hazel frowned. "What else?"

She reached into her bag and drew out a second sheet, older, softened at the folds, drawn in faded ink on paper gone the color of weak tea. He knew it before she finished unfolding it. He'd know it anywhere—the river valley rendered in Elias Vann's cramped, certain hand, the depth soundings, the little crosses marking rooftops under the flood, the church bell circled twice.

"Elias's map," Hazel said. "How do you—"

"He gave it to me. At the end. Day nineteen, after the deep dive, when Tomas was in the rig and you were asleep on your feet." Marguerite smoothed it flat on the chart table. "He'd written a letter to go with it. For whoever kept everyone alive. I never opened it. It didn't feel like mine."

The wheelhouse went quiet. Even the engine argument below seemed to hold its breath.

Hazel set the helm on course and crossed to the table. The envelope tucked behind the map was brittle, sealed, addressed in a shaking hand: *For the one who brings them home.*

June had come up from below. Tomas stood in the companionway, wiping his hands on a rag. Nobody spoke. The Kestrel ran steady north through the swell, and the letter sat on the chart table between them like a compass needle finding its bearing.

"You kept half your promise," Marguerite said quietly. "Maybe he wrote you the other half."

Hazel picked up the envelope. The paper was light as a fish scale. He thought about the wreck that morning, empty of secrets, honest at last. He thought about a dying man in a dockside bar trading a map for a promise, trusting a stranger because strangers were the only ones left who could be trusted.

He broke the seal.

Inside was a single page. Hazel read it once in silence, then again aloud, because some things were never meant to be carried alone:

*"If you're reading this, you brought them back, which means you're better at keeping promises than I was at making them cheaply. So here's the second half, and it's shorter than the first. Tell them the valley wasn't my discovery. I found the map in my father's effects, and my father was one of the forty-one. He rowed out the night the water came up and never forgave the country that drowned his street. I spent my life digging for gold because I couldn't bear to dig for him. You dug for him instead. Whatever you found down there—say their names. All forty-one. Names are the only cargo that keeps."*

Below the signature, in the same failing hand, was a list. Forty-one names, numbered, each one written out carefully, as if the man had been afraid of running out of ink before he ran out of fathers and mothers and children.

Nobody moved for a long moment. Then June reached across the table and touched the list with one finger, tracing the first name, and said, "Say them where?"

"Everywhere," Hazel said. "Starting with us."

So they did it right there, underway, in a wheelhouse smelling of diesel and salt. Hazel read the first name, and June took the second, and Tomas the third, and Marguerite the fourth, and they went around the table through all forty-one, voices steady, the Kestrel holding her course through the open swell, until the last name was spoken and the current took it north with them.

When it was done, Hazel folded the list along its original creases and slid it into the frame of the wheelhouse window, where it would ride every voyage, facing forward.

Tomas cleared his throat. "Aldermoor's sixty miles out. We'll make the grounds by dawn if we hold this heading."

"Then hold it," Hazel said, and took the wheel.

The sea ahead ran open and unmarked, no sealed compartments, no lies in the manifests, no clock ticking down anything but the honest distance between them and the work. Behind them, in the framed window, forty-one names kept watch over the bow.

Hazel steered into the current, and for the first time in his life, it was going the same way he was.
