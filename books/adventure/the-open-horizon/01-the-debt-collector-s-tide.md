## 1. The Debt Collector's Tide

The harbormaster's office smelled of lamp oil and old paper, and Vane sat behind his desk the way a spider sits at the center of its web — perfectly still, perfectly patient, letting you come to him.

Elodie Sterling stood on the worn floorboards and did not sit, though he'd gestured to the chair twice. Sitting meant settling in. Settling in meant this was a conversation. It wasn't. It was an execution with paperwork.

"Thirty days," Vane said again, sliding the ledger across the desk so she could see the figure written there in his clerk's neat hand. The number had not grown smaller since the last time she'd looked at it. Numbers like that didn't shrink. They only waited.

"I've paid every month," Elodie said. "Eleven months."

"You've paid interest." Vane laced his fingers together atop the ledger. He was a lean man gone soft at the middle, with a face like a folded map — all creases and no country. "Principal sits untouched. You understand how that works, Captain. The debt isn't a wound. It's a tide. It comes back whether you fight it or not."

"The Kestrel is my boat."

"The Kestrel is my collateral." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. Cruelty you could hate. This was just arithmetic. "Thirty days. Four hundred crowns, or I take the boat and auction her, and whatever she fetches comes off what you owe. You know what a used riverboat fetches these days."

She knew. She knew to the copper.

Elodie looked at the window behind him, where the harbor lay gray under a low sky, masts swaying like a field of dead wheat. Somewhere out there her boat rode at anchor — forty years of tarred oak and stubbornness, the only thing she'd ever owned outright, bought with seven years of runs upriver hauling everything from timber to coffins. Now it belonged to Vane in every way that mattered.

"I'll get your money," she said.

Vane smiled. It was the smile of a man who had heard that sentence ten thousand times and believed it exactly never. "I hope so, Captain. Truly. I take no pleasure in this." And the terrible thing was that he seemed to mean it. There was no pleasure in him at all. Only the tide.

* * *

She ran cargo that night because running cargo was the only prayer the river answered.

The Kestrel slipped her moorings two hours past dark, riding low in the water with a deckload of contraband salt — taxed salt, salt the coastal syndicates had cornered until the price inland tripled, salt worth more than silver to the hill villages. Tam had the wheel, seventeen years old and all elbows, grinning into the wind like the night was a gift instead of a gamble.

"Full dark by the point," Tam said. "No moon till after midnight. We'll be ghosts."

"We'll be ghosts if we don't hit the shoal off Marrow Light," Brannick said from the engine hatch, where he crouched over the old steam plant like a priest over relics. He was sixty if he was a day, hands scarred white with old scalds, and he'd told her the engine had maybe two seasons left in it for three years running. "Third cylinder's knocking again. Sounds like a debtor at the door."

"Everything sounds like a debtor at the door lately," Elodie said.

Tam laughed. Brannick didn't.

They ran downcoast with lights doused, hugging the black line of the shore where the charts showed nothing but empty coast — the Gray Reach, the chart-makers called it, thirty miles of cliff and shingle nobody bothered mapping because there was no harbor, no fresh water, no reason to come. Elodie had run this coast a hundred times. She knew it the way you know a scar: by feel, in the dark, without looking.

The salt buyers would meet them at the mouth of the Aldern, forty miles south. If they made it. If the revenue cutters were asleep. If the engine held.

If, if, if. Her whole life had narrowed to that one syllable.

She stood at the rail behind Tam and watched the water, and the water watched her back. That was the thing about the sea at night — it made you feel counted. Audited. Every wave a line in some ledger too big to read.

"Captain," Tam said quietly. "Look at the waterline."

Elodie looked. Then she looked again.

The tide was wrong.

She'd known tides her whole life the way farmers know rain, and this one was falling like a stone — not the slow breath of the usual ebb but a long, dragging withdrawal, as if something offshore had pulled the plug on the ocean itself. The water fell away from the cliffs in sheets. She could hear it, a vast hollow rushing, and see the wet gleam of seabed emerging where no seabed should show at this hour, this season, this anything.

"Spring tide?" Tam asked.

"Not for nine days yet," Elodie said. "And spring tides don't do that."

Brannick came up from the hatch wiping his hands, followed the line of her gaze, and went still in a way that made the hair rise on her arms. Old men who worked engines for forty years weren't startled by machinery. They weren't much startled by anything. But Brannick was staring at the retreating water like a man watching a door open that had been painted onto the wall.

"My grandfather fished this coast," he said slowly. "Told me once about a night the sea walked backward. Said his father called it the Debt Collector's Tide. Said the water goes out to collect what's owed underneath."

"That's a fisherman's story," Tam said.

"So's most of what keeps us alive," Brannick said.

Elodie brought the glass up. Ahead, where the charts showed unbroken cliff, the falling tide had peeled the coastline open like a letter. A shelf of black rock stood exposed below the cliff base, and in the face of the cliff itself — a cave. A wide, arched mouth of darkness, dry at its threshold, dripping at its lip, sitting forty feet above the current waterline and clearly carved by water that had spent centuries reaching for it.

It was not on any chart. She knew the Gray Reach better than the chart-makers did, and it was not there. Which meant either it had never been found —

— or it had been found, and kept.

"Captain," Tam said. "We're losing time. Buyers are waiting."

Elodie lowered the glass. The sensible thing — the only thing, with four hundred crowns hanging over her head and contraband salt sweating in her hold — was to log the anomaly, mark the bearing, and run on. She was a captain. Captains ran schedules, not mysteries.

But the tide was still falling. Falling like it was making room. Like it was showing her something on purpose.

Vane's voice sat in her skull: *The debt isn't a wound. It's a tide.*

Maybe tides ran both ways. Maybe something was coming in.

"Bring her alongside the shelf," Elodie said. "Easy. Stern-first, watch the kelp."

Tam stared at her. "There's nothing there. There's never been anything there."

"There's something there tonight." She took the wheel herself, feeling the Kestrel answer under her palms, the old boat leaning into the turn toward the exposed rock with the eagerness of a dog that has caught a scent its master hasn't. "And I'm done leaving things unexamined because they're not on the charts. Charts never paid my debts."

Brannick eased the engine to a whisper. The Kestrel ghosted in over seabed that should have drowned her keel, and Elodie felt the strangeness of it in her boots — steering through water that was giving way, giving ground, opening like a hand.

The shelf rose black and glistening off the port bow. Beyond it, the cave mouth breathed cold air out at them, air that smelled of stone and salt and something older underneath — something like iron, like coin, like locked rooms.

Elodie put the helm over and let the Kestrel kiss the rock.

"Tam, you have the deck. Lantern, and stay where I can hear you." She swung herself over the rail onto the wet shelf, boots finding purchase on weed-slick stone, and stood for a moment looking up at the arch of darkness.

Somewhere behind her, far down the coast, a revenue cutter's lamp winked once against the horizon and went dark. Time was bleeding out of the night. The buyers wouldn't wait forever, and neither would the tide — whatever strange force had pulled the sea back would pull it home again, and when it did, that cave mouth would drown.

She climbed.

The rock was barnacled and treacherous, and the cave swallowed her lantern light three steps in, so that she moved inside a small yellow room with walls of pure black. Drip-water ticked somewhere deep. Her own breathing came back at her doubled, like another person keeping pace.

Then the light touched the floor, and Elodie stopped.

Bones.

A skeleton sat against the cave wall with the patient posture of a man who had arranged himself to wait — ribs curved like the stays of an upturned boat, legs crossed at the ankle, one arm resting on something that glinted dully in the lantern glow. Brass. A compass, crusted green with age, clutched in fingers that had long since become part of the stone's business.

Beside the bones, flat against the rock where the dead man had placed it to be found, lay a shape wrapped in oilskin and bound with wax-sealed cord.

Elodie knelt. The pressure of the debt was still there — it never stopped being there, a hand between her shoulder blades — but beneath it, for the first time in eleven months, she felt something else. Not hope exactly. Hope was a word for people with margins. This was colder and sharper. This was the crack of light under a door in a drowning room.

The sea had gone out to collect what was owed underneath.

And it had led her here.

Her fingers closed around the oilskin bundle. Behind her, far out in the black, the tide turned — she heard it before she understood it, a long low sigh from the deep water, the sound of the ocean drawing breath to come home.

She had minutes. She had a dead man's parcel in her hands and a buyer waiting forty miles south and four hundred crowns standing between her and the end of everything she owned.

Elodie Sterling tucked the bundle inside her coat, took one last look at the skeleton — *thank you, whoever you were; forgive me, whoever you were* — and started down toward the light and the waiting boat as the first thin tongue of returning water licked across the shelf below her boots.
