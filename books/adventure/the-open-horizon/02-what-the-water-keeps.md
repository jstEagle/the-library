## 2. What the Water Keeps

The tide went out farther than it had any right to.

Elodie noticed it from the wheelhouse of the Kestrel, moored in the lee of Gullrock Point with a hold full of contraband flour and a ledger full of lies. The water had pulled back from the coast like a held breath, exposing mudflats that the charts swore didn't exist, and beyond them—a seam of black stone where no seam should be. An opening. A mouth.

She told herself she was only looking. That was the first lie of the night, and she was honest enough with herself to know it, though not honest enough to stop.

The Kestrel's dinghy scraped over sand that should have been six feet underwater. Elodie shipped the oars and let the little boat grind ashore, and for a long moment she just sat there, hands still on the wood, listening to the drip and tick of a coastline rearranging itself. Somewhere behind her, Tam was keeping watch on deck, sworn to wake her if Vane's men came sniffing up the channel. Ahead of her, the cave breathed cold air against her face, slow and tidal, like something sleeping.

She had thirty days. Twenty-nine now, if you counted the way Vane counted, which was in hours dressed up as days. Four hundred crowns, or the Kestrel. He'd said it with the pleasant flatness of a man quoting a price for nails. *Ships sink, Captain Sterling. Debts don't.* And then he'd smiled, and she'd understood that the smile was the threat—the part that came after, when the polite fiction fell away.

Her lamp threw a wobbling circle on wet stone as she stepped inside. The floor sloped down and back, ribbed with ridges like the gills of some enormous fish, and the walls wept mineral light—thin veins of quartz catching the lamplame and throwing it back broken. The air tasted of salt and old iron and, beneath that, something sweeter. Resin. Wax.

*Just looking,* she told herself again, and the lie was wearing thin as river ice.

Twenty paces in, the passage turned, and the sea's noise fell away until her own breathing seemed too loud. She swept the lamp low across the floor—and stopped.

A man sat against the wall as if resting.

He had been sitting there a very long time. What the water kept of him was bone and leather and the brass buttons of a coat that had once been fine, its collar still bearing the ghost of an anchor stitched in thread gone green with age. His boots were good boots. His hands were folded in his lap with a patience that made her throat tighten—not the sprawl of a man who'd died badly, but the stillness of one who'd chosen his spot, arranged himself, and waited.

"Hello," Elodie said softly, because it seemed wrong not to.

The smuggler—if that's what he'd been—didn't answer. Of course he didn't. But beside him, where his right hand rested, lay a brass compass in a hinged case, its glass fogged from the inside, and tucked under his crossed ankles like a letter waiting for a postman, a oilskin-wrapped roll bound in waxed cord.

She crouched. She should have felt fear. Instead she felt the particular hush of a chapel, the sense of having interrupted something sacred—or been invited into it. The dead man's face was gone; the skull wore the cave's damp like a second skin. But there was dignity in how he'd been left. Someone had cared about him once, or he had cared about himself, all the way to the end.

The compass opened under her thumb with a small click that echoed. The needle swung, wobbled, settled—pointing not north but upstream, upriver, into the high country where the charts went blank and the mapmakers wrote *Here be rapids* in their careful, cowardly italics. She frowned at it. Tapped the case. The needle held. Brass doesn't lie by accident; someone had weighted it, or magnetized it against something, or simply trusted the river more than the pole.

"You were going somewhere," she murmured.

The oilskin crackled as she lifted it. The wax seal had held for decades—she broke it anyway, pressing her thumbnail through it with a strange, guilty tenderness, as if she were breaking a promise made to her instead of by her. Inside: a chart, hand-drawn on paper gone the color of weak tea, dense with annotations in three different inks. The lower river she knew by heart, every shoal and snag rendered in a confident hand. Then the ink changed, and so did the confidence. Past the last charted mile, past the gorge, past the stilt-villages that officially did not exist, the lines climbed. Rapids marked with numbers she'd never seen on any hydrographic table. A chute through black rock labeled, in a cramped, urgent script: *Runnable at spring melt. Once.*

And then the river narrowed on the page to a single silver thread, climbing and climbing, until it ended at a symbol she had to hold close to the lamp to read: a mountain drawn in section, like an anatomist's cutaway, and inside the mountain, a chamber. A vault. Around it, in that same cramped hand, words written and rewritten, pressed deep into the paper:

*The glacier keeps what the war could not take. Sealed. Sound. Waiting for a captain worth the risk.*

Elodie sat down on the cold stone without meaning to.

There was treasure in the high country. Pre-war coin, pre-war plate, whatever the old families had run upriver ahead of the burning years and sealed behind ice rather than surrender to the armies. Every child on the coast grew up on those stories, and every sensible adult buried them under the weight of tides and taxes and the simple arithmetic of survival. Sensible adults did not believe in glacier vaults. Sensible adults paid their harbormaster and kept their heads down.

Sensible adults did not owe Vane four hundred crowns.

She looked at the skeleton. At the compass. At the map. And she heard her own voice in her head, practical and reasonable and utterly without mercy: *This is salvage. Maritime law is clear enough—derelict goods, unclaimed property, finder's rights. You're not stealing anything. You're recovering it. There's a difference, and the difference matters, and you can tell yourself the difference every night until the debt is paid.*

It was a good argument. It was the kind of argument that had carried her through ten hard years of running cargo nobody asked questions about, and it fit her hand like a tiller. But underneath it, fainter, another voice—the one that sounded like her mother, if her mother had ever bothered to sound disappointed—said: *You went out on a dying tide to a cave that isn't on any chart. That's not salvage, girl. That's answering a knock.*

The tide was turning. She could feel it before she heard it—a change in the cave's breathing, the cold exhale faltering, the first thin tongue of seawater sliding along the channel toward her boots. She had minutes, not hours.

Elodie wrapped the map along its old creases. She took the compass too—told herself it was to verify the needle's quirk, that any competent navigator would do the same, that leaving evidence was worse than taking it. Both items went into her coat, against her body, where they lay warm and heavy as coals.

She stood over the skeleton a moment longer. "If anyone comes asking," she said, "you died alone, and I never found you." It wasn't quite an apology. It was close enough for a woman who'd stopped expecting forgiveness from the sea.

Then the water came for the cave, patient and total, and Elodie Sterling ran for the dinghy with a dead man's fortune pressed against her ribs and the taste of salt and wax still on her tongue.

* * *

Tam met her at the rail with a lantern hooded and a question in his face. "Find anything?"

"Tide pools," Elodie said. "Good ones. Crab."

He looked at her the way he sometimes did—too long, too level, a boy raised on boats who could read a skipper's hands better than her charts. "You've got wax on your sleeve."

"Sealed jars," she said. "Preserves."

He nodded slowly, unconvinced and choosing, for reasons of his own, not to be. Below them the Kestrel rocked at her moorings, and beyond her, upriver, the dark hills stood in ranks like an audience waiting for a play to begin.

In her cabin, door barred, lamp trimmed low, Elodie unrolled the map on her bunk and weighted its corners with four brass buttons off her spare uniform coat. The vault symbol caught the lamplight. *Waiting for a captain worth the risk.*

Four hundred crowns. Thirty days. A route no living soul had run, through country no chart dared name, to a door in a mountain that might be legend, might be silt, might be a hole in the ice that swallowed captains whole.

Or it might be enough. Enough to pay Vane. Enough to be free. Enough, whispered the practical voice, dressing itself again in its workman's clothes, to finally own something outright instead of owing everything to everyone.

She traced the line of the upper river with one finger, all the way to the glacier, and did not let herself think about why her chest felt like this—like the moment before a chute, when the water drops away and you commit or you die.

Outside, far off in the black, a boat bell rang twice. Not Vane's pattern. Not any pattern she knew.

Someone else was on the river tonight.
