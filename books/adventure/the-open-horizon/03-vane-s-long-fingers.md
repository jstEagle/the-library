## 3. Vane's Long Fingers

The question was small. That was the trouble with it.

Elodie had asked it three times now, in three different ways, and each time she'd told herself it meant nothing — a captain checking her charts, nothing more. But the man behind the counter of the pilot office at Grayharbor had a memory like bilge water: everything settled to the bottom and stayed there.

"Upper river," he'd repeated, tapping his pencil against the ledger. "Past the last survey buoy, you mean."

"Just curious where the soundings end."

"Soundings end where the chart ends." He'd smiled the way men smile when they think they've caught something. "And the chart ends at Mile Forty-One. Nobody goes past Mile Forty-One."

She'd laughed it off, bought a coil of rope she didn't need, and walked out into the gray morning light feeling the question trailing her like a hook still set in her jaw.

Now, two days later, she stood in the pilothouse of the *Kestrel* while Tam coiled line on the deck below, and she unrolled the smuggler's map for the hundredth time. The wax seal lay broken beside it, a curl of red like a wound. The parchment was river-stained, soft as cloth at the folds, and the ink had gone brown with age, but the hand that drew it had been sure. The river ran up the page like a spine, and past Mile Forty-One the little annotated marks began — *shoal, port side*; *deadfall, clearable*; *the narrows sing before they bite* — and then, near the top of the sheet, where the paper had been handled so often it shone, a symbol like a mountain wearing a crown, and beneath it two words in a dead man's careful script:

*THE SOURCE.*

"You keep looking at that thing like it's going to apologize," Tam said from the doorway. He was nineteen and all elbows, and he read her better than she liked.

"It's salvage," she said.

"Sure." He leaned against the doorframe. "Salvage you check on every watch. Salvage you sleep next to." He nodded at the chart drawer where she'd taken to keeping it, which was true, and which she hadn't admitted even to herself until he said it.

"I'm working out whether it's real."

"And?"

Elodie looked out the window. Downriver, the town of Grayharbor sat low against the water, its warehouses hunched like beggars, and somewhere in the middle of it was the harbormaster's office, and somewhere in that office was a ledger with her name in it, and a number beneath her name that had grown fat on interest for six years. Fourteen thousand crowns. Thirty days. Vane had said it gently, almost kindly, the way a doctor says *it's spread.*

"And I think," she said, "that a dead man drew a map to enough money to buy my boat back twice over."

Tam was quiet a moment. "Or enough money to get you killed."

"That too."

She rolled the map away before he could see how much her hands wanted to shake.

* * *

Quill Hask did not look like a dangerous man. He looked like a filing cabinet that had learned to walk — narrow, gray, precise, with ink stains on his right middle finger and a habit of standing slightly too close. Harbormaster Aldous Vane kept him the way a spider keeps silk: not for company, but for catching things.

On the third morning after Elodie's visit to the pilot office, Quill laid a slip of paper on Vane's desk and stepped back to his exact distance, which was one pace, no more, no less.

"The Sterling woman," he said. "She asked about the upper river. Soundings past Mile Forty-One. She also bought rope — forty fathoms, best grade — and asked the chandler whether anyone had ever run the Gorge Chute and lived."

Vane didn't look up from his ledger. His pen moved in small, patient strokes. "Did the chandler have an answer?"

"No one's tried since the war."

"Mm." Vane finished his entry, blotted it, and set the pen down in its groove with the care of a man who believed everything had a place. Only then did he raise his eyes. They were pale, unhurried eyes, the color of shallow water over sand, and they made people want to confess things just to make him blink. "And what else has Captain Sterling been doing lately?"

"Tides are running low. Spring lows, unusually deep. She's been taking the *Kestrel* out on night runs along the empty coast north of the point. No cargo manifests filed. Three nights running."

Vane turned his chair toward the window. Below, the harbor lay brown and busy, barges nosing through the chop, gulls screaming over the fish market. Somewhere out there was a woman who owed him fourteen thousand crowns and had begun, quietly, to behave like a woman who expected not to pay.

"Three nights," he said. "Along an empty coast."

"Yes, sir."

"What's on that coast, Quill?"

"Nothing, sir. According to the charts."

Vane smiled. It was a thin expression, more arithmetic than warmth. "Charts are written by men who came once, in fair weather, and left. The coast knows more than the charts do." He rose, took his coat from the stand, and settled it across his shoulders. "Have the *Marrow* fueled. And bring me the file on Sterling. All of it. Her father's debts, the fire at the Meridian yards, the insurance she never collected. Everything."

Quill hesitated — a fractional pause, but Vane caught it the way he caught everything. "Something else?"

"There's talk," Quill said carefully, "at the fish market. That she came back from one of those night runs with something. A crate, maybe. Or—" he lowered his voice, though there was no one within ten feet, "—something off a body."

For a long moment Vane said nothing. Then he picked up his pen again, considered it, and put it back down.

"A body," he repeated, softly, tasting it. "How interesting. Prepare the launch."

* * *

He came aboard at dusk, without warning, which was how Vane did everything.

Elodie was in her cabin reviewing fuel figures when she heard the deck change — the particular quality of footsteps that weren't Tam's or Brannick's, measured and unhurried, and then Quill Hask's voice saying, "The captain will see the harbormaster," in a tone that wasn't a question, and Tam's voice answering with something tight and wordless.

She had thirty seconds. She used twenty-nine of them. The map went up under the floorboard beneath her bunk, under the loose plank she'd pried up the first night, and the wax seal went into her coat pocket because she couldn't explain a broken seal, and then she straightened her collar and opened her cabin door just as Vane reached it.

"Captain Sterling." He removed his hat. He had a long face built for disapproval, softened tonight by something worse: patience. "May I come aboard? Technically, of course, I may regardless. The *Kestrel*'s papers list me as lienholder. But I prefer invitations."

"You have one," she said flatly, stepping aside.

His gaze swept the cabin the way a customs officer's hands sweep a hold — quick, thorough, missing nothing and letting you know it missed nothing. It paused on the chart drawer. It paused on the fresh scratch marks around the floorboard by her bunk — new wood showing pale against old varnish — and moved on without stopping, which was how she knew he'd seen them.

"Foul weather coming," he remarked, settling into her single chair uninvited. "They say the spring tides this year are the lowest in a decade. Remarkable, what the water gives back when it takes its leave. Wrecks. Caves. Things men lost and rivers kept." He folded his hands. "You've been out on those tides, Captain. Three nights. North of the point. Where, according to every chart in my office, there is nothing to find."

"Testing my anchor gear."

"At midnight."

"Best time for it."

Vane smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which made it far less pleasant. "You know what I admire about you, Elodie? Your father was a dreamer. Borrowed against tomorrow to build for the day after. You're not a dreamer. You're a survivor. You took a burned yard and a drowned name and you built a boat and a living out of the wreckage, and for six years you've paid me — late, yes, partially, often in coin I suspect you earned in ways we won't discuss — but you paid. Until now." He leaned forward. "So when a survivor like you starts behaving like a treasure hunter, I ask myself: what changed?"

"Nothing changed."

"Then you won't mind opening that floorboard."

The silence stretched between them like a hawser pulled taut. Outside, Tam's shadow crossed the frosted glass of the cabin window and stopped there, deliberately, a silhouette standing guard. Brannick's engine room ticked and breathed beneath them both, the old man's presence rising through the deck plates like heat.

Elodie held Vane's gaze. "That's my cabin, Aldous. Not your warehouse."

"Everything on this boat is collateral," he said pleasantly. "You signed for it. Page four, paragraph nine. I can quote it if you like; I wrote the amendment myself." He rose, unhurried, and stood over the bunk, looking down at the floorboard the way a man looks at a locked door he already owns the key to. "But here's what I actually came to say, so let's dispense with the theater."

He turned. In the lamplight his face had gone smooth and serious, a moneylender's face transfigured into something almost like sincerity.

"Fourteen thousand crowns, thirty days, and we both know you don't have it. If you default, I take the *Kestrel*, auction her, and you'll be lucky to clear the difference — you'll walk away owing me still, minus a boat, minus a crew, minus a life. That's the ruin path. I'm offering you another one." He let that sit. "Whatever the tide gave you, north of the point — whatever the dead man left — I want in. Not as a creditor. As a partner. My boats, my guns, my knowledge of the upper river — and I know more than any chart shows, Captain; my grandfather ran contraband past Mile Forty-One before the war — in exchange for half. Half of whatever's up there. And your debt burns to ash the moment we cast off."

Elodie felt the trap close around her with a click so quiet it might have been a latch, might have been a bone. He knew. Of course he knew. Quill Hask collected whispers the way other men collected coins, and she had spent hers freely — the pilot office, the chandler, three nights of lights-off running along a coast that was supposed to be empty. She had thought herself invisible. She had been a lantern in a dark field.

"And if I say there's nothing?" she said. "No map. No cave. Just a woman testing anchors."

"Then I take the boat in thirty days and we part as enemies, and I'll spend years wondering, and I always find out, Elodie. Always. Ask anyone whose secret I ever wanted." He put his hat back on. At the door he paused, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Your father chose pride over partnership once. I watched what it cost him. Don't make his mistakes. Think about it. You have until the *Kestrel* next casts off — I'll know when that is. I always know."

His footsteps receded up the deck, measured as a metronome. The gangplank creaked. Then the harbor sounds closed over him, and Elodie stood alone in her cabin with her heart going like a paddle wheel.

Tam slipped inside without knocking. "Well?"

"He knows about the map."

"Does he know where—"

"He doesn't need to know where. He needs me to go, and he needs to follow." She sank onto the bunk, above the board that hid the dead man's handwriting, and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "It's not an offer, Tam. It's a leash. He partners with you right up until the moment he doesn't need you anymore. My father learned that lesson with his whole life."

"So we burn it."

She lowered her hands. Through the porthole, Grayharbor's lamps were coming on one by one across the brown water, and beyond the town, invisible but utterly present, the river ran north into hills neither of them had ever seen, toward a source no chart admitted existed, toward a glacier that was, right now, at this very hour, beginning its slow groan toward summer.

"Burn it, and in thirty days I lose the boat and Vane finds the cave himself someday anyway — tides like these come once a decade, and Quill Hask reads harbors the way priests read scripture." She stood, and something in her had already shifted, some decision settling into her bones like ballast, though her mouth hadn't said it yet. "Or we run it first. Upstream, past Mile Forty-One, fast and quiet, before his gunboats can steam."

Tam stared at her. "That's suicide."

"That's a head start." She pulled the floorboard up and took out the map, and the lamplight caught the little crowned mountain at its top. "Wake Brannick. Tell him I want steam by dawn — full pressure, and the old girl's going to need everything he's got, because nobody's ever run what we're about to run."

Tam hesitated at the door. "And if Vane's waiting for us at the mouth of the river?"

Elodie looked at the map, and at the harbor lights, and at the open black water beyond them where anything could be waiting.

"Then," she said, "we'd better be faster."
