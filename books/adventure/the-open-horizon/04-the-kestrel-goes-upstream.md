## 4. The Kestrel Goes Upstream

The last charted mile ended at a bend where the cartographers had simply given up. Elodie knew it because the map in her cabin—the honest one, the one printed by the Survey Office forty years ago—bore a small inked annotation there, faded to the color of weak tea: *Here be rapids. Not recommended.*

*Not recommended.* As if the river were a dish on a menu.

She stood at the Kestrel's wheel with that other map folded against her ribs, inside her coat, over her heart, like something stolen from a church. The smuggler's wax had cracked along its folds now; she'd opened it twice in the night and twice told herself she was only checking the channel marks. But a map like that didn't get checked. It got obeyed.

"Water's high," Brannick said behind her. He'd come up from the engine room without being called, the way he always did when he smelled trouble, wiping his hands on a rag that had been black since before the war. "Snowmelt's early this year. That means more water in the channel—and more rock moving under it."

"I know."

"You know the way you know a thing you've read." He settled against the rail beside her, old and square as an anvil. "Different from knowing it in your hands."

Elodie watched the current come around the bend ahead, green water folding into white, and felt the familiar arithmetic begin in her chest: thirty days of debt, Vane's soft voice, the interest accruing like silt. Every mile upstream was a mile further from his harbor, his ledgers, his long fingers. And every mile was also a mile into country no steamboat had ever returned from.

"Tam!" she called.

Her deckhand came up the ladder two rungs at a time, nineteen years old and all elbows, a coiled line already over his shoulder because Tam believed any problem could be solved with enough rope. "Aye, Captain?"

"Soundings. Start at the head of the white water and work back. I want depth readings every ten feet, and I want you alive at the end of it."

He grinned like she'd given him a present. "You want me in the water?"

"I want you in the skiff, with the lead line, staying out of the worst of it. If the channel reads less than six feet anywhere in the first half-mile, we tie off and think again."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we go through."

Brannick made a sound low in his chest—not quite a laugh. "Six feet. You're giving yourself a foot of grace under our draft. Generous."

"The river will decide how generous I am. I just want to know what it's decided before it tells me."

They worked the bend for two hours. Elodie took the Kestrel down to quarter steam and held her nose into the current while Tam dropped the lead line again and again, shouting numbers across the water with the glee of a boy calling dice. Five fathoms. Three. Two and a half. A shelf of rock that would have opened the Kestrel's hull like a tin can, marked and avoided. Then deep water again, cold and fast, running between walls of granite so close together the paddlewheel's wash came back to them as an echo.

By noon they were through the first chute, and Elodie discovered that fear and joy, fed into the same furnace, burned at exactly the same temperature.

* * *

The second day the banks narrowed and the world went vertical. Pines crowded down to the waterline, their roots gripping stone, and the air coming off the heights smelled of snow even though the sun was out. Somewhere above them, unseen, water fell—a constant whispering, like the river being told secrets.

Elodie ran the boat by eye and instinct and the map, which showed a channel the Survey Office had never dreamed of. The smuggler had drawn it in a hand that shook—fear or cold or excitement, impossible to say—but he'd drawn it true. Twice the map took them through gaps that looked, from the wheel, like certain death, and twice the gap opened at the last moment into a pool of brown-green calm, and Tam whooped from the bow, and Brannick said nothing at all, which from Brannick was applause.

It was on the third morning that Tam spotted the smoke.

"Captain." He was at the rail astern, squinting back downriver the way he did every hour, a habit Elodie had drilled into him until it stuck. "Smoke. Behind us. Two bends back, maybe three."

She took the glass from its bracket and found it: a smudge of gray against the green, low and deliberate, the smoke of a boat burning coal carefully. Not a trapper's fire. Not driftwood. Coal meant a steamer, and a steamer this far up meant someone was following the same map she was following—or following *her*.

"Brannick. How much can we make her do?"

"She'll do eleven knots in flat water and pray for forgiveness in anything else." He'd appeared again, silent as ballast. "But every knot past eight is a knot I can't give you back when you need it."

"Whoever's back there is reading the same water we are," Elodie said. "If they're slower, they're cautious. If they're faster—" She lowered the glass. "Nobody's faster. Not on this river. Not yet."

"That's not an answer, Captain."

"It's the only one I have." She turned the wheel a spoke. "We keep our pace. We don't burn ourselves out running from a shadow. But Tam—I want eyes astern every fifteen minutes, not every hour. And if that smoke gets within sight of my stern wheel, you wake me, whatever hour it is."

"Aye, Captain."

They ran on. The river climbed. By evening the banks had become canyon walls, and the light lasted only in the upper air, and the water turned the color of milk steeped in stone—that particular glacial green that looks beautiful and means cold enough to kill in minutes. Elodie stood her watch alone after midnight, the engine murmuring below her feet like a sleeping animal, and let herself feel the thing she'd been refusing to feel since the cave:

She was doing it. She was actually doing it. Past the last charted mile, past everything anyone had ever told her about where a riverboat could go, with a dead smuggler's map against her ribs and thirty days on Vane's ledger burning down behind her like a fuse.

And somewhere back there in the dark, a boat with coal smoke and a patient wake was climbing toward her through the same impossible water.

* * *

The rapids proper began on the fifth day, and they began without ceremony—one moment smooth water, the next a mile of chaos that sounded like the end of the world.

Elodie stood at the wheel with her hands bare so she could feel the boat answer her, and Brannick gave her everything the old girl had, and Tam rode the bow line ready to throw, and the Kestrel went into it.

There is no describing honestly what it is to steer a hundred-foot wooden vessel through water that does not want you. The river picked the Kestrel up and argued with her, slammed her port side, lifted her stern, threw spray over the pilothouse windows in sheets so thick Elodie steered by memory and prayer. The map said bear left at the split rock. The split rock appeared out of the roar like a judgment, and she bore left, and the channel was there, barely, a throat of green water threading between two boulders the size of churches, and then they were through, and the roar fell away behind them, and Tam was laughing so hard he couldn't coil the line, and Brannick came up on deck and looked at the water ahead and said, quietly, "Well. Nobody's done that."

Elodie realized her hands were shaking. She also realized she was smiling, and hadn't decided to.

"We camp above the next bend," she said. "Wood and water. And Tam—"

"Aye?"

"Check astern."

He checked. He came back slowly, and the laugh had gone out of him. "Captain. The smoke. It's closer. Maybe a mile. Maybe less—it's hard to tell in these narrows."

Elodie took the glass. In the last of the light, framed between the canyon walls downstream, she saw it clearly for the first time: not a smudge but a shape, a long low hull with a derrick folded flat along its deck, moving with the unhurried confidence of something that knew exactly where it was going. Painted on the bow, too far to read and yet somehow readable anyway, a name she already knew from harbor gossip and salvage-broker talk.

*Merrow.*

Cass Merrow's boat. Cass Merrow's crew. Following her upriver like a debt of a different kind.

"They're not trying to catch us," Brannick said at her shoulder. "Look at their speed. They're matching us. Herding distance."

"Why?" Tam asked. "Why follow and not press?"

Elodie lowered the glass. Above them, past the next bend, the canyon opened—she could hear it opening, the water spreading and quieting—and beyond that, faint as a rumor, the white shoulders of the high country where the river was born.

"Because whoever's steering that boat wants us to find the way," she said. "Let us break the ice, run the rapids, mark the channel. Then they take the route off our bones." She turned for the ladder. "Anchor above the bend. Double watches tonight. And nobody shows a light after full dark."

She paused at the top of the stairs, one hand on the rail, and looked back once at that patient hull in the gathering dusk.

Tomorrow, she thought, they would reach the stilt village the map hinted at—the place where the river people lived who had never been on any chart. Tomorrow she would need allies, or answers, or both.

Tonight, the Kestrel swung at her anchor in the middle of the river, dark and quiet, while a mile downstream a salvage boat anchored too, close enough that in the stillness Elodie could hear, very faintly, a harmonica playing.

Someone aboard that boat was in no hurry at all.

She stood her own watch until dawn, the map warm against her ribs, and told herself the tightness in her chest was only the cold coming off the water.
