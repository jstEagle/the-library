## 5. Riverfolk

The river changed its mind three times in the last mile.

It braided, unbraided, and braided again around gravel bars the color of old bone, and every channel looked as honest as the next and lied just as well. Elodie stood at the wheel of the Kestrel with her boots planted wide, reading water the way her father had taught her to read weather — by the seams, by the shadows under the surface, by the places where the current smoothed itself over something it didn't want you to see.

"There," Tam said from the bow rail, pointing with his chin. "Smoke. Thin. Cooking smoke, not engine."

She'd have missed it herself if she hadn't known to look. The village didn't sit on the river so much as hide behind it — a stand of black alders grown thick along the bank, and through their trunks, on stilts driven into the shallows of a backwater lagoon, a cluster of houses with cedar-shake roofs gone silver with moss. Boardwalks stitched them together like the rigging of some grounded fleet. A dozen dugout canoes rested against the pilings, hulls worn smooth as river stone.

"Nobody lives here," Brannick said from the engine hatch, wiping grease off his hands with a rag that had been black since before the war. "I've run this stretch thirty years. Charts say empty."

"The charts say a lot of things," Elodie said.

That was the trouble. She was about to do exactly what she'd sworn not to do — bring the map's business to people who had built their whole lives on being unmapped. But the Kestrel's starboard seam was weeping again, slow and steady, and Brannick said flatly there was no caulking it with what they carried. They needed pitch, seasoned timber, maybe iron. And the only human beings within two days' run were the ones who'd spent generations making sure no one found them.

"Drop anchor in the channel," she said. "We go in by canoe. One boat, three of us, hands visible."

Tam grinned. "Like a parley."

"Don't make it cute. If they tell us to leave, we leave."

* * *

The alder passage swallowed them whole. The canoe slid through water so still it held the trees upside down, and the sound of the main current faded until all Elodie could hear was the drip off the paddle and her own pulse. Then the trees opened, and the village was there, close enough to touch, and it was not empty at all.

People stood on the boardwalks. Old men with braided gray hair. Women with fish knives still in their belts. Children peering between the legs of adults, eyes wide and unafraid in a way that unsettled her more than fear would have. Nobody spoke. Nobody ran. They simply watched the strangers come, the way herons watch a boat pass — motionless, patient, certain the moment will resolve itself without their help.

An old woman stepped forward onto the lowest walkway. She was small and straight-backed, wearing a coat made of pieced canvas that might once have been a sail, and her face was brown and seamed like driftwood. She looked at Elodie for a long time.

"You're the one running the drowned man's paper upstream," she said.

Elodie's stomach dropped. "I don't know what you mean."

"Yes, you do." The woman's voice wasn't cruel. It was tired, the way a lock is tired of being tested. "Word travels faster than boats on this river. We heard you took something out of the tide cave at Graytooth Point. We heard who's chasing you." Her gaze went past Elodie's shoulder, downriver, toward the horizon where Cass Merrow's salvage boat had been hanging like a bad smell for two days. "And we heard the harbormaster Vane put his fingers on your throat."

"I'm not here for the map," Elodie said. "My boat's taking water. I need pitch and timber, and I'll pay for them or work for them. That's all."

The old woman studied her the way Brannick studied an engine — listening for the knock underneath the smooth running. "You'll eat first," she said finally. "Nobody bargains hungry. My name is Sera. Come up."

* * *

They ate smoked whitefish and wild rice and berries so dark they stained their fingers blue, on a boardwalk outside the largest house, while half the village pretended not to watch and failed completely. Tam talked too much, the way he did when he was nervous, telling stories about the Kestrel's worst runs until some of the children crept close enough to listen openly. Brannick sat quiet and let the food settle into him like ballast.

Sera sat across from Elodie and asked questions that sounded casual and weren't.

"Where were you born?"

"Port Ceres. Harbor district."

"Your people?"

"My mother worked the fish markets. My father crewed coastal freighters until the sea took him." She paused. "I never knew which port he belonged to. Neither did he, most days."

"And the Kestrel is yours?"

"Mortgaged to Vane down to the rivets."

Sera nodded slowly, as if each answer confirmed something she'd already written down. "You know why we ask. Every few years someone comes up this far. Surveyors. Timber cruisers. Men with instruments who want to put our homes on paper so other men can tax them, log them, drown them behind a dam. We've hidden from chart-makers for six generations. So when a captain comes carrying a dead smuggler's map to the source of the river—" she met Elodie's eyes, "—we need to know what kind of person holds it."

"And if I'm the wrong kind?"

"Then you eat, you sleep, and in the morning your boat has all the pitch and timber it needs, and you never find this place again. Not because we hurt you. Because the river forgets the way back, for those it wants to forget."

* * *

It was the children who decided it, though Elodie wouldn't understand that until later.

Near dusk, while Brannick argued happily with the village's ancient boatwright about scarf joints, a girl of perhaps nine came and stood beside Elodie at the rail. She didn't speak. She held out her hand, palm up, and in it lay a small carved wooden bird — a swift, wings swept back, its body polished by years of handling.

"For you," the girl said.

"Why?"

The girl shrugged with the magnificent indifference of childhood. "You looked like you needed it."

Elodie took the little bird and turned it over in her fingers and felt something crack open in her chest that she hadn't known was sealed. She thought of every harbor she'd ever tied up in, where she'd been cargo first and person second, where her name mattered only on debt ledgers. She thought of Vane's clerk counting her days like coins. And here was a child in a village that owed her nothing, giving her a bird because she looked like she needed it.

When she looked up, Sera was watching her with an expression that had softened by a single degree, which Elodie would learn was roughly equivalent to an embrace.

"Come inside," Sera said. "There's something you should see before you go any further upriver."

* * *

The house was long and low, its interior lit by seal-oil lamps, its walls hung with nets, tools, and generations of small carved things. At the far end, behind a curtain of woven reeds, Sera led her down three steps into a room that was older than everything above it — the stilts and planks gave way to mortised timbers black with age, joined without a single nail.

On the wall hung a map.

Not paper. Birch bark panels, sewn together with sinew, drawn in ochre and charcoal, and it showed the river — *their* river — from the delta to the ice. Elodie stepped closer and her breath caught. There was the gorge. There was the confluence where Cass Merrow had tried to buy her. And there, at the top, where the smuggler's wax-sealed chart had shown a dotted line ending at a glacier wall marked with an X, the bark map showed something else entirely: a mountain with a door in it, and figures going in, and figures coming out carrying bundles, and then — further down the same panel, in a different hand, a later hand — the door drawn shut, and no figures at all.

"What is it?" Elodie asked quietly.

"Our refuge," Sera said. "Long ago, when the war came up the coast, our ancestors carried everything that mattered — seed, medicine, the records of the families, the carvings of the dead — up past the rapids to a vault in the rock beneath the glacier. Cold keeps things. Ice keeps secrets. They meant to go back when the fighting passed." Her finger traced the later drawing, the closed door. "The fighting passed. The ice did not give it back. Calving season opens the mouth for a few hours some years, and closes it forever in others. Our elders sealed the knowledge away rather than let anyone die trying. What your drowned smuggler found — the X on his paper — that's our door."

Elodie stood very still. The lamp flame bent in a draft, and the ochre figures seemed to move.

"So the treasure," she said slowly, "is yours."

"It is our grandmothers and grandfathers. It is the proof that we existed before anyone drew a border through this valley." Sera turned to face her fully. "Vane thinks it's gold. The syndicate men think it's gold. Maybe some of it is — our people traded with everyone, once. But gold is the smallest thing in that room, and the men who want it will burn every village on this river to carry it out. Including this one. Including yours, Captain Sterling, if you still have one."

The word *yours* landed harder than any blow. Elodie looked down at the little wooden bird in her pocket and understood that the test hadn't been the questions over dinner. The test was standing in this room, seeing what the treasure actually was, and choosing.

"I haven't decided anything yet," she said honestly. "Vane has a partnership around my neck. Merrow made me an offer at the confluence. Both of them think I'm coming back with coordinates."

"And are you?"

"I'm coming back with a leaking boat and a crew I won't trade for any vault." She met the old woman's eyes. "Beyond that, I don't know. I won't lie to you in your own house."

Sera was silent a long moment. Then something moved through her face that might, in another woman, have been a smile.

"Honesty," she said. "Rarer than gold. Keep it, and the river will keep you." She touched the birch-bark map gently, the way one touches a sleeping child. "Go repair your boat. Go upstream if you must — the calving will open the door soon; we can hear the mountain groaning at night already. But understand what you carry, Elodie Sterling. You aren't leading thieves to treasure anymore. You're deciding whether the world gets to know this river's heart, and knowing, take it."

* * *

They left at first light, the Kestrel patched with fresh pitch and new-sawn cedar, riding higher than she had in weeks. Half the village stood on the boardwalks to see them off. The girl with the swift was there, and Elodie raised a hand to her, and the girl raised both of hers.

Tam cast off the last line and glanced at Elodie at the wheel. "So what's the play, Captain? Vane expects coordinates. Merrow expects a partner. We've got a vault full of somebody's ancestors sitting under a glacier that's fixing to shrug."

Elodie looked upriver, where the water ran fast and clear toward mountains that had begun, faintly, in the cold morning air, to make a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

"The play," she said, "is that we get there first — and decide who else gets to arrive at all."

Behind them, unnoticed by any of them, a canoe slipped from the alder passage and followed, keeping to the shadowed bank: Sera's grandson, sent to watch what the river's newest daughter did with its oldest secret.
