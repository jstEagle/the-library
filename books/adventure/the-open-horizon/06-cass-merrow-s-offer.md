## 6. Cass Merrow's Offer

Cass Merrow had learned long ago that the best lies were told with the truth, and the best truths were told at dawn, when a person's defenses were still soft with sleep. So she timed her approach to the confluence for first light, when the mist lay thick on the water and the Kestrel would be running dark, banked fires, drifting easy on the slack current where the western fork poured its cold green melt into the main channel.

She'd been shadowing Elodie Sterling for three days. Her pilot, a leathery riverman named Okonkwo who claimed to have run every fork of this water before the charts were burned in the war, said she was wasting coal. "Sterling's headed for the glacier whether you talk to her or not," he'd said. "You could just follow her wake."

"Following gets me a share of whatever she finds," Cass said. "Talking gets me a partner. There's a difference, and the difference is worth more than coal."

The truth—which Cass kept folded away like a letter she never sent—was that following also meant Vane's gunboats would find them both eventually, and Cass had no intention of being downstream when that happened. She needed Sterling. Not her boat, not her map. *Her.* Because Elodie Sterling had done something in the stilt village that Cass's own crew still talked about in hushed voices: she'd been welcomed. The riverfolk didn't welcome anyone. They'd fired warning arrows at Cass's scouts two seasons back and left one of her deckhands strung up by his ankles as a message.

So at dawn, in the mist, Cass brought the *Magpie* alongside the sleeping Kestrel with fenders out and engines at a whisper, and she climbed over the rail herself, alone, unarmed, carrying a bottle of plum brandy and a ledger book bound in oiled leather.

The watchman—Tam, the boy with the quick eyes—nearly put a boathook through her ribs before she raised both hands.

"Easy," she said. "I'm here to make your captain rich."

* * *

Elodie came up from below decks with a revolver already in her hand and her hair unbound, and Cass thought, not for the first time, that the woman was wasted on debt. There was a coiled readiness in her, a way of moving like the deck might tilt at any moment, that spoke of someone who had learned early that safety was a story other people told.

"Cass Merrow." Elodie didn't lower the gun. "You've been riding my wake since the rapids. I counted your smoke."

"I know. That's why I stopped hiding it. A shadow you can see is a handshake waiting to happen." Cass set the brandy on the capstan and the ledger beside it, keeping her hands slow and visible. "May I?"

"You may talk. From there."

"Fair." Cass leaned against the rail and let the mist curl between them. Downriver, the *Magpie*'s crew waited with the studied idleness of people pretending not to listen. "Here's what I know. You found a dead man's map in a drowned cave. You're running it upstream to a vault in the ice. And Harbormaster Vane is coming behind you with gunboats and a bill of sale he thinks makes him your owner."

Something flickered across Elodie's face—the map, the cave, the trap of it—and Cass pressed the advantage gently, the way you press a bruise to prove it's real.

"You want to know how I know about Vane? Because six months ago he tried to buy *my* salvage charter. I turned him down, so he went to my financiers instead and tried to have my license revoked. Vane doesn't partner, Captain. He acquires. And right now he's acquired you."

"He hasn't." Elodie's voice was flat. "Not yet."

"No?" Cass opened the ledger—not her real ledger, but close enough, columns of figures copied from documents she'd bought from a clerk in Vane's own office for more money than the clerk made in five years. "Then explain this. Page after page of correspondence between Vane and the Corvane Mining Syndicate. Dated eight months back. He's not chasing your treasure, Captain. He's chasing the *route*. The vault is a bonus. What Vane is selling Corvane is the upper river itself—dredging rights, barge access, a company port at the gorge. They'll strip the banks for ore and leave the water running gray."

She watched Elodie read. Watched the woman's jaw tighten at the figures, at the phrase *native relocation contingent upon route security*, which was syndicate language for burning villages off their stilts.

"The riverfolk," Elodie said quietly. "He knows about them."

"He knows everything about them now. Your visit painted a target on every stilt house on that fork." Cass let that land, let it hurt, because the hurt was useful. Then she reached for the brandy, poured two fingers into a tin cup, and held it out. "Which brings me to my offer."

* * *

The offer was simple, and Cass had rehearsed it until it sounded like honesty.

"I'll buy out Vane's claim. All of it. I have the capital—I've spent ten years pulling wrecks out of this river and I've never once come up empty. Whatever paper he holds against the Kestrel, I retire it today. You owe nothing to anyone. In exchange: we split the vault. Fifty-fifty. My crew runs the salvage, your name runs the charter, and neither of us ever has to look at a harbormaster again."

"And the route?" Elodie said. "The one you say Vane wants to sell. If you own his claim, you own his Corvane papers too. What do you do with them?"

Cass smiled and took her time answering, because the pause was part of the architecture. "I burn them."

"Liar."

The word came out without heat, almost gentle, and it caught Cass off guard for half a heartbeat—a half-heartbeat she covered with a laugh. "Captain. I'm offering you freedom. Most people at least pretend to consider it."

"I am considering it." Elodie finally holstered the revolver, and Cass felt the small victory of it, the door opening one inch. "Here's what I'm considering. You buy Vane out, yes. And then you hold the only legal claim to the upper river in your hands, and you sit down with Corvane yourself, because fifty percent of a treasure is a fortune but exclusive dredging rights are an empire. You don't burn those papers, Merrow. You frame them."

"That's a cynical reading."

"It's an accurate one. I've been poor my whole life. I know exactly what people do with leverage." Elodie picked up the tin cup of brandy, considered it, and poured it over the rail instead of drinking. "And there's something else you left out of your arithmetic. The riverfolk. The vault isn't just gold to them, Merrow. It's theirs. Their ancestors built it, sealed it, drowned the maps to keep it safe. Every coin in that ice is a promise somebody made to somebody's grandchildren. You're asking me to help you break that promise and call it partnership."

"I'm asking you to be practical," Cass said, and heard, even as she said it, how much she sounded like Vane. The realization sat in her chest like swallowed ballast. She pushed past it. "Fifty-fifty of a pre-war vault sets you up for life. Sets up your crew. Tam could go to school. Your engineer could die warm. Or you can protect some strangers' ghosts and spend the rest of your life owing men like Vane, until one day they take the Kestrel anyway and you're left standing on a dock with nothing but your principles."

Elodie was quiet for a long moment. The mist was thinning; light came up the river in a pale wash, and Cass could see the far fork now, the green water sliding cold and patient toward the ice.

"You met them yet?" Elodie asked suddenly. "The riverfolk. Face to face?"

"No."

"Then you don't understand what you're pricing." Elodie stepped closer, and Cass held her ground, though every instinct said otherwise. "They fed my crew. They mended Brannick's hands when he couldn't hold a wrench. An old woman sang a mourning song for a man I killed in the rapids—sang it for *him*, not for me, because grief belongs to everyone on this water. I have sailed fifteen years without a single person on any dock caring whether I came home. Fifteen years, Merrow. And these people looked at me like I was worth looking at." Her voice didn't rise; it got quieter, which was worse. "You're offering me enough money to never need anyone again. I've had that offer my whole life. It's always been a lie."

* * *

Cass stood at the rail after Elodie withdrew below decks, and she did not immediately signal the Magpie alongside.

Okonkwo called up from the other boat, "Well? Do we have a partner or a problem?"

"Neither yet," Cass said.

She looked at the ledger lying open on the capstan, the forged-and-bought columns, the careful fiction of her good faith. She had told herself the Corvane papers were bait, nothing more, that she'd burn them once the vault was split. But Elodie had seen through her in under an hour, the way you can see the bottom of a river only where the water runs clear, and the humiliating truth was that Cass wanted to be seen that clearly. Wanted it so badly it frightened her.

Ten years of salvage. Ten years of pulling treasure out of drowned things and telling herself the drowning was nobody's fault. And now the most valuable wreck on the river was a promise, sealed in ice, and she was holding the crowbar.

She closed the ledger. Left it on the capstan—let Sterling read every page, including the ones that implicated Cass herself in two quiet betrayals Cass had never confessed to anyone. If the woman was going to judge her, let her judge the whole account.

"Merrow," Okonkwo called again. "Vane's smoke. Far bank, maybe four hours out. Gunboats."

Cass looked upriver, toward the glacier she could not yet see but could feel, the way you feel weather in old scars. Four hours. Enough time to run, enough time to fight, not enough time for both crews to pretend anymore that they weren't all sailing toward the same reckoning.

"Signal the Kestrel," she said. "All hands. Tell Captain Sterling—" She paused, tasting the words, finding them true for the first time in years. "Tell her Vane's coming, and I'd rather be on her side of the river when he arrives."

Below decks, faintly, she heard the Kestrel's engine turn over.
