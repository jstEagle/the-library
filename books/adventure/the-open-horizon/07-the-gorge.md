## 7. The Gorge

The gorge announced itself an hour before we saw it, as a change in the river's voice. Upstream of the stilt village the Kestrel had been running easy water, broad and brown and slow, the kind of current a captain can steer with two fingers on the wheel while she drinks her coffee. Then the banks began to lean in, the water darkened from tea to iron, and the sound came up through the hull — not the murmur of a river but a grinding, a chewing, like something enormous working its jaw beneath us.

"Half a mile wide at the mouth," Brannick said, standing beside me at the rail with his hands folded behind his back, the way he did when he was pretending to be calm. He'd been an engineer on ocean freighters before the war took his hearing in one ear and gave him this river instead. "Narrowing to maybe forty yards at the throat. I've seen it from the ridge once, thirty years back. Looked like a wound."

"It's the only way up," Tam said from the pilothouse steps. He was nineteen and had never seen a man die, and he said things like that — flat, factual, as if geography were a kindness.

I didn't answer him. I was watching the water ahead, where the current had begun to stack itself into long ridges, and I was watching the sky behind us, where the morning haze sat low and gray over the water we'd come from.

Vane's gunboat was somewhere in that haze.

We'd picked up her smoke at dawn — a smudge on the southern horizon, too steady to be a brush fire, too deliberate to be anything else. Cass Merrow's salvage boat had vanished in the night, slipped away downstream or into some side channel, and I'd told myself that was good news, one less knife at our back. But Vane didn't need knives. Vane had bought himself a gunboat with my debt money before I'd ever signed the first note against the Kestrel, and now he was coming to collect everything: the map, the boat, the route, me.

"Captain." Tam's voice cracked on the word. "Smoke's closer."

"I see it."

"We could beach her. Take to the hills. The riverfolk said there are paths—"

"The riverfolk hid from chart-makers for three hundred years by *running*," I said. "If we run, Vane follows the smoke of our cookstove and finds every village, every landing, every family that ever fed us. And then he doesn't need the map anymore. He just needs soldiers."

Brannick grunted. It was the sound he made when he agreed with something and hated agreeing with it.

"So we go through," I said. "Through the gorge, before he can close the mouth behind us."

"He'll have guns, Elodie."

"Then we'd best be quick."

* * *

The walls rose around us like the ribs of some drowned cathedral. Stone the color of wet slate, streaked white with centuries of birdlime, dripping in threads. The river narrowed and the current doubled and the Kestrel's old hull began to talk to me — a shudder in the deck plates, a groan in the sternwheel shaft, the particular vibration I'd learned over eleven years meant *I am holding together, but ask me no favors.*

I had her at full steam. Brannick stood in the engine room hatch with one hand on the throttle linkage and his good ear turned toward the machinery, listening to it the way a doctor listens to a chest.

"Forty yards," Tam called, reading the gap off the rock walls ahead. "Maybe less."

The gunboat came around the bend behind us like a rumor made flesh. She was a converted patrol launch, black-hulled, a swivel gun bolted to her foredeck under a tarp they hadn't bothered to remove. Her wake spread white across the brown water. Through my glass I could see Vane standing at her rail — that lean, patient silhouette, that stillness he wore like a tailored coat. He wasn't hurrying. He didn't need to hurry. A gorge has only one door, and he intended to stand in it.

"Elodie," Brannick said quietly. "She's gaining. We're heavy — still carrying the village's trade goods, and the coal's wet from the rain two nights back."

"Then throw the trade goods overboard."

"That's their winter—"

"*Throw it.*"

Tam and I heaved crates off the main deck until our arms burned, bolts of cloth and tins of oil and a crate of glass lantern chimneys that burst on the rocks like a flock of startled birds. The Kestrel lifted by inches. Behind us the gunboat's bow wave grew teeth.

The first shot came without warning — a flat crack echoing off the canyon walls, and then the *whip* of something passing overhead, and then, a half-second later, the report arriving properly, as if the sound had been delayed in customs. Water geysered twenty feet off our port bow.

"They're ranging," Brannick said.

"They're telling us to stop."

"Are we stopping?"

I put the wheel hard to starboard, threading the Kestrel between two boulders that the charts — such as they were, for no chart-maker had ever survived this stretch — insisted did not exist. "What do you think?"

The second shot took the pilothouse roof. Glass rained down on Tam and me, and a splinter of roofing iron spun past my cheek close enough to feel its heat. Tam made a sound I'd never heard from him before, high and involuntary, and grabbed the wheel beside me, and to his credit his hands held.

"Get below," I told him.

"No."

"Tam—"

"You'll need someone on the lines when the wheel jams, because it *will* jam, you always say so yourself." His chin was trembling. His voice wasn't. Something in my chest turned over and refused to right itself.

The gorge narrowed. Forty yards became thirty. The current, compressed, stood up in places — whole sections of river moving faster than the Kestrel could push, so that steering became a negotiation rather than a command. Ahead the channel bent left around a spur of rock, and beyond the bend I could see white water, real white water, the kind that eats boats.

And beyond *that*, half-hidden in spray, a chute.

I'd heard the riverfolk describe it the night before, around their fire, when the old woman Sefa had drawn it in the ash with a stick: a slot in the rock where the whole river funneled through a gap barely wider than the Kestrel's beam, dropping eight feet in the length of a boat. *No one runs the chute,* she'd said. *The chute runs whoever is foolish enough to enter it.*

Behind us, the gunboat fired again. This round struck the main deck and tore a hole in it, and I heard Brannick swear below in a voice that carried even through steel.

"Brannick!" I shouted down the hatch. "Report!"

"Rivet popped near the firebox. I'm plugging it. Keep her straight, girl, whatever else you do — keep her *straight*—"

The third shot found the engine room.

I felt it before I understood it — a lurch in the Kestrel's stride, a stutter in the sternwheel's rhythm, the deck going suddenly quiet in the way a room goes quiet when someone stops breathing. Steam screamed from below decks, white and furious, venting through the shot hole.

"*Brannick!*"

His voice came up faintly, muffled, unhurried, terribly calm. "Valve's sheared. I've got to hold it closed by hand or she loses all pressure. You take her through on what's left, Captain. Don't you dare stop for me."

"Don't—" My voice broke on it. Eleven years. Eleven years of his coffee in my galley, his hands rebuilding my engine with parts scavenged from wrecks, his terrible singing on night watches, his one good ear always turned toward my voice like I was the most important sound on the river. "*Don't you do this.*"

"Someone's got to hold the door," he said. "It's what engineers are for. Now run the chute, Elodie. Run it *now*, before that gunboat gets her gun angled down into this slot, because if she does, none of us sees the glacier."

The gorge walls closed to twenty-five yards. The chute opened ahead of us like a wound in the world — a slot of churning green-white water, narrower than any sane captain would attempt, the drop hidden inside its own spray. The gunboat was two hundred yards back and closing, and I could see men scrambling at her swivel gun, re-angling it.

I looked at Tam. Tam looked at me.

"Hold onto something," I said.

I drove the Kestrel at the chute with the last of her dying steam, dead center, throttle wide open, the way you'd drive a blade between ribs. The bow entered the slot and the river took us — took the wheel out of my hands, took the choice out of my hands, took eleven years of careful seamanship and threw it down eight feet of falling water in four seconds that lasted a season. We hit the bottom of the drop with a blow that buckled my knees and threw Tam across the pilothouse floor. The Kestrel groaned like a living thing wounded, shipped a ton of green water over the rails, and then — impossibly, gloriously — she *rose*, wallowing, listing, but rising, and the channel widened and the current gentled and we were through.

Behind us, in the slot, the gunboat's searchlight swept the spray. Her engines idled. She would spend hours finding another way around — portaging, winching, waiting for the water to shift — and by then we'd be days upstream, past caring.

I stood at the broken wheel, shaking, and listened to the silence from below.

"Brannick," I called. Nothing. "BRANNICK."

Tam went down the ladder. I heard him moving through the steam, heard him calling, heard the calling stop.

He came up slowly, gray-faced, holding the brass pressure gauge in both hands like an offering. Brannick had unscrewed it from the manifold before the end — the last working instrument on the boat — and left it where Tam would find it, wrapped in a rag, with a strip of pencil on the back of an old work order:

*Keep her straight. She'll carry you if you carry her. — B.*

Tam set it on the chart table and couldn't look at it. I looked at it for a long time.

The gorge fell silent behind us. Ahead the river ran wide and cold and fast toward the white line on the horizon that was the glacier, and the vault, and the end of everything Vane wanted. My hands wouldn't stop shaking on the wheel, so I let them shake, and I steered anyway.

"Tam," I said finally. "Find me every scrap of dry coal on this boat. We're not slowing down again."

He wiped his face with his sleeve and went.

I stood alone in the shattered pilothouse, the gauge gleaming on the chart table, and felt grief and fury fuse in my chest into something with a hardness I'd never carried before. Vane had taken my boat's heart and my oldest friend in one afternoon, and thought he'd taught me a lesson about debt.

He had. Just not the one he intended.

Ahead, the mountain waited, and the ice was already beginning to sing.
