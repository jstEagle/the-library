## 8. Calving Season

The river stopped being a river an hour before they saw the ice.

It widened into a grey-green lake of meltwater, still as poured glass, and at its far end stood the glacier — a wall of blue-white ice two hundred feet high, scarred with crevasses like old knife wounds, running out of the mountains in a frozen tongue that had been dying for a hundred years. Elodie cut the Kestrel's engine and let them drift. The silence was so complete she could hear her own blood.

"There," Tam said softly, pointing.

The smuggler's map had shown it: a notch in the glacier's western flank, half-hidden by a shoulder of rock, where the ice curved inward like a cupped hand. Behind that curve, if the map's ink could be believed, lay the vault mouth — sealed since before the war, opened only when the calving season tore the face apart.

Elodie raised her glass. The notch was there. And around it, faint as breath on a window, the ice was weeping. Meltwater ran in silver threads down the blue face, feeding the lake beneath them.

"It's early," Brannick would have said. Brannick wasn't there to say it. His absence sat in the engine room like a fourth crew member, filling the space where his voice used to be. Elodie had caught herself twice turning to give him an order he'd never hear.

"How long?" she asked instead.

Tam squinted at the face. "Days. Maybe hours. You can hear it sometimes — before it goes. Like a door groaning on rusted hinges."

As if the mountain had heard him, it groaned.

The sound rolled across the water, deep enough to feel in the deck planks, in Elodie's teeth, in the soles of her boots. Somewhere inside all that ice, something the size of a cathedral had shifted its weight. A flock of birds burst from the rock shoulder above the notch and wheeled away south.

"Captain," Tam said, and didn't finish.

"I know." Elodie turned the wheel. "We anchor in the shallows by the rock shoulder. If it opens, we go in fast and we come out faster. Nobody lingers. Nobody touches anything we don't need."

*And what do we need?* asked a voice in her head that sounded uncomfortably like Vane. *You've come four hundred miles for gold you already know you can't keep.*

She silenced it. There'd be time for choosing later. Right now there was only the ice, and the window, and the sound of the mountain beginning to die.

* * *

Cass Merrow had run rivers all her life, and she had never hated a river more than this one.

The upper reaches had nearly killed her twice — once in a chute full of submerged timber, once when her helmsman froze at the sight of white water stacked three men high. She'd replaced him with a boy who didn't know enough to be afraid, which was nearly as good as courage. Now the *Marrow's Luck* sat anchored in the shadow of the rock shoulder, half a mile down-lake from the Kestrel, close enough to watch, far enough to pretend otherwise.

Through her glass, Cass watched Elodie Sterling's crew lower a skiff. Three figures. They rowed for the notch in the ice.

"Still no sign of Vane?" her mate, Odile, asked.

"Not yet." Cass lowered the glass. That bothered her more than the glacier did. Vane hadn't spent twenty years squeezing a harbor dry by letting other people do his errands. He'd left Grayharbor two days behind them with two gunboats and a hold full of armed men, and a man like that didn't lose a race he'd already rigged.

Unless he didn't need to win the race. Unless he only needed to arrive.

"Odile. Ready the longboat. Six men, rifles under the tarpaulin."

"We going in after Sterling?"

Cass watched the skiff vanish behind the rock shoulder, into the notch, into whatever the ice was about to give up. She thought about the offer she'd made at the confluence — buy out Vane's claim, split the vault — and how Elodie had looked at her afterward, like a woman doing arithmetic with things that couldn't be counted.

"No," Cass said. "We're going to wait until they find it. Then we're going to take it off them at gunpoint, and when Vane shows up — and he will show up — we're going to be standing between him and the treasure looking very reasonable." She smiled the smile that had charmed three harbormasters and buried one. "Let the captains fight over who owns the river. I just want what's in it."

Above the glacier, the clouds were tearing open. The light coming through them was the color of old brass.

And the mountain groaned again — longer this time, deeper, a sound like the world clearing its throat before it spoke.

* * *

Inside the notch, the air was ten degrees colder and smelled of stone and ancient water.

Elodie's lantern found walls of solid ice shot through with gravel and black streaks of volcanic ash — winters upon winters, pressed into glass. The passage narrowed, then opened without warning into a chamber the size of the Kestrel's hold, and there the ice ended.

Stone began.

A wall of fitted blocks, mortared with something dark and seamless, rising out of the glacier floor and vanishing into the ice above. No ornament. No inscription. Just craftsmanship so old and so sure of itself that it made everything Elodie had ever built feel like a child's sandcastle.

In the center of the wall was a door.

Not a vault door, nothing with hinges or locks. A single slab of granite, taller than Tam, carved with a single line of script in a language that predated every chart Elodie had ever stolen a glance at. And below it, set into the stone at knee height, a round depression the size of a dinner plate — empty, waiting, shaped like nothing either of them recognized.

Except Tam did recognize it.

"Captain." His voice had gone strange. He was holding the brass compass they'd taken from the smuggler's skeleton, turning it over in his hands. The needle spun lazily, uselessly — they were too far north, too far from anything magnetic. But on the compass's reverse, which Elodie had never examined closely, a brass disc sat flush with the casing. A disc the size of a dinner plate.

"That's why he carried it," Tam whispered. "That's why he died up here. It's not a compass. It's a key."

The mountain chose that moment to scream.

Not a groan this time — a crack like the sky splitting, and the whole chamber shuddered, and somewhere above them a mass of ice the size of a village sheared away from the face. They heard it hit the lake outside, a boom that came up through the stone floor and into their bones. Water sloshed at their ankles. The meltwater in the passage was rising.

"The calving," Elodie said. "It's starting. That's the window — the map said the vault opens with the first break. The ice is the lock, Tam. When the face comes apart—"

She never finished. Because the wall answered.

Deep in the stone, something released — a sound like a held breath finally let go — and the great slab ground sideways, six inches, a foot, shedding a century of frost. Cold air breathed out of the gap behind it, air that had not moved since before the war, before the chart-makers, before any of them. It smelled of dust and cedar and, faintly, impossibly, of oranges.

Tam laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound. "It's real. It's actually real."

Elodie stood at the edge of the opening with her lantern raised, and the light fell into a corridor lined with cedar shelving, and on the shelving, wrapped in oilcloth and wax, ranked in rows that vanished into darkness, lay the cargo of a world that had ended. Crates stenciled with dead companies' names. Strongboxes. Barrels sealed with lead. Enough wealth to buy the Kestrel a thousand times over. Enough to buy the harbor. Enough, if the wrong hands took it, to buy the river itself — every mile of it, every village, every family that had hidden from the charts for five generations.

*Blood money or burning,* she thought. *Here it is. All of it.*

"Captain," Tam said urgently. "Water's rising. We have maybe an hour before the passage floods."

"Then we look and we leave. Nothing comes out of here today."

"You came four hundred miles to say that?"

"I came four hundred miles to *know*," Elodie said. "There's a difference."

They were still arguing when the light changed outside — a hard white flicker against the ice wall, sweeping across the chamber entrance. Lantern-light. More than one lantern.

Then a voice, amplified by the ice, carrying beautifully across the water the way voices carry in cold places:

*"Miss Sterling! Don't trouble yourself coming out — I'll come to you!"*

Vane.

And behind his voice, muffled but unmistakable, the sound of rifle bolts working, one after another, like insects.

* * *

Cass heard the shots before she understood them.

From the longboat, crouched behind the rock shoulder, she watched Vane's gunboats slide into the lake with their lamps doused, silent as sharks, and she understood that she had been wrong about everything. Vane hadn't raced them. He'd *followed* them — let Elodie find the vault, let Cass shadow Elodie, let everyone do the dangerous work of locating a treasure that appeared on no chart. And now he was collecting.

Rifles cracked across the water. From the notch came answering shouts — Tam's voice, Elodie's voice, the flat crack of return fire. Someone screamed.

"Odile." Cass's mouth had gone dry. "Change of plan."

"What plan? You said we'd wait for them to—"

"They're killing them, Odile. Both crews. Every witness." Cass grabbed the boat's rail as a second salvo flashed in the notch. "He doesn't want partners. He never wanted partners. He wants a story with no survivors."

She should take the *Luck* and run. Every instinct that had kept her alive through thirty years of salvage said *run*. But she was watching the notch, and through the gunfire she could see lantern-light moving inside the vault mouth, and she thought about Elodie Sterling's face at the confluence — that arithmetic look, that woman weighing things that couldn't be counted — and she thought about what it would mean to be the only person alive who knew what was behind that wall of ice.

Or the only person alive who knew what it *cost*.

"Odile," Cass said again, quietly. "Get me close to the notch. And wake everyone aboard."

"Why?"

"Because in about ten minutes," Cass said, drawing both her pistols and checking them by feel in the dark, "there's going to be a lot of unattended gold, and I intend to commit a double cross so magnificent they'll sing about it in Grayharbor."

Above them, the glacier gave a long, splintering roar, and the first true block of the calving season — a cliff of ice tall as a church — leaned slowly, majestically, out over the lake, and began to fall.
