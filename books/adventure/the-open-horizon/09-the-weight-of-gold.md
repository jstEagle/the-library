## 9. The Weight of Gold

The vault breathed cold.

Elodie stood in the antechamber with water dripping from her coat and the lantern shaking in her hand, and the light went out ahead of her like a rumor — across a floor of black stone, past pillars the riverfolk's ancestors had cut when their grandmothers were girls, and into a chamber that swallowed the glow and gave back gold.

Not coins. Not bars. Not the crude plunder of some sunk merchantman. This was wealth arranged like an argument: chests of minted sovereigns stacked in walls, ingots racked in lattices like cordwood, plate and candlesticks and a bishop's crozier lying in state on a slab of granite, all of it furred with frost, all of it glittering under her lantern as if it had been waiting a hundred years for someone to come and admire it.

Behind her, boots rang on stone. Vane came in with two of his men, their rifles slung, their breath smoking, and even Vane — even Harbormaster Silas Vane, who wore greed the way other men wore skin — stopped at the threshold and let out a long, slow exhale.

"Well," he said softly. "There she is."

Cass Merrow stood off to the left, near the wall, arms crossed, saying nothing. Elodie hadn't seen her slip inside. She didn't know yet whose side Cass was on this hour; she suspected Cass didn't either.

Tam was somewhere behind them with the riverfolk guides, down at the vault mouth where the glacier had peeled open like a door. Above them, through forty feet of ice, the mountain groaned — a sound Elodie felt in her teeth, in the soles of her feet, in the old scar along her ribs. The calving had begun.

"Look at it," Vane murmured, walking forward, his gloved fingers trailing over a chest lid. "Enough to buy the whole river three times over. Enough to buy the *government*." He turned to her, and his smile was the one he used at auctions, when he could smell blood in the room. "You understand what happens now, Captain."

"I do," Elodie said.

"The syndicate pays your debt. Pays it twice over. You walk away rich, the Kestrel gets new engines, and this route—" he tapped the drowned smuggler's map, unrolled now on a crate between them, its wax seal cracked, its inked line running upriver to this very chamber "—becomes a freight road. Barges. Dredges. Company towns every fifty miles." He spread his hands. "Progress, Captain. That's all it is. Progress was always coming up this river. All you're deciding is whether you get paid for holding the door."

Elodie looked at the gold.

She made herself look at it properly, because she owed herself that much honesty. She thought about the ledger in her cabin with its red column that never shrank. She thought about thirty days, then twenty, then nine. She thought about standing on the Kestrel's deck at night with no cargo worth carrying and no port that would have her, and how the word *home* had always been a place other people had.

The gold would fix all of it. That was the terrible thing. It would fix everything, cleanly, the way a bullet fixes a headache.

And then she thought of Maren's stilt village in the dawn fog, smoke rising from cookfires, children running the walkways barefoot. Old Sefa teaching her the channel markers that weren't on any chart — the leaning alder, the split rock, the white stone that meant *shoal, turn hard*. The way they'd tested her and then fed her, and how nobody had asked what her family name was, because on the river nobody cared. How Tam had said, two nights ago, watching the glacier burn orange in the sunset: *"They'll dredge the shallows first, Cap'n. Kill the eel runs. Then the villages, because villages are in the way. It won't be cruel. It'll be worse than cruel. It'll be efficient."*

Vane watched her face while she thought all this. He was good at reading faces. He'd built a life on it.

"You're hesitating," he said. "That's sentiment, Captain. Sentiment is just arithmetic you haven't finished."

"No," Elodie said. "It's arithmetic I finished years ago. I just didn't like the total."

She reached into her coat — slowly, because the riflemen shifted at the motion — and drew out not the map but a small brass cylinder, dented, salt-corroded, taken from the smuggler's skeleton in the cave alongside the compass. A detonator. Brannick had found the Kestrel's scuttling charges wired to her keel the day they bought her, left over from some earlier owner's paranoia, and had never gotten around to cutting them out. *Never know when you'll want to sink your own ship proper,* he'd said, laughing. He wasn't laughing anymore. He was dead in the gorge behind them because of this man's gunboats, and the grief in Elodie's chest had cooled into something with an edge.

"What's that?" Vane said. His voice changed. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Brannick's last joke," Elodie said.

She turned the key.

* * *

The charge went off eleven seconds later — Brannick had set the delay himself, years ago, and Elodie had checked it twice by lantern light before they ever entered the ice — and the sound arrived through the glacier as a deep, felt concussion rather than a noise, a blow struck against the bottom of the world.

Down in the gorge, half a mile below and behind them, the Kestrel died well.

Elodie knew it without seeing it. She knew the way the hull would lift as the charges blew her seams open below the waterline, knew how Tam — God forgive her, she'd left him at the wheel with orders and no explanation — would feel the deck go soft under his boots and know exactly what his captain had done. She pictured the river pouring in through the engine room, around the boiler Brannick had kept alive for nineteen years, filling the hold that had carried everything from timber to contraband to hope. The Kestrel would settle onto the gorge floor in the narrowest throat of the channel, on her beam ends, wedged between walls no barge could pass, and there she would stay. A shipwreck as a gate. A grave as a barricade.

Vane's gunboats were in that gorge. Two of them, riding low with armed men, waiting for the vault party to come back down so they could escort the treasure out — or make sure no one else did. When the calving came, when the ice came down and the river surged, those boats would need that channel clear.

It wouldn't be clear. The Kestrel would see to that.

"You stupid—" Vane started, and then the glacier answered for her.

The groan became a crack, the crack became a report like artillery, and dust sifted from the vault ceiling in silver threads. One of the riflemen bolted for the entrance. The other looked at Vane, and Vane looked at the gold, and Elodie watched a man lose an argument with himself in real time.

"We can still take a share!" Vane shouted over the rising roar. "One chest! One, and we run—"

"Run where?" Elodie said. "Your boats are pinned in the gorge behind my ship."

The understanding came into his face then, all of it at once, and it was almost a relief to see. He lunged for her. She was faster — she'd been expecting it since the cave, since the harbormaster's office, since the day she'd signed his paper — and she put the lantern between them and swung it low and hard, and Vane went down onto the frost-furred gold with a sound like a dropped anchor.

Above them, the mountain began to give.

* * *

The vault mouth was a chaos of falling light.

Elodie came out of the ice into a blue-white blaze — the whole glacier face calving at once now, seracs the size of churches peeling away from the summit and dropping five hundred feet into the source-lake, and each impact sent up a wave that hammered the shore and a thunderclap that rolled up the valley and came back changed. The air was full of ice-dust, glittering, blinding, beautiful in the way that only enormous destructive things are beautiful.

Maren was there, hauling the last of the village elders up the scree path. Tam was there — soaked, ash-faced, having swum the outflow channel after beaching the launch — and when he saw her he didn't shout at her, though she could see he wanted to, might later, deserved to. He just grabbed her arm and pointed down-valley.

Through the dust: the gorge. And in the gorge, wedged against the rock walls, the dark shape of the Kestrel already sitting heavy in the current, taking the river's weight, holding the door shut exactly as designed. Vane's gunboats were backing water frantically, trapped between her hull and the rising surge, their crews scrambling onto the rocks like ants off a drowning log.

Vane burst from the vault mouth behind them, alone now, his men scattered, his coat torn, gold sovereigns spilling from his pockets with every stride because in the last moments he'd stuffed them there like a man stuffing fire into his shirt. He got ten yards down the scree before the first wave hit.

Elodie lost sight of him in the spray. She told herself she didn't look for him again.

Cass Merrow appeared at her shoulder, breathing hard, empty-handed, and for a moment the two captains just stood there in the roaring white, watching a hundred years of sealed treasure vanish under the calving ice — the lake surging into the vault mouth, the vault mouth collapsing, the glacier advancing over its own ruins like a tide erasing a footprint.

"You sank your own ship," Cass said. Not accusation. Something closer to wonder.

"I did."

"For people who aren't even your people."

"They are, though," Elodie said. "That's the thing I finally worked out. Took me losing everything to add it up right."

Cass looked at her a long moment. Then she laughed — short, rough, genuine — and clapped Elodie once on the shoulder. "You're the worst businesswoman on this river, Sterling." She turned toward the high trail, toward her own boat and her own survival. "Come find me when you want to be poor together sometime."

Then she was gone into the dust, and the mountain came down, and Elodie stood with Tam and Maren and the riverfolk on the high scree and watched the source of the river close itself forever — ice grinding over ice, the vault, the gold, the map's promise, all of it sealed beneath a hundred feet of fresh blue glacier, unfindable, unsellable, free.

The thunder faded. The dust began to settle. Far below, the river ran brown and churning past the black hull of the Kestrel, standing her last watch in the gorge.

Tam spoke first, quietly. "What do we do now, Cap'n?"

Elodie realized she was smiling. Her face was wet — spray, she decided, mostly spray — and her hands had finally stopped shaking, and for the first time since she'd signed Vane's paper, since maybe ever, the horizon ahead held nothing she owed anyone.

"Now?" she said. "Now we go home."

She turned down the trail, toward the gorge, toward the wreck, toward the stilt-village lights already kindling in the blue dusk downstream — and behind her, with a sound like a held breath released, the glacier settled into silence over everything it had taken, and everything it had saved.
