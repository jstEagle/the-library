## 10. The Open Horizon

The mountain died in the time it took to say a name.

Elodie stood on the black shelf of rock above the gorge, soaked to the bone, and watched the glacier's face come apart like a cathedral falling. Chunks the size of harbor warehouses sheared away and plunged into the flooded vault mouth, and the sound was not a crash but a groan — deep, tectonic, almost mournful — as if the ice itself understood what it was burying. The spray reached her a heartbeat later, cold as a hand on the back of her neck.

Below, where the gorge had been a throat of white water, there was only churning chaos. The Kestrel lay on her side across the channel's narrowest point, her hull split on the rocks Elodie had aimed her for, her decks already gone under. Vane's gunboats — both of them, the *Iron Vow* and the smaller tender — were pinned against the wreck, their engines screaming against the current, their decks awash.

She could see Vane. That was the thing she would carry afterward, the thing she would not be able to set down even when the riverfolk tried to take it from her: she could *see* him.

He stood at the bow rail of the *Iron Vow* in his good gray coat, the one with the brass buttons he kept polished even on the river, and he was shouting orders that no one could hear over the calving. His men were scrambling. Two of them went over the side with lines. One made it to the rock shelf. The other the river took, quick and indifferent, the way it took everything it decided to take.

Vane did not jump.

That was the second thing she would carry. The water rose over the *Iron Vow*'s gunwales and he stood there, gripping the rail, staring upstream — not at Elodie, not at his drowning crew, but at the glacier, at the vault that was sealing itself beneath a hundred thousand tons of falling ice. He was still looking at the treasure when the water took him. His ambition went down with his eyes open.

The river folded over the spot where he'd been and smoothed itself flat, and it was as if no man had ever stood there at all.

* * *

Cass Merrow found her an hour later, when the gorge had gone quiet and the ice had settled into its new shape and the only sound was the river running free and cold through a channel it had never been allowed to run so clean.

Elodie was sitting on the shelf with her boots in the water, watching the current carry splinters of the Kestrel downstream. A plank here. A piece of rail there. A life ring she'd never once needed, spinning slowly in an eddy. Thirty years of her life, broken into kindling, and she felt — she kept turning the feeling over, looking for the grief, and finding instead something that frightened her more.

She felt light.

Cass came down the scree alone, no crew, no weapons she could see, her salvage coat torn at the shoulder and her dark hair plastered to her face. She stopped a few yards off, hands loose at her sides, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"Your boats?" Elodie said finally.

"Gone. Vane's men took the tender's skiff when the shooting started, and the *Iron Vow* took the rest with her." Cass lowered herself onto the rock, an arm's length away, careful as a cat near a dog it didn't know. "I got a look inside the vault, you know. Before it sealed. Forty seconds, maybe. Enough to see the gold. Enough to reach for it."

"And?"

"And the ice came down between me and it like a door slamming." Cass laughed — a short, raw sound with no humor in it. "Forty seconds from the richest woman on the river. I've been chasing that vault for eleven years, Sterling. I sold my boat's engine to fund the last survey. And the mountain kept it."

"The mountain kept it for them," Elodie said. "For the people it belonged to all along."

Cass was quiet a while. The river ran. Somewhere upstream, the riverfolk were already moving — Elodie could hear the low voices, the creak of the stilt-village boats coming down through the gorge to see what the mountain had done.

"You could have taken it," Cass said. "You were inside longer than me. You had time to fill a satchel. Enough to pay Vane twice over, if he'd lived. Enough to disappear."

"I know."

"That's the part I can't work out." Cass turned and looked at her, and there was no charm in her face now, no salesmanship, just a salvage diver's flat honest curiosity. "I've met a hundred captains. Every one of them would have filled the satchel."

"I met one who didn't," Elodie said. "She was standing on a stilt deck three weeks ago being fed fish stew by people who had no reason to trust her, and she realized she'd spent her whole life being paid by people who had every reason to own her." She looked at the water. "I didn't leave the gold because I'm better than you, Cass. I left it because I finally had something worth more."

Cass studied her for a long time. Then she nodded, once, like a woman closing a ledger.

"Vane's syndicate will send someone else," she said. "Men like him don't drown alone. There'll be lawyers. Surveyors. Within a year, maybe two, they'll come up this river asking questions about a wreck in the gorge and a vault that isn't there."

"The vault was never there," Elodie said. "Ask anyone. Ask the riverfolk. There's a glacier, and there's a gorge, and there's a story the old people tell their children to keep them away from the ice." She almost smiled. "By the time the syndicate's men arrive, the story will be so old and so boring that no one will bother digging further."

"And the Kestrel?"

Elodie watched a length of deck planking — the one with the burn mark from her first year as captain, when Tam had nearly set the galley alight — turn in the current and slip beneath the surface for the last time.

"The Kestrel is doing her final job," she said. "Blocking the channel. She'll be a reef by spring. Fish will nest in her engine room." Her voice caught, and she let it. "She was always better at protecting things than carrying cargo."

Cass stood, brushed the scree from her coat, and offered her hand. Elodie took it.

"I'm going downstream," Cass said. "There are other rivers. There are always other rivers." She hesitated. "If the syndicate comes, and you need someone on the inside who knows how they think — send word to Merrow Salvage, care of any harbor south of the delta. I owe the mountain a debt now too."

Then she was gone, climbing the scree toward the ridge, a dark figure against the pale ice, and Elodie sat alone with the river until the riverfolk's boats came through.

* * *

They buried Brannick on the bluff above the village, in the manner of the river people: wrapped in oiled canvas, weighted with stones from the gorge, given back to the current at the widest bend where the water ran deep and slow and kept what it was given. Tam wept without shame. Old Maren, the village matriarch, sang a song in a language that predated every chart on the river, and the whole village sang the refrain, and Elodie sang it too, badly, the words unfamiliar in her mouth but the grief in them universal.

Afterward, Maren found her on the stilt deck where Elodie had been sleeping since the mountain died — the same deck where she'd been fed fish stew as a stranger, three lifetimes ago.

"You're still here," Maren said. It wasn't a question, but it waited for an answer.

"I don't have a boat," Elodie said. "I don't have a debt. I don't have—" She stopped. "I don't have anything, Maren. I'm a captain of nothing."

Maren sat down beside her, slowly, the way old women do when sitting is a negotiation. Below them the village moved through its evening — children running the walkways, nets mended by lamplight, the smell of woodsmoke and river water. Uncharted. Unmapped. Free.

"Let me tell you what you have," Maren said. "You have a gorge full of fish, because that wreck will make the best reef this stretch of river has seen in a century. You have a syndicate that thinks its treasure drowned, because you made it so. You have a debt collector at the bottom of the gorge, which means every family on this river that owed him money is free this morning, and there are more of those families than you know." She took Elodie's hand, and her grip was dry and strong as root-wood. "And you have us. You had us before the mountain, girl. The mountain just made it official."

Elodie looked out at the water. The evening light was going gold and rose across it, and the river ran wide here, running toward a delta she'd never seen, toward an open horizon she'd spent her whole life staring at from the deck of a boat that was never really hers.

She had spent thirty years thinking the horizon was something you sailed toward. Something out there. Something you'd reach if you ran enough cargo, paid enough debt, survived enough tides.

It wasn't. It was this. A lamplit deck. A village that knew her name and used it kindly. A river that had taken everything she owned and given her back to herself.

"Stay," Maren said.

"I'm staying."

"Then there's one more thing." Maren's eyes glinted, and for the first time Elodie saw the mischief in them that the children of the village must see. "The elders met this afternoon. We've been keeping something since before the chart-makers came — the original refuge records, from the sealing of the vault. Our ancestors' own hands, their own words. They should not sit in a chest under my floor anymore. They should be read. Studied. Kept by someone who understands what it costs to protect a river."

Elodie went still. "Maren, I'm not—"

"You're the only person alive who wrecked a boat on purpose to save a people she'd known three weeks," Maren said. "You're exactly the person. The elders have already voted. We only waited to ask because we wanted to see if you'd stay without being asked to stay." She squeezed Elodie's hand. "Keeper of the refuge records. It doesn't pay anything."

Elodie laughed — really laughed, for the first time since Brannick died, maybe for the first time in years — and the sound went out over the water, and the water carried it downstream toward the open horizon.

"I've had worse terms," she said. "From better-dressed men."

Below them, the river ran on, dark and silver and free, carrying the last splinters of the Kestrel toward the sea — and somewhere in the gorge, in the cold and the green and the quiet, a reef was already growing.
