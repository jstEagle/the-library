## 1. The Debt in the Dirt

The tide went out at four and took the town's pretense with it. By half past, Puerto Salado was all ribs and rust — fishing boats tilted on their keels like sleeping animals, the mudflats steaming under a sun that hadn't yet learned mercy, and the smell of salt rot so thick you could chew it. Dominic Delacroix had been in the mud since dawn, on her knees in the shallows of a drowned churchyard, teasing a silver spoon out of three hundred years of silt with a trowel blade worn thin as a promise.

She was good at this. That was the trouble. Good enough that people kept finding her.

"Delacroix." The voice came from the seawall, flat and unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once had to raise it to be heard. "They said I'd find you elbow-deep in something."

Dominic didn't turn around. She finished loosening the spoon's stem, eased it free, and held it up to the light — tarnished black, but whole. Somewhere behind her, Ivo Marchetti's shadow fell long across the water, reaching almost to her knees, as if he'd already laid claim to everything she touched.

"Churchyard's consecrated ground," she said. "Salvage rights run through the diocese. You're standing on somebody's grave, Marchetti."

"I'm standing on an opportunity. You never could tell the difference." He came down the steps anyway, careful with his shoes, a man who crossed mud the way other men crossed borders — deliberately, and only when there was something worth taking on the far side. Up close he smelled of cedar and engine oil, the cologne of a man who funded expeditions the way other men bought racehorses. "How's the debt?"

The word landed the way he'd intended. Dominic set the spoon in her tray, wiped her hands on her thighs, and finally looked at him. Ten years since she'd seen him last, at a funeral where he'd shaken her hand too hard and said *your mother would be proud* with the sincerity of a man reading someone else's eulogy. He hadn't aged so much as hardened — silver at the temples now, the same surgical calm in his eyes, the eyes of a man who catalogued people the way he catalogued artifacts: by condition and provenance.

"My debt," she said, "is my business."

"It's forty thousand, give or take. To a man in Belém who doesn't forgive, only refinances." He smiled without warmth. "I do my research. It's what I'm paying you for, after all."

The tide sighed back in over the flats, cold water finding her shins, and Dominic let it. Better than letting him see her flinch. Around them the salvage crew — Tomas and the twins, old Ferreira with his bad hip — had gone conspicuously quiet, pretending to work while listening with their whole bodies. Family, more or less. The kind you chose, which meant the kind you could lose.

"You tracked me across two countries to talk about my finances," she said. "Say what you actually came for."

"The Sombra."

The name went through her like cold water through cloth. She kept her face still because she'd had ten years of practice keeping her face still, but inside, something old and buried shifted its weight.

"There is no Sombra source," she said. "There are stories. Drunk boatmen, missionary letters, a conquistador's diary nobody's authenticated. My mother chased a story and the river kept it."

"Your mother got within thirty kilometers of it." Ivo crouched beside her tray, studying the spoon with idle interest. "We both know that. Her field notes say so."

The world narrowed to a point. "Her notes burned with her."

"Some of them did." He straightened, brushing mud from his knee with two fingers, fastidious even now. "A copy survived. Don't ask me how — provenance is a delicate subject between us, I think. The point is this: I have the route. What I don't have is anyone who can read that river the way she could. The rapids, the villages, the places where the maps go blank. I need a Delacroix."

"No."

"You haven't heard the number."

"It isn't the number." She stood, water streaming off her, and faced him fully. This was the part she'd rehearsed for a decade, in dive bars and detention cells and the gray hours before dawn: the refusal, clean and final, the inheritance declined. "You want to put my mother's name on your expedition. You want to walk into the place that killed her wearing her handwriting as a map. And you want *me* to hold the compass. I've spent ten years digging other people's holes so I wouldn't have to stand in that one."

Ivo watched her with something that wasn't quite respect and wasn't quite amusement — the expression of a man confirming a specimen matched its description. Then he took an envelope from inside his jacket and laid it on the seawall's flat capstone, weighting it with a stone against the wind.

"Fifty thousand clears you in Belém. Another fifty on arrival at the head of navigation. Fifty more when we make the source." He said it evenly, like reading coordinates. "And the notes stay mine. Your name goes on nothing but the payroll."

"That's supposed to tempt me?"

"What tempts you," he said, "is that you've read those notes. Or you think you have. You were nineteen when she died, and you've spent every year since telling yourself she was reckless, that she chose the river over you, that there was nothing out there worth what it cost." He picked his way back toward the steps, then paused, not turning. "But you'll come, Dominic. Because if you don't, some other archaeologist reads her route, and her last words become someone else's footnote. And because the third payment buys you something you've wanted longer than you've wanted the money."

"And what's that?"

He glanced back, and for one unguarded moment there was something almost human in his face — or a very good imitation of it. "To know why she went. Everyone else gets to wonder. You get to stand where she stood."

Then he was gone, up the steps and into the white glare of the street, leaving the envelope pinned under its stone like a butterfly under glass.

* * *

She didn't touch it until dark.

Dominic sat on the porch of the room she rented above Ferreira's boathouse, the envelope on her knee, the harbor going violet below her. Moths bumped the lamp. Somewhere down the coast a generator knocked along, and the tide came in over the churchyard she'd been stripping all day, covering the graves again, doing what water always did — keeping things, and giving them up only on its own schedule.

Fifty thousand. The number sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Belém would burn the paper. The crew would eat for a season. Tomas could get his daughter's teeth fixed; Ferreira could retire the hip he complained about and secretly loved complaining about. All of it downstream of a yes she hadn't given yet.

She opened the envelope. Inside: a contract, dense and bloodless, and beneath it a single photocopied page. Her mother's handwriting hit her like a hand to the sternum — the sharp left-leaning script, the little arrows she used to mark current direction, the marginalia in Portuguese that she'd always said was for keeping secrets from the crew. *Sombra, km 214. The water sings before you see it. If I don't come back, tell Dominic the compass points home, not north.*

Dominic read it four times. On the fifth, she noticed what she should have noticed first: the paper wasn't old. The copy was recent, but the original ink — visible faintly through the back — was newer than her mother's death. Someone had written this *after*. Someone had finished her mother's sentence.

Or forged it.

Either way, Ivo Marchetti had it, and he thought she wouldn't know the difference, and he was wrong, and knowing she was wrong-proof gave her no comfort at all, because it meant the only way to learn the truth about the last page of her mother's life was to follow it into the green dark of the river country with the monsoon coming and a man who measured everything in claims.

She signed the contract at the boathouse table by lamplight, the pen scratching like something small trying to get out. Then she climbed the stairs and woke Rhea Okafor, who took one look at her face and reached for the kettle without asking questions, which was why Dominic loved her.

"How much?" Rhea said.

"Enough."

"How bad?"

Dominic looked out the window at the black harbor, at the rain starting somewhere offshore, too far to hear yet, coming all the same.

"She left a note," Dominic said. "It's in her handwriting. And it isn't old."

Rhea poured the tea and said nothing for a long moment, steam rising between them like a small ghost.

"When do we leave?"
