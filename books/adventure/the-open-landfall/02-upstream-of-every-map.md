## 2. Upstream of Every Map

The river changed its mind about us on the third day.

One morning it ran brown and slow as spilled tea, and by noon it had turned the color of old glass, green-black and deep enough to swallow our sound. The Sombra narrowed between walls of karst that rose like the ribs of something buried, and the engine noise we'd carried for two days bounced off the stone and came back doubled, so that it seemed we were being followed by another boat, another crew, another woman at the tiller who kept looking over her shoulder.

Rhea sat in the bow with her journal open on her knees, shielding the page from the spray with her body. She wrote the way she did everything—quickly, without apology, her pencil stub worn down to a nub she kept behind her ear like a cigarette. I'd known her six years. I knew how she took her coffee (burnt), how she slept (badly, one hand always on her knife), how she lied (badly, which was why I trusted her). I did not know what she was writing now, and that was beginning to matter.

Ivo stood at the stern with his map case open against the rail, checking bearings he didn't need to check. He'd hired me to chart the route, and then he charted it himself every morning, in a small precise hand, consulting a notebook he kept zipped inside his jacket even in this heat. I'd told myself it was habit. Rival archaeologists are made of habit; it's what's left when you burn through everything else.

But habits don't make you steer around a sandbar that isn't marked on any survey.

"Left," Ivo said quietly, not looking up. "Take the left channel."

"The right is deeper."

"The left." And when I hesitated: "There's a submerged tree at the bend. It's been there eleven years."

Eleven years. My mother had been dead twelve.

I took the left channel because arguing would have meant explaining, and I wasn't ready to explain anything, least of all to myself. The boat slid past the drowned trunk, black and furred with weed, exactly where he said it would be, and I felt the cold of it settle into my shoulders like a passenger.

> **From the journal of Rhea Okonkwo, day 9:**
>
> The villages up here have no reason to lie to us and every reason not to speak at all, but they remember her. That's the strange thing. We stop for water and an old man looks at Dominic and says nothing, and then he brings out a tin box, and inside the tin box is a photograph gone soft with damp—a woman standing on this same bank with her sleeves rolled, laughing at someone outside the frame.
>
> She has Dominic's mouth. Nobody says so. Everybody thinks it very loudly.
>
> They feed us river fish and cassava and tell us the water will rise before the week is out. They tell us about the year the strangers came, the ones with the good boots and the bad manners, and how one of them went upriver alone and did not come back, and how the river keeps what it takes. Then they look at Dominic again, and one of the old women touches her wrist, just briefly, the way you'd touch a doorframe for luck, and goes back inside.
>
> Dominic stands there holding a fish she hasn't asked for, wearing her mother's face like a coat that doesn't fit, and I watch her decide not to cry. I've watched her do it for six years. I'm starting to wonder what it costs.

* * *

That night we camped on a shelf of rock above a set of rapids the maps called unrunnable and the villagers called, simply, *the teeth*. Ivo built the fire too close to the waterline and I moved it without asking, and he watched me do it with an expression I couldn't read, and that frightened me more than the rapids did. You can survive a man who is your enemy. It's the unreadable ones who get people killed.

He spread his charts on a flat stone and weighted the corners with pebbles, and I made the mistake of looking properly for the first time. Not glancing—looking. The paper was old. The ink had bloomed where damp had gotten into it years ago, and the fold lines were worn soft as cloth, creased and re-creased along the same axes a hundred times. Surveyors' charts don't age like that. Field notes do. Notes that have been carried in a jacket pocket through twelve years of weather, taken out, studied, folded again along the same tired lines.

And in the margin, in a hand I knew better than my own—the quick forward slant, the way she crossed her sevens—a notation. A depth sounding. A date. The monsoon month, the year my mother stopped writing letters home.

"You've seen these before," Ivo said. He hadn't turned around. He'd heard my breathing change, or he'd been waiting for exactly this, or both.

"They're not yours."

"They're mine now." He said it mildly, the way men say terrible things when they believe the terrible thing is justified. "She signed them over. There was a contract. There was money involved, Dominic, there was always money involved—your mother was not the saint the villages have decided she was. She sold me that route when she still had breath to sign."

"She died before she could reach it."

"She died *because* she wouldn't turn back." He finally looked at me, and his face was open and reasonable and utterly without warmth. "Which is why I hired you. Not for your charts. For your instinct for when to turn back. I'm paying you to be the thing she couldn't afford to be."

I should have thrown his charts into the fire. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone, that I thought about it—I thought about it with my hands already moving—and then I looked downstream at the dark water and the sleeping crew, at Rhea curled against her pack with the pencil still behind her ear, at Tomas and the twins wrapped in their tarp like a single animal, and I understood that burning those notes would burn the only map out of these gorges that anyone possessed. My mother's handwriting was the trail. Every mark she'd left was a warning or a waypoint, and I couldn't tell yet which was which.

So I folded them back along their soft old lines and handed them to him, and hated myself with a thoroughness that surprised me.

* * *

> **From the journal of Rhea Okonkwo, day 11:**
>
> She knew.
>
> That's what I can't get past. All that business at the dock about needing to survey fresh, needing soundings, needing weeks—she knew the route. Knew it the way you know a scar. Today at the second village she steered us through the narrows before Ivo finished giving the order, called the eddy by name, *the widow*, same word the old ferryman used, and when I asked her afterward how she knew the local names she looked at me with that flat gray honesty of hers and said, "I've read the reports."
>
> Reports. Right. I've read the reports too. They don't contain pet names for eddies.
>
> Here's what I think: she took this job for the money, fine, we all take jobs for the money, that's the whole rotten economy of our lives. But she took it knowing whose footsteps we'd be walking in, and she let us walk in them anyway, and she let Ivo pretend his little mime show of expertise while he navigates off a dead woman's stolen notebook, and she said nothing. Days of nothing. While the villages fed us and touched her wrist for luck and mourned a woman she never once mentioned.
>
> I'm angry at her. I keep writing that sentence hoping it'll come out differently. What I actually am is scared, because if she knew this much and said nothing, what else is she carrying? What else does she know about what's waiting up there?
>
> Tomorrow I ask her. Properly. Face to face, no river between us.
>
> God, I hope she lies well for once. It would be easier to forgive.

* * *

She didn't give me the chance to lie at all.

It came at dusk on the twelfth day, in the last village before the gorges closed overhead for good—a village of nine houses and a shrine to the river built from boat ribs, where the headman took one look at me and sent a boy running for a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. Inside was a coil of rope, river-stiff, and a carabiner stamped with an expedition mark I recognized from photographs I'd spent ten years avoiding. My mother's gear. Left here like a deposit against a return that never came.

The headman pressed it into my hands and spoke slowly, so I'd understand him. "She said, if her daughter comes, give it back. She said her daughter would come from downstream, following the money." His eyes were kind and pitiless. "She said to tell you: the notes are lies after the third gorge. She fixed them wrong on purpose. Ask the man who bought them why he trusts them."

Rhea heard every word. Of course she did; Rhea hears everything, it's her gift and her curse. She stood very still beside me with the oilcloth dripping from my hands, and I watched the arithmetic finish itself behind her eyes—the eddies, the narrows, the widow, the left channel, eleven years, twelve years—all of it clicking into place with an almost audible snap.

"You knew," she said. Not loud. Worse than loud. Quiet, the way ice is quiet. "You knew the whole time, and you let him lead us by the nose off a dead woman's forged map, and you said nothing. To me. Six years, Dom, and you said *nothing*."

"Rhea—"

"How far?" Her voice cracked on it. "How far did you know? From the dock? From the first night? When I showed you the route I'd plotted—was that a joke to you?"

"It wasn't like that."

"Then tell me what it was like." She stepped closer, and the firelight caught the wet at the corner of her eye, and she hated herself for it, I could see her hating it. "Because tomorrow we enter the gorges, and I need to know whether I'm following you or following *her*, and whether either of you gives a damn if the rest of us come back."

Upstream, thunder walked along the rim of the world, and the first fat drops of the monsoon began to strike the river like small dark stones.

I opened my mouth to answer her, and found I honestly did not know which woman I'd be leading them to meet.
