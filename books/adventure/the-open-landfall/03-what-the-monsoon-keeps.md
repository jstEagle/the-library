## 3. What the Monsoon Keeps

The first storm band came in from the west like something with intent.

Dominic felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind the ears, a hush along the river where the birds had been arguing all morning and then simply stopped. The water went the color of gunmetal. Then the light changed, greenish and strange, and the rain arrived not as a curtain but as a wall, marching across the jungle toward them with a sound like a crowd applauding.

"Tarps! Double-stake everything!" she shouted, and the crew moved. They'd been moving together for six weeks now, long enough that she didn't have to say it twice. Mateo threw himself at the supply crates. Rhea was already cinching the canoe lines with the quick, economical knots of someone who had grown up on water that could kill you. Even Okafor, who complained about everything from the mosquitoes to the coffee to the humidity's effect on his sinuses, worked without a word.

Ivo stood apart under the lean-to, watching the storm come, and Dominic watched him watch it. There was something wrong in the set of his shoulders. Not fear—calculation. She had spent ten years reading men like him across auction tables and dig sites: men who looked at catastrophe and saw leverage.

The rain hit them like a dropped ocean.

* * *

They sheltered through the worst of it in a hollow beneath an overhang of black stone, the river rising below them in brown, muscular surges, carrying whole trees past their camp like matchsticks. The sound was enormous. It got inside your chest and rearranged things.

Rhea found her there, pressed against the rock, and handed her a tin cup of something hot and terrible. "The bridge," Rhea said.

"I know."

"The cables were frayed when we crossed this morning. I saw it. I told Ivo."

Dominic closed her eyes. "What did he say?"

"He said we'd cross back before dark." Rhea's voice was flat, which for Rhea meant furious. "Dominic. The storm came early. He knew the forecast bands were stacking. The pilot radioed it twice."

She opened her eyes and looked downriver, to where the rope bridge should have been. She couldn't see it through the rain, but she could hear it—or rather, she couldn't. Somewhere in the last hour, the Sombra's floodwater had taken it.

Twelve people on the wrong side of a rising river. One claim site, three days' climb above them, on dry high ground the water would never touch. And one man whose notes—the notes he'd sworn were his own survey work, the notes Dominic had recognized in week two as her mother's handwriting, her mother's abbreviations, her mother's obsessive little marginal stars—needed the crew to reach it before anyone else did.

"He wants us cut off," Rhea said quietly. "Stranded up here until the water drops. Until we're desperate enough to push straight for the source instead of going back for resupply. He said as much to Okafor. Okafor told me because Okafor is many things but he isn't that."

Dominic turned the cup in her hands. Her mother had died on this river. Not on this bend, maybe not within fifty kilometers of this bend, but on this river, chasing the same gray water that was currently trying to eat their camp. Everyone had always assumed it was obsession that killed her. Standing here, soaked and cold and certain, Dominic understood for the first time that it might have been arithmetic. A man like Ivo, doing sums with other people's lives.

"We go down," she said.

Rhea stared at her. "Through that?"

"The river crests tonight and starts to fall by morning if the upper bands break. There's a chute on the south bank—I marked it years ago, off my mother's notes, before I ever knew what they were. Limestone shelf, forty meters of scramble, comes out below the flood line." She paused. "It's not safe. Nothing about tonight is safe. But waiting here is just drowning slowly while Ivo decides what we're worth."

Rhea looked out at the rain for a long moment. Then she said, "You knew the route all along. This whole time."

"Yes."

"You let him lead us like we were—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. When she spoke again her voice was low and steady, which was worse than shouting. "After the bridge, you tell me everything. Or I take the crew down that chute myself and you can explain it to Ivo."

"Deal," Dominic said, and meant it, and hated how much it cost to mean it.

* * *

They went down in the dark, because there was no version of the night that wasn't dark; the storm had eaten even the lightning's generosity. Dominic went first with a headlamp shuttered to a sliver, calling holds back over her shoulder, and the crew came after her in a roped line, twelve bodies moving through the rain like one slow animal.

The limestone was slick as soap. Water ran down the shelf in ropes and sheets, finding every crack, prying at every handhold. Twice the rock gave way under someone—once under Mateo, once under Okafor—and both times the rope held and both times Dominic's heart performed some violent gymnastic routine and both times she kept her voice level, because the line moved at the speed of the calmest voice on it.

Halfway down, wedged into a hollow the size of a coffin, she stopped the line and pressed her palm against the wall. Under the slime, under the rain-cut grooves, there was a mark: a chiseled star, five-pointed, small, deliberate.

Her mother's star. The same one that marched down the margins of the stolen field notes. The same one Dominic had been doodling unconsciously since she was nine years old, without ever knowing why.

She put her forehead against the wet stone and let the rain hammer her back, and for three seconds she allowed herself to grieve in a place where no one could see her face.

Then she called the next hold, and the line moved.

* * *

They came out of the chute at two in the morning onto a gravel bank well below the flood line, and the river beside them was a monster in full voice—wide, brown, tearing at its banks—but they were above it. Barely. Dry-ish. Alive.

Dominic counted heads twice. Twelve. She counted a third time because the first two didn't feel real.

Rhea got a fire going under a tarp lean-to, coaxing flame out of damp wood with the patience of a saint or a smuggler. People stripped off soaked outer layers and huddled, steaming, passing around the emergency rations Mateo had had the foresight to waterproof. Someone laughed—a short, disbelieving bark—and then several someones laughed, the ragged laughter of people who had been afraid together and come out the other side.

Ivo was not among them. He had stayed above the flood line with the survey gear and two of the porters who owed him money. In the morning, Dominic knew, he would radio down with concern in his voice and instructions in his mouth. *Push for the source. Time is short. The claim window closes.*

She sat at the edge of the firelight with Rhea and told her everything. The debt—not gambling, not vanity, but the salvage company she'd guaranteed for a man who skipped to Cartagena with the bond money. The contract. The field notes she'd recognized on day one and said nothing about, because saying something meant admitting her mother's handwriting still had power over her, and she had built ten years on the belief that it didn't.

Rhea listened all the way through without interrupting. When she finished, Rhea fed a stick into the fire and said, "Your mother's notes. Do they end at the source?"

"They end before it," Dominic said. "There's a gap. Three days of route, missing. And then a final page I've never been able to read—it's water-damaged, sealed in wax paper, and I've never let myself open it."

"Why not?"

"Because everything else in those notes led somewhere bad," Dominic said. "And because part of me thinks whatever's on that last page is the only true thing she ever wrote down, and I'm not sure I want to know what it says."

Rhea nodded slowly, watching the fire. Then she looked up, and her eyes went past Dominic's shoulder, up the south bank, to where the limestone cliff rose black against the storm-light.

"Dominic," she said. "Look at the base of the shelf. Fifty meters upstream. Is that—"

Dominic turned. The rain had eased to a steady whisper, and where the firelight reached the cliff face, the rock had been cut away—long ago, deliberately—in a clean vertical seam, half-hidden by roots. And carved beside it, weathered but unmistakable, small and precise:

A five-pointed star.

Below it, in letters her hands knew better than her own signature, a single word:

*SOMBRA.*

The river roared. The rain kept falling. And Dominic Delacroix stood up out of the mud, her mother's ghost standing up with her, and started toward the seam in the rock.
