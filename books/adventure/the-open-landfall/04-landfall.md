## 4. Landfall

The rain stopped the way it always does at the end of a monsoon band—not gradually, but all at once, like a held breath finally released. In the sudden quiet, the source cave exhaled. Cold air moved against my face, carrying the mineral smell of deep water and something older, something like wet stone and crushed leaves and the ghost of smoke from a fire that had gone out years ago.

We stood at the lip of the chamber, five of us, soaked to the bone, our headlamps cutting cones through the dark. Behind us, the passage we'd descended was already running with water, the flood reclaiming the route inch by inch. Ahead, the cave opened into a cathedral of stone, and at its center, fed by some artery of the mountain we would never see, the Sombra rose from a black pool without a sound.

I had imagined this moment my whole life. I had imagined it as my mother's moment, mostly—her triumph, her vindication, the thing she'd traded everything for. I'd imagined standing where she stood, reading her name in the water. What I hadn't imagined was how small it would make me feel, and how, strangely, that smallness would feel like relief.

"Dom." Rhea's voice was careful. She had her hand on my shoulder, and I realized I'd been standing there a long time, not moving, water still dripping off my elbows into the mud. "The passage. We've got maybe an hour before it's gone."

"I know."

"Whatever's in here—we take it and go."

I turned my lamp toward the far wall, where a shelf of dry rock jutted above the pool's high-water line. And there it was. A tin cache, rusted orange at the seams, wedged into a crack and sealed with wax that had gone amber with age. My mother's mark was scratched into the lid—the little spiral she used to sign her field notes, the one I used to draw in the margins of my schoolbooks without knowing why.

I crossed to it with my heart going like a fist against a door.

The wax crumbled under my thumb. The lid came up with a groan of oxidation. Inside: a notebook wrapped in oilcloth, dry as the day it was sealed. A bundle of survey flags, faded past their colors. And at the bottom, a brass compass, its glass clouded, its needle hanging motionless—broken, or maybe just done.

I unwrapped the notebook with hands that wouldn't quite obey me. My mother's handwriting filled the first pages—elevations, flow rates, the careful shorthand of a woman who trusted measurements more than people. But the further I read, the less it was a survey. The entries shortened. The numbers gave way to sentences.

*Day 41. The river doesn't want us here. I keep telling myself that's superstition. It isn't. It's arithmetic.*

*Day 47. Sent the others back down. Told them the funding collapsed. It didn't. I couldn't carry them into this. If I'm wrong, I'm wrong alone.*

*Day 52. I found it. God help me, I found it, and the first thing I felt was fear.*

*Day 53. I understand now why the villages seal this place. Not because it's sacred. Because it's enough. Anything this true will be taken apart by people who need it to be less than it is. I came here to claim it. I'm leaving it claimed by no one.*

*To whoever finds this—my daughter, if it's you—I'm sorry. Not for coming. For making you think you had to follow.*

The cave was very quiet. Somewhere above us, thunder moved through the mountain like a slow animal turning over.

I read the last page twice. It was dated the day before she died—before the flood took her on the descent, before the river wrote its own ending to her expedition. One line, underlined twice in ink gone brown:

*Some things are not doors. They are walls, and the wall is the gift.*

Rhea came and stood beside me. She didn't read over my shoulder. She just waited, the way she'd waited through every bad night on this river, every silence I'd mistaken for privacy and she'd known was grief.

"She got here," I said. My voice came out rough. "She got here and she chose to leave it."

"Then you already know what to do."

I looked at the compass in my hand. The needle that had pointed me up every river of my adult life, the one thing of hers I'd carried through a decade of other people's dig sites, other people's debts, other people's names on my contracts. It was broken. It had probably been broken since the day she sealed it here. I'd been navigating by a dead thing and calling it inheritance.

I knelt at the edge of the pool. The water was cold past cold, older than weather. I wrapped the compass back in the oilcloth, set the notebook on top of it, and pressed them both into the silt at the waterline, where the rising flood would take them the way it had taken everything else—completely, and without malice.

The Sombra moved over them without a ripple. The mountain closed its account.

"Okay," I said. I stood up, and my knees ached, and I had never felt lighter. "We're done here. Let's go home."

* * *

Ivo was waiting at the high camp when we came up out of the gorge, and he knew before we said anything. I watched him read it in the way we moved—in the absence of sample cases, in the mud to our thighs, in the fact that none of us were running.

"Tell me you at least surveyed the approach," he said. "Tell me you brought back coordinates."

"I brought back something better." I held out the waterproof case I'd taken from his own equipment cache two nights before, when the rope bridge was still standing and I still believed the failure was an accident. Inside: the correspondence that proved the bridge had been cut, that proved he'd planned the stranding, that proved he'd been willing to trade four lives for a claim he couldn't have reached without us. "Your own handwriting, Ivo. It travels well."

He looked at the case for a long moment. Rain started again, soft and businesslike, beading on his shoulders.

"You'd burn ten years of your mother's work to spite me."

"I'd burn it to keep my crew," I said. "That's the difference between us. You think the site is the treasure. It isn't. It was never the treasure."

"And the evidence?"

"Goes to the expedition insurers, the river authorities, and every funding board you'll ever approach—unless you sign a statement that the source is unreachable, that the route is destroyed, and that the Sombra expedition failed. All of it. In your own words, in your own hand." I let the rain run down my face while he did the arithmetic. He was good at arithmetic. It was people he'd always mispriced.

He signed it on the tailgate of the supply truck, using my back as a desk, and if his signature was furious, well. Fury keeps too.

* * *

We went downriver through the villages that remembered my mother's name, and this time I gave them the truth instead of the legend: she came, she turned back, she chose to leave it alone. The old woman in the third village—the one who had fed us eels and suspicion in equal measure on the way up—listened, and then she pressed a handful of river stones into my palm, gray and green and worn smooth.

"For the one who let the water keep it," she said.

I didn't correct her. I'd spent my whole life correcting people about my mother. It turned out the correction had never been mine to make.

We reached the coast on a morning so clear it felt like an apology from the sky. The crew stood on the dock in the salt wind—Rhea, Okafor, the twins, all of us sunburned and thinner and permanently rearranged—and nobody asked what came next, because we all already knew. There would be other contracts. There would be other rivers. There would be work, and we would do it together, and the name on the contract this time would be ours.

Rhea found me at the rail as the town came up out of the haze, and she was writing in that battered journal of hers, the one she'd kept through the whole ascent, and she didn't hide it when I looked. She turned it toward me instead, open to the last page, and let me read.

* * *

*From the journal of Rhea Okonkwo:*

*We came back down the river with nothing, which is how I know we got what we came for.*

*I keep thinking about the compass. Dom never told me what it meant to her, but I watched her carry it for ten years like a question she was afraid to answer, and I watched her put it down in the dark and stand up different. You don't bury something like that unless you've finally read it right. It was never pointing north. It was pointing at her.*

*The insurers will want a report. The boards will want coordinates. Ivo will want revenge, eventually, because men like Ivo keep their losses in a drawer and take them out on anniversaries. Let them all want. We have the stones, and the eels in the third village, and the way Okafor sings off-key in the rain, and the twins' terrible card games, and Dom at the rail this morning looking like a woman who has finally arrived somewhere instead of fled somewhere.*

*She asked me last night if I thought her mother would have minded. The burial. The silence. The whole expedition ending in a statement signed in the rain.*

*I told her the truth: that her mother wrote an apology instead of a map, which means she left the only thing she had left to give—permission. Dom just had to walk far enough upriver to read it.*

*Landfall tomorrow. I don't know what we'll be when we're not an expedition anymore. I think we'll be a family. I think we already are.*

*Dom says there's a wreck off the southern shoals, unclaimed, unsalvaged, and a crew berth with my name on it if I want it.*

*I want it.*
