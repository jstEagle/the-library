## 1. The Tide That Lied

The tide went out so far that morning it seemed to be making a point.

Wendell Eriksen noticed it first as a wrongness in the sound of the water — the gulls were complaining from rocks that should have been under six feet of sea, and the fishing boats at Khoral Harbor sat tilted on their moorings like drunks leaning against a fence. He had lived on this coast for four years, long enough to know its moods the way a man knows a dog that bit him once: with respect, and without any illusions about affection.

"Look at that," said Mrs. Pellish, his client for the day, standing on the shingle with her hands clasped behind her back in the manner of someone who had read that guides appreciated enthusiasm. "The sea's gone and left."

"The sea does that," Wendell said. "It comes back too. Usually when you've gotten comfortable."

He was thirty-eight years old and he guided tourists along the cliff paths of the Khoral coast for eleven coppers a day, twelve if they wanted the lighthouse story, which everyone did because the lighthouse story involved a ghost and a shipwreck and ended on a suitably melancholy note that made people feel they'd gotten their money's worth of tragedy without any of it sticking to them. It was not the life he had imagined for himself. The life he had imagined had involved summits.

But that was the arithmetic of disgrace: you took what was left after the subtraction.

Mrs. Pellish was a widow from the inland wheat towns, sixty if she was a day, with a walking stick she used more as a pointer than a support and an unquenchable delight in everything the coast offered. She was, Wendell had decided within the first hour, the best kind of client — the kind who was happy before anything happened to make her happy.

"That rock there," she said now, pointing the stick out toward the exposed seabed, "it looks like a sleeping animal."

"It's called the Sow," Wendell said. "Local children are told it will wake up and eat them if they swim past the breakwater. This is a lie, but an efficient one. Drownings dropped by half after they started telling it."

"You're very good at this," Mrs. Pellish said, in a tone that suggested she meant it as a compliment and possibly also as a diagnosis.

"I'm very good at lying pleasantly," Wendell said. "It's the same skill set."

This was the sort of thing he said to clients because it sounded like self-deprecating humor, and people laughed, and nobody ever asked what he meant by it. Four years of practice had made him fluent in the dialect of the almost-true. Say something honest enough to be disarming, vague enough to be safe. It was the same principle as walking a scree slope: commit your weight only where you can see the ground hold.

Except today the ground was doing something unprecedented, and Wendell felt the old itch start up under his sternum like a splinter working loose.

* * *

By midmorning half the town had come down to look. The tide had retreated beyond anything in living memory — old Manco Ferro, who claimed to remember everything back to the war and lied about most of it, swore he'd seen nothing like it since he was a boy, and even then only half as far. The seabed lay bare for nearly a quarter mile: ribbons of kelp drying in the sun, stranded starfish curled like angry fists, pools of trapped fish flashing silver as gulls dive-bombed them in a frenzy of opportunism that Wendell found frankly relatable.

And there, at the far edge of the exposure, where the cliffs dropped into the sea —

"There's a door," Mrs. Pellish said.

There was indeed a door. Or rather, a mouth. A cave opening in the base of the headland, tall as a church door and twice as dark, framed in rock that the sea had spent centuries carving into something almost architectural — columns, lintels, a suggestion of deliberate design that made the skin prickle. Water still trickled out of it in thin sheets, draining off the stone like the cave itself was sweating.

Around Wendell, the crowd had gone quiet in a way crowds rarely did. He noticed it professionally, the way he noticed weather. Fishermen stood with their arms folded. Women held their children's hands tighter. Nobody moved toward it.

"That's the one," said a voice near him — young Tam Wicker, the harbor boy, fifteen and all elbows, who hung around the ferry office hoping to be useful and mostly succeeded at being underfoot. Tam's voice had dropped into a hush usually reserved for funerals. "Nobody goes in there."

"Why not?" Mrs. Pellish asked, delighted. "Is it dangerous?"

"It takes a toll," Tam said solemnly.

"A toll! Like a bridge?"

"Like a *toll*." Tam looked around to make sure he had his audience. "The cave charges. Anything you carry in, it keeps a piece of. Sailors used to leave coins at the mouth before the water covered it again. My gran said her gran saw a man go in with a full purse and come out with empty pockets and no memory of the walk home."

"And what do you say?" Wendell asked him quietly.

Tam shrugged, suddenly awkward. "I say my gran liked a story." But he didn't move any closer either.

Wendell looked at the cave mouth and felt the itch sharpen into something with edges.

Here is what you need to understand about Wendell Eriksen: he had built his entire posthumous life — because that's what exile was, a life lived after the death of the other one — on a single rule, carved into him deeper than any commandment. *Curiosity is how people die.* Not recklessness, exactly. Curiosity. The wanting-to-know. The extra hundred yards past the last safe marker. The question asked one time too many of a mountain that had already given its answer. He had watched curiosity kill three people whose names he still said to himself some nights, like a man counting debts, and he had learned to strangle his own in the cradle. When he felt the itch, he named it, and naming it was supposed to disarm it. *That's curiosity. Curiosity killed the party.* And then he would turn away, and the turning-away would get easier every year, until it wasn't even effort anymore. Just habit. Invisibility as a discipline, practiced daily like scales on a piano.

So he named it. *Curiosity.* And he turned to Mrs. Pellish and said, "We should continue along the cliff path. The tide will turn by afternoon and this whole shelf becomes a trap."

But Mrs. Pellish was already looking at him with the particular bright-eyed expression of widows from the wheat towns, who had buried a husband and raised children and outlived two dogs and were therefore entirely out of patience with being managed.

"You want to go look at it," she said.

"I absolutely do not."

"You've got the face of a man who wants to go look at it. My husband had that face. It's why we ended up married." She pointed the stick at the cave. "Go on. I'll wait here with the boy. We'll watch your bag."

"Mrs. Pellish—"

"Wendell." She pronounced his name carefully, having extracted it from him earlier with the persistence of a customs officer. "I have walked up a volcano on my honeymoon because my husband said the view was better from the top, and he was right, and I have never once regretted being the sort of woman who says *go on*. Go on."

* * *

Later — much later, in the version of this morning he would tell himself at night, revising it the way he revised everything — Wendell would maintain that he crossed the exposed seabed for professional reasons. A new hazard on his guiding route needed assessing. Liability. Due diligence. He was, after all, responsible for the safety of anyone he walked these shores with, and a cave that appeared once a decade was precisely the sort of thing a thorough guide ought to know about.

It was a good lie. He told lies well; it was practically his trade. The trouble with this one was that nobody was listening except himself, and he'd stopped believing his own voice years ago.

The seabed underfoot was treacherous in a way his feet catalogued automatically — wet kelp here, algae-slick stone there, a pocket of soft silt that could swallow a boot to the ankle. His body did this work without consulting him, reading the ground the way other men read print. It was the one part of his old profession that had followed him into exile intact, faithful as a dog, and he both loved and resented it.

The cave mouth grew as he approached, and with it grew the smell — cold stone and salt and something older underneath, something mineral and deep, like the inside of a mountain that had been drowned. The trickle of water running out of it made a sound like whispering. Of course it did. Caves always whispered. It was just geology. Water finding its level through cracks in the rock.

He stopped ten yards from the entrance and stood there like a fool, wind pulling at his coat, and conducted the argument he'd been conducting with himself for twenty years.

*Nothing in there concerns you.*

No. Nothing did.

*You are a man who walks widows along cliffs and tells them about ghosts. That is the whole of your jurisdiction.*

Yes.

*Whatever is in that cave belongs to whoever put it there, or lost it there, or died there, and none of those people are you.*

And that was true, and it should have settled it, and it would have — except.

Except that as he stood there, the light shifted. A cloud slid off the sun, and the morning came down the cliff face and into the cave mouth at a low angle, and for perhaps five seconds the interior was lit like a room instead of a throat. And Wendell saw, forty feet inside, past the reach of any wave, a shape that did not belong to geology.

A box. Squat and square, sitting on a ledge of stone above the high-water line, furred with barnacle and salt-crust, glinting dully where the light caught it. Brass, or had been brass, once. The kind of box that existed to keep things in, and had kept them, against all odds, through years of tides.

The cloud passed. The cave went dark again.

Wendell Eriksen stood on the seabed of the Khoral coast and felt the rule — his rule, the load-bearing wall of his entire reconstructed life — crack straight down the middle.

Because here was the thing about the rule that he had never admitted to anyone, least of all himself: it had never actually been about safety. Safety was the respectable word he'd dressed it in. The truth was smaller and uglier. After the disaster, after the inquiries and the blacklistings and Magistrate Corven Hale reading his testimony aloud in that courtroom voice like a man reciting a shopping list of failures — after all of it, Wendell had sworn off curiosity because curiosity was *wanting*, and wanting was what had made him believe he deserved to lead people into the high country in the first place. Wanting was the sin. A man who wanted nothing could hurt nothing. A guide who desired no summit would never talk a tired party into one more ridge.

He had made not-wanting into a religion and called it humility, and for four years the congregation of one had attended services faithfully.

And now a dead-low tide had rolled back the sea and shown him a brass box in a cave the locals wouldn't name, and every service, every prayer, every disciplined turning-away collapsed at once, and what was left underneath was just a man on a wet seabed, wanting.

"Oh, hell," Wendell said, out loud, to no one.

He looked back. Mrs. Pellish and Tam were small figures on the shingle, watching. Tam had his arms crossed. Mrs. Pellish gave him a little wave, the stick raised like a standard.

The tide, Wendell knew, would turn. Tides always turned; it was their single reliable virtue. Whatever the window was — hours, certainly not more — it would close, and the sea would take the cave back, and whatever the box held would stay held for another decade, or forever, and Wendell Eriksen would go back to the cliff path and the lighthouse story and the eleven coppers a day, and he would tell himself it was the sensible choice, the safe choice, the choice of a man who had learned his lesson.

He thought about the lesson. He thought about how it had actually been taught, and by whom, and what it had cost, and whether the price had bought wisdom or merely fear wearing wisdom's coat.

The water whispered out of the cave mouth. Somewhere behind him a gull screamed what sounded suspiciously like encouragement.

Wendell walked forward.

The threshold stone was worn smooth as a church step — worn, he noted, by feet, by many feet over many years, which meant the cave had a history of visitors and the toll stories had a history of being earned. Inside, the air changed instantly: cold, dense, tasting of deep stone. His boots found footing on slick rock and his body read it and adjusted, old skills surfacing like fish. Forty feet in, the daylight thinned to a gray glow, and there it was.

The box sat on its ledge exactly as the light had promised, crusted white with salt, its hasp fused shut by years of the sea's slow welding. Beside it, half-tucked into a crevice as if placed with care rather than dropped, lay a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone stiff as wire.

And beside *that*, arranged with a neatness that stopped Wendell's breath —

Bones. A skeleton, seated against the cave wall with its back straight, its hands resting in its lap, in the posture of a man who had sat down to rest and simply continued. Tatters of cloth clung to the frame. On the skull's crown, absurdly, almost touchingly, perched a surveyor's leather cap, shrunk tight as a second bone.

Wendell knew the cap. Every guide on two coasts knew that style of cap. He had worn one himself, in another life, on ridgelines that no longer appeared on maps bearing his name.

The man in the cave had been one of his own kind. A mapper. A measurer of high places. Someone who had come down out of the mountains carrying something worth sealing in brass, and had sat down at the sea's door to wait — for a tide, for a boat, for help — and the waiting had won.

Wendell stood in the cold dark a long moment, guilt and grief and that cursed itching want all tangled together in his chest like fishing line.

Then, because the tide was already thinking about turning, and because a dead man's bundle is not going to unwrap itself, and because four years of discipline had just proven to be exactly as sturdy as a sandcastle at spring tide — Wendell Eriksen reached out and lifted the oilcloth bundle from beside the bones.

It was lighter than he expected. It rattled faintly, like paper.

Behind him, far off, muffled by stone, he heard Tam Wicker's voice echoing down from the shingle, cracking with alarm:

"Mister Eriksen! The water's coming back!"
