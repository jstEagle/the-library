## 3. The Blacklist

Eleven years before the tide that lied, Wendell Eriksen had a reputation worth more than his gear.

He was thirty-one then, and the Karst Range had already taught him everything it intended to teach him about humility—just not yet about consequence. He ran parties out of Vellmark for the Aldergate Mountaineering Company, and clients asked for him by name. Not because he was the strongest guide on the roster, though he could carry a man's pack and half his complaints up a col without breaking rhythm. They asked for him because Wendell had a gift that no certificate could issue: he made frightened people feel like the mountain was on their side.

"Your job isn't to get them to the top," old Master Guide Ferrow had told him in his first season, spitting into the wind off the shoulder of Crag Anselm. "Any mule can do that. Your job is to get them home loving the thing that nearly killed them."

Wendell had taken that as gospel. For nine years it worked.

The party of June—six clients, one junior guide named Pell, and himself—was meant to be routine. The Grey Stair traverse, two nights at the Halloway huts, summit day on Mount Corvane if weather allowed. The clients were pleasant enough: a wool merchant with better boots than sense, a retired schoolmistress who turned out to be the toughest of the lot, a pair of brothers from the coast who bickered like gulls over a fish head, a young surveyor's clerk who kept a notebook, and a quiet banker who had lied on his health declaration.

That last detail mattered later. It mattered so much that Wendell would spend eleven years turning it over like a stone in his pocket, feeling for the edge he'd missed.

* * *

The storm came early.

Every guide in the Karst knew the pattern: build cloud over the western ridges by noon, watch the barometer like a jealous spouse, and be off the high ground by two o'clock or accept that you were gambling. The forecast out of Vellmark had been clean. The sky at dawn had been the pale, apologetic blue that the range wore when it was deciding whether to behave.

At eleven, the barometer fell like a man pushed.

Wendell stood at the notch below the Grey Stair with the wind climbing his collar and did the arithmetic every guide does—the kind that has no right answer, only answers you can live with or answers you can't. Down and around the long way: four hours exposed on the northern flank, which funneled weather like a chimney. Up and over the Stair: ninety minutes of scrambling above the treeline, then shelter at the Halloway hut, stone-walled, iron-roofed, built by men who'd understood mountains better than they'd understood accounting.

His own judgment said down. His own judgment said *down now*, hard, drop the summit ambition, eat the client disappointment, take the long safe crawl around the mountain's hip.

But the company dispatch—relayed that morning by wire to the hut, signed by the regional director—had been explicit. Weather window confirmed. Proceed via the Stair. The merchant had paid a premium for the summit. And there was a newer consideration, one that Wendell would hate himself for weighing at all: the director had mentioned, in the dry way directors have, that the Aldergate Company's contract with the Vellmark tourism board came up for renewal in the autumn, and that a season of turned-back summits made for awkward conversations.

So Wendell did the thing that ruined him. He obeyed.

Not blindly—he wasn't a blind man. He checked knots twice, shortened the rope intervals, put Pell on point with strict instructions, set a turnaround time of one o'clock sharp and announced it to the party like a wedding vow. He did everything obedience required of a careful man. What he didn't do was listen to the small cold voice underneath the dispatch, the one that sounded, he realized much later, exactly like Ferrow.

The storm hit them on the third pitch of the Stair.

It didn't build. It arrived, the way a debt collector arrives, all at once and already inside the house. Wind went from insult to violence in the space of four breaths. Snow came sideways, then came from below, ripped off the ledges and flung upward so that the world became a white drum. Visibility collapsed to the length of an arm. Temperature dropped through the floor of what the schoolmistress's wool coat had been designed to survive.

Wendell called the turn. One o'clock, as promised—but they were forty minutes short of the notch, and turning a roped party around on exposed ground in a whiteout is not the clean maneuver it sounds like in a planning tent. It is a slow, terrified shuffle, each client unclipped and reclipped, each anchor equal parts engineering and prayer.

The banker went second-to-last. He was gray under his hood, breathing wrong, and Wendell should have seen it—not just the altitude but the heart, the lie on the health form finally coming due. But Wendell's whole attention was a rope-length ahead, where the wool merchant had frozen at an exposed step and was refusing, in a rising shriek that the wind ate whole, to move either direction.

Pell solved the merchant. Pell was twenty-three and brave and quick, and he talked the man across the step with the flat calm of someone who has not yet learned what fear costs. Wendell heard him doing it through the roar and felt the specific pride a senior guide feels when his junior rises—and that pride cost him perhaps ten seconds of divided attention.

Ten seconds.

When he turned back, the banker was down.

* * *

They got him moving again. That was the horror of it—they got him *moving*, half-dragged between the brothers, and for a while Wendell let himself believe the mountain had accepted its toll and the ledger was closed. Then the banker stopped complaining, which in the mountains is the most alarming sound a man can make, and shortly after that he stopped answering, and by the time they reached the notch he was beyond anything Pell's strong young arms or Wendell's nine years of craft could reach.

Corvane took him somewhere on the upper Stair, quietly, between one whiteout and the next. They couldn't even recover the body until three days later. Wendell carried the man's pack down himself, because someone had to, and because carrying things was the only prayer he knew.

Two other clients lost fingers. The schoolmistress lost toes. Pell lost something less visible, though Wendell saw it happen in the boy's eyes over the following weeks—the same erosion he felt in his own.

And then the inquiries began, and Wendell learned that a mountain takes lives quickly, but a bureaucracy takes them slowly, with paperwork.

* * *

The first hearing was almost fair. That was the cruel trick of it. The inquiry board sat in Vellmark for six days, and Wendell testified honestly, completely, holding nothing back: the dispatch, the barometer, his own doubt, his decision to obey, the turnaround time, the ten seconds. He gave them the truth like a man emptying his pockets at customs.

The board found what actually happened: severe weather event, pre-existing cardiac condition concealed by the deceased, operational decisions within accepted practice given the company dispatch. Negligence, where it existed, sat with the dispatch and the director who'd signed it. The report used the phrase "systemic pressure on field judgment," which Wendell thought, reading it later, was the most honest sentence anyone wrote about the whole affair.

Then Magistrate Corven Hale read the report.

Hale was not on the inquiry board. He didn't need to be. He was the magistrate of the Vellmark district, and the tourism contract—the one the director had been so delicately protecting—was administered through Hale's office. More than that: the dead banker, it emerged, had been a cousin of Hale's wife. Grief wants a shape, and grief with power wants a guilty party, and a dead director retires comfortably to the coast while a living guide stands in front of everyone wearing a rope and a name.

Hale reopened everything. Not as an inquiry—an *opinion*. A magistrate's published opinion on the conduct of one Wendell Eriksen, issued with the full weight of his office, in which the word "negligence" appeared eleven times and the word "dispatch" appeared none.

Wendell went to protest it. That was the second mistake, though calling it a mistake feels obscene; it was simply the instinct of an honest man walking into a room built for dishonest ones. He brought his field logs. Hale's clerk received them, stamped them, and they were never seen again. He brought witnesses—Pell among them, white-knuckled and loyal—and watched their testimony get reframed in the summary as "coached subordinates." He brought the weather telegrams, and learned that the wire office's records had a gap that week, an administrative misfortune, terribly common.

"You're a careful man, Eriksen," Hale told him at the last session, and the strange thing was that it sounded almost like respect. Hale was not a fool; that was the worst of it. He was clever, patient, and genuinely convinced of his own rectitude, which is the most dangerous combination a powerful man can carry. "Careful men make the best defendants. They keep such thorough records. It gives us both something to work with."

"I followed the dispatch," Wendell said.

"Yes." Hale folded his hands. "You did. And a man is dead, and the company's contract will renew smoothly this autumn, and you will find that the mountains of two nations have become very narrow country for you. I haven't banned you from anything, guide. I've merely written my opinion. What people do with opinions is their own affair."

What people did with opinions was everything. The Aldergate Company declined to renew his certification, citing "reputational considerations." The neighboring republic's guiding association, which shared examiners with Vellmark, followed within the month—a courtesy between institutions, a paper border closing like a gate. Ferry offices grew vague about his bookings. Hut wardens, who had known him for years, developed sudden policies about unregistered parties. No one ever said *banned*. Everyone simply arranged their affairs so that Wendell Eriksen had nowhere left to stand.

Pell lasted another season, then left for the lowlands and a job surveying roads. He sent one letter, which Wendell kept for years until it hurt too much to keep: *They asked me to sign a statement about your judgment. I wouldn't. So they stopped asking me things. I'm sorry I'm not braver. You were the bravest man on that mountain and it doesn't matter at all.*

* * *

Wendell drifted south along the Khoral coast, because the coast was the one place with no summits to blacklist him from. He took work hauling crates, then work pointing tourists at interesting rocks, then—because a man cannot stop being what he is—work guiding them properly, informally, off the books, along cliff paths and tidal shelves where no certification was required because no one had thought to require it.

He built a new religion out of it. Its commandments were simple.

Be invisible. Be useful. Want nothing that requires a signature. Never be interesting.

Curiosity, he decided, was the tax that visibility collected. The guides who got noticed were the guides who poked at things—who reported the strange wreck, who mapped the unnamed cave, who wrote letters about the odd lights, who asked why a village called Merrow's Reach had vanished from the new coastal charts as thoroughly as if the sea had drunk it. Every one of those guides ended up in a room with a polite man folding his hands. Wendell had watched it happen to a cartographer named Doss, who'd gotten curious about some discrepancies in the land registries up-valley and who now ran a very successful oyster farm and never, ever discussed maps.

So Wendell practiced not-seeing the way other men practiced scales. When tourists asked about the cave mouth below the cliffs—the one the locals wouldn't name, the one the old songs said charged a toll—he laughed and told them it was a sea lion den and the lions were shy. When he found odd stonework in the hills, older than any road on any chart, he stepped over it and looked elsewhere. When a shepherd once tried to tell him about his grandfather's village, Wendell bought the man a drink and changed the subject to weather, and the shepherd's face did something complicated and sad that Wendell pretended not to see.

Invisibility, he told himself, was pragmatism. A blacklisted man who stayed small stayed free. It was simple self-preservation, nothing more.

It took him years to admit that it was also penance. That part of him believed he deserved the smallness—that a man whose obedience had killed someone ought to want nothing, go nowhere, be no one. That the shrinking was not safety but punishment, self-administered, on a schedule no court had set.

He was still telling himself it was pragmatism the morning the tide went out farther than it had gone in a decade, and the sea peeled back from the cliffs like a curtain, and showed him a cave mouth that every rule he'd built told him to walk past.

He stood on the wet sand for a long time, wind pulling at his coat, feeling the old argument inside him—the careful man and the curious one, the obedient guide and the one who should have trusted his own voice on a mountain eleven years ago.

Then he broke his own first commandment, and walked toward the dark.

* * *

Later—much later, after the journal, after the girl with her father's handwriting, after everything—he would understand that the cave hadn't tempted him at all. Temptation implies a choice between good and evil. What the cave offered was stranger: a chance to be visible again, in the one way that might finally matter.

But that morning, crouched at the threshold with salt water dripping from the stone above him, Wendell Eriksen knew only this: he had spent eleven years learning to disappear, and some stubborn drowned part of him had just decided it was finished hiding.

Behind him, up the coast road, a ferry horn sounded—bringing passengers, cargo, and, though he could not possibly have known it, a woman carrying letters in a dead surveyor's hand, asking after a man named Vane in a town where only one person had ever spoken that name aloud.
