## 4. A Daughter of Ledger Paper

*Isolde*

The ferry office in Saltmere smelled of tar, wet wool, and the particular despair of a man who had been selling tickets for forty years and no longer believed anyone was going anywhere good.

Isolde Vane stood at the counter with her father's letters in a satchel against her ribs and asked her question for the ninth time that day.

"I'm looking for a mountain guide," she said. "Wendell Eriksen."

The ticket man — a squat fellow whose nameplate read *BODKIN* and whose expression suggested he'd been named ironically — looked up from his ledger with the wariness of a man who had heard this question before.

"You're the fourth one today," he said.

"The fourth person asking about Eriksen?"

"The fourth *you*, miss. Young woman, traveling alone, asking after Wendell Eriksen." He counted on thick fingers. "There was one Tuesday. One Wednesday morning. One yesterday who claimed to be his cousin, though she had red hair and a Lowmarsh accent and didn't know his mother's name, which I asked, because I'm thorough."

"And what did you tell them?"

"Nothing. Because I don't know anything." He said it with the serene confidence of a man who knew a great deal. "Eriksen doesn't take bookings through an office. He works off the dock, word of mouth, tourists who want the cliff walks and don't ask questions. If you want him, you stand where he can see you looking like you've got money and bad judgment, and eventually he'll come to you."

Isolde considered this. It was, she had to admit, exactly how she would have operated, if she were a disgraced guide hiding from two governments. You didn't post a sign. You let the sign be your reputation.

"Thank you," she said, and put a coin on the counter, because information freely given still deserved paying for. Her father had taught her that. *Everything has a price, Isolde. The trick is knowing which prices are worth paying.* She had spent eleven years learning which ones weren't.

She stepped out into the salt wind and looked down the harbor road, and there he was.

She recognized him from the description in her father's last letter, though the letter had been written years ago and described a younger man. Even so. Broad-shouldered, weathered to the color of old rope, standing at the rail of the sea wall talking to two tourists in matching yellow oilskins. He gestured at the cliffs with the easy authority of a man pointing out scenery he privately despised. The tourists laughed. He did not.

*That's him,* she thought. *That's the man my father trusted with nothing, and trusted anyway.*

Because that was the strange arithmetic of Aldous Vane's final year, the year Isolde had pieced together from letters and silences: her father, meticulous surveyor, careful man, a man who triple-checked his knots and his sums, had taken the most dangerous thing he owned — the truth about Merrow's Reach, wrapped in paper and brass — and carried it into a cave on a coast where the only person he could think to trust was a guide with a ruined name.

Why Eriksen? She had turned that question over for three days on the boat, turning it like a stone looking for what lived underneath. The answer, when it finally came to her, was simple enough to hurt: because a ruined name is the safest place to hide something. A man with nothing left to lose cannot be bought. A man already convicted of everything cannot be blackmailed. Her father hadn't chosen Eriksen despite his disgrace. He'd chosen him *because* of it.

And now here she was, about to do the same thing.

She watched him a while longer. He finished with the tourists, took their coins without counting them — a small tell, that; honest men count — and walked toward the chandlery with the particular gait of someone who wanted very much not to be followed.

So naturally, she followed him.

*Wendell*

He had made it four steps past the chandlery door when he noticed the woman.

Noticed her properly, that is. He'd seen her earlier outside Bodkin's office — hard not to; she had the look of a heron standing in a duck pond, all angles and patience among the round, hurrying fishing folk — but guides learned early to catalog strangers and forget them, and he'd filed her under *tourist, unaccompanied, probably lost.*

Then he saw her again. Same street. Different direction. And now she wasn't walking like a tourist. Tourists walk like people reading a map with their feet. This woman walked like someone following a thread, and the thread was him.

Wendell's rule against curiosity had served him well for six years, but it had never covered being curious *about himself*, and that was what this was: the sudden, unwelcome awareness that he had become interesting, and that interesting things in Saltmere tended to end badly.

He cut through the chandlery, nodded to Marta behind the counter, exited through the rope yard, crossed the fish-flaying yard — apologizing to Old Tammer mid-stride, because you always apologized to Tammer even when you hadn't done anything yet — and came out by the drying racks, where the cod hung in silver rows like the ghosts of better decisions.

The woman came around the corner thirty seconds later. Not running. Not even hurrying. Just *arriving*, the way weather arrives, inevitable and unhurried, as if she'd known exactly where the rope yard emptied and had simply waited for him to demonstrate it.

"Mr. Eriksen," she said.

"Madam." He kept moving. "Cliff walks start at nine. Bring water."

"I'm not here about the cliffs."

"Nobody's ever here about the cliffs. They're here about the cliffs until they see the price, and then they're here about the lighthouse instead."

"My name is Isolde Vane."

He stopped.

It was a small stop, half a stride collapsing into itself, but he felt it go through him like a crack through ice, and he knew — with the sick certainty of a man who has spent six years perfecting the art of showing nothing — that she had seen it too.

"Aldous Vane's daughter," she said, into the silence.

"The surveyor," Wendell said carefully. "Vanished. Eleven years back. I remember the notices."

"Do you." It wasn't a question. It was a scalpel laid politely on a tray. "Because I've spent eleven years writing to every port office, every survey guild, every records house between here and the capital, and do you know what they all say? *No record.* No record of his last contract. No record of his landing. No record of the commission he told me he'd taken, in his own hand, in a letter I still have." She opened her satchel — unhurried, deliberate, a woman laying cards face-up — and drew out a fold of papers. "But you remembered the notices. Within a second. Of a missing man nobody in this town talks about."

"The cave," Wendell said. "People talk about the cave."

"People talk *around* the cave. There's a difference. My father taught me the difference." She held out the topmost letter. "You'll recognize the hand."

He should not have taken it. Every instinct honed over six years of invisibility said: *smile, shrug, direct her to Bodkin, become furniture.* But his eyes were already on the paper, and the handwriting hit him like cold water — the same precise, upright script, the same peculiar way of crossing t's with a single impatient stroke, that he had spent three nights reading by candlelight in a dead man's journal.

"It matches," she said quietly, watching his face. "Doesn't it."

Wendell handed the letter back. His hands were steady. He was professionally proud of that, and privately appalled at how much it cost him.

"I couldn't say, madam. I'm not acquainted with the gentleman's hand."

"No," Isolde agreed. "Of course not. You're not acquainted with anything. That's rather the legend, isn't it? Wendell Eriksen, the man who knows nothing, sees nothing, and guides tourists along cliffs so dull that even the gulls are bored." She folded the letters away. "I sat in Bodkin's office for an hour this morning. Wonderful gossip, Bodkin. Did you know he keeps a ledger of everyone who asks after you? Four of me now, in five days. He thinks we're debt collectors."

"He would."

"He also mentioned — unprompted, over tea he insisted on making me, terrible tea — that a stranger came through town last week asking after *caves*. A big fellow, soft-spoken, paid cash, said he was a buyer for a salvage concern out of Corvess. Asked whether the tide ever went far enough out to open the sea caves on the north stretch." She tilted her head. "Funny thing, Mr. Eriksen. Bodkin said the stranger asked *him*, but Bodkin doesn't know anything about caves. And then Bodkin said — and this is the part I liked — that the stranger must have been mistaken, because the only man in Saltmere who knows that coastline tide by tide is you, and you'd have told the fellow the truth, wouldn't you. Being famously honest these days."

The wind moved through the drying racks. Somewhere below, a gull expressed an opinion.

"What is it you want, Miss Vane?" Wendell said.

"Truthfully? I want to know why a salvage buyer from Corvess cares about a cave my father died in front of. I want to know why the fourth woman asking after you in five days is me, and the other three were men in good boots pretending to be something else — yes, I found the third one's bootmaker's receipt in the tavern, I'm thorough, it's a family affliction. And I want to know—" her voice caught, just once, a single loose stitch in otherwise flawless canvas, "—what my father was doing that required him to disappear so completely that even the people who erased him left no record of the erasing."

"That's a long list."

"I make long lists. I'm a ledger-keeper's daughter. We believe everything balances eventually." She looked at him then, straight on, and Wendell saw the thing he'd been circling since the rope yard: she was lying too. Not about the grief — the grief was real, worn smooth as sea glass from eleven years of handling. But underneath it, in the set of her shoulders and the patience of her questions, was a woman who had rehearsed this conversation the way he rehearsed his own harmless answers, and who was watching him watch her do it.

Two liars, recognizing each other's craft across a fish yard. It was almost companionable.

"Here's what I'll offer," she said. "I know you have something. I don't know what — a journal, papers, I couldn't say, I'm not acquainted with anything either. I'm not asking you to show me. I'm asking you to tell me one true thing. Just one. And in exchange, I'll tell you why the man from Corvess frightens me more than any magistrate ever could."

"Why would he frighten you?"

"Because salvage buyers pay for wrecks," Isolde Vane said. "My father isn't a wreck. My father is a *loose end*. And somebody has finally noticed the difference."

---

*Isolde*

She watched him decide.

It was, she thought later, like watching a mountain decide the weather — slow, internal, entirely indifferent to what anyone standing below might prefer. Wendell Eriksen looked at her for a long moment with eyes the gray of the horizon before rain, and whatever argument was happening inside him, neither side won quickly.

"One true thing," he said finally.

"One."

He glanced at the drying racks, the yard, the rooftops — checking sightlines, she realized, with the automatic thoroughness of a man who had been hunted before and expected to be again. Then he said, very quietly:

"The journal exists. I've read part of it. Your father was alive eleven days after his last letter to you. What he was doing, and why, and for whom — that I'll not say on an open street. But he didn't vanish, Miss Vane. He was *put* somewhere. There's a difference, and the difference has a shape, and I think you've already guessed the shape."

Isolde's breath went out of her slowly. Eleven years. Eleven years of *no record*, of polite letters and closed files, of her mother dying with the question still open between them like an unswept floor — and this stranger, this disgraced guide she'd cornered among cod, had just given her more truth in ten words than the entire machinery of records and offices had produced in a decade.

"Eleven days," she repeated. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "After the last letter."

"He dated things. Meticulous man." Something moved behind Wendell's face — not quite warmth, but the memory of it, like sun through fog. "You have his habit. The lists. The receipts. The way you asked Bodkin his mother's name."

"How did you—"

"The red-haired cousin told me. Yesterday. Over ale she bought me and pretended not to be pumping me." The ghost of humor crossed his face, dry as driftwood. "I told her my mother's name was Gertrude. It's Anneli. If you'd asked me directly, I'd have told you the same lie. That's the difference between us and them, Miss Vane. They lie to find things. We lie to keep them."

"We," she said.

"I didn't say that."

"You said 'we.'"

"I said the wind picked up." He was already moving toward the yard gate, and she understood suddenly that the conversation was ending not because he wanted it to but because standing still was, for this man, a form of bleeding. "Ferry comes in at dusk. If I were a woman with long lists and a family affliction for thoroughness, I'd be on it, going home, where it's safe."

"If I were that woman," Isolde said, "I'd have been that woman eleven years ago."

He stopped at the gate. Didn't turn. For a moment he just stood there, a broad dark shape against the silver light on the water, and when he spoke again his voice had changed — lower, wearier, the voice of a man opening a door he'd nailed shut.

"There's a tavern called the Drowned Bell. Back room. Tomorrow, after the evening boats, when the regulars are too deep in their cups to notice strangers. Come alone. Don't be followed — and if you *are* followed, don't lead them there, lead them anywhere else, and we'll try again the night after." A pause. "One more true thing, since you collect them."

"Yes?"

"I broke my rule for your father's sake once already, and I haven't decided yet whether it was the stupidest thing or the first right thing I've done in six years. Tomorrow night, you may help me decide." He pushed open the gate. "Bring the letters. All of them. And Miss Vane—"

She waited.

"—the man from Corvess. When you find him, and you will, because men like that are easy to find when they want to be found — don't talk to him. Don't let him talk to you. He'll ask about caves. Everyone asks about caves lately." Wendell Eriksen stepped through onto the harbor road, and the crowd of Saltmere closed around him like water closing over a dropped coin. "Everyone asks about caves. Nobody asks what's *in* them. Start wondering why."

Then he was gone, and Isolde Vane stood alone among the drying racks with her heart going like a hammer and her mind already doing what it always did: making lists.

*One,* she wrote mentally. *He has the journal. Confirmed.*

*Two. He knows about the Corvess man, which means the Corvess man knows about him, which means the clock is already running.*

*Three.* She looked out at the gray water, at the north stretch of coast where the cliffs bent around out of sight toward a cave mouth that the whole town refused to name. *Three: tomorrow night, the Drowned Bell. And between now and then, I need to find out what a salvage buyer looks like when he's hunting a loose end.*

Behind her, unnoticed, a lean man in good boots set down the net-mending he'd been pretending to do for twenty minutes, and walked away toward the telegraph office, walking exactly like a tourist, which was how he always walked, and thinking exactly like a hunter, which was how he always thought.

The tide, far out beyond the point, had begun its long patient crawl back toward the land.

It would reach the cave in nine days.

It would not be low enough again for another decade — and everyone involved was about to learn that some doors, once opened, do not wait politely to be shut.
