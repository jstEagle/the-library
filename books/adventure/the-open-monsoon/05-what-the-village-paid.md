## 5. What the Village Paid

My father taught me to read the way other fathers teach their children to swim—early, patiently, and with the firm conviction that one day it might save my life. He never said so outright. He simply left journals everywhere, half-finished, and when I asked what a word meant he would hand me the dictionary instead of the answer, saying, "Look it up, Isolde. A thing you find yourself, you keep."

I was eleven years old the last time I saw him. He kissed the top of my head, smelled of ink and pipe smoke and the particular salt of a man about to go to sea, and told me he'd be back before the lantern festival. I have spent eleven years learning every way a person can fail to come back before the lantern festival.

So when Wendell Eriksen set Aldous Vane's journal on the table between us—a fat, oilcloth-wrapped book gone stiff as a ship's biscuit—I understood that I was not being handed a book. I was being handed my father's last conversation. And like all his conversations, it would require work on my part before it made any sense at all.

* * *

We worked in the back room of the Ferryman's Rest, because Wendell refused to work anywhere with windows facing the harbor. "Hale has eyes," he said, in the tone of a man stating that water is wet. "He's had eyes on this coast for years. The only reason we're still talking is that nobody expects the disgraced guide and the surveyor's daughter to be sitting in a room together doing arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," I repeated.

"Ledgers," he said, and pushed the journal toward me. "Your father didn't keep a diary, Miss Vane. He kept accounts."

The room smelled of tallow and old rope. A single lamp burned between us, and Wendell had turned his chair so that his back was to the wall and his face to the door, which I had decided was either paranoia or professionalism and had not yet asked which. He was not what I expected from the rumors. The rumors said *drunkard*, said *coward*, said *the man who walked down the mountain while his clients stayed up on it*. What sat across from me was a lean, weathered man who drank his tea without sugar and watched everything, and who had, without being asked, already sharpened three pencils and laid them out parallel like surgical instruments.

"You've read some of it," I said.

"Some." His jaw did something complicated. "Enough to know I shouldn't have read it alone. Some pages are yours before they're mine."

That was the first honest thing anyone had said to me since I stepped off the ferry, and it undid me slightly. I covered it by opening the journal.

The early pages were ordinary enough—surveyor's notes, bearings, tide tables written in my father's small, slanted hand, each figure as neat as a hymn. But by the fourth page the entries began to change. Whole lines were struck through. Margins filled with symbols: circles, crossed circles, a strange recurring mark like a fishhook bent into a ring. And threaded through the navigation notes, in a cipher of his own devising, ran sentences that made no sense at all.

*"Toll collected, third bell. The old woman sings well tonight."*

*"Payment received in full. Tell her the reach remembers."*

*"They are buying silence again. Same buyers. Same price. God forgive the arithmetic."*

"He was a smuggler," Wendell said quietly. It wasn't an accusation. It sounded almost like relief, as if smuggling were a comprehensible sin in a world short on those.

"No," I said, because something in my chest knew before my head did. "Read it again. He wasn't taking goods *out*. Look at the direction of the entries. Everything moves inland. Everything comes *from* the cave."

Wendell took the journal back and read, lips moving slightly, and I watched a man's face rearrange itself around a new idea the way a coastline rearranges around a storm.

* * *

It took us two days.

Two days of lamp oil, bad coffee, and the Ferryman's Rest's landlady, Mrs. Tallow—a woman built like a barrel of opinions—who brought us food we hadn't ordered and commentary we hadn't requested. ("Cipher-work, is it? My second husband did ciphers. Turned out he was just illiterate and embarrassed.") She approved of me immediately and of Wendell never, and refilled his cup with the expression of someone poisoning a rival's well with kindness.

But it was Tam—the runaway boy who swept the tavern floor for scraps and slept in the woodshed—who cracked it, though he doesn't know it yet. He came in the second evening to beg bread and lingered over the journal pages spread across the table, and said, "That's a tide-cipher. Fishermen use it. You match the letters to the moon phases, see? My gran used it to write to her sister in Khoral Bay so the excise men couldn't read her gossip." He demonstrated on a scrap of paper, tongue between his teeth, then pocketed his bread and vanished, leaving behind him the entire architecture of my father's secrecy.

Moon phases. Tide tables. Of course. My father had hidden his true account inside his false ones, keyed to the very instrument of his death—the sea, and its lying tides.

By midnight we could read him.

* * *

Here is what the village paid. Here is what my father wrote in a dead man's cipher, in a dead man's cave, believing no one would ever find it—and here is what I read aloud in a lamplit back room while Wendell Eriksen sat so still he might have been carved:

Merrow's Reach was real.

Not a legend, not a cautionary tale grandmothers used to keep children off the cliffs—an actual village, forty-one households, a chapel, a herring fleet, a schoolhouse with a bell. It stood in the valley beyond the Saddle of Ashes until nineteen years ago, when the deeds changed hands. All of them. At once. To a buyer whose name my father wrote out in full, letter by careful letter, as if he wanted even a stranger deciphering this decades later to feel the weight of it:

**Corven Hale, acting for the Hale family trust.**

The village didn't sell. The village was *condemned*—declared unsound, flood-prone, blighted, by surveys commissioned by the same trust that acquired the land for a fraction of its worth. The villagers were moved "for their safety" to settlements that had been promised and never built. Some scattered. Some died waiting. And the records—the parish rolls, the land registries, the maps themselves—were purchased, amended, and burned, until Merrow's Reach existed nowhere but in the memories of people who had every incentive to forget.

Except the inheritance. That was the part that wouldn't burn.

"The toll," I read, my voice going strange in my own ears, "was never a monster's fee. It was the village's money. When the condemnation came, the elders of Merrow's Reach did the only thing left to them. They moved their common wealth—the dowry funds, the burial society, the school endowment, forty-one families' savings—out through the old smugglers' cave, past the tide line, into keeping. Aldous Vane was hired—he believed himself hired—to survey a safe route for it. He was the only outsider they trusted, because he had once mapped their chapel bell for a church commission and gotten the height exactly right, and they said a man that careful with a bell would be careful with a fortune."

Wendell made a sound low in his throat. "And then?"

"And then the payments stopped coming." I turned the page. My hands were steady. I want it recorded somewhere, even if only in my own memory, that my hands were steady. "Listen. *'The trust has found the route. Hale's men met the last shipment at the far side. There was nothing I could do. They let me live because a dead surveyor raises questions and a silent one raises none. I have the ledger copies. I will keep them where the tide can guard them, and I will spend what remains of my life making sure this account survives its accountants.'*"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere upstairs Mrs. Tallow dropped something heavy and swore at it with real artistry.

"He went back," I said. "Year after year. He checked the cache. He copied the bribes—every official paid to erase the village, every signature, every sum. Eleven years of entries. And the last one—" I stopped.

Wendell waited. He was good at waiting; I'll grant him that. Most men fill silence like a leaking boat fills with water. He simply let it be there.

"The last entry is dated nine days before he disappeared. It says: *'Someone followed me up from the ferry today. Young man, gray coat, magistrate's bearing. If anything happens to me, the key is with the Sisters at the shrine above the treeline, and the truth is with my daughter, who reads everything twice.'*" I looked up. "He wrote that to me. Eleven years ago. He wrote to me across a cipher and a decade and a cave full of salt, and he trusted me to be reading."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a deck tilts. I gripped the table.

Wendell spoke carefully, the way you speak to someone standing on ice. "Miss Vane. Isolde. Your father didn't die of curiosity or greed or bad luck. He was killed—or abandoned to be killed—for carrying an account that powerful men would still very much like to see drowned. Which means the moment we started reading this, we stopped being tourists."

"I've been stopped being a tourist since the ferry office," I said. "You cornered me there, if you recall. With your rule against curiosity and your eyes like a customs officer's."

"I cornered you because you were asking dangerous questions badly," he said, and then, with the ghost of something that on another man might have been a smile, "and because you lied about why you were here with the same technique I use. Left hand showing the coin, right hand doing the work. I recognized the craft."

"Flattery," I said. "From the blacklist of two nations."

"It's not flattery if it's accurate. It's testimony." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the scar through his eyebrow, and for the first time since I'd met him he looked less like a man hiding and more like a man aiming. "Here's what matters. The strongbox is still in the cave—we couldn't open it, salt-fused shut, and the key is at a mountain shrine. The ledger copies your father hid are evidence. Real evidence, signatures and sums, the kind that un-erases a village. Forty-one families, Isolde. Descendants. Some of them are probably standing on docks within sight of this window, hauling fish for the grandchildren of the men who took their grandparents' homes."

"I know," I said. "I've been counting them since the second page."

"Have you also counted what it costs to carry this to the pass?" He tapped the journal. "Five weeks before snow closes the Saddle of Ashes, if the shepherd I talked to is honest, and shepherds are honest the way rivers are straight—mostly, and only where it suits them. We'd need supplies, permits we can't get, a route Hale knows better than we do, and we'd need to do all of it without becoming interesting to the magistrate. Because the moment Corven Hale hears that Vane's journal surfaced in the tide cave, he won't send a clerk. He'll send men. And they won't be carrying writs."

I looked down at my father's handwriting—at the little fishhook rings marching along the margin like a string of devotion, at the sentence he'd written for me across eleven years—and felt something in my chest finish hardening into a shape I recognized. Grief, I had learned, was mostly water. It sloshed. It drowned. But grief given a direction becomes ballast. It keeps a hull upright in weather that capsizes happier ships.

"They erased a village," I said. "They killed my father for writing it down. And they've spent nineteen years being comfortable about both." I closed the journal and put my hand flat on its cover, the way you'd steady a shoulder. "I'm not asking you to guide me, Mr. Eriksen. I'm asking you to help me make them read it aloud."

For a long moment Wendell Eriksen said nothing. Then he reached over, took one of the three parallel pencils, and began, in a guide's quick sure strokes, to draw the mountains.

* * *

We were still drawing when the knock came.

Three knocks, soft, deliberate—the kind that means the knocker is not afraid of you, which is worse than loud knocking. Wendell was on his feet before the third one finished, journal into his shirt, lamp pinched dead, his hand finding my wrist in the dark and pressing it once: *stay quiet.*

Through the door came a voice I didn't know, pleasant as a customs stamp:

"Evening, Mrs. Tallow. Magistrate's business. We're looking for a gentleman matching a certain description—big fellow, drinks his tea bitter, thinks nobody sees him. And they say he's taken up company." A pause, silk over stone. "A lady visitor. Surveyor's daughter, they're calling her."

In the dark, Wendell's grip tightened on my wrist.

Mrs. Tallow's voice floated down the stairs, magnificent, unhurried, and utterly unhelpful: "Company? In *my* house? Sir, the only visitors I get are creditors and the ghost of my second husband, and neither of them tips."

Boots on the stairs anyway. Coming up.
