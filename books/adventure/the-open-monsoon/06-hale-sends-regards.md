## 6. Hale Sends Regards

The trouble with living invisibly, Wendell had learned, was that it only worked until someone decided to look.

He'd spent eleven years perfecting the art of being forgettable. He paid his rent in coin nobody asked about. He drank at the Salt Loom but never past the second cup, because the third cup made men chatty and chatty men got remembered. He kept his cabin on the cliff path deliberately shabby—peeling shutters, a roof that leaked in exactly one spot he'd never bothered to fix, a garden of weeds so convincing that old Marta from the fishery once offered him advice about them.

The journal sat under the third floorboard from the hearth, wrapped in oilcloth, and for six days it had been the loudest thing in his life.

He hadn't opened it again. That was the discipline. He'd read enough that first night to know what it was—Vane's hand, Vane's careful surveyor's script, dates and bearings and a man's mind laid out in ink—and then he'd wrapped it back up and put it away the way you'd put away a live coal. But knowing it was there changed everything. The cabin felt smaller. The floorboards felt thinner. Twice he caught himself lying awake listening to footsteps on the cliff path that turned out to be nothing but wind and his own guilty heart.

*You could burn it,* he told himself each morning, standing at his window with tea going cold in his hands, watching the gulls argue over the tide line.

He never quite believed himself. Burning it would be easy, and Wendell Eriksen had learned long ago that the things which were easy were rarely the things that let you sleep.

On the sixth day, the boy came.

Wendell knew him by sight—a dockhand, one of the half-grown lads who hauled crates off the coastal packets, all elbows and salt-cracked knuckles. He'd carried Wendell's gear a few times over the years, always silent, always quick, tips pocketed without counting. His name, Wendell thought, was Pip. Or possibly Nip. Something small and sharp.

The boy came up the cliff path at a dead run, which was itself an alarm, because nobody ran up the cliff path unless something was wrong or they were young enough not to know better. Wendell was out splitting kindling—he kept an axe visible for exactly this reason; men with axes looked like they belonged where they were—and the boy skidded to a stop close enough to spray wood chips.

"They're coming," the boy said. "Up from the ferry office. Four of 'em. Grey coats. One's got a paper he keeps reading, like he can't remember what it says."

Wendell set down the axe with great care. "Grey coats."

"Magistrate's men. Hale's." The boy glanced over his shoulder, down the path where the pines swallowed the view. "They was asking at the Salt Loom last night. About you. About—" he dropped his voice, though there was no one within a mile, "—about whether you'd been acting strange lately. Whether you'd come into anything."

"And what did you tell them?"

"That you're the dullest man on the coast." The boy almost smiled. "Told them you count your firewood twice and fall asleep before dark. They didn't believe me much."

"No," Wendell said quietly. "They wouldn't." He looked at the boy properly then—really looked, the way he hadn't let himself look at anyone in years—and saw what he saw: a kid who'd run two miles uphill to warn a man who'd never done him any kindness beyond a fair tip. "Why are you telling me this?"

The boy shrugged, suddenly fascinated by his own boots. "You carried a lady's trunk down the ferry stairs last month when the porter was drunk. Didn't have to." Another shrug. "Seemed like the sort of thing worth remembering."

It was, Wendell thought, a terrible reason to risk a beating. It was also the best reason he'd heard in eleven years.

"How long do I have?"

"Ten minutes. Maybe less if they stop to argue with the fishery lot."

"Then listen." Wendell was already moving, pulling up the third floorboard, wrapping the journal tighter. "In an hour, maybe two, a woman will come asking after me. Dark hair, ledger-paper eyes, probably pretending she's a clerk or a widow or both. Her name is Isolde Vane."

"You know her?"

"I'm going to." He pressed a coin into the boy's hand—too much, deliberately, the way you'd pay a debt you couldn't name yet. "If she asks where I've gone, tell her the shepherd's track above the quarry. Tell her I said the dry season is shorter than she thinks."

"The what is shorter than who thinks?"

"Exactly," Wendell said. "That's the idea."

The boy frowned, filed it away somewhere behind his eyes, and went back down the path at a jog, unhurried now, a dockhand with nowhere particular to be. Wendell watched him go and felt something in his chest he refused to call hope.

Then he took the journal, put on his good coat—the one without holes, saved for funerals and emergencies, and this felt like both—and walked out the back of his own life through the gap in the fence he'd maintained for eleven years and never once needed.

Until now.

* * *

He watched the search from the tree line above the cove, flat against a granite shoulder with the patience of a man who'd spent whole days doing nothing but watching weather.

Four grey coats. They didn't knock, which told him the warrant was broad enough not to require courtesy. They went through his cabin the way tide goes through a wreck—thorough, indifferent, thorough again. He saw one of them find his strongbox of savings under the bed and weigh it thoughtfully before setting it down untouched, which meant they weren't thieves. They were worse. They were professionals.

The tall one—the one with the paper—stood in the middle of the room turning slowly, reading the cabin itself the way Wendell read a mountain face. Looking for the crack. Looking for the thing that didn't fit.

Wendell found himself absurdly proud of the leaky roof. *Go on,* he thought. *Judge me. Everyone does.*

Then the tall one knelt by the hearth, and Wendell's stomach dropped through the cliff and into the sea.

Because here was the flaw in eleven years of invisibility: he had been so careful to be unremarkable that his cabin was a perfect record of a man with nothing to hide. And a man with nothing to hide was, to a professional, the most interesting object in any room. The tall one ran his palm along the floorboards near the hearth—not searching, just feeling, the way you'd feel a wall for a hollow—and Wendell watched him pause at the third board and tap it once with a knuckle.

The sound it made was wrong. Even from two hundred yards, Wendell knew it was wrong.

They had the journal inside four minutes. He watched the tall one unwrap the oilcloth with the reverence of a man opening evidence, watched him flip through pages while his companions gathered round, watched the moment the tall one went very still and said something that made the others stop smiling.

Then they started looking for him in earnest.

Wendell should have gone right then. Down the far side of the ridge, along the drover's road, gone before noon, gone clean. That was the discipline. That was the religion. *Be smoke. Be fog. Be nothing.*

Instead he stayed, because he saw the boy.

Pip—or Nip—had come back up the path, drawn by the same fatal curiosity that had dragged Wendell into a cave a week ago, and the grey coats had caught him at the gate. Not arresting him. Just talking. The tall one crouched to the boy's level, friendly as a Sunday sermon, and showed him the paper, and the boy shook his head, and the tall one said something else, and the boy shook his head again, and Wendell read the whole conversation in body language the way he read scree slopes: *Where did he go. I don't know. Are you sure. I don't know. Are you sure you want to be sure.*

The boy spat at him.

Wendell closed his eyes. When he opened them, two of the grey coats were holding the boy's arms and the tall one was still crouched, still friendly, explaining something patiently, and the boy was saying something with his chin up that was either defiance or a list of words his mother would have washed his mouth for.

*Walk away,* said eleven years of discipline. *You owe him nothing. You owe everyone nothing. That's how you survive.*

Wendell Eriksen stood up out of the heather.

Not to fight. God, no—four of them, and he was fifty-one years old with a bad knee that forecast rain better than the almanac. He stood up, and he picked up a stone the size of a loaf, and he threw it down-slope into the rocks below the cabin with a crack like a rifle shot, and then he shouted—in his best mountain voice, the one that carried across valleys—"*Down here! You want me, I'm down here!*"

Every head turned. Two grey coats broke toward the tree line. The tall one stayed with the boy, which was the best Wendell could manage, and it wasn't nothing—it meant the beating, if it came, would be administered by fewer hands.

Then Wendell ran.

He ran the way he'd taught clients to run downhill for twenty years, weight back, feet quick, letting gravity do the work while he chose every step half a beat early. Behind him he heard shouting, and once, a whistle—two notes, high, repeated. Signaling someone. He didn't let himself think about who.

He lost them at the quarry, doubling back along the rim where the spoil heaps gave him cover, and came down into the fishing village from the north like a man returning from an ordinary walk, breathing hard, knee screaming, dignity in ruins. The Salt Loom was open. The Salt Loom was always open. He went in, took his usual corner, ordered his usual single cup, and discovered that his hands were shaking too badly to lift it.

"You look terrible," said the innkeeper, who was seventy percent deaf and one hundred percent observant.

"Long walk."

"Mm." She poured him something stronger than what he'd asked for and didn't charge him, which frightened him more than the grey coats had.

* * *

They found the boy an hour later, or rather the boy found them—came staggering into the Salt Loom holding his side, lip split, one eye already closing like a shy flower, and sat down across from Wendell without being asked.

"I told them nothing," the boy said, thickly, proudly.

"I know."

"They asked where you'd go and I said the quarry and they believed me, which was stupid of them, because why would anyone go to the quarry." He winced, touched his ribs, winced again. "The tall one hit me anyway. Said it was for spitting. Said next time it'd be for lying." He leaned forward, and his good eye was bright and furious and eleven years old in all the ways that mattered. "Was it true? What I told the woman? About the dry season?"

Wendell went cold. "What woman."

"Dark hair. Came asking after you an hour ago, all official, said she was your late brother's solicitor, which is rubbish because you haven't got a brother, I checked years ago out of boredom." The boy grinned through the blood. "I gave her the message. Shepherd's track, above the quarry. She said—" he screwed up his face, recalling, "—she said, 'the dry season,' like she already knew what it meant. Then she went up the hill like she had somewhere to be."

Wendell sat very still, holding his cup, watching steam rise off it in the lamplight, and understood with perfect clarity that his invisible life was over. Not ended—over, finished, used up, the way a rope is used up: not broken, just frayed through, ready to part at the worst possible load.

They had the journal. Every bearing, every date, every page of Aldous Vane's careful hand. Which meant Hale would have it by week's end, and Hale could read, and whatever the journal held, the magistrate would hold it too.

But they didn't have the strongbox. Because the strongbox's location lived in exactly one place: the last page of the journal, the page Wendell had torn out and burned in his hearth six nights ago, feeding it to the fire page by page while telling himself he was being prudent rather than tempted. Coordinates, tide tables, the cave's true name written in Vane's shorthand—all ash. He alone carried the knowledge now, locked behind his eyes, and for the first time in eleven years, Wendell Eriksen was the most important man on the Khoral coast.

And the most findable.

"You're making a face," the boy said. "A thinking face. I don't like your thinking face."

"Drink this." Wendell pushed the untasted cup across the table. "And then you're going to walk home the long way, and for the next month you're going to be the dullest boy on the coast. Count firewood. Fall asleep before dark."

"What'll you be doing?"

Wendell looked out the window, up the hill, toward the shepherd's track where a woman with ledger-paper eyes was presumably waiting, having decoded a message she should never have been able to decode.

"Apparently," he said, "I'm going to be seen."

He left the coin under the cup, took his good coat off its hook, and climbed the hill toward the quarry with his knee complaining and his heart, for the first time in eleven years, loud.
