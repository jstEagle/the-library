## 7. The Dry Season Clock

The shepherd's name was Edda, and she was older than the road she lived beside, which was saying something, because the road had been there when Wendell's grandfather was a boy.

They found her at the edge of the high pasture above Tarnholt, mending a wall that did not need mending, which Wendell understood immediately to be the point. A woman who lived alone at altitude learned to keep her hands busy so her mind could do its real work, which was watching.

"You're the one asking about the Saddle," she said, without looking up from her stones.

"We're told you know it," Isolde said.

"I know it the way you know a husband who drinks. Fondly, and with reservations." She set a stone in place, patted it once as if it were a dog, and finally looked up. Her eyes were pale as river ice and missed nothing—not Wendell's weathered hands, not Isolde's city boots gone soft with trail dust, not the journal wrapped in oilcloth under Wendell's arm like something stolen, which, in fairness, it was not, though he carried it that way all the same.

"Sit," Edda said. "People who ask about the Saddle of Ashes are always in a hurry, and hurrying people don't listen."

Wendell sat on the wall. Isolde remained standing for exactly three seconds, then sat too, which told him something about her: she could be stubborn, but she was not stupid, and she knew the difference between the two better than most clever people did.

"The pass opens when the snows melt," Edda said. "That's the easy part. Anyone can tell you that. The hard part is the other end of it. The Saddle doesn't close like a door. It closes like a trap."

She held up two hands, palms facing, and brought them slowly together.

"Meltwater runs through the scree all summer. You can hear it under your feet, if you're the listening kind. Then the cold comes, and the water freezes in the cracks, and everything that was loose becomes locked. For about a week, it's the finest walking in the world—frozen ground, firm footing, clear skies. And then the snow comes down on top of it, and after that, nobody crosses until spring. Not nobody wise, anyway." She looked at Wendell. "You'll have heard of people who crossed late. There's a reason you heard of them and not their route."

"When?" Isolde asked. "When does it close?"

Edda smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. It was the smile of someone who had been asked this question by many people, some of whom had believed her and lived.

"That depends on who's asking and why. If you're asking because you want to cross and come back, I'd say turn around now. If you're asking because you want to cross and *stay*—" she studied them both, "—then I'd say you have five weeks. Perhaps four and a half, if the wind off the Icefield turns early this year, and I think it might. My knees say so. My knees have been right more often than the magistrate's surveyors."

Wendell felt something tighten in his chest that was not quite fear and not quite hope. It was the feeling he got at the start of every season, looking up at a mountain he intended to climb: the arithmetic of it, the body counting what the mind had already promised.

Five weeks.

He had crossed worse passes in worse conditions with less warning. He had also, once, ignored his own judgment on a mountain because a man with authority told him to, and three people had gone home in boxes. The memory arrived on schedule, the way it always did, and he let it sit for a moment before setting it aside. That was the discipline. Not never thinking of it—that way lay madness—but thinking of it and continuing anyway.

"It can be done," he said. "In five weeks. With the right party and the right pace."

"Of course it can be done," Edda said. "Everything can be done. The question is what it costs, and whether you've brought enough to pay." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and pointed east, where the range stood up against the sky like the jawbone of the world. "You see that notch? The low one, left of the horn?"

Isolde squinted. "I see a lot of rock."

"That's the Saddle. From here it looks like a gap you could stroll through. From here, everything looks possible. That's the first trick of mountains—they show you their best face from a distance." She gathered her crook. "Come back tomorrow. Bring questions written down, because you'll forget half of them, and bring nothing else you value, because I'll want to know how you react to losing things."

"She's testing us," Isolde said, after the old woman had gone over the rise.

"Yes."

"And we're going back."

"Yes."

Isolde looked at the notch in the skyline a moment longer, then at Wendell. "Good," she said. "I'd have been disappointed in a guide who flinched at an old woman."

* * *

The town of Tarnholt smelled of tallow and wet wool, and its single inn—the Drowned Bell, named for a church bell lost in a flood no one alive remembered—had exactly one room worth sleeping in, which was why they did not have it. Wendell took the corner near the stove; Isolde took the table by the window and spread out her father's journal like a general spreading maps.

By lamplight, she worked. By habit, Wendell watched.

He had spent eleven years making himself unremarkable, and part of that discipline was learning to read people quickly and then pretend he hadn't. Isolde Vane read like a ledger: precise entries, small margins, everything accounted for. But ledgers had a way of hiding what mattered in the footnotes, and her footnote, he was beginning to suspect, was grief. She carried it the way he carried his guilt—compactly, efficiently, in a place where it wouldn't slow the walking.

She looked up and caught him watching. Neither of them pretended otherwise.

"Routes," she said, tapping the table. "Talk to me about routes."

"Two ways over." He dragged his chair around and pulled the journal toward them both, opening it to Vane's survey sketches. "The southern approach follows the old Merrow road as far as the river gorge. It's longer—call it nine days to the Saddle instead of seven—but it stays low through the treeline, which matters if the weather turns early. Water, wood, shelter. All the things that keep people alive when the mountain changes its mind."

"And the northern route?"

"Faster. Exposed. One bad storm and you're pinned on bare rock above the treeline with nothing to burn and nowhere to dig in." He tapped Vane's inked line, which hugged the southern valley like a hand in a glove. "Your father marked the southern route. Twice. Underlined it."

"He wasn't a cautious man."

"No," Wendell agreed. "But he was a thorough one, and thorough men underline things for a reason." He paused. "There's a third option. We go fast and light on the north trail and gamble on the weather holding."

Isolde looked at him steadily. "And you won't suggest it."

"No."

"Because of what happened before."

The inn noise went on around them—dice, argument, a fiddle tuning itself out of tune—and Wendell found, to his mild surprise, that he didn't mind answering. "Partly. Mostly because a guide's job isn't to give people the fastest route. It's to give them the route they survive. Fast routes are for people traveling alone, and even then, only the ones who enjoy being wrong."

Something in Isolde's face shifted—a fraction of trust, banked like coin. "Then south. How many days total, crossing and descending?"

"Nine up. Four down, if the far side cooperates, which far sides rarely do. Call it fourteen days of walking, plus rest days, plus margin for weather. Five weeks gives us room. Barely. Comfortably barely, which is the best kind of comfortable there is."

"And supplies for how many?"

"That depends on the party." He leaned back. "Which brings us to the awkward question. Who, exactly, is coming? Because right now it's a disgraced guide, a surveyor's daughter, and a dead man's diary, and I've crossed easier-looking parties into worse-looking places."

Isolde smiled for the first time that evening, and it transformed her face from something careful into something quick and bright. "I've been making inquiries," she said. "As it happens, so has everyone else in this valley. Word travels. A woman came through last week asking after graves. A boy at the ferry office reads stars the way other children read penny dreadfuls. And there's talk of an engineer living rough past the mill—exiled, like you, though I gather his crimes involved bridges rather than testimony."

"Bridges," Wendell said.

"So I'm told."

"Bridge engineers are either very trustworthy or catastrophically not. There's no middle ground. It's a profession that punishes approximation."

"Then we'll find out which he is." She turned back to the journal, and her voice softened by a degree. "My father wrote that the toll was never a monster. It was a price. Somebody paid it for years, quietly, so that a village could keep what was theirs. He wanted that returned. So do I. Whatever it takes to get it over that saddle—" she touched the page, gently, the way you touch a thing that belonged to someone you loved, "—we'll need people who understand what it costs to be erased. People with practice surviving it."

Wendell looked at the lamplight on her hands, at the journal, at the notch of the Saddle invisible now behind walls and roof, present all the same, five weeks away and closing.

"Then we shop carefully," he said. "And we leave early."

* * *

Isolde negotiated the way her father must have surveyed: patiently, precisely, and with an attention to detail that made grown merchants sweat.

The outfitter in Tarnholt was a broad man named Pell whose shop smelled of hemp and lamp oil and whose prices changed depending on how desperate you looked. Isolde made sure she looked anything but.

"Rope," she said, running her thumb along a coil. "Sixty yards, best grade. This is river rope. I want mountain rope."

Pell folded his arms. "Mountain rope costs double."

"Mountain rope holds double. Funny how those numbers work out." She set the coil down and picked up the next one, reading the weave like text. "This one. And before you tell me the price again, consider: I'm buying for a crossing everyone in this valley says is impossible, in a season everyone says is too late. When we come back—and we will come back—every caravan heading over that saddle will buy from the man who equipped us. That's not a sale. That's an advertisement with legs."

Pell blinked. Wendell, pretending to examine pitons by the window, hid a smile. Eleven years of invisibility had taught him every dialect of the coast, but he was hearing a new one now, and it was fluent.

"Pitons," Isolde continued, laying them out in a row like surgery tools. "Twelve. Two tents that actually shed water—I'll test them in your yard with a bucket if you like. Dried meat for six, forty days. And whatever your cook swears by for altitude sickness, because I'm told willow bark is a lie sold to tourists."

"The cook swears by garlic and coca leaf," Pell admitted, "but that's between you and him."

"Everything is between me and someone," Isolde said pleasantly. "That's rather the point of commerce."

It took her the better part of the afternoon, and by the end of it she had spent nearly all of the money she'd carried from the coast, haggled Pell down to prices he would later describe to his wife as ruinous, and extracted, as a final concession, a promise of credit against their return. Wendell carried the loads to the inn in two trips and found her at the window table again, tallying receipts in the journal's margin, using her father's blank pages without ceremony, as if he would have approved.

"You're good at that," he said.

"I grew up poor in a rich house. There's no better training." She blotted the ink. "We have food for six for thirty-five days, gear for six, and money for perhaps one more recruit. Which means the next problem is people, not provisions."

"Then tomorrow we solve people." He hesitated. "One condition. Whoever we take, I lead on the mountain. No committees above the treeline. Committees freeze."

"Agreed." She said it instantly, and he realized she'd been waiting for him to claim it. "But below the treeline, Mr. Eriksen, decisions get discussed."

"Below the treeline," he said gravely, "I wouldn't dream of deciding anything."

Outside, the light was going amber over the eastern peaks, and somewhere up there, past the visible edge of the world, a notch of stone waited with its week of frozen ground and its winter coming on. Five weeks. Maybe four and a half, if an old woman's knees knew what they knew.

Hope, Wendell thought, should probably come with fewer deadlines attached. But then, so should everything worth having.

He was still thinking that when the inn door opened and a girl of maybe sixteen walked in, star charts stuffed in her coat, asking the innkeeper—in a voice pitched carefully casual—for directions to the house of anyone who'd been asking about the Saddle of Ashes.

Wendell and Isolde exchanged a look across the table.

Word traveled. Apparently faster than they did.
