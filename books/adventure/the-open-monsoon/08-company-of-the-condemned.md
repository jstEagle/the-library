## 8. Company of the Condemned

Isolde Vane had spent her adult life among people who measured worth in signatures and seals, and she had learned to read a room the way her father read contour lines—by what rose, what fell, and where the ground was about to give way.

The back room of the Saltcellar tavern did not rise or fall so much as sag. It smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and the particular despair of men who drank before noon on a Tuesday. Wendell Eriksen stood at the far end of it with his arms crossed, wearing the expression of a man who had been dragged somewhere against his will and intended to make that everyone else's problem.

"You said you had contacts," he said.

"I said I had *names*," Isolde corrected. "Contacts imply mutual affection. Names are just places to start."

"Names of whom? The tide-pool boy?"

"The tide-pool boy," Isolde said, "can navigate by stars that haven't risen yet. I checked."

Wendell's eyebrows performed a small skeptical pilgrimage toward his hairline. "You checked."

"I asked him which way north was at midnight, three nights running, under cloud. He got it right every time. Then he told me the moon would be occluded by Thursday and he'd been sleeping through his shifts because there was no point watching a sky that was going to lie to him." She let that sit. "That's not a boy showing off. That's a boy who knows the sky the way you know rockfall."

Wendell said nothing, which from him was practically applause.

* * *

Tam Quill turned out to be seventeen, freckled to the point of camouflage, and currently employed—loosely—bailing bilge off the ferry *Maren's Grace*. He received them on deck with a mop in one hand and an expression of profound suspicion, which Isolde respected. Suspicion was the correct response to most adults.

"I'm not going anywhere with strangers," Tam announced.

"Sensible," Isolde agreed. "We're not asking you to. We're asking if you want to see the Saddle of Ashes before the snows close it. Nobody's ever mapped the approach from the south side properly. My father tried. He died trying."

Tam's mop paused mid-swish. "The surveyor? Vane?"

"You knew of him?"

"Everyone on the water knows of him. He paid for star readings all up this coast eleven years ago. My mother did one for him once—she kept the coin." His eyes narrowed further, which Isolde hadn't thought anatomically possible. "You're his daughter."

"I am."

"And the mountain man is—" Tam squinted at Wendell. "You're the one from the trial. The guide who lost the whole party on the Kestrel Col."

Isolde felt Wendell go still beside her, the stillness of a man bracing for a blow he'd taken many times before. But Tam only shrugged and went back to mopping.

"My uncle says you were framed," the boy offered, to the bilge water rather than to either of them. "My uncle says a lot of things. But he also says the Kestrel report has a page missing, and pages don't go missing by weather." He looked up. "What's the pay?"

"There isn't any pay," Wendell said flatly. "There's food, and there's getting home alive, and there's standing on a pass no one's stood on since your grandmother was young."

Tam considered this with the gravity of a magistrate weighing a verdict. "Does the food include the dried peaches? Because Doss hordes the dried peaches and I've been eating weevily biscuit for four months."

"Dried peaches can be arranged."

"Then I'm in." He stuck out a hand still slick with bilgewater, and Wendell, after a visible internal negotiation, shook it. "But I want it noted that if anyone asks, I ran away to sea, and now I'm running away to mountains, and both times it was somebody else's idea."

"Noted," Isolde said, and found herself smiling for the first time in days.

* * *

Brakka was harder.

They found him—or rather, they found the wreckage of his workshop first, a lean-to behind the ropewalk where a broad-shouldered woman with iron-gray braids was repairing a waterwheel bearing with tools she had clearly built herself because no one would sell her any. Her left hand ended at the second knuckle. She worked around it without appearing to notice it existed.

"Brakka Solenne?" Isolde called.

The hammer stopped. "Depends who's asking and what they're selling."

"Nothing. We're buying. Or rather—we're offering work."

"Work." Brakka set down the hammer and turned, and Isolde saw that her eyes were the pale gray of glacier ice and about as welcoming. "I know what my work is worth here, girl. It's worth a fine and a warning, and next time it'll be worth a cell. Your magistrate doesn't license engineers who trained across the border. So unless you're offering me a pardon, you're wasting both our mornings."

"We're offering you a bridge," Isolde said.

Brakka laughed—a short, barking sound with no humor in it. "There's no river on the southern approach worth a bridge."

"There's a gorge above the Merrow road. My father's journal marks it. He wrote: *'Crossing impossible without engineering. Local bridges burned by order.'*" She watched the woman's face carefully. "Burned by order, Mistress Solenne. Somebody gave that order. Somebody wanted the road forgotten badly enough to burn its crossings. And I have reason to believe the same somebody erased the village the road served."

The silence stretched long enough that Isolde could hear the ropewalk creaking, the gulls arguing over something dead, Wendell shifting his weight in the way she was learning meant *this is a mistake, why do I follow her into these*.

"What village?" Brakka said finally.

"Merrow's Reach."

Something moved behind the glacier eyes. Not softening—recalculation. "My grandmother came from up-valley," Brakka said slowly. "She never said from where, exactly. Just 'up-valley,' like the name itself was a debt she couldn't pay." She wiped her good hand on her apron. "You're telling me there's a reason she wouldn't say."

"I'm telling you there are records that say otherwise than she did. And I'm telling you the people who made those records are the same people who'll be riding after us once they realize what we're carrying."

"And the crossing?"

"Impossible," Isolde admitted. "By your own profession's standards, probably. My father thought a fallen span on the north abutment might carry a rebuilt cradle—if someone could engineer one light enough to haul and strong enough to trust."

Brakka looked at her ruined hand, then at the waterwheel turning behind her, then at something much farther away than any of it.

"How many days' food do you have?" she said.

* * *

Sister Ondine they did not find at all. She found them.

They were loading supplies at the ferry office the next morning—rope, flour, the promised dried peaches, over Wendell's strenuous objections about weight—when a small woman in a traveling habit faded out of the crowd the way fog fades off water. She was perhaps sixty, with a face like folded linen and hands that clasped themselves in front of her as though holding something invisible and precious.

"You are going to the high country," she said. It was not a question.

"We're going to a pass," Wendell said carefully. "Pilgrimage. Family matter."

"Mm." Ondine's gaze traveled over their gear with unhurried precision—the rope coils, the survey case strapped to Isolde's pack, the brass fittings of Brakka's tool roll peeking from its canvas. "A pilgrimage with an engineer. How modern." She smiled, and it transformed her face entirely, into something sly and warm at once. "I keep the graves past the river bend, Master Eriksen. Unmarked ones. Condemned ones. The ones the parish rolls call *moved* when everyone knows nothing was ever moved but the stones." Her voice stayed gentle throughout, which somehow made it worse, or better; Isolde couldn't decide. "Thirty-one stones I tend. Thirty-one names I copied from a ledger fragment before it was sold for scrap. Do you know what a ledger fragment is worth, to people like me?"

"No," Isolde said honestly.

"Everything. It's the only proof some of those people lived at all." Ondine unfolded her clasped hands, and Isolde saw she held a small book—hand-bound, oilcloth-covered, worn soft as cloth at the corners. "I have come to ask passage. Not charity. Passage. I can cook, mend, set bones, and pray over anything that needs it, including magistrates, though in their case I find other methods more effective."

Wendell rubbed the back of his neck. "Sister, this isn't a pilgrimage. What we're doing—it may get ugly. There's a man named Hale who—"

"I know exactly who Corven Hale is." For the first time, the gentleness in her voice acquired an edge, thin and bright as a scalpel. "He signed the condemnation order for Merrow's Reach. I have read his signature more times than he has, I suspect. He practiced it smaller, in the early years." She tucked the grave-book away. "You needn't decide now. I will simply be walking the same direction, at the same pace, until you come to your senses. Old women walk slowly, but we are very difficult to discourage."

Isolde caught Wendell's eye. He wore the expression of a man watching his careful expedition acquire passengers like a hull acquires barnacles.

"She stays," Isolde said.

"You don't even—"

"Thirty-one names, Wendell. Written down before the ledger was destroyed. That's not a passenger. That's evidence with legs."

Ondine beamed at being described as evidence with legs, which Isolde suspected was not a compliment she received often.

* * *

They met that evening in the Saltcellar's back room, because it was cheap, private, and depressing enough that no one eavesdropped voluntarily. A single lamp. A map weighted down with teacups. Five people who, Isolde reflected, would have been turned away from any respectable company of travelers in the Khoral coast: a blacklisted guide, a smuggler's daughter with forged letters of introduction, a runaway bilge-boy, an unlicensed engineer, and a nun who collected the names of the officially dead.

It should have frightened her. Instead, looking around the lamplight—at Tam demonstrating with salt grains how the stars would lie about north during the crossing, at Brakka sketching a cradle-bridge on the back of a supply list, at Ondine quietly correcting the map's river bend from memory of a road that predated the map—Isolde felt something loosen in her chest that had been clenched for eleven years.

Her father had died alone in a cave with a strongbox and a confession nobody would ever read. He had died believing the truth about Merrow's Reach would stay buried under salt and tide forever.

He had not counted on this. Whatever *this* was. This ragged, argumentative, improbable thing.

"We leave in four days," Wendell was saying, tapping the map. "Before word travels. Which means before Hale travels. We move light, we move quiet, and we do not tell anyone—not anyone—where we're going or why. Agreed?"

Four heads nodded. Isolde nodded too, and meant it, and felt the small warm machinery of the plan turning beneath her hands like her father's old brass instruments.

It was Tam, packing up his salt, who broke the spell.

"One question," the boy said. "If we're not telling anyone where we're going—how come the ferry clerk already asked me this morning if the mountain man had booked northbound passage?"

The lamp flame leaned in the draft. Nobody spoke.

Somewhere outside, down by the water, a horse was being walked slowly past the tavern. More than one horse. Shod, heavy, unhurried—the pace of men who were not in any rush because they already knew where they were going.

Wendell blew out the lamp.
