## 9. The Tollgate Town

The town was called Gravenlow, which Wendell had always thought was a name somebody gave a place after a long night of drinking and a short morning of regret. It sat in the last fold of green country before the mountains took over, a scatter of slate roofs and one ambitious church spire, and it had exactly two reasons to exist: the road forked there, and the magistrate's men collected tolls there.

Everything else about Gravenlow seemed embarrassed by these facts.

They came down off the hills on the fourth evening out from the shepherd's hut, six of them and a mule named Duchess who had opinions about bridges. The light was going amber and long, and the mountains ahead had put on their evening colors — grey turning violet, snowfields catching fire along their edges. Beautiful, Wendell thought, in the way that beautiful things are beautiful right up until they kill you.

"Checkpoint," Tam said, from his perch on the mule's back, where he'd claimed squatter's rights somewhere around noon and refused all eviction notices. He had good eyes. "Two men at the barrier. One's got a crossbow."

"One has a crossbow," Brakka corrected, "and one has a *list*. I can tell from here. The list is worse."

"The list is always worse," Wendell agreed.

Isolde came up beside him, walking with her chin up and her jaw set, which he'd learned was her way of being tired without admitting it. Sister Ondine walked beside her with her hands folded into her sleeves, serene as a pond, though Wendell had noticed over the past days that the serenity had edges. You didn't spend twenty years tending the graves of an erased village without developing edges.

"They'll have the warrant," Isolde said quietly. "Hale's fast when he's frightened."

"He's not frightened," Wendell said. "That's the problem. A frightened man makes mistakes. Hale just sends paperwork."

The warrant would have gone out to every toll station, ferry crossing, and bored garrison within three days' ride: *Wendell Eriksen, guide, blacklisted, wanted for questioning regarding stolen documents.* And probably — because Hale was thorough the way rot is thorough — descriptions of anyone traveling with him. A young woman matching the Vane description. An exiled engineer built like a collapsed barn. A boy too thin and too clever. A nun of no registered order.

Six people, one mule, and a strongbox wrapped in oilcloth and disguised as a coffin lid, because Brakka had insisted that if you're going to smuggle something through a checkpoint, you should give the guards a reason not to look at it too closely, and nothing made men look away faster than death.

Which was, as it turned out, where the plan came from.

* * *

It had come to Wendell somewhere on the second day, watching Ondine stop yet again to fix a cairn or straighten a leaning marker stone they passed on the old track. She did it without thinking, the way some people straighten picture frames in other people's houses. She couldn't help herself. The dead deserved order, and she was the only one left to give it to them.

"You've buried people," he'd said to her that evening, by way of opening a conversation, which was not his strength.

"Hundreds," she said, in the tone another woman might use to say *dozens of pies*.

"And you know the rites. All of them."

"All of them." She'd looked at him then, and her eyes were quick and dry and entirely unfooled. "You're building toward something, Mr. Eriksen."

"I'm building toward a lie," he said, "and I need it to be a good one."

He told her the shape of it, and she listened all the way through, and then she said the thing that made the whole plan stand up:

"You want me to be dead. Wendell, I have been dead to the diocese for eleven years. They struck me from the rolls when I kept saying Merrow's Reach out loud in places where it wasn't allowed. There is no record anywhere that says I'm alive. Being dead is the most honest work I've ever been offered."

And so here they were, at dusk, at the tollgate of Gravenlow, and Wendell Eriksen — blacklisted, disgraced, a man whose professional reputation consisted entirely of the fact that he had once failed to keep four people alive — stepped forward to do the only kind of guiding he had left.

He was going to lead this party past danger using nothing but his voice.

* * *

The checkpoint was a timber barrier across the road, a toll shed with a stovepipe smoking cheerfully, and two men. The one with the crossbow was young and looked like he mostly used it to threaten chickens. The one with the list was older, grey-whiskered, with the particular slouch of a man who had been given authority slightly larger than his imagination.

"Well then," said the list man, as they drew up. "Toll's two coppers a head, five for the mule."

"We're not travelers," Wendell said. "We're a burial party."

The word did what words like that do. The chicken-threatener actually took a half step back from his own crossbow, as if it might be contagious.

"A what?" said the list man.

"Burial party." Wendell let his voice drop into the register he used for explaining weather to clients who didn't want to hear about weather — calm, unhurried, faintly sorry. "Sister Ondine, of the Order of the Quiet Field. She died three days ago, up at the high pasture. Fever. We're bringing her down to be laid in consecrated ground before—" he paused, and let the pause do its work— "before anything else happens to her."

"Before what else—" the young one started.

"Fever," Brakka rumbled, from the back, with the gravity of a landslide considering its options. It was, Wendell reflected, deeply unfair how convincing a man could be simply by being enormous and sad.

The list man's eyes went to the coffin lid lashed across the mule's pack frame. Oilcloth, rope-knotted, with a sprig of rowan tucked under the lashings — Ondine's idea, that. Rowan for the dead. It was the kind of detail a lie needed. A lie without details was just a story; a lie with a sprig of rowan was a fact.

"That's her, then?" the list man asked. "In there?"

"She is," Wendell said solemnly, "in the sense that matters to us. What's in there now is ash and a shroud. We burned her at the pasture, per her instructions. Her remains travel under the lid. Her orders were very specific and I am not authorized to discuss them further."

This was, Wendell felt, the masterstroke. Nothing disarmed a small official like the suggestion that somewhere above him was a larger official with specific instructions. He watched it land. The list man's shoulders did a small defeated thing.

"There's a warrant," the list man said, but he said it the way a man says *there's weather coming*, already looking for the door of the conversation. "For a party coming through. Guide named Eriksen. Blacklisted man. Trouble."

"Yes," Wendell said.

Everyone stopped. Even Duchess stopped chewing.

"You know him?" The list man's hand moved toward his list with sudden new purpose.

"I *was* him," Wendell said. "Eleven years ago. Before the ban." He let out a breath, and made it sound like eleven years of weight leaving his chest, which required almost no acting at all. "I'm done with guiding. Done with the ranges, done with passes, done with all of it. I drive a hearse cart now, out of Lowmarsh. This is the last job I'll ever do that smells like mountains, and I promise you I will be glad to see the back of it."

He said it flatly, tiredly, a man reciting his own obituary, and he watched the list man decide — visibly, almost gratefully — that a broken blacklist case was nobody's promotion.

"The woman with you," the list man pressed, because he was not entirely without spine. "Dark-haired. Young. There's talk of a Vane girl."

"My daughter," Isolde said.

She hadn't rehearsed that. Wendell hadn't asked her to. She stepped forward into the torchlight with her chin still set and her eyes wet — genuinely wet, Wendell realized, because whatever else was true, Aldous Vane's daughter was carrying her father's ashes of a different kind, and grief doesn't check whether it's convenient.

"She's been ill herself," Isolde went on, steady now, laying each word like a stone in a wall. "Grief-fever. That's why the sister's people are with us. That's why we're late getting home. My father went into those mountains eleven years ago and never came out, and I am taking my family home to bury our dead, sir, and if your warrant has a place on it for that, then write me down."

Silence. The stovepipe smoked. Somewhere in the town behind the barrier a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

The list man looked at her for a long moment. Then at the coffin lid. Then at Sister Ondine — who was, Wendell noted with admiration, standing at the rear of the party with her hood up and her hands folded, playing her own corpse's chaperone with perfect stillness — and then back at his list, which suddenly seemed to weigh a great deal.

"Burying the dead's not a crime," he said finally. "Never has been, even in bad years." He waved them forward. "Toll's waived for burial parties. Diocese rule. Go on, then. And you—" he pointed a whiskered finger at Wendell— "you stay off the high routes, hear? Whatever you're done with, stay done."

"Sir," Wendell said, with complete sincerity, "it is the deepest wish of my heart."

* * *

They rolled through Gravenlow as the lamps came on, past the church with its embarrassed spire, past a tavern called the Drowned Salmon that appeared to be doing brisk business drowning salmon, and out the far side onto the mountain road before anyone spoke.

Then Tam slid off the mule, landed, and said, "That was the greatest thing I have ever seen in my life, and I once saw Brakka lose a wrestling match to a goat."

"The goat cheated," Brakka said.

"You *lied*," Tam went on, delighted, circling Wendell. "You lied to an armed man with a *list*. You lied with, like, *layers*. The ashes! The instructions! 'Not authorized to discuss them further' — where did you even learn that?"

"Every client meeting I ever sat in," Wendell said. "Half of guiding is telling people things are settled when you haven't decided yet."

But Isolde wasn't laughing. She fell into step beside him, and after a while she said, quietly, "You said you were done. With the mountains."

"I said what worked."

"It sounded true."

"It was true," Wendell admitted. "Right up until a low tide showed me a cave. Now I don't know what's true. Ask me again at the pass."

Ahead of them the road climbed into the darkening shoulder of the range, and the first stars were out, and Tam — star-reader, runaway, boy who could find north blindfolded — went quiet all at once.

"Wendell," he said. His voice had changed. "There's dust on the back road. Behind us. A lot of it."

Wendell turned. Far down the valley, where the low country flattened out, a pale thread of dust hung in the last light, moving. Not a cart. Carts didn't move like that. Carts didn't move in a line, six or seven abreast, eating the miles like they had a schedule and a warrant.

"Riders," Brakka said grimly. "Fast ones."

Wendell looked at the mountains, then at the dust, then did the arithmetic he hated most in the world — distance, hours, the weight of a mule, the speed of men who'd been paid to be quick.

"How long to the old Merrow road?" Isolde asked.

"If we walk through the night," Wendell said, "we might reach the river ruins by dawn. After that, the maps go blank, and so does their warrant." He tightened Duchess's girth strap and found, to his mild surprise, that his hands were steady. "So we walk through the night."

Behind them, very far away now, someone in Gravenlow was probably telling the tavern at the Drowned Salmon about the strange burial party that had come through at dusk. Wendell hoped they made a good story of it. It was, he suspected, the last easy tale anyone would get for a while.
