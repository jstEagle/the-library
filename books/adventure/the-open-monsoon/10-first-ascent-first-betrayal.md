## 10. First Ascent, First Betrayal

My father taught me to read people the way he read contour lines—by what they left out. A mapmaker's trick, he called it. *The lie is never in the ink, Isolde. It's in the space between two marks.* I had spent eleven years practicing his craft on customs officers and harbor clerks, learning which silences meant boredom and which meant suspicion.

I should have applied it to Brakka Voss sooner.

The signs had been there since Tollgate Town, small as survey pins: how he always volunteered for the rear watch, where a man could see who followed without seeming to look; how he'd asked Wendell, twice, casually, which col we intended to cross—the Saddle of Ashes or the longer northern route—and then never mentioned it again; how his hands had gone still whenever Tam talked about the stars, as if the boy's cheerful navigation talk were something being recorded.

But I was tired, and grief makes a poor sentry. Grief wants company. And Brakka had been good company—grumbling, funny in a dry way, endlessly competent with rope and pulley. When he fixed my boot heel at the last camp with a strip of tin and a length of wire, humming some engineer's hymn under his breath, I had thought: *here is a man who builds things.* I did not think: *here is a man who once built things for people who paid him.*

The mountain began our education early. The Saddle of Ashes approach was three days of switchbacks through pine that smelled like a church full of incense, then scree, then nothing but rock and sky. Sister Ondine climbed like a woman walking to market, which unnerved everyone. Tam climbed like a goat with opinions. And Wendell climbed like water finding its level—no wasted motion, no drama, reading the mountain the way other men read weather.

It was on the second morning above the treeline, at a place Wendell called the Ledge of Regrets ("because you will regret eating your breakfast here," he said, and he was right), that everything came apart.

* * *

We had stopped to rewrap blisters and argue about rations, which by then had become our most reliable form of worship. Tam claimed he could live on dried apricots alone. Ondine said apricots were a heresy against the stomach. Brakka said nothing, which was itself unusual—he normally had strong views about food, all of them wrong.

Wendell had gone ahead twenty paces to scout a snow bridge over a crevasse field. I watched him crouch, press his ice axe flat to the surface, listen. He did this often, listening to the mountain through the metal, and I had privately decided it was half superstition and half genius, proportions unknown.

That was when I saw Brakka's hand move.

Not toward his pack. Toward his coat's inner pocket—a small, quick gesture, the kind a man makes when he is checking that something is still there. A paper-shaped something. And when he caught me watching, he smiled, and the smile was a fraction too late, arriving like a courier after the news had already spread through town.

"Something in there worth smiling about?" I asked lightly.

"Wire. Spare wire." He patted the pocket. "Engineer's habit."

"You checked your spare wire four times since dawn."

"I'm a thorough man."

Tam, sprawled on a rock with his star charts, looked up with the terrible innocence of the young. "Brakka, are you writing letters up here? Are you in love? Is it Ondine?"

"Sister Ondine would bury me alive," Brakka said, "and she'd do it kindly."

"With a proper marker," Ondine agreed serenely. "You'd get a name and dates. Better than most."

Everyone laughed. Everyone except me, because I was watching Wendell come back down the slope, and I was watching his face, and Wendell's face had gone very quiet in the specific way of a man who has just found something on the ground that shouldn't be there.

He held it up between two fingers as he approached. A scrap of oilcloth, wax-sealed, dropped—or placed—at the edge of the trail where a rider coming up behind them might find it. Or where a man walking rear guard might leave it.

"Brakka," Wendell said mildly. "You want to tell me about this?"

* * *

Here is what I learned about interrogations on a mountainside: there is nowhere to run, so everyone becomes honest, or everyone becomes very creative. Brakka chose honesty, but he made us drag him to it inch by inch, like hauling a crate up a cliff with fraying rope.

The oilcloth held a note in a careful clerk's hand. Coordinates. Our route. Our numbers—five, corrected to four-and-a-suspicious-priestess in a later line. And a date: three days hence, the mouth of the high valley, where riders could enter the mountains from the eastern passes before the snows closed them.

Hale's men. Coming up behind us, invited.

"Three days ago," Wendell said, "at the tollgate. You slipped out during the burial farce. I counted heads wrong—I assumed you were praying."

"I was," Brakka said. "Briefly. Then I met a man selling roasted chestnuts who was not, strictly speaking, a chestnut merchant."

"And you gave him our route."

"I gave him a route." Brakka's jaw worked. "The northern one. The long one. I swear it on every gear train I ever built—that's what the note says, look at it, the coordinates are the north col—"

"The north col closes a week earlier than the Saddle," Wendell said. "Which means Hale's riders take the long way and arrive exactly when we descend into the valley below. You didn't send them away, Brakka. You scheduled them."

The silence that followed had weight, like snow load on a roof. Tam sat up slowly. Ondine folded her hands. I found I was gripping my axe handle without having decided to.

"Why?" I asked. That was the question that mattered. Not how, not when. Men sold routes for gold all the time; the mountains were full of such arithmetic. But Brakka had taken no purse at Tollgate Town. I had watched him haggle over flour like it was a blood sport.

Brakka looked at me, and for the first time since I'd known him, the grumbling humor drained out of his face entirely, leaving something older and much more tired.

"Because Magistrate Corven Hale holds a warrant for my arrest that goes back nine years," he said. "Bridge collapse. The Kestrel Span, over the Vorry River. Forty-one dead." He said the number the way men say numbers they've counted ten thousand times. "I designed that span. I signed off on the timber specs. What I didn't know—what I *couldn't* have known—was that the contractor swapped the seasoned oak for green wood and pocketed the difference, and that the inspection report I trusted had been bought and paid for. The span came down at market hour."

"And Hale?" Wendell asked quietly.

"Hale was the junior magistrate who signed the arrest order. Never mind that the contractor was his cousin by marriage. Never mind that the real inquiry died in a drawer somewhere. My name went on the failure because my name was on the design." Brakka laughed, and it was an awful sound, like a beam settling. "So when a chestnut merchant told me the magistrate himself wanted word of five travelers on the Khoral range, and offered me a signed pardon in exchange—well. Nine years in exile buys a lot of obedience."

"You'd trade us," Tam said. He wasn't angry. He sounded wounded, which was worse. "For a piece of paper."

"I'd trade *anything* for a piece of paper that lets me go home," Brakka snapped, and then his voice cracked down the middle. "Do you know what exile is, boy? It's not a place. It's a door that stays shut. Every night. For nine years."

I understood him. That was the humiliating truth of it. I stood on that ledge understanding him completely, because I had spent eleven years chasing a dead man's handwriting across half a coastline for exactly the same reason—not for justice, whatever I told myself, but for a door. And a man who understands a betrayer cannot fully hate him, which is its own kind of trap.

"What exactly did the pardon promise?" I asked.

"That I'd walk free if the party was 'intercepted without incident.'" His eyes wouldn't meet mine. "No one was supposed to be hurt. That's what he said. Intercepted. Words on paper, and I believed words on paper, which is how I got exiled in the first place, so you'd think I'd have learned."

Ondine spoke then, for the first time. She had been very still throughout, hands folded, and when she finally opened her mouth I expected scripture and got something much more practical.

"The pardon isn't signed yet, is it?"

Brakka blinked. "...What?"

"A magistrate doesn't pre-sign pardons. He promises them. Which means what you actually hold is a promise from Corven Hale, and the only collateral backing that promise is Corven Hale's honor." She let that sit in the cold air. "Child, I have read the graves of the men that man ruined. His honor weighs less than their headstones."

"He'd have honored it," Brakka said, but without conviction. Even he could hear how it sounded.

"Would he?" I pulled the note from Wendell's fingers and read it again. "Look at the date. Three days. Look at the correction—'four and a suspicious priestess.' He knows our count changes. He knows our route bends. Do you know what happens to a witness who's delivered his cargo, Brakka? The same thing that happened to the contractor's inspection reports. You're not buying a pardon. You're buying a shallow grave with your name spelled correctly on it."

* * *

What to do with him was the harder question, and we argued it in low voices while Brakka sat apart on a boulder, radiating the specific misery of a man waiting to learn whether he'll be buried or merely abandoned.

Tam voted, predictably, for drama. "We tie him to a rock and let the eagles negotiate."

"We are not tying anyone to a rock," Wendell said.

"Fine. A *small* rock."

Ondine voted for mercy on theological grounds, though I suspected her theology had practical roots: a man abandoned on a mountain dies, and she had come up here to un-erase names, not add new ones.

I surprised myself by voting to cut him loose. Not from kindness—from arithmetic. A bound man slows us. An abandoned man becomes Hale's informant twice over, out of spite. But a man set free with nothing, stripped of usefulness, is simply a man walking downhill, and downhill was where Hale already was.

Wendell listened to all of us, then walked over to Brakka alone. I couldn't hear what he said. I watched instead—watched Brakka's shoulders drop, watched him nod once, twice, watched Wendell hold out his open palm until Brakka, after a long moment, surrendered the inner-pocket papers: the chestnut merchant's token, a scrap of the promised pardon draft, everything. Wendell fed the pardon draft to his small stove flame and let the wind take the ash.

Then he came back and said only: "He walks south. Alone. If he follows us, or signals anything that moves, I'll treat him as terrain hazard, not company." He said it without heat, which somehow made it worse and better at once.

Brakka shouldered his pack. At the edge of the ledge he turned, and I braced for apology, and he knew it, and I watched him decide against it—which I respected, oddly. Some apologies are just another transaction.

"The river crossing tomorrow," he said instead. "The old Merrow road. Your surveyor marked it—there's a stone with a V-cut, third pillar past the ford. Trust the stone, not the bank. The bank lies." He hesitated. "Vane was right about the timber specs too. On the Kestrel Span. He surveyed it, years back. Told me the oak was green. I didn't listen." A breath. "Tell his daughter I didn't listen. That's not an excuse. It's just true."

And he went, down the scree, smaller and smaller, until the mountain folded him away.

* * *

We climbed on. Of course we climbed on; the snows did not pause for our heartbreak. But something had changed in the shape of us, the way a rope changes after a shock load—still holding, but every strand now known, counted, distrusted a little.

That evening, camped in a hollow below the glacier's shoulder, Tam sidled up to me while I cleaned the lamp glass. He did his sidling the way teenagers do everything: loudly enough to announce itself.

"Isolde. Question."

"Mm."

"When you find out someone's a liar—" he polished his star chart with his sleeve, aggressively casual, "—do you stop believing everything they ever said? Even the true parts?"

I considered the question honestly, because Tam deserved honesty; he'd given us his whole careless, brave self.

"No," I said. "You ration it. You keep the true parts, mark them clearly, and check them twice before you build anything on them." I snapped the lamp glass back into its frame. "Trust isn't a wall, Tam. It's a ledger. Entries and corrections."

He nodded slowly, absorbing this, and then said, "So Brakka's river-stone advice—is that a debit or a credit?"

"Ask me tomorrow," I said, "when we're standing on it."

Above us the stars came out in their brutal thousands, and Tam named them one by one, quietly, like a boy taking attendance. Below us, somewhere in the dark valleys, I imagined riders moving, carrying a forged promise and a corrected headcount. And ahead, past the glacier's white shoulder, the pass waited with its single condition: *arrive before the snow.*

We had lost a man. We had gained a deadline. And in the morning, we would cross a river that remembered everything—including, if Brakka's last gift was true, the exact spot where my father had stood and driven his mark into stone.

I lay awake a long time, listening to the meltwater talking to itself in the dark, and I could not decide whether the sound was a warning or a welcome home.
