## 11. The River That Remembers

The river announced itself an hour before we saw it—a low grinding roar, like a millstone turning somewhere beneath the world. By the time we came down through the last stand of pines to the bank, the sound had worked itself into my teeth.

"That," said Tam, "sounds like a river that has opinions."

"Glacial meltwater," Brakka said, in the tone of a man greeting a colleague he didn't respect. "It's carrying rock. Look at the color—that grey-white. That's a mountain being eaten and delivered downstream."

We stood on the south bank and looked at what the maps called nothing at all. The chart I'd carried for six years showed this valley as blank green—an unremarkable fold of terrain between the lowlands and the high country, good for nothing, worth no one's attention. What it actually contained was a quarter mile of churning water, braided into three channels around gravel bars, running fast enough to make my knees feel the cold from twenty feet away.

"It was on the old road," Isolde said quietly. She had her father's journal out again, wrapped in oilcloth against the spray. "There's a bridge here. He drew it."

She turned the page toward us. Aldous Vane had been a careful draftsman—his lines were fine and certain—and there it was: a stone bridge, single arch, spanning the river at exactly the place where we now stood. He'd annotated it in his cramped hand. *Merrow road, third crossing. Bridge sound. Masons' marks on the upstream parapet.*

"There is no bridge," Brakka said flatly.

"No," I said. "There isn't."

But there had been, once. And once you know a thing like that, you can't unknow it. The absence of the bridge became its own kind of landmark—the way a missing tooth changes the whole shape of a smile.

* * *

We spent the better part of two hours finding a way across, and I will not pretend it was dignified.

The southern channel was the worst—deep, fast, and cold enough that a man submerged in it would have perhaps ninety seconds of useful consciousness. We roped up, four of us linked with Ondine in the middle because she was the lightest and the least likely to admit she needed help. Brakka went first, planting himself in the current like a fence post with grievances, and we crossed one at a time behind him, leaning into the rope, feet searching for stones that shifted under us like bad promises.

Tam went down to his hip on the second channel and came up laughing, which frightened me more than screaming would have. There's a kind of laughter people make when they don't yet understand what nearly happened to them. I'd heard it before, years ago, on a mountain whose name I still couldn't say without tasting copper.

"Hold," I told him, when he reached the bar. "Feel your toes?"

"Wiggling. All ten. It's a personal record."

"Keep them wiggling. If they stop, you tell me immediately." I said it lightly, but I watched him after that. The boy had been complaining of headaches since the treeline, and I'd been telling myself it was the sun off the snowfields. I was getting very good at telling myself things.

The last channel was knee-deep and almost friendly. On the far bank we wrung ourselves out and stood steaming in the thin sun, and Brakka surveyed his sodden boots with the expression of a man betrayed by machinery.

"I have crossed rivers on four continents," he announced, "and every single one of them was colder than advertised."

"You're an engineer," Isolde said. "Build a better river."

"I did. It's back there, being a glacier."

Ondine laughed—a dry, surprised sound, as if she'd caught herself doing something unbecoming. Sister Ondine's humor lived mostly below the waterline; you only saw the tip of it, and then rarely. She was looking upriver, though, past our little comedy, and her face had gone still in a way that stopped the rest of us talking.

"What is it?" Isolde asked.

"The banks," Ondine said. "Look at the banks."

I looked. At first I saw only what I expected: grey shingle, tumbled boulders, the usual litter of a glacial valley. Then the expectation fell away, and I saw what she saw. The boulders weren't random. They were courses. Cut stone, squared faces, laid in rows that followed the curve of the bank for fifty yards before the river had torn them apart. Here and there a pier still stood—a stub of wall rising from the gravel like a broken tooth, mortared so well that eleven years of floodwater hadn't shaken it loose.

And under the largest pier, half-buried, a keystone. An arch's keystone, furred with moss, sitting in the shingle like a coin dropped by a giant.

"The bridge didn't fall," Brakka said slowly. He'd waded back out and was running his thumb along a cut face, reading the stone the way other men read print. "Bridges fall all at once, or they rot gradually. This was taken apart. Pried loose, course by course, and thrown in the river. Someone paid to have it done properly."

"Why would anyone tear down a perfectly good bridge?" Tam asked.

"Because bridges connect places," Ondine said. "And someone wanted Merrow's Reach connected to nothing."

Nobody answered that. The river filled the silence for us, grinding its rocks, patient as a clerk filing papers.

* * *

We found the road an hour later, and I want to be honest about how it found *us*, because none of us were looking for it. We were looking for the trail marked on Vane's map—the shepherd track that climbed the eastern wall toward the Saddle. Instead, the ground simply changed under our feet. The scree gave way to something level. Something graded.

A road. Two carts wide, cambered for drainage, its surface packed hard by decades of wheels that official history insisted had never rolled. It ran along the contour of the valley, unhurried, confident, built by people who expected it to still be here in a hundred years. In places it was overgrown, alder and birch shouldering up through the paving. In others it lay bare and clean, as if the mountains themselves had declined to bury it.

"They erased the village from the maps," Isolde said. Her voice had a strange evenness to it, the way ice looks solid right before it goes. "They tore down the bridge. They let the forest have the road. But nobody thought about the fact that roads are made of stone, and stone doesn't care what the magistrate's office says."

"Stone keeps records," Ondine agreed. "It's the most stubborn archive there is."

Brakka had walked ahead, and now he stopped and crouched, and beckoned without turning around. When we caught up to him, he was brushing moss off a post driven into the roadbed's edge. Iron-tipped wood, black with age, but the iron cap bore a stamped mark: a triangle over a horizontal line, and beneath it, small numerals.

"Survey marker," he said. "Chainage forty-one. This is a benchmark chain. Whoever surveyed this road measured every rod of it." He looked up at me. "Your dead man's work?"

I took out the journal. Vane's sketches included marker posts—same stamp, same style. And beside one sketch, a note in that cramped hand: *Markers re-cut and re-set, 3rd month. Ordered by the Reach council. They wanted their road measured properly, whatever the county says. Good people. Terrible wine.*

Isolde read that last line twice. Something moved across her face—not quite a smile, not quite grief. "He liked them," she said. "My father liked them."

"He wrote about everyone he met like that," I said. "Half the journal is measurements. The other half is complaints about local hospitality, which is to say, praise of it."

We followed the markers north. Every hundred rods, another post, another triangle-and-line, standing at attention along a road that existed nowhere except in stone and memory and one dead surveyor's notebook. It was eerie in a way I couldn't immediately name, and then I could: it felt like walking through a house where the family had been removed but the furniture remained. Everything in its place. Everyone gone.

* * *

Near dusk we came to the shrine.

I say shrine, though it wasn't marked as anything. It was a widening in the road where the markers paused, and a low wall of fitted stone curved around a stand of rowan trees—old ones, thick-trunked, their berries hanging red as wounds. The wall was waist-high and ran maybe thirty feet, and it was covered in carving.

Not decoration. Names.

Row upon row of them, cut deep and clean, the letters sharp enough that the moss hadn't managed to colonize them. Family names, and beneath each, given names, sometimes a dozen to a family, sometimes just one or two. Some entries ended with dates. Most ended with nothing at all—just the name, and the empty space where an ending should have been.

Ondine walked the length of the wall without speaking. She trailed her fingers along the carved letters the way you'd touch a sleeping child's head—lightly, respectfully, as if waking them would be a trespass. At the far end she stopped, and knelt, and stayed kneeling for a long time.

We kept a respectful distance. Even Tam understood, and Tam understood approximately nothing about solemnity; the boy treated every silence as a challenge to be filled. But he stood quiet, hands in his pockets, watching her with an expression I hadn't seen on him before. Something older than his years.

Finally she rose and beckoned us close.

"This is a grave registry," she said. "Or near enough. Do you see? Not graves—there are no bodies here—but names, recorded. Carved in public. In stone. Anyone who passed this road could read them." She touched a name near the end of the wall. "Merrick family. Eleven names. The Merricks of Merrow's Reach. I've tended their memorial in the lowland churchyard for nine years—nine names there, because that's all the parish record preserved. Here are eleven."

"Two more than the record," Isolde said.

"Two children," Ondine said. "Died young, before the condemnation. The parish never bothered entering them—they'd never been taxed." Her voice was level, but her hand pressed flat against the stone. "Their family remembered them anyway. Carved them here, where the tax assessor would never look."

She moved along the wall, reading aloud now, softly, giving each name its breath. "Halloway. Ferren. Ashcombe—there's a large family, look, nineteen names, three generations. Duncarrie. Pell. Voss." She stopped. Read it again. "Voss."

"What?" Isolde asked.

Ondine turned and looked at me, and there was something in her face I couldn't parse—grief and recognition tangled together. "Wendell. How many riders does Hale keep?"

"Twenty-two on the rolls. Sixteen active, usually."

"And how many of those men come from the hill country? From villages like this one?"

I started to say I didn't know, and then I did know, or at least I knew enough. Hale recruited from the poor valleys—he always had. Men with no prospects and no leverage, who owed their uniforms and their bread to the magistrate's patronage. Men whose grandfathers might have walked this very road to market.

"Voss," Ondine said again. "There's a rider named Corwin Voss in Hale's company. I've seen him at the garrison chapel. Big fellow. Quiet. He puts flowers on the paupers' plot every spring, and nobody knows why, and nobody asks him." She tapped the carved name. "This is likely why."

The evening had gone gold and then grey while she read, and the cold was coming down off the icefield above us in long slow breaths. We made camp in the lee of the shrine wall, which struck me as fitting—the dead sheltering the living for one more night, out of habit.

* * *

I took first watch, which meant I sat with my back against the registry wall and listened to the river and tried not to think, which is the one activity at which I am a lifelong failure.

Isolde couldn't sleep either. She came and sat beside me after a while, wrapping herself in both blankets, and for a long time neither of us said anything. That was the thing about Isolde—I was learning it the way you learn a route, by walking it twice—she could be silent with a person. Most people fill silence the way Tam fills it, urgently and from all directions. She let it sit.

"My father walked this road," she said eventually. "Measured it. Set these markers." She nodded at the nearest post, pale in the dark. "Eleven years ago. And then he came back down the mountain and started asking questions, and within two years he was gone, and everything he'd written was scattered or burned, and the village was a rumor, and I grew up thinking my father was a man who abandoned us." She pulled the blanket tighter. "He didn't abandon us. He was murdered for arithmetic."

"We don't know that," I said. "Not for certain."

"Wendell."

"I know. I'm just—" I stopped. Honesty, I've found, is mostly the discipline of finishing sentences you want to abandon. "I'm telling you what I tell myself. It's a bad habit. It's how I survived the last eleven years, telling myself softer versions of true things."

"And how's that working?"

"Terribly. Thank you for asking."

She huffed—almost a laugh—and knocked her shoulder against mine, which from Isolde was roughly equivalent to a full embrace from anyone else. We sat with the dead names at our backs and the living river in our ears.

Then Tam's voice came out of the dark, thin and wrong: "Wendell?"

I was up before the second word. The boy was sitting upright in his bedroll, and even in the starlight I could see his color—or rather the lack of it. His lips had gone a bluish grey, and when I put my hand on his forehead it was clammy-cold, and his breathing was fast and shallow, and when I asked him questions he answered them correctly but a half-beat too late, like a clock losing time.

"How long have you felt like this?" I asked.

"Since the river. Maybe before. I figured it was just—everyone feels awful at altitude, right? You said—"

"I said tell me if your toes stop wiggling. You didn't tell me the rest." I kept my voice gentle. Inside, something cold was assembling itself with great efficiency. Headaches since the treeline. Now this. I knew the pattern. I had learned this pattern on the worst day of my life, and I had promised myself I would never watch it happen again.

"Brakka," I said. "Light the stove. Isolde, get my kit. Sister—"

"I'm awake," Ondine said, already moving. She'd slept in her boots for thirty years; I suspect she slept in her vows too.

As the camp stirred around the boy, I looked east, toward the Saddle of Ashes, invisible in the dark but fixed in my mind like a debt. Three days' climb, in good conditions, with a healthy party. The sky above the pass was clear and viciously starred, and the wind coming down from it carried a smell I knew too well and had hoped not to meet for another week.

Snow, somewhere up there. Waiting. And behind us, days back down the valley, I couldn't stop wondering whether Brakka's silence had a price tag on it after all—whether word of our route was already climbing toward us through the dark, wearing Hale's colors.

The past refused to stay buried. I was beginning to suspect the present intended to bury some of us instead.
