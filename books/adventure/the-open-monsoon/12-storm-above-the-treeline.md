## 12. Storm Above the Treeline

The mountain told us it was coming a full day before it arrived, and I was fool enough to listen only half-heartedly.

It started with the birds. Or rather, with the absence of them. For three days we'd climbed through pine country that rang with unseen choirs—chatter and complaint from every branch—and on the fourth morning the forest went quiet in a way that made the hair on my arms stand up. Tam noticed it first. He always noticed things first; that was his gift and his curse, a boy who read the sky the way other people read letters from home.

"Where'd everybody go?" he asked, squinting up into the green silence.

"South," I said. "Smart ones go south."

Brakka grunted from the rear of our line, where he'd taken to walking ever since his betrayal had been forgiven but not forgotten. "Barometer's falling too. My knee's been singing hymns since dawn."

"Your knee sings?"

"Loudly. It's a terrible voice. No pitch at all."

I should have turned us back then. Should have found the treeline bivouac Ondine had marked on her map—a shepherd's shelter, half-ruined, two hours behind us—and sat out whatever the sky was brewing. But the Saddle of Ashes was four days ahead, and the dry season clock in my chest ticked louder than any barometer, and so I did what I had sworn off doing years ago: I gambled weather against distance.

The sky called my bluff by noon.

* * *

We were an hour above the treeline when the wind arrived—not as a gust but as a wall, a single sustained shove that nearly took Isolde off her feet and sent Tam staggering into Brakka, who caught him by the collar with one enormous hand without breaking stride.

"Shelter," I shouted, and the word barely survived the trip between us.

There wasn't any. Not proper shelter. What there was, was a shelf of granite leaning against a larger mass of granite, forming a wedge of shadow maybe ten feet deep—the kind of place goats slept and men regretted. I'd noted it on the way up with the vague professional interest of a guide cataloguing bad options, never imagining I'd be grateful for it.

We got the tarp rigged across the mouth of the wedge with Brakka's pitons and Isolde's bootlaces, because Isolde Vane was the sort of woman who carried spare bootlaces the way other people carried grudges: everywhere, and ready for use. We got the stove lit in the lee of a rock. We got everyone inside.

And then the snow came down like the sky had been holding a grudge of its own.

Not dry-season snow. Not the polite, decorative flurry that dusts the high country in late summer and melts by morning. This was the real thing, early and angry, the first true snowfall of winter arriving five weeks ahead of its invitation, and it came sideways, filling the air so completely that within twenty minutes I couldn't see the tarp's edge from inside the shelter.

"Five weeks," Isolde said quietly. She was watching the white wall the way you watch a debt collector cross the room toward you. "You said five weeks."

"The mountain renegotiates." I fed another pinch of fuel to the stove. "It always had the stronger lawyers."

Sister Ondine, wrapped in every layer she owned, was humming something low and steady—an old burial hymn, I realized, though she was using it the way one uses a kettle, for warmth rather than ceremony. Tam lay against the back wall with his eyes closed, and I told myself he was sleeping, because telling myself anything else would have required me to do something about it, and right now there was nothing to do but wait.

That lasted until midnight, when I checked on him and found him not sleeping at all.

* * *

His lips were blue.

Not chilled-blue, not shivering-blue. That particular bruised color I knew from the Karst expedition, from a porter named Edda whom I had failed, whose lips had been exactly that shade when she sat down in the snow and said, very politely, that she'd just rest a moment. Altitude sickness doesn't announce itself with drama. It arrives like a bureaucrat, quietly, with paperwork, and by the time you understand what it wants it has already taken the keys.

"Tam." I shook his shoulder. His eyes opened, unfocused, swimming. "How many fingers?"

He looked at my hand with genuine effort. "Three," he said, and I was holding up two.

My stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"Headache?" He nodded. "Nausea?" Another nod, weaker. I pressed the back of my hand to his forehead—hot, dry, wrong—and then to his chest, and felt the fluttering race of a heart working far too hard for the air it was being given.

Isolde was awake instantly. She didn't ask questions; she saw my face and understood, which is the particular education of people who have watched someone die slowly. She got water heating. Ondine began murmuring prayers that were mostly practical instructions dressed in scripture—*sit him upright, keep him warm, small sips*—and Brakka simply moved to the mouth of the shelter and stood there, a wall of exiled engineer between the boy and the storm, as if he could personally hold the weather accountable.

"How bad?" Isolde asked me. Low voice. Just for me.

"Moderate now," I said. "Could turn severe."

"And if it turns severe?"

I didn't answer, and my not-answering answered.

Here is the arithmetic that kept me awake the rest of that night, staring at the tarp while the wind tried to peel it off the mountain. Below us, two hours down through storm-buried switchbacks, was the treeline and thicker air. Above us, three days' climb, was the Saddle of Ashes, and beyond it the descent into the valley where Merrow's Reach waited to be un-erased. If we went down, we lost days—we might lose the pass entirely, might watch the season close with the strongbox still fused shut and the confession still buried and Hale still victorious. If we stayed put until the storm broke and then pushed hard, we might make the Saddle before Tam's body finished its quiet mutiny.

If. The whole profession of mountaineering is built on that one letter, and I have buried people under it.

Eleven years ago, on the Karst glacier, I had faced this same fork. A client—impatient, wealthy, paying for summits rather than judgment—had ordered the party upward through deteriorating weather, and I had obeyed, because I was young and employed and believed that guides who argued with clients stopped being guides. Edda sat down in the snow. Two others followed. By the time I finally countermanded the order, the mountain had collected its toll, and afterward, when the inquiries came, my obedience was rewritten as negligence and my testimony as self-exculpation, and Magistrate Corven Hale stamped the verdict with the relish of a man settling an old family score. I learned then that the truth is a currency, and some men run mints.

So here was the wound, laid open again on a granite shelf above the treeline: I had ruined my life by obeying when I should have refused. And now every instinct I possessed screamed *go down, go down*, while everything we had crossed mountains for screamed *hold course*. And I no longer trusted myself to know the difference between caution and cowardice, or between resolve and pride wearing resolve's coat.

Tam, feverish, propped against the wall, caught me looking at him and managed a grin. "Don't make that face," he said. "I've seen you eat trail food. *That's* suffering."

Despite everything, I laughed. The boy could read stars and read me, both, and chose humor at the exact moment I needed it most.

"Sleep," I told him. "I'll take first watch."

"You always take first watch."

"Somebody has to set a good example for the rocks."

He drifted off mid-eye-roll, which was the best medicine available, and I sat with my back to the storm and did the thing I had spent eleven years avoiding.

I thought honestly about the Karst.

* * *

Dawn came gray and windless, which meant the storm's eye, not its end. Snow still fell, softer now, straight down, burying the world in a silence so complete I could hear my own pulse. The tarp sagged under six inches of fresh load. Brakka knocked it clear with a broom handle he'd whittled from a stave of firewood, muttering engineering complaints about tarps generally and mine specifically.

Tam was worse.

Not catastrophically—his color had improved with rest and Ondine's ministrations—but the headache hadn't broken, and when he tried to stand to relieve himself he swayed like a sapling and had to catch the wall. Three fingers again. Still three. The mountain was charging interest.

I gathered them all inside, all four faces turned to me, and I felt the old weight settle onto my shoulders—that specific weight of being the one whose decision becomes other people's consequences. It fit differently now than it had at thirty. Heavier in some ways. In others, almost comfortable, like a coat I'd outgrown and been given back.

"Here's where we are," I said. "Storm breaks tonight, maybe tomorrow morning. After that we have a choice. Down to the treeline, regroup, lose two days minimum—maybe more, if the season closes while we sit. Or up, fast, light, straight for the Saddle, and pray the boy's head holds."

"Pray," Ondine repeated gently. "From you, that's practically a theological position."

"I'm hedging."

"And if you go up," Isolde said, watching me with those ledger-paper eyes, "what happens if it gets worse? If he can't walk?"

I had the answer ready. I'd had it ready since midnight, turning it over like a stone, looking for the crack in it. "Then we split. Someone stays with him at the last camp—I won't move a sick boy higher—and the rest push for the Saddle, retrieve the key and the cache, and come back for him. Two days. Three at worst."

Silence. The kind of silence that has opinions.

"That's your plan," Brakka said. "Split the party. On a mountain. In early winter."

"It's the plan that loses nothing we can't afford to lose."

"No," Isolde said quietly. "It's the plan where you go on alone and send the rest of us home safe, because that's what you've been building toward since the ferry office. You carry the guilt, we carry the supplies. You think we haven't noticed?"

I opened my mouth to deny it and found, to my considerable irritation, that there was nothing there to say. She was right, and she knew she was right, and worse, she'd known it long enough to have organized her argument in advance.

"I blacklisted myself once already," I said finally. "By listening to someone else instead of the mountain. I'm not doing it again. Not with him." I nodded at Tam, asleep again against the wall, breathing too fast. "But I'm also not doing the other thing. Not abandoning the Reach to Hale's ledger because the weather turned inconvenient. Both things are true. So we split the difference, literally, and nobody carries more than their share."

Ondine smiled at me over her folded hands. "You know, Wendell, most men who break take years to say a true sentence aloud. You've compressed the process admirably."

"Grief is a great teacher. Terrible office hours, though."

That earned a laugh—even Brakka, even Isolde, a short sharp exhale that might have been a laugh or might have been surrender—and the tension in the shelter loosened by a degree. We ate. We melted snow. We repacked, dividing loads according to the plan: the strongbox knowledge and the journal with me; Ondine and Brakka assigned to Tam's care at the high camp; Isolde—

"Isolde comes with me," I said.

"She does," Isolde agreed, before anyone could object, including me. "You'll need someone to carry the confession down if you fall into a crevasse, and I'm the only one who's memorized the route. Also," she added, with the ghost of a smile, "you lie better with an audience."

* * *

The storm broke at dusk, the way storms do when they want to show off—clouds tearing open in the west to spill gold light across a world remade in white. The peaks burned. The snowfield below us glittered like spilled sugar. It was, objectively, the most beautiful thing I had seen in eleven years, and I barely looked at it, because I was watching Tam.

He'd rallied with the pressure drop. Color back, appetite back, enough sass restored to complain about the food twice, which from Tam constituted a clean bill of health. But his breathing was still quick, and when I checked his fingers count he got it right—"Two, obviously, I'm sick, not stupid"—but slow, thinking each answer through.

"High camp," I told him. "One day up. Then you rest with Ondine and Brakka while we run the Saddle. Deal?"

"Deal." He stuck out his hand, and we shook on it, solemn as magistrates, and I thought: *this is the part where the old story would have me shake hands and march him upward anyway.* The old story had cost three lives and my name. I was done with old stories.

We moved at first light.

The climbing was good—firm snow, cold air, the mountain in one of its brief forgiving moods—and we made the high camp, a hollow beneath a leaning fin of rock, by early afternoon. I hated leaving Tam. Every step away from him felt like a small theft. But Ondine took his hand in both of hers and said, "Go. We will keep him. I have kept harder things," and Brakka clapped my shoulder hard enough to relocate it, and Isolde was already roping up, patient, certain, waiting for me to stop performing reluctance and start moving.

So we moved.

Two of us on the rope, the Saddle visible ahead—a pale notch in the black spine of the range, impossibly far and impossibly near—and behind us, dwindling, three small figures in a hollow of snow. I looked back once. Tam raised his arm, whether in farewell or triumph I couldn't tell, and then the ridge curved and took them from view.

We climbed into the afternoon, and the light went strange.

I noticed it before I understood it: the shadows lengthening wrong, the western sky taking on a bruised violet tint, the temperature dropping in sudden steps like stairs. Isolde felt it too. She stopped, tested the wind with a licked finger, and looked at me with a question she didn't need to ask.

"That's not evening," I said. "Evening comes from the west. That's coming from the north."

Another front. Behind the first. Stacked, the way they get when a season changes its mind all at once—the dry season dying not with a whimper but with a brawl, winter arriving early and bringing relatives.

"We can't outrun it to the Saddle," Isolde said. It wasn't a question either.

"No. But we might reach Vane's cache." I pointed to a cluster of rocks a half-hour ahead, where the surveyor's cairn should be, where the final marker of a dead man's conscience waited under however many years of snow. "Shelter and answers in one hole. Or we dig in here and hope."

"And if the cache isn't there? If the snow's taken it?"

"Then we learn what kind of luck we've been spending."

She tied off the rope without another word, and we pushed for the rocks as the violet deepened to black, and somewhere behind us, faint under the rising wind, I thought I heard a sound that didn't belong to weather at all.

A whistle. Three notes, repeated.

Tam's signal. The one we'd agreed on for trouble.

I turned, and the storm swallowed the mountain whole.
