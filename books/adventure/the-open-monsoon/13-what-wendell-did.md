## 13. What Wendell Did

The storm did not howl so much as lean. All night it pressed its shoulder against the bivouac wall, a wall of stacked stones and canvas that Brakka had engineered with the smug competence of a man who had once built bridges and now built apologies. Snow came through every seam anyway, fine as sifted flour, settling on our blankets in a gray dusting that Tam kept brushing off with the diligence of someone who had decided, somewhere above the treeline, that small tasks were what kept a person tethered to the world.

He was worse tonight. That was the thing none of us said aloud, because saying it aloud would make it a fact, and facts at twelve thousand feet had a way of becoming verdicts. His headache had gone from complaint to silence, which was worse; a boy who complains is fighting, but a boy who goes quiet has started negotiating terms. His lips had a bluish cast even by lamplight, and when Sister Ondine checked his pulse she held his wrist a moment longer than she needed to, the way you hold something you're afraid might be borrowed.

"He needs to descend," she told Wendell quietly.

"I know."

"Soon."

"I know," Wendell said again, and there was nothing else in him, just those two words worn smooth as river stones from being turned over all day.

We were pinned. The Saddle of Ashes lay two days' climb above us, and behind us the scree slopes we'd crossed were, by now, either buried or booby-trapped with hidden rock under fresh snow — impassable going down, which meant the only direction was up, and up was closed until the storm spent itself. So we waited, five people and one mule named Deacon (whom Tam had named, and whom the mule tolerated the way royalty tolerates a particularly earnest subject) in a stone hut that some shepherd had abandoned a century ago and that the mountain had been slowly reclaiming ever since.

I sat near the lamp with my father's letters spread out on my knees, copying figures into the margin of Vane's journal by the light of a candle stub. It was work for my hands so my mind wouldn't do its own arithmetic: two days up, one day down if the weather broke, and Tam's clock ticking underneath everything like water under ice.

Wendell sat across the hut, sharpening an axe he didn't need sharpened. He'd been doing it for an hour. The blade had been sharp since noon.

* * *

It was Ondine who left us alone, though I don't think she planned it as anything so deliberate as leaving us alone. She simply gathered Tam's damp socks, announced that wet wool was a sin against God and common sense, and went to dry them by the stove with the air of a woman who had been arranging other people's confessions for thirty years and knew exactly when to be elsewhere. Brakka took the hint with less grace — he fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, in the middle of explaining why the hut's roof angle was structurally superior to anything the Romans managed, which may have been true and may have been the exhaustion talking.

That left the two of us, the lamp, the storm, and the axe.

"You're going to wear a groove in that edge," I said.

"It passes the time." He set the axe down anyway. Looked at it. Looked at me. And I saw him arrive at some decision the way you see a climber commit to a hold — no hesitation afterward, only weight.

"You asked me once," he said, "on the ferry dock, why I stopped guiding high routes. You asked if it was the blacklist or something before the blacklist."

"I remember."

"You've earned a straight answer. You've paid for it, actually, in advance — hauling a sick boy's gear over ground nobody should be crossing. So." He turned the axe flat across his knees, like a man laying down a weapon without quite letting go of it. "Here's the story I've told for eleven years, first. Then here's the one that's true."

The storm leaned harder against the wall. I pulled my blanket tighter and waited.

"The version everyone got," he said, "goes like this. Four years ago — no, six. Six years ago, autumn. I was leading a party of eight up the Colverne Traverse, a commercial route, guided, insured, the whole apparatus. Bad season, early winter pressing down. On the third day the weather turned and I made the call to push for the saddle instead of turning back, because the client who'd hired me — a man named Aldous—"

He stopped. Not dramatically. Just stopped, the way a rope goes slack.

"Aldous Vane," I said.

"Aldous Vane." He said the name carefully, like setting down something breakable. "No. That's not right either. That's the version where I get to be interesting. Let me start again."

He rubbed his face with both hands, and when they came away he looked older and younger at once, the way men do when they stop performing their own history.

"Six years ago. Eight clients. The weather turned on the third day. My judgment said turn back. But the expedition was run by a company, and the company had a lead guide above me — a man named Sorrel Fenn — and Fenn had a contract with penalties for aborted summits, and Fenn looked at the sky and said, and I quote him because I have quoted him to myself every night for six years, 'We can beat it. Keep them moving.'"

"And you disagreed."

"I *knew*. There's a difference, Isolde, and it took me four years to learn the difference, and people died while I was learning it. Disagreement is an opinion. Knowing is your gut telling you the mountain has changed its mind. Every hair on my arms was standing up. I'd seen that sky kill before. And I said—" his voice caught on it, not from grief but from disgust, "—I said, 'Yes, sir.' Because he was senior. Because the contract. Because if I pushed it and we turned around and the weather cleared, I'd look like the jumpy junior guide, and guides who spook clients don't get rehired, and I had rent, and I had pride, and I had a hundred small cowardices dressed up as professionalism."

"What happened?"

"Storm hit four hours later, higher than forecast, faster than forecast. We were on the exposed ridge. We got three tents up. Two survived. One didn't." He was looking at the stove, not at me. "Three clients died. A woman named Petra Hollis who was climbing because her doctor told her to live more. A banker named Ede who talked too much and whose last words were asking whether his wife would get the deposit back. And a nineteen-year-old, Milo, who'd saved up for two years for this trip, who spent his final hour apologizing to me for slowing the group down."

The axe trembled slightly on his knees. Or perhaps that was the lamp flame moving. In a stone hut in a storm, it's hard to say where the shaking comes from.

"Afterward, the inquiry wanted a story. Fenn gave them one: the junior guide, Eriksen, failed to voice safety concerns. And here's the part I can't forgive myself for, the part that isn't even about the deaths — I let him. I stood in that hearing room and I said nothing. Because contradicting Fenn meant admitting I'd known and obeyed anyway, which was worse than negligence. Negligence is an accident. What I did was a choice. So I let them call it an accident of judgment, and I lost my certification, and Corven Hale — who sat on the inquiry board, who owed Fenn's company favors — made sure the ban followed me across two borders. And I took it. I took the blacklist like it was penance, because it was easier than the truth, which was that I hadn't been punished at all. I'd been *spared*. The real sentence was the one I carried out on myself every single day since: never be responsible for anyone again."

"That's why you guide tourists on the coast," I said. "Tide pools. Day walks."

"That's why I have a rule against curiosity. Curiosity leads to involvement. Involvement leads to responsibility. Responsibility—" he finally looked at me, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady, "—responsibility is where I buried three people."

The hut creaked. Somewhere in the walls the wind found a gap and played it like a bad flute. Tam shifted in his sleep and murmured something about stars, and Ondine, drying socks by the stove, pretended very hard to be deaf.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "Now?"

"Because tomorrow I have to decide things again. Whether to move early through unstable snow with a sick boy, or wait and lose the pass. And I know exactly what my gut says, and I know exactly what the safe answer is, and I know that this time I'm the one who'll have to give the order, because there's no Fenn above me. There's just me." He picked the axe up, then put it down again, decisively, as though setting it aside for good. "And I needed to say it out loud to someone who'd know what it costs. You've been lying about who you are since the ferry office, Isolde. Half your name isn't even yours. Don't glare — I read handwriting for a living, and yours changes when you write your father's initials. I figured if I was going to ask you to trust my judgment on a mountain, you deserved to know what my judgment cost the last people who relied on it."

I didn't say anything for a while. The candle stub guttered. Outside, the storm went on leaning, patient as a creditor.

"My father," I said finally, "spent eleven years writing letters he never sent. I used to think that was weakness. Now I think maybe he was doing what you're doing — carrying something the honest way, just privately." I folded the journal closed. "You obeyed a bad order once. I've spent a year obeying a dead man's silence. Between us we might make one functioning adult."

Something moved across his face that on anyone else I'd have called a smile.

"There's one more thing," he said. "The reason I recognized Vane's name in that cave. After the inquiry, Fenn's company hired Vane — the surveyor — to map new routes, safer ones, routes that would let them sell the traverse year-round. Vane quit halfway through. Left a note saying the mountains he was asked to survey weren't the mountains he was finding. Everyone thought he'd gone mad. I thought he'd found something. And then I stopped thinking about it, because thinking about it was curiosity, and curiosity was forbidden." He laughed, short and without much humor. "Eleven years later the tide goes out and hands me his skeleton. The mountain keeps better books than any magistrate."

"Then tomorrow," I said, "we follow the mountain's ledger. And when you give the order — whatever order — I'll back it. Not because you're infallible. Because you're the only one among us who knows exactly what a wrong decision weighs, and that's the only kind of leader worth following."

He nodded once. Then, because the moment had apparently become unbearable to him, he said: "Your father's figures in the margin are wrong, by the way. Page forty. You carried a three instead of an eight."

"*Don't*—"

"Handwriting tells the truth even when the writer doesn't," he said, and for the first time all night, the warmth in it reached his eyes.

* * *

The storm broke near dawn, abruptly, the way mountains end arguments — not with a fade but with a door closing. We crawled out into a world remade: white to every horizon, blue overhead so hard it rang, the air thin and clean as a struck bell. The Saddle of Ashes stood visible to the north, still two days off, its flanks holding last night's snow in long pale ribs.

Tam was worse. He tried to stand and swayed, and Ondine caught his elbow, and he grinned at her with gray lips and said, "I'm fine, I just need breakfast," which was the bravest lie I'd heard in months, and I include my own.

Wendell stood looking north for a long time. Then south, toward the buried scree. Then at Tam. I watched him do it — watched him stand in the exact place where six years ago he had said *yes, sir*, and choose.

"We go up," he said. "Today. Slow pace, rope team, Tam in the middle with Ondine. If the weather holds we make the saddle tomorrow dusk. If it doesn't—" he exhaled, "—it doesn't, and we dig in, and I'd rather dig in high with a chance than low with none. That's my call. Anyone who disagrees, say it now."

Nobody disagreed. Even Deacon the mule seemed to nod, though that may have been him shaking off snow.

As we packed, Brakka appeared at my elbow, unusually quiet, unusually sober. He glanced back the way we'd come, at the white silence of the scree, then at me.

"Isolde," he said, low, so only I could hear. "There's something you need to know about the route behind us. About who else might be on this mountain." His jaw worked. "I did a thing. Back at the tollgate town. And I think it's already walking up behind us."
