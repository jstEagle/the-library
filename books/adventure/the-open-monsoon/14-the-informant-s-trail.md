## 14. The Informant's Trail

Tam had learned the stars from a man who'd sold him to a tannery, which was the sort of joke the sky liked to make. The man—Doss, his name had been, though Tam thought of him as *the man who smelled of lime*—had taught him constellations the way other people taught children prayers: as protection. *Learn where north sleeps,* Doss said, *and no one can ever tell you you're lost.* Then Doss sold him anyway, and Tam ran from the tannery at fourteen with nothing but the stars and a grudge he kept polished like a knife.

So when the stars started lying to him on the fourth night above the treeline, he took it personally.

He was on first watch, wrapped in the good blanket Isolde had bullied out of the tollgate town's innkeeper, sitting on a rock that still held a little of the day's warmth. Below him the camp breathed—Wendell's slow even rhythm, Brakka gone now, cut loose two days back, Sister Ondine murmuring in her sleep the way she did, as if she were taking attendance even unconscious. Isolde slept like a woman keeping one eye on a ledger.

The lie was small. That was what made it frightening. The northern stars sat exactly where they should sit, but there was a smudge near the saddle of the eastern ridge—a light that didn't twinkle right. Firelight does that. Firelight sits still and orange and patient.

Tam counted. He was good at counting; the tannery had taught him that too, hides stacked and tallied until numbers lived in his fingers. Three lights. Maybe four. Moving along the ridge line in the careful way people move when they don't want to be seen moving.

*Brakka,* he thought, and the name tasted like the lime smell. Big soft Brakka, who'd cried once about a broken bridge and carried Tam's pack up the switchbacks without being asked. Who'd looked at them all with those sad ox eyes and sold them anyway—not for coin, Wendell had said, but for a pardon. For a chance to stop running.

They'd spared him. Cut him loose with three days' rations and Wendell's warning like a stone tied round his neck: *If word reaches Hale through you, I'll find you, and I won't be merciful twice.*

Well. Word was reaching Hale. It was arriving on horseback, by the look of it.

Tam slid down the scree to the camp, deliberately loud on the last few steps so he wouldn't startle Wendell awake. You didn't startle Wendell Eriksen awake. Isolde had told him the story on the second night, quietly, the way you tell someone not to touch a stove: a party on a mountain, orders given and obeyed, four dead. Since then Tam had noticed how Wendell woke—all at once, completely, like a rope snapping taut.

"Wendell." He crouched by the guide's bedroll. "Riders. East ridge. Four, maybe five."

Wendell was already sitting up. In the starlight his face was just planes and shadow, but Tam had learned to read him anyway—the jaw set, the hands flat on his knees, doing sums.

"How far behind?"

"A day. Less if they know the route."

"They know the route." Wendell said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse. "Brakka walked it with us. Every camp, every water source." He exhaled through his nose. "Wake Isolde. Gently."

"Gently" turned out to be unnecessary. Isolde Vane woke the way her father's journal read—precisely, and with an edge. By the time Tam finished his report she was standing, hair braided back, already pulling the map from its oilcloth.

*

They broke camp in the dark, which Wendell hated and said so.

"Night travel on scree is how ankles break," he muttered, kicking dirt over their fire pit. "Broken ankles are how parties die."

"And riders catching us on open ground is how parties get arrested," Isolde said. "Or worse. Choose."

"I'm choosing. I'm allowed to complain while choosing. It's in my contract."

"You don't have a contract," Ondine said serenely, shouldering her pack. The sister moved like a much younger woman when there was work to be done; Tam suspected this was a theological matter.

"I'm drafting one. Clause one: complaining is load-bearing."

Despite everything, Tam grinned. This was the thing nobody warned you about fear—it made jokes funnier, the way cold made fire warmer. They climbed east in the blue-black hour, away from the route Brakka knew, onto a shelf of loose stone that Wendell called "the devil's ballast" and then apologized to Ondine for the language, and she said the devil could stand to do some hauling for once.

By full light they could see them.

Riders, five of them, strung along the valley floor they'd left in the night, tiny as toy soldiers. One raised something to his face—a glass—and even from here Tam felt the weight of being *looked at*. Being seen. His whole life he'd survived by being small and quick and unremarkable, a boy-shaped gap in the world's attention. A magistrate's glass across a valley made a mockery of all of it.

"He's marked us," Isolde said. Her voice was flat, but her knuckles were white on the map case. "He'll push through the night. We can't outrun horses on scree."

"No," Wendell agreed. "But we can outrun them *up*. Horses are for valleys. Scree is for people with good ankles and bad tempers." He looked at Tam. "You've got the best head for heights. I need you on point. There's a chute ahead—Vane marked it, a gully cutting straight up toward the shoulder. If we take it, we gain a day they can't buy back."

"And if we fall down it?" Tam asked.

"We fall down it together," Wendell said. "It's in the contract."

*

The chute was exactly as terrible as promised.

It was a throat of broken slate, steep enough that Tam climbed with his belly to the stone, hand over hand, dislodging little avalanches with every grip. Above him the sky went on forever, insultingly blue. Below him, when he dared glance, the others came up in a line—Isolde fierce and precise, Ondine steady as a bell tolling, Wendell last, watching the valley, watching them, watching everything, because that was what Wendell did instead of sleeping.

Halfway up, the rock spoke.

Not a sound exactly—a vibration, a shiver through the slate under Tam's palms, and then a clatter below and Isolde's voice, sharp: "*Rock!*"

A chunk of stone the size of a loaf bounced past Tam's ear close enough to feel the wind of it. He pressed himself flat. More followed, a rattling spill of them, and then a voice from below—not one of theirs, deep and carrying, with the bored authority of a man reading a warrant.

"*Wendell Eriksen! Magistrate Hale extends his regards!*"

Tam's stomach dropped somewhere into the chute's depths. They hadn't come around behind. They'd sent climbers—two of them, maybe three, working up the same gully from the valley side, and every stone they knocked loose came hunting the party above.

"Keep climbing!" Wendell bellowed, and his voice had a register Tam had never heard from him—not the dry guide-voice, not the confession-voice from the storm-bound tent. This was the voice of a man who had buried people and refused to bury more. "Don't look down, climb!"

Tam climbed. His arms burned. Slate bit his palms bloody. Another volley of stones came down and he heard Ondine cry out—a short sound, bitten off—and his heart went with it, but then her voice floated up: "I'm whole! Keep moving, child!" And higher, absurdly, Wendell calling, "Clause one, everyone! Complaining is load-bearing!" and Tam laughed with blood on his hands because what else could you do.

They gained the shoulder as the sun tipped past noon. Below, the climbers stopped pursuing—they'd reached the base of the chute and found it wanting—but the riders below were already swinging wide, taking the long trail around, patient as debt.

And that was when Tam saw the mule train.

Or rather, saw what had been the mule train. In the scramble, in the dark start, in the chaos of the chute, they'd done the arithmetic wrong. Packs had been lightened, cached, abandoned—one bundle of dried fish left at the last camp, the flour sack slashed open by rockfall, and worst of all, the rope coil and half the rations gone with a slipped harness somewhere on the devil's ballast. Wendell inventoried them on the shoulder with the grim efficiency of a surgeon counting instruments.

"Half," he said finally. "We've lost half our supplies. Five days of food becomes two and a half. Water we can find. Food—" He shook his head. "There's a shepherd hut marked on Vane's map, another day on. If it's stocked. If it exists. If Brakka didn't sell that too."

Nobody said Brakka's name after that. It hung in the air anyway, like weather coming.

*

They made a cold camp that night in a hollow of stone, sharing a single strip of dried fish each, chewing slowly as if slowness could stretch it. Tam sat next to Isolde, because Isolde didn't ask questions, and tonight he wanted very badly not to be asked any.

Instead she said, "You counted the lights. On watch. Nobody taught you to count fires."

"The tannery," Tam said, before he could stop himself. Then, since the word was out: "I worked hides from the time I was nine. Lime pits, scrapers. A man named Doss taught me stars and then sold me to the place. I ran at fourteen." He picked at the fish strip. "Everyone I ever trusted sold me or died. So I learned to count things. Fires. Coins. Days. Things that don't lie."

"Stars lie," Isolde said. "My father wrote me letters full of stars. *All my love, all my constancy*, and then eleven years of nothing." She looked at the sky. "But I keep reading them anyway."

Tam understood that completely. It was the most honest thing anyone had said to him since the mountains began, and it made his chest hurt in a way he decided not to examine.

Across the hollow, Wendell sat apart, as he always did, turning his compass over in his fingers. But Tam caught him looking their way—not at Isolde, at *him*—with an expression Tam couldn't file anywhere. Not pity. Something more like recognition. Two people who'd learned the world by being sold by it.

Ondine broke the silence softly. "In Merrow's Reach," she said, "when supplies ran short before a hard winter, families would set one extra place at table anyway. For whoever might come. They said hunger shared was hunger halved." She smiled at their miserable fish strips. "We are a very halved hunger tonight."

"Halved hunger," Wendell said, "and doubled company. Worst trade I've ever made."

"Liar," said Isolde, and even Wendell smiled.

*

The attack came at dawn, and it came for the packs.

Tam was dreaming of the tannery—lime and salt and Doss's voice saying *learn where north sleeps*—when Wendell's hand closed on his shoulder and hauled him upright. No words needed. Shouts below, down-slope, and the ring of hooves on stone.

"They're not chasing us," Wendell breathed. "They're going for the cache—we cached the reserve bundles at the notch yesterday. Hale knows we lost half. He means to take the rest."

For one mad moment Tam thought: *let them.* Let the riders have the flour and the fish and the rope, let the party starve gently on a mountainside rather than run bleeding over scree. Then he saw Isolde's face in the grey dawn light—saw what was in it, the ledger of her father's death balanced against these bundles—and understood that some things you did not get to let go of. Not the inheritance. Not the truth. Not the food that stood between five living people and the Saddle of Ashes.

They ran.

Scree fighting them at every step, stone sliding in sheets underfoot, Tam leading because Wendell put him there and because being trusted, it turned out, was a fuel stronger than food. They reached the notch as the first rider did—a lean man in magistrate grey, dismounting, reaching for the cached bundles—and what happened next happened fast and ugly and mostly without plan.

Wendell shouted, huge, arms spread, a landslide of a man coming down the slope, and the rider hesitated—that half-second of *is this one man or five?*—and Isolde darted past him and snatched a bundle. Tam grabbed another. A stone Wendell kicked loose took the second rider's horse in the foreleg and the animal screamed and wheeled and the morning dissolved into shouting and dust and the terrible drumming of hooves.

They got three bundles. The riders got two, and nearly got Tam—he felt fingers graze his collar, heard a voice yell "*Hold, boy, hold, you're worth more alive*"—and then Ondine was simply *there*, stepping between Tam and the rider with her arms open and her habit snapping in the wind, and the horse shied from her as if from a cliff edge, and Tam stumbled free with the bundle hugged to his chest like a child.

Then they were running again, uphill, away, lungs full of knives, and behind them the riders regrouped with their stolen supplies and did not pursue further—because they didn't need to. Everyone on that mountain knew the mathematics now. Five days of country, two and a half days of food, and Hale's riders fat and supplied at their backs.

They stopped in a fold of stone an hour later, gasping. Isolde counted the bundles twice, as if the number might improve with attention. It didn't.

"Two days," she said. "Maybe three, if we eat like monks."

"Monks eat better than this," Ondine said.

"That's the joke, Sister."

Tam sat with his back against the stone and his bloody palms open in his lap and thought about Doss, about Brakka, about the rider's voice saying *you're worth more alive*—worth what, to whom, he'd never asked, and the not-knowing sat in him like a swallowed stone. Everyone on this mountain was worth something to somebody else. That was the whole trouble with people.

Wendell crouched in front of him. Up close, the guide looked ten years older than he had a week ago, and steadier than he'd looked all journey.

"You stepped between a horse and a boy today," Wendell said. "That was either very brave or very stupid."

"Both run in my family."

"You don't know your family."

"No," Tam agreed. "But I'm hoping they're both."

Something passed across Wendell's face then—quick, quickly hidden—and he stood and turned to the others, and his voice went guide-flat and certain. "Shepherd hut's a day on. Vane swore it was stocked against emergencies. We make it by tomorrow night, we eat. We miss it—" He shrugged, and the shrug said the rest.

"And if it's empty?" Isolde asked. "If Brakka sold that too?"

Wendell looked south, back down the long grey slope toward the valley, toward the riders, toward the lowlands where a magistrate sat counting their days like coins. When he spoke, his voice was quiet enough that only the four of them heard it.

"Then somebody in this party is going to have to be worth more alive," he said, "and I intend to find out who, before the mountain decides for us."

No one laughed. High above them, far up the shoulder of the range, a thin white line had appeared along the peaks that had been bare brown yesterday—the snowline, come down early to meet them, patient as everything else on this mountain.

Tam counted the lights of it and wished, for the first time in his life, that the stars would lie to him just once more.
