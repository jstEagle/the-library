## 15. Ondine's Ground

The shrine had no name, which suited Sister Ondine perfectly. Things with names could be torn down; things without names simply persisted, like weather, like grief, like the habit of folding one's hands when frightened.

It stood where the Merrow road bent away from the river—a squat cairn of stacked stones older than any of the maps that pretended the road didn't exist, crowned with a leaning wooden post from which hung strips of cloth, each one faded past its original color. Pilgrims had once tied them here. She had counted forty-one on her way up the mountain, and she had tied a forty-second herself, because forty-two was the number of graves she kept in her registry, and a registry that was never wrong was the only kind of weapon she trusted.

"You're certain about this," Wendell said. He said it the way he said most things—flatly, as though emotion were a weight he'd learned to leave at base camp.

"I am certain about everything," Ondine replied, "except whether I packed enough tea. Everything else is settled."

They stood together in the grey light of late afternoon, the four of them and the boy, though Tam was less a boy today than a collection of complaints wrapped in a blanket. His altitude sickness had worsened through the morning, and every hour they spent hauling him uphill was an hour Hale's riders gained behind them. The arithmetic was simple and cruel: someone had to slow the riders, or none of them would see the Saddle of Ashes.

Ondine had done the arithmetic first. She always did. Fifty years of tending forgotten graves taught you that somebody always had to stay behind with the dead, and after enough practice, staying behind stopped feeling like sacrifice and started feeling like a profession.

"It won't work," Isolde said, for the third time. Her hands were red from wringing Tam's spare sock, which she had been wringing in place of anything useful. "You're one woman with a book of names against six armed riders."

"Seven," Wendell corrected grimly. "Brakka's tip mentioned seven."

"Seven, then. Seven armed riders."

"Yes," Ondine agreed pleasantly. "Isn't it wonderful?"

Isolde stared at her. Tam, propped against his pack, managed a wheeze that might have been a laugh. Even Wendell's eyebrows performed a small, reluctant dance.

"Sister," Isolde began, "I've watched you bury people. You weep at everything. You cried over a *marmot* two days ago."

"He had been someone's marmot," Ondine said with dignity. "There was a little collar. Grief is not weakness, dear, it is accuracy. I cry because I see clearly. And what I see clearly is that Magistrate Corven Hale is terrified of witnesses—not of knives, not of guns, but of *witnesses*. A dead woman proves nothing. A living one, standing in his path with a registry of forty-two names and a memory like a ledger, proves rather a great deal."

She patted the satchel at her hip. Inside it lay the grave-registry of Merrow's Reach, copied out twice, the second copy sewn into the lining of her habit where even a thorough search would find only scripture. She had spent eleven years walking the drowned valley's edges, copying names off stones before the moss took them, interviewing the scattered survivors who had drifted through her parish pretending to be from somewhere else. Everyone assumed a sister's wandering was devotion. Nobody thought to ask what she was devoted *to*.

"Hale can't shoot me," she went on. "Not here, not on an open road with his own men watching. Every rider he has is some mother's son, and half of them, if Vane's journal is right, are grandsons of Merrow's Reach. They don't know it yet. But their mothers' lullabies know it. You'd be astonished how much a man's bones remember even when his head has been bought." She smiled at Wendell. "Besides, you need me to do exactly what I'm best at. I'm going to hold a service."

"A service," Wendell repeated.

"A memorial. For the dead of Merrow's Reach. At the roadside. With candles, if I can manage candles, and singing, certainly singing—I have a voice like a hinge, which is ideal, because nobody interrupts a bad singer. When Hale's riders come up this road, they will find an old woman publicly mourning the village their magistrate erased. And they will have to decide, each of them, in front of the others, whether to ride past a funeral or dismount and take off their hats." She folded her hands. "Men are very brave in groups until someone starts singing."

"That's your plan," Isolde said slowly. "Hymns."

"Hymns, names, and inconvenience. I'll read all forty-two aloud. Twice, if the light holds. And I'll ask each rider his name, loudly, so the others hear, and I'll write it down where they can see me writing it. A man being written down by a sister behaves differently than a man who thinks no one is counting." She turned to Wendell. "How long will that buy you?"

Wendell looked up the trail, doing whatever quiet mathematics guides did with ridgelines and weather. "A night. Maybe two, if the men are sons of the Reach. Less if Hale himself comes."

"Then go quickly, and don't waste my night."

* * *

They argued for another hour, which Ondine permitted because arguing was how these people loved each other and she had learned not to interrupt other families' liturgies. In the end it was Tam who settled it, which surprised everyone including Tam.

"You shouldn't," he croaked from his blanket. "It's not fair. You're old."

"Thank you, dear. So are mountains, and look how well they've done."

"But—" He struggled upright, grey-faced. "The marmot. You cried over the marmot. What if they hurt you?"

Ondine knelt so they were eye to eye. Up close, the boy smelled of sweat and fear and the particular sourness of altitude, and beneath it, faintly, of lamp oil—his father had been a lamplighter once, before the towns stopped affording lamps, another small extinction nobody recorded.

"Tam," she said, "I have spent fifty years among the dead, and I will tell you the great secret of that profession: the dead are not frightening. The dead are patient. It's the living who frighten me—the ones who think a signature can erase a valley. Those men I take seriously." She touched his cheek. "But I have outlived three bishops, two epidemics, and one extremely persistent goat. I intend to outlive this too. Now drink your tea and let the adults be foolish."

He drank his tea. It was terrible tea. She had known it would be; she had packed the terrible tea deliberately, saving the good tin for whoever reached the pass alive. There was a theology in that, probably. She'd work it out later.

At dusk they left her. Wendell shook her hand like a man closing a business arrangement, which was his way of saying things his face refused to say. Isolde hugged her hard enough to rearrange ribs and whispered, "If you die, I'll never forgive you," which was Isolde's way of saying the same thing. Tam waved until the trail bent him out of sight.

Then Ondine was alone with the shrine, the wind, and forty-two names.

She got to work.

* * *

First, the candles. She had nine, hoarded from three parishes, and she set them along the cairn in a line, lighting them one at a time with hands that were steadier than she'd expected. The wind gusted; she cupped each flame like a bird. By full dark, nine small lights burned on the shoulder of a mountain that official maps insisted was uninhabited, and Ondine sat down on a flat stone, arranged her habit, and began to sing.

Her voice was, as promised, like a hinge. It carried anyway. Sound behaved strangely up here—it rolled down the switchbacks and pooled in the valleys below, and she knew from long experience that a hymn heard across a dark valley did something to a man that no sermon could. It reminded him of being small and safe, and it made him ashamed of whatever he was currently doing with his life.

She sang the old harvest hymn first, the one every Khoral child knew regardless of what they believed, then moved to the names.

"Maren Ostler," she read, from the registry. "Drowned in the flood of the Reach, aged sixty-one. Keeper of bees."

Wind answered. Somewhere below, a nightbird complained.

"Pietr Ostler, her son. Drowned, aged thirty-four. Mason. He built the church steps I still climb."

She worked through them in order, unhurried, giving each name its full weight and its small biography, because a name alone was easy to dismiss but a life was not. *Ilse Brandt, midwife, delivered half the valley including, she suspected, at least two of the men now riding toward her.* *The twins Anselm and Rudi Kessler, aged nine.* That one she read twice, and let the silence afterward do its own reading.

It was near midnight when she saw the torchlight on the lower switchbacks.

Seven points of light, moving in the slow, deliberate rhythm of men climbing who didn't want to climb. She estimated two hours before they reached the bend where the shrine stood. Enough time for a rehearsal and a rest. She banked the candles, ate half a biscuit, and practiced looking serene, which required no practice at all; serenity was simply what her face did when she was more afraid than she wished to admit.

When the riders came around the bend, their torches found an old woman in a grey habit sitting beside nine candles and a cairn of stones, a leather registry open on her knees.

They reined in. Torchlight swung across her. She saw young faces under the helmets—young, all of them, the youngest perhaps nineteen—and she saw the moment recognition flickered through them like fire through dry grass. They knew what a sister was. They knew what a memorial was. Their grandmothers had dragged them to enough of both.

"Sisters keep to the low valleys," said the lead rider. He had a sergeant's bar and a face that was trying very hard to be hard.

"This one keeps to the truth," Ondine said, "wherever it's posted." She lifted the registry slightly, letting the candlelight catch the ink. "You've arrived during a service. For the dead of Merrow's Reach. You're welcome to stay; there is no charge for mourning. Or you may ride past, and I'll note the time, and the manner of your passing, in the record." She produced, from her sleeve, a pencil. It was a short, chewed pencil, deeply unimpressive, and she held it the way other women held pistols.

The sergeant's eyes went to the registry. Then to the candles. Then, briefly and tellingly, to the cairn—to the forty-one prayer-strips stirring in the wind, each one a color that had once meant something.

"What village?" asked a younger rider, and Ondine's heart did the thing hearts do when the hook sets.

"Merrow's Reach," she said clearly. "Condemned eleven years ago. Flooded for the reservoir that was never built. Its deeds passed, by means I could describe in detail if anyone here enjoys paperwork, to the family of Magistrate Corven Hale. Forty-two souls displaced. Eleven died in the relocation winter. The rest scattered." She looked at the young one gently. "Your grandmother's name wouldn't happen to be Brandt? You have Ilse Brandt's chin."

The boy's hand went to his chin as if to check.

"Riders," the sergeant said sharply. "We have orders. There's a party ahead—"

"There is a party ahead," Ondine agreed, "carrying an inheritance home. And behind them is a magistrate carrying nothing but signatures. I wonder which of the two your grandchildren will be told about." She opened the registry to a fresh page, licked the pencil tip with theatrical thoroughness, and looked up. "Now. Names, please, gentlemen. For the record. I'll begin with yours, Sergeant, unless you'd prefer to be remembered anonymously, which"—she smiled warmly—"is of course also a choice some men make."

For a long moment, nobody moved. The wind turned the prayer-strips. Nine candles leaned and recovered, leaned and recovered, like small stubborn congregations.

And then the youngest rider—the one with Ilse Brandt's chin—dismounted, pulled off his helmet, and stood with bowed head before the cairn of stones.

The sergeant closed his eyes, the way men do when they realize they have already lost and are merely negotiating the terms. "Two hours," he said at last, not unkindly. "We'll rest the horses two hours. No longer, Sister."

"An hour and a half," Ondine countered, "and I'll include your names favorably in the record. History is written by whoever bothers to write it down, Sergeant. Consider which side of the page you'd like to be on."

He almost smiled. Almost. "An hour and a half."

She wrote down all seven names. Six of them gave them willingly. The seventh hesitated, glanced at the candles, and gave a false one—which was fine; she recognized a lie when she heard one, having heard better ones from bishops, and she wrote it down anyway, because a false name in a registry was itself a kind of confession, and she had all night to work out whose.

* * *

They rode on at the appointed hour, slower than they'd left, and Ondine sat alone again with her candles burning down to stubs and the wind picking up out of the north, carrying with it a smell she knew too well and had been praying, in her professional capacity, not to smell yet.

Snow.

Early snow. The dry season breaking ahead of schedule, the sky shedding its first soft white lies over the high country where four travelers and a sick boy were climbing toward a saddle that would not stay open much longer.

Ondine gathered her registry, snuffed the last candle, and looked up at the black bulk of the range, invisible now behind the falling white.

"Well," she told the shrine, tying her forty-second strip firmly to the post. "We did our part. The rest is between them and the mountain."

Then she shouldered her satchel and started walking—not down, as any sensible woman would, but up, following the riders' torch-glow through the thickening snow, because the registry had a final entry to make, and Sister Ondine had never once in fifty years left a record unfinished.
