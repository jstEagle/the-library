## 16. The Saddle of Ashes

The last hour to the pass was the kind of walking that made a man argue with himself.

Wendell had done the arithmetic three times and gotten three different answers, none of them good. Dusk was coming down the western sky like a lid closing. The wind had picked up at midday and hadn't let go since, funneling through the high country with a low whistle that sounded almost conversational, as if the mountains were muttering about them behind their backs. And Tam was still moving—slowly, stubbornly, one boot in front of the other—but the boy's color was wrong, a grayish pallor under the sunburn, and he'd stopped making jokes somewhere below the last cairn.

"Tell me again why we couldn't have camped," Tam said, breathing hard between words.

"Because there's no water up here worth the name, and because tomorrow morning this saddle might be wearing six inches of new snow." Wendell glanced back at him. "And because you complained about camping last night."

"I complained about the *wind* last night. There's a difference."

"There is not," said Isolde, from ahead of them both, "and I heard every word of it."

They came around the shoulder of the ridge together, the four of them strung out like beads on a fraying cord—Wendell leading, Isolde close behind, Tam third, and Brakka hauling the bulk of the supplies on his broad back like some patient, disgruntled mule who had volunteered for the job and regretted it hourly. And then the ridge dropped away, and there it was.

The Saddle of Ashes.

Wendell had imagined it a hundred times over five weeks of maps and arguments and sleepless nights: a notch in the spine of the range, a col between two gray shoulders of stone. The reality was both less and more than that. Less, because it was narrow—a saddle indeed, barely fifty paces across, wind-scoured down to bare rock and gravel the color of old fire. More, because of what stood on it.

Vane's markers.

Three of them, exactly where the journal's final pages promised: iron rods driven into the rock, each capped with a small brass plate gone green with eleven years of weather. They leaned at slightly different angles, like three old men who'd been standing in the same conversation so long they'd forgotten how to sit down. Beyond them, the ground fell away northward into shadow—the far side, the descent into the valley where Merrow's Reach had once stood and would, if Isolde had her way, stand again.

"We made it," Isolde said. She said it quietly, the way people say things they don't quite believe yet.

"We made it to the top," Wendell corrected. He didn't mean to be grim about it. It just came out that way. It usually did. "The top is the easy half."

"You are a *delight*," Brakka announced, dropping the supply pack onto the gravel with a thud that Wendell felt through his boots. "Five weeks. Five weeks I have listened to this man say things like 'the easy half' and 'statistically survivable.' In my professional opinion—and I built bridges, before anyone asks—we should all push him off the far side and be done with it."

"He'd probably land on something soft," Tam said, and then spoiled the joke by swaying.

Wendell caught him by the arm without looking, the way you catch a falling tool. The boy's skin was cold and clammy despite the windburn. Altitude, and not the mild kind. Every hour they lingered up here was an hour borrowed against Tam's recovery, and every hour of descent tomorrow would be paid back with interest if the snows came early.

*Get the cache. Get down. Get him low.* Three things. Wendell could hold three things in his head at once; it was practically his only talent.

"All right," he said. "Ten minutes. We rest, we eat, we find what Vane left us, and then we get below the treeline before the sky makes up its mind. Brakka, get the boy sitting against the eastern marker, out of the wind. Isolde—"

"I know," she said. "The cache. Third marker, two paces north, beneath the flat stone." She was already moving, and Wendell watched her go with the particular feeling he'd been carrying for weeks now and refusing to name, because naming it would require deciding what to do about it, and he had decided nothing about anything except which foot to put forward next.

She found it in under a minute. Of course she did. Isolde Vane had spent her whole life reading documents written by men who didn't want to be read, and her father's last instructions were no different—just another cipher, this one carved in geography instead of ink.

"Here." She knelt and lifted the flat slab aside. Beneath it, nested in a cavity lined with oilcloth gone stiff and cracked, lay a small tin box and a leather-wrapped bundle tied with waxed cord.

Nobody moved for a moment. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"Well," said Brakka, from his seat against the marker, "open it, then. Some of us are dying of curiosity, and one of us is dying of altitude, and neither of us enjoys waiting."

Isolde looked at Wendell. Not asking permission—she'd stopped asking him permission somewhere around the river—but something adjacent to it. Acknowledgment. This had been his find, back in the cave, back when he'd been a man with a rule against curiosity and a strongbox he couldn't open. Eleven years Aldous Vane's brass box had sat fused shut by salt, and eleven years its key had waited up here in the wind, and now the two halves of the thing were finally going to meet, and somehow it felt right that Wendell should be the one to bring them together.

He took the tin from her hands. Inside, on a bed of felt that had once been red and was now the color of dried blood, lay a key. Small, brass, unremarkable—the kind of key that opened a clerk's drawer or a schoolmaster's desk. And beside it, folded twice, a letter addressed in the same careful hand as the journal:

*To whoever climbs this far. You are either a fool or a friend. I have been both.*

Wendell huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Your father had a sense of humor."

"He had a *terrible* sense of humor," Isolde said, but her voice caught on the middle of it, and she turned away to unwrap the leather bundle while she got it back under control.

The bundle held papers. Dozens of them. Wendell spread them carefully on the oilcloth while the light failed by degrees around them, gold going to copper going to ash. There were copies—land deeds, tax assessments, shipping manifests, all bearing dates from twenty and thirty years past. There were letters, official correspondence between offices whose seals Wendell recognized from the magistrate's own stationery. And there was one document longer than the rest, written in Vane's hand across many pages, dense and unhurried, the writing of a man who knew he might not get a second chance to say it.

Isolde read the first page. Then she went very still, the way water goes still just before it freezes.

"Papa named them," she said. "All of them. Every name. Every payment." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "Wendell. He kept accounts. He kept *accounts*."

Wendell took the page she offered and read.

It was a confession, but it was more than that—it was a ledger dressed as a confession, or a confession dressed as a ledger, and knowing Aldous Vane, Wendell suspected the distinction had amused him. Entry after entry, dated and witnessed and cross-referenced: sums paid to the surveyor's office to reroute the official road away from Merrow's Reach. Sums paid to the cartographers to drop the village from the new maps. Sums paid to the parish registrar to misfile the death records when the fever came and the village, already erased, could not ask for help. And at the bottom of the third page, in a hand Wendell knew better than his own these days—had seen it on warrants, on notices nailed to ferry-office doors, on the blacklist that had followed him across two borders—

**C. Hale, Magistrate — received 400 crowns, "administrative consideration," spring of the Reach's condemnation.**

There was a signature beside it. Not a copy. The original ink, browned with age, pressed deep enough into the paper that Wendell could feel the groove of it under his thumb, the way you'd feel a scar.

Hale hadn't just profited. Hale had *signed*. Had sat down at some desk with a view of the harbor and signed his name to the erasure of four hundred souls, and then gone home to dinner.

"Wendell?" Isolde's voice, careful. "You've gone white."

"I'm fine." He wasn't, particularly. It wasn't horror, exactly. It was the opposite of horror—the strange, settling calm of a thing long dreaded finally being known. For five weeks he had carried the journal like a coal in his chest, half-hoping it would burn out, half-afraid of what it would set alight. Now here it was, in black and brown ink, undeniable, witnessed, *signed*, and there was no version of the world anymore in which he could hand it over, or bury it, or pretend he'd never climbed this far.

Triumph, he thought, was supposed to feel warmer than this.

"It's real," Brakka said quietly. He'd come over without any of them noticing—he could move quietly when he chose, which Wendell had always found suspicious in a large man. He looked down at the signature for a long moment. "I sold information to Hale's people for the chance at a pardon, and I told myself he was a hard man doing hard work in a hard country." He shook his head slowly. "He was a shopkeeper. Selling a village by the pound."

"Don't," Isolde said—not cruelly, but firmly, the way you stop someone from picking at a wound. "Whatever penance you're drafting for yourself, save it. We'll need your strength tomorrow more than your guilt tonight."

"She's right," Wendell said, and began gathering the papers with hands that wanted to shake and mostly didn't. "We move now. Light's nearly gone, and I want the first mile of descent behind us before full dark."

But Tam, wrapped in blankets against the eastern marker, had been watching them with the sharp, silent attention of a boy who'd learned young that adults' faces told truer stories than their words. "What is it?" he asked. "What does it say?"

"It says," Isolde told him, folding the confession into the oilcloth with a gentleness that undid Wendell a little, "that my father finished his work. That he carried the truth all the way up here when no one would carry it anywhere else. And that tomorrow we carry it down."

Tam nodded slowly, processing this, and then ruined the solemnity entirely by saying, "So it's heavy paper, basically."

"The heaviest," Wendell agreed, and got a ghost of a grin out of the boy, which he counted as a professional triumph.

* * *

They made two miles down the northern slope before the light died completely, and camped in the lee of a granite shelf with the valley invisible below them and the stars ferociously clear overhead. Tam slept—or drifted near sleep—with his head in Isolde's lap while she murmured to him, stories or prayers or inventory lists, Wendell couldn't tell and didn't ask. Brakka took first watch without being asked, which was its own small apology, ongoing.

Wendell sat apart with the confession in his hands, reading it again by the light of a shielded candle stub, committing it to memory the way he'd once memorized routes. If the papers were lost—if the weather took them, or worse—he wanted the truth living somewhere besides ink.

*Received 400 crowns.* He ran his thumb along the groove of the signature again. Somewhere below them in the dark, perhaps ten miles as the wind flew, Hale's riders were climbing. Brakka's betrayal had bought them a day's lead, maybe two, and the storm had cost most of that. Which meant that somewhere on the southern slopes, at this very hour, Corven Hale was likely warming himself at a fire, confident, patient, certain that the mountain would do his work for him.

*Let him climb,* Wendell thought. *Let him see the markers. Let him read the plates.*

Then he thought of the riders. Hale traveled with men, and those men had families, and some of those families, Ondine had told them, had come originally from the Reach itself—sons and grandsons of the erased, wearing the magistrate's colors without knowing what the magistrate had signed away. When the reading came—and it would come; Isolde would accept nothing less, and Wendell found he didn't want to give her anything less—those men would learn whose bread they'd been eating.

That was tomorrow's problem. Or the day after. The mountains had a way of scheduling things.

He was folding the papers away when Isolde settled beside him, quiet as snowfall, and looked out at the dark valley below.

"He knew," she said softly. "Doesn't it strike you? He mapped the pass, he cached the key, he wrote everything down and hid it where only someone determined would ever find it. He knew he wouldn't make it back down. He climbed up here to die with the truth safe above the snowline." She pulled her blanket tighter. "Eleven years, Wendell. He waited eleven years for someone to come."

Wendell thought about the skeleton in the cave, seated neatly against the wall, journal on its knees. Waiting with the patience of a man who had made his peace.

"He wasn't waiting for someone," Wendell said. "He was waiting for the tide. Everything else was luck."

"Luck." Isolde smiled faintly. "You guided us across a range that kills guides, past a magistrate who owns the law, through a storm that arrived a month early. If that's luck, I'd hate to see your bad days."

"You've seen my bad days. You were there for most of them."

Below them, far down in the black valley, a light flickered. Just once. Then again—steady, deliberate. A lantern, being shown and hidden and shown again. A signal.

Wendell went cold in a way the wind had nothing to do with. He counted the seconds between flashes, out of old habit, and the rhythm was wrong for any signal he knew—which meant it belonged to someone who didn't expect him to answer.

Someone who only wanted him to know they were there.

"Isolde," he said quietly. "Wake Brakka. Don't make a light."

Down in the dark, the lantern blinked again. Patient. Certain.

Closer than it should have been.
