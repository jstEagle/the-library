## 17. Snowline

The snow did not fall so much as arrive—suddenly, decisively, like a creditor who had finally found the right address.

Isolde felt it first as a change in the light. They had been descending the far side of the Saddle of Ashes for half a day, picking their way down slopes that Vane's journal described as "gentle scree, well-drained," a phrase she was beginning to suspect the man had written while drunk on surveyor's optimism. The sky had been the color of old pewter all morning. Then, between one step and the next, the pewter turned white.

"Snow," said Tam.

"No," said Wendell, who had a gift for stating the obvious in a voice that made it sound like strategy. "Snow."

"It's early," Tam said. "You said we'd beat it."

"I said we'd *try* to beat it. There's a difference, and that difference is currently landing on your nose."

Isolde watched the boy try to catch a flake on his tongue and fail twice before succeeding, and something in her chest went tight and warm at once. He was sixteen, starved for most of those years, reading stars like other boys read penny dreadfuls—and he was delighted by snow. She made herself memorize the look of him, because some part of her, the part that had spent eleven years learning to read her father's handwriting and therefore to read men, already knew what was coming.

By nightfall they were below the pass but not below the weather, camped in the lee of a granite shoulder with the strongbox lashed between Wendell's pack and hers like a third, extremely heavy member of the party. The snow came down in earnest now, fat flakes that hissed against the canvas.

"We descend tomorrow at first light," Wendell said, spreading the map on his knees inside the tent. His hands were steady. Isolde had learned that this meant nothing; Wendell Eriksen's hands were always steady, the way a locked door is always shut. "If we make the treeline by midday, we're out of the worst of it."

"And if we don't?"

He looked up at her. In the lamplight his face was all planes and honesty, which was its own kind of lie, because she knew now what he carried—the orders he'd obeyed instead of his own judgment, the party he'd led into a whiteout because a client insisted, the four who hadn't come down. He'd told her on the mountain, in the dark, in a voice like a man paying off a debt coin by coin. She had never told him how much it changed things for her. That a man could be ruined by obedience rather than malice. That the blacklist on his name was less a punishment than a misdiagnosis.

"Then we don't," he said simply. "But we will."

* * *

They didn't.

The storm followed them down the slope like a dog that had decided they belonged to it. By midmorning the wind had found its voice, a long low howl off the icefields above, and the snow stopped being flakes and became a horizontal fact. Visibility shrank to twenty feet, then ten, then to the length of a rope.

Tam began to stumble around noon.

At first Isolde thought it was simple exhaustion—he'd been carrying more than his share since the skirmish on the scree, since they'd lost half their supplies and everyone had quietly doubled their loads without discussion. But then he sat down in the snow, mid-stride, the way a lamp goes out, and when Wendell pulled him up the boy smiled at a point somewhere over Wendell's left shoulder and said, cheerfully, "I can see the whole valley from here."

There was no valley to see. There was white, and more white, and the sound of the wind.

"Altitude sickness," Wendell said, and Isolde heard it—the flatness in his voice, the door locking. She had heard that tone twice before: once at the bivouac, once the night he told her about the dead. It was the sound of Wendell Eriksen meeting an old enemy across a crowded room.

"He needs to go down," she said. "Fast."

"Fast is how people die on mountains." He crouched, got his shoulder under Tam's arm, hauled him upright. The boy swayed like a sapling. "We descend together, roped, slow, steady. We find shelter. We wait out the worst of it."

"Wendell." She kept her voice level. "How bad?"

He didn't answer immediately, which was answer enough. Instead he turned to Brakka's absence—Brakka, who was three days behind them and a traitor besides, cut loose on the scree with half a pardon and no supplies worth the name—and Isolde saw Wendell's eyes do something complicated and arithmetic.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. Rope up."

* * *

They descended through the afternoon in a world reduced to rope-lengths. Wendell led. Isolde came second, one hand on the line, the other steadying Tam, who walked now only because she and Wendell walked for him, propping him between them like a man being escorted from a tavern. He talked the whole time, which frightened her more than silence would have. He talked about the stars he couldn't see. He talked about his mother, whom he'd never mentioned before, who had sold hair ribbons in a town whose name he garbled twice. He asked Ondine—Ondine, who was a day and a shrine behind them, holding a pass with nothing but names and nerve—whether the dead got cold, and then answered his own question: no, that was the whole point of them.

"The treeline," Wendell said at last, and there was such naked relief in it that Isolde nearly wept. "There. Do you see it?"

She saw nothing. But then the wind gusted, parted the white like a curtain, and there they were: the first ragged pines, black and bent, clinging to the slope two hundred feet below like survivors themselves.

Two hundred feet. She would think about those two hundred feet for the rest of her life.

Tam collapsed fifty feet short of the trees.

It wasn't dramatic. He simply folded, the way laundry folds, and his weight took both of them down with him, and suddenly the three of them were a tangle in the snow with the wind screaming and the trees impossibly close and impossibly far. Wendell got up first. Isolde saw him try to lift the boy alone and fail, saw the failure land on him like a sentence, saw—in one terrible flash—the younger Wendell on some other mountain, obeying, ascending, four lives folding away from him in the white.

"We can't carry him," she shouted over the wind. "Not both of us, not through this."

"Then we take turns dragging him."

"That'll take until dark!"

"Then we take turns until dark!" And there it was, briefly, the crack in the door—not panic exactly, but its cousin, the thing panic grew from. The old wound, reopening in real time. *This is where I failed. This exact place. This exact arithmetic.*

And that was when Brakka came out of the whiteout.

* * *

Later, Isolde would try to describe it and fail. He simply materialized, the way large men do in storms—first a shape, then a shadow, then a bearded exiled engineer with frost in his eyebrows and a coil of rope over one shoulder, standing three feet away as though he'd been there all along, waiting politely to be noticed.

Wendell's hand went to his knife.

"Don't," Brakka said. His voice was rough as gravel underfoot. "I've thought about what you'd say. I've had three days and a great deal of walking to think about it. I've composed apologies in four languages. None of them are good enough, so I'll skip to the useful part." He knelt in the snow beside Tam, put two fingers against the boy's throat, and frowned. "His pulse is fast and shallow. He needs warmth and descent tonight, not tomorrow. You know this."

"You sold us," Wendell said. "You sold our route to Hale's informant."

"Yes."

"For a pardon."

"Yes." Brakka looked up, and in the flat grey light Isolde saw that his eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted and utterly without excuse-making in them. "I have been trying to earn my way back into my own country for nine years. Nine years of fixing other people's bridges and mills and drainage ditches, and every year the letter comes back: *not yet, not yet, not yet.* Hale offered me a yes. I have wanted a yes so badly for so long that I would have signed anything." He paused. "Including, apparently, my own soul. Which, I can report, weighs considerably less than I expected and is much harder to carry."

Tam stirred and mumbled something about ribbons.

"He's getting worse," Isolde said. "Whatever you're going to do, do it while I still believe you."

Brakka nodded as though she'd said something reasonable, which, she supposed, she had. He shrugged off his pack and began pulling out gear with the brisk competence of a man building something—she'd forgotten, watching him eat and complain and betray them, that he had been an engineer, that his hands knew how to solve problems. He produced a canvas groundsheet, a small spirit stove, a flask of the foul tea he brewed from pine needles, and a harness arrangement of straps and buckles that he assembled in the snow in under a minute.

"A litter," Wendell said slowly. "You built a litter."

"I built six of these for a mining concern in the Verrik valleys before they exiled me for asking where the profits went. It takes me under a minute now. Grief makes you efficient." He cinched the last buckle. "Here is my proposal. I haul him. Alone. Down to the treeline, into shelter, and I keep him alive through the night with fire and tea and whatever nonsense he talks about stars. You two follow with the box. If I'm lying—if this is some scheme of Hale's—you'll find me frozen at the bottom with the boy, having accomplished nothing, and you may spit on my grave, which I'm told is traditional."

"Why?" Isolde said. It came out harder than she intended. "Why would you come back? You had your pardon coming. All you had to do was stay away."

Brakka was quiet for a moment. The wind filled the silence with its long grey howl.

"Because on the second day of walking away," he said, "I realized that Hale's yes was just another *not yet*. A pardon from Corven Hale is a debt with my name on it, and I have spent nine years climbing out of debt." He looked down at Tam, at the boy's blue lips and foolish, trusting, snow-crusted face. "Also, I like the child. He showed me the southern constellations and explained that sailors navigate by them, and for one evening I remembered why I became an engineer in the first place. One does not sell the route of a boy who teaches you stars."

Nobody moved. The snow fell on all of them impartially, like ash, like forgiveness.

"Rope him in," Wendell said at last. His voice was hoarse. "And Brakka—if he dies—"

"If he dies, I will walk back up this mountain and let it keep me. Now stop talking and start believing. Believing is faster than verifying and we haven't the time for both."

* * *

They went down separately after that—Brakka ahead with Tam lashed into the litter, hauling the boy over his shoulders in a fireman's carry modified by nine years of exile into something Isolde suspected no textbook had ever described, and she and Wendell following with the strongbox slung between them, taking each other's weight on the rope when the slope steepened.

The treeline, when they reached it, was not salvation but it was near enough. Brakka had found a hollow beneath a leaning pine, walled it with cut boughs, and gotten a fire going—a small, furious, improbable fire, fed by deadwood he must have gathered at a dead run. Tam lay wrapped in every spare layer they owned, drinking pine tea, arguing weakly with Brakka about whether the constellation Brakka kept pointing at was actually a constellation or just "some stars that happen to be near each other, which is different."

"It's different," Tam insisted, "in the same way that a bridge is different from some rocks that happen to be near each other."

"Blasphemy," said Brakka, and handed him more tea.

Isolde stood at the edge of the firelight and let herself shake. She hadn't allowed it on the mountain—there had been no time, and shaking wastes heat—but now, with the boy arguing and the fire snapping and the storm roaring harmlessly overhead instead of through her ribs, she let the fear out of its cage and stood with it a while, the way you stand with a dog that has been barking all day.

Wendell came and stood beside her. For a while neither of them spoke. The strongbox sat between them in the snow, salt-fused and patient, carrying a village's stolen inheritance and a confession with a magistrate's signature on it, and somewhere below them in the dark was the valley, and somewhere behind them was Ondine alone at her shrine with nothing but the names of the dead, and somewhere above them the snow kept falling on the Saddle of Ashes as though trying to bury the whole story before it could be told.

"He came back," Isolde said finally. "After everything. He just—came back."

"Yes."

"I don't understand it."

Wendell considered this. Snow gathered on his shoulders and in his hair, and he made no move to brush it off, as though he were slowly becoming part of the mountain himself.

"People aren't their worst hour," he said. "That's the whole trick of it. Everyone—including Hale, including me—is standing in some hour or other, and you don't get to choose which one the world remembers. Brakka chose a new one. That's rarer than courage. Courage is cheap compared to coming back." He paused. "I should know. It took me eleven years."

Isolde looked at him—at this disgraced, stubborn, honest man who had broken his one rule against curiosity for a dead stranger's journal, and who was now, at this very moment, almost certainly calculating how many hours of sleep he could afford before pushing them down toward the valley—and she felt the thing she had been refusing to feel since the ferry office settle over her, quiet and enormous, like snowfall.

"You're smiling," Wendell said suspiciously.

"I'm cold."

"You're smiling *and* cold. Those are different conditions."

"Go check on Tam," she said, laughing despite herself, despite everything, the laugh steaming in the freezing air like proof of life.

He went. And Isolde stayed at the edge of the firelight a moment longer, looking north down the darkening valley, where the trees ran out and the lowlands began and Merrow's Reach waited with its erased name and its unmarked graves.

That was when she saw the lights.

Downslope, far off, threading through the black ranks of the pines: lanterns. Four of them. Five. Moving in formation, unhurried, disciplined—the unmistakable procession of riders who had dismounted and were climbing.

Hale had found another way down.

She turned to call Wendell's name, and the words froze in her throat, because the nearest lantern had stopped—and its bearer had raised one hand, palm out, in a gesture that was unmistakably, deliberately, a signal of peace.
