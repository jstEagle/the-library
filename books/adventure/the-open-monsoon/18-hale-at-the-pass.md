## 18. Hale at the Pass

The snow found them before Hale did.

It came down in fat, indifferent flakes, settling on the switchbacks below the Saddle of Ashes like a debt collector who knew exactly where to wait. Wendell counted his party twice out of habit—Isolde with the strongbox strapped across her back like a second spine, Tam slung between Brakka's shoulders and Wendell's own arm, the boy's breath coming in shallow, wet-sounding pulls that Wendell did not let himself name aloud. Altitude sickness was a word for fair weather. Up here it was just arithmetic: how much lung, how much time.

"Stop," Isolde said. Not a question. She had learned, over five weeks of mountain, when to stop asking.

They huddled under an overhang of gray stone while the snow thickened. Wendell chewed a strip of dried fish he didn't taste and watched the track below—the old Merrow road, Vane's survey cairns still standing at intervals like patient witnesses—and told himself the truth he'd been avoiding since dawn: they were not going to outrun anyone today. Tam couldn't walk. The snow was erasing their backtrail, which cut both ways. And somewhere behind them, riding through weather that would have turned any sensible man home, were six riders led by Magistrate Corven Hale, who had never once in Wendell's acquaintance been sensible about anything except getting what he wanted.

"He'll catch us here," Wendell said quietly.

Isolde looked up from tucking the blanket around her father's strongbox—her hands always finding it first, the way other people's found prayer beads. "You sound almost relieved."

"I am." He surprised himself saying it. "I've been running from this man for eleven years, Isolde. Running is exhausting. At least if he catches us, I get to stand still."

"That's not a strategy."

"No," Wendell agreed. "But it's honest, which around you has become something of a novelty."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then Tam coughed—a deep, rattling sound that made Brakka's big head snap around—and the almost died.

* * *

Hale came up the switchbacks at midday, or what passed for midday under the snow, and he came alone to the last twenty yards, as though even he understood that six armed riders arriving at a sick boy's bedside reads poorly. He dismounted with the careful dignity of a man whose joints had opinions, stamped snow from his boots, and looked at Wendell Eriksen across eleven years and forty feet of falling white.

"Eriksen." He said it the way he'd said it in the hearing room, all those years ago: like the name itself was evidence.

"Magistrate." Wendell stayed where he was, one hand on Tam's shoulder. "You're a long way from your courthouse."

"I'm a long way from many comfortable things lately." Hale's eyes went past him—to Isolde, to the box, to Brakka standing with his arms crossed like a wall that had learned to hold grudges. "You have something of mine."

"It was never yours," Isolde said. "That's rather the entire point of it."

"Miss Vane." Hale inclined his head with genuine courtesy, which was somehow worse than rudeness. "My condolences on your father. He was a talented man. Stubborn, but talented."

"He wrote down everything you paid him to bury," Isolde said. "I'd call that talented too."

Wendell felt the old heat rise in his chest and put a hand on it, the way he'd once put hands on frightened clients. *Not yet. Let him talk. Men like Hale always talk first; it's the talking that tells you the price.*

"You're dying by inches up here," Hale said, and now his voice changed—warmer, reasonable, the voice of a man offering a chair to someone standing in the rain. "The boy especially. That's mountain sickness turning into something worse. There's a surgeon in Kesselvale, two days' ride below. Good man. Fixed my knee after the Harrow campaign." He spread his gloved hands. "I can have Tam there tonight. I can have all of you fed, warm, and free before the week is out."

"And the price?" Wendell asked.

"The box. The journal. Every page of Aldous Vane's little confession." Hale smiled, and it was almost a kind smile, which was the most frightening thing Wendell had seen all day. "And a signature. Yours, Eriksen. A statement clarifying certain historical misunderstandings. Nothing onerous. You were young, you were following protocol, the disaster was regrettable but no one's fault. The board reopens the file, your blacklist dissolves, and you walk away a guide again instead of a ghost."

There it was. Eleven years of exile, offered back to him in one sentence, gift-wrapped in snow.

Wendell became aware that everyone was looking at him. Isolde's eyes were sharp and unreadable. Brakka's were not unreadable at all—they said *take the deal, you fool, some of us only ever dreamed of one*. And Tam, half-conscious against the stone, opened his eyes and looked at Wendell with the terrible trust of a teenager who has decided an adult is worth deciding on.

"You'd pardon us," Wendell said slowly. "All of us. Even Brakka."

"Even Brakka," Hale agreed. "Even the runaway. Even the nun, though frankly she frightens me more than the rest of you combined."

"And Merrow's Reach?"

Hale's smile didn't move, but something behind it did—a shutter closing, a ledger balancing. "Merrow's Reach is a matter of historical record. Records can be... contextualized."

"Contextualized," Wendell repeated. "You mean erased. Again."

"I mean *resolved*, Eriksen. There's a difference, and the difference is that resolved villages don't produce orphans with grievances. Everyone lives. You get your name back. The girl gets her father's effects returned—sentimentally, of course, after review. The boy gets his lungs. What does the world lose? A few pages of old paper." Hale stepped closer, boots crunching, voice dropping to the register men use for secrets they intend to keep. "I read your file, Eriksen. All of it. Do you know what I saw? A man who obeyed orders because the orders came from above him, and then spent a decade punishing himself for it. We are *the same animal*, you and I. I'm simply offering you the mercy no one offered either of us."

For a moment—one long, white, falling moment—Wendell let himself feel it. The weight lifting. His name spoken without flinching. A permit in his hand, a rope on his shoulder, clients who trusted him. He thought about how tired he was. He thought about Tam's wet breathing.

Then he thought about a dockhand named Pell, beaten bloody in an alley for the crime of warning a stranger. He thought about Sister Ondine, alone at a shrine behind them, spending her courage like coins so they could get this far. He thought about names carved in river stones by people who had been told, officially, that they had never existed.

"No," Wendell said.

Hale exhaled through his nose. "Eriksen—"

"You're right about one thing," Wendell said. "We are the same animal. But you got tame, Corven. Somebody fed you, and you forgot what the mountains are for." He nodded at Tam. "Here's my counteroffer. Your surgeon rides with us. Your men carry the boy down. In exchange, we all come to Kesselvale together, and the confession gets read—not filed, not contextualized—*read*, publicly, where the sons of Merrow's Reach can hear it. Half your riders, Magistrate. Have you asked yourself why they follow you through weather like this?"

Something flickered across Hale's face—fast, quickly buried, but Wendell had spent eleven years reading men's faces across dangerous ground, and he knew what it was. It was doubt. It was the look of a man who has just realized his own rope team might be looking at the slope differently than he is.

"You're bluffing," Hale said.

"I'm a guide," Wendell said. "Bluffing is for card tables. On a mountain, you tell people the truth about the route, or people die." He glanced at the sky, at the thickening snow, and gave Hale the professional courtesy of accurate information. "Also, that cornice above your riders is going to let go within the hour. I'd move them off the switchback if I were you. Consider it a free consultation."

Hale stared at him for a long moment. Snow gathered on the magistrate's shoulders like a slow accusation.

"You always were a stubborn bastard," Hale said finally, and there was, buried deep under the contempt, something that sounded almost like respect—or grief wearing its coat.

"I learned from the best," Wendell said. "You blacklisted me thoroughly."

Above them, high on the ridge, the wind changed note—a low, groaning shift, the mountain clearing its throat. Every experienced person in the party looked up at the same instant.

Hale's hand went to his sword hilt. "What was that?"

Wendell smiled for the first time in days, and it wasn't a warm smile at all.

"That," he said, "is the mountain reading the confession next."
