## 19. The Reading

The snow had stopped, which was the cruelest thing the mountain could have done. A storm would have given them cover, an excuse, a white curtain to slip behind. Instead the sky cleared to a hard blue enamel, and every figure on the slope below stood out sharp as ink on paper—Hale's riders, eight of them, fanned across the descent in a crescent that said *we were always going to catch you here*.

Isolde Vane stood with her back to the pass, the strongbox's contents pressed flat against her chest inside her coat: her father's confession, eleven years folded and refolded in her imagination, now real enough to crease.

"Wendell," she said quietly. "There are eight of them."

"I counted nine." He was squinting into the sun. "One's holding the horses back. Smart. Horses spook on scree."

"That wasn't reassurance, Wendell."

"No," he agreed. "It was stalling." He glanced at Tam, who sat propped against a boulder with his lips gray-blue and his breath coming in shallow, whistling pulls. Brakka crouched beside the boy, one enormous hand splayed on his back as if he could hold his lungs open by force of will. "And it wasn't for you."

Below, Magistrate Corven Hale urged his horse up the last stretch of scree and dismounted with the deliberate care of a man who wanted everyone to watch him do it. He'd lost his hat somewhere between the valley and the saddle; without it, wind-ruffled and frost-nipped, he looked almost human, almost tired. Isolde found this more frightening than the warrant she knew was folded in his breast pocket.

"Eriksen." Hale's voice carried beautifully in the thin air. Ten years of courtrooms had taught him how to throw it. "You're a difficult man to corner. I'll give you that."

"You gave me a ten-year head start," Wendell said. "I only used five of it being lazy."

Someone among the riders laughed—a short bark, quickly smothered. Isolde marked who. A young rider on the left flank, broad-shouldered, with a face like weathered oak. She filed him away.

Hale ignored it with professional grace. "Here is what happens now. You give me the document Vane left at the pass—the confession, the ledger pages, all of it—and I give you what no magistrate has offered you in twelve years. A clean slate. Full pardon, both countries, your name struck from every blacklist I can reach." He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; Isolde could see the courtroom habit in the way he held his hands loose at his sides. "And the boy gets down this mountain alive. There's a surgeon in Kellin Ford. There's nothing up here but prayer, and Sister Ondine isn't with you anymore, is she?"

The mention of Ondine landed like a stone dropped down a well. They hadn't heard from her in two days. Isolde chose to believe that silence meant success.

"And me?" Isolde stepped forward before Wendell could answer. If Hale thought Wendell Eriksen was the one making decisions on this mountain, he'd already misunderstood everything. "What do I get for handing over my father's last words?"

Hale turned to her, and something flickered behind his eyes—not recognition exactly, but the unpleasant arithmetic of a man matching a face to a file. "Miss Vane. Or should I say—" he smiled thinly, "—whatever name you're traveling under. You get to go home. Wherever home is, once this is finished. That's not nothing."

"It's less than nothing," Isolde said. "Home doesn't exist. You made sure of that. Your family bought the deeds, your clerks burned the maps, and the village my father surveyed—the village these men—" she swept her arm toward the riders, "—were born in, some of them, if I've read the registry right—was declared never to have existed at all."

"Careful," Hale said softly.

"Oh, I'm being extremely careful." Her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. Eleven years of rehearsing this conversation in bathhouses and ferry queues and sleepless hostel beds, and in every rehearsal her voice shook. "That's why I'm not giving you the document. Because careful people read things first."

* * *

It had been Wendell's idea, two nights ago, huddled in the bivouac while the storm screamed. He'd laid it out in the flat, practical tone he used for route planning, as if justice were just another problem of gradients and load weights.

"Hale won't shoot us in front of his own men. Not unless they'll let him. And they won't let him—half those riders are Merrow boys. Ondine said so. Grandsons of families on her registry. Men don't like hearing their grandmothers' names read out as evidence of a crime."

"So we read it to them," Isolde had said.

"So we read it to them." He'd shrugged, that infuriating shrug. "Worst case, they shoot us anyway and we're no deader than the snow would make us. Best case—" he'd paused, and for a moment the guilt that lived permanently behind his eyes had shifted aside, showing something underneath that might, generously, be called hope, "—best case, justice happens outside a courtroom for once. Courtrooms belong to men like Hale. Mountains don't."

Now the mountain belonged to all of them, and the moment had arrived wearing its worst possible outfit: clear skies, gray-lipped boy, nine armed men.

"He's dying," Brakka rumbled, nodding at Tam. "Whatever you're deciding, decide faster."

"We're not deciding anything," Isolde said. "We already decided." She drew the folded papers from her coat. The wind took the top sheet immediately, snapping it like a small flag, and she had to pin it against her chest with both hands. "Magistrate Hale. Before anyone moves, before anyone draws anything—I want it understood that copies exist. Three of them. One went north with a caravan out of Tollgate Town six weeks ago, addressed to the provincial assizes. One is buried. And one is in my hands, being read aloud right now, whether you arrest me or not."

It was a lie. There were no copies. There was a buried strongbox key and a great deal of faith. But Hale couldn't know that, and neither, importantly, could the riders—and Isolde had learned from watching Wendell lie to checkpoint sergeants that a lie told calmly, with specific details, in front of witnesses, becomes a fact by sheer social pressure. Six weeks ago. Caravan north. Provincial assizes. The riders would carry those details home whether or not any copy existed.

Hale's jaw tightened. "You're bluffing."

"I'm reading," Isolde said, and unfolded the first page.

* * *

Her father's handwriting had changed over the years she'd known him—from the crisp surveyor's script of her childhood letters to the cramped, hurried scrawl of his final months—but the voice was the same. Dry. Precise. Occasionally, unexpectedly, funny. Reading it aloud, she felt him standing at her shoulder, and for the first time since the cave, since the skeleton fused to its brass box, that presence was a comfort instead of a wound.

"*My name is Aldous Vane, surveyor, licensed under the crown, and this is a confession, though not of the crime you will expect.*"

She raised her voice to carry. Not shouting—she'd learned from a career of lecturing indifferent students that volume invites resistance, but rhythm compels attention. She read the way Ondine read names over graves: steady, unhurried, each sentence given its full weight.

"*In the autumn of my last commission, I was engaged to survey the Khoral high country for a road that official documents describe as proposed and abandoned. It was neither. The road exists. It has existed for sixty years. It served the village of Merrow's Reach, population four hundred and eleven at last census—a census I have seen, because I stole it.*"

She heard movement below. Not toward weapons—toward attention. Riders shifting in their saddles to hear better. The young oak-faced one had stopped pretending to watch the flanks.

"*Merrow's Reach paid taxes. It kept roads, rang its bell, buried its dead in a churchyard older than the magistrate's office. In the year of the condemnation, it was declared blighted—unfit, unsound, a hazard—on the testimony of an inspection that was never performed. I know because I was asked to perform it, and refused, and was paid anyway. The payment is listed in the ledger I have enclosed. My signature is on page three. I am not innocent in this. I am simply the only one dishonest enough to write it down.*"

"Enough." Hale's voice cracked across the slope like a whip. "This is sedition. This is a dead man's forgery, read aloud by a—"

"—by his daughter," Isolde said, not stopping, not raising her voice, which somehow made it worse for him. "*Page four records the acquisition of Merrow's Reach deeds by the firm of Hale & Associates, purchase price nominal, sellers coerced through denial of grazing rights and water access. Page five records the transfer of those deeds to land that appears, in the new surveys, as unclaimed crown pasture. Page six lists the officials compensated for the erasure of the village from the maps. There are fourteen names. I will read all fourteen.*"

"Don't," Hale said, and there it was—the first true crack, not in his voice but in his certainty. He took a step forward and his boot skidded on scree and for one absurd second the most powerful man on the Khoral coast windmilled his arms like a drunkard before catching himself. Nobody laughed. Everybody noticed.

"*Name the first,*" Isolde read, "*Assessor Dunmore, who certified the blight. Name the second, Clerk Rennick, who amended the maps. Name the third—*"

"Shoot the papers out of her hands," Hale snapped at his riders.

Nobody moved.

Isolde looked up. Nine men on a mountainside, and not one of them reaching for a weapon. The oak-faced rider had dismounted. So had two others. They weren't mutinying—they were listening, the way you listen in a churchyard, the way you listen when someone reads names over ground where names are owed.

"Corven," said the oak-faced rider, and Isolde's heart stumbled, because the familiarity of it, the *given name*, meant they'd grown up together, meant this man had eaten at Hale's table once, meant— "Let her finish."

"She's reading lies, Marta's-boy. Forged paper from a dead surveyor who got lost in a cave—"

"My grandmother was Merrow," the rider said simply. "Marta Ostrehan, née Merrow. She's on the sister's registry. I checked, after the shrine. I've been checking for two days." He looked at Isolde, and his eyes were wet and furious and utterly steady. "Read the rest."

* * *

She read the rest.

It took the better part of an hour. The sun crawled. Tam drifted in and out of a shallow sleep against Brakka's shoulder, and twice Brakka looked up at Isolde with naked pleading—*faster, girl*—and twice she mouthed *soon*. Wendell stood slightly apart, arms crossed, watching Hale with the expression of a guide watching weather: not hostile, just professionally attentive to which way it would break.

She read the bribes. She read the coerced sales, the burned granary that was officially attributed to lightning, the bell of the village church—which had not been melted down, as reported, but carted away and sold, buyer listed, price listed, Hale's initials in the margin. At that one, the oak-faced rider—Ostrehan—made a sound low in his throat, and two other riders dismounted too, until only three remained mounted, and those three looked less like loyalists than men waiting to see which way the rockfall would run.

She read her father's final entry:

"*If anyone finds this, I ask one thing, and it is not vengeance. Vengeance belongs to the magistrates and they will misuse it as they misused everything else. What I ask is the reading. Read the names aloud, all of them, the dead and the thieving alike, in the open air, where the wind can take them. A village erased from paper can be restored to breath. That is all restitution ever is. Breath, returned.*"

Silence, then. Wind moving through the saddle. Somewhere below, a horse stamped.

Isolde lowered the pages. Her hands were shaking now—late, useless, honest.

"Well," she said. "That's the document, Magistrate. You may have it, if you still want it. Though I'd point out that taking it by force, in front of nine witnesses, mostly confirms pages six through nineteen."

Hale stood very still. Isolde watched him do the arithmetic she'd been doing all hour: warrants, pardons, the weight of nine men's grandmothers pressing down on the scales. She watched him arrive at the answer she'd feared and hoped for in equal measure—the one where pride outweighed sense.

"You realize," Hale said quietly, "that none of this survives contact with a real court. I own the courts."

"Then it's fortunate," Wendell said, speaking for the first time in an hour, "that we're not in one."

Hale turned on him with the whole of his fury gathered at last, and Isolde saw his hand move toward the pistol at his hip—not a decision, she understood later, but a reflex, the twitch of a man whose entire architecture of power had just been read aloud to him in his own men's hearing—

And that was when the mountain spoke first.

A sound above them, deep and wrong, like the range clearing its throat. Wendell's head snapped up. Every guide instinct Isolde had watched him deploy for a thousand miles arrived on his face at once, and he shouted—not at Hale, not at the riders, but at everyone, at the whole slope:

"*Down! Move off the scree! NOW!*"

Above the Saddle of Ashes, where the storm had loaded the cornices for two days, the snow let go.
