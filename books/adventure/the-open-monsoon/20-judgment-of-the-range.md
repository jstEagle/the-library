## 20. Judgment of the Range

The mountain gave us perhaps a hundred heartbeats of warning, and I spent every one of them wishing I'd listened better.

It began as a sound I knew from my old life—a deep, structural groan, like a house settling in its bones, except this house was three thousand feet of storm-loaded snowpack above us. The wind had died in that unnatural way it does before something terrible happens, and the silence on the saddle was so complete I could hear Tam's breath whistling through his chapped lips, could hear Isolde's fingers creaking against the leather strap of the strongbox.

Hale heard it too. I watched him tilt his head, the way a man does when he hears ice crack under his boots. For one moment—just one—the Magistrate Corven Hale looked less like an empire's instrument and more like a man standing on a slope he didn't understand.

"Last chance, Eriksen," he said, but there was no conviction in it. His voice had gone thin at the edges.

"Hale." I kept my tone level, the way you talk to a frightened client on exposed ground. "Put your hand on the rock. Feel that."

He didn't, of course. Pride is heavier than any pack.

"The mountain's talking," I said. "I'd listen."

His riders shifted behind him. There were nine of them left—ten had come up out of the valley, but one had turned back at the shrine where Sister Ondine stood her ground, and I suspected Ondine's words were still working on them like water into stone. These nine sat their horses badly on the scree, breath steaming, faces raw from the cold. And here is what I noticed, what I have thought about every day since: not one of them looked at Hale. They looked at me. At Isolde. At the box.

They were sons of the Khoral coast, most of them. Sons of villages with names that had been quietly scraped off the survey rolls. I had read Vane's confession aloud to them not twenty minutes ago, my voice cracking on the names, and I had watched something move through their ranks like weather.

"You heard the document," Hale said, louder now, performing for his men. "Forged. Sedition. Every man who stands aside shares the charge."

"Sir." It was the young one, the rider with the frostbitten cheek—I'd learn later his name was Merrick Sol, and that his grandmother had been born a Halloway of Merrow's Reach. He said it quietly, almost apologetically. "My gran used to sing a song about the Reach. About the bell they drowned when the tax men came. I always thought she made it up."

"It *is* made up," Hale snapped.

"No, sir," Merrick said. "It's just been *unmade*. There's a difference."

And then the mountain decided it had heard enough argument.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because afterward there will be those who call it providence, and those who call it justice, and both words are too clean for what actually occurred. What occurred was physics. What occurred was eleven years of snowpack loaded onto a slope that Vane himself had flagged in his journal as unstable—*the Saddle sheds its crown after first snows; do not linger below the western wall*—and we had lingered, all of us, because men with warrants forget they are also men on mountains.

The crack came again, deeper, and then a third time, and the whole western wall above us seemed to shrug.

"DOWN THE EAST SIDE!" I roared, and this time even Hale's pride couldn't argue with me, because I was already moving, hauling Tam's arm over my shoulder—he weighed nothing, the poor lad, altitude had eaten him down to bird-bones—and shouting the only order that mattered. "DROP THE TORCHES! MOVE! DON'T LOOK UP!"

Isolde had the box strapped across her back and she ran like a woman carrying something holy, which is exactly what she was doing. Brakka—Brakka, who had betrayed us and bled for us in the same week, who had appeared out of the whiteout two days prior with Tam half-frozen across his shoulders—Brakka scooped the boy clean off his feet and ran with him like a sack of flour, swearing in three languages, none of them polite.

Behind us, Hale shouted something about orders, about duty, about *arrest them*, and I heard his horse scream—that awful sound horses make when they understand death before their riders do.

Then the mountain spoke over everything.

It was not loud so much as *total*. The avalanche did not crash; it erased. The western wall let go in slabs first, then in a river, and the sound came down the slope ahead of the snow itself, a pressure you felt in your chest and your teeth, and I threw myself behind a granite fin I'd clocked on the way up—one of Vane's old survey markers, as it happened, a rusted iron rod driven into stone forty years ago by a dead man who had, in his careful way, probably saved our lives twice over.

The world went white and silent and heavy.

* * *

Buried people are supposed to dig themselves out. That's the rule. You spit, you find the direction of the light, you claw upward. But I wasn't buried—not quite. The granite fin had split the flow around us like a prow, and when the roar finally guttered into that ringing post-avalanche quiet, I dug myself out of maybe four feet of snow into a world remade.

The slope below the wall was gone. Smoothed. A white shroud laid over two hundred yards of scree where nine riders had been sitting their horses badly.

For a long moment I couldn't breathe, and it wasn't the snow. It was the arithmetic. Nine men. Nine sons of the coast. I had read them their grandmothers' history and then led them into a killing field, and whatever else happened today, that arithmetic would live in me.

"They scattered," Brakka said beside me, spitting out snow. He'd set Tam down against the marker rod, wrapped in both their cloaks. "Look. Tracks. Some of 'em bolted east before it cut loose."

He was right. The slide had taken the center of their line—the horses, the pack animals, the men slowest to abandon their magistrate's command in favor of a guide's. But five sets of tracks fled eastward through the trees, and one figure was visible even now, climbing out of a drift near the treeline, dragging himself upright.

Corven Hale stood up out of the snow alone.

I have imagined killing that man. I'll admit it here, in the privacy of my own account, because honesty costs nothing on paper. In the worst years, after the blacklist, after the hearings where my testimony was bent into a noose for my own neck, I imagined it with great specificity. And now here he was, alive, hatless, one boot gone, his warrant case split open and its papers scattering on the wind like black birds, and I felt—nothing I expected. Not triumph. Something closer to pity, and beneath the pity, a kind of grim recognition.

There but for the grace of good judgment go I.

Because that was the difference between us, finally, laid bare on a mountainside. Eleven years ago I had obeyed orders instead of my own reading of the mountain, and people had died, and I had carried it. Hale was standing in the wreckage of the same sin at larger scale. He had ordered men to ride into country he did not understand, for a purpose he would not examine, and the mountain had filed its own verdict.

"ERIKSEN!" His voice came across the snow, ragged. He floundered toward us, fell, got up. "You did this—you *knew*—"

"I warned you," I called back. My voice sounded strange to me. Calm. "I told you to feel the rock. The mountain doesn't take sides, Hale. It just keeps accounts."

Isolde came to stand beside me, the strongbox still strapped to her back, snow in her dark hair, her father's eyes blazing out of her face. She said nothing. She didn't need to. Her presence was the accusation.

Hale stopped ten feet away, chest heaving, and looked at us—at me, at her, at the box—and I watched the last of the Magistrate drain out of him and leave only the man. It was the most human thing I ever saw him do.

"My men," he said. Hoarse. "Some of my men are under that."

"Yes," I said.

"They followed my order."

"Yes," I said again, gently, and that gentleness hurt him worse than any blow could have. I knew it would. I had learned gentleness as a weapon during my years of being nobody, and I am not proud of how well I wielded it.

He sank to his knees in the snow. Not praying. Just—stopping. A machine running out of fuel.

* * *

The survivors came back.

That's the part I want recorded properly, because the ballads—if there ever are ballads, and knowing the coast there will be, sung badly in dockside taverns—will tell it wrong. They'll say the riders abandoned Hale in righteous fury, struck the corrupt chains from their collars, marched away singing. What actually happened was colder and truer.

Merrick Sol came back first, wading through the drifts with his frostbitten cheek bleeding where ice had scoured it. He didn't look at Hale. He looked at the burial field of snow and took off his helmet, and held it against his chest, and stood there. Then he started saying names. Quiet ones. "Doss. Young Pell. Corporal Anse." Men he'd served with. Men whose grandmothers, it turned out, had sung songs about a drowned bell.

One by one the others emerged from the treeline and joined him. Five riders, standing in the snow, naming their dead. None of them went to their magistrate.

Sister Ondine would have known what to say. Ondine, who had stayed behind at the shrine two days ago armed with nothing but a registry of graves and a magistrate's fear of witnesses, and whose absence ached through me now like a missing tooth. She would have said the right words over that snow. All I could manage was to stand with them, hat off, until the wind filled the silence.

It was Merrick who broke it.

"Eriksen." He turned to me, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "The document. The confession. Read it again. All of it. Names and sums."

"Merrick—" Hale started, from his knees.

"Shut up, sir." No heat in it. Just finality, the way you close a ledger. "You're not giving orders anymore. Look around and count."

Hale looked. Five riders. One guide. One daughter of a dead surveyor. One engineer, one star-reading runaway, one unconscious boy. And a mountain that had just voted.

Merrick held out his hand—not for the box, I understood, but for the paper itself, Vane's confession, which I had carried inside my coat since the cache at the pass, close to the skin where the cold couldn't make it brittle. I hesitated. Isolde put her hand on my arm.

"Read it yourself if you don't trust us," she said to Merrick. "But read it *here*, on this ground, where the mountain can witness. Because once you ride down with it, there will be a hundred reasons to lose it, and you know the men who'd pay for it to be lost."

"She's right," Brakka rumbled. "Paper survives better in company."

So I read it again. Third time in two days, and my voice held better each time, though the names never got easier—Vane had written them all, every official who had touched Merrow's Reach's condemned deeds, every bribe entered in his neat surveyor's hand like coordinates, and at the bottom, dated eleven years and some months past, the signature that tied the whole rotten survey together:

*Received and approved. C. Hale, Magistrate.*

When I finished, the wind had picked up again, soft now, sifting snow off the broken slope. Merrick folded the copy I'd made for him at the pass—it was the original Isolde insisted on keeping, and God help anyone who tried to take it from her—and tucked it inside his coat, over his heart, the same place I'd kept mine.

"We ride down to Khoral Harbor," Merrick said. "To the assizes, not to the magistrate's office. This goes to judges who don't answer to him." He glanced at Hale, kneeling in the snow, and something moved across his face that wasn't hatred. Pity, maybe. Or memory. "You can walk down with us, sir. As a prisoner or as a man. Your choice. It's the last one anybody's offering you."

Hale knelt there a long moment. Snow gathered on his shoulders like the beginnings of a shroud, or maybe just a burden. When he finally spoke, his voice was the thinnest I'd ever heard it.

"Eriksen." He didn't look at me. "In the hearing, eleven years ago. If you had it to do again—would you still testify?"

I thought about it honestly, because he deserved one honest thing from me, and because I'd had eleven years to turn the question over.

"I'd testify louder," I said.

Something that might have been a laugh escaped him—short, bitter, surprised out of him like blood from a wound. He got up. Took Merrick's offered arm. And Corven Hale, Magistrate of the Khoral coast, destroyer of maps, walked down off the Saddle of Ashes between the men he'd commanded, a prisoner of his own signature.

* * *

We buried the fallen as best we could. There was no digging through avalanche debris, not with our hands, not with our supplies gone and Tam burning with fever against Brakka's back. So we did what the coast people do, what Ondine had taught us at the forgotten graves of Merrow's Reach: we marked the place. Brakka drove one of Vane's spare survey rods into the toe of the slide. Isolde carved into a flat stone with her knife—she had her father's patience for exact work—and I held the lantern while she wrote:

*Here lie Doss, Pell, and Anse, and their horses. They came as instruments and leave as sons of the coast. The mountain keeps what it must. We remember what we choose.*

Tam stirred against Brakka's shoulder while she carved, and mumbled something we chose to interpret as a complaint about the food, which Brakka took as a good sign, since complaining about food was apparently the boy's baseline state of health.

"He'll live," Brakka pronounced, with the unearned confidence of a man who'd nearly gotten us all killed a week ago and had been paying it back in frozen labor ever since. "Fever's lower. He asked for stew. Twice."

"That's not a medical sign," Isolde said.

"It's the best medical sign there is," Brakka said. "Dead men don't want stew."

We laughed. All of us, there on the slaughter-slope, laughing like fools, and I think it was necessary. Grief needs a valve or it becomes a weight you carry forever, and I had carried enough weights.

The light was going gold and long across the saddle when Isolde came to stand beside me at the edge of the slide. Below us, the eastern descent opened into the valley—the true valley, the one the maps had lied about, where terraces slept under new snow and a river ran silver through land that had belonged to families whose names were coming home.

"They'll want to give you everything, you know," she said. "Down there. When the truth lands. Land, money, a pardon with ribbons on it."

"I know."

"And?"

I looked north, where the range climbed away into country no map had ever bothered to ruin. Somewhere past those peaks was a stretch of coast I'd never seen, and beyond it, other ranges, other tides. Miles. Just miles.

"And I'll decide at sea level," I said. "One thing at a time. First we get the boy down the mountain. Then we get the truth to the assizes. Then—" I shrugged, and felt lighter than I had in eleven years, "—then I'll see how far my legs feel like walking."

Isolde smiled, and for a moment she looked so much like her father that I had to look away, down at the valley, where lights were beginning to show in windows that hadn't held light in a generation. Word traveled fast in the mountains. Someone down there had seen the riders come up, and someone had seen them come down without their magistrate, and by morning the story would be in every kitchen on the far side of the range.

By morning, Merrow's Reach would begin to exist again.

"Come on, then," I said, shouldering my pack. "Snow's coming, and I promised the boy stew."

We started down, all six of us, into the valley that remembered our names.
