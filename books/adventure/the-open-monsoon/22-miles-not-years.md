## 22. Miles, Not Years

The valley of Merrow's Reach smelled of cut timber and woodsmoke in the mornings now, and Wendell Eriksen had learned to wake to it without flinching.

That was the strange part. For eleven years he had woken to the smell of nothing in particular—cheap lamp oil, wet wool, the brine of whatever coast town would have him—and called that peace. Now there was a village rising around him like a stubborn argument with the maps, and every morning it smelled of something being built instead of something being hidden.

He was hammering a roof beam into place on the third of the new houses when Isolde found him. She came up the ladder with the particular expression she wore when she was about to ruin his day politely.

"You've got a visitor," she said.

"I don't get visitors."

"You do now. He's standing at the edge of the commons looking like a man who stepped in something and is trying to decide whether to blame the cow."

Wendell set down the hammer. "Hale."

"Hale."

"Alone?"

"Two riders at the treeline. He came the rest of the way on foot." She paused. "His men wouldn't come closer. I counted."

Of course they wouldn't. Half of them had grown up hearing their grandmothers' names read aloud from a stone registry by a woman in grey. The other half had watched an avalanche bury the road behind them and drawn their own conclusions about whose side the mountains were on.

Wendell wiped his hands on his trousers, which accomplished nothing, and climbed down.

* * *

Corven Hale had aged a season in a month. The magistrate's coat was the same, dark and immaculate, but it hung on him differently now, the way a uniform hangs on a man who has stopped believing in the office and started merely wearing it. He stood at the edge of the commons where the old market square used to be, hat in hand, which was its own kind of confession. Magistrates did not remove their hats for villages. They removed them for funerals.

"Eriksen."

"Magistrate."

They regarded each other across ten feet of mud and new-laid foundation stone. Somewhere behind them, Tam was arguing cheerfully with Brakka about the correct angle for a water chute, and Brakka—who had once sold their route to save his own neck and then hauled a dying boy through a whiteout to unsell it—was losing the argument on purpose, because it made the boy laugh.

"You built all this," Hale said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The village built itself. I hit things with a hammer and try not to fall off."

"You were blacklisted from two countries for negligence."

"And yet here I am, hitting things with a hammer. The standards are lower when nobody's watching."

Something moved at the corner of Hale's mouth. It might once have been a smile; it had been buried so long it came up pale, like a root. "I'm not here about the houses."

"No," Wendell said. "I didn't think you were."

Hale looked out at the valley. The morning fog was burning off the terraces, and below them the river ran bright over stones that had been part of a road once, before the maps forgot it. "I've read the confession," he said quietly. "All of it. Vane was thorough. My father's signature. My grandfather's. Mine—I signed the condemnation order, Eriksen. I was twenty-six years old and I told myself it was procedure." He turned the hat in his hands. "I've spent thirty years telling myself things were procedure."

"There's a lot of that going around," Wendell said. "I told myself my testimony was just testimony. That what happened on the Kestrel Col wasn't my fault because I'd done what the client asked. Procedure." He shrugged. "It took me eleven years and a dead surveyor's journal to stop lying about it. You got the same deal in one avalanche. You're welcome."

"That's why I'm here." Hale straightened, and for a moment the old authority came back into his spine like a man remembering a dance step. "There will be an inquiry. There has to be—it can't look like a mob of mountain men forced a magistrate's resignation at snowpoint. It has to be *orderly*. And an orderly inquiry requires witnesses who are credible." His eyes came up. "You are not credible, Eriksen. You're a twice-blacklisted guide with a disaster in your history. Everything you say will be weighed against everything you've been accused of. Unless—" he drew a folded document from inside his coat, "—unless you aren't anymore."

Wendell looked at the paper without taking it. "A pardon."

"A full pardon. Both countries. Your record sealed, your name cleared, the Kestrel Col findings officially revised to reflect your original testimony." Hale held it out. "In exchange, you sign an addendum to Vane's confession. A clarification. Stating that while irregularities occurred in the acquisition of Merrow's Reach lands, they were consistent with the legal practices of the era, and that no individual officeholder acted with criminal intent. My family's names remain in the historical record. They simply stop being *accusations*."

The wind came down the valley and stirred the loose end of the paper. Wendell thought about how long Hale must have practiced saying *irregularities* so smoothly.

"And the village?" Wendell said.

"The land claims proceed regardless. Ondine's registry is unimpeachable—that woman could make a stone testify. The restitution stands." Hale's voice dropped. "This isn't about Merrow's Reach anymore. It's about me. Whether I go down as a criminal or as a man who made an error of judgment in a different century."

"Those sound suspiciously similar from where I'm standing."

"They don't from mine."

"No," Wendell agreed. "I imagine they don't."

* * *

Isolde found them there twenty minutes later, because Isolde found everyone everywhere eventually; Wendell suspected she'd been born with a talent for triangulation. She arrived carrying two cups of tea, handed one to each man as though they were old friends at a fair, and stood between them radiating the patient menace of a person who had crossed a mountain range to deliver her father's bones to the truth and was not above delivering a magistrate's too.

"He hasn't signed," Hale said to her.

"He won't," Isolde said, sipping her tea. "But do go on. This is better than the theater in Corvassa, and that had a bear in it."

"It's a fair offer," Hale said, with the first note of genuine feeling Wendell had heard from him. "God help me, it's more than fair. He gets his life back. Eleven years of exile, undone by a signature. Any reasonable man—"

"That's the flaw in the design," Wendell said. "You keep hiring reasonable men. That's how we got here."

He took the paper after all—but only to read it properly, turning it in the light, letting Hale watch him read every clause. Then he folded it along its original creases, which took some care, and handed it back.

"My turn to make an offer," Wendell said. "I'll testify at your inquiry. Fully, freely, under oath, in whatever room you put me in. I'll say exactly what happened on the Kestrel Col, including the parts that make me look like a fool who obeyed orders instead of trusting his own eyes. I'll stand next to Vane's confession and confirm every page of it, including your signature. And when it's done, I'll walk out of that room still blacklisted, still disgraced, still the man whose name comes up when people want an example of what happens to guides who fail." He paused. "And you'll sign the resignation before the inquiry opens, not after. Publicly. On the steps."

Hale stared at him. "That's not an offer. That's a sentence."

"It's the truth with the price tag left on. You want to be a man who made an error of judgment? Fine. Errors of judgment cost something. That's what makes them errors instead of bargains." Wendell nodded toward the terraces, where Sister Ondine was walking the rows of new grave-markers with her registry under her arm, touching each stone like a schoolmistress checking homework. "She forgave you before you ever came down here, you know. She forgives everybody. It's infuriating. But forgiveness isn't the same thing as forgetting, and neither is a pardon."

For a long moment Hale said nothing. The wind moved through the young pines they'd planted along the commons, thin whips that bent easily and sprang back.

"You could have had everything back," Hale said finally.

"I know." Wendell smiled, and found, to his mild surprise, that it didn't hurt. "I already did."

* * *

Hale left before noon. He walked out past the treeline to his waiting riders, and Wendell—watching from the roof he'd abandoned—saw him pause once at the old road stones by the river, the ones with the names carved in them, and stand there for a long time with his hat in his hands again.

Three weeks later, word came up the valley that the inquiry had opened in the provincial capital, that Hale had resigned on the steps as promised, and that a guide named Eriksen had testified for six hours without once raising his voice. Word also came that both blacklists had been formally reviewed and—this was the delicious part, delivered by a grinning courier who clearly enjoyed the irony—*upheld*. The findings of negligence were confirmed. The bans remained. Somewhere in two countries' archives, Wendell Eriksen remained exactly what he had been: a cautionary tale.

Tam thought it was the funniest thing he'd ever heard. "You told the whole truth and they punished you anyway?"

"They weren't punishing the truth," Wendell said. "They were protecting the paperwork. Paperwork hates surprises."

"So what do you get out of it?"

Wendell considered. Below them, the last house of the season was getting its roof, and Brakka was up there directing operations with the air of a general, and Ondine had fallen asleep in a chair with her registry open on her chest, and Isolde was at the table drafting the charter that would put Merrow's Reach back on the maps—not erased, not restored, just *written*, plain and permanent.

"Nothing," Wendell said. "I got nothing out of it. Best trade I ever made."

* * *

He left in the spring, when the passes opened.

Not because anyone asked him to go—he'd been offered a house, a plot, a place at the table where the village planned its future, and Isolde had made it clear the offer had no expiration date. He left because he had woken three mornings in a row looking north, the way a compass wakes pointing, and he had learned eleven years ago what happened to men who ignored that particular instinct. It didn't mean he belonged anywhere else. It meant he hadn't finished moving yet.

Ondine gave him a blessing and a small cloth bundle of travel bread and told him, with terrifying serenity, that he would be back within five years. "You'll see," she said. "Roots grow slower than roads, but they grow deeper. Go on, then. Walk your miles. We'll be here."

Brakka shook his hand and said gruffly, "If you ever need an engineer who's banned from four countries, you know where I'm not."

Tam walked him as far as the old road stones and then stopped, hands in pockets, fourteen years old and trying very hard to be casual about it. "You could write," the boy said. "To us. If you wanted."

"I don't much like writing."

"You could draw maps, then. You're good at those."

Wendell looked at him. "I might do that."

Isolde came last, of course. She walked him to the head of the valley where the trail climbed toward the pass—the Saddle of Ashes, snow-free now, almost gentle in the morning light—and handed him a leather folder.

Inside were papers. New ones. Clean ones, with seals he half recognized and a name written across the top in careful clerk's hand:

**Aldous Wren.**

He stared at it. "You named me after a dead surveyor and a bird."

"After two men who wrote things down and refused to stop," Isolde said. "It seemed fitting. Besides—" and here she allowed herself the smile that had gotten them through checkpoints and blizzards and one extremely tense burial-party ruse, "—you can't spend eleven years being Wendell Eriksen and expect to enjoy it. Choose your own name. It's the one piece of property they never managed to take from any of us."

Wendell closed the folder. Aldous Wren. A man with no blacklist, because there was nothing to blacklist—a man who existed only as far as the next mile of trail, defined by nothing except which way he was walking.

"Where will you go first?" Isolde asked.

He looked north, where the ranges went on and on, blue and white and utterly indifferent to ledgers, and felt the old familiar lightness settle over him—not emptiness this time, but space. Room.

"North," he said. "Then further north. After that, I'll consult the weather."

They embraced briefly, the way mountain people do—quick, hard, complete—and then he shouldered his pack and started up the trail, and she stayed at the head of the valley watching until the switchbacks took him out of sight.

He did not count the years behind him. He never had been any good at that arithmetic. What he counted, instead, was the thing under his boots: the miles. One, and then another, and then another, each one chosen, each one his own—

—and somewhere past the third ridge, where the trail split at a cairn some forgotten traveler had stacked against all reason, Wendell Eriksen—Aldous Wren—stopped, consulted the wind, grinned at nothing, and took the path that wasn't on any map at all.
