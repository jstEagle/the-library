## 1. The Weight of Water

The Serevan River in monsoon season is not a river so much as a rumor of one—a brown, muscular thing that has forgotten its banks and intends to keep it that way. I'd chased storms for eleven years, and I want to be clear about something up front: I was never the reckless kind. Reckless men die young and get statues. Careful men like me just die eventually, quietly, owing money.

I owed the Vane brothers a great deal of money. I won't say the figure out loud, even to myself, because numbers have a way of becoming true when spoken, and I preferred mine to remain theoretical. The loan had started sensibly enough—engine parts, hull repair, a season's fuel—and then the interest had done what Vane interest does, which is grow like mold in a wet wall: patiently, invisibly, until one day you notice the whole structure is soft.

So when the storm cell built up over the highlands that Tuesday, I did what careful men do. I went toward it.

Not because I wanted to. Because the Vanes paid for footage. Storm imagery from the upper Serevan was worth good money to insurance adjusters and news syndicates and, once, memorably, a man who claimed to represent a shipping concern and paid triple for close shots of flood crests. I didn't ask what he did with them. Asking questions is how careful men stop being careful.

The *Kestrel's Mercy*—yes, I'd named my boat after a country that no longer existed, and no, at the time I thought nothing of it; there were Kestrel flags in half the pawnshops along the delta, sold as curiosities, and the name had sounded dignified when I was drunk and sentimental—the *Kestrel's Mercy* was thirty feet of riveted iron and stubbornness. She'd been a cargo skiff before she was mine, and she handled like one: honest, slow to turn, and utterly without a sense of humor.

I took her upriver past Kilometer Nine, where the channel narrowed and the water went from brown to something darker, angrier, carrying whole trees sideways like it was late for an appointment. The sky ahead had gone the color of a bruise going bad. Barometric pressure falling so fast my ears popped twice by the time I passed the old customs house, which stood empty now, its windows boarded, its flagpole bare since the Republic folded.

Here is what I knew about the storm: it was big. Here is what I didn't know: it was bigger than the instruments said, because the instruments were cheap, because everything I owned was cheap, because cheap was the only thing the Vanes' interest rate left room for.

The first gust hit me broadside about two kilometers past the customs house. Not wind exactly—wind has texture, direction, a kind of grammar. This was more like being slapped by the entire atmosphere at once. The *Mercy* heeled hard, my coffee pot went into the bilge, and I said a word I won't repeat here because my mother reads nothing I write but I've developed superstitions anyway.

I should have turned back. Any reasonable man would have turned back. But the crest was building behind me and the footage from inside a cell like this—the rain hammering horizontal, the lightning walking across the water on white legs—was worth three months of payments. Three months of not thinking about Ossler Vane's smile, which was the single most frightening thing in the delta, and I include crocodiles in that assessment.

So I pushed on, and the river decided to educate me.

* * *

The second hour was when things stopped being weather and started being event. Rain so dense I couldn't see the bow from the wheelhouse. The channel markers gone—either submerged or simply surrendered. I was navigating by memory and the feel of the current through the hull, which is a skill, and like most skills it works fine right up until the moment it doesn't.

The debris came out of nowhere. That's the honest truth, though I know how it sounds. One moment open water, the next a wall of it—a raft of logs and roofing tin and half a floating dock and, God help me, a piano, upright, keys still attached, playing a low discordant note every time the swell rolled under it. The river had collected an entire village somewhere upstream and was delivering it downstream with no return address.

I cut the engine to avoid sucking logs into the intake, which meant I gave up all control, which meant the river had me. It spun the *Mercy* like a coin losing momentum. I remember grabbing the wheel with both hands and having the wheel spin freely, uselessly, the rudder cable snapped somewhere below, and I remember thinking—not in words, thoughts don't come in words at moments like this, they come in cold clean pictures—I remember the picture of the rocks at Bitter Narrows, and how far away they were, and how fast I wasn't going to be anywhere else.

We hit the debris field stern-first. There was a sound like the world's largest door being kicked in, and the *Kestrel's Mercy* began, with great dignity, to sink.

I got the dinghy loose. I got the waterproof case with the camera gear. I got the strongbox with my papers and the little tin box that held everything else I owned that mattered, which fit in one hand, which tells you something. I did not get the engine. I did not get the fuel stores. I did not get the boat, and watching her go down—stern first, slow, her name plate catching one last flash of lightning like a salute—watching her go down felt less like losing property and more like losing a witness. That boat had seen eleven years of me. Now the river had her, and the river doesn't testify.

I rode the dinghy through four hours of hell, bailing with a bucket, tied to the debris raft itself at one point because the current in mid-channel would have taken me to the sea and I needed to stay near the shore I knew. Near the debts I knew. A drowning man clings to whatever floats, and apparently what floated for me was obligation.

By dawn the storm had moved off east, dragging its thunder like a coat it was tired of. The rain thinned to the ordinary monsoon drizzle that never actually stops, just changes its mind about intensity. And I found myself washed into a backwater slough off the main channel, surrounded by the river's harvest: branches, tin, a child's bicycle with the wheels still turning slowly in the current, and that piano again, silent now, half-submerged, the most expensive piece of driftwood in the delta.

I sat in the dinghy and took inventory, because inventory is what I do instead of despair. Despair is expensive. Inventory is free.

Camera gear: soaked but salvageable, maybe. Papers: dry, thank God for oilcloth. Money: forty-two drals and some foreign coins I kept for luck, though looking at my luck, I may retire that practice. Boat: gone. Income: gone. Debt: intact, growing, indifferent. The interest on the *Kestrel's Mercy* alone would now accrue against a wreck sitting in forty feet of brown water, and the Vanes' contracts had a clause for that—I remembered the clause, because Ossler had read it aloud to me with the relish of a man reciting poetry—salvage value assessed by the lender's own surveyor, at the lender's discretion.

I did the arithmetic I always do, the one where I try to find the version of events where I survive this, and for the first time in eleven years of careful living, the arithmetic came back blank.

That's when I saw the chest.

* * *

It was snagged in the root ball of a fallen fig tree, maybe twenty meters along the slough, riding high enough that the receding surge had left it perched in the branches like something roosting. Iron. Black iron gone green-black with age, banded with straps, corners capped in brass gone dull. About the size of a sea chest, but heavier-looking, denser, the kind of box built to keep things in or keep things out, and built by people who expected someone to try both.

My first instinct—which I'm proud of, honestly, given the morning I'd had—was to leave it alone. Careful men don't open boxes the river gives them. The river gives nothing; it only lends, and its terms make the Vanes look charitable.

But here's the thing about curiosity: it doesn't care about your survival strategy. It's not a hunger you can starve or a debt you can service. It just sits there, patient, while you do the sensible thing, and then it clears its throat.

I paddled over. Of course I paddled over. You would have too. Don't pretend.

The chest was wedged tight, and getting it free cost me twenty minutes and nearly cost me the dinghy when the root ball shifted and tried to take us both down. But the current had done the heavy lifting already—it had scoured the mud from underneath, and once I rocked it loose it came away with a reluctance that felt almost personal, like it had been waiting a long time and resented being rushed.

It was heavier than it looked. Heavier than iron should account for. I got it into the dinghy and it sat there between my boots like a passenger who hadn't bought a ticket, and I poled us back to the slough's edge where there was a strip of higher ground—an old levee road, mostly drowned, but with a stretch of gravel showing above the waterline like the back of some enormous sleeping animal.

I sat on the gravel next to the chest and the dinghy and the ruins of my professional life, and I ate the last of my biscuits, and I looked at it.

There was a seal stamped into the lid. Wax had long since crumbled away, but the impression remained, pressed into a lead plaque screwed flat to the wood beneath: a tower with seven stars above it, and beneath the tower, a broken chain.

I knew that seal. Everyone in the delta knew that seal, the way everyone knows a face from old currency. The Republic of Kestrel. Gone fifteen years now—dissolved, partitioned, renamed, redacted. Its stamps were souvenirs. Its flags were in pawnshops. Its history belonged to whoever had won, and the winners had been thorough.

A chest bearing the Kestrel state seal, riding a flood out of the highlands, out of country that used to be Kestrel before the maps were redrawn. Sealed. Heavy. Built to survive water, which suggested whoever packed it had expected water, or fire, or both.

I told myself it was probably government stationery. Tax ledgers. Land surveys. The bureaucratic sediment of a dead nation, worth nothing but weight.

I told myself that for about ten minutes, standing there in the drizzle with a crowbar I'd pried from the debris raft, working it into the seam under the lid, telling myself all the way down that I'd stop if it fought me, that I'd leave it shut, that a man in my position could not afford mysteries.

The lock was old but the iron was honest, and the crowbar found the gap, and the lid came up with a groan like something waking.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, packed tight as bricks: documents. Hundreds of pages. Folders. Dispatch envelopes, some still sealed, wax intact. And on every page I could see, every folder face, every envelope flap—the tower and stars and broken chain. Official. Numbered. Filed by hands long dead or long promoted.

I put the crowbar down. My hands weren't steady, and I've spent a lifetime keeping them steady; it's part of the careful-man kit.

The rain picked up again, drumming on the lid like a knock at a door. Somewhere downriver, in a comfortable office smelling of good tobacco, Ossler Vane was already doing his own arithmetic, and his arithmetic never came back blank. Ten days, maybe fewer, before a collector found me sitting on a gravel bar with no boat, no income, and a debt that had just lost its only collateral.

I should have thrown the chest back in the river. I knew that even then, with the lid open and the rain coming down. Whatever was in those folders, it belonged to a country that had been erased on purpose, and countries don't get erased by accident, and papers don't get sealed in iron and drowned by accident either. Somebody had wanted this lost. The river, in its indifference, had disagreed.

Careful men walk away. I know, because I'd been one for eleven years, and I'd have sworn to you—sworn on the tin box, sworn on my mother's grave—that I was still one.

I closed the lid. I lashed it into the dinghy. And I started poling for the fuel dock at Halfway Reach, where a man could buy diesel on credit and think clearly, and where—if the fates were feeling generous—somebody with a boat might be willing to carry a stranger and a chest upriver, for a price.

I didn't open another folder. Not yet.

But I'll tell you the truth, since apparently I've started: I lay awake that night under the dock awning with the chest beside me and my hand flat on the cold iron, and I could feel it humming. Not literally. Chests don't hum. But fate does, sometimes, faintly, through metal, when it's finally dealt you a card—and I'd spent eleven years folding every hand I was dealt, and lying there in the dark, I caught myself wondering, for the first time, what it would cost to play this one through.
