## 2. Optimists with Ledgers

The Vane brothers kept their office above a fish cannery on the Serevan's lower bend, which told you everything you needed to know about them. They liked the smell of money being processed—gutted, packed, sealed—and they liked it better when the smell came with a view of the water, because most of their business arrived by boat and left the same way, usually with a different owner.

I climbed the stairs at nine in the morning, still damp from the river, wearing the only dry shirt I owned and carrying my dignity in a bucket that had already leaked out somewhere around Kilometer Four. The chest I'd left hidden under the dock awning, wrapped in a tarp, because there are things you don't bring to a debt collection even if you don't yet know why.

The office smelled of brine and old paper and, faintly, of the candied ginger Aldous Vane chewed constantly, like a man who'd once been seasick and had never forgiven the sea. He sat behind a desk wide enough to land a skiff on, doing nothing in particular with great attention. His brother Rennick stood at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the river the way shepherds watch hillsides—for movement, for trouble, for anything that might wander off with what belonged to them.

"Quentin Delacroix," Aldous said warmly, as if I were an expected guest rather than a problem with legs. "You look terrible."

"Boat sank."

"So we heard." He spread his hands, delighted. "The river took your *Kestrel's Fancy*—no, wait, what did you call her? The *Marta June*. Terrible name for a boat, by the way. Sounds like an aunt who disapproves of dancing." He clicked his tongue. "And the river took her with two hundred and forty vellars of our money lashed to her deck. That's the part that interests us."

"The cargo was insured," I said.

"Your cargo was insured. Our loan was not. That's the difference between optimism and arithmetic, Quentin, and we are not optimists. Well—" he smiled, showing all his teeth, which were very good teeth, the kind bought rather than inherited, "—we're optimists about *you*."

Rennick turned from the window. Where Aldous was soft and cheerful, Rennick was built like something the river had failed to drown on several occasions, and he looked at me the way a man looks at weather he intends to walk through anyway.

"Sit down," Rennick said.

I sat. There is a particular quality to sitting down when two men who own your life are watching, and it is not unlike sinking. You go down slowly, and everyone can see you doing it.

Aldous opened a ledger—I want to be clear that this man actually kept a physical ledger, leather-bound, brass-cornered, because paper records could be burned but a ledger in Aldous's own hand was scripture to him—and ran one finger down a column with the tenderness of a priest touching relics.

"Here we are. Principal, interest, penalties for late payment, penalties for the boat, penalties for the delay between the boat and this conversation—which, incidentally, is a penalty I invented last night while chewing ginger, so let's hear no complaints about its novelty." He spun the ledger around and slid it across the desk. "The total comes to three hundred and eleven vellars."

"I have forty-one."

"And a boat-shaped hole in the water." Aldous beamed at me. "You see how the numbers speak to each other? It's beautiful. It's almost poetry. Three hundred and eleven against forty-one. The gap between those numbers is where men like you live, Quentin, and men like us collect rent."

Rennick spoke without inflection. "Ten days."

"Aldous gets the poetry," Rennick said. "I get the calendar. Ten days. Three hundred and eleven vellars, or you forfeit everything. The mooring rights, the salvage gear, whatever's left of the *Marta June* if she washes up, and the debt converts to labor terms. You know what labor terms are."

I knew. Labor terms meant working their boats until the debt worked itself out of you, which, given the interest structure, was roughly never. Men died in labor terms. Not dramatically—they just stopped coming upriver, and their names got a small line in the ledger, and the line got a line through it.

"Ten days," I repeated.

"You've been reliable before," Aldous said, and there it was—the hook inside the bait, the reason they hadn't simply broken my fingers on the spot. "Eleven years, thirty-two loans, every one repaid. You're the best kind of debtor, Quentin. A superstitious one. You pay because you believe the alternative is worse, and we love that about you. We want to keep believing in you." He leaned forward, ginger-sweet, sincere as a knife. "So this isn't a threat. This is an *opportunity* to remain the man we think you are."

"That's generous."

"We're generous people. Ask anyone who owes us." He laughed at his own joke, and Rennick didn't, and between the two of them I understood exactly how much rope I'd been handed and how long it was.

I stood to leave. At the door, Rennick said, quietly, "Ossler will find you before the ten days are up. Just to talk. Be findable."

"Being findable has never been my strength."

"It's about to become it."

* * *

I walked back along the levee in a rain that had given up on being dramatic and settled into being constant, the monsoon's long gray middle act. Three hundred and eleven vellars. I turned the number over in my head like a stone with no good side. My salvage gear might fetch sixty. The wreck, if she surfaced, maybe twenty. I could sell my father's compass, which I'd sworn I'd die before selling, which is a vow that sounds noble right up until the moment someone says *ten days* in a flat voice.

Or.

I stopped at the top of the levee and let the rain run off my chin.

Or there was the thing under the tarp.

I want to be honest about the order of events, because honesty was going to become important to me later, in ways I couldn't have predicted standing on that levee: I did not go back to the dock thinking about justice, or history, or the dead. I went back thinking about three hundred and eleven vellars and a locked iron box I'd pulled out of a flood, and the small hot hope that the box contained something a rich fool would overpay for. That's the truth. Whatever else happened afterward grew out of that first ugly seed, and anyone who tells you they found their conscience before they found their greed is either lying or has never been in debt.

The chest sat where I'd left it, tarped, wedged behind a stack of crab traps. The dock was empty except for a boy mending nets two berths down, too absorbed in his work—or too well paid—to look my way. I hauled the chest into the shadow under the awning and knelt beside it and really looked at it for the first time.

Iron, banded with bronze gone green at the seams. No lock hasp that I could find—instead a plate across the front, stamped with a seal. I wiped the mud away with my sleeve.

A crest. A bird with spread wings over crossed keys, and beneath it, in lettering worn nearly smooth: REPUBLIC OF KESTREL.

I sat back on my heels.

There is no Republic of Kestrel. There hadn't been for thirty years. Every schoolchild in the delta learned the shape of that history the way they learned the shape of the river: Kestrel had been a nation, then a war, then a province, then a name people used carefully, the way you step around a grave. Its crest appeared now only on museum placards and on the armbands of old men who met in back rooms and were politely ignored.

And here it was, stamped on a chest that had come down the Serevan in a flood, snagged on my wrecked boat like it had been addressed to me.

I should say something here about how a sensible man would have walked away. I'd like to report that some instinct older than greed whispered *leave it, push it back in the water, some things float downstream to you for a reason and none of the reasons are good.* And maybe the instinct did whisper. But greed talks louder, and desperation talks loudest of all, and I had ten days and forty-one vellars and a ledger upstairs in a cannery office with my name in it.

I went looking for tools.

* * *

Prying open a sealed iron chest with salvage tools is loud, slow, humiliating work. The seal plate wasn't a lock at all—it was lead, poured into the seam, stamped with the crest while soft. Someone had wanted that chest closed in a way that announced itself. Breaking it wouldn't be theft so much as testimony.

I broke it anyway.

The lead gave in a long tearing peel, and the lid came up on hinges that screamed like the *Marta June* had screamed going down, and inside, packed in oiled cloth gone stiff with age, were documents. Dozens of them. Bundles tied with wire, folders swollen with damp, envelopes sealed with wax the color of dried blood. All of it stamped with the same bird-and-keys. All of it official. All of it thirty years dead and somehow, sitting there in the lamplight under the dock awning, not dead at all.

I lifted the top folder. Military letterhead. Typed text, fading brown. I read the first page standing up, and then I read it again kneeling, because my knees decided the matter for me.

It was an order. A routing of troops, a designation of a village—I didn't recognize the name, a Kestrel place-name, guttural and old—and a paragraph near the bottom that used words like *pacification* and *final resolution*, which are the words soldiers use when they mean something they don't want to write down. It was signed. The signature was a general's. I knew the name the way everyone in the delta knew it—from the schoolbooks, from the statues in Corvane square, from the national holiday where children laid flowers at his monument. A hero. The hero of the unification.

The order was dated four days before the village's name disappeared from every map.

I put the folder down. My hands weren't steady, and I noticed them the way you notice weather—as a fact about the world rather than a fact about yourself.

Here is what I understood, kneeling on a wet dock at midnight with a dead republic's secrets in my lap: those pages were worth a fortune. Not to collectors—well, yes, to collectors, the vile kind who paid well for exactly this—but more usefully, more immediately, to the men who wanted them destroyed. There are always such men. A hero's statue stands on a foundation of paperwork, and somewhere in the capital there were families, ministries, whole careers balanced on the story those pages contradicted. Somebody upstream had lost this chest in a flood, and somebody else would pay any price to make sure it stayed lost.

Three hundred and eleven vellars suddenly looked like small change.

That was the intoxicating part, and I won't pretend otherwise. For about ten minutes I sat there doing arithmetic of a new and glittering kind—not what I owed, but what I held. Fate's card, face-up on the table. Eleven years of folding hands, and finally, finally, the deck had dealt me something worth playing.

Then I turned to the second bundle, and the arithmetic changed key.

Because the documents weren't only orders. Some of them were reports. After-action accounts, casualty figures, lists. Lists of names—villagers, recorded in neat columns, ages noted, causes of death noted, witnesses noted, and then, appended in a clerk's careful hand, a final disposition line for each name: *remains interred at site. No further action.*

No further action. Two hundred and some names, each one a person who had gotten up on an ordinary morning in a country that no longer exists, and each one filed away by a bureaucrat with decent handwriting under a heading that meant *we are done thinking about you.*

I closed the folder.

I want to tell you I felt horror, and I did, but underneath the horror—this is the part I'm ashamed of, and I'm putting it in anyway because the whole point of what came later was learning to stop being ashamed in the wrong direction—underneath the horror was relief. Cold, practical relief. Because I had thought I was holding treasure, and treasure attracts thieves. What I was actually holding was a wound, and wounds attract a different kind of attention entirely: survivors, tribunals, historians, the stubborn unforgiving living. People who would protect these pages, not just pay for them.

People who might, if it came to it, stand between me and the Vanes.

Or get me killed faster. Both options were on the table. With documents like these, both options are always on the table.

I rewrapped the bundles in their oilcloth, closed the lid on the ruined lead seal, and dragged the tarp back over the chest. The rain kept up its gray whisper on the awning. Down the levee, the town lights burned low and yellow, and somewhere among them Aldous Vane was sleeping soundly beside his ledger, dreaming in columns, certain that in ten days I would be exactly where arithmetic said I must be.

Ten days. Three hundred and eleven vellars. One chest full of a murdered country's conscience.

I lay back under the awning with my boots off for the first time in two days and stared at the wet canvas overhead, and I made myself a promise that felt very firm at midnight and would feel less firm by morning: I would sell the papers to the highest bidder, take the money, pay the Vanes, and never learn a single name from those lists. Read nothing further. Care about nothing further. Take the fortune and stay invisible, the way I'd been invisible for eleven years, the way invisibility had served me so well that I now owed three hundred and eleven vellars to two cheerful men above a fish cannery.

Somewhere out in the dark, the river moved past the pilings, patient as a creditor, carrying things down from the highlands toward the sea. It had carried the chest to me. It had carried other things too, I'd learn—other people, other debts, a pilot with a brother's name in one of those neat, terrible columns.

But that night I didn't know that. That night I only knew that fate had dealt me a card, and that I'd promised myself to play it dirty and quick, and that my hand, resting on the cold iron through the tarp, had already begun to shake.

In the morning, I'd start reading again. I told myself it was research—a man should know what he's selling.

Even then, lying to myself, I was getting better at it.
