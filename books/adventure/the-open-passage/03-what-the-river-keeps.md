## 3. What the River Keeps

The rain had opinions about my lamp. It came through the tarpaulin in fine needles and hissed against the glass chimney, and every few minutes the wind shifted and the flame ducked like a man dodging a punch. I sat in the wreck of my cabin with a towel around my neck, a bottle of cheap brandy at my elbow, and a nation's dead history spread out on my chart table to dry.

I want to be honest about what I was feeling that night, because what I felt first wasn't horror or grief or any of the noble things a better man would report. What I felt first was arithmetic.

There were forty-one documents in the chest. I counted them twice, because counting is a thing you do when your hands need a job while your head catches up. Each one was sealed in wax once, though the river had gotten into most of them, bloating the paper soft as bread. The crest stamped into the wax—the ones that still had wax—was a kestrel with wings spread over crossed anchors, and beneath it a motto in old Kestreli script: *From the Water, Truth.* Which was funny, in a way I didn't appreciate yet. The water had kept the truth for thirty years, and now it had handed it to me, a man who owed the Vane brothers eleven thousand crowns and change.

I spread the pages out like a gambler fanning a hand he doesn't understand yet. The ink had run on some, gone ghostly gray, but the good linen stock the Kestrels used for official correspondence held its print even drowned. You could read them. That was the thing. Whoever had packed this chest had meant for it to survive. They'd wrapped the bundles in oilcloth, nested them in wool, soldered the chest's seams. Somebody had loved these papers enough to armor them, and then the chest had gone into a river anyway, and thirty years later the river gave it back to a debt-ridden salvage rat because the universe has a sense of humor and it is not a kind one.

* * *

Let me tell you what I knew about the Republic of Kestrel before that night, so you can measure how far I fell.

Kestrel was the delta country upriver—rich silt, rich ports, rich people at the top and everyone else arranged below them in the usual geometry. It died in the Reconciliation Wars, when the coastal coalition rolled up the river republics one by one and stitched them into the Federation we all live under now. Every schoolchild learns the same story: Kestrel's government was corrupt, its army brutal, and when the end came, the Kestrel forces did terrible things in their death throes, burning what they couldn't keep. The Federation's victory was mercy itself. We got that story with our morning milk.

And towering over the whole story stood General Aurel Vosk. The Hero of the Delta. The man who, according to every plaque and statue and schoolbook, ended the war *early*—who took the surrender of the last Kestrel garrison at a place called Miren Hollow and thereby spared the region a bloodbath. There's a statue of him in Corvane harbor forty feet tall, pointing nobly at the horizon, which if you ask me is where all statues point because none of them want to look at us. His face had been on currency. His grandson—I learned this later, and it matters—sat in the Federation senate. When Vosk died, they buried him under a slab the size of a dining table, and half the country wept.

That was the story. I'd never had reason to doubt it. Doubting stories costs money, and I've never had money to spare for luxuries.

* * *

The first document I read closely was a signal log. Dated the ninth of Brumaire, final year of the war. Station: Miren Hollow garrison command. Most of it was routine traffic—ammunition counts, a request for medical supplies marked *denied*, the usual bureaucratic sediment of an army losing. But at the bottom of the third page, in a hand different from the clerk's, heavier, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*No prisoners to reach the coast road. None. The world must not see what we built here. — A.V.*

I read it four times. The lamp guttered. Somewhere outside, the river kept talking to itself in the dark, that low continuous mutter floodwater makes, like a crowd waiting for something.

*No prisoners.* A garrison doesn't take prisoners, there are no prisoners. And Miren Hollow, in the official history, was where Vosk accepted the honorable surrender of three thousand Kestrel soldiers who were then, according to the histories, paroled and dispersed. There are no parole records. I know that now. I didn't know it then; I only knew that the sentence in front of me was written in the general's own hand, and that the general's own hand was famous—it was on the surrender document reproduced in every textbook, that big confident scrawl, the A like a kestrel in flight.

Same hand. Same bird-shaped A.

I put the page down and drank some brandy, not because I wanted it but because my mouth had gone dry in a way that felt structural, like drought, like something that wouldn't resolve with one swallow.

* * *

Here is where I should describe my moral crisis in suitably dramatic terms, and believe me, one was coming. But first came the other thing, the shameful thing, the thing I've decided to confess precisely because it's shameful: I did the math.

Documents proving a national hero ordered a massacre. Documents in the general's hand. Documents that contradicted the founding story of the Federation itself. Who would pay for such a thing? Not historians—they're poor and honest, worst combination. But collectors? Governments? The general's family, whose name was worth a fortune in contracts and influence, and who would pay considerably more to make a thing unexist than anyone would ever pay to make it exist? The Vanes themselves moved money for men whose names I only knew from whispers. Somewhere out there was a buyer for whom forty-one sheets of drowned paper were worth more than my boat, my debts, my next ten years.

I caught myself smiling. That's the part I keep coming back to. Sitting alone in a ruined cabin with the corpses of strangers drying on my chart table, I smiled, because fate had finally dealt me a card, and I was already deciding how to play it.

Then I read further, and the smile died somewhere in my chest and didn't leave a body.

* * *

The second bundle was reports. After-action reports, though after-action is a generous term for what they described. A lieutenant named Corben—his signature was small and cramped, a man writing as little as possible—reported the disposition of "detained personnel" at Miren Hollow following the capitulation. Three thousand one hundred and six souls, by his count. He reported that they had been marched to the old quarry roads east of the town. He reported that the general's directive had been "executed in full." He reported, in a sentence that had clearly been written by a man staring at a wall, that the quarry pits had been "prepared" beforehand, and that fuel had been requisitioned from the engineering corps, and that by dawn on the eleventh the site had been "returned to agricultural use."

Returned to agricultural use. Three thousand men folded into the ground under a thin skin of topsoil, and a staff officer describing it in the language of crop rotation.

There were photographs. I didn't want to look. I looked. The river hadn't reached those; they'd been in a separate tin, wrapped in waxed canvas, and they were nearly clean. Trenches. Rows. Men standing at the edges of pits in their Kestrel gray, some of them—you can see this, you can't stop seeing it—some of them with their hands in their pockets, the way men stand when they've been told to wait and have stopped believing anything is coming. Officers with pistols at parade rest. One photograph was taken from far enough back that the dead were just a texture in the earth, a shadowing of the soil, and the officers stood in the foreground looking directly into the camera with expressions I recognized from funerals: the solemn blankness of men performing grief they do not feel, except these men weren't grieving. They were documenting. Someone wanted proof of a job well done.

And in the corner of that last photograph, half-turned away, tall, unmistakable even at distance, the posture of a man surveying property: Aurel Vosk. The Hero of the Delta. Watching his order become geography.

I went outside then. I don't remember deciding to. I just found myself standing in the rain at the edge of my listing deck, breathing wet air, looking at the black river that had given me this thing, and I said out loud, to nobody, "Wrong chest. Wrong man. Take it back."

The river declined to comment.

* * *

By the smallest hours I had read enough to understand the shape of it, and understanding is a weight with no bottom to set it down on. The massacre at Miren Hollow wasn't a war crime committed in the fog of battle. It was policy. The signal logs showed the directive traveling down the chain of command, acknowledged, planned, resourced. Fuel requisitions. Quarry surveys. A chaplain's note—God help me, a chaplain's note—requesting guidance on rites for "the interred," and the reply, initialled A.V.: *Rites are for the living. Proceed.* The whole machine of murder laid out in triplicate, because armies run on paperwork the same as banks do, and this army had been thorough right up until the moment someone realized the war was ending and the paperwork had better disappear.

So someone packed a chest. Someone—an archivist, an aide, a clerk with a conscience, I'd learn who eventually, but that night he was just a ghost with careful hands—took the evidence that could hang a hero's memory and sealed it in iron and gave it to the river, maybe meaning to come back, maybe meaning to send it downriver to a foreign consulate, and never did. Thirty years the chest sat in the silt off the Serevan bend while statues went up and textbooks got printed and grandchildren got elected to senates.

And then the flood of the century pried it loose and dropped it into the arms of Quentin Delacroix, who owes eleven thousand crowns to two brothers who smile too much, and who has exactly ten days to produce it.

Ten days. I'd forgotten about the ten days for almost three hours. That tells you something about how thoroughly the papers had colonized my attention. I remembered it now, sitting in the gray before dawn with the lamp burned to a coin of oil, and the fear came back and shook hands with everything else and made itself comfortable.

* * *

I tried, that morning, to talk myself into the simple version. This is what I'm trained for, in a sense—self-persuasion. A man who lives on salvage and borrowed money becomes an expert negotiator in his own skull. So I laid it out like a business plan, addressing myself in the tone of a reasonable associate:

*You find a buyer. Quietly. There are always buyers for dangerous truths; the market in buried shame is older than any government. You sell the whole chest, contents unseen, cash on delivery, and you vanish the debt in a single transaction. Nobody gets hurt. The papers go to whoever pays, and what they do with them is their affair. You didn't kill anyone. You didn't lie to anyone. You found a thing in a river. Finding things is your trade. You are a finder, not a judge.*

It's a good speech. I've given better ones since, and worse. The flaw in it—and I could feel the flaw even then, the way you feel a soft place in a deck plank—is the word *nobody*. Because I'd seen the photographs, and once you've seen men standing at the edge of a pit with their hands in their pockets, *nobody* stops being available to you. Somebody's daughter had spent thirty years not knowing where her father went. Somebody's mother had died waiting for a parole notice that was never coming because her son was under a farm field east of Miren Hollow, filed under *agricultural use*.

If I sold the chest to the wrong buyer—and the buyers for this particular merchandise were, almost by definition, the wrong buyers—those pages would burn. That was the entire point of buying them. And the families would wait forever, politely, the way people do, telling themselves the war was chaos, that records were lost, that surely if there were news someone would come.

Someone had come. The river sent me.

I hated that thought. I want that on the record. I hated it the way a drowning man hates the rope thrown to him, because it meant obligations, and obligations were the one cargo I had spent forty years refusing to load.

* * *

Near dawn the rain quit, the way monsoon rain does—not ending, just pausing between arguments. I went out on the deck. My boat sat on the mud like a beached whale with opinions, hull holed amidships, rigging trailing, everything I owned soaked and salted and worth less than nothing against what I owed. Downriver, toward the port towns, toward the Vanes' cheerful offices with their ledgers full of patient violence, the sky was going the color of old tin.

I thought about Ossler's smile. I thought about the way Callum Vane had once explained, over brandy I couldn't afford, that the syndicate never chased a debtor who ran—they simply waited, because the delta is small and the water remembers every hull. Ten days. Eleven thousand crowns. Or the boat, and then the other things they take when the boat isn't enough, things with names I preferred not to say even inside my own head.

Against that: a chest full of ghosts. Against that: a statue forty feet tall pointing at the horizon, and a family that would burn the world rather than let it read.

I went back inside and did the only responsible thing left to me, which was to stop reading. I bundled the papers back into their oilcloth, dry as I could make them, and I locked the chest, and I told myself the deal was simple: sell it whole, sight unseen, no more lamplight, no more photographs, no more names. A finder, not a judge. I repeated it until it sounded almost true.

But here's the trouble with reading a thing: you can't unread it. The words were in me now, the heavy embossed scrawl, the bird-shaped A, *no prisoners*, *rites are for the living*. I had carried drowned furniture and rusted engines out of that river for twenty years and slept fine. I knew exactly why this cargo was different, and knowing made it worse, because the difference wasn't in the chest. It was in me. Something had cracked open in the night, some compartment I'd kept sealed since boyhood, and the river had gotten in, and things float up when water gets where it shouldn't.

I was still standing there holding the padlock, arguing with myself in circles, when I heard the engine.

Not my engine—mine was dead and would stay dead until money happened to it. This was a shallow-draft workboat, twin outboards, coming up the Serevan channel slow, the way boats move when they're looking at the banks instead of the horizon. Through the torn mist I watched it come: a supply ferry, patched and practical, flying no colors, piloted by a woman who stood at the wheel the way some people stand in church.

She looked straight at my wreck. She looked straight at me. And she cut her throttle, and began to turn toward my mudbank, and I stood there with a nation's secrets under my arm and a liar's speech rehearsed in my throat, and I understood that whatever the river had started the night before, it had no intention whatsoever of letting me finish it alone.
