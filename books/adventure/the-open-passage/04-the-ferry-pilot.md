## 4. The Ferry Pilot

The delta in monsoon season is a country that keeps redrawing its own borders, and I am one of the few cartographers who works in water instead of ink.

I'd been running supplies since before dawn—rice and diesel up to the stilt villages at Serren Bend, a crate of antibiotics that three different people had tried to buy off me before I even left the dock, and a coffin, empty, because old Mother Cassel had finally lost her argument with her heart and her family wanted her buried on high ground before the river decided otherwise. That's the trade, if you're wondering. Everything moves by boat when the water comes up: the living, the dead, the things people can't bear to leave behind, and the things they should.

My ferry is called the *Lark*. She's forty years old, held together by welds my brother Tam and I made ourselves, and she handles like a drunk aunt at a wedding—but she's mine, or she was, back when ownership meant something more than a registration paper and a debt of my own that I don't talk about. The *Lark* and I have an understanding. She leaks when she feels like it, and I pretend not to notice, and between us we get where we're going.

I was at the Kessel Point fuel dock just past noon, waiting on a pump that worked about as fast as the government that installed it, when I saw his boat.

You have to understand what it means, recognizing a boat out here. In the delta, boats are faces. You know them the way you know your neighbors' walks—the way the *Sable Ann* lists to port because Rennick never fixed the ballast after his wife left, the way the twins' barge always smells of fish and turpentine no matter what they swear they're carrying. So when a stranger's boat shows up at a dock where strangers don't come, every eye on the waterfront finds it inside of ten minutes, and every tongue starts moving inside of fifteen.

This one had done something to earn attention. It was a chase boat—a proper storm-runner, low-slung, twin screws, the kind of hull you only see anymore on men who chase weather for hire or for the pure stupid joy of it—and it looked like it had been through an argument with a bridge abutment and lost on points. The starboard rail was stove in. There was flood-scum up to the windows. And there was a man on the deck, alone, hauling a tarp over something heavy with the particular care of a person who does not want anyone asking what's under the tarp.

Which, of course, is exactly how you make everyone ask.

I watched him from the wheelhouse while the pump gurgled. He was tall, lean in the way of someone who forgets to eat when he's working, dark hair going gray early at the temples, hands that moved like they knew boats better than they knew people. He worked fast and he worked quiet, checking his lines twice, glancing up at the sky the way chasers do—not worried about rain, reading it. Taking its temperature. Old habit. I knew that look. My brother used to wear it.

Tam read the sky too, right up until the day the sky read him instead.

I told myself I wasn't going to get involved. I want that on the record. I had cargo, I had a schedule, I had a rule that had kept me alive ten years in this delta: *everyone is a story someone else is telling, and none of them are yours.* But then the fuel dock attendant—a boy named Pilo who would sell his own grandmother's dentures if the margin was right—leaned into my wheelhouse and said, conversationally, "That fella paid cash. Double rate. Didn't haggle."

In the delta, that sentence is a siren. Nobody pays double without haggling unless they're rich, desperate, or hiding something, and rich men don't drive boats that look like they lost a fight with a river.

"Did he say where he was headed?" I asked, hating myself a little for asking.

Pilo shrugged with the entire philosophy of a nineteen-year-old. "Upriver. That's all. Said it like it was a secret even though everybody goes upriver."

Everybody does go upriver. That's the trouble with directions—they don't tell you anything about the why.

* * *

I gave it twenty minutes. Then I tied off, walked down the dock with the unhurried stride of a woman who has nowhere to be and no interest whatsoever in anyone else's business, and made a show of inspecting my own mooring lines, which needed no inspection, two slips down from his boat.

Up close he was worse than the rumors were going to be. There was a cut healing above his eyebrow, salt-crusted, a few days old. His clothes had dried wrong after getting soaked, which meant he'd been wet recently and hadn't changed, which meant either poverty or urgency, and his eyes said both. He had the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like—constantly, in the background, the way some people hum.

"You're leaking," I said.

He didn't startle, which I'll give him credit for. He just looked at me with the flat, measuring patience of a man deciding how much of me he needed to take seriously. "Everyone's leaking," he said. "It's monsoon season."

"Your bilge pump's been running eleven minutes." I nodded at the trickle of gray water dribbling off his transom into the slip. "Either you've got a hole below the waterline or you've got a very dedicated shrimp living in your engine compartment."

Something shifted behind his eyes. Not warmth, exactly. Recognition—the look of a man realizing the person talking to him knows boats, and therefore knows him, at least the part of him that matters out here. "Starboard seam," he admitted. "Took a wave broadside near the Serevan bend. Patched it, but the patch is aspirational."

"Aspirational patches sink people."

"So I've heard." He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last week. "Can I help you with something?"

And there it was—the question underneath the question. *Are you trouble? Are you the kind of trouble I already have enough of?* I've been asked versions of it my whole life, usually by men who thought they were being subtle. This one wasn't even pretending.

"Funny," I said. "I was about to ask you the same thing."

"I'm not selling anything."

"Neither am I. That's the interesting part." I let that sit. Down the dock, Pilo was watching us with the open delight of a boy who could smell a story and intended to retell it, improved, by nightfall. I lowered my voice anyway. "You paid double for fuel and didn't blink. You're loading something under a tarp like it owes you money. And you're heading upriver in the worst season in nine years, alone, in a boat that's one bad seam from becoming a reef. I run supplies through this delta for a living, friend. I know what desperation looks like, because I see it in the mirror most mornings. You've got it coming off you like heat off asphalt."

For a long moment he said nothing. Rain started—not hard, just the constant fine drizzle that the season wears like a second skin—and beaded on the tarp over whatever he was protecting. I watched his hand drift toward it, small and involuntary, the way a man touches a wound.

"And what do you want?" he asked finally. Not hostile. Genuinely curious, in the tired way of a man who has learned that everything has a price tag and is bracing himself to read it.

"A question first. What's under the tarp?"

"No."

Honest refusal. No lie, no deflection, no charming smile. I found I liked that more than I wanted to. Liars smile. This man looked like smiling cost him money.

"Fair enough," I said. "Then here's my offer, and you can chew on it while you decide whether to trust me, which you shouldn't, but you're going to, because you don't have anyone else. I'm the *Lark*. I go upriver tomorrow at first light, through the flooded channels, past the checkpoints, all the way to Corvane junction if that's where you're pointed. My boat draws less water than yours, my engines are tuned for the shallows, and I know which officers at Kilometer Nine take bribes and which ones take trophies. You follow me in that leaky tub, you'll lose the patch somewhere around the mangrove narrows and probably your cargo with it. You ride with me, you keep your hull and your secrets."

He studied me the way you study a river crossing—looking for the current under the calm. "What's it cost?"

"That depends."

"On what?"

"On whether you're the kind of man who can pay it." I turned and started back toward my wheelhouse, because there's an art to walking away from a negotiation, and the art is meaning it. "First light, chase-man. Slip fourteen. Bring your tarp and whatever's under it, or don't. The river doesn't care, and neither do I."

Both statements were lies. The river cares about everything—it's the most attentive creature I've ever met—and I cared so much it embarrassed me, standing there with my back turned, listening to hear whether he'd call after me.

He didn't. Good. Men who call after you are men who need you more than they trust you, and that's a debt that compounds.

* * *

That evening I sat in the wheelhouse of the *Lark* with a cup of tea gone cold beside me and Tam's photograph on the bulkhead, and I did the thing I do maybe twice a year: I let myself think honestly.

Here is what I know about myself, and I'll say it plainly because nobody else will: I do not trust people. Trust is a door you leave unlocked in a country full of thieves. Ten years ago I trusted the world to be roughly fair, and the world took my brother, called it an accident, stamped a report with an official seal, and filed him away in a drawer somewhere in a ministry that no longer answers letters. Since then I have trusted exactly four things: the *Lark*, the river, my own two hands, and the certainty that the official version of any story is the version somebody was paid to write.

So why did I offer passage to a stranger with a hidden cargo and a haunted face?

Because I recognized him. Not his name—I didn't have it yet, though I'd get it soon enough, along with a great many other things I wasn't ready for. I recognized the *shape* of him. A man carrying something too heavy to set down and too dangerous to explain, moving through the world with that careful, coiled watchfulness, trusting nothing, telling no one, doing the arithmetic of survival in the background of every conversation. I knew that shape. I wore it. Some mornings I woke up inside it before I remembered who I was supposed to be.

Two people who trust no one can't be friends. But they can recognize each other across a fuel dock in the rain, the way soldiers do, or survivors of the same wreck—and something in me, some part I'd have sworn was dead, sat up and took notice.

There was another reason, one I didn't examine closely, because examining it would have required admitting it existed. Whatever was under that tarp had frightened him. Not the weight of it—the *meaning* of it. I've hauled enough contraband to know the difference between a man guarding goods and a man guarding truth. Goods get you robbed. Truth gets you erased. And a man carrying truth upriver, in monsoon season, alone, broke, and hunted—

Well. I'd once carried a letter like that. It didn't save Tam. But I'd have carried a thousand of them, and I'd have carried them gladly, if carrying them meant that someday, somewhere, the official seal got cracked open and the drawer got pulled out and somebody had to look at what was inside.

I finished the cold tea. Outside, the rain thickened against the wheelhouse glass, and the river rose another finger's width against the pilings, patient as a creditor.

* * *

He came at first light, as instructed, which surprised me. Men who talk big at fuel docks rarely show up at dawn; dawn strips away the performance. But there he was at slip fourteen in the gray half-dark, duffel over one shoulder, tarp-wrapped bundle in his arms—carrying it himself, not rolling it, not letting it touch the wet dock—and a look on his face like a man stepping off a ledge and hoping the fall turns out to be a flight.

"Quentin Delacroix," he said, when I raised an eyebrow at him. "Since you're apparently going to know it anyway."

"Mara Ives." I took the bundle from him before he could protest—he hesitated, and I watched him decide to let it go, watched him choose, consciously, to hand a stranger the thing he'd guarded all night, and I understood that this small surrender cost him more than the money he owed whoever he owed it to. I stowed it amidships, wrapped and lashed, dry as a sermon. "Rules are simple. You don't touch the wheel. You don't lie to me about anything that affects the boat. And when I tell you to get down, you get down, and you don't ask what for until we're clear."

"That's it?"

"For now." I fired the engines and felt the *Lark* wake beneath us, that familiar shudder running up through the deck plates like a dog shaking off rain. "Prices get negotiated as we go. Monsoon rates."

He settled onto the bench by the wheel, close enough to watch the channel, far enough to keep his options, and for a while neither of us spoke. The delta opened around us—gray water, drowned trees holding their branches up like drowning men, the occasional roof of a house that wouldn't be a house again until September. Somewhere ahead, thunder muttered its endless monologue.

"You didn't ask where I'm going," he said eventually.

"I know where you're going. Everyone goes upriver." I eased the throttle forward and the *Lark* leaned into the current. "What I don't know yet is what you're running from. But I figure the river will tell me. It always does."

He glanced at me then—really looked, the first time—and I saw him arrive at the same conclusion I'd reached at the fuel dock: that we were the same shape, he and I, two wrecks that had learned to float. He didn't say it. Neither did I. We just let the rain come down and the current carry us, and for the first hour of that long ugly season, the silence between us was almost comfortable.

Almost. Because as we cleared the breakwater and swung north, I caught sight of a man standing at the end of the Kessel Point dock, motionless in the rain, watching us go. He wasn't dressed for weather. He wasn't dressed for the delta at all—clean coat, polished shoes, the stillness of a man who has never once hurried because he's never once needed to. He raised one hand, not quite a wave, not quite a salute, and held it there until the rain took him.

Quentin hadn't seen him. I made sure of that, angling the wheel so my body blocked the view aft.

But I'd seen him. And I knew that kind of stillness when I saw it, the way you know a shark fin—something patient, something paid to wait, something that had marked my passenger and was now marking me.

I opened the throttle all the way, and the *Lark* ran for the flooded country with the rain hammering down and the price of passage still unwritten between us.
