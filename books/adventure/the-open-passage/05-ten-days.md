## 5. Ten Days

The morning Ossler found me at the dock, I had been awake since four, which was not unusual, and lying to myself since five, which was becoming a habit.

The lie went like this: *You're going to sell the papers quietly, pay the Vanes, and never think about any of it again.* It was a good lie. It had structure. Beginning, middle, end. The kind of lie you could build a life on if you didn't look too hard at the foundation. I lay in my bunk aboard the *Magpie*—what was left of her, half her planking scarred, her engine coughing like a consumptive—and I rehearsed it until it sounded almost true.

Then I went topside and saw Mara Ives's ferry already tied off at the fuel dock, low in the water, her hull painted the gray-green of river water so she'd disappear against it when she wanted to. She stood on her deck coiling rope with the economy of someone who'd never once wasted a motion, and she watched me come down the pier with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Fuel by eight," she said. "I leave at nine."

"I'll be here."

"You said that yesterday."

"And here I am."

"Here you are," she agreed, in a tone that suggested she was keeping score somewhere, in a ledger of her own, and that my column did not look healthy.

I was paying for fuel I couldn't afford with money I didn't have, loading supplies onto a boat that wasn't mine, for a journey I hadn't fully committed to, all while carrying an iron chest full of dead men's secrets under a tarp in my hold. If you'd told me a month ago this would be my life, I'd have asked what you were drinking and whether there was more.

That's when I felt him before I saw him. You learn to feel certain men the way you feel weather—the pressure drop, the stillness, the sense that something large is coming up the river behind you. The dockside dogs stopped barking all at once. Even the gulls got quiet.

Ossler walked down the pier like a man strolling through his own garden.

He was a big man gone soft at the edges, or pretending to be—there was muscle under the softness the way there's a hook under bait. He wore a linen suit the color of oatmeal, spotless despite the mud of the docks, and he carried a paper-wrapped parcel under one arm and a smile on his face that reached all the way to his eyes, which was the terrifying part. Men who smile without their eyes are performing. Men who smile with them mean it.

"Quentin Delacroix," he said warmly, as though we were old friends who'd shared a table at a wedding. "Look at you. Still vertical. That's more than most of Aldous's clients can say after a season on the Serevan."

"Aldous Vane sends his regards?"

"He sends his arithmetic." Ossler stopped three paces away—not too close, never too close, everything about him calibrated—and unfolded the parcel on a bollard. Inside were two meat pies, still steaming. He offered me one. "Eat. You look terrible. No offense. Debt looks terrible on everyone. It's the one fashion that flatters nobody."

I took the pie because refusing seemed worse than eating, and because my hands needed something to do besides shake. It was good. Of course it was good. The Vanes fed you well right up until they fed you to the river.

"Ten days," Ossler said, around his own pie, pleasant as a man discussing cricket. "Aldous wants you to hear it from a friend rather than a stranger. Ten days, then the *Magpie* comes to us, and whatever else you've got that floats, sinks, or can be carried. He's being generous, really. The number's grown, you understand. Interest doesn't sleep, and neither does Aldous."

"Ten days." My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"Ten days." He dabbed his mouth with a handkerchief monogrammed with a *V*. "And Quentin—I want to be honest with you, because I like you, and because honesty is cheaper than violence and lasts longer." He glanced, casually, terribly, at the tarp-covered shape in the *Magpie*'s hold. The chest. I hadn't covered it well enough. Or maybe I had, and he simply knew anyway, the way vultures know. "That's new. That wasn't there last week."

"Salvage," I said. "River junk. Flood debris. There's a whole industry in it—you should see what people throw away."

"Mm." He looked at the tarp the way a jeweler looks at paste. "River junk."

"Flood takes houses, houses give up furniture. Furniture gives up boxes. Boxes give up—" I shrugged, a masterpiece of boredom. "Rust."

"Rust," Ossler repeated, savoring it. Then he smiled again, eyes and all, and clapped me on the shoulder with a hand heavy as an anchor. "Ten days. I'll find you. I always find you—that's not a threat, Quentin, it's just geography. Rivers only go two directions, and I know both of them."

He left the second pie on the bollard for me, which was somehow worse than taking it back, and walked up the pier whistling something old and slow, and the dogs started barking again only after he'd turned the corner, as if they'd been holding their breath too.

* * *

Mara appeared at my elbow before the whistling had fully faded.

"Who was that?"

"Debt collector."

"That was not a debt collector. Debt collectors sweat. That man has never sweated in his life. That man is what debt collectors tell stories about to frighten each other."

"His name's Ossler. He works for the Vane brothers. And he's right about the geography—rivers only go two directions." I pulled the tarp tighter over the chest, an act with all the protective value of pulling a blanket over your head during an artillery barrage. "We should load faster."

"We?" Her eyebrows went up. "There's no *we* yet, Delacroix. There's a fare, and there's a passenger, and there's a difference between them wide enough to drown in."

"How much for the difference?"

She studied me for a long moment. Rain had started again, that fine monsoon drizzle that isn't weather so much as a condition, and it beaded on her cropped hair and ran down the creases of her oilskin. Behind her, the ferry sat waiting, engines ticking as they cooled, patient as a held breath.

"Double," she said finally. "For the cargo that makes collectors visit."

"It's rust."

"Then double for the rust." She turned and walked back up her gangplank, calling over her shoulder: "Nine o'clock, Delacroix. Rust and all."

* * *

Loading the chest onto her ferry was a comedy performed entirely in whispers. It weighed as much as a drowned man—which, I suppose, in a way it was; it had spent years underwater wearing the seal of a country that no longer existed, and countries don't die quietly, they just stop answering letters. We wrestled it up the *Magpie*'s gunwale, across the gap between hulls, and down into Mara's forward hold, where she'd cleared a space among crates of rice and lamp oil and, I noticed, a crate marked MEDICAL that rattled suspiciously like ammunition.

"Supply run," she said, catching me looking.

"Naturally."

"The delta runs on supplies. Some supplies keep people alive. Some supplies keep people *free*." She kicked the crate lightly with her boot. "Same thing, out here."

I let it go. Everyone on this river had a crate that rattled wrong. Mine just happened to contain the entire moral history of a nation.

By nine we were casting off, and by nine-fifteen I had made myself useful in the way passengers do—badly, enthusiastically—and by nine-thirty I had settled into the rhythm of the river and begun, foolishly, to relax.

Which is, of course, when paranoia arrived to take its seat.

It started small. A fisherman at the second bend who watched us pass a beat too long. A skiff running parallel for ten minutes before peeling off. Every boat became a question. Every smoke signal on the far bank became a message. By noon I had catalogued seventeen potential spies, three of whom were probably herons.

"You're doing the thing," Mara said. She stood at the wheel with one hand, barefoot, utterly at home in a way I had never been anywhere.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at every boat like it owes you money. Relax. Half the boats out here are smuggling something. Nobody cares about yours."

"Somebody cares about mine."

"Then somebody already knows where you are, and staring won't change it." She adjusted the wheel a degree. "On this river, either you're invisible or you're interesting. Interesting gets you followed. Invisible gets you home. Right now you're the most interesting man on the Serevan, and you're standing on my deck making it contagious."

"So what do I do?"

"Breathe. Chop rice bags. Look boring." She nodded toward the galley. "Boring is a skill. Most people never learn it. You're going to learn it in the next ten days or die interesting, and I'd prefer the first, because the second makes a mess of my decks."

*Ten days.* The words had weight now, real weight, the way stones get heavier the longer you carry them. Ten days until Ossler found me again, and he would—he'd promised, and promises from men like Ossler are the only ones on this river that never break. Ten days to decide what a chest full of rust was worth, and to whom, and whether selling it made me a survivor or something less forgivable.

I spent the afternoon chopping open rice bags and re-sewing them, which is exactly as boring as it sounds, and it worked, mostly. My hands learned the rhythm. My shoulders came down from around my ears. For whole minutes at a stretch I forgot about iron chests and smiling men in clean suits.

Then, near dusk, Mara called down from the wheelhouse: "Boat behind us. Been holding our wake since the checkpoint. Gray hull, twin engines, running dark."

My stomach performed a maneuver the navy would have charged me for.

"Vanes?"

"Could be. Could be customs. Could be nobody." She killed the running lights and eased the throttle back until we were barely moving, a shadow on shadow. "Get below. Sit on your hands. Whatever happens topside, you're cargo tonight, and cargo keeps quiet."

I went below and sat in the dark beside the chest, close enough to feel its cold coming through the wood slats of the hold floor, and listened to the other boat's engines rise and fall behind us like breathing. They passed, eventually—a patrol skiff, unmarked, moving fast, heading nowhere we were. Probably nothing. Probably everything.

In the dark, with my shoulder against the cold iron, I caught myself talking to it. Low. Embarrassed. The way you talk to a dog, or a grave.

"Ten days," I told it. "Whatever you are, whoever you belonged to—you'd better be worth it."

The chest said nothing. Chests are good at that. But underneath the fear, underneath the arithmetic of debt and interest and smiling men, something in me had already decided, and the deciding scared me more than Ossler ever could.

Because I hadn't opened it again since that first night. And I knew—with the certainty of a man who knows he'll finish the drink he swore he wouldn't pour—that tomorrow, or the day after, I would.

Above me, Mara rang the engine bell twice, and the ferry swung north, into the dark heart of the delta, and the rain came down like it meant it.
