## 6. Monsoon Rules

The Serevan in monsoon season is a liar with a beautiful face. It looks like a road. It is not a road. A road doesn't change its mind every six hours, doesn't swallow sandbars whole and spit them out downstream wearing new names, doesn't hold grudges against the people who trust it most. I've been running this river since I was fourteen years old, and I still give it the respect you'd give a drunk uncle at a wedding—affectionate, watchful, and never within arm's reach of anything valuable.

Quentin Delacroix did not understand any of this on our first morning out of Tallow Bend.

"Which channel do we take?" he asked, standing at my shoulder while I studied the water ahead. The rain came down in that gray monsoon curtain that makes the whole world look like it's being viewed through dirty glass. Ahead of us the river split around a drowned island—three channels, all of them looking equally reasonable, all of them lying.

"The middle one," I said.

"Why?"

"Because the left one has a submerged fence from before the war, and the right one has a ferry wreck that the charts still haven't caught up with."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I put the ferry on the right one." I glanced at him. "Nobody else's fault but mine."

He didn't laugh. That was the first thing I noticed about how he traveled—he treated everything as information to be filed rather than weather to be endured. Most passengers either panicked or went slack. Quentin stood there in his ruined good coat, watching the water like it owed him an explanation, and I found myself thinking: this man has been hunted before. Not by rivers. By something with better aim.

I didn't ask yet. Asking was a currency, and I wasn't spending mine until I knew what he was carrying in that iron chest, and why a man deep enough in debt to drown would spend his last coins on passage upriver instead of running for the coast.

* * *

Here are the rules nobody writes down, the ones I learned from my mother, who learned them from her father, who ran cargo through two wars and died in bed, which on this river counts as winning:

Rule one: bribe the hungry, never the bored. A checkpoint officer who hasn't eaten since dawn will take your money and forget your face by lunch. An officer who's comfortable, well-fed, and bored will take your money *and* your name, because your trouble is the only entertainment he's had all week. You can tell the difference by the state of their boots. Hungry men have wet boots. Bored men have dry ones.

Rule two: never be the most interesting thing on your own boat. If they're going to search you—and during monsoon, they will—you want them searching your rice sacks, your fuel drums, your crates of dried fish. Give them something to find that isn't the real cargo. I keep a locked box under the pilot seat with nothing in it but old letters and a dead man's watch. It has bought me out of more inspections than any amount of cash.

Rule three: the river keeps no secrets, but it keeps excellent company. Everyone on the water is running from something or toward something, and mostly both. The fisherman mending nets at Kilometer Four? He sees everything that passes and tells the fuel dock man, who tells the ferry pilots, who tell me. The river has a nervous system. Learn where its nerve endings are and you'll hear about trouble three bends before it arrives.

Rule four: when the sky goes green, get to shore. No cargo is worth the green sky. I've seen men argue with a green sky. The river keeps their boats as souvenirs.

I taught him these over the course of that first day, not because I liked him—I hadn't decided yet—but because a passenger who gets us boarded at a fatal checkpoint is a problem that becomes mine very quickly. Self-interest wears a helpful face. It always has.

He listened the way he watched the water: completely, silently, filing everything. And then, near dusk, he asked the question that told me he'd understood more than I'd given him credit for.

"The officer at Kilometer Nine," he said. "Dry boots?"

"Dry boots," I confirmed. "That's Rennick. He's bored. We don't bribe Rennick. We go around Rennick, or we go through Rennick with a story so good he enjoys it."

"What kind of story does a bored man enjoy?"

"One that makes him feel cleverer than he is." I cut the throttle and let the current carry us into the narrows, where the banks closed in and the rain sounded like applause on the canopy. "You let him find something. Something small. Something that makes him think he's beaten you, so he can go home feeling like a hero instead of wondering what he missed. Bored men don't want money, Delacroix. They want a story to tell tonight."

He nodded slowly. "And the chest?"

"Is exactly the kind of thing a bored man wonders about forever." I looked at him straight. "So we're going to have to be smarter than a box."

* * *

We tied up that night in the lee of a collapsed trading post, hull wedged between two pilings that used to hold up a roof. The rain kept on. It always keeps on. There are stretches of monsoon where you forget the concept of dry, where wet stops being a condition and becomes a substance you live inside, like water lives inside water.

I cooked rice and dried fish on the little stern burner, and he ate without complaint, which raised him half a point in my estimation. Passengers who complain about food on my boat get to swim with their complaints.

He took first watch. I told him not to bother—we were tied up in a dead place, nothing moving but the rain—but he insisted, and I was too tired to argue, so I rolled into my bunk behind the pilot seat and let him have it.

I woke once, near midnight, to check on things the way you do, the way my father did, eyes opening on schedule whether you want them to or not.

He was asleep sitting up, back against the wheel housing, chin dropped to his chest. And his arms were wrapped around the iron chest.

Not casually. Not the way you drape an arm over luggage to guard it. Both arms, wrapped around it the way a child holds something in the dark—the way my brother held his knees the night after the soldiers came through Vessel Row, the way people hold the only solid thing left when everything else has turned to floodwater.

His face, even asleep, even slack, had an expression on it. Not fear exactly. Something more tired than fear. The look of a man holding a thing that holds him back.

I sat there in the dark and watched him for a long minute, and I thought about what kind of cargo gets held like that. Gold gets held like that. Letters from the dead get held like that. Confessions get held like that.

I went back to sleep and dreamed about my brother, which I hadn't done in weeks, and woke up angry at Quentin Delacroix for reasons I couldn't yet name.

* * *

Day two, the river rose.

It does that—not gradually, not politely, but in jumps. You anchor for the night in four meters of water and wake up to seven, and the tree line you moored against is now underwater to its shoulders. Monsoon isn't a season on the Serevan. It's a siege.

We ran light that day, just cargo for a mission clinic at Halfway Bend—antibiotics, sutures, a crate of solar panels strapped down under tarpaulin. Legitimate work. The kind of work that keeps my papers clean and my name off lists. Quentin helped without being asked, hauling crates with a competence that surprised me. City hands, I'd assumed. But he knew knots. Real ones, working ones, the kind you learn from boats, not books.

"Who taught you rope?" I asked.

"My grandfather. He ran barges on the Corvane stretch, before the war made the river expensive." He cinched a bowline without looking at it. "I spent summers on the water. Then winters in offices. The offices paid better and taught me less."

"And now?"

"Now I'm in debt to the Vanes, so apparently neither."

There it was, offered flat, no self-pity in it. I gave him points for that too. Men who lie about their troubles are exhausting; men who drown you in them are worse. He did neither. He stated his situation like a chart notation: hazard, position, depth.

"Vane money is why Halfway Bend has a clinic at all," I said. "They lend to everyone. Doctors, smugglers, widows. Doesn't make them good. Just makes them everywhere."

"I've noticed 'everywhere' is their best quality."

"It's their only quality." I throttled up past a floating mat of debris—a door, a chair, half a shrine, all traveling together like refugees. "Everything on this river belongs to somebody, Delacroix. The trick is knowing which somebodies matter."

"And which ones are you?"

I laughed despite myself. First time in days. "Ask me when we're through Kilometer Nine."

* * *

Kilometer Nine deserves its own chapter in anybody's book, but I'll compress it here, because the full story comes later and it doesn't end well for anyone.

Rennick's checkpoint sits where the river narrows to a throat, with a cable strung bank to bank that they raise and lower like a fishing line and boats are the fish. Officially it exists to inspect for contraband. Unofficially it exists because Rennick likes existing. He's been there eleven years. The war ended, governments changed, the Republic of Kestrel became a word in history books, and Rennick stayed, growing comfortable, growing bored, growing into the landscape like a bridge piling.

"We slip him at night?" Quentin asked, studying the checkpoint lights from two kilometers back, standing beside me under the canopy with rain streaming off the brim of his hat.

"No. Night runs read as guilty. Guilty gets you searched twice." I pointed to a cluster of lanterns upstream of the cable—a funeral gathering, boats tied along the bank, white cloth hung from the trees. "See that?"

"A funeral."

"A funeral. Which means tomorrow morning, there'll be a coffin boat going through the checkpoint, because the family bury their dead across the river in the old ground, and Rennick lets coffin boats through untouched. Has for years. Superstition, maybe. Or boredom finding a way to feel important." I looked at him. "How do you feel about riding with a dead man?"

He didn't flinch, which I was beginning to expect. "Whose funeral?"

"Don't know yet. We'll find out. Rule five, Delacroix, and write this one down: on this river, grief is the safest cargo there is. Nobody searches grief. Everybody owes it."

He was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed the canopy. Somewhere ahead, the funeral lanterns swayed in the wind like slow fireflies.

"You speak from experience," he said. Not a question.

"Everyone on this river speaks from experience. That's rule zero." I turned back to the wheel. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we're going to need to be very respectful of the dead."

He went aft. I heard him settle in, heard the small sounds of a man arranging himself around an iron box, and I kept my eyes on the black water and thought about my brother again, and about coffins, and about how many kinds of cargo a boat can carry when you count the invisible ones.

* * *

Later that night, second watch, I found him awake and sitting across from me under the canopy, and for the first time he talked without my asking.

Not about the chest. About the debt. About how it started—engine repairs, then parts, then a bad season, then a worse one, then the particular arithmetic of owing the Vanes, where the interest grows like floodwater and the terms rearrange themselves in the night. He told it plainly, almost bored with his own ruin, and somewhere in the middle of it I realized he wasn't confessing. He was paying me in honesty because he suspected I'd been underpaid in something, and he'd noticed.

Most men pay in charm. Charm is cheap and I'm immune. Honesty from a man who lies for a living—that's rarer coin.

"You could run," I said. "Coast, open sea, new name. People do it."

"People who run leave debts behind that grow teeth." He shook his head. "Ossler found a man in Corvane who'd run to another country. Another continent. Took them eight months." A pause. "The man came back in pieces. Politely. In boxes. Labeled."

"That's grim."

"That's accounting." He almost smiled. "The Vanes are very thorough accountants."

I looked at him then—this soaked, broke, stubborn man with his iron chest and his careful silences—and I felt the thing I'd been refusing to feel since Tallow Bend, which was respect. Not trust. Trust is a different animal and I don't own one. But respect, grudging and unwelcome, settling in like damp.

"You held the chest while you slept," I said.

Something moved behind his eyes. "You were watching."

"I always watch. It's rule negative-one." I let the silence sit. "People hold gold like that. Or letters from the dead. Or confessions."

He didn't answer. He looked out at the rain, and the rain looked back, and the river muttered under the hull like it knew something.

"Get some sleep, Delacroix," I said finally. "Tomorrow we ride with a coffin."

I killed the lantern and lay back in my bunk, and just before sleep took me, I heard him say it—quiet, to himself or to the chest or to the dark, I couldn't tell:

"Soon. I promise. Soon."

And I lay there in the wet dark, wide awake now, wondering what kind of man makes promises to a box—and what, exactly, was inside it that needed promising to.
