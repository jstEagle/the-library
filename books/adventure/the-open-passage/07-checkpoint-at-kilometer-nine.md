## 7. Checkpoint at Kilometer Nine

The rain had a rhythm to it by the third day on the river, and I had learned to hate it the way you hate a song stuck in your head—intimately, personally, with a resentment that had outlived all reasonable proportion. It came down in sheets that weren't quite sheets, in curtains that weren't quite curtains, in a fine gray insistence that found every seam in my oilskin and every seam in my patience and dripped into both.

Mara stood at the wheel of her ferry with her collar up and her eyes on the water, looking like a woman who had made peace with the weather long ago and now regarded it as a dull acquaintance. The *Sable Tern*—her boat, though "boat" was generous; she was more of a barge with ambitions—plowed upriver through water the color of old pewter, pushing against a current that seemed to take our progress as a personal insult.

"Kilometer Nine," she said, without turning around. "Checkpoint's about an hour out. Maybe less if the river's feeling generous."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we sit in the queue and wait for some teenager with a rifle to decide whether today is the day he becomes important." She spat over the rail, a habit I'd noticed but never commented on. "You want to know what I know about Kilometer Nine?"

"I assumed you'd tell me regardless."

A ghost of a smile crossed her face. "Kilometer Nine is where the river narrows and the government remembers it exists. There's a boom across the channel—chains, actually, old ones from the dredging days—and a customs shed that leaks worse than my bilge. Two officers, sometimes three. They're not looking for anything in particular. That's what makes them dangerous."

"How does that make them dangerous?"

"Because a man with no purpose will invent one," Mara said. "Give him a list of things to check for and he checks for those things. Give him nothing and he checks whatever frightens him that morning. You can bribe fear. I've done it. But it's expensive, and it doesn't keep."

I looked back at the chest. It sat amidships under a tarpaulin, lashed down with rope I'd double-knotted twice, looking for all the world like nothing more than salvage—which, technically, it was. Iron, green with river rot, heavy as sin. I'd spent two nights telling myself it was just metal and paper, just a dead nation's paperwork, just a thing to be sold to whichever nervous man would pay most for its disappearance.

The trouble with lying to yourself is that you have to be very good at it, and I'd never been. Every time I looked at the chest, I thought about the general's signature. Every time I thought about the general's signature, I thought about how much a man might pay to keep that signature buried. And every time I thought about *that*, I thought about Ossler, who had smiled at me across a desk with the warmth of a man discussing weather while explaining precisely what would happen to my hands.

"You're doing the thing again," Mara said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes somewhere else and your hands go quiet. I've been running this river eleven years. I know what a man looks like when he's carrying something he shouldn't be."

"And what does he look like?"

"He looks like you," she said, and turned back to the wheel.

* * *

We came around the bend at Kilometer Nine an hour later, and there it was: the chains sagging across the channel in lazy catenaries, dripping brown water; the customs shed listing on stilts like a drunk leaning on a friend; a concrete pad where three men waited under a corrugated awning, one of them smoking despite the deluge, the cigarette sheltered under his palm with the tenderness of a man protecting something small and good in a large and bad world.

There were four boats ahead of us in the queue. Cargo punts mostly, low in the water with rice or fuel drums or lumber, their pilots hunched and patient. Rain drummed on the awning like applause from a hostile audience.

Mara brought us alongside and killed the engine, and the silence that followed was enormous—the kind of silence the river makes when it wants you to hear your own thinking.

"Stay here," she said quietly. "Let me work. And Quentin—" she fixed me with a look that could have caulked a hull, "—whatever happens, do not look at the tarpaulin. Not once. Men who look are men with secrets, and men with secrets get searched. Can you manage that? Looking bored?"

"Boredom is my native language."

"We'll see."

She stepped onto the concrete pad, and I watched her greet the smoking officer by name—Vess, I caught, or maybe Ves-something—and hand him something small and folded that vanished into his pocket with the smoothness of long practice. They talked. She laughed at something. He laughed at something. It was all very civilized, the way extortion is civilized when both parties have grown fond of each other.

Then the second officer came out of the shed, and everything changed.

He was younger than Vess, leaner, with the kind of restless energy that never sits well in a man whose job is standing still. He said something to Vess. Vess's smile didn't drop so much as freeze, the way a smile freezes when it's being held up by money and the money has stopped arriving. The young officer looked past them, directly at the *Sable Tern*, directly at me.

Mara came back aboard without hurrying, which told me she was hurrying inside.

"Change of plan," she murmured, firing up the engine just enough to hold position. "That's Dren. He's new—three weeks, maybe. Vess says someone radioed ahead this morning asking after a salvaged chest on a ferry heading upriver. Description matches us."

My stomach performed a maneuver I hadn't authorized. "Ossler."

"Or someone working for him. Doesn't matter. What matters is Dren wants to search, and Vess owes me enough to stall, but Vess won't risk his pension for either of us." She glanced at the queue. Ahead of us, third in line, was a flat-bottomed funeral punt draped in black cloth, carrying a coffin and a knot of mourners in dark, soaked clothing—an entire family's grief out in the monsoon because the dead don't wait for dry weather. "And Dren is bored, and bored men with rifles are the worst kind."

"So what do we do?"

Mara was quiet for a moment. Rain ran off the brim of her hat in a steady thread. Then she said, "How attached are you to your dignity?"

"Not very. It's one of the few things I own outright."

"Good." She nodded toward the funeral punt. "Because I'm going to get us behind that coffin, and you're going to help carry it."

* * *

It took twenty minutes of queue-jumping diplomacy that I will not describe in detail because it involved Mara's last bottle of decent rum and a lie about a dying mother in Corvane, but we slid into position directly astern of the funeral punt, close enough to read the name plate on the coffin through the rain: **EDESSA VOLL**, beloved mother, aged seventy-one.

"She seems like she was a nice woman," I said.

"She was a fishwife from Lower Marsh," Mara said. "I ferried her son's wedding party once. She drank everyone under the table and danced on a table herself. She'd have loved this."

"Loved what, exactly?"

"The indignity of it. Edessa Voll hated officials. Hated checkpoints. Her family will understand." Mara killed the engine and turned to face me fully, and for a moment I saw something in her expression I hadn't seen before—not the wariness, not the calculation, but something almost like apology. "Here's how it goes. We offer to help carry her up the bank to the burial ground—it's tradition, travelers help, nobody questions it. The coffin goes first. While Dren's busy deciding whether to search a funeral, we ask Vess to wave the *Tern* through as a courtesy to the grieving family. The chest stays aboard, under the tarp, and Dren searches the coffin instead."

"That's insane."

"It's elegant."

"It's *sacrilegious*."

"It's practical," Mara said. "And Quentin—if Dren boards us and finds that chest, we're not fined. We're not delayed. Men who radio ahead about iron chests don't send teenagers with rifles unless they intend to collect. You understand? This isn't a checkpoint anymore. It's a trap with a roof on it."

I looked at the coffin. I looked at the chest under its tarpaulin. Somewhere upriver, a man named Ossler was waiting, patient as floodwater, and somewhere behind us, ten days were ticking down to zero.

"All right," I said. "But if Edessa Voll's ghost comes for me, I'm telling her it was your idea."

"Tell her whatever you like," Mara said. "She'll probably agree with it."

* * *

The mourners received our offer with the weary gratitude of people too wet and too sad to refuse help. The eldest son—a broad, exhausted man named Pell—shook my hand and said, "Mother would've laughed," which either meant he knew what we were doing or grief had made him generous, and I decided not to investigate which.

So there I was, thirty minutes later, helping six strangers carry a coffin up a muddy bank through driving rain, my boots sucking at the earth with every step, while behind us Mara talked Vess into waving the *Sable Tern* through as an act of mercy. The coffin was heavier than it should have been—Edessa Voll must have been built like a river barge herself—and the mud was treacherous, and the mourners sang something low and repetitive that sounded like it had been keeping grief company for centuries.

Dren watched us the whole way.

I felt his attention like a hand between my shoulder blades. I kept my eyes down, kept my grip honest, kept my face arranged into the blank courtesy of a traveler doing a kindness. *Boredom,* I reminded myself. *Boredom is survival.* But my heart was hammering out a rhythm that had nothing bored about it, because every step took me further from the chest and closer to a man with a rifle and a radio and orders I could only imagine.

At the top of the bank, the procession paused at the burial ground—a rise of ground with wooden markers leaning at angles, names bleached illegible, the whole cemetery slowly surrendering to the river. And that's when I heard it: raised voices below, Dren's voice, sharp with authority, and Vess's voice, smooth and stalling, and then—

"Search the boat."

Pell's hand closed on my arm. "Your friend's boat?" he asked quietly.

"Our boat," I said, and my mouth had gone dry. "Yes."

What happened next happened fast. I heard boots on the *Tern*'s deck, heard Dren shouting at Mara to step away from the cargo, heard the tarpaulin come off with a wet slap. And I did the single stupidest, bravest, most Edessa-Voll thing I have ever done: I set down my end of the coffin, told the mourners I'd be right back, and went down the bank at a half-slide, mud to my knees, into the path of a man holding a rifle.

"Dren," I said, and I was amazed my voice came out level. "Is there a problem?"

He swung the rifle toward me, then thought better of it, then settled on pointing it at a spot roughly near my sternum, which I suppose was meant to be diplomatic. He was younger up close. That was the worst part—young enough that the authority was still new and shiny, young enough to need to use it.

"Step aside. Customs inspection."

"On what grounds? This is a licensed ferry. Mara Ives runs this route—"

"On the grounds that I say so." His eyes flicked to the tarpaulin, now crumpled on the deck, and to the shape beneath it. "What's under there?"

"Salvage," I said. "River iron. Worthless."

"Then you won't mind showing me."

And here is the thing I learned about myself at Kilometer Nine, standing in the rain with a rifle pointed at my chest and a dead woman's funeral proceeding above me: I am a better liar than I ever believed, and a worse man than I hoped—but I am quick. Quicker than brave. Quick enough to see, in the half-second before I answered, that Dren wanted a fight, wanted a reason, wanted the story he'd radio ahead about to end with something dramatic.

So I gave him boredom instead.

"Show you," I repeated, and sighed—the sigh of a man put upon by the universe since birth. "Fine. Uncover it. Mind the rust, it'll tetanize you. It's ballast, mostly. I pulled it out of a snag below the falls. You want to inspect forty pounds of river-rotted iron, be my guest. Write it up in triplicate. I'll wait."

Something in him hesitated. Boredom, offered sincerely, is hard to argue with.

But then he reached for the tarp anyway, because he was young and suspicious and someone had told him there'd be a chest worth finding, and I understood with terrible clarity that the performance was over, and that the next ten seconds would decide whether I ended this chapter of my life as a free man, a drowned man, or a man who'd sold a dead republic's sins to the highest bidder out of simple cowardice.

That's when the singing stopped.

Above us, on the rise, the mourners had gone silent—all of them, the whole sodden congregation of them, standing at the graveside with their heads bowed. And then Pell Voll's voice carried down through the rain, clear and furious:

"This man helped bury my mother! If you've got time to harass ferrymen, Dren, you've got time to dig!"

And the other mourners turned, all of them, a dozen pairs of eyes, and looked at Dren with the particular contempt that rural people reserve for officials who interrupt funerals. It wasn't a threat. It was worse than a threat. It was *witness*. A dozen witnesses to a young officer harassing a funeral party, and word travels upriver faster than boats, and pensions are fragile things.

Dren's rifle wavered.

Behind him, Vess appeared in the shed doorway, arms folded, wearing the expression of a man watching a colleague walk into a hole he'd warned him about. "Let them through, Dren," Vess said mildly. "Ferry's clean. I checked her myself this morning."

"You checked her this morning?"

"Before the radio message," Vess said, and let the words sit there, letting Dren do the arithmetic himself: *before* meant Vess had known, and knowing and saying nothing meant Vess was either careless or bought, and either way, pressing the point would cost Dren more than it cost anyone else.

The rain filled the silence. On the rise, the mourners waited. In the coffin, Edessa Voll kept her counsel.

Dren lowered the rifle. "Go through," he said. "Move along."

* * *

We cleared the chains at Kilometer Nine twenty minutes later, the *Sable Tern* wallowing upriver through water gone the color of bruised slate, the funeral punt's black drapery receding behind us in the gray. Mara held the wheel and didn't speak for a long time. Neither did I. My hands, I noticed, were shaking—not from fear exactly, or not only from fear, but from the specific aftershock of having stood in front of a gun and lied, and lied well, and enjoyed the competence of it even while despising the necessity.

"You lied to him," Mara said finally.

"I lied to everyone. I lied to a corpse."

"To a corpse's *family*."

"They helped willingly."

"They helped because you asked them to help you commit a crime, and they did it because their mother hated officials more than she loved propriety." Mara shook her head, but there was no judgment in it—or none aimed only at me. "You realize what happened back there, don't you?"

"That we nearly died?"

"That you chose." She glanced at me, rain streaming off her hat brim. "You could have handed it over. One iron chest, one teenage bureaucrat, one radio call, and Ossler gets his papers and you get your quiet life back. Ten days become irrelevant. You'd have owed the Vanes, sure, but you'd have owed them alive. Instead you hid a chest in a funeral and stared down a rifle. Why?"

I opened my mouth to say something clever and found nothing there. The honest answer was rising in me like floodwater, and I couldn't stop it, and I didn't especially want to.

"Because I read them," I said. "The documents. All of them. Last night, after you were asleep. There's a list of officers present at Kestrel. There's a supply requisition signed three days before the village burned—extra fuel, extra ammunition, marked 'for sanitary operations.' Sanitary operations, Mara. They called it *cleaning*." My voice cracked on the last word, and I let it. "Somebody wrote those orders. Somebody signed them. Somebody filed them. And a nation decided to build a hero out of the man who did it, and everybody who knew went along, and the river swallowed the evidence, and the river gave it to *me*. Of all the drifting wrecks on all the flooded miles, it washed up against my hull. You want to know why I didn't hand it to Dren? Because handing it over felt like signing the next order myself."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. The rain hammered the deck. Somewhere ahead, the river bent north toward country I'd never traveled, toward whatever waited at the end of ten days.

"That's the first true thing you've told me," she said at last.

"It won't be the last."

"No," she said, and something in her voice shifted—warmed and hardened at once, like steel coming out of a quench. "It won't. Because I saw the manifest this morning, before we cast off. The requisition lists. The officer signatures." Her knuckles had gone white on the wheel. "Quentin—my brother's name is in those pages. Not as a victim. As a *witness statement*, taken two days before he disappeared. Which means whoever signed that document knew where he was, and what happened to him, and filed it anyway."

The river roared past. I had no words, and for once I didn't reach for any.

"So here's my price," Mara said, turning to face me fully, rain running down her face like the river itself had gotten into her eyes. "Not money. Not passage fees. When we find who signed for my brother—I'm there. Whatever that costs. Whatever it breaks. You don't sell that chest, Quentin Delacroix. You deliver it. Or you and I are finished, and I'll put you off at the next dock, and you can explain to Ossler alone why his radio tip came up empty."

I looked at her—at the fury and the grief and the strange, fierce honor underneath both—and I understood that somewhere between a funeral and a checkpoint, this had stopped being about debt. The Vanes owned my ledger. But the dead owned something older, and the interest on *that* account compounded daily, and there was no buying it out.

"All right," I said. "We deliver it."

"Say it like you mean it."

"We deliver it," I said, "or we die trying, and either way Edessa Voll keeps her secret."

Ahead, the rain thickened toward the horizon, and the river rose to meet it, and I thought I heard—though I may have imagined it, though I hope I didn't—laughter from a fishwife's ghost, rolling downstream behind us like thunder.
