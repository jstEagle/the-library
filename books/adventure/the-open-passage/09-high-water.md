## 9. High Water

**MARA**

The river had been rising for three days when it stopped pretending to be a river and became a country of its own.

I'd run the Serevan delta through eleven monsoon seasons, and I had a private taxonomy for floods. There was the polite flood, which came up green and slow and gave you time to move your fuel drums. There was the rude flood, which took a fence line or a chicken coop as payment for passage. And then there was what I saw from the ferry's wheelhouse on the morning of the fourth day: brown water moving at the speed of a galloping horse, carrying an entire roof—tiles, ridge beam, and one indignant goat—past us like it was late for an appointment.

"That's not a flood," Quentin said beside me. He'd been quiet since Kilometer Nine, in the way of a man who has recently hidden cargo in a coffin and is recalibrating his relationship with God.

"No," I said. "That's the river eating."

We were forty kilometers above the checkpoint, running dark on the flooded back channel behind Serel Island because the main channel had checkpoints of its own now—the government's, the loyalists', and at least two that were just men with guns and opinions. The rain came down in sheets so thick that the world beyond the bow was a rumor. My hands knew the channel anyway. That's the thing about rivers: you can lie to people all day long, but you can't lie to water, and water doesn't lie to you either. It just tells you where everything stands.

What it told me that morning was that we weren't going to make Corvane by the high route. The back channel ended at a drowned village called Vessik, and past Vessik the water spread out into the flooded forest like spilled ink, no banks, no markers, nothing to steer by but treetops. In whiteout rain. With a boat already riding low from the weight of an iron chest full of other people's sins.

"We need to get out of the current," I said. "Find high ground and wait it out."

"Wait how long?"

"However long the sky wants."

Quentin looked up at the sky, which offered him a wall of gray with no visible top. "I have nine days left on my debt," he said. "Maybe eight. The river doesn't care about my ledger."

"The river," I said, "is the only honest creditor you have."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. He'd been doing that lately—almost smiling, almost saying something, almost being a person instead of a man hauling a secret around like ballast. I found it irritating, mostly because I recognized it. I'd spent four years being a woman hauling a secret around, except mine didn't clank.

Vessik appeared out of the rain as a suggestion: a church steeple standing in six feet of water, its bell still hanging, and below it the rooftops of a village doing its best impression of an archipelago. The church sat on the highest ground for miles, which meant the highest ground for miles was currently underwater to about waist height on a tall man. We tied off to the steeple's lightning rod bracket—I've never trusted a church that didn't come with a lightning rod—and waded in through the side door, chest-high, holding the documents wrapped in oilcloth above our heads like an offering.

Inside, we found the rest of the drowned world.

**QUENTIN**

There were maybe thirty people in that church, and they had organized themselves with the grim efficiency of people who had done this before. Blankets hung on lines between the pews. A cookfire burned on a slab of slate laid over the altar steps, which struck me as either blasphemy or the most honest use of an altar I'd ever seen. A woman with forearms like dock rope was ladling out rice porridge with the authority of a customs officer. Nobody asked anybody's name. Names are a luxury of dry land.

Mara and I dripped in the doorway until the porridge woman pointed her ladle at two empty pews near the back, and that was that. We belonged. The flood had issued the invitation.

It should have felt like relief. Instead I spent the first hour doing what I'd been doing since Ossler put his hand on my shoulder at the dock: counting exits. Two doors, both underwater. Windows, stained glass, breakable. Roof beams, climbable if you were desperate. I caught myself mapping sight lines to the chest—which Mara had insisted we stash under the floorboards of the vestry, beneath a loose plank she'd found in about ninety seconds, because of course she had—and I realized I was doing arithmetic with other people's lives again. If trouble comes through the front, go out the windows. If trouble comes through the windows—

"You're doing it again," Mara said quietly. She was watching the room over the rim of a tin cup, the way I was, and I suppose that's why we understood each other: we were both counters.

"Doing what?"

"Spending everyone in this room like currency. I can see it going on behind your eyes. It's very loud."

"I'm trying to keep us alive."

"I know." She drank. "So am I. But there's a difference between keeping watch and hoarding, and you crossed it somewhere around the coffin."

I wanted to argue. What stopped me wasn't the argument itself—it was the old man three pews ahead of us who had turned around at the word *coffin*, slowly, the way old men turn when they've spent a lifetime listening for particular words. He had a face like a dried riverbed and eyes that did not miss things. He wore a cardigan that had been mended more times than most boats, and on the third finger of his right hand was a ring I recognized, because I'd spent two nights staring at one pressed into wax on a hundred documents: the seal of the Republic of Kestrel. A kestrel falling, wings folded, talons open.

The Republic hadn't existed in thirty years. Its rings didn't circulate among rice-porridge refugees.

The old man looked at me for a long moment, then at Mara, then back at me, and said, in the mild tone of a man asking about weather: "You're the ones who pulled the chest out of the Serevan bend. Aren't you."

The church noise went on around us—porridge, coughing, a baby complaining about existence—but for me the room went very quiet, the way it does when a gun comes out, except no gun had come out. Just a sentence.

"How could you possibly know that?" Mara said.

"Because I put it in the river," he said. "Thirty-one years ago. And I've been waiting ever since for somebody foolish enough to take it back out."

* * *

**MARA**

His name was Edran Voss—not the general Vosk, he was quick to say, different family entirely, though he pronounced the similarity with a bitterness that suggested the two names had been tangled together for decades. He had been assistant archivist to the Republic of Kestrel's military records office, third grade, which he described as "the rank of a man who knows everything and is authorized to say nothing."

We sat with him at the far end of the church, under a window where the stained glass had blown out years ago and the rain came through in a fine cold mist. He warmed his hands at a candle stub and talked the way old archivists talk: precisely, chronologically, with an unbearable patience, as if history were a filing system and he alone remembered where everything was cross-referenced.

"There were eleven chests," he said. "Officially, ten were destroyed in the shelling of the records annex. That was the story, and the story was even mostly true—ten chests went into the furnace. The eleventh went into the river, because I was younger then and believed that paper drowned." He smiled without any humor in it. "Paper floats, Ms. Ives. Paper waits. Paper is the most patient material mankind ever invented. Stone crumbles. Iron rusts. Paper just sits in the dark, holding its breath, waiting for someone to read it aloud."

"What's in it?" Quentin asked. His voice was careful. Too careful. I'd learned to hear the seams in it by now.

"You know what's in it. You've read enough of it to be afraid, and you haven't read enough of it to be certain. So let me be certain for you." He held up fingers, ticking them off like a ledger. "Order 114-K, signed by General Aldous Vosk, authorizing the 'pacification' of the Kessel valley villages—eleven villages, some four thousand people, most of them women, children, and old men, because the fighting-age ones had already gone. Follow-up correspondence confirming the order was carried out as written, not as later claimed—as a tragic accident of war, an artillery error, a fog-of-war misfortune. Rosters of the officers present. Supply requisitions proving the operation was planned six weeks in advance, down to the number of rounds allocated per village." He paused. "And a list of witnesses, with disposition notes. Which is archival language for what was done to the people who saw it happen."

My hands had gone cold, and it wasn't the rain.

"My brother," I said, "was a witness. He was a ferryman too. They said he died in the artillery accident."

Edran Voss looked at me with those eyes that missed nothing, and I watched him decide something—decide whether I could bear the truth, decide whether it mattered whether I could bear it. Old men get to make that decision quickly. They've run out of time for gentleness.

"He was executed on the embankment at Kessel town," he said. "Along with nineteen others. The signature on the disposition order is Captain Reiner Halt's. He was Vosk's adjutant. He lives comfortably in Corvane now, I'm told. Chairs a shipping charity."

I don't remember standing up. I remember the candle flame bending as I passed it, and Quentin's hand catching my wrist—not stopping me, just anchoring me, the way you tie off a boat in a current. I stood in the cold rain-mist from the broken window and let the grief move through me like floodwater through a house: it takes what it takes, and then it recedes, and what it leaves behind is the shape of your life afterward.

Four years I'd believed my brother died badly by accident. It turns out he died badly on purpose, and someone had filed it.

"Sit down, Ms. Ives," Edran said gently. "Grief is a debt too. But unlike the Vanes' kind, this one gets paid by living."

* * *

**QUENTIN**

Here is what nobody tells you about carrying a fortune in stolen paper: the moment someone credible tells you it's real, it gets heavier.

For days I'd been able to sustain a comfortable fiction—that the chest might be worthless, that the Vanes' terror was disproportionate, that I could sell it to some collector and walk away clean, a scavenger, not a thief of anything that mattered. Edran Voss dismantled that fiction in twenty minutes, sitting in a wet church, ticking atrocities off on his fingers. The documents were authentic. He confirmed it the way only their author-adjacent custodian could: watermark patterns, ink batches, the secretary's hand on the carbon copies—"that's Pellam's script; I'd know it in my sleep; I catalogued four thousand pages of it"—and finally, worst of all, the ring on his own finger matched the crest seals exactly.

Worthless? No. Priceless—to the tribunal at Corvane, to every survivor family in the valley, and, priced rather differently, to every man whose name appeared in those rosters and had spent thirty years building monuments to his innocence.

And explosive. Edran used that word himself, and he used it the way an engineer uses it: not as drama, but as load calculation.

"You understand," he said to me, while Mara slept—actually slept, curled on a pew with her boots on, the first real sleep I'd seen her take since Kilometer Nine—"that you are now the most dangerous object in this flood zone. Not the chest. You. Because the chest can be sunk, burned, stolen. But you have read it. You know what's in it. Men have drowned whole archives for less than what's in your head."

"I'd rather they didn't know what's in my head."

"They will. That's how these things work. Someone will look at your face wrong, or right, and word will travel faster than this water." He fed the candle another breath. "You have a choice coming, Mr. Delacroix. Everyone does, eventually, with paper like this. You can sell it and spend the rest of your life rich and small. Or you can deliver it and spend whatever's left of your life poor and large. I made my choice thirty-one years ago, sinking a chest in the dark and telling myself it was cowardice when it was really hope—hope that someday the river would give it back to someone braver than me." He looked at Mara sleeping. "It appears the river has a sense of humor."

"I'm not brave," I said. "I'm in debt."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "Most bravery is just debt that's come due."

I should have hated him for that. Instead I sat there in a drowned church full of strangers sharing porridge and blankets, listening to rain hammer a roof that had no business still standing, and I felt something I hadn't felt since before the Vanes bought my boat's mortgage: not safety—there was no such thing anymore—but company. The woman across the aisle had given half her blanket to a child not hers. The porridge woman refilled cups without being asked and remembered who liked theirs salted. Nobody here could fix anything. Everybody here was sharing anyway.

Fragile, Edran called it later, when I mentioned it. Communities in flood zones always are. "But fragile things," he said, "are the only things that grow."

* * *

**MARA**

On the second night, the water reached the church floor, and we moved everything—and everyone—up into the gallery and the bell loft. The cookfire came last, carried up the stairs in its slate cradle by two men arguing cheerfully about the best angle of ascent. Somebody started singing. It was a Kestrel song, an old one, the kind they'd banned after the Republic fell, and for a moment nobody breathed—thirty people waiting to find out if the past was safe to sing out loud—and then Edran Voss's cracked voice joined in from the bell loft, and then the porridge woman, and then everybody, badly, in three different keys, and the baby stopped crying to listen.

Quentin stood at the gallery rail beside me, not singing—he didn't know the words, and I suspect he doesn't know the words to much of anything yet, this man who has spent years making himself unmemorable—but listening the way you listen to a language you're trying to learn.

"My mother sang that," I said. "Before Kestrel was a dirty word."

"It's a good tune."

"It's a true one. Those aren't always the same thing, but tonight they are."

Below us the water lapped at the pews, patient, indifferent, carrying the whole drowned world past the windows. Somewhere out in that brown darkness were men with guns looking for us—Ossler with his polite voice, Callum Vane with his ledgers, whoever else the chest had flushed into the rain. I knew that. Quentin knew that. We were two fugitives standing in a choir loft, and for one evening we let ourselves believe the flood had closed the roads behind us and ahead of us alike, and that this borrowed hour of porridge and bad singing was a life we might somehow keep.

Then the bells moved.

Not rang—moved. A single low toll, muffled, as if the bell had been touched from outside. The singing faltered. Edran Voss appeared at the loft rail, and his face in the candlelight had gone the color of the paper he'd spent his life guarding.

"That bell hasn't swung in sixty years," he said. "It's wired to the steeple frame. For it to move, something has to hit the tower—or climb it."

I was at the window before he finished, Quentin beside me, both of us looking down into the rain-hammered dark at the black water surrounding the church on every side.

A lantern answered us. One light, far out on the flood, bobbing in a boat that made no sound under the rain—and then a second lantern beside it, and a third, spaced evenly, the way searchers space themselves when they are being paid by the hour and have all night.

Quentin's hand found mine in the dark. Neither of us said anything. There was nothing left to say that the lanterns hadn't already said.

They had found the church.
