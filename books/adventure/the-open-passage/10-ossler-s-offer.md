## 10. Ossler's Offer

There's a particular kind of silence that precedes bad news on a river. The birds go quiet first, then the water flattens out like a held breath, and if you've spent enough years listening, you learn to feel it in your teeth before your ears confirm it. I was feeling it the evening Ossler found me alone at the Corrin Landing fuel dock, which is why I wasn't surprised when I turned around and there he was, leaning against a mooring post like a man waiting for a bus he knew would be late.

Mara had gone up the slope to the trading post to barter for fuel and tinned food, which meant I had maybe twenty minutes of guaranteed solitude. Ossler had clearly done his arithmetic on that too.

"Quentin Delacroix." He said my name the way a dentist says *this might sting a little*. "You're a hard man to find. Or rather, you're an easy man to find who keeps being somewhere else when we arrive. There's a difference, and the difference costs money."

"Ossler." I kept my hands where he could see them and my voice at the register of a man discussing weather. "You're a long way from the Vanes' office."

"I'm exactly where the Vanes' office needs me to be." He smiled. Ossler's smile was the worst thing about him, and everything else about him was already bad—soft voice, clean shirt that never stayed clean in the monsoon, the patient eyes of a man who'd once watched a drowning for a full hour out of professional interest. "You've made my job difficult, Quentin. I want you to know I take that personally. Not angrily. Personally. There's a difference."

"There it is again. You and your differences."

"Differences matter." He pushed off the post and walked a slow half-circle around me, looking at the ferry with the appraising squint of a man pricing a coffin. "Nice boat. Not yours, though. Belongs to the Ives woman, doesn't it? Mara Ives. Sister of the late Tomas Ives. Ferry pilot, smuggler of small goods, keeper of a fuel ledger at three different docks under three different names. Resourceful woman." He let that sit in the wet air between us. "My brother-in-trade Callum thinks you've hidden something valuable on this boat. I told him no. I told him Quentin Delacroix is a drowning man, and drowning men don't carry treasure, they carry ballast."

"And what did Callum say?"

"Callum said, *then why is he running?* And I had no good answer for that, Quentin. I still don't. So I'm here to give you one. Or rather, to offer you the chance to give yourself one."

I leaned against the fuel pump and tried to look bored, which is difficult when your heart is doing the drum solo of your life. The chest was under the tarp in the forward hold, wrapped in oilcloth, wedged under a false floor panel Mara had built for contraband years ago. I knew that. Ossler didn't know that. The entire architecture of the next five minutes depended on the distance between those two facts.

"Let's skip the theater," I said. "What's the offer?"

Ossler beamed like a proud teacher. "The offer is this. The Vanes forgive the debt. All of it. The boat loan, the interest, the penalties, the little extra Callum tacks on for the pleasure of the arithmetic—gone. Clean ledger. You walk away a free man with your hands empty and your skin intact. In exchange, you hand over whatever it is you pulled out of the Serevan River three nights before the flood took your boat. The iron chest. Yes, Quentin, we know about the chest. A fisherman on the lower bend saw you winch it aboard and remembered it because he'd never seen a man look at a box the way you looked at that one."

So much for the false floor. So much for the architecture. I kept my face still through sheer spite.

"Here's the thing," I said. "The chest is full of wet paper and river rot. I opened it. There's nothing in it worth a debt."

"Then the trade costs you nothing," Ossler said pleasantly, "and you'd be a fool to refuse."

And there it was, the trap inside the offer, laid out as neat as a snare line. If the chest was worthless, I should give it up for a clean ledger. If I refused to give it up, it wasn't worthless, and Ossler would know it, and Callum would know it, and the price would stop being money. He'd built the offer so that every exit led deeper in. You had to admire it, the way you admire a well-set leg trap: from a distance, with someone else's leg in it.

"Let me think about it," I said.

"Of course." He checked a pocket watch that probably cost more than my boat had. "Take until dawn. But Quentin, let me be clear about the alternative, because I'd hate for us to have a misunderstanding born of my failure to communicate." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped into the register he probably used on his own children, if he had children, which I sincerely hoped he didn't. "If you don't hand it over, we don't come for you first. We come for the woman. Mara Ives has a boat, a route, a sister in Corvane, and a habit of stopping at the same three fuel docks. She's findable in a way that you, with your talent for being elsewhere, are not. The Vanes don't hurt people as a rule. It's bad for the brand. But a rule is just a price that hasn't been met yet, and you, my friend, are about to set the price."

I looked at him and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that he meant every word and would enjoy none of it, and that this made him more dangerous than any man who enjoyed it. Men who enjoy violence can be baited. Men who file it under *business* cannot.

"You'd kill a ferry pilot over some rotten papers," I said.

"We wouldn't kill anyone." He said it gently, correcting a child's arithmetic. "We'd make an inquiry. She'd fail to answer it. The river does terrible things this time of year, and rivers don't leave notes." He tucked the watch away. "Dawn, Quentin. I'll be at the bend below the landing. You can bring the chest, or you can bring an apology, but bring one of them."

He walked up the slope toward the trading post, and I watched him go, and it took everything I had not to shout after him, not to run him into the water, not to do any of the hundred stupid things that would have ended it there with Mara's name already on his list. Instead I stood at the fuel pump in the rain and shook, quietly, like a man with a fever, until the shaking stopped and something colder took its place.

* * *

Mara came back down the slope with a sack of tinned food and a look on her face that told me she'd already seen him. Of course she had. Ossler didn't travel anywhere without being seen; that was half his method.

"He was in the trading post," she said, dropping the sack on the deck. "Buying nothing. Talking to the owner about river levels. He asked about me by name, Quentin. He asked what time I usually take on fuel."

"He's giving us until dawn."

"Until dawn for what?"

I told her. All of it. The offer, the clean ledger, the fisherman on the lower bend, and the part about her—the part I made myself say out loud even though saying it made it more real, the way writing a name in a ledger makes a death more real. She listened without interrupting, which was worse than if she'd screamed. When I finished she looked at the tarp over the forward hold for a long moment.

"He's right about one thing," she said. "I am findable. Tomas used to say I had a homing instinct for trouble." Her jaw tightened around her brother's name, and I watched her decide, right in front of me, not to let it bend her. "So we don't run. Running confirms the chest is worth the debt. We do the opposite."

"The opposite of running is standing still. Standing still is dying."

"The opposite of running," she said, "is lying. You've never lied to them, have you? In all these years with the Vanes. You've paid late, you've begged, you've hidden, but you've always told them the truth, because that's the deal a drowning man makes—he keeps his honesty because it's the last thing he owns." She turned and looked at me, rain running off her chin. "They're counting on that. Ossler just built his whole offer on the assumption that Quentin Delacroix is too honest to lie well."

"And if he's right?"

"Then we're dead by Thursday." She said it the way she said *storm's coming*—flat, factual, already planning around it. "So don't be honest. Be something else for one night. You can go back to being yourself when we're out of the delta."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low and the rain ticking on the glass and rehearsed, and this is the humiliating truth of it, I rehearsed a lie the way I used to rehearse speeches I never gave, mouthing the words until they stopped tasting like words and started tasting like fact. The chest was already gone. That was the spine of it. I'd looked inside at Corrin Landing, seen nothing but ruined paper, and dumped it in the flood channel two nights back, weighted with chain. Let the river have it. The river takes everything eventually; it was the one lie this delta would cosign.

The trouble with the lie wasn't its plausibility. The trouble was that to tell it I had to become, for the length of one conversation, a man who would throw away the only thing keeping a hundred dead people from staying dead. I had to wear that man's face. And the thing nobody warns you about wearing a face is that some of them don't come off.

Dawn came up gray and dripping. I left Mara with the ferry at the landing—she'd argued to come, and I'd argued harder, because if this went wrong it needed to go wrong on me alone—and took the skiff down to the bend below. Ossler was there, of course. He was sitting in a covered launch with a thermos, and he poured a second cup before I'd even tied off, which was its own kind of message: *I knew what you'd choose before you did.*

"No chest," I said, before he could open his mouth.

"No chest," he repeated, tasting it.

"I opened it the night I pulled it out. Papers, waterlogged, mostly pulp. Names, dates, stamps—old stamps, dead-country stamps. I read enough to know it was history, and history doesn't pay interest." I shrugged, and I want it recorded that the shrug didn't shake. "So I weighted it with chain and dropped it in the flood channel past the second marker buoy. You want it, dredge for it. The Vanes have the equipment."

He looked at me for a long time. The rain came down between us in gray ropes. Somewhere upstream a barge horn moaned, low and patient, like the delta itself clearing its throat before it spoke.

"You dumped it," he said. "The one thing standing between you and a clean ledger, and you dumped it in a river."

"I'm a drowning man, Ossler. You said it yourself. Drowning men throw away ballast."

Something moved behind his eyes—not belief, not quite, but the absence of a reason to disbelieve, which in his profession is the same currency. He'd built his trap on my honesty, and I'd handed him a lie shaped exactly like the truth, with a location, a method, a chain. Every detail a man invents is a detail a man has to believe.

"Callum won't like it," he said at last.

"Callum doesn't like anything. Callum likes ledgers, and a ledger with a sunk chest on it balances the same as one with a paid debt."

The ghost of a smile crossed his face and died there. He poured the second cup of coffee out over the side—an odd, wasteful gesture, almost ceremonial—and stood. "I'll report what you told me. Word for word. Whether Callum believes it is above my station." He stepped down into his launch, then paused, one hand on the rail, and gave me the smile again, the dentist's smile. "For what it's worth, Quentin, I hope you're lying. It would mean there's still a version of you worth collecting."

He pulled away into the rain, and I sat in the skiff and watched him go and waited for the relief to come.

It didn't. What came instead was quieter and much more alarming: I realized I hadn't lied about the flood channel by accident. I'd chosen it because it was deep, because it was fast, because no dredge would ever find anything there. I'd chosen it the way a man chooses a grave. And I'd done it smoothly, instantly, without the old familiar tax of conscience coming due. Somewhere between the fuel pump and the bend, Quentin Delacroix—the man who always told the Vanes the truth, the last honest debtor in the delta—had quietly died and been replaced by someone who could do this. Someone who could look a patient man in the eye and hand him a fiction with a chain weight on it and feel nothing but the rain.

I rowed back to the landing with my hands aching from the cold and my head strangely clear. Mara was waiting on the ferry deck, and she read the answer off my face before I said a word.

"He believed you?"

"He believed me enough."

"Then we move now," she said, already untying the forward lines. "While he's reporting. While Callum's deciding whether to believe him. There's a window, and windows on this river close fast."

I took the wheel. The engine caught on the second try, that old diesel cough that had been the sound of my whole adult life, and we pulled out of Corrin Landing into the gray morning with the chest still under the false floor and a dead man's name in my mouth where my honesty used to be.

Mara stood beside me at the wheel, watching the bend where Ossler's launch had disappeared.

"Where to?" I asked.

"Upstream," she said. "Past Kilometer Nine." And then, quieter, almost to herself: "We're going to need a coffin."

I looked at her. She was already studying the river, reading the rain on the water like a chart, and she didn't explain, and I understood that on Mara Ives's boat, explanations were cargo you loaded only when you needed them.

The window was open. The river was rising. I put the wheel over and ran for it.
