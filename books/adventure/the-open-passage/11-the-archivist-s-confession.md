## 11. The Archivist's Confession

The drowned church had become a village in miniature by the second night. Its nave flooded to the third pew, its bell tower dry as a granary, and between those two extremes the stranded travelers of the Serevan delta had arranged themselves with the instinctive territorialism of cats. A fisherman's family occupied the vestry. Two traders who'd lost their barge played cards on the altar rail and cheated each other cheerfully. And in the corner near the collapsed confessional, wrapped in a blanket that might once have been white, sat an old man nobody had spoken to, watching Quentin's chest the way a starving man watches bread.

I'd noticed him the first evening. Not because he was remarkable—half the delta was old men wrapped in blankets—but because he never looked away from it. Everyone else looked at the chest too, but they looked the way people look at other people's luggage: idle, incurious, already forgetting. This man looked like he was reading something written on the iron.

"Your friend has been staring at our cargo for two days," I said to Quentin, low, while we pretended to sort rope.

"I noticed."

"He's not staring at it like it's valuable. He's staring at it like it's guilty."

Quentin tied off a knot that didn't need tying. "Maybe he's just bored."

"Nobody's bored in a flood, Quentin. Boredom is a luxury of dry land."

He grunted, which was his way of agreeing without spending anything on it. We'd reached a strange equilibrium since the coffin incident at Kilometer Nine—since I'd found my brother's name in the margin of a butcher's ledger and made him tell me everything. Equilibrium wasn't trust. Trust was a currency I'd stopped carrying after Tomas died. But we were rowing the same boat now, literally, and I'd learned you could know a man's weight before you knew his heart.

The old man solved the problem for us that night.

Rain hammered the roof tiles loose from their mortar, and in the gaps between thunderclaps the church went quiet enough to hear the water rising against the pilings. Most of the refugees slept. I sat up keeping watch out of habit—you sleep light when your brother's killer might be looking for you—and I saw him rise, slow as smoke, and cross the flooded nave in his bare feet, wading thigh-deep through black water toward where Quentin slept with one arm flung over the chest like a jealous lover.

I was on him before Quentin woke. Knife out, flat against his throat, my voice pitched under the storm. "That's far enough."

He didn't flinch. That alone should have told me something. Old men flinch; they've earned the reflex. This one just turned his head carefully, the way you do when a blade is involved, and looked at me with eyes gone pale as river fog.

"You're Mara Ives," he said.

My knife didn't move. "Who told you that?"

"No one. You're the ferry pilot who runs the upper delta. Red hull, engine coughs on the left bank. Your brother was Tomas Ives." He said the name gently, the way you set down something breakable. "I signed the paper that has his name in the margin."

* * *

Quentin woke, naturally, at the worst possible moment, and came up swinging a boat hook until I called him off. Then the three of us retreated to the bell tower—the only dry room left—and the old man introduced himself as Emeric Saul, formerly senior archivist of the Republic of Kestrel, which had ceased to exist eleven years ago along with most of its records.

"You helped bury them," Quentin said. It wasn't a question. He'd heard enough of the story on the stairs to put the shape together. "The documents. The ones in my chest."

"I helped destroy them. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point." Saul lowered himself onto an overturned crate with the care of a man whose joints had opinions. "When the Republic fell, there was an order. Total records purge. Everything from the war years—courts-martial, field reports, supply requisitions that didn't add up, all of it. Burned in the courtyard of the Hall of Records over six days. I know because I fed the fire for four of them."

The rain filled the silence. Below us, someone's baby started crying and was hushed.

"But you're alive," I said, "and so are some of the papers."

"Some of the papers are alive, yes." He almost smiled. "A fire that size needs tending day and night. And a man feeding it begins to notice things. Which crates burn hottest. Which ones the officers stand closest to. I was sixty-one years old and I had spent thirty years believing that paper was sacred—that a record kept was a promise made. So on the fifth night, when they brought up the last convoy from the eastern garrisons, I did what any coward does." He spread his hands. "I hid the best of it and lied about the rest."

"The eastern garrisons," Quentin said quietly. "Kestrel Province."

"Sector Nine. The villages along the Vosk corridor." Saul looked at me then, and I made myself hold it. "You've read them. You know what General Vosk ordered, and you know who countersigned."

"I know," I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "Tomas Ives. Executed at Ferrow's Bend, listed as a deserter. He was nineteen and he'd gone back for a wounded friend."

"Yes." The old man's eyes closed briefly. "I've read every page I saved, Ms. Ives. More times than I could bear. I know your brother's file better than I know my own children's faces, and I have no children, so that tells you something about how I've spent the last decade."

"Why now?" Quentin asked. He'd gone still in the way he did when he was doing arithmetic he didn't like. "Ten years, and you're sitting in a flooded church. Why hasn't this surfaced before?"

"Because paper without a courier is just kindling waiting to happen." Saul's voice hardened. "Twice I tried. Once through a journalist in Corvane—he took copies and vanished, and three weeks later his apartment burned with him in it. Once through a tribunal clerk I thought I could trust. She reported me instead. I spent two years being very careful and very quiet, and I learned the lesson you learn eventually: the record doesn't need a keeper. It needs a runner. Someone fast enough and desperate enough and angry enough to get it where it's going, and stubborn enough not to sell it on the way."

He looked at Quentin when he said the last part, and Quentin had the grace to look uncomfortable, because we both knew exactly how close that particular knife had come.

"And us?" I said. "Why hand it to strangers in a church?"

"Because you're not strangers. You're the only two people in this delta with a reason to see it through. He owes money to men who'll kill him for less than this chest holds. You—" he nodded at me "—have a name in the margin. Desperation and grief make honest couriers. Greed makes unreliable ones, and idealism makes dead ones. You're the rare combination that might actually arrive."

"That's the most insulting compliment I've ever received," Quentin said.

"Wait until you hear the route."

* * *

Saul unrolled a map on the crate—a real map, waxed canvas, older than the Republic—and weighted the corners with shell casings. His finger traced the river north through the delta channels, past Kilometer Nine, past the drowned farmlands, all territory we'd already survived once.

"To here, you know the water better than I do. But past the confluence, the game changes." His finger stopped at a bend marked with a faded red triangle. "Corvane sits at the head of navigation, forty kilometers up the Vane. The international tribunal has been sitting there for three years, hearing testimony, taking evidence. They want Kestrel documents—they've publicly requested them twice. What they lack is anyone willing to carry them, because everyone who knows the route knows the problem."

"The loyalists," I said.

"The general is dead, but the general's army isn't. Call them veterans, call them the Old Guard, call them what they are—which is men who would hang rather than let Sector Nine become a word again. They control the last stretch of the Vane from the Red Bend to the city docks. Checkpoints on the water, paid informants in every fuel yard, and a patrol fleet that flies no flag because flags invite questions." He tapped the red triangle. "Every pilot who's run cargo to Corvane in the last five years has been searched. Some have been searched twice. One was searched so thoroughly they never found enough of him to search."

"So we don't take the Vane," I said.

"There is no other way to Corvane. There's road, but the roads are worse than the river—roadblocks, and no bribes large enough. No." Saul leaned forward, and in the lamplight his face had changed, the archivist surfacing through the old man. "But there is a way to take the Vane that they don't watch, because it isn't navigable. Officially."

Quentin made a sound that was half laugh, half groan. "There it is."

"The Open Passage," Saul said. "The flood channel behind Sallow Point. Every chart marks it as dead water—sandbars, submerged fence lines, a channel that shifts with every season's silt. In a normal year, it is dead water, and the loyalists know it, which is why they don't post a single man there. But this is not a normal year." He looked up at the rain hammering the tower. "This is the highest water in forty years. High enough that the Passage runs deep—if you know where the channel lies. And the channel is marked."

"Marked how?" Quentin asked.

"By me. Twenty-two years ago, before the purge, before any of this, I surveyed that stretch for the Republic's hydrographic office. I kept my field notes. They're in Corvane already—I sent them ahead years ago, to a woman named Odile Ferrant who keeps a chandlery on the tribunal quay. She's expecting a delivery she gave up hoping for." He met Quentin's eyes. "You'd be finishing my survey. The last leg of it. Thirty years late."

Quentin stared at the map for a long moment. I watched him do the thing he does—weighing the current against the debt, the debt against the odds, always arithmetic, always looking for the exit priced in something he could afford. I'd seen him price everything in our weeks together: fuel, risk, loyalty, me. But when he finally spoke, the arithmetic wasn't in his voice.

"What happens to you?" he asked Saul. "If we take it and go?"

The old man waved a hand at the flooded church, the stranded travelers, the whole sodden world. "I'm seventy-three, Mr. Delacroix. I've been dead since the fifth night of the burning; I've just been slow about confirming it. If they come for me, they'll find an old man who fed a fire for four days and remembers nothing else. Let them. The papers were never mine. I was only ever their custodian, and a custodian's job ends at delivery." He gathered the map, rolled it, and held it out to me. "Take it. Take the chest. Take my confession too, if you want it—I wrote it out years ago, sealed it, and it's been waiting in a drawer for a courier longer than either of you have been alive."

I took the map. It was heavier than paper should be, or maybe that was everything else it carried.

"One more thing," Saul said as we turned to go. "The loyalists' commander on the lower Vane is Dain Vosk. The general's son. If he takes you, he won't kill you quickly, and he won't kill you for orders. He'll kill you for the lie—because the lie is the only father he has left. Understand that before you meet him. You're not carrying documents anymore. You're carrying the truth about a man's father, and men forgive theft faster than they forgive that."

We climbed down from the tower into the smell of wet stone and lamp oil. Quentin stood a moment at the edge of the flooded nave, looking at the chest where it sat on the pew above the waterline, and I saw something settle in his face—not peace, nothing so cheap, but direction. A man who'd been running in circles finds a bearing.

"We leave at first light," he said. "Before the collectors start asking questions in the camps downstream."

"Through the Open Passage," I said.

"If the charts are right."

"They're not right," I said. "They're twenty-two years old and the river's moved everything. But I grew up reading water, Quentin. Between Saul's notes and my eyes, we'll find the channel."

He looked at me then—really looked, the way he rarely let himself—and whatever he saw in my face made him nod slowly.

Outside, the storm had shifted, and the wind coming off the water carried a new sound underneath the rain: engines. Far off, downstream, more than one, moving against the current toward the church, toward the stranded travelers, toward us.

Quentin's hand found the chest's handle.

"They're early," I said.
