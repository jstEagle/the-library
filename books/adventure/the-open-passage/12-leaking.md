## 12. Leaking

The patrol boat came out of the rain like a rumor you'd been dreading all day finally confirmed.

I heard it before I saw it—a diesel throb out of rhythm with the storm, too steady to be the river, too close to be ignored. Mara heard it at the same moment. She didn't say anything. She just looked at me across the wheelhouse, and in the green wash of the radar screen her face had gone very still, the way water goes still right before something rises through it.

"How far?" she asked.

"Behind us. Maybe eight hundred meters."

"Then we're already late." She shoved the throttle forward and the *Kestrel's Mercy*—her name for the ferry, though the paint on the bow said something older and sadder—leaned into the current like a mule deciding to cooperate.

We'd been running since dawn, since Dain Vosk's men let us go with one hand and reached for their radios with the other. The archivist's route was supposed to be clean: back channels, dead sloughs, water so shallow the patrol boats couldn't follow. But the river had other ideas. The monsoon had spent three days eating the banks, and every shortcut the old man had drawn from memory forty years ago was now either open water or a wall of drowned trees.

The first shell hit the water twenty meters off our port side and threw up a column of brown spray that came down on the deck like a brief, furious second rain.

"They're not warning shots," I said.

"No," Mara said. "Warning shots cost money."

The second one didn't miss. It took the stern rail and a good piece of the transom with it, and the sound was unlike anything I'd ever heard—not an explosion exactly, more like the river itself being torn open. Shrapnel sang past the wheelhouse windows. One piece punched through the glass two feet from my head and buried itself in the chart table, quivering, hot enough that I could smell scorched paper.

Mara didn't flinch. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone who keeps records: she did not flinch. She just corrected for the sudden drag on the starboard side and said, in a voice as flat as a tide table, "She's taking water."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being shot at: afterward, everything gets very quiet inside your skull, and very loud outside it, and the two volumes never quite match again. The engine noise became enormous. The rain became enormous. My own heartbeat became enormous. And underneath all of it, small and clear as a coin dropped in a well, one thought kept ringing: *the chest is dry, the chest is dry, the chest is dry.*

I'd wrapped it in tarpaulin and oilcloth and wedged it under the forward berth, and when I got below decks the ferry was listing hard enough that walking felt like wading uphill. Water was coming through the transom damage in a gray-green tongue, curling across the deck plates, finding the low places the way water always does, the way it had been doing its patient work on my life for years without me once thinking to patch anything.

I hauled the chest up onto the chart table—the same table the shrapnel had opened like a tin of sardines—and held it there while the boat groaned around me. It was heavier than iron had any right to be. Or maybe it wasn't the chest. Maybe it was everything folded inside it: a general's signature, a village's worth of names, a brother I'd never met lying in those pages like a stone at the bottom of a clear pool.

Above me, Mara was shouting coordinates at the river as if the river were a crew member who'd gone soft.

"Starboard! Starboard, you beautiful idiot—" And then the world tilted, the keel kissed something solid with a long grinding shudder, and the *Kestrel's Mercy* settled into the mangroves with the finality of a woman sitting down after a long shift.

The engine died. The patrol boat's throb faded upstream, satisfied or called away—I'd never know which. The rain went on, indifferent, professional.

I climbed out onto the tilting deck and found Mara already in the shallows to her thighs, hauling on a mangrove root as if she meant to pull the whole delta back into alignment by hand.

"We're aground," I said, because apparently I had a gift for stating the obvious under fire.

"We're *parked*," she corrected. "Aground means we can't get off. Parked means we wait for the tide and fix what's broken." She looked up at me, rain streaming off her jaw, and for the first time since I'd known her she looked tired all the way down to the keel. "Quentin. Hand me the hammer before I start saying things I mean."

* * *

We ran her up as far as the mud would allow, into a pocket of mangrove thick enough to hide us from the channel, and waited for the tide to drop and give us her hull. By the time the water fell away and the damage stood exposed in the gray afternoon light, Mara could barely stand.

I noticed it the way you notice weather changing—not all at once, but in details. Her hands shook when she coiled the line. She misjudged a simple measurement twice. When she bent to look at the hole in the transom, she stayed bent a beat too long, as if the horizon had become optional.

"You're burning up," I said.

"I'm working."

"You're fevered. Sit down before you fall down."

"I've patched hulls in worse than this with a worse fever than this," she said, but her voice had gone thin and bright, the way voices do when they're stretched over something hollow. "It's a scratch. We plug it, we wait for high water, we run the last stretch at dawn."

"Mara."

"What?"

"Sit. Down."

And here's the thing about the two of us: we'd spent the entire voyage conducting negotiations in a language made entirely of not-saying. Not saying why she really came. Not saying why I really stayed. Not saying what it meant when she handed me coffee she'd made without being asked, or when I stood watch over her sleeping so she could have four uninterrupted hours. An entire grammar of silence, fluent in both directions.

So when she sat down on the wet deck plating and put her back against the wheelhouse bulkhead and closed her eyes—and when she *let* me take the hammer out of her hand without a fight—that silence said more than either of us had managed in ten days of talking.

"I'll do the patching," I said. "You do the supervising. Supervise loudly. It's what you're best at."

"That's not what I'm best at," she murmured, eyes still closed.

"No?"

"No." A ghost of her usual crooked smile. "But I'm not telling you what is. You'll get ideas."

* * *

Patching a hull by firelight in the middle of a flooded mangrove swamp is exactly as romantic as it sounds, which is to say not at all. I built a driftwood fire on a hummock of high ground where the smoke would scatter in the rain, and I worked by its light with a sheet of salvaged aluminum, a rubber gasket off an old hatch cover, and every fastener the ferry had left to give. The hole was ugly—a fist-sized tear with ragged petals of steel curled inward, one of which had come within a hand's width of the fuel line. Another centimeter and we wouldn't have been patching anything. We'd have been a story fishermen told each other.

I worked for three hours while the fever climbed in the woman behind me. I knew because she kept narrating.

"The rivet spacing's wrong," she said at some point, dreamily.

"It's fine."

"It's wrong. You're supposed to stagger them. Like bricks." A pause. "My father taught me that. He taught me everything about boats except how to leave them."

"Sounds like a good man."

"He was a careful man. Careful men die in shallow water, Quentin. Remember that." Another pause, longer. "Tomas wasn't careful. Tomas was brave. Look how that turned out."

I set down the rivet gun. The rain ticked against the tarp I'd rigged over her. In the firelight her face was flushed dark and damp, and her eyes when they opened were too bright, fixed on some middle distance where the dead keep their appointments.

"Mara. You should sleep."

"Can't. If I sleep, you'll put the rivets in straight lines and we'll sink in the Corvane channel and the documents will drown and it will all have been for nothing." She laughed, a small cracked sound. "Do you know what's funny? I don't even care anymore. About the tribunal. About justice. I used to think I wanted the general's name dragged through the mud. Now I just want—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "Now I just want to finish something. Just once. Just one thing finished."

"You will," I said. "We will."

"Don't promise me things. Promises are debts, and look what debts have done to you."

That landed harder than she probably intended, fever loosening her aim. I turned back to the hull and drove the next rivet, and the next, and let the work hold me together the way work always had. That was the arrangement I'd made with the world years ago: hands busy, heart locked, accounts balanced, nothing owed to anyone that couldn't be paid in cash or silence.

Except the arrangement had been leaking for days now, same as the hull. And I was tired of bailing.

* * *

Near midnight the fever broke its banks. She started shivering so hard the deck plates rattled, and when I put my hand on her forehead it was like touching a stove door. I got the blankets off the berths, all of them, and wrapped her, and built the fire higher, and dug through the medical kit for the antibiotics that were mostly expired and the fever tablets that were entirely gone.

"Quentin." Her voice had gone small. Young, almost.

"I'm here."

"If I die out here, don't let them bury me in the water. My mother's plot is on the hill above Ferrow Bend. Dry ground. She'd never forgive me."

"You're not going to die. You're going to wake up tomorrow insufferable, and you're going to tell me I riveted like an amateur, and we're going to make Corvane by Thursday."

"Thursday," she repeated, as if tasting the word. Then, barely audible: "Stay."

"I'm staying."

"No. I mean—" Her hand found my sleeve in the dark, grip fierce despite everything. "Everyone leaves. Everyone always leaves, they leave or they die, and I stopped keeping count because counting is worse. So stay. Even after. Even when the chest is delivered and the debt is paid and there's no reason left. Stay anyway."

The rain came down. The fire spat. Somewhere out in the black water a bird made a sound like a question nobody would answer.

And I understood, with the strange clarity that arrives only when everything else has burned away, that this was the moment. That tenderness, real tenderness, only ever gets spoken in the narrow window between disaster and safety, when pretending is more expensive than honesty. We'd both been waiting for safer ground. There was no safer ground. There was only this: a broken boat, a dying fire, a fever, a war we'd volunteered for, and a woman asking me to stay in the plainest words she had.

"Mara." I crouched down so we were eye to eye. "Ten days ago I was a man who owned nothing and owed everyone, and I liked it that way, because nothing owned can't be taken. Then I pulled a chest out of a river and met a woman who flies ferries like she's arguing with them, and somewhere between Kilometer Nine and tonight I stopped being that man. I don't know what I am now. But I know I'm not leaving. Not after Corvane. Not after whatever comes after Corvane. You're stuck with me, and I'm sorry, because I'm told I'm difficult."

Her eyes opened. Too bright, feverish, but focused on me now, wholly.

"You are difficult," she whispered.

"Extremely."

"And you owe money to terrible people."

"Not for much longer."

"And you talk too much when you're scared."

"I'm terrified right now, so yes."

Something happened to her mouth then—not quite a smile, something older and more fragile than smiling. She reached up with a shaking hand and touched my face, briefly, like checking whether I was real.

"Then stay," she said again. "Terrified. I don't care. Just stay."

"I'm staying."

She slept after that—really slept, the fever finally spending itself—and I sat beside her with my back against the wheelhouse and watched the fire and listened to the rain and felt, for the first time in longer than I could measure, no particular desire to be anywhere else in the world.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin, the rain down to a drizzle, the river running high and quiet as a held breath. Mara woke lucid, weak as a kitten, and immediately inspected my rivets.

"Straight lines," she said hoarsely. "Amateur."

"But watertight."

She pressed a palm flat against the patched hull, feeling along the seam, and grudgingly admitted it might hold. The tide was turning. In an hour we could float free and run the last stretch to Corvane, where a tribunal waited, where Ossler and Callum Vane waited, where Dain Vosk and his father's loyalists waited—all the debts of our journey converging on one dock like debris caught in an eddy.

Mara stood at the wheel, pale but upright, and studied the channel ahead. Then she frowned.

"Quentin." Her voice had changed. Flat. Careful. "Come look at this."

I joined her at the window. Out where the channel bent toward the open river, riding at anchor with no lights showing, sat a boat. Low, black-hulled, familiar in the way nightmares are familiar.

Not a patrol boat. Not loyalists.

A syndicate runner—and tied to its rail, unmistakable even at this distance, a figure watching us through glasses that caught the first pale light.

Callum Vane had found us first.
