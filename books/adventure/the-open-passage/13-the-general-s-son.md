## 13. The General's Son

They came for us at dawn, which I suppose was a courtesy. Most men who intend to ruin your life at least have the decency to do it after sunrise.

I'd been awake since four, listening to the river breathe against the hull and counting the sounds that didn't belong. Quentin slept the way he always did now—one arm flung over the chest like it might roll off the bunk and drown without him. I'd stopped teasing him about it somewhere around Kilometer Nine. You don't tease a man about the thing keeping him alive.

The sound that didn't belong was an engine idling too quietly, upstream, behind the screen of deadfall where the channel narrowed. Then a second engine matching its rhythm. Patrol boats don't idle in pairs unless they're waiting for permission.

"Quentin," I said.

He came up out of sleep like a man surfacing from deep water—no thrashing, just sudden total wakefulness, eyes open before his body finished moving. That's a debt collector's trick. I'd seen it in my brother too, near the end, when every noise had to be sorted into *harmless* or *run* before the brain fully booted.

"How many?" he asked.

"Two engines. Maybe three."

He looked at me, and I looked at him, and we both arrived at the same arithmetic at the same moment: no fuel to run, no current to run on, mangrove on both banks thick enough to swallow a boat but not two people carrying an iron chest that weighed as much as regret.

"We could swim for it," he said, without conviction.

"With the chest?"

"The chest can swim."

"It's iron, Quentin."

"Iron floats if you believe in it hard enough." He sat up, ran both hands through his hair, and for one unguarded second he looked twenty years old and terrified. Then the shutters came down and he was himself again—the man who lied to Ossler's face and discovered he was good at it. "How long do we have?"

The answer arrived before I could give it. A voice amplified through a loudhailer, flat and unhurried, speaking in the careful accent of someone educated on the coast: *"Ferry at the narrows. Kill your engine. This is not a request, but it is a polite one."*

Quentin and I exchanged a look. There is a particular kind of dread in being addressed politely by armed men. It means they've already decided everything except how much ceremony you get.

* * *

They boarded us six strong. I counted boots, rifles, and the quality of their uniforms—good cloth, well kept, none of the patchwork of militia or the shine of parade soldiers. These were professionals. The kind who'd been paid regularly for thirty years to remember something everyone else had been paid to forget.

Their commander came last, and I knew him before anyone said his name, because I'd grown up on the face of Aurel Vosk. It hung in every schoolhouse of my childhood, that square jaw and that medal-bright stare, the Hero of the Kestrel Frontier, the Father of the Reconciliation. His son had the same jaw. He had the same eyes, too, and that was the worst part—not cruelty in them, but something wearier. Something that had already read the ending of this story and shown up anyway.

"Dain Vosk," he said, by way of introduction, as though the name were a formality rather than a detonation.

"Mara Ives," I said. "This is Quentin Delacroix. We're cargo runners."

"Cargo runners." He let the word sit there like a coin he was deciding whether to bite. "With a Kestrel naval chart case lashed under your tarp, and a course that bends around every checkpoint between here and Corvane except the ones nobody mans. My father's maps are in my blood, Miss Ives. I know what a smuggling line looks like when it's drawn toward a tribunal."

Quentin, to his credit, tried. "We found the chest in flood debris. Salvage law says—"

"Salvage law says nothing, because there is no law out here, Mr. Delacroix. There is only what people agree not to look at." Dain Vosk turned to his men. "Bring it aboard my boat. Gently. If so much as a page tears, the man responsible will spend the rest of a very short career explaining why."

They carried the chest off our ferry like pallbearers, and Quentin watched them do it with his hands opening and closing at his sides, and I understood that for him this wasn't documents. This was ten days of Vane terror, a coffin at Kilometer Nine, a patched hull, all of it walking away on other men's shoulders.

"They'll burn them," he said quietly. Not to Vosk. To me. Or maybe to himself.

"Yes," Vosk said, overhearing, and there was no triumph in it. "That is generally the plan."

* * *

They took us to the larger patrol boat and gave us tea, which somehow made it worse. There is nothing more menacing than an enemy with good manners and a kettle. We sat in the wheelhouse while rain needled the windows and the river ran brown and swollen past the glass, and Dain Vosk broke the seal on the chest with a knife he handled the way a surgeon handles a blade he's used many times.

He read.

I want to tell you he read quickly, or angrily, or with the performative boredom of a man confirming what he'd always suspected. He didn't. He read slowly, page by page, drying each sheet against the stove before turning it, and the wheelhouse went so quiet I could hear the river working at the banks outside, patient as anything that intends to win.

I watched his face because I couldn't help it. I'd spent my whole life hating a signature—that looping, self-assured scrawl at the bottom of the order that sent my brother Tomas into a ditch outside a village whose name the maps had eaten—and I needed to see what that signature did to the man's own blood.

What it did was nothing. That was the horror of it. He reached the order itself—the one dated the ninth of October, the one with the general's name and the words *no witnesses to be retained* in his father's handwriting—and his expression didn't change at all.

"You knew," Quentin said.

Dain Vosk set the page down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off. "I have known since I was nineteen years old. I found a copy in my father's study, the night before his state funeral, while the whole nation wept on television." He poured himself tea he didn't drink. "I sat on the floor of that study until morning, holding papers that would have unmade every eulogy spoken that day. And then I put them back, and I closed the drawer, and I have been closing that drawer every day for twenty-two years."

"Why?" I asked. And then, because I couldn't stop myself: "He murdered my brother. Do you understand that? Not lost him. *Murdered* him, on paper, in advance, with a signature. And you sat on the floor and put it back in the drawer."

"Yes," he said.

Just that. No defense offered. And somehow that was more infuriating than any defense would have been.

"My father was a monster who believed he was a surgeon," he said finally, staring at the rain. "He amputated a village to save—what? A narrative. A country's good opinion of itself. And the country took the limb and thanked him for it, and built statues, and named schools after him, and taught my generation that grief has a correct version and an incorrect one." He looked up at me, and I hated that his eyes were red-rimmed. "You think I don't know what's in that ditch, Miss Ives? I've read the appendices. I know the names. I've known them longer than you've known your brother was on them."

"That's supposed to make this better?"

"No. It's supposed to make it honest." He stood, crossed to the window, and watched the water. "Here is what happens next, if you're foolish enough to hear it. Those pages go into the stove tonight. In the morning, your boat is returned to you, your fuel tank is full, and no one—no syndicate, no tribunal, no loyalist cell—ever hears that either of you existed. You go back to running cargo. You grow old. The country keeps its comfortable lie, and the lie keeps its peace, and the peace keeps forty thousand people from dying in a civil war that would start the week those pages surface. That is the trade. That is the only trade on the table."

"And if we refuse?"

He didn't turn around. "Then I follow my orders, and my orders come from men who loved my father without ever once reading him, and those men are not capable of the conversation we are having right now. They will not offer you tea."

* * *

They locked us in the aft cabin while they decided—or while he pretended they were deciding, though I suspect the decision had been made before we ever saw the engines idling behind the deadfall. Quentin paced. I sat on the bunk with my knees drawn up, doing the thing I do instead of crying, which is calculating.

"He's right about one thing," Quentin said. "If those pages surface wrong—if they surface through some syndicate middleman, some auction, some newspaper that gets bought out before the ink dries—it's worse than nothing. The Vanes would sell them to whoever pays to bury them. Half the tribunal delegates are on somebody's ledger already."

"So you're considering it."

"I'm considering everything. That's my whole personality now." He stopped pacing. "Mara. If burning them buys us out of this river alive—"

"Tomas was twenty-three," I said. "He drove a ferry, same as me. Same route, actually, before they closed it. The order lists him as *collateral, non-combatant, regrettable.* Regrettable. Like weather." I heard my own voice go flat and strange. "There's an old woman in the burned village who keeps vigil over names nobody official will say out loud. She thanked me for carrying them home. Home, Quentin. These pages are the only place my brother exists as anything other than a rounding error."

Quentin sat down beside me. For a while neither of us spoke, and the rain filled the silence the way rain does, generously, asking nothing.

"He already knew," Quentin said at last, wonderingly. "All these years. He's been carrying it longer than we have. You know what that makes him?"

"A prisoner," I said. "A well-dressed one, with a boat and a gun, but a prisoner. He's not offering us a deal. He's offering us his cell."

* * *

Near dusk, they brought us food and took away the tray, and then—unexpectedly—Dain Vosk came alone. He dismissed the guard at the door with a nod and stood in the corridor with his cap under his arm, looking suddenly less like a commander and more like a man at a graveside trying to remember whether he'd locked his house.

"I'm going to tell you something I have never said aloud," he said. "And then I'm going to pretend I didn't, and tomorrow we'll be enemies again, cleanly, the way it's supposed to work."

I waited.

"When I was a boy, my father took me fishing on the Serevan. Above the village. Before." His thumb moved along the cap's brim, back and forth. "He was different there. Patient. He taught me to read the water—the way it goes quiet over deep holes, the way debris tells you where the current's been. I loved him completely. That's the thing no one tells you about monsters. Someone loved them first, thoroughly, before they earned the title." He looked at me. "Your brother's name is Ilya Ives. Page fourteen. He was the last one they listed, and the clerk wrote *regrettable* in the margin, and someone above the clerk crossed it out and wrote *acceptable.* I've thought about that margin for twenty-two years. Whoever crossed it out—I've dreamed of finding him. Of asking him one question."

"What question?"

"*Was it?*" He said it very quietly. "Was it acceptable. Was any of it. And every year I wait to ask, the men who could answer die comfortably in their beds, one fewer witness, one smoother lie." He put his cap back on, and the commander reassembled around him piece by piece, like armor going on. "The stove is lit in the wheelhouse. At midnight, the pages burn, and by morning this never happened. Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless something goes wrong between now and midnight." For the first time since the loudhailer, something moved behind his eyes—something that might, in better light, in a braver man, have been called hope. "Rivers are unpredictable in monsoon season, Miss Ives. Even I can't account for everything. Neither, thankfully, can my guards."

He left. Quentin stared at the closed door, then at me.

"Did he just—"

"He told us the stove is lit and the guard rotation is imperfect," I said, "in the accent of a man describing the weather. Yes. I believe he did."

Quentin was already on his feet, testing the door hinge, checking the porthole latch, his mind visibly sprinting ahead through tide tables and guard posts. But I stayed sitting a moment longer, thinking about a boy on the Serevan learning to read water from a murderer's patient hands, and about drawers closing every day for twenty-two years, and about how the enemy, it turns out, is sometimes just another person standing inside the same lie, holding the door shut from the other side.

Outside, the rain was slackening. The river had begun its slow exhale toward slack tide.

Midnight was four hours away.
