## 14. Escape at Slack Tide

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before a tide turns. The water stops arguing with itself. The current forgets which way it was going. Everything holds its breath, and in that held breath, a man can do things he'll spend the rest of his life explaining.

I'd been counting on that silence for two days.

The loyalists kept us at the mouth of the Serpent's Cut, in a stilted boathouse that had once belonged to a fishing cooperative and now belonged to Dain Vosk by virtue of the fact that he had more guns than the fishermen did. Our ferry sat moored alongside their patrol boat like two dogs tied to the same post—one mangy but beloved, one sleek and mean. Mara was locked in the loft above with an armed boy of maybe nineteen watching her, and I was given the run of the dock, which told me everything about how they saw me: not a threat, just luggage that could walk.

Dain Vosk came down at dusk with the documents under his arm. He'd read them all, every page, twice through by lamplight while I watched him from across the room and tried to guess what a man looks like when he learns his father was a butcher. Answer: he looks like a man reading someone else's mail. He looks calm. That's the frightening part.

"You understand," he said, "that burning these is a mercy."

"To whom?"

"Everyone." He set them down on the table between us, squared the edges with a care that bordered on tenderness. "My father died believing he saved the Republic. There are forty thousand people in the interior who believe the same thing. You take this away, you don't give them justice. You give them a hole where their history used to be."

"Some holes need digging," I said. "That's generally how you find out what's buried."

He almost smiled. I'll grant him that—he was the first man in this whole rotten season who almost smiled at my jokes instead of reaching for a weapon. "You're not what I expected, Delacroix. Ossler described you as a rat."

"Ossler describes everyone as rats. It saves him having to learn names."

"He also said you'd sell anything to anyone." Dain looked at me then, really looked, the way Mara looked at weather coming over the horizon—measuring it, weighing whether to outrun it or stand into it. "So here is my offer, one last time. The chest burns tonight. You and the pilot walk away. No tribunal, no trial, no general's ghost hanging over your neck. You go back to being nobody, and nobody survives in this country."

"And if I say no?"

"Then tomorrow morning my men will find something to do with the pilot that will make the rest of your life very quiet and very sad."

I said nothing, because there was nothing true left to say out loud. Inside me, some small cold accountant ran the numbers—the way he always had, the way that had gotten me into debt with the Vanes in the first place—and for the first time in my life, the accountant came back with a figure I couldn't accept. Not because the math was wrong. Because I no longer cared about the math.

"I'll think about it," I said.

He nodded, satisfied, and took the chest up to the loft himself. Which was a mistake. But I'm getting ahead of myself.

* * *

Here's what I knew about tides, because Mara had drilled it into me somewhere around kilometer nine, back when she still spoke to me like I was a slow student rather than a partner: the delta didn't run on clocks, it ran on breaths. Twice a day the sea pushed in and the whole river system backed up, sluggish and deep. Twice a day it pulled out and the channels ran fast and shallow. And in between—maybe twenty minutes, sometimes less, sometimes barely ten—there was slack water. The pause between arguments. When the patrol boats rode easy on their lines and the sentries, who had spent all day fighting current, got drowsy in a way that felt earned.

Mara had taught me that. She'd also taught me, without ever saying it directly, that every boat has a belly. A soft place below the waterline where a hull can be opened with a hammer and a nail and closed again with a plug that will hold until exactly the moment you want it not to.

I waited until full dark, then made my round of the dock the way a worried prisoner paces, which is to say naturally, apologetically, hands visible. The guard on the patrol boat was a heavyset man named Brannoc who had spent the evening drinking rice wine with his relief and had reached the stage of drunkenness where he saluted the moon. I complimented his boots. He showed me his boots. Men love showing you their boots; it's the one vanity that costs nothing to feed. While he balanced on one leg like a heron, I dropped off the dock edge into the black water and hung there by one hand, breathing through my nose, feeling along the patrol boat's hull until I found the seam I wanted.

The water was warm as bathwater and thick with silt. Somewhere below me, fish were doing whatever fish do at night—probably holding their own tribunal. I worked the nail into the caulking seam, three inches, four, working blind, trusting Mara's geography of hulls the way I'd learned to trust her geography of rivers. Then I seated the plug—a carved hardwood dowel I'd whittled that afternoon from a fish-drying rack, which is the sort of detail that would have made the old Quentin proud, the Quentin who thought cleverness was a substitute for courage—and I climbed back up dripping, and Brannoc saluted me too, and I saluted him back, and we were friends forever, or for another hour.

The tide turned at half past two. I know because Mara had told me it would, and because when it did, the whole river seemed to exhale.

* * *

Getting up to the loft was the part I hadn't solved, right up until I realized I didn't have to solve it. All I had to do was make noise downstairs.

I knocked over the fuel drums. Not all of them—I'm reckless, not suicidal—just enough to send a metallic clatter rolling through the boathouse like a church bell falling downstairs. The young guard went down the ladder to look, as young guards have done since the invention of ladders, and I went up it past him like a man climbing toward his own funeral, which, given the odds, wasn't far wrong.

Mara was awake. Of course she was awake. She'd probably been awake for days, sleeping the way she did—in quarters, banking rest like a miser banks coin. She took one look at me, one look at the iron chest under Dain's cot, and one look at my face, and understood all three in order.

"Tide?" she whispered.

"Turning now."

"Their boat?"

"Belly's open. Plug's in."

She was already moving. She pulled the chest out, and I swear the thing weighed as much as a drowned man, but she lifted it like it owed her money, which in a sense it did—it owed her a brother. We got to the top of the ladder just as the young guard came back up, and I want to be honest here: I hit him. Open hand, heel of the palm, behind the ear, a trick a Vane collector named Ossler once demonstrated on me with my own face as the teaching aid. The boy folded like laundry. He was nineteen. I'd remember his face longer than I wanted to.

Below us, the boathouse erupted. Brannoc and his relief stumbled out onto the dock shouting questions at each other, and Dain's voice cut through them like a blade through wet canvas—*the loft, the loft*—and then we were moving, the two of us, chest between us, down the outside ladder on the river side while boots hammered up the inside stairs.

Our ferry was thirty feet away. Their patrol boat was between us and it.

We went over the patrol boat's deck instead of around it—across her stern, quick and quiet, two shadows and a box—and I felt the plug under my palm as we passed, smooth and waiting, and I gave it one hard pull. It came free with a sound like a cork leaving a bottle, small and absurd, the least dramatic sound of the entire war I seemed to be fighting. Water began to come in below the waterline, patient and total, the way water does.

Then we were on our own deck, and Mara had the engine turning over before I'd even cast off the line, and I cast off the line, and the slack tide caught us and carried us out sideways like a hand opening.

Shots came then. Of course they did. Muzzle flashes stitching the dark, rounds slapping the water around us, one punching through the cabin wall close enough to my head that I heard it arrive and depart in the same instant. Mara steered with her whole body, shoulders and wrists and teeth, threading the stilt pilings while I crouched behind the chest, which was, I reflected, the first time in its long dishonest life that the thing had actually protected anyone.

And then Dain Vosk stepped out onto the end of the dock with a rifle, alone, his men still scrambling, and put the muzzle on me across thirty feet of black water, and neither of us fired.

I had the revolver out—Ossler's own pistol, taken off him in a moment I still hadn't fully explained to myself—and I had him centered, and he had me centered, and the current was pulling us apart at walking speed, and for perhaps four seconds the entire delta consisted of two men deciding.

"Let it burn," he called over the water. "It's not too late. It's never too late."

"That's the problem," I called back. "It's always too late for somebody."

His finger tightened. Mine tightened. And then Mara put the ferry between us—not by accident, not by panic, but deliberately, swinging the bow across his line of fire the way she swung across everything, like the river was a sentence and she already knew how it ended—and the moment passed, and the dark took him, and I stood there with a loaded gun pointed at empty rain and discovered something about myself that I've been carrying ever since, heavier than any chest:

I couldn't do it.

Not wouldn't. Couldn't. My finger was on the trigger and my whole body refused to complete the transaction. Because Dain Vosk, standing there defending his father's lie with a rifle and tears he'd deny to his grave, was exactly the kind of man I'd been running from since the day the Vanes first smiled at me across a ledger. A man who'd decided that some lives were arithmetic. And if I pulled that trigger—if I solved him the way everyone in this story kept trying to solve everyone else—then the chest might reach Corvane, but I wouldn't. Something essential would stay behind on that dock, and it would never come back aboard.

"Should've shot him," Mara said later, when we were miles downstream and shaking.

"Yeah," I said. "Probably."

"But you didn't."

"No."

She was quiet a while, steering through the rain. Then: "Good."

* * *

Behind us, the patrol boat settled. I heard it before I saw it—a change in the sound of the river, a gurgle where a gurgle shouldn't be—and when I looked back, her running lights were lower in the water than lights should be, tilting, and men were shouting at each other about pumps. By dawn she'd be a navigational hazard and a very expensive lesson in hull maintenance. I felt no triumph. Sabotage, it turns out, feels mostly like plumbing.

But the slack tide was ending. The ebb was starting, and the ebb runs fast through the Cut, and fast water is a door closing behind you.

Mara found the channel by memory and nerve, the way she always did, calling depths from a childhood spent running these waters illegally and lovingly. Rain came in sideways. The chest sat between our feet like a third passenger, silent, judgmental. And somewhere behind us in the whiteout, I knew—knew without evidence, the way you know a storm is building before the barometer admits it—that Ossler was coming, and Callum Vane was coming, and the whole converging mess of debts and loyalties and lies was bending around the final bend of this river toward us like weather.

"We can't outrun all of them," Mara said. "Not in this boat. Not in this season."

"No," I agreed. "But there's one route they won't follow us down."

She looked at me. Rain ran off her chin. "You're talking about the Passage."

"I'm talking about the Passage."

Nobody survived the Open Passage. Every pilot on the delta said so, and pilots on the delta were rarely wrong about water, because being wrong about water was fatal and they were, most of them, still alive. It was a flood channel through the reef bar at the delta mouth, a mile of standing waves and shifting sand, open only at the highest flood tides of the worst years, and this was the worst year in living memory, which meant—for the first time in a generation—it might be open at all.

Mara stared at me for a long moment. Then she did something I hadn't seen her do since I'd known her: she laughed. Short, sharp, half horror, entirely real.

"My brother used to say the Passage only takes you if you're carrying something worth drowning for."

"What did he carry?"

"Nothing," she said. "That was the tragedy."

She put the wheel over. The ebb took us. Ahead, through the rain, the horizon was a bruise, and the storm behind the bruise was coming to meet us, and I gripped the rail of a dying ferry loaded with a dead nation's sins and felt, against all evidence and arithmetic, ready.

"Hold on to something," Mara said. "And Quentin—whatever happens in there—don't let go of the chest, and don't let go of me, and those are in reverse order of importance."

The river narrowed. The roar ahead grew teeth.

We ran for the Passage.
