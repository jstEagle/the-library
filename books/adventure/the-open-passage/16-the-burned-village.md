## 16. The Burned Village

There is a particular kind of silence that follows you after you've refused to kill a man. It's not relief and it's not regret. It's more like the quiet in the hold of a ship after cargo shifts — everything still aboard, just sitting differently now.

I'd had three days of it by the time Mara cut the engine and pointed at a bend in the river that wasn't on any chart I'd ever seen.

"That's not a village," I said. "That's a rumor."

"It's a village," she said. "It just got erased."

"Villages don't get erased. Villages get flooded, burned, abandoned, taxed into oblivion — my personal favorite — but they leave bones. Foundations. A church somebody sued somebody else over."

"Not this one." She killed the engine entirely and let us drift. The mangroves leaned over the water like conspirators. "The maps show open delta here. Survey maps, shipping maps, military maps. You know what that costs? Erasing a village isn't one lie. It's a hundred coordinated ones, kept up for thirty years."

I looked at the water. Somewhere under all that brown silk was a road, probably. A well. A schoolyard where children had learned their letters and then, on one recorded afternoon, stopped learning anything at all.

"And we're going in because—"

"Because the archivist said the survivors keep vigil," she said. "Because the documents have names in them, and names belong to people, not to iron boxes." She glanced at me. "And because you haven't slept since Dain Vosk put a gun in your hand and you chose to be someone else. You need to see what's at the end of all this running. Not the tribunal. This."

She was right, which annoyed me, because she usually was, and because being right is a luxury I'd spent most of my life avoiding. Being right about things gets you involved. Involvement gets you debt, and debt gets you men like Ossler standing on your deck with a smile like a filed-down hook.

But I didn't argue. I'd discovered somewhere between the drowned church and the mangrove shallows that arguing with Mara Ives was like arguing with weather. Technically possible. Deeply unproductive.

* * *

The channel in was barely a channel. Mara took us through on memory and instinct, poling where the ferry drew too much, and I stood at the bow holding the chest — I always held the chest; it had become less luggage than organ — and watched the delta open up around us like a held breath.

Then the trees ended, and there it was.

Not ruins. That's what I hadn't understood. Ruins are dramatic. Ruins have walls and stories. What lay along the bank was quieter than ruin: a scatter of stumps where houses had been, rows of them, visible only as shadows in the silt when the light came low. A bell frame with no bell. And everywhere, planted in the mud like crooked teeth, small wooden markers. Hundreds of them. Thousands, maybe, following the curve of the old street out of sight into the reeds.

Some were carved. Most were just wood, darkened by decades of rain, each one tied with a strip of cloth that had long since faded to the color of nothing.

"They replant every year," Mara said softly. "After flood season. The vigil-keepers. The markers rot, so they make new ones."

"From what? There's no forest here."

"There's a grove two kilometers inland. They planted it themselves. Trees for the dead, grown by the living." She brought us alongside a rickety dock that had no business holding anything heavier than a heron. "Come on. Bring the chest."

"You want me to carry it *in*?"

"I want them to see it," she said. "What happens after that is theirs to decide."

* * *

The vigil-keepers turned out to be eleven people, which seemed impossibly few until I did the arithmetic of what had happened here and decided that eleven was, in fact, a miracle wearing plain clothes.

They lived in three stilt houses at the edge of the marker field, and they lived the way people live who have made peace with being forgotten: carefully, communally, without waste. An old man named Sef mended nets that would never catch anything worth eating, because the ritual mattered more than the yield. Two middle-aged women kept a garden that the floods tried annually to murder. Children — actual children, grandchildren of survivors — ran the dock to meet us before we'd even tied off, because visitors were apparently rarer than honest government.

And then there was the old woman.

Mara had told me about her on the way in — Amma Vess, they called her, though nobody seemed to know if that was her name or an honorific or both. She'd been a girl of nine the night the general's Third Column came through. She had survived by floating downstream inside a hollowed log while her village burned behind her, and she had come back at nineteen, and she had never left again.

She was tiny. She was bent like a question mark. She walked toward us across the mud with a stick and the unhurried certainty of someone who has outlived everyone entitled to rush her.

Her eyes went past me. Past Mara. To the chest.

I want to tell you I felt noble in that moment. I want to report that I thought of justice, of history, of the tribunal waiting at Corvane like a scale waiting for its weights. But the truth is that my first thought was purely mercantile — the oldest reflex I own: *that box is worth more than everything I've ever owned, and this woman is looking at it like it's hers.*

Which, I realized as her hand came up and touched the iron, trembling, it was.

"You opened it," she said. Her voice was dry leaves and river wind.

"The lock surrendered," I said. "Under sustained negotiation."

Something moved across her face that might have been a smile in a younger country. "Thirty years I prayed someone would find it. Thirty years I told myself the river keeps what matters." She looked up at me, and her eyes were pale and fierce and utterly without self-pity. "You're the one the river sent."

"I'm the one the river wrecked," I said. "It sent me by accident."

"Child." She patted the chest twice, gently, the way you'd pat a sleeping animal. "Nothing comes here by accident. Not anymore. Everything else was taken from us by design. The river is the only thing left that acts freely." She turned to Mara, and something in her posture changed — softened, gathered, became ceremonial. "And you. You're carrying the papers north."

"To Corvane," Mara said. "To the tribunal."

Amma Vess nodded slowly. Then she did something I will remember on my deathbed, whatever deathbed I'm granted. She bowed. To Mara. A full, deliberate bow, deep enough that her stick wobbled and I stepped forward without thinking to steady her elbow.

She shook me off with the mild irritation of a queen declining assistance.

"Don't," she said. "This is the one bow I've had thirty years to practice. Let me land it."

* * *

They fed us, because of course they did. People who have lost everything feed you first and ask questions second; generosity is the last possession grief can't confiscate. We sat in the largest stilt house around a table that had been a door once, and ate fish and bitter greens and a root vegetable whose name I've already lost, and the eleven vigil-keepers studied us with the patient curiosity of lighthouse keepers watching a ship that might, finally, be coming home.

Sef, the net-mender, asked the questions. He was maybe seventy, with forearms like mooring rope and a face that had been handsome before the river finished its editing.

"The general," he said. "In the papers. Is he named?"

"He's named," Mara said quietly. "Ordered in his own hand. Dated. Countersigned."

The table went very still. One of the women closed her eyes. A boy — twelve, thirteen — looked from face to face, reading the room the way children do, learning the shape of a grief older than he was.

"And the officers?" Sef's voice didn't change at all, which is how I knew how much it cost him. "Are the officers listed?"

"Present and accounted for," Mara said. "All of them. Signatures."

Sef set down his spoon with exaggerated care, the way you handle something that might detonate. "My brother was taken that night. He was sixteen. They marched the men and boys to the riverbank and—" He stopped. Started again. "For thirty years, the official record says the village died in a munitions accident. Our own government sent a condolence delegation. They laid a wreath. On our graves. While we were standing in it."

"We know," Mara said.

"No," Sef said, and now his voice did change, fraying at one edge. "You know the *facts*. Everyone with ears knows the facts. What we have never had — what no one has ever had — is the *paper*. Proof with a seal on it. Because paper is the one weapon they can't outrun. Men die, tribunals dissolve, borders move. Paper stays." He looked at the chest, sitting against the wall where I'd set it, and I swear the whole room looked with him, thirty pairs of eyes pulling on it like tide. "That box is the first true sentence anyone has written about us in thirty years."

I felt something shift in my chest that had nothing to do with the fish.

Here's the confession I've been circling since the night I pried that lock open: for weeks, the chest had been currency to me. Leverage. A lottery ticket with a body count attached. Even after Ossler, even after Callum's velvet offer of partnership, some cellar of my mind kept the ledger open — *this could still buy your way clear.* Debt does that. It installs a second heart that beats only for escape.

But sitting in that house, watching Sef look at an iron box like it was his brother's headstone finally delivered, I understood that I had been carrying a grave. And you don't sell graves. You don't leverage them. You carry them where they're supposed to rest, and you set them down, and then — only then — you get to stand up straight.

* * *

At dusk, Amma Vess took us out among the markers.

She walked slow, and we matched her pace, and the light came low and gold across the silt so that the shadow-rows of vanished houses appeared like negatives of photographs. She stopped at a marker near the front — newer wood, fresh cloth, this year's planting — and rested her hand on it.

"My mother," she said. "Replanted forty-one times."

"Mara," she said then, and turned, and took both of Mara's hands in her small ones. "The name Ives. Your brother. Tomas Ives."

Mara went rigid. I watched it happen — watched the word go through her like a spear through water, no splash, just a straight line of damage.

"How do you know that name?"

"Because the survivors memorized the lists," Amma Vess said simply. "Before the burning, we kept parish records. After, we kept them in our heads. Every name taken that night. Every officer seen. Tomas Ives was a ferry pilot too — did you know? Ran supplies up this same river. He was caught carrying medicine to us. They executed him on the bank, and the officer who ordered it signed the order, and a copy reached a clerk in Kestrel's archive, and that clerk was brave for exactly one day, and hid the papers in a chest, and the chest went into the river, and the river—" she smiled, terrible and gentle, "—the river kept what matters."

Mara was crying. Silently, without changing expression, the way people cry who learned young that tears cost extra. "He was my brother," she said, uselessly, the way we say the biggest things.

"Yes," Amma Vess said. "And now you're the sister who carried the names home. Do you understand what that makes you, here? Not a courier. Not a witness." She pressed Mara's hands to her own forehead. "Kin. Whatever happens at Corvane — whatever they rule, however they hang it or bury it — you carried them home first. No tribunal can give that and none can take it away."

I looked away, because some moments aren't meant for witnesses, and also because my eyes were doing something unsanctioned.

* * *

We stayed the night. Of course we stayed the night; the river was rising and the vigil-keepers wouldn't hear of departure, and honestly neither would I. They gave us the good hammocks and the bad coffee, and sometime past midnight I woke to find the chest gone from beside me and my heart going off like an alarm—

—and then heard, outside, voices. Low. Many.

I came out onto the stoop and stopped.

Every keeper was awake. All eleven, plus the children, standing in the marker field by lamplight, and Amma Vess stood before the open chest, lifting documents out one at a time with cotton-gloved hands — where she'd gotten gloves I'll never know — while Sef read the names aloud into the dark. Not the officers' names. The dead. Page after page of the dead, read slowly, each name given its full weight, the crowd answering each one with a single word in the old dialect that I didn't know but understood completely.

*Present.*

They weren't mourning. I realized that after a while. Mourning was thirty years done. This was something else — a muster. A roll call across the gap. The abstract made flesh, the statistic made sacred, one voice at a time, until the field full of wooden markers was full of people again.

Mara stood at the edge with her arms crossed tight, listening for one name in particular. When Sef reached it — *Tomas Ives* — the answering word came from every throat including hers, cracked and fierce, and I saw her shoulders drop half an inch, like something she'd been carrying in them for years had finally been set down.

I stayed on the stoop. Some roll calls you attend; some you guard the door for. I'd spent my whole life guarding doors for other people's reckonings. It felt different, this time, knowing I was carrying the reckoning itself.

* * *

Near dawn, Amma Vess found me packing the chest. She watched me fold each document back into its oilcloth with the tenderness I'd have denied under torture, and she said, conversationally:

"You were going to sell it. Early on. Don't bother denying — I was a girl in a burned village, not a fool. Men like you always consider selling."

"Men like me," I said, "consider selling constantly. It's practically a hobby."

"What changed?"

I thought about lying to her, out of habit, and decided I'd retired from that. "A man put a gun in my hand," I said, "and I found out I'd rather lose everything than become the thing I've been running from. And then a woman read a name off a page and answered *present* to her own dead brother. Hard to price a box after that."

Amma Vess nodded as if I'd said something profound instead of merely accurate. "Debts," she said. "Everyone arrives here owing something. The river collects from all of us." She patted my arm with a hand like a dried leaf. "Pay yours forward, ferryman. It's the only currency the tribunal accepts."

We cast off midmorning, into a sky that had gone the color of bruised pewter, and the vigil-keepers stood on their dock — all eleven, plus children — and did not wave, because waving is for goodbyes and this was something closer to a dispatch. Behind us the marker field gleamed in the wet light, thousands of small wooden truths planted against the current.

Mara stood at the wheel, and I stood beside her, and the chest sat between us like a third crew member, and for the first time since I'd hauled it dripping out of the Serevan it weighed nothing at all.

Then Mara tilted her head, listening to a sound under the engine's drone, and her face changed.

"Quentin," she said. "That's thunder. And that's not thunder."

Behind the pewter sky, far upriver where Corvane waited, the horizon had gone a green-black I'd only seen once before — the day the Serevan ate my boat and handed me a fortune I never asked for.

The season's worst storm was coming down the valley to meet us.
