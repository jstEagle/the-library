## 17. Storm Front

**Quentin**

The barometer fell like a man jumping from a burning building.

I'd been watching it all morning—the needle sliding down the dial with the calm inevitability of a debt collector climbing your stairs—and by noon it had dropped so far so fast that even Mara, who treated weather as a personal acquaintance rather than a threat, stopped joking about it.

"That's not a storm," she said, standing in the ferry's wheelhouse with her arms crossed and her jaw set in a way I'd learned meant she was doing arithmetic she didn't like. "That's the storm. The one the old pilots used to name."

"Name it what?"

"Doesn't matter. Names are for things you expect to see again." She spat over the rail, an old delta habit that had somehow failed to offend me in weeks of close quarters. "We need to be through the bend at Kilometer Thirty-One before it lands, or we're going to be a story other pilots tell."

I looked at the sky. It had gone the color of wet slate, low and bruised, and out to the west there was a wall of gray that didn't look like cloud so much as like a decision the world had already made about us.

Behind us—maybe six kilometers back, maybe less—the river carried other decisions. Ossler's launch. Callum's armored barge. And somewhere between them, Dain Vosk and whatever remained of his father's loyalists, nursing their humiliation from the slack-tide escape and, I suspected, considerably more motivated than either of the Vane brothers, because Dain wasn't chasing money. He was chasing silence. Money you can count. Silence you have to kill for.

Three boats. One river narrowing toward a single bend. And us, in a patched hull with a shrapnel scar under the waterline that Mara swore would hold and I swore I could hear weeping every time we took a wave wrong.

"You're smiling," Mara said. "Why are you smiling? Nothing about this is smileable."

"I was just thinking that two months ago my biggest problem was a ledger with numbers in it."

"And now?"

"Now the numbers have names attached." I checked the chest where it sat lashed beneath the chart table, iron and stubborn and heavier than any object I'd ever owned. "Ossler. Callum. Dain Vosk. A general who's been dead twenty years and still has people willing to drown strangers for him."

"Sentimental," she said, but she put her hand on the throttle, and I saw her do the same arithmetic again, faster this time, because the first gust hit us then—a long shove of wind that laid the reeds flat along both banks and turned the river's surface to hammered tin.

The rain came not gradually but all at once, like a curtain dropping, and the world went white.

* * *

**Mara**

Here is what they don't tell you about whiteout rain on a river: it doesn't reduce visibility. It deletes it. The world becomes a sheet of paper someone spilled milk on, and you are expected to pilot a boat across it at speed because stopping is its own kind of dying.

I flew by sound. The slap of current off the left bank told me the channel bent hard to starboard ahead. The drumming on the cabin roof thickened where the overhanging figs began—that meant the narrows, which meant the wreck of the old steamer *Coriander* lay just past them, half-submerged these forty years, waiting with the patience of all wrecks for one more hull to add to its collection.

"Port," I said. "Hard port, then hold."

Quentin threw his weight against the wheel without asking why. That was the thing about him I'd stopped being surprised by and started depending on: he'd arrived at my dock a liar and a debtor, a man who'd learned that questions were liabilities, and somewhere upriver between a coffin and a drowned church he'd become the kind of man who moved when you said move and trusted you knew the shape of the dark.

"Talk to me," he shouted over the engine.

"There's a wreck on our right in thirty seconds. After that the channel splits. We take the left fork."

"Why?"

"Because the right fork is where anyone following us will go, because it's wider, and because wide feels safe to men who don't know this river." I felt the *Coriander* slide past us on the starboard side—I couldn't see it, but I heard the rain change note where it struck rusted iron instead of water, a duller voice, and I swear the old ship groaned, though that was probably the storm. "And because the left fork gets shallow enough that Ossler's launch has to slow down, and slow men think, and thinking men hesitate, and hesitation is the only weapon we've got that fires straight."

Through the wheelhouse glass there was nothing but white. Behind us, faint under the rain's roar, an engine note that didn't belong to us. Deeper, flatter, more patient. A big boat pushing a big wake.

Callum Vane hadn't slowed for anything in his life. I'd known that about him before I ever saw his barge; it was in how his collectors walked, in how the syndicate's interest rates were posted openly on the fuel docks like scripture. Callum believed the river belonged to him the way some men believe in God—completely, and without needing evidence.

"He's gaining," Quentin said.

"He's always gaining. Right up until the river asks him a question he can't answer." I wiped the glass with my sleeve, which accomplished nothing except giving my hands something to do that wasn't shaking. "There's a bend coming. Kilometer Thirty-One. After the bend the channel runs straight for four kilometers to the mouth of the Open Passage."

He went quiet. I felt him go quiet, the way you feel a change in ballast.

"We're not running the Passage," he said.

"No."

"Mara."

"Not in this. Not in a hull with a patch on it. Nobody runs the Passage in weather like this. Nobody runs the Passage at all, mostly." I kept my eyes forward, on the white nothing, reading it like a page. "But if the Passage is the only door left, I want to be standing in front of it when we decide."

Another gust hit us broadside and the ferry leaned, groaning, and for one long second the deck tilted toward the black water and I thought: *this is it, this is how the river takes its tithe,* and then she came back upright and we drove on.

In the back of my head, where I keep the things I don't say out loud, my brother's name sat where it always sat. Tomas. Twenty-two years old, standing in a line outside a schoolhouse in a village no map bothered with, while a general whose statue now stood in three provincial capitals gave an order that a clerk wrote down and a clerk sealed and a clerk hid in an iron chest that a drowning man with bad debts pulled out of a flooded river twenty years later.

The river keeps everything. My mother used to say that. She meant it as comfort. Lately it sounded more like a promise being kept at gunpoint.

"Bend," I said. "Hold on to something that loves you."

"What if nothing does?"

"Then hold on to me," I said, before I could stop myself, and then the bend took us and there was no time to be embarrassed, which may have been the plan all along.

* * *

**Quentin**

The bend nearly killed us. I'll describe it briefly because I've made a private vow never to describe it fully, even to myself.

The current at Kilometer Thirty-One ran against the wind, and where opposing forces meet on a river you get standing waves—gray walls of water that appear from nowhere and stand in the channel like unpaid debts. Mara threaded us through them the way a needle goes through cloth, which is to say impossibly, repeatedly, with a confidence I chose to believe was skill rather than luck. Something tore loose below decks and banged around for a full minute. The chest strained its lashings and I put my boot against it and held it there, and for the length of the bend the iron was the only solid thing in the universe.

Then we were through, and the channel straightened, and behind us the storm swallowed the bend whole.

"They made it," Mara said. She hadn't turned around. She didn't need to; she read engines the way priests read scripture. "All of them. Ossler's launch first—he's light, he'll ride the waves better. Then the barge. Then—" She paused.

"Dain."

"Dain's boat draws shallow. He'll cut inside the bend, through the flooded cane fields. He knows this stretch. His father's patrols ran it." Her knuckles were white on the wheel. "Which means in ten minutes there will be three sets of headlights behind us and one door ahead, and the door is the Passage."

Ahead, through a momentary thinning of the rain, I saw it—or rather I saw where it wasn't. The Open Passage announced itself as a gap in the mangrove wall, a slot of darker water between roots that looked, in the lightning flashes, like the ribs of something enormous. Every pilot on the delta had a story about it. None of the stories ended well. The channel shifted with every flood season; sandbars migrated overnight; the locals called it the Open Passage because it was open exactly once, in living memory, and the man who ran it bought his boat afterward with insurance money and never piloted again.

Four kilometers of straight channel between us and it. Four kilometers with three hunters closing from behind and the worst storm in a generation landing on top of all of us like a lid.

"Options," I said. "Go."

"Surrender the chest to Callum, live indebted forever. Give it to Dain, watch it burn, live short. Run the Passage and probably die honest." She glanced at me. "You forgot the fourth option."

"There's a fourth option?"

"You stop running. You turn this boat around, you put yourself between the chest and everyone chasing it, and you let them come to you." She said it evenly, testing me. "You'd lose. But you'd lose on your feet."

Lightning walked across the sky ahead of us, and in its light I saw something that stopped my breath: a boat, small and low, sitting in the mouth of the Passage itself. Riding at anchor. A single lantern swinging at its stern, patient as a toll gate.

Mara saw it too. Her whole body changed around the wheel.

"That's a pilot boat," she said slowly. "That's *Sela's* boat. Sela Rennick. She guides smugglers through the Passage—for a price."

"For what price?"

"Whatever she decides it is, at the moment you ask. That's the arrangement. That's why she's alive." Mara's jaw worked. "She's the only person who's run it more than once. If anyone can take us through in this—"

A shape came out of the rain behind us.

Not Ossler's launch. Not the barge. Low, shallow-drafted, running the flooded cane fields exactly as Mara had predicted, mud to the gunwales and a dozen loyalist rifles glinting under a tarp. Dain Vosk stood at the bow with the rain streaming off him, and even at two hundred meters I could see he wasn't looking at us.

He was looking at the lantern in the mouth of the Passage.

"He knows," I said. "He knows what we're going to do, and he's going to get there first."

Then the second shape came out of the rain—Ossler's launch, fast and mean, sliding down the straight channel with the storm at its back—and beyond it, vast and implacable, the running lights of Callum Vane's barge, three threads and a knot, all of us converging on a gap in the mangroves barely wide enough for one desperate hull.

Mara's hand found mine on the wheel. Rain hammered the roof like a verdict being read.

"Quentin," she said. "Decide."

* * *

**Mara**

Later—if there was a later—I would remember this moment as the quietest of my life. The storm was screaming, three boats were converging, and inside my own head there was a strange, clear stillness, the kind I'd only felt twice before: once jumping from a burning seaplane, once at my brother's funeral, when the priest's voice went on and on and I understood that the rest of my life had already started whether I agreed to it or not.

Quentin looked at the lantern in the Passage. He looked at Dain's boat cutting the corner. He looked at me.

"The chest," he said. "If we run it and make it—Corvane. The tribunal. Whatever happens after, happens."

"If we run it and don't make it—"

"Then it doesn't matter what happens after." He almost smiled. "That's the cleanest deal I've been offered all year."

Behind us, a rifle cracked—Dain's people firing at Ossler's launch, or Ossler's people firing at Dain, or everyone firing at the storm for daring to complicate their business. In whiteout rain, at range, on moving water, nobody hits anything. But the sound did what sounds do: it started the clock.

Sela Rennick's lantern swung once, twice, and then—a signal I recognized from the old days, from pilots who spoke in light because voices carried too far—three quick flashes. *Come. Now. Before I change my mind.*

Mara opened the throttle.

The ferry surged forward into the white, the patched hull groaning its objections, the chest lashed below like a heart in a rib cage, and the mouth of the Open Passage rose up out of the rain to meet us—dark water, pale roots, and on either bank, closing fast, the lights of every debt I had ever owed.

The last thing I saw of the open river was Callum Vane's barge, huge and unhurried, turning toward us with the patience of a man who believed the river belonged to him.

He was about to learn otherwise. So were we all.
