## 18. The Open Passage

There is a particular kind of silence that comes just before a man does something unforgivably stupid. I'd heard it twice before in my life—once before I signed the Vanes' ledger, and once before I told Ossler I didn't know what was in the chest. Both times the silence had been the sound of my own good sense packing its bags and leaving without me.

The third time came at the mouth of the Open Passage, with three boats behind us and the worst storm of the season chewing the horizon into pieces.

"You're not serious." Mara stood at the wheel beside me, rain streaming off her jaw, staring at the channel the way a man stares at his own grave marker. "Quentin. Look at it."

I looked at it. That was the problem.

The Open Passage wasn't a passage so much as a rumor the river told about itself—a two-kilometer throat where the Serevan split around a drowned ridge and reassembled itself on the other side through a maze of submerged rock that shifted with every flood season. The charts marked it with a notation I'd always found admirably honest: *unsurveyed — no safe transit*. The pilots called it the Meat Grinder when they called it anything at all, which was rarely, because pilots are superstitious people and superstition survives by not being spoken aloud.

Every year the river took someone there anyway—some drunk running from a wife, some smuggler running from a patrol—and every year the river kept them. The delta had a way of collecting debts. I would know. I owed several.

"Behind us," I said, "is Callum Vane, who wants to make an example of me. Somewhere behind him is Ossler, who made a promise about Mara that I intend to see broken. And somewhere in this soup is Dain Vosk, who has soldiers and a grudge and nothing left to lose but his father's name." I wiped rain out of my eyes and failed completely. "Ahead of us is a channel that kills everyone who tries it."

"So we're choosing between certain death and probable death."

"When you say it like that it sounds reckless."

"It sounds reckless because it *is* reckless."

"Mara." I turned to face her, and the ferry bucked under us, and I put a hand on the wheel to steady both of us, or maybe just her. "I've spent three years being careful. Careful got me a boat I don't own, a debt I can't pay, and a chest full of dead men's names that half a country wants burned. Careful is what they're counting on. Nobody—not Callum, not Ossler, not Dain Vosk with all his daddy's maps—nobody thinks we'll take the Open Passage. It's the one advantage we have left: we're crazy enough to be unpredictable."

She looked at me for a long moment. Rain ran between us like a curtain. Behind us, maybe four kilometers back, a searchlight stabbed through the murk and swept the water, patient as a lighthouse, hungry as everything else in this country.

"I've run it once," she said finally.

My stomach did something acrobatic. "You *what*?"

"Six years ago. My brother dared me. We were young and immortal and he'd had half a bottle of cane spirit." Her voice went flat in the way it always went flat when Tam's name came up, and I understood suddenly that she wasn't telling me a story. She was giving me a chart. "I remember every turn. Every rock. The river's higher now—it'll be different—but the bones underneath haven't moved."

"The bones are the part that kills you."

"The bones are the part I remember." She took the wheel from me. Her hands were shaking, I noticed. So were mine. There's a difference between fear and cowardice, and the difference is mostly whether your hands shake while you do the thing anyway. "Get forward. When I shout a number, throw your weight to the opposite rail. If I shout 'down,' you get flat and you pray to whatever's listening, because something will be coming over the bow."

"And if you shout nothing?"

"Then we're already dead and it won't matter."

I kissed her then. Quick, hard, tasting of rain and diesel and the iron tang of a woman who'd been awake for thirty hours. Not because either of us needed romance—we needed ballast and luck and about forty more horsepower—but because if those were the last thirty seconds of my life I was damned if I'd spend them not doing it.

"That," she said, "was a terrible idea. Now I'm thinking about it instead of the rocks."

"Good. You think better angry."

"I think better *alive*."

"Same thing, today."

I went forward. Behind us the searchlight swung wider, and I heard it even under the storm—the deep, ugly bellow of Callum Vane's armored launch, the one he used for collections on the big rivers, the one with the mounted gun that had ended two smuggling careers and one marriage, though not necessarily in that order. They'd seen us. Of course they'd seen us. We were the only idiots on the water.

Mara opened the throttle.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about running rapids in a monsoon: it doesn't feel like speed. It feels like the world ending in installments.

The first kilometer was almost kind. The channel narrowed, the current picked up, and the water went from brown chaos to brown chaos with opinions. Waves came at us sideways, stacked and steep, and the ferry climbed each one like a tired animal climbing stairs, hung at the top for a heartbeat that lasted a year, and fell.

"Two!" Mara shouted, and I threw myself against the port rail as she swung us hard starboard, and something pale slid past beneath the hull close enough to touch—a ridge of limestone wearing the flood like a hat, waiting politely to open us stem to stern.

"One!" Back to port. The wheel spun under her hands and she rode it, arms rigid, feet braced wide, hair plastered to her skull, and I want to tell you she looked afraid but she didn't. She looked *lit*. Like the storm had finally found the thing it had been looking for all season.

"Down!"

I flattened against the deck and the world went white. Water came over the bow in a single solid sheet, tons of it, and for a moment I was underwater on my own deck, eyes open in the brown, seeing nothing, feeling the hull shudder beneath me like a struck bell. Then the water drained and I was coughing and alive and the ferry was still moving, still pointed downriver, still ours.

"Tam hit that wave wrong," Mara called back, and her voice cracked down the middle. "He hit it wrong and I hit it right and I never forgave myself for the difference."

"You hit it right because he taught you!"

"I know!" she screamed, furious, alive, magnificent. "That's why it hurts! Three!"

I threw my weight starboard. The channel bent around a drowned church spire—I could see the cross below the surface, tilted, reaching up like a drowning man's hand—and beyond it the river simply *ended*, dropping away into a boil of white where the ridge crossed our path and the only way through was a gap no wider than the ferry itself, somewhere in all that violence, somewhere Mara's memory insisted existed.

"Where?" I shouted. "Mara, WHERE?"

"Left of the spire! Trust the foam!"

"Trust the WHAT?"

"Where the foam folds inward! The current marks the hole! Everything else is rock!"

I looked. The boil was a hundred meters of churning insanity, white on brown on white, and I will be honest with you: I saw nothing. No fold, no mark, no mercy. Just water trying to kill us in every direction at once.

But she'd run it once, drunk, at nineteen, with her brother laughing beside her. And she remembered. God help us, she remembered.

"Left of the spire," I said. "Foam folds inward. I'm with you."

She glanced back at me—one second, half a second, an eternity—and whatever she saw in my face must have been enough, because she nodded once and turned back to the wheel and said, quietly, under the roar, words I barely caught and will keep forever:

"Hold on to something that matters."

* * *

The gap, when we hit it, was exactly as wide as advertised, which is to say not wide enough.

The ferry went in sideways. I don't know how else to describe it—Mara fought the wheel and the current fought her and the compromise was a screaming diagonal slide between walls of standing water taller than the mast. Rock ground along the hull somewhere below, a long horrible grinding note like the river clearing its throat, and I felt it through the deck plates and into my teeth. Then a wave took us from behind and shoved, and the grind became a shriek became a scrape became—

Nothing. Open water. The far side.

We shot out of the throat of the Meat Grinder into the broad flooded plain beyond, and the sudden quiet of it—relative quiet, storm-quiet instead of rapid-quiet—rang in my ears like a slap. The engine was making a new sound, a sick irregular knocking, and smoke curled up from somewhere aft, and the port rail was bent like a paperclip, and we were through.

We were *through*.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. I stood up in the flooding cockpit, drenched half-drowned and laughing like a madman, and Mara looked back at me and started laughing too, wild and disbelieving, both of us howling at the sky like the storm owed us money and had just paid up.

Then I looked back the way we'd come.

The gap in the boil was already closing behind us, the channel swallowing its own secret. And on the far side, at the mouth of the passage, the searchlight stopped.

Callum Vane's launch sat dead in the water, lights sweeping, going nowhere. I watched the beam swing across the boil, across the spire, across the closing foam, and I imagined Callum standing at his rail with his expensive coat and his expensive gun, doing the arithmetic every pilot in the delta could have done for free: *no one survives the Open Passage, and no one worth chasing is worth following in.*

"They're turning," Mara said, watching their running lights pivot. "They'll go around. The long way through Kessel Bend—that's six hours in this weather, if they don't lose the channel in the dark."

"Six hours." I sagged against the rail. My legs had decided independently that they were finished, and I let them. "Six hours is a lifetime."

"It's enough." She killed the throttle to spare the engine and let us drift, and for a moment there was only rain on the roof and the knock of the wounded motor and the two of us breathing like landed fish. "Quentin."

"Mm."

"You trusted me. Back there. You didn't know what you were trusting—you couldn't have—and you did it anyway." She wasn't laughing anymore. The storm-light made her face hard to read, but her voice wasn't hard at all. "Nobody's ever done that. Not Tam, not anyone. Tam *knew* the passage. You just believed me."

"You remembered," I said. "That's better than knowing. Knowing goes stale. Remembering, you carry it with you."

She crossed the cockpit and put her forehead against mine, and we stayed like that while the rain came down and the river carried us toward Corvane and the tribunal docks and whatever waited for us there, which, knowing my luck, was probably a firing squad with excellent paperwork.

"We should check the hull," she murmured.

"In a minute."

"The engine's dying."

"In a minute."

"There are three armed men who want us dead and the worst storm in forty years directly astern of us."

"Mara." I closed my eyes. "In a minute."

She laughed again, softer this time, and kissed me, and for one full minute—which is a long time, on a sinking boat, in a dying storm, with the law and the syndicate and the ghost of a general all converging on the same stretch of dark water—for one full minute, neither of us thought about any of it.

Then the engine gave a final, theatrical knock and quit altogether, and the current began, gently, patiently, to carry us sideways toward a line of mangroves with the unmistakable silhouette of an anchored patrol boat showing lights beyond them.

Dain Vosk's colors.

Mara saw it the same moment I did. Our eyes met over the dead engine.

"Well," she said. "At least it's not the Vanes."

"At least," I agreed, "it's only the army."
