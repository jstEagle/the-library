## 19. Reckoning at Corvane

There is a particular kind of silence that comes after you've run a river that wants you dead, and it is not silence at all. It's your own blood in your ears, the tick of a cooling engine, the drip of the delta off every surface of a boat that should have been scrap metal two days ago. When we came around the last bend and saw the Corvane docks—actual docks, with actual people, cranes and customs flags and a tribunal banner snapping in the wind that had followed us all the way from the Open Passage—my first thought was not triumph.

My first thought was: *we look like drowned rats delivering state secrets, and no one here is going to believe a word of it.*

The *Kestrel's Mercy*—Mara Ives, you sentimental fool, you named the boat after the country that stopped existing—limped into the harbor listing to port, hull patched in three places with resin, canvas, and in one desperate spot, a flattened cooking pot. The storm was still behind us, a wall of green-black cloud chewing the horizon, and the harbor water was choppy as a dog's back. Quentin stood at the wheel with his hands wrapped in strips of his own shirt because he'd torn the skin off his palms on the winch line, and he was grinning.

I want to be clear about that. After everything—the checkpoints, the coffin, the shrapnel, Dain Vosk's men, the Open Passage itself, which I will never speak of again unless paid handsomely—he was *grinning*, teeth bared against the rain, like a man arriving at his own funeral in a good mood.

"Don't," I said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't look like that. We're not done. Look at the docks, Quentin. Count the boats."

He counted. I watched him count. His grin died by degrees, which was at least honest.

Because moored at the tribunal pier, riding easy in the swell like it had all the time in the world, was a long black launch with twin engines and no registration numbers, and standing on the dock beneath a borrowed umbrella was a broad man in a dry coat, hands folded, patient as Sunday.

Callum Vane had come to hear the testimony personally.

* * *

We tied off because there was nothing else to do. You don't run. I learned that from my brother, in a way—Tomas ran once, from a checkpoint at Kilometer Nine, and the running is what got him shot. Standing still gets you searched. Running gets you buried. Of the two, I've always preferred being searched.

Callum Vane did not point his gun at us right away. That was the courtesy of the man. He waited until we'd made fast, until the harbormaster's boys had scattered—smart boys—until it was just the five of us on the wet planks: me, Quentin, the chest between us like a third passenger, Callum under his umbrella, and Ossler, who had come up the dock behind his brother with the expression of a man attending a wedding he disapproves of.

"Miss Ives." Callum nodded to me like we were neighbors discussing drainage. "Mr. Delacroix. You're late. The tribunal adjourns at six."

"Traffic," Quentin said.

I have to hand it to him. His voice didn't shake. His hands did, but he kept them behind his back, and Callum Vane was not a man who looked at hands. Callum was a man who looked at ledgers.

"Here's how I see it," Callum went on, conversational, rain drumming on the umbrella. "You've got a box of old paper. Paper's only worth what someone will pay. The tribunal will pay you nothing—they'll *take* it, and they might take you with it, salvage theft, obstruction, God knows what else my lawyers can invent. I, on the other hand, am prepared to be generous." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was the smile of a man who had never once been surprised by an invoice. "Walk away. Both of you. The debt dies. The chest stays with me, and by tomorrow night it's ash, and everyone goes on living in the world as it is. Which, I'll remind you, has been perfectly comfortable for thirty years."

"And the people in the box," I said. "The two hundred and six names. They go on not living in it."

Callum looked at me then, really looked, and for a moment I saw the arithmetic behind his eyes—the calculation of what it would cost to have me fall off this dock, and whether the rain would cover it. It would have. It was that kind of day.

"Everyone in that box has been dead a long time, Miss Ives. Ash keeps no appointments."

"Neither do we," Quentin said, and stepped forward, and put his ruined hands flat on the chest.

I'll tell you what I felt in that moment, because I've had thirty years to rehearse braver versions of it and the truth is less flattering: I felt sick. I felt the way the river looks before it takes a bank—calm on the surface, all the violence underneath. Because I knew what Quentin knew, which is that Callum Vane's generosity had a shelf life of about four seconds, and that Ossler had already drifted two steps to the side, cutting off the gangway, and that the tribunal building was forty yards away and might as well have been forty miles.

"Last chance," Callum said. "I'm told I'm sentimental. My brother disagrees."

"He's right," Ossler said. "You are."

And that—God help me—that was when Quentin opened the chest.

* * *

I don't know what I expected. A standoff, I suppose, guns and shouting, the whole dock erupting. What happened instead was worse and better: nothing. Quentin knelt in the rain with his bleeding hands and lifted out the top folder—the one with the general's own signature, Vosk's order, the one that turned "tragic accident" into "necessary measure"—and he held it up, and the rain hit the paper, and he didn't flinch.

"Thirty years," Quentin said. "You're right, Callum. It's been comfortable. For you." He turned, and he raised his voice—not shouting, just carrying, the way a man speaks across water—and he said it to the whole harbor, to the tribunal windows, to the customs officers frozen at their posts. "This is the order for the Kestrel crossing. Signed. Dated. It was never an accident. It was policy. And this—" he pulled a second page, "—is the requisition for the fuel. And this is the after-action report, and this is the list of officers present, and every name on it is a man who ate dinner afterward and slept fine."

"Put it down," Callum said, and the umbrella tilted back, and the gun came out from under his coat, and it was not a courtesy anymore. "Quentin. Put it down. I am offering you your life at a discount and you are haggling."

"I'm not haggling." Quentin stood. Rain ran off his chin. "I'm testifying."

"You're standing on my dock, forty yards from a building full of judges, holding stolen property, and the only witnesses are a ferry pilot with a grudge and my brother, who works for me." Callum's voice stayed pleasant. That was the horror of it. "Who do you think the tribunal hears first?"

"Me," said Ossler.

And the whole dock went still.

I have watched men betray each other my whole life—over cargo, over fuel, over a woman in Port Anselm who was worth maybe half what they thought. I have never watched a man betray his own brother with such *relief*. Ossler stepped forward out of the rain, and his gun was already out, already level, and he wasn't pointing it at us.

"Ossler," Callum said, without turning his head. "Point that somewhere useful."

"You're done, Callum." Ossler's voice cracked on the second word, and I realized, watching him, that this wasn't a decision he'd made on the dock. This was a decision he'd made weeks ago, maybe on the day Quentin first lied to him about the chest, maybe before. "You heard him. Signed order. After-action report. You sent me to retrieve it—do you understand what that makes you? Not a creditor. An *accomplice*. You put your signature next to Vosk's the moment you offered to buy this quiet." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, wet and short. "I'm not going to burn for your ledger."

"The tribunal will hang you too," Callum said. "You carried the gun."

"The tribunal doesn't hang witnesses." Ossler's eyes flicked to the tribunal building, to the tall windows where, I swear on my brother's grave, faces had begun to gather. "It hangs principals. I'm about to become a witness."

And Callum Vane—patient, pleasant, umbrella-holding Callum—finally understood the shape of the trap, and I watched him do the arithmetic one last time, and this time the numbers came out wrong no matter how he arranged them. Shoot Ossler, and the tribunal hears the shot and finds two bodies and a chest of documents. Shoot us, same problem, plus Ossler's testimony. Run, and Ossler's testimony follows him like the tide.

Callum Vane, who had never once been surprised by an invoice, was surprised.

He raised the gun anyway. Men like him always do. The last entry in a failing ledger is always written in the same hand.

He never fired. The tribunal guards came down the pier at a run—someone in those tall windows had been watching the whole performance, and I'd like to think it was the archivist's doing, the old man's letters arriving ahead of us like a tide of their own—and they took Callum's gun and then Callum, and he went quietly, which disappointed me. I wanted him to scream. I wanted him to make a sound like the ones in the documents.

Ossler stood with his hands up, gun on the planks, and when the guards reached him he said, clear enough for everyone: "I'll testify. All of it. The syndicate, the orders, the buyers. Everything I know." And then, quieter, to Quentin: "You lied to me at the dock. Ten days ago. About the chest."

"I did," Quentin said.

"It was a good lie," Ossler said, and something in his face almost became a smile. "That's what saved you. If you'd told me the truth, I'd have killed you and felt bad about it later."

* * *

They took our statements in a room that smelled of wet wool and floor polish, and the rain kept coming, and somewhere in the middle of it I stopped shaking and started talking and found I could not easily stop.

I told them about Tomas. I told them about Kilometer Nine, about the ferry runs, about the name I'd found in the margin of the after-action report—*Lt. H. Brandt, commanding*—and how I'd read it by lamplight in a leaking cabin while the man who found the papers slept holding the chest like a child. I told them about the burned village, and the old woman who thanked me for carrying the names home, and I got that far before my voice went, and Quentin picked it up without missing a beat, my half of the story folded into his like we'd written it together.

Because we had, I suppose. That's what the river does. It takes two currents and makes one water.

The tribunal clerk—a tired woman with ink-stained fingers who'd clearly heard a hundred stories and believed perhaps four—kept asking Quentin the same question in different ways: *why did you keep reading? You could have sold it unread. Why did you open the chest at all?*

And Quentin looked at her, and then at me, and said, "Because I was drowning, and I figured the water had sent me something. I was wrong about what it was. It wasn't a fortune." He flexed his bandaged hands on the table. "It was a bill. Mine."

The clerk wrote that down. I hope it goes in the record. I hope it's read aloud in a courtroom someday, because I have never heard the man say anything truer, and I include everything he's said to me in the dark.

They authenticated the first documents that same evening—photographs, seals, the archivist's affidavit, which had arrived by courier two days ahead of us because that old man had been waiting thirty years and was not about to let a monsoon slow him down. The general's name went onto an indictment. Posthumous, of course. The dead general gets a trial he can't attend; the dead of Kestrel get a verdict they'll never hear. I know how that sounds. I know the arithmetic doesn't balance. But I stood in that corridor while they read the charges, and I heard my brother's name spoken into an official record—*Tomas Ives, ferryman, age twenty-four*—by a stranger in a gray robe, in a building that exists, in a country that still does, and I am here to tell you that it balanced enough. It balanced enough to walk on.

Ossler testified until midnight. They gave him coffee and a blanket and, eventually, a lawyer. Callum Vane sat in a cell two floors down and, the guards said, asked twice for his umbrella.

* * *

It was nearly dawn when they let us go—released pending formal proceedings, our statements taken, the chest sealed in evidence under a tribunal seal that looked, frankly, less impressive than the Republic of Kestrel's. We came out onto the harbor steps and the rain had finally, *finally* stopped. The storm had spent itself overnight and the sky over Corvane was doing that pale, rinsed, apologetic thing that skies do after they've tried to kill you.

The *Kestrel's Mercy* rode at her mooring, listing slightly, patched, pot-soldered, alive. My boat. Our boat. Whatever it was now.

Quentin sat down on the wet steps beside me, wincing, and for a long time neither of us said anything. The harbor woke up around us—gulls, winches, a customs launch grumbling out past the breakwater. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a world that had gone on being comfortable for thirty years and would go on being comfortable, mostly, still.

"They'll want me in the courtroom," Quentin said at last. "As a witness. Maybe worse. The salvage theft, the—" he waved a bandaged hand, "—everything. I could be looking at months."

"I know."

"I'd have sold it, you know. At the start. If you hadn't—"

"I know that too." I looked at him. "You'd have sold it, and you'd have hated yourself quietly for the rest of your life, and you'd have been bad at it, because you're bad at lying to anyone who matters. Ossler told you. It was a good lie. It was also the last one you had in you."

He laughed—a short, cracked sound, the laugh of a man running on no sleep and borrowed blood. Then he went serious, in that way of his where the seriousness comes on like weather.

"Mara. The debt. With Callum in a cell and the syndicate—"

"Will collapse," I said. "Syndicates always do, once someone testifies. Ossler's giving them all to the tribunal one ledger at a time. Your debt died on that dock, Quentin. It's in evidence now, sealed in a box, like everything else."

He stared out at the water. The light was coming up gold behind the breakwater, and the river beyond the harbor was running high but steady, the flood beginning its long retreat back into the sea.

"So what am I, now?" he said. "If I'm not a debtor?"

I thought about it. I thought about a man kneeling in the rain holding a dead country's signature above his head. I thought about hands wrapped in shirt strips, about a cooking pot soldered over a shrapnel hole, about the way he'd slept holding the chest—not like a man guarding treasure, but like a man guarding someone else's child.

"Dry," I said. "For the first time in a long while. Start there."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "The boat needs real repairs. Proper hull work. A new wheel. There's a yard upriver, past the burned village—"

"I know the one."

"—and the tribunal will want us back for the hearings, so we'll be running this stretch all season, and I don't have a boat of my own anymore, and you don't have a crew." He turned and looked at me, and there it was, the whole proposal, wrapped in river logistics like a man proposing marriage through a cargo manifest. "I'm saying I could work. If the work existed."

"The work exists," I said. "The pay's terrible. The weather's worse. You'll be half-drowned by August."

"I've got references," Quentin Delacroix said. "Drowned, mostly. But references."

I laughed, and it hurt, and it was the best I'd felt in thirty years, and I was still laughing when the tribunal clerk came back out onto the steps with a folder under her arm and an expression I'd learned to dread on the faces of officials.

"Mr. Delacroix. Miss Ives." She held out the folder. "There's a complication. The tribunal's investigators opened the sealed chest this morning for full cataloguing." She paused, and rain-water dripped off the steps behind her, and the gulls went suddenly quiet. "There are documents in the bottom layer that weren't in the archivist's inventory. Sealed orders. Recent ones." Her eyes went from me to Quentin and back. "And the first name on the list is Dain Vosk."

The gold light kept coming up over the breakwater, indifferent and lovely, and somewhere upriver, in a country that had just learned what its hero had done, a general's son who had let two strangers walk away was about to learn what that mercy had cost him.

Quentin took the folder. He didn't open it right away. He just held it, the way he'd held the chest, and looked at me, and I already knew what he was going to say before he said it.

"We'll need the boat," he said. "And the good weather. While it lasts."
