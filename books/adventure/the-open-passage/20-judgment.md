## 20. Judgment

They say a man's life passes before his eyes when he drowns. Nobody warns you that the same thing happens when you're asked to testify.

I stood in the well of the Corvane tribunal hall—white stone, high windows, rain still drumming on the glass like it had followed us upriver out of spite—and I watched my life scroll past: every debt, every dodge, every small dishonesty I'd ever committed in the name of staying afloat. It was not an impressive reel. Mostly it was receipts.

The chest sat on a table between me and the bench of magistrates, and it looked smaller than it had any right to. Three weeks ago that iron box had weighed as much as my future. Now, under the tribunal's clean electric light, it was just a box with a broken seal and a dead nation's crest, waiting to be opened by people who would decide what it meant.

"State your name for the record," said the presiding magistrate, a woman named Aldous with spectacles that caught the light like twin coins.

"Quentin Delacroix."

"And your occupation?"

I thought about it. Salvager. Debtor. Courier of other people's sins. "Boatman," I said.

A ripple went through the gallery—not laughter exactly, but the sound a room makes when it doesn't quite believe you. Fair enough. I didn't entirely believe myself either.

* * *

The authentication took two days, which surprised no one who had ever watched a bureaucracy try to move faster than a funeral. Experts came from three capitals. They wore white gloves and carried magnifying lenses and handled General Aurel Vosk's signature the way priests handle relics—reverently, and with a certain hunger.

I watched them work from a wooden bench near the back, next to Mara, who had not slept properly since we'd run the Open Passage and whose hands still trembled faintly when she forgot to hide them. She held them flat on her knees now, deliberately, like a woman keeping a lid on something.

"They're going to argue about the watermark," she said quietly. "Watch. There's always one expert who builds a career on doubt."

She was right. On the second afternoon, a thin man from the Kestrel succession states spent four hours explaining why the paper stock on the third document was inconsistent with wartime rationing, and therefore the whole archive might be forged. The gallery murmured. The magistrates' faces went carefully blank.

Then the old archivist, Wrenn, was brought forward on a cane, and I learned what eighty years of patience looks like when it finally gets its turn to speak.

He didn't raise his voice. He told them about the paper mills above Kestrel City that had burned in the last winter of the war, forcing the quartermaster corps to mix stock from three different suppliers—which explained the inconsistency precisely, down to the lot numbers, which he recited from memory because he had catalogued those documents himself, forty years ago, before he helped bury them in a river vault he'd sworn never to reveal until someone brave enough or foolish enough came asking.

His eyes found mine across the hall when he said *foolish enough*. I decided to take it as a compliment.

The thin expert sat down. The watermark argument died quietly, the way arguments do when they meet a witness who was actually there.

On the third morning, Magistrate Aldous read the finding aloud: the documents were authentic. The massacre at Serevan Bend—four hundred and eleven civilians, ordered by General Vosk, covered by nine officers whose signatures appeared in the margins—was not an accident of war. It was policy.

The general himself was beyond hanging; he'd been dead eleven years, buried under a monument taller than the church we'd sheltered in during the flood. But monuments, it turns out, can be taken down. His name was struck from the rolls of national honor before lunch. Two of the nine signatories were still alive—one a provincial governor, one a beloved author of memoirs—and both were arrested before dinner. I heard later that the governor wept and the memoirist demanded better coffee. History is not dignified at close range.

The gallery applauded when the indictments were read. Mara did not. She sat very still with her brother's name folded inside her fist—a scrap of paper with *Tomas Ives, age nineteen, executed without trial* written on it in her own hand—and she watched the proceedings like a woman counting coins she'd been owed for twenty years.

I understood then that this verdict wasn't justice for her. It was a down payment.

* * *

Here is the part they don't put in the ballads: on the afternoon of the fourth day, after the indictments and before the closing session, two tribunal marshals came to the dockside inn where we were sleeping off the monsoon and asked me, very politely, to accompany them.

"Accompany where?" I said, though I knew.

"The tribunal wishes to question you regarding the provenance of the recovered materials."

"Provenance." I almost laughed. "You mean you want to know if I stole them."

The younger marshal had the decency to look uncomfortable. The older one just waited. Waiting, I've learned, is the oldest trick in the collector's book—the Vanes used it, Ossler used it, and now the law was using it on me. Silence plus time equals confession, if you're guilty, and equals dignity, if you're not, and either way you end up talking.

"I salvaged them from flood debris," I said. "Public waterway. No owner living. That's salvage under maritime custom."

"That's an argument," said the older marshal. "Not a legal claim. The Republic of Kestrel's state archives were never formally dissolved. Technically, everything in that chest remains state property, and you removed it from state custody without authorization."

"You mean the state that hasn't existed in forty years."

"Laws outlive countries, Mr. Delacroix. It's one of their more annoying features."

So they took me. Not in chains—they let me walk between them through the rain, which I appreciated, since chains and wet weather make a man philosophical in ways that lead to regrettable quotes—but into detention all the same. A room with a cot, a window too high to see out of, and a great deal of time to think about the irony of a man who delivered justice being charged with theft by the people dispensing it.

Mara would have said something wry about it. I could hear her voice in my head: *Congratulations, Quentin. You finally own something outright, and it's a cell.*

I lay on the cot and laughed until it turned into something else, and then I slept, which was the first real sleep I'd had since the storm took my boat apart on the Serevan.

* * *

They questioned me twice. Once about the salvage—dates, locations, witnesses—and once, unexpectedly, about the Vanes.

The second interviewer was a different animal: a lean investigator named Corso with ink-stained fingers and the patient eyes of a man who reads ledgers for pleasure. He spread out pages I recognized with a lurch—Vane syndicate loan records, seized somehow, or copied, or bought. My name was on several of them. My boat's registration was on others. And there, circled in red, were entries I'd never seen: disbursements to a man named Ossler marked *river collection*, and one entry, dated the week before my loan, marked *Kestrel archive — acquisition inquiry*.

My blood went cold in a warm room.

"They knew," I said. "The Vanes knew the chest existed."

"We believe they've been looking for it for years," Corso said. "A client paid them to recover and destroy certain materials. We don't yet know who the client was. We were rather hoping you might help us with that."

"I'm a debtor, not an informant."

"You're both, potentially. They're not mutually exclusive in my experience." He tapped the ledger page. "Your debt, Mr. Delacroix. What was it, at the end?"

"Everything I had, plus ten percent a month."

"And if the tribunal voids those loans as instruments of an ongoing criminal enterprise—as we are inclined to do—you walk away owing nothing. In exchange for names."

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the whole point of this story, and I have learned the hard way that a man who tells the truth only when it's convenient isn't telling the truth at all. So: for about four seconds, I wanted it. A clean slate. A signed release. The weight gone from my shoulders like floodwater off a deck.

And then I thought about Ossler standing on the dock with his polite voice and his terrible patience, and Callum with his offer of partnership dressed up as friendship, and Dain Vosk reading his father's signature in silence while his whole inheritance curdled in his hands. Every one of them a prisoner of the same lie, and every one of them willing to drown strangers to keep it afloat.

If I gave Corso names, men would come for those names. Men with boats and guns and ledgers. And some of them would come asking questions of a ferry pilot who happened to share my wake.

"No names," I said. "But I'll tell you how they operate. Routes, methods, the checkpoint system, how they buy officers. Take the machine apart if you can. Just leave the people I passed on the river out of it."

Corso studied me for a long moment. "That's an unusual bargain."

"It's the only one I'm offering."

He wrote it down. I never learned whether he honored it. That's the thing about deals made in detention rooms—you sign them blind and hope the other party's word weighs more than yours did.

* * *

On the sixth day they charged me. Not with anything dramatic—no conspiracy, no smuggling, nothing the ballads would touch. Just *unauthorized removal of state property*, a salvage theft, a paperwork crime, the kind of charge that exists so that laws can be seen to apply even to men who've done the right thing the wrong way.

Magistrate Aldous herself presided over the disposition hearing. She looked at me over those coin-bright spectacles and asked if I had anything to say before testimony was taken.

"Just this," I said. "I didn't open that chest looking for justice. I opened it because I was broke and curious, and curiosity is cheaper than most vices. Everything good that came out of it came out sideways. If the tribunal needs someone to punish for how these papers reached you, I'm the available candidate. But I'd ask you to remember that the alternative custodians wanted them burned."

A murmur ran the length of the hall. Aldous didn't smile, but something behind her eyes moved, like current under ice.

"Thank you, Mr. Delacroix. We will take testimony now. Does anyone speak for you?"

"I do," said Mara Ives.

* * *

She walked to the stand in the same clothes she'd worn running the delta—salt-stiff canvas, boots that had been patched twice since Kilometer Nine—and the whole room adjusted itself around her the way rooms do around people who aren't asking permission.

She told them about Tomas. Nineteen years old, conscripted, executed at Serevan Bend for refusing an order, his name recorded in the margin of a document in a clerk's tidy hand, his body never returned. She told it plainly, without performance, and it was worse than any performance could have been. Half the gallery was crying by the end. I kept my eyes on her hands, flat and steady on the rail, and I did not cry, because one of us had to keep watch.

Then she turned to my part in it.

"I met Quentin Delacroix at a fuel dock, and I offered him passage because I recognized something in him," she said. "I assumed it was desperation. It was. But it was also the fact that he hadn't sold the chest yet, when selling it would have solved every problem he had. Anywhere along that river, there were buyers. He knew it. I knew it. He kept carrying it instead."

"Did he ever attempt to profit from the documents?" Aldous asked.

"He attempted to survive," Mara said. "There's a difference, and this tribunal ought to know it, because half the people in this room have confused the two at some point in their careers." A rustle went through the benches. Aldous let it die before answering.

"Noted," she said dryly. "Continue."

"He lied to a debt collector to protect the chest. He hid it in a coffin. He ran the Open Passage—the channel no pilot survives—with me calling depths from memory, because I asked him to and because he believed the papers mattered more than his life. And when the general's son offered him freedom in exchange for burning them, he put a gun on the man and couldn't pull the trigger, and I have never been gladder of anything." She paused, and her voice dropped to the register she used on the river at night, when the rain made the world small. "Quentin Delacroix stole those documents. From a country that stopped existing, on behalf of people who were never going to ask for them back. If that's a crime, then I'm an accomplice, and so is everyone in this hall who ever kept a truth alive past its expiration date."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Aldous removed her spectacles, polished them unhurriedly, and put them back on. "Mr. Delacroix," she said, "in light of the testimony, the disposition is as follows. The charge of unauthorized removal is sustained. The tribunal notes that the property in question was recovered intact, surrendered voluntarily, and proved instrumental in the indictment of General Vosk and the arrest of two living accomplices to mass murder. The sentence is forfeiture of salvage rights—" I braced; there was nothing left to forfeit but pride, and I'd already spent that "—a fine of fifty crowns, payable within ninety days, and a formal censure to be entered in the record." She looked at me over the spectacles. "Which means, Mr. Delacroix, that you are officially a thief, and unofficially the reason four hundred and eleven people may finally rest. Both facts will appear in the record. We do not edit history here. We have recently learned better."

"Fifty crowns," I repeated.

"You may consider it the tribunal's rate for a clear conscience. It's the cheapest anyone's ever sold you."

A laugh went through the hall before it could stop itself, and this time it included me. Fifty crowns. After everything—the Vanes, the monsoon, the loyalists, the Open Passage, Ossler's gun and Callum's smile—I owed the law less than I'd ever owed anybody. It was, by a wide margin, the best debt of my life.

"I'll need a boat to earn it," I said.

"Then I suggest you acquire one honestly," said Aldous, and struck the gavel, and that was that.

* * *

They released me at dusk. The rain had finally quit—the first dry evening in weeks—and the light over the Corvane docks was the color of brass, and the river smelled like mud and ending.

Mara was waiting on the pilings, sitting with her boots dangling over the water like she'd been born there, which in a sense she had. She didn't get up when I came out. She just looked at me with that assessing squint she used instead of smiling.

"Fifty crowns," she said.

"Don't start."

"A fine. For stealing justice. That's the most sensible crime I've ever heard of."

"Mara."

"I'm putting it in the logbook. Official vessel record: cargo, one chest, priceless. Penalty, fifty crowns. Profit margin, incalculable."

I sat down beside her on the piling. Below us the water slid past, brown and heavy with the season's leavings, already starting to drop. Somewhere upriver the floodwaters were letting go of the fields, the roads, the drowned churches, giving everything back a little at a time, the way rivers do—imperfectly, late, but giving it back.

"How does it feel?" she asked. "Being officially a thief?"

"Honest," I said, and meant it. "First honest thing on my record in years. I find I'd like to keep it that way."

She bumped her shoulder against mine, which from Mara was a speech. We watched the light go off the water.

Then, down the quay, a figure detached itself from the shadow of a warehouse and started toward us—broad-shouldered, unhurried, moving with the particular confidence of a man who owns whatever ground he stands on. Mara saw him before I did. Her hand found my arm.

"Quentin," she said quietly. "Isn't that—"

It was. Callum Vane. Walking toward us alone, no collectors, no gun visible, wearing an expression I had never once seen on a Vane brother's face.

He looked worried.
