## 21. Settled Accounts

They kept Quentin for nine days.

I know because I counted them the way you count anything in Corvane—by the tide tables pinned to the wall of my rented room, by the number of times I walked past the tribunal annex and pretended I hadn't. Nine days of gray stone corridors and clerks who spoke in the soft, apologetic voices of men who process human beings like cargo manifests. Nine days of me sitting across from a prosecutor named Halloway who asked the same questions in different orders, hoping I'd trip on my own arithmetic.

"Ms. Ives," he said on the fourth day, "you understand that Mr. Delacroix removed the chest from the river without salvage authorization."

"I understand he pulled it out of a flood that was trying to kill him," I said. "If the Republic of Kestrel wanted its paperwork back, it should have filed a claim before it stopped existing."

Halloway wrote something down. He had the habit of writing things down even when nothing needed writing, which I've learned is what passes for power in buildings like this. On the river, power is simpler. Power is knowing where the sandbars moved since last season and being willing to bet your hull on it.

"You're aware," he said, "that your testimony could be read as self-interested."

"Everything is self-interested. The question is whether the interest is worth anything." I leaned forward. "Prosecutor, I flew supplies through that delta for six years. I buried my brother with a closed casket because there wasn't enough of him left to need an open one. Those papers are the only reason anyone will ever say his name out loud in a courtroom. If protecting Quentin Delacroix is self-interest, then hang us both, because I'd do it again with the boat on fire."

He didn't write that down. Some things even clerks know better than to commit to paper.

* * *

On the ninth day they let him out.

I was waiting at the annex steps when the doors opened—waiting the way you wait at a dock when you're not sure the boat's coming, casual as a woman reading a shipping schedule, except I'd read the same schedule eleven times and it hadn't changed. He came out blinking in the light like something hauled up from deep water, thinner than when they'd taken him, wearing the same ruined shirt they'd confiscated and returned with an inventory tag still knotted to the collar.

"They gave me a warning," he said, sitting down heavily beside me on the steps. "Verbally. Very solemn. If I ever again remove items of historical significance from navigable waterways without a permit."

"A permit."

"There's apparently a form."

"For salvaging evidence of a massacre from a flooded river during a monsoon."

"There's a form for everything here. That's the whole point of Corvane. It's a city built out of forms." He rubbed his face. His hands were steady, but it was the steadiness of effort, not calm—I knew the difference by then. "Mara. The general. Did they—"

"Posthumous indictment. Read into the record yesterday. They're calling it the Kestrel Findings." I watched his face while I said it, because some news you have to watch land or it doesn't count. "Four living officers arrested already. Two more fled north; there are warrants. The tribunal's opening a reparations commission for the survivors' villages."

"And Vosk?"

"Dain turned himself in the day after we docked. Voluntarily. Gave a full statement." I shrugged, though it wasn't shrug-able, not really. "The prosecutors didn't know what to do with him. A man who confesses to knowledge he never acted on. He's cooperating. Naming names."

Quentin was quiet a long moment. Down at the harbor a crane swung a load of timber over the quay with the patience of something that had done it ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more.

"And the Vanes?" he asked, and I heard it—the flinch under the question, the old reflex. Even now, free, cleared, walking around in daylight like a man with a future, part of him still braced for Ossler's polite voice or Callum's warm hand on his shoulder.

"That's the strange part," I said. "Come on. There's someone you need to drink with, and I'd rather you hear it from him than from a newspaper."

* * *

We found Dain Vosk at a dockside tavern called the Half Tide, which was exactly the kind of place a man goes when he wants to be found by almost no one and everyone equally. It smelled of tar and fried fish and the particular dampness that gets into Corvane's river district every afternoon and never fully leaves. Dain sat alone at a table near the window with a bottle of the local brandy—harsh stuff, distilled by people who clearly held grudges against their own throats—and three glasses, already poured.

He looked smaller than he had with a gun in his hand. Or maybe just more honestly sized. The tailored coat was gone, replaced by a plain fisherman's sweater that fit him badly, and without the loyalist insignia he could have been any middle-aged man drinking away an afternoon, except for the eyes. The eyes were still doing arithmetic about the world.

"Delacroix," he said, standing. It wasn't quite a bow and it wasn't quite a handshake offer; it was a man negotiating, in real time, what courtesy owed between people who'd pointed guns at each other. "Ives."

"Vosk." Quentin sat down slowly, like the chair might be a trap. Old habits. "You sent for me?"

"My lawyer sent for you. I don't send for anyone anymore; it looks bad in testimony." Dain pushed a glass across the table. "But yes. I wanted to tell you myself, because if you hear it secondhand you'll spend weeks wondering what was left out, and I know how you are with incomplete information. You chew on it like wire."

Quentin looked at the glass. Looked at me. I picked mine up—if Dain Vosk wanted to poison us he'd hardly have chosen a tavern with this many witnesses, and besides, I'd drunk worse brandy in worse company for worse reasons.

"The syndicate's finished," Dain said. "The Vane brothers. Your creditors."

For a moment Quentin didn't move at all. Then he set the glass down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"Tell me."

"It started the night we reached Corvane. Ossler turned on Callum at the docks—you were there, you saw it. What you didn't see is what came after. Ossler gave the tribunal everything. Ledgers, routes, the names of every magistrate and harbor official the Vanes had bought over twenty years. He bought himself immunity by selling his own brother." Dain drank, grimaced at the brandy as if it had personally disappointed him. "Callum's awaiting trial. Ossler's been relocated somewhere with a new name and no river nearby—he struck me as a man who'd drown within sight of water, out of professional habit."

"And the debt?" Quentin's voice was very level. Too level. "My account. My boat. Everything I owed them."

"Debts aren't transferable, Delacroix. Not legally, not morally. A debt is a relationship between two specific parties. When the parties die, the debt dies with them." Dain spread his hands. "The Vanes aren't dead, exactly. But the ledger is. Seized, catalogued, entered into evidence. Somewhere in a tribunal warehouse there's a box with your name in it, and no one alive has the authority to collect."

I watched Quentin take that in. It went into him slowly, like water into dry ground, and I could see the exact moment it reached whatever root it had been looking for—his shoulders dropped half an inch, and something in his face unclenched that I hadn't realized was clenched, because it had been clenched since the day I met him.

"Ten days," he said quietly. "That's all it was, in the end. Ten days, and then the whole machine ate itself."

"Machines like that always do," Dain said. "It's the only mercy in them. They run on fear, and fear doesn't scale. Sooner or later the collectors get frightened too, and then the whole thing turns cannibal." He refilled the glasses. "I'd know. I spent thirty years inside a different machine built on the same fuel. My father's lie. I fed it, guarded it, killed for it—and the entire time I told myself I was protecting the country, when really I was protecting the version of my father that made my life make sense." He looked up. "You had me at gunpoint on that boat. In the mangroves. And you didn't fire."

"You know why."

"I want to hear you say it anyway. I've said my piece to the tribunal until the words wore smooth. I want to hear yours while they're still rough."

Quentin turned the glass in his fingers. Outside, rain had started—not monsoon rain, just the ordinary gray drizzle that Corvane wears like a coat—and it ticked softly against the window.

"Because I'd finally figured out what I was running from," he said. "And it wasn't the Vanes. It was the arithmetic. The arithmetic that says a man can be weighed against a chest, a chest against a debt, a debt against a life. Everyone I was afraid of lived inside that arithmetic. Ossler. Callum. The general, ordering a massacre because the numbers worked. If I'd pulled that trigger, I'd have balanced my books the way they balance theirs—one death against one problem, settled." He lifted the glass. "I'd rather have gone bankrupt forever than keep accounts like that."

Dain nodded slowly, and raised his own glass. "Then here's to bad bookkeeping."

"To bad bookkeeping," I said, and drank, and the brandy burned all the way down like it was clearing a path.

* * *

We stayed at that table most of the afternoon, and it was the strangest evening of my life, and I include the night I slept in a drowned church with an archivist who'd helped bury a nation's conscience under its own floorboards.

Because it turned out that Dain Vosk, stripped of his father's name and his father's lie, was—there's no other word for it—funny. Dry and self-lacerating, the humor of a man who has audited his own soul and found the discrepancies embarrassing. He told stories about growing up in the general's house: the portrait in the hall that was repainted twice to make the hero look taller, the state dinners where his father would toast the fallen of Kestrel with genuine tears in his eyes, and young Dain sitting there knowing, having known since he was nineteen, since the night he found the dispatch in his father's study.

"He cried real tears," Dain said. "That's the part I can't get past. He mourned them. Sincerely. He just never once thought of them as people he'd killed—they were a tragedy that had happened, weather, geology. And I stood next to him at those dinners holding his glass of water, because he liked me to, and I said nothing." He turned his glass. "Thirty years of saying nothing. You two said something in six weeks, and half of that time you were running aground."

"We had help," Quentin said. "An archivist who waited forty years."

"Yes. Old Sef. He testified last week." Something moved through Dain's face, brief and complicated. "I sat in the gallery. He talked about my father—about the day the records were sealed, the day my father shook his hand and thanked him for his discretion. Sef said, 'I have thought about that handshake every day for forty years.' And the whole chamber went quiet, and I thought: that's it. That's the sentence. That's what a life costs when you spend it keeping a lie." He looked up. "I'm going to spend whatever years I have left testifying. Naming names. Digging up what my father buried. It won't balance anything. But it's the only currency I have."

I thought about Tomas then. I think about Tomas most days, but that afternoon he arrived without permission, the way the dead do—my brother at sixteen, teaching me to read the delta fog off the water, laughing at how seriously I took everything. His name was in those pages. Officer present. Signature witnessed. For six years that signature had been the largest fact of my life, the fixed star I steered by, and now it was exhibit forty-one in a public record, read aloud by a clerk with a monotone, argued over by lawyers, and slowly, steadily becoming history instead of wound.

I hadn't known that was possible. That grief could be processed. Filed. Turned from a private weight into a shared inheritance. It doesn't stop hurting—that's the lie nobody tells you, that justice heals. It doesn't heal anything. It just moves the hurt from a room with no windows into a room with a door.

"So what happens to you now?" Dain asked Quentin. "Man walks out of a tribunal with a verbal warning, a dead debt, and a boat with shrapnel holes. What does he do with all that freedom?"

"Patch the hull," Quentin said. "Mostly."

"And after?"

Quentin glanced at me. Just a glance, quick as a man checking a compass bearing, and I looked back at my brandy and pretended not to notice, and both of us were lying, and both of us knew the other was lying, and it was the most honest exchange at the table all afternoon.

"After," Quentin said, "I expect somebody will need cargo moved upriver. Once the rains end and the trade lanes open. There'll be rebuilding. Reparations shipments. All those villages on the tribunal's list that haven't seen a supply boat in years because the syndicate owned the contracts." He shrugged. "Seems like work that wants doing."

"Upriver work," Dain said. "Through the delta."

"Through the delta," I confirmed. "Where the checkpoints are honest now, or at least unemployed. Where the worst thing on the river is the weather." I smiled despite myself. "Which, granted, is genuinely terrible."

Dain laughed—a real laugh, rusty, like a winch that hadn't turned in years. Then he sobered and did something I didn't expect. He took out a small notebook, cheap paperbound, the kind students use, and tore out a page, and wrote something on it, and slid it across to Quentin.

"What's this?"

"The location of a cache. My father kept a second set of records—private ones, worse than what you carried. Things even the tribunal doesn't know yet. I found them after he died and I couldn't bring myself to open them, and I couldn't bring myself to burn them, so I hid them, and I've been carrying that page for four years like a stone." He pushed it flat. "I'd go myself, but everything I touch becomes testimony. You two have a talent for finding things the river buried and getting them where they need to go. Consider it—" he paused, searching for the word, and landed on it with visible discomfort— "a payment. For the gun you didn't fire."

Quentin looked at the page a long time. Then he folded it and put it in his breast pocket, over the heart, which is where pilots keep the things they actually intend to remember.

"We'll need provisions," he said. "And a hull survey. And about a week."

"You'll need more than a week," I said. "That hull's held together with resin and stubbornness. And I'm not taking her upriver until I've re-rigged the fuel lines myself."

"Two weeks, then."

"Three. And you're cooking."

"Deal."

Dain watched us negotiate like a man watching weather he'd forgotten existed—something ordinary, something that just happens to people whose lives aren't currently on fire. He raised his glass one more time.

"To settled accounts," he said.

We drank. And outside the rain kept falling on Corvane, gentle and endless, washing the salt off the cranes and the tar smell off the streets, and somewhere south of the city the great river was dropping, inch by inch, revealing its sandbars and its secrets, getting ready to carry boats again.

Three days later, at dawn, I walked the quay to check on the ferry and found Quentin already aboard, sleeves rolled, hauling the first panel of patch plating out of its wrapping. He'd pinned a copy of the tribunal's village list to the cabin bulkhead—forty-seven names—and crossed out the first one.

"Forty-six to go," he said, and handed me a hammer.

The horizon was already turning gold.
