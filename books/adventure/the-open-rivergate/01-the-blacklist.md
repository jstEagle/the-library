## 1. The Blacklist

There are three ways to disappear in the Serran range, and I had tried all of them.

The first is altitude. Go high enough and the air thins your name right out of you. But the high country was closed to me now—permits checked, ridgelines patrolled by men who'd once bought me coffee—and besides, the mountains above the tree line were where my old life lived, and I wasn't ready to share a tent with it.

The second is winter. Snow buries tracks, and tracks are just another kind of reputation. But winter also buries work, and a woman who doesn't work starts asking questions of herself she'd rather not answer.

The third—the one that actually held—was the low valleys. The green folds south of the Kestrel line where the river goes slow and fat and nobody with authority bothers to look. That's where I'd been living for two years under the name *Marta Vane*, guiding hunters after chamois and wild boar through country so tame it practically apologized for itself. Flat trails. Marked crossings. Game that hadn't learned to fear anything but the season.

It was, in short, exactly the kind of guiding I'd sworn at twenty-two I would never stoop to. Which made it perfect. Nobody looks down far enough to find you.

* * *

That October morning I was up before the light, because hunters always want to be in position before the animals finish being embarrassed about dawn. My client was a man named Ostrick from the capital—a soft-handed accountant type who'd read too many hunting magazines and owned a rifle worth more than my cabin. He'd booked three days. He wanted a trophy head. He wanted, judging by the way he kept touching the stock like it might purr, mostly to be photographed holding something dead.

"Marta," he said, around a mouthful of the eggs I'd fried him, "you've got good hands. Steady. You've done this a long time."

"Long enough," I said.

"You don't talk much."

"That's usually what people pay for."

He laughed. Clients always laugh at that, because they think it's a joke. It isn't. It's a service agreement.

We went up the Lowmark trail while the mist still sat in the valley like something poured. I let him set the pace, which meant we stopped four times in the first hour for him to catch his breath and tell me about his brother-in-law's elk camp in the north, where the guides were ex-military and carried satellite phones and the whole operation cost more per day than I made in a month. I nodded in the places that seemed load-bearing.

The truth was I could have walked that trail blindfolded, drunk, and carrying Ostrick on my back. The truth was I had walked harder ground in worse weather with better men bleeding on it. Two years ago I'd been Lior Almeida, first-name basis with half the rangers in the Serran district, the guide people requested by name when the route looked impossible on paper. Now I was Marta Vane, whose references were a post-office box and a smile I practiced in the reflection off my rifle scope.

You get used to lying about your name faster than you'd think. What you don't get used to is lying about everything else. Every time a client asked *how long have you been doing this*, I had to decide which woman was answering. Marta said eleven years, vaguely, waving a hand at the question. Lior said twenty, precisely, and could have named every storm.

I'd gotten so I only answered as Marta. It was safer. It was also, some mornings, like watching someone else wear my hands.

* * *

We found sign by mid-morning—boar, fresh, rooting up the south slope below the scree—and I put Ostrick behind a fallen beech with a sightline on the wallow and told him not to move unless something was eating him. Then I settled into the ferns ten meters off and did what I do best, which is nothing, extremely well.

Stillness is underrated work. People think guiding is about walking. Walking is the easy part; any goat can walk. Guiding is about reading weather three valleys away, about knowing which silence means game and which silence means something else, about keeping a frightened man calm without letting him know he's frightened. It's about noticing things. And I noticed, somewhere in the second hour of doing nothing, that Ostrick kept checking his phone even though there was no signal this deep in the fold of the hills.

Not bored-checking. Nervous-checking. Thumb hovering, screen lit, face falling, pocket again.

At noon he gave up on the boar—we'd heard them crash off through the brush an hour earlier, spooked by a party of hikers two ridges over, because even the low valleys weren't as empty as they used to be—and we ate cold sausage on the trail while he complained about the hikers, the weather, the sausage, and finally, mercifully, nothing.

It was on the way back down, in that gold hour when the light comes sideways through the alders, that my life ended for the second time.

* * *

He came up the trail toward us with a fishing rod over his shoulder and a stringer of grayling swinging at his hip. Ordinary man. Fiftyish, heavy through the shoulders, city boots worn wrong for the terrain. I marked him the way I mark everything—threat, no threat, threat later—and had almost finished filing him under *no* when he stopped.

Stopped dead, actually. Rod tip dipping. And his face did the thing faces do when the present suddenly has to make room for the past: a small collapse, then a scramble, then something terrible and hopeful breaking through.

"Lior?" he said. "*Lior Almeida?*"

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. Somewhere downslope the river kept talking to itself, indifferent. Ostrick looked between us like a man watching a tennis match played with knives.

"I'm sorry," said Marta Vane, in her flat lowland vowels. "You have me confused."

But he was already shaking his head, already closing the distance, and I recognized him now—God help me, I recognized him, because memory is a bastard that way. Halloran. Tomas Halloran. Three seasons of client, one bad winter on the White Saddle, the man I'd talked down a cornice slope with his crampons icing up and his nerve gone, my hand on his pack strap for four hundred meters of nothing but air and my voice telling him the next step was easy, the next step was easy, the next step was easy. He'd cried at the bottom. He'd sent me a card every solstice after.

He knew my voice. That was the problem. Not my face—faces change, and mine had changed, thinner, harder, sun-damaged in new places—but my voice. Voices don't age the same way. Voices keep their shape.

"The saddle," he said, softer now, certain. "White Saddle. You talked me across it. You said—" and here he did the cruelest thing possible, he quoted me, back to me, in my own cadence: "*the next step is easy.*"

Nobody else alive knew that sentence. I'd never written it down. It existed in exactly two heads, and one of them was standing in front of me holding a fish.

I felt something crack quietly inside my chest, some seam I'd spent two years caulking.

"Tomas," I said, and gave up the ghost, because there was no version of this where Marta survived the next thirty seconds. "Hello, Tomas."

Ostrick made a sound like a kettle deciding. Halloran stuck out his hand and I took it, and his grip was warm and stupidly glad, and for one full second I let myself be touched by it.

"They said you were finished," he said. "After the Ferren thing. They said—well. Everyone says things." His eyes flicked to Ostrick, to the rifle, back to me, and I watched him do the arithmetic: the false name, the low valley, the client who didn't know her. Watched him arrive, with visible discomfort, at the sum.

"I go by Marta now," I said. "Professionally."

"Right." He swallowed. "Right. Of course. I won't—I mean, obviously I wouldn't—"

"No," I agreed. "Obviously you wouldn't."

And here is the thing about people: most of them are good, and most of them are weak, and those are not the same thing but they travel together. Halloran was good. Halloran would keep my secret the way you keep a secret you're proud of—which is to say badly, warmly, over drinks, with a hand cupped around his mouth, to exactly one friend, who would tell exactly one friend.

I smiled at him. I asked after his wife. I listened to ninety seconds about his daughter's university applications. And the entire time, underneath, some cold quiet part of me was packing.

* * *

Ostrick wanted dinner and stories, so I fed him river trout and gave him Marta's stories, invented ones, a career built out of borrowed weather, and he drank enough wine to believe them and went to bed in the bunkhouse snoring like a landslide. I sat up after with the lamp turned low and did the accounting.

Two years. I'd lasted two years, which was longer than I'd given myself, honestly. Two years of Marta Vane, of the low valleys, of guiding men to animals that barely counted as wild, of sleeping alone in a cabin I'd built with my own hands because building things was the only prayer I still trusted. Two years of hearing my real name maybe twice, both times drunk, both times from strangers who'd known of me rather than known me, and both times followed by that flinch, that little social recoil, *oh—you're the one who—*

The Ferren thing. That's what they called it. A client named Ferren, a route I'd never chosen, a rockfall nobody could have predicted and everybody needed someone to blame for. The inquiry found falsified route maps in the expedition file—maps approved by the survey office, maps bearing signatures I'd trusted—and somehow, by the end of it, the falsification had flowed downhill until it pooled on me. My certification revoked. My name entered into a district register with a note beside it that I never got to read. Blacklisted, in the plain bureaucratic sense: not imprisoned, not prosecuted, just *unusable*. Erased from the profession the way you'd erase a misprinted line. No trial, no appeal worth the postage. Just a door closing so smoothly it didn't even sound like a door.

They say grief comes in stages. What nobody tells you is that professional death has its own sequence, and it runs: outrage, appeal, exhaustion, invisibility, and then—this is the stage they really don't warn you about—relief. Because invisibility, it turns out, is comfortable. Invisibility is a warm coat. Nobody expects anything of a ghost. For two years I had told myself I was hiding, but sitting there in the lamplight with Halloran's voice still hanging in the room, I had to admit the uglier truth: some part of me had *stayed*. Had liked the smallness. Had been grateful, in a way I couldn't say out loud, for a life with nothing left to lose, because a life with nothing left to lose asks nothing of you either.

That was the grief, if I'm honest. Not the blacklist itself. The relief.

I got out the map case—the old one, oilcloth, corners soft as leather from twenty years of hands—and spread the lowland sheets across the table. South, the valleys ran out into farm country and checkpoints. East, the range rose into the closed districts, all permit gates and patrol radios. West, the river.

The river. The Serranta, coming down out of the high basin in that long white staircase everyone called the Rivergate, though no map I'd ever seen showed why it was called that. Above a certain elevation the maps simply went vague—contours thinning out, features unnamed, a blankness that wasn't blank the way unmapped land is blank but blank the way a page is blank when someone has decided what shouldn't be written on it. I'd noticed it years ago and filed it under government laziness, which is what you file everything under when you still trust governments.

I traced the river down from the blank place, past the high gorges, past Ansel's Reach, past Lowmark, down to Kestrel Ford, where the water went wide and slow and the valley opened its hand. Where Maren lived. Where, two winters ago, a woman I'd never met had taken in a disgraced guide with a false name and a duffel bag and asked no questions except whether I ate meat.

Kestrel Ford was forty kilometers downstream. Close enough to reach in three hard days. Far enough that Halloran's warm mouth wouldn't carry there before I did.

I rolled the maps. I cleaned the stove. I wrote a note for Ostrick—*family matter, refund enclosed, sorry for the shortened trip*—and left it weighted under the tin plate, along with more money than the days deserved, because Marta Vane at least could afford a conscience.

Then I sat on the step with the last of the coffee and watched the dark come up the valley, and I let myself feel it properly for the first time: not anger, not injustice, just the plain weight of it. A life cut in half. Twenty years on one side of the line, two years of shadow on the other, and no way back across that didn't run through the people who'd cut it.

Somewhere upstream, in the blank space on the map, the river was doing whatever rivers do in the dark. Coming down. Carrying things.

I didn't know yet what it was carrying.

I went in, banked the fire, and lay down in my clothes with the duffel packed at the foot of the bed. Moving on. Again. The oldest skill I had.

In the morning, before light, a boy from the Ford would come up the trail with a crate strapped to a mule, and everything would start.
