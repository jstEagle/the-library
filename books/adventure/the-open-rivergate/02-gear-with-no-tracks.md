## 2. Gear With No Tracks

The knock came at dusk, which in the low valleys of the Serran range is less an hour than a mood—the light going amber and apologetic, the mountains putting on their evening air of indifference. I was rendering a ptarmigan that had died with more dignity than it had lived, and my hands were greasy, so I answered the door with them held up like a surgeon mid-operation.

Tomas Hedvik stood on my step holding a crate.

I should explain Tomas. He was seventy if he was a day, built like something the river had decided to keep rather than return, and he possessed the particular patience of a man who has spent six decades reading water and finding it consistently more honest than people. He fished the lower Serran out of season, trapped a little, and was one of perhaps four souls in the valley who knew the woman called "Lena Voss" who guided hunters through the foothills was not, in fact, named Lena Voss. He'd never said so aloud. That was the arrangement: he knew, I knew he knew, and neither of us spent the knowledge, because knowledge spent carelessly is just gossip with ambitions.

"Found this," he said, setting the crate down on my step without waiting for an invitation, which was correct, because I wasn't going to give him one while my hands looked like that. "Two miles up from the narrows. Wedged in the rocks above the high-water line."

"Yours?"

"Do I look like a man who owns government crates?"

He did not. He looked like a man who owned three shirts and resented even that number. But I made him say it anyway, because when a wilderness presents you with an object, the first question is always provenance and the second question is always *who else knows*, and Tomas was the sort of man whose answers came pre-honest.

"Nobody," he said. "Wasn't there in spring. Wasn't there last week, when I ran that stretch for eel. There Tuesday."

I washed my hands. I let him inside, which cost me something—I have a rule about letting people see where and how I live, a rule born of experience and sharpened by necessity—but the crate was already sitting on my step radiating questions, and rules are for weather you can predict.

* * *

Here's what was in it, once we pried the latches—military pattern, aluminum, the kind of box designed to survive being dropped from things:

A theodolite, wrapped in oiled cloth, dry as a sermon. A field case of survey instruments—levels, ranging rods' tips, a plumb bob worn bright at the ring where a hand had gripped it ten thousand times. Six sealed cans of ration biscuits, government stamp, batch number legible. A canvas satchel containing notebooks—blank ones, which disappointed me more than I can properly articulate—and a leather roll of drafting pens, each nib capped, each cap seated. A signal mirror. Two emergency blankets, still in their foil sleeves. And at the bottom, folded with a care that struck me as almost liturgical, a survey team's banner: the double-headed crest of the State Survey Directorate, the kind they photograph holding when a project begins, so the newspapers have something to print besides numbers.

Dry. Every bit of it dry. The biscuits hadn't swelled. The foil hadn't dulled. The notebook paper lay flat and unwarped, and paper tells you things—it drinks humidity the way a drunk drinks anything, and these pages had tasted nothing.

That was the first wrongness, and it was considerable. The Serran river in autumn runs cold enough to kill a swimmer in nine minutes and damp enough to rot a leather boot in nine days. Anything that had traveled *in* that water would bear the water's signature. This crate bore none. It might have been packed yesterday in some quartermaster's warm office and set on my step by a considerate ghost.

The second wrongness was location. I got the map out while Tomas watched me over his tea—he drank tea the way other men drink whiskey, slowly and with judgment—and I made him show me the spot twice. Above the narrows, two miles up from where he'd said, wedged in a cleft of granite at the base of a scree chute. Which meant, if you know the ground the way I know it—and I do, or I did, before knowing it became a liability—that crate sat better than eleven kilometers from the nearest legal trailhead, in country closed to civilian travel since the incident, in terrain so broken that even I would have wanted daylight and a good reason.

Eleven kilometers. Through trackless country. Carrying nothing, apparently, because there were no tracks.

"No footprints?" I asked him, for the third time.

"Lior." He used my real name deliberately, quietly, the way you set a heavy thing down. "There was frost on those rocks every morning this week. A goat leaves prints up there. I saw goat prints. I saw marmot sign. I saw nothing with boots."

No footprints. No drag marks. No scree displaced along any approach line I could reconstruct—and believe me, I reconstructed all of them, sitting up half the night with the map and a pencil, running lines from every trailhead, every ridge saddle, every game corridor, looking for the path a crate takes when it walks itself uphill.

Crates don't walk uphill. So someone had carried it, or flown it, or lowered it, and none of those options left the absence of evidence that this crate wore like cologne.

* * *

You want to know what I did with it, and I'll tell you, but first you need to understand the shape of my life at that point, because a riddle means different things to different people depending on how empty their evenings are.

Mine were very empty.

I rose before light and took clients—mostly hunters, mostly foreign, mostly men who wanted to shoot something famous and go home with a story—and I walked them through foothill country I could navigate blindfolded, smiling the smile of a woman named Lena Voss, and I brought them back, and I counted money that didn't feel like mine, and I ate dinner alone in a cabin I kept deliberately bare, because bare rooms leave quickly. Somewhere south of here was a life I'd been evicted from: a name on guide registries, a reputation, a face that appeared in trade journals next to words like *reliable* and *decorated*. All of it gone in an afternoon, four years ago, over a client's death that was nobody's fault except everyone's, most especially the fault of maps that lied and the people who'd approved the lies. I'd stopped asking why the truth hadn't saved me. Truth is a currency; it spends differently depending on whose pocket it's in.

So when a crate appears out of clean air, dry as bone, eleven kilometers from anywhere, stamped with the crest of the same government that erased me—

Understand: I didn't think *conspiracy*. I'm not a romantic. My first thought was *airdrop*. Supply drops happen; survey teams get resupplied in remote basins; a crate tumbling from altitude into scree explains the location and the lack of tracks, if not entirely the dryness.

But then I turned the crate over, and found the stenciled markings, and stopped being able to breathe quite normally for a moment.

Team designation: SS-7. Sector assignment: Upper Serran Basin. And beneath that, in smaller stencil, the phrase that rearranged my whole evening: **RIVERGATE SURVEY — RESTRICTED**.

The Rivergate. The basin above the falls, where the Serran pinches itself through a gorge and comes out the other side changed—colder, faster, carrying a mineral tang the old-timers swore by and the geologists ignored. Closed country. Not merely restricted—*closed*, the way a mouth closes. Nobody surveyed the Rivergate. Everybody knew that. It was one of those facts the valley ran on, like the ice-out date and the church bell and the understanding that you didn't ask the garrison at the checkpoint about their business.

Except somebody had surveyed it. Somebody had packed a theodolite and drafting pens and a banner for the photograph, and sent them up into the closed basin, and now the equipment was sitting in my cabin, having arrived without a history.

And the survey team—here was the thing I made myself say plainly, because avoiding plain speech is how people drown—had been missing for five weeks. Everyone knew that too. It was in the low-country papers between the grain prices and the horse sales: *State survey team overdue in upper Serran sector; search ongoing.* Five weeks. Long enough for the official searches to thin out, for the word *tragic* to start replacing the word *missing*, for families to begin the terrible arithmetic of hope.

Long enough for their gear to come downstream.

Gear comes downstream. Bodies come downstream. That's the river's oldest service, older than any mill or bridge: it returns what the mountain takes. Fishermen find jackets. Trappers find packs. In bad years, worse things. The river delivers, and the living identify, and grief gets its paperwork done.

But this crate hadn't come downstream. It had come *upstream*—or rather, it had never traveled at all. It had simply arrived, intact and dry, above the high-water line, in a cleft too precise to be chance deposition, as though placed. As though *left*.

Left for whom?

I want to be honest with you, since honesty is the one luxury I've tried to keep through all of this: for about an hour, I considered burning it. Or rather—not burning it, I'm not an idiot, aluminum doesn't burn—but burying it, walking away, going back to my ptarmigan and my false name and my small, survivable life. Because I knew exactly what that crest meant. I knew what it meant to hold government property that the government hadn't reported losing. I knew how questions worked when they were pointed at you instead of by you. Four years ago I'd learned that the state does not lose things; it *relocates* them, along with blame, and the person left holding the relocated object becomes the new location of the problem.

Tomas watched me weigh it. He's a sharp old bird under all that weathered calm.

"You're going to keep it," he said.

"I'm considering my options."

"You're going to keep it." He drained his tea and stood, joints announcing themselves one at a time. "Because you're the only one up this valley who reads ground, and whoever left that crate either knows that or will learn it, and either way it's yours now. That's how the mountain does things. It doesn't ask."

"That's not how ownership works, Tomas."

"It's exactly how ownership works up here." At the door he paused, and gave me the look—the one I imagine he gives the river when it rises past a mark no one else has noticed yet. "One more thing. The cleft it was wedged in. Granite chute, north face, shaded all day. Frost never melted off it this week."

"So?"

"So the rocks around the crate were clear. Melted. Like something had been lying against them, keeping them warm." He shrugged into his coat. "Warm rock, cold week, no tracks. You read ground, girl. Read that."

Then he left, and I stood in my bare cabin with a government crate full of impossible cleanliness, and outside the dark came down the way it does in the high valleys—all at once, like a lid.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I'll save us both the hour-by-hour account and give you the summary: I examined everything twice, catalogued everything once, and by midnight had moved from *unease* through *fascination* into the particular white-hot focus that I recognize, in retrospect, as the beginning of obsession. It felt like the old days. It felt like planning a route. It felt, God help me, like being *myself* again, and that should have warned me more than the warm rocks did.

The instruments told me things. Serial numbers, matched sets, calibration tags dated fourteen months back—a long deployment, fully equipped, funded like a serious operation. The ration batch codes were recent. The banner was creased from folding but had never been flown; whatever photograph it was packed for hadn't been taken, or hadn't survived, or hadn't been allowed.

And the crate itself—here was the detail that finally broke the night open for me—was *clean*. Not just dry. Clean. No grit in the seams. No pine needles. No rodent droppings, no insect casings, no spider silk. Eleven kilometers of wild country, weeks of exposure, and the interior was cleaner than my kitchen. Something had sealed it—or someone had opened it, tended it, and resealed it. Recently. Carefully.

Someone was maintaining this equipment. Someone upstream, in the closed basin, was keeping government gear dry and serviced and hidden, and had chosen—deliberately, with intent—to put one crate where the only person in the valley who reads ground would eventually find it.

Not the garrison. Not the search parties, who'd walked the legal trails and found nothing and gone home. Not the papers. Me. Or someone like me—which is to say, someone who could follow the message the rest of the way upstream.

I sat back on my floor among the neat, dry, impossible order of it, and I laughed, once, without much humor. Because this was a trap or a plea, and I genuinely could not tell which, and both possibilities pointed the same direction: upriver. Into the Rivergate. Past the falls, past the closure line, past every rule I'd spent four years learning to live inside.

The stove ticked as it cooled. The river spoke its one long sentence below the bluff, the same sentence it always speaks, patient and indifferent: *everything comes down eventually.*

Everything except, apparently, this.

I looked at the stenciled crest one more time. Then I began, methodically, the way you pack for a route you may not come back from, to make a list of what I'd need—just a list, just ink on paper, nothing committed, nothing anyone could call evidence of intent.

Which is when the dogs started up at the checkpoint, three kilometers down-valley, and did not stop.
