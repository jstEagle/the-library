## 3. The Summons She Refused

He came up the valley road on a Tuesday, which told me something before he said a word. Government men travel on Tuesdays. It's the day that feels official without being memorable—no one remembers a Tuesday the way they remember a Friday or a Sunday, and men whose work is editing stories prefer days that don't stick.

I was splitting wood when his car stopped at the bottom of my track, because I split wood most mornings whether I needed it or not. There's a kind of honesty in an axe. You swing it, and either the log opens or it doesn't, and nobody files a report about it afterward.

The man who climbed out wore civilian clothes that had been chosen by someone who understood civilians only as a category. Good boots, wrong laces. A wool coat cut for a city, buttoned against a cold he clearly hadn't expected to be this personal. He stood at the foot of my steps with his hands visible at his sides—a courtesy, or a technique—and looked at my cabin the way surveyors look at ground they intend to reclassify.

"Almeida," he said.

Not a question. Not "Ms. Almeida" or "the woman who lives here." Just the name, dropped like a stone into still water, and we both watched the ripples.

"You've got me confused with someone," I said. "This is the Halden place. I keep it for a cousin."

"Lior Almeida." He said it again, slower, giving each syllable its due weight, like a man laying cards face-up one at a time. "Certified high-altitude guide, Serran Mountain Guild, class one. Struck from the register four years ago. Currently residing under the name Halden, which belongs to a woman who died in Lowmark two winters back and would be surprised to learn she's been renting out her cabin."

So much for the cousin. I set the axe down—not because I was frightened, but because a woman holding an axe while talking to a government man reads a certain way, and I wasn't going to give him the scene he'd already written in his head.

"And you are?"

"Captain Voss. State Liaison Office." He produced a leather folder but didn't open it, which is a trick I recognized. The folder is a prop. The offer inside it is the weapon. "May I come up? It's cold, and what I have to say goes better sitting down."

"It goes better not at all, usually."

"I imagine so." He smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced but not false, the smile of a man who has learned that honesty disarms better than charm. "Four years ago you were offered amnesty. You took it. It cost you your license, your name, and your livelihood. I'm aware of this. I read the file. All of it—including the parts that were redacted before you ever saw them."

That landed somewhere under my ribs, where I keep the things I don't let reach my face. "You're very well informed for a liaison."

"It's my job to be well informed." He glanced at the woodpile, the smoke rising straight from my chimney, the frost still silvering the fence rails. "It's also my job to ask for help from the best person available, and in this valley, for this particular piece of country, the best person available is a disgraced guide living under a dead woman's name. So here I am. On a Tuesday. Asking."

* * *

We sat at my table because refusing him coffee felt like admitting he mattered, and I'd rather he think I was indifferent than rattled. He wrapped both hands around the mug like a man warming himself on more than heat, and laid the folder open between us.

Photographs first. Eight faces, printed on state stock, the kind of paper that's designed to survive being handled by many people who don't care about them. A survey team. Geologists, hydrologists, a cartographer—young, hollow-cheeked, glasses too big for his face. And a woman in her fifties with gray coming in at her temples and eyes that went through the camera lens and kept going.

Ilsa Brandt.

I want to say my heart did something dramatic. It didn't. It went very quiet, the way a river goes quiet above a drop.

"The Meridian Survey," Voss said. "Nine personnel, entered the upper basin three weeks ago to complete a resource assessment. They were due out eleven days ago. They have not come out." He turned a photograph toward me: a tent camp seen from the air, small as a stitch mark on green cloth. "There's been no radio contact since day six. No distress signal. Nothing."

"People go quiet in the high country all the time. Radios fail. Batteries die. Weather closes passes."

"For eleven days?"

"For eleven days, sometimes. Especially if there's a reason to stay quiet."

"What reason would there be?"

"That's their business, isn't it. Not mine."

Voss leaned back. He had the patience of a man who fishes with set lines rather than a rod—he puts the bait in the water and lets time do the pulling. "Here's the offer. Full restoration of your guild certification. Expungement of the incident record. Your name cleared, publicly, in writing. In exchange, you guide the search party into the basin. You know that country better than anyone alive who isn't currently missing in it."

I looked at the photographs instead of him. The young cartographer. The hollow cheeks. And Ilsa Brandt, whose testimony—or whose silence, depending on which version of events you believed—had ended my life as thoroughly as an avalanche ends a road.

"Say the rest," I said.

"There is no rest."

"There's always rest. Men like you don't climb valleys to offer clean deals." I pushed the folder an inch toward him. "The last amnesty I was offered came with a clause I found out about later. Sign here, walk free—and by signing, accept the official finding that the client's death was guide error. My error. Signature as confession. Nobody told me that page was a noose until it was around my neck. So say the rest, Captain, or take your folder and your Tuesday and drive back down to wherever stories get written."

For a long moment he studied me, and I'll grant him this: he didn't perform offense, didn't reach for indignation the way most officials do when a civilian sees through them. He just looked tired, briefly, in a way that seemed real.

"The search is classified," he said quietly. "That's the part I'm permitted to tell you. There are elements of the team's assignment I'm not permitted to discuss, and the terms of any agreement would include confidentiality provisions. I won't pretend those provisions don't exist. But I will tell you that nothing you signed last time would resemble anything you'd sign this time. Different office. Different oversight."

"Different noose, same rope maker."

"You're allowed to hate the state, Ms. Almeida. Plenty of people do it professionally." He closed the folder. "But eight people are missing up there, and whatever you think of me, some of them are someone's children. The weather window closes in three weeks. After that, the storm season takes the basin, and if they're alive, they winter in it—if they can. If they can't—" He stopped himself, and I watched a man decide not to finish a sentence, which is its own kind of confession.

"If they can't," I said for him, "then the mountain keeps them, and the state gets to write whatever ending it likes. Is that the shape of it?"

He stood, buttoned the city coat, and left a card on the table—cream stock, embossed, absurdly clean for this valley. "Three days," he said. "I'm at the garrison post at Ford Hollow. Think about the names, not the politics. That's all I'd ask of anyone."

At the door he paused. "One more thing, and then I'll leave you in peace. The equipment manifest. Nine packs went up. We recovered two, downstream, snagged in willows below the gorge. Both dry. Both intact. Both packed."

"Packed how?"

"Neatly. Bedrolls rolled, stoves disassembled and nested, food secured. As if the owners had broken camp properly and walked away." He met my eyes. "People who fall into rivers don't fold their gear first, Ms. Almeida. Whatever happened up there, somebody planned it. I'd very much like to know what they were planning for."

Then he was gone, down the track, and the car sound faded, and the valley closed over the silence behind him like water over a thrown stone.

I sat at the table a long while with the card in front of me. Cream stock. Embossed letters. I turned it over. Blank. Of course blank—the message was never on the card; the message was that he could find me, that the name I'd built out of a dead woman's identity was worth exactly as much as the paper it wasn't written on.

Eight faces. I made myself look at them again, one at a time, the way you check a rope knot—deliberately, twice. The geologists I didn't know. The cartographer I didn't know. Ilsa Brandt I knew like a scar knows rain.

And underneath the old anger, unwelcome as water finding a crack, a question had begun to move: *packed neatly.* Who breaks camp in the middle of a survey and walks away from everything—wages, reputation, rescue—with their bedroll rolled?

People who aren't lost, I thought. People who are hiding.

* * *

I didn't sleep well, which was fine, because I'd planned not to.

After Voss left I did the things a careful woman does. I walked my boundaries in the failing light and read the ground the way other people read newspapers. Cattle tracks along the south fence, a fox's signature crossing the creek, and on the north side, where the alders thin out near the old logging spur—nothing. Too much nothing. No deer, though the browse was fresh. Game avoids places where men have recently passed, and the animals had voted with their feet.

So I banked the stove, hung my pack by the door where it would look forgotten, and took myself up into the loft with the quilt and the patience of a stone. Above the loft boards, through a gap I'd widened years ago for exactly this purpose, I could see most of the main room, and hear all of it.

They came at two in the morning, which flattered me. Amateurs search at midnight, when every creak means something and fear makes noise. Professionals wait for the deepest trough of the night, when even dogs give up, and they came quiet—two of them, maybe three, boots wrapped or removed. I heard the latch lift without a sound, which meant they'd oiled it, which meant they'd watched the cabin long enough to know the latch complained. Days, probably. Since before Voss knocked, I'd have bet.

A pencil-thin light moved across the floorboards. They worked the room in the order professionals work rooms: doorways first, sight lines, then the table where Voss's card had sat—I'd left it there deliberately, face-down, a crumb for them to follow. Then the shelves. Then the bedroom, slow, methodical, drawers opened and closed with a softness that must have taken practice.

They didn't find what they wanted, because what they wanted wasn't there. What they wanted, I suspected, was the thing Voss hadn't said aloud: whatever the survey team had been doing that required a classified search, and whatever evidence of it might have washed downstream into the hands of a woman who lived alone and asked no questions. The crate was under the floor beneath the stove, wrapped in oilcloth, and I'd have put money on the fact that they never even considered it, because I'd scattered sawdust and split kindling over those boards until they looked like forty years of honest neglect.

What they did find was my journal—or the journal I keep for them, the one with dull entries about weather and firewood and aching knees, written in a hand slightly rounder than mine. I heard the pages turn, unhurried. Let them read it. Let them file it. A bored woman in a borrowed cabin is exactly the story I want in their report.

Then one of them spoke, barely above breath, and the words drifted up through the gap in the boards like cold air finding a crack.

"—manifest doesn't match. Two packs recovered, nine issued. If she's got the others—"

"She hasn't. Voss checked the story. She hasn't been upstream in months."

"Then where's the ninth pack?"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that settles on a room like snow. Somewhere below me a man was doing arithmetic with missing equipment, and I lay still as timber and listened to him arrive at the answer neither of us liked.

"They didn't lose their gear," he said finally. "They took it with them. All of it. You don't carry nine packs out of a basin unless—" A pause. "Unless you're not planning to come back down the way you went up."

"Or unless they went higher."

"Nobody goes higher. There's nothing up there but the source and the legal line."

"Then why," the voice said, very quietly, "does the map show the river starting thirty kilometers lower than it actually does?"

I stopped breathing.

Because I knew that map. Every guide in the Serrans knew that map—the official survey, the one hanging in the guild hall, the one the permits were written against. The headwaters marked at the Grey Stair, tidy and legal and closed above. And every guide also knew, in the wordless way guides know things, that the river ran longer than its own paperwork. We called it the Rivergate, the hidden stretch above the line, and we didn't go there, partly because it was forbidden and mostly because the ones who talked about going there were the kind who came back wrong or didn't come back.

But hearing it said aloud—in my cabin, at two in the morning, by a man whose job was to make sure no one said it aloud—that was different. That was the sound of a story being edited in real time, and the editor's pen slipping.

The light clicked off. Boots, soft as falling snow, crossed my floor. The latch settled home with its little complaint, oiled almost to silence. A car engine turned over far down the valley, patient as a heartbeat, and faded.

I stayed in the loft until the sky went gray at the edges, not moving, turning it over. A survey team vanishes with all its gear, packed neatly, upward. An official map that lies about where the river begins. A captain who offers amnesty with one hand and searches my cabin with the other, on the same day, and calls it procedure.

Eight people hadn't gotten lost. Eight people had *gone* somewhere—and the state was less afraid of where they'd gone than of what they might say when they got there.

Below the floorboards, under the stove, wrapped in oilcloth, the survey crate waited with its government stamps and its dry, intact, impossible cargo. I'd been avoiding opening it for a week, telling myself it was evidence, that touching it made me a target.

But I'd just spent a night listening to men hunt a ninth pack through my kitchen, and I understood now that I'd been a target since the river handed me that crate. The only question left was whether I'd be a blind one.

When the light was strong enough to work by, I pried up the boards.
