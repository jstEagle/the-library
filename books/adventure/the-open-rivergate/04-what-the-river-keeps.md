## 4. What the River Keeps

The river had a voice, and after three days alone with it I had started answering.

Not out loud. Mostly. But there's a particular kind of silence you get in the high valleys—the kind that isn't empty so much as attentive—and a person fills it however she can. I'd narrate my route choices. I'd argue with the weather. I'd give names to the boulders that tried to kill me. The one at the second gorge I called Aldous, because it had the smug, immovable quality of a man who has never once been wrong in a meeting.

"You're being ridiculous," I told myself on the morning I found the cairn. "This is what happens when you talk to rocks."

The cairn, for its part, said nothing. It just sat there above the flood line on the north bank, where cairns have no business being, built by hands that understood stone the way I understood it—which is to say, built to last and built to be found, and not by deer.

I should have walked past it.

Let's be honest about that up front. I was three days upstream of the last legal trail, in a basin the maps shaded with that particular bureaucratic gray that means *don't*, carrying food for a week and reasons for none of it, and every survival instinct I'd spent fifteen years sharpening said: *Lior. Turn around. Curiosity is how clients die and how guides get blamed.*

But here is the thing about instincts: they're calibrated to the dangers you already know. A rockfall, a whiteout, a river in spate—those I could read like a menu. This was different. This was the wilderness presenting me with a sentence I couldn't parse, and I have never in my life been able to leave a sentence unfinished. It's why I read the last page of novels first and then the whole book anyway, furious the entire time.

I crouched beside the cairn. Five stones, flat ones, stacked with the long faces inward and the weight settled—someone who knew masonry, or at least knew mountains. Not a trail marker. Trail markers are built fast and sloppy by people in a hurry to be somewhere else. This had been built by someone who wanted it to survive winters.

The capstone was the size of a loaf of bread. I lifted it with the respect you give a thing that might be nothing, and underneath, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax that had gone amber with age, was a page.

Just a page. Torn from a bound journal, the ragged spine edge still showing the ghost of the binding thread. The oilcloth had done its work; the paper was dry as August. Someone had gone to considerable trouble to keep exactly one page of their own handwriting alive.

I sat down on a stone that wasn't Aldous and read it three times.

The top half was survey data. Elevations, bearings, flow rates, written in the cramped, disciplined hand of someone trained to make numbers legible under bad conditions. I know that hand. Every guide knows that hand. It's the handwriting of people who expect their notes to be read by lawyers or coroners.

The coordinates were what stopped my breath. I won't pretend I read them like a native—I've spent more years reading ridgelines than reading latitudes—but I know the legal boundary of the Serran reserve the way you know the property line of the neighbor whose dog hates you. These were above it. Well above it. Deep in the gray shading, in the heart of the Rivergate basin where the maps stop pretending to be maps and start being arguments for why you shouldn't go.

Someone had surveyed the forbidden country. Carefully. In winter, by the notations about ice anchors. And then, instead of carrying the data home, they had built a little stone safe deposit box above the flood line and left it for the river to keep.

Below the numbers, in a different pressure of pen—harder, faster, the nib nearly through the paper—was a single line:

*The map is wrong on purpose.*

* * *

I did what any sensible person does with a piece of evidence that could ruin or kill them. I read it again, photographed nothing because I don't own a camera that isn't my own memory, and then I sat with the page in my lap for the better part of an hour while the river said nothing useful.

Here's what I could reconstruct. A survey team comes up the valley—government issue, I'd bet the boots on it, because only government work bothers to be this meticulous and this secret at the same time. They go past the boundary. They find something worth measuring. And then at least one of them decides the findings are dangerous, and rather than destroy them or carry them home, they waterproof a page and build it into a cairn.

That's not hiding. Hiding is throwing it in a crevasse. This is *archiving*. This is someone who wanted the page to outlast whatever happened to them.

*Whatever happened to them.*

I turned the phrase over and felt it turn back on me. Because here's the other thing I knew, the thing the whole low valley knew, the thing the checkpoints and the closed roads and the state liaison's polite letters all gestured at without saying: a survey team had gone into the Serran highlands in the spring and had not come down. The official word was *delayed by conditions*. The unofficial word, in the taverns, delivered at the volume of men who don't want to be overheard, was nothing at all, because the unofficial word had been purchased.

I'd stayed out of it. That had been my whole strategy for four years—stay out of everything, be no one, guide hunters who couldn't tell a contour line from a contour of my jaw, keep my head below the ridgeline of public attention. The blacklist had taught me the arithmetic of invisibility: one name on a list, one career subtracted, one life cut neatly in half at the fold. I knew exactly what it cost to be *someone* in this country.

And now I was holding a piece of paper that would make me very much someone.

I should mention what the blacklist was, I suppose, in case you're new. Three years guiding, spotless record, and then a client named Harl Wennerd died on a route I'd chosen, in weather I'd called, on a slope I'd judged sound. The inquest found my judgment faulty. The guild struck my certification. What I learned later—what I've had years to turn over like a stone with something dead underneath—is that the route map I'd been given had been approved by people who should have flagged it, and the weather window I'd trusted had been signed off by an office that never once looked at the sky. My judgment wasn't faulty. My sources were. But the sources had names, and names protect each other, and I had a name too, just not the right kind.

So when I tell you I know what it means when a map is wrong on purpose, I want you to understand I'm not being dramatic. I'm being precise. I have personally walked into the gap between the map and the truth, and I buried a man there.

*The map is wrong on purpose.*

Six words, and they rearranged the entire valley in front of me. The gray shading on the maps stopped being caution and became concealment. The closed roads stopped being administrative and became architectural. And the vanished survey team stopped being a tragedy and became a question with a very sharp edge: *wrong on purpose by whom, and wrong to hide what?*

The river kept moving. Rivers are smug that way. They know where everything goes eventually.

* * *

I made camp that night two ridges back from the cairn, which is the kind of decision that looks paranoid until you remember that paranoia is just pattern recognition with the volume turned up. Cold camp. No fire. I ate cold oats and cold venison and told myself it was character-building.

The page I kept against my chest, inside the inner pocket where I used to keep the letters from my sister before she stopped writing. I'd like to say I was being tactical. Mostly I think I wanted it somewhere I could feel my own heartbeat against it, to confirm that at least one of us was still alive and taking notes.

Here's what I did instead of sleeping. I went through everything the page implied, the way you'd probe a tooth that's started to ache.

One: the survey was real, official, and extended into closed country. That meant authorization from somewhere high, because the boundary isn't the kind of line a field team crosses by accident or ambition. Someone with a title had signed the order.

Two: the data was dangerous. Not *embarrassing*—dangerous. People don't build wax-sealed cairns over embarrassing data. They build them over data that gets teams disappeared and guides blacklisted and valleys closed.

Three: the team hadn't all agreed. The line about the map was written in anger, or fear, or both, and the cairn itself was a schism you could hold in one hand. Somebody on that team had looked at what they'd found and decided the state couldn't be trusted with it. Somebody else had presumably disagreed, and I found myself wondering, in the ugly small hours, which disagreement the mountain had settled.

Four—and this was the one I kept circling back to, the one with teeth—*someone had to have built that cairn recently enough that the wax hadn't cracked*. Which meant the team's story wasn't finished. Which meant somewhere above me, in the gray country, the rest of that sentence was still being written.

I lay in the dark and listened to the river and understood, with the clarity that only arrives at two in the morning to ruin you, that I had already made my choice. That was the humiliating part. I'd spent the whole day telling myself I was deciding whether to go on, weighing it, being the responsible adult who'd learned her lesson about curiosity and consequence. But I'd cached my return-route food at the second gorge that morning. I'd taken the north bank at the fork where the south bank led home. I'd talked to the river like a partner on a rope.

The body decides before the mind consents. Every guide knows this. It's why we train the body so hard.

* * *

Morning came up gray and indifferent. I broke camp, checked the page one more time—dry, intact, still incriminating—and did the one smart thing available to me, which was to build a false cairn of my own a mile downstream with a decoy oilcloth packet inside containing, after some consideration, a note that said *Nice try.*

Petty? Yes. But if someone came looking for the cairn, I wanted them to know they'd been beaten to it, and I wanted them to wonder by whom, and I have learned that confusion is a perfectly respectable defensive position. Also, I'll admit, there was a version of me that had been silent for four years, and she had opinions about leaving messages in stone.

Then I went upstream.

The valley narrowed the way valleys do when they're hiding something—shoulders drawing in, walls steepening, the river getting louder and more proprietary. The legal boundary was a survey post, rotted and leaning, with the state's faded paint still ghosting the wood. Crossing it felt less like trespass than like stepping off the edge of a sentence. On the far side the maps simply gave up: no trails, no elevations, no names. Just gray, and the river running through it like a vein through a closed fist.

My name is Lior Almeida. I want that on the record, in my own hand, the way the surveyor put their line in the cairn—hard, fast, nib through the paper. For four years I've been someone else, a false name on guide registries, a woman with no history and no claims, and I told myself that was survival. It was. It's also a kind of slow drowning, being no one. You stay under so long you forget there's a surface.

The page in my pocket was a surface. It was also, I knew with equal certainty, a hook.

Because here's the arithmetic I couldn't escape, the one I'd run all night: whoever built that cairn had trusted the river to keep their secret. But rivers don't keep secrets. Rivers *deliver*. That's the entire job description. Everything upstream arrives downstream eventually—silt, seeds, bodies, truths. The surveyor hadn't hidden the page. They'd *mailed* it, to whoever was stubborn enough to walk up the current and check the post.

And if I could find it, so could the people who made the team vanish. The state had checkpoints on every road out of the low valleys, liaison officers with polite titles, and—this was the part I'd been refusing to look at directly—a very specific interest in a basin that was supposedly empty. Empty basins don't get sealed roads. Empty basins don't get *letters*.

I climbed through the morning, past the boundary post, into the gray, and the higher I went the more the valley felt less like wilderness and more like a room where something had been interrupted. A cairn built to last. A page built to be found. A team built to disappear. None of it was natural. All of it was arranged.

At midday I found the second sign, and this one took the choice away entirely.

Boot prints. At least two sets, weeks old by the weathering, heading upstream. Government-issue soles—I knew the tread pattern from the checkpoints, from the escort officers who'd once walked me to a border I wasn't allowed to cross. And pressed beside them, lighter, half-erased: a third set. Smaller stride. Uneven, favoring the left.

Someone was ahead of me. Someone was possibly running from the people whose prints walked beside them.

I stood in the gray country with the page against my ribs and felt the thrill and the dread arrive together, the way they always do, twins holding hands. Because this was it—this was the feeling I'd been blacklisted for chasing, the feeling of being the only person on the mountain who knows something true. It's the best feeling in the world. It's the last feeling a lot of people get.

"The map is wrong on purpose," I said out loud, to the river, to the valley, to whatever attentive silence was keeping the minutes.

And downstream, far below, at the mouth of the valley where the roads were sealed and the letters were polite, I would later learn that someone had just arrived asking after a woman with my face and my old, dangerous name.

But that morning all I knew was the river, pulling upstream at my back like a hand at my collar, and the prints ahead of me going up into the gray, and the six words in my pocket that I could no longer give back.

I followed them.
