## 5. The Defector

I knew someone was in my cabin before I opened the door, because the third stair from the bottom had stopped complaining.

That stair had creaked under my boots for two years—through mud season, through frost, through the night I'd carried a drunk fur-trapper up to my porch and let him vomit into the woodpile. It was a reliable stair. A loyal stair. And now it was silent as a held breath, which meant somebody had stepped over it with the care of a man who understood stairs the way I understood them: as informants.

I set down the pail of water I'd hauled up from the river and considered my options. The sensible option was to walk back down, circle through the trees, and wait for whoever it was to leave. The sensible option had kept me alive for two years under a name that wasn't mine.

But the crate had been sitting on my table for six days now, government-issue and dry as a sermon, and the sensible part of me had been losing arguments all week.

I went around back instead, quiet on the frozen ground, and came in through the gap where the porch boards didn't quite meet the wall. From there I could see the whole main room by the light of my own banked stove. Someone was kneeling at my supply shelf with his back to me, going through my stores with an efficiency that was almost insulting. He'd already found the dried fish. He was working on the oats.

He was thin enough that his jacket hung off him like laundry left out in wind, and he moved like a man who hadn't slept indoors in weeks. He also, I noticed, had laid everything he touched back exactly where it had been. Neat. Methodical. A man who catalogued things for a living, maybe.

I picked up the iron pan hanging by the door and said, "Touch the tea and you die."

He froze. Then, without turning around, he said, "You're Lior Almeida."

The pan got heavier in my hand. Nobody had spoken that name aloud in this valley since the day I arrived under another one. "Wrong," I said. "Nobody here by that name. Just a guide who doesn't like thieves."

"You're her." He turned then, slowly, hands visible, and I got my first good look at his face. Hollow cheeks, a week of beard, eyes red-rimmed from wind or weeping or both. "I've been looking for you for eleven days. Do you know how hard it is to find a person who's been erased? There's no record of you anywhere. No permits, no licenses. You don't exist. I checked every registry between here and the capital."

"Congratulations," I said. "Now put the oats down and explain why I shouldn't break your wrist."

"I'm Elias Renner." He said it like he was handing me something fragile. "Cartographer. Second Serran Survey Expedition. And before you swing that pan—I'm the reason you should burn that crate instead of staring at it."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about walking out of a mountain range that has decided to kill you: the walking is the easy part.

Elias Renner, writing this now in the margin of a map he no longer has any right to carry:

I walked out of the high country on the ninth of the month with a pack full of instruments I was supposed to have died with, and I did it badly. I fell twice on the scree. I drank water I shouldn't have. I spent three days in a hollow under a deadfall eating pine bark because moving meant being seen, and being seen meant being found, and being found meant—

Well. Being found meant what happened to Oskar.

So when I say I spent eleven days looking for Lior Almeida, understand what that cost. Every trailhead had a patrol car at it by the fourth day. Every village had new men in grey coats asking cheerful questions about strangers. The state wanted its missing surveyors found, alive preferably, and it wanted even more to find out who else knew what the survey had found. I was worth more to them silent than alive, and they were very thorough about silence.

But I needed her. Not the guide on the registries—the ghost. Because a ghost knows how to move through a country that wants you gone, and I had become, in the space of nine days, the most hunted man in the Serrans.

I tracked her the way she must once have tracked game. A woman buying too much salt in Lowmark. A stranger with a guide's shoulders renting a room in Kestrel Ford and leaving before dawn. A cabin upstream of nowhere, tucked into the alder like a stone in a shoe. I watched it for two days. I counted her steps. On the third night, hungry enough to be stupid, I went in for food.

And she caught me, because of course she did. She caught me with an iron pan and a voice like a drawn wire, and when she heard her own name she went white in a way that told me everything: this was not a woman hiding comfortably. This was a woman whose old life was a wound she kept bandaged under other names.

She made me sit at her table with my hands flat where she could see them. She took my knife, my matches, and the folded papers from my inner pocket, and she read them by lamplight while I watched her face change.

"What are these?" she said finally.

"Route maps," I said. "The ones we filed. And the ones we actually walked."

"They're not the same."

"No," I said. "They're not."

* * *

Lior again.

Let me tell you what I believed about the vanished survey team, after six days of turning that crate over in my mind like a stone looking for the fossil inside. I believed accident. Rivers take people; mountains take people; I had buried enough clients to know the arithmetic of it. Eleven souls goes up, weather turns, river spikes, and the state sends searchers and holds funerals. Tragic. Unusual only in its size.

Then a man sat at my table and told me, in the flat voice of someone reciting a confession he'd rehearsed until it stopped sounding true, that the team had voted.

"Voted on what?"

"On disappearing." He rubbed his face with both hands. "We found something in the basin. Something the contract said belonged to the state and the contract was wrong about. Half the team wanted to go public. Half wanted to come home, file the report, collect the pay, and never think about it again. And the escorts—" he stopped, restarted. "The escorts weren't escorts. Not really. They reported to a different ministry than we did. We figured that out too late."

"So you faked it. All of you. Eleven people just—walked off the map."

"Nine voted yes. Two voted no. Oskar was one of the noes." His voice did something complicated on the name. "He changed his mind about changing his mind, later. Too late for it to matter."

"And the other no?"

"He's still up there," Renner said. "Or he isn't. I don't know. I ran."

I looked at him for a long time. He looked back, and there was no performance in it—that particular shamelessness of the truly ashamed, the way they stop bothering to hide it because hiding takes calories they need elsewhere.

"Why come to me?" I asked. "Of all the ghosts in these hills, why knock on mine?"

"Because you're the best guide the Serran service ever blacklisted," he said, "and because I read your file before they burned it. And because—" he hesitated, and I watched him decide to spend the truth like a man spending his last coin, "—because Ilsa Brandt said if it ever came to this, find Almeida. She said you were the only one who'd ever caught the falsifications on your own routes. She said you had an instinct for maps that lie."

Something cold went through me at the name. Ilsa Brandt. My old chief. The woman whose testimony at the inquiry had been measured, regretful, and fatal—*the route deviation was Almeida's decision alone*. I hadn't heard her name spoken in two years and hearing it now felt like a splinter working toward the bone.

"If Brandt sent you," I said, "she can rot."

"She didn't send me. She doesn't know I'm here. She thinks I'm dead, probably. Everyone thinks everyone's dead; that was rather the point." He leaned forward. "Almeida. Listen to me. The crate your villager found—it's ours. Deliberately cached, deliberately findable. We seeded the river valley with evidence on our way up, so that if we vanished, someone would eventually start pulling the thread. That was the vote. Vanish loudly enough that the disappearance itself becomes the story, and the story forces the question: what did they find that was worth eleven lives?"

"And did it work?"

"It worked beautifully," he said bitterly. "For nine days. Then the escorts stopped searching and started sealing. Roads closed. Checkpoints doubled. They're not looking for bodies anymore—they're making sure nobody goes up and nobody comes down. Which means the plan failed, which means the ones who stayed up top are trapped up there, which means—" his voice cracked, just slightly, on the arithmetic, "—which means I'm the only one carrying the truth, and I can't get it anywhere that matters without a guide who doesn't exist."

I stood up. I walked to the window. Outside, the river ran dark and constant, doing the one honest thing in the valley.

"You understand the position you've put me in," I said. "You know my name. You know my cabin. If I turn you out and you're taken, you trade my name for your life inside an hour."

"I know."

"If I keep you, and anyone finds you here, I hang beside you."

"I know that too."

"So we have a problem, cartographer. Because I can't let you leave, and I can't let you stay, and I very much want to sleep tonight."

He almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, exhausted and crooked, but it was the first human thing I'd seen him do. "Then I suppose," he said, "we're each other's hostage."

"That's one word for it."

"What's yours?"

"Allies," I said, "if you can cook. Hostages if you can't."

* * *

From the notebook of E. Renner, undated, written by stove-light while the woman called Marta Voss—no, I won't write her false name either, I've done enough lying for one lifetime—while Lior slept in the chair by the door with her boots on:

She made me show her everything twice. The filed maps against the walked ones. The coordinates above the legal limit. The line in Halvard's journal—*the map is wrong on purpose*—and what it referred to: a thermal anomaly reading so large Halvard assumed instrument error until three separate instruments agreed.

She asked questions like a surgeon asks questions. Short, precise, aimed at things I'd have preferred to leave intact. How many escorts. What caliber rifles. Whether the radios were state-issue or private. Whether I'd seen the fissure myself or only the data. When I said I'd seen it—steam rising off open water in a basin that should be glacier, snow refusing to settle along a line half a mile long—she closed her eyes for a moment, and I understood that some part of her had been hoping I was mad.

Then she opened them and said, "Show me on the map where you crossed the gorge," and I knew I hadn't lost her yet.

I should say plainly what I have not said plainly to anyone: I am afraid of her, and I am afraid for her, and I cannot always tell the difference. She has the stillness of people who have already survived the worst thing and are simply waiting to see if there's a second one coming. When I told her about the villages—the dam plans, the reservoir line, the three names that will become three depths on a navigation chart—she didn't gasp or curse. She went to her shelf and took down a battered survey folio and found, within a minute, the exact contour of the gorge floor, and traced with one finger where the water would rise, and said nothing at all, which was worse than screaming.

I think she has people down there. In those villages. I think the blacklist drove her somewhere and the somewhere kept her, the way valleys keep strays.

We are allies now, or hostages, or whatever you call two people bound by the same secret, each holding the other's life as collateral against betrayal. She keeps my knife. I keep her real name, though she doesn't know that's what I'm keeping. Mutual destruction, politely arranged over dried fish.

Tomorrow she says we go downstream. To Kestrel Ford. She wouldn't tell me why. But she took down her old pack and started checking the straps, and there was something in her face I recognized from the high camp—from Halvard, the night before the vote. The look of a person who has stopped calculating whether to act and started calculating how.

God help us both. God help the valley. It's going to be spring flood soon, and rivers rise faster than the truth does.

Unless, of course, someone gives the truth a boat.
