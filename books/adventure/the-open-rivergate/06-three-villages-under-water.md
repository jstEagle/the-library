## 6. Three Villages Under Water

There is a particular kind of cowardice that looks, from the outside, like competence. I know this because I spent eleven years perfecting it. A cartographer's whole trade is deciding what to leave off the map—the swamp that isn't worth the ink, the dead-end trail, the village too small to matter. You tell yourself it's efficiency. You tell yourself the map is a tool, and tools aren't responsible for what hands do with them.

I believed that right up until the morning I drew a blue line across my own birthplace.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. That morning—my second day as Lior Almeida's unwelcome houseguest—I was still pretending to be a burglar with poor planning skills.

She'd tied me to her own chair with my own rope, which I felt was thematically appropriate, and she'd made tea anyway, which I hadn't expected. She set the mug on the floor where I could reach it without bending, an act of mercy so precisely calibrated it told me more about her than any interrogation would have. This was a woman who had been on the wrong end of someone else's arithmetic before. She knew exactly how much comfort to allow a prisoner.

"You've been in my supplies twice," she said. "You ate half a brick of pemmican and you took the good matches."

"The windproof ones."

"Those are for emergencies."

"I'm having one."

She didn't smile. I want that on the record: I tried, and she didn't smile. She just sat there across the cabin with her forearms on her knees, watching me the way you watch weather come over a ridge—not hostile, exactly, but already calculating what you'll cost her.

"Start talking," she said. "Why does a government cartographer walk out of a survey alive and then spend three weeks stealing from cabins?"

And here's the thing about being caught: once you're caught, honesty costs nothing extra. The lie was already spent. So I talked.

* * *

The Serran Geothermal Survey was supposed to be a six-week instrument run. Eleven of us—six scientists, four escorts, and me, the map man. Our mandate was narrow and boring: confirm the heat gradient readings from the aerial pass, stake the monitoring points, come home. The escorts were there because the upper basin sits above the legal survey limit, inside the Rivergate closure, and the state wanted witnesses who could shoot.

Captain Voss led them. You'll hear plenty about Voss later, most of it from people who never saw him drink his coffee black at five in the morning while everyone else complained, so let me say now that he was not a monster. He was worse, in a way. He was a man who followed orders and slept well, and I have never once managed to hate him properly for it, which annoys me.

We went up through the gorge in late summer, when the river runs low and mean between walls of granite. Four days of hauling instruments over scree. And then we came around the shoulder of the mountain called the Anvil, and the river did something no map said it could do.

It came out of the ground.

Not a spring. Not a seep. The entire Serran headwater boiled up out of a fissure lake—a mile-long tear in the earth, steaming, glassy, ringed in sulfur-yellow stone, feeding the river from below like a heart pumping into an artery. Every reading we took came back wrong. Too hot. Too steady. Too big. The aerial surveys had guessed at a minor thermal field. What was under us was a system, vast and connected, running under the whole upper valley like veins under skin.

Dr. Okafor, our hydrologist, stood at the edge of that lake for a long time without saying anything. Then she laughed. It wasn't a happy laugh.

"Do you know what this is?" she asked me. "This is free power for a century. This is the biggest geothermal field on the continent."

"And nobody knows," I said.

"Nobody knows." She looked at me, and I watched the thought arrive behind her eyes before she said it. "And if nobody knows, they'll dam the river instead."

* * *

Here is what I need you to understand about maps, since you're letting me tell you this story: a map is a decision. Every line on it is a sentence somebody wrote. And the state had already written its sentence years before we found the lake—it just hadn't known how good its handwriting would look.

The Rivergate Dam Project wasn't secret. It was public, proud, printed in glossy pamphlets. *Powering the Future of the Serran Provinces.* A concrete wall across the lower gorge, a reservoir flooding the upper valley, cheap electricity for the coastal cities. The only thing standing between the blueprint and the bulldozers was the inconvenient fact that the upper valley held three villages—Kestrel Ford, Lowmark, Ansel's Reach—and some four hundred people who would have to be "relocated."

Relocated. There's a word that does a lot of work. It sounds temporary, administrative, like moving furniture. It does not sound like what it is, which is drowning a church, two graveyards, a schoolhouse where my mother taught arithmetic for thirty years, and every fence post my grandfather ever drove into the ground at Kestrel Ford.

I was born in Kestrel Ford. Third house past the ford itself, the one with the blue door my father painted every spring until he died. When I was nine years old I fished the pool below the mill race. When I was sixteen I mapped the valley by hand, on school paper, because I wanted to be a cartographer and the valley was what I had. I drew every building. I drew the blue door.

Eleven years later the state handed me their base maps and asked me to draw the reservoir boundary, and I sat down with a ruler and drew a blue line that put my father's house forty feet under water, and my hand did not shake, because I was a professional, and professionals don't shake, and that is the whole obscene trick of it. Competence is just cowardice with better posture.

* * *

Lior listened to all of this without interrupting, which I have since learned is her most frightening quality. Most people interrupt. Interrupting means they're composing their answer while you talk; it means they're safe. Lior doesn't compose. She just takes things in, files them somewhere behind those flat grey eyes, and lets you hang yourself with your own rope—which, again, thematically appropriate.

"So the survey found the basin," she said finally. "The state wants the dam. Where does faking your own disappearance come in? That's not a surveyor's move. That's a desperate move."

"It became a desperate move on the fourth night," I said. "When Voss got new orders."

Because here's the part I still can't say without my voice going strange. The orders came down by radio, encrypted, and Voss read them alone in his tent, and afterward he was different. Quieter. The next morning he announced a change of plan: the team would extend the survey, push deeper into the closure zone, map the full extent of the thermal field. Officially: thoroughness.

Unofficially—and Dr. Okafor figured this out within a day, because she was smarter than the rest of us put together—the state had realized what it was sitting on. A geothermal field that size made the dam obsolete. Made it worse than obsolete: made it dangerous. Because a dam holds back a river, but nothing holds back heat. Build a hundred-meter wall of concrete across a valley laced with live fissures, flood it, load it, and wait. The pressure finds the cracks. The cracks find each other. Okafor ran the numbers twice and got the same answer both times: within twenty years of impoundment, induced seismicity along the fissure network, rupture of the geothermal seal, and contamination of the watershed for a hundred miles downstream. Poisoned water. Every town on the Serran, all the way to the coast.

They weren't going to build the dam despite the basin. They were going to build it *because* of the basin—or rather, because the dam project was already funded, already announced, already the Minister's legacy, and admitting the basin existed meant admitting the whole thing was a mistake. So the plan became: finish the survey quietly, classify everything, demolish the villages on schedule, pour the concrete, and let the future sort out the earthquakes.

"They're not just flooding three villages," I told Lior. "They're building a bomb out of a valley and calling it progress."

* * *

The vote happened on the fifth night, around the stove in the mess tent, after Okafor laid it all out. Eleven people. Two options, as she framed them: go down the mountain and go public—press, parliament, whatever channels were left—or stay silent, complete the classified survey, and take the state's money and its silence in return.

What she didn't frame—what none of us said out loud, because saying it would have made it real—was the third option. The one the escorts' presence implied. You could also simply not come down.

The vote split. Five for going public. Six for silence. And the five who voted to go public looked at the six of us, and the six of us looked at the five of them, and Captain Voss checked the magazine of his sidearm in a very deliberate, very visible way, and everybody understood that the survey had just become something else.

"We stayed three more days," I said. "Pretending to work. Then the five walked out at night—Okafor, Brandt, the twins, and young Petrov. Took the north route over the Anvil shoulder. Voss sent two men after them at dawn."

"And?"

"And they didn't come back either way. Not the five, not the two. Voss reported the whole team lost to a rockslide event. Official story: the upper basin is unstable, treacherous, a graveyard. Which has the convenient double effect of explaining our disappearance *and* discouraging anyone else from going up and looking." I shrugged against the rope. "Except Voss couldn't report me lost, because I'd gone down early with the instrument crates—he sent me ahead two days before the split, probably to get me away from whatever happened next. So I walked out alive, filed a report saying nothing, collected my pay, and then I ran. Because I'd read the after-action notes. I knew what 'lost to a rockslide' meant when Voss wrote it."

Lior was quiet for a long moment. The stove ticked. Outside, the river kept up its low constant argument with the rocks, the sound I was learning meant her cabin, meant safety, meant the one place in the world where I wasn't hunted.

"You said Brandt," she said.

"What?"

"The five who walked out. You named a Brandt."

"Ilsa Brandt. Senior geologist. Why?"

Something moved across her face—fast, buried fast, but I was watching for it by then. Everyone who lives under a false name gets good at watching for it.

"No reason," she said, which is what people say when there's a reason.

* * *

I should tell you about the guilt, since that's apparently what this chapter is for. Not the dramatic kind—not the tearing-of-shirts, crying-at-the-ford kind. The other kind. The kind that lives in your hands.

Every night since I left the mountains, I dream about drawing. Just drawing. The reservoir boundary line, over and over, in different colors, trying different routes, and always the same result: the blue door goes under. In the dreams I'm very calm about it. I adjust the contour intervals. I note the submerged structures in the legend. *Submerged structures.* That phrase is in the actual official documentation, in my actual handwriting. My mother's schoolhouse is a submerged structure. The graves are submerged structures. I labeled my childhood in the legend key and initialed the corner box, and the corner box says *Drawn by E. Renner, Cartographic Section,* and some clerk will file that box forever, long after the water comes, and nowhere in it will be any indication that the hand that drew the line grew up on the wrong side of it.

That's the guilt. Not that I did evil. That I did my job well. That the machinery worked exactly as designed, and I was a well-made part of it, and parts don't get to have birthplaces.

"Why are you telling me this?" Lior asked, near the end. "Really. You could have bought my silence with half this story. You could have lied."

"Because you found the crate," I said. "Government-issue gear, wedged in the rocks, dry, no tracks. That was ours. That was the twins' spare kit—they cached it on the north route in case they needed to come back down fast. Which means either they came back down, or someone brought it down, and either way—" I stopped, because my throat had closed, and I had to work around it. "Either way, five people I voted against are dead or hiding on that mountain, and four hundred people in three villages are about to lose everything, and I am the only person alive who knows both halves of it. I've been sitting on this for three weeks because I'm a coward with good posture. I can't anymore. But I can't go down there either—they know my face, they'll have my description at every checkpoint. I need someone the mountain trusts."

Lior stood up. Walked to the window. Stood there a while with her back to me, and I watched the river-light move on her shoulders and understood that I was asking her to spend something she'd already spent once. Whatever had put her under a false name in these low valleys, it had cost her the same currency: a life cut in half, a name she couldn't use, a face that used to open doors and now opened nothing.

"If half of what you're saying is true," she said, without turning around, "then the villages have maybe weeks before the diversion tunnels close and the surveys start. Maybe less, if the storm season pushes the schedule."

"That's my estimate."

"Then we go down to Kestrel Ford tomorrow." She turned around, and her face had settled into something that wasn't kindness and wasn't anger—something harder and more useful than either. "And you're going to walk into the place you drew underwater, and you're going to tell them yourself. Every word. I'm not carrying your confession for you."

"No," I said. "I wouldn't ask you to."

She untied the rope. Then she pointed at the pemmican.

"You still owe me for that."

* * *

That night I lay on her floor—she kept the rope loose and the door barred, which I respected—and listened to the river, and thought about the blue door.

Tomorrow I would stand in Kestrel Ford and tell four hundred people that the water was coming. Tomorrow I would watch my mother's neighbors hear my mother's son say it. And the worst part, the part I couldn't stop turning over in the dark, was that some of them wouldn't believe me. Some of them would look at the low brown river, so patient, so familiar, and decide that a man who'd left for the city and come back speaking of catastrophe was just another kind of weather—loud, distant, passing.

They'd believe the river. People always believe the river. It's the oldest liar there is, and it has never once had to raise its voice.

I fell asleep sometime before dawn, and dreamed, for the first time in weeks, not of drawing—but of the pool below the mill race, and a fish pulling hard on a line held by a boy who didn't yet know what maps cost, and the water running cold and clean and endless toward a sea it would reach long before any of us learned its name.
