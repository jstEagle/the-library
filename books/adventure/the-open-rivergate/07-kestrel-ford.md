## 7. Kestrel Ford

The river told us we were close before the village did. It changed its mind about being a mountain stream and started behaving like a river—widening, slowing, picking up a silt-brown confidence it hadn't had upstream. Elias noticed it too, though he said nothing for a long while. He'd been quiet since dawn, and not the good kind of quiet. The kind where you can hear a man rehearsing.

"You grew up here," I said, because someone had to say it.

"Lowmark," he said. "Kestrel Ford is my aunt's side. I used to swim in that bend—" He pointed at a curve of water so placid it looked embarrassed to be part of a flood plan. "Every summer until I was fourteen. There's a sandbar in the middle. We called it the island."

"It'll be eleven feet under," I said.

"I did the math myself." He said it flatly, the way you say things when saying them any other way would cost more than you have. "I drew the contour lines. My own hands put the island underwater."

I didn't answer. There wasn't an answer. You can't comfort a man with words when what he needs is a time machine and a different career.

We came down off the sheep track in late afternoon light, the kind that makes everything look like a memory of itself, and Kestrel Ford arranged itself below us: forty-odd roofs, a mill with its wheel turning slow as a thought, smoke standing straight up from chimneys because there was no wind to argue with. Fields stitched to the slopes in terraces older than the state that wanted to drown them. A church bell rang the hour, unhurried, as if bells had nothing to fear from reservoirs.

"They don't know," Elias said.

"No."

"And when they know—"

"Some will pack tonight. Some will tell us we're liars. Some will tell us we're right and pick up rifles." I shifted my pack. "And most of them will do all three before breakfast."

He looked at me sideways. "You've done this before."

"I've been a village's bad news before. Different news, same job." I started down. "Come on. Bad news doesn't get better overnight."

* * *

Maren Oduya recognized me before I got within thirty feet of her, which shouldn't have surprised me and did anyway. I'd spent four years being nobody—a false name, a shaved-down accent, a face kept deliberately forgettable—and she looked up from the fish racks where she was splitting whitefish, took one glance, and said, "Well. The river does bring things back."

Not my name. Not "Lior Almeida, disgraced guide, blacklisted after the Corven expedition." Just that, like I was a piece of driftwood she'd once pulled out of the water and here it was again, a little more waterlogged.

"Maren." My voice did something embarrassing on the second syllable. Four years of armor and it cracked on a fish-splitting woman in an apron.

She set down her knife, wiped her hands on it out of habit, and studied me the way she used to study weather coming over the pass. "You're thinner. You're carrying a stranger. And you walked in from the high track, which nobody uses unless they're avoiding the road." Her eyes went to Elias, then back. "So either you're in trouble or you're bringing it."

"Both," I said. "Is there somewhere we can talk?"

"There's always somewhere. That's the trouble with villages—nowhere to be alone and nowhere to hide." She untied her apron, hung it on a peg with the precision of a woman who believed order was a form of prayer, and led us up through the lanes toward her house. People nodded to her. People looked at Elias and me longer than they needed to. In Kestrel Ford, strangers were measured by eye weight, and we were coming in heavy.

Her kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and dried thyme. She put water on without asking if we wanted any, because in Maren's house the answer was always yes, and then she sat across from us, folded her hands, and waited. She'd always been good at waiting. It was how she'd gotten the truth out of me the first winter, when I'd arrived half-frozen with a name that wasn't mine and a story I wouldn't tell, and she'd simply made room for me at her table, week after week, until the story fell out on its own like a rotten tooth.

I gave it to her faster this time. The survey team. The equipment wedged in the rocks. The journal page in the cairn. *The map is wrong on purpose.* Elias's testimony—the geothermal basin, the dam, the reservoir line drawn through three villages. I laid it out plain, no drama, because drama gives people somewhere to hide, and I watched her face go through weather of its own: disbelief, calculation, and then something worse than either. Recognition. She'd seen the survey stakes herself, months ago, down by the ford. She'd assumed they were for a bridge.

"A bridge," she repeated, when I finished. "They staked it out and let us think it was a bridge."

"That's how it's done," Elias said quietly. "Nobody votes to be drowned. They just find out too late to matter."

Maren stood, went to the window, and looked out at her village—at the mill wheel, at the terraces, at children chasing a dog between the drying racks—with an expression I couldn't read and then could, all at once, because I'd worn it myself for four years. It was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want to do.

"How long?" she asked.

"If the diversion tunnels close on schedule? Weeks. Maybe less if the storm season comes early, and the river's already running high."

"Weeks." She turned around. "Lior. How sure are you?"

"I've read the dead man's own handwriting. I'm sure enough to have walked three days through country I swore I'd never go back to."

That landed. She knew what the high country meant to me—or rather, she knew what it had cost me, because I'd never told her the whole of it, only that the mountains had taken something and I didn't discuss it. She sat back down. For a moment the kitchen was very quiet, just the kettle beginning its low argument with the stove.

Then she said, "We'll need to call the village council. Tonight. And Lior—" her eyes found mine, steady and terrible, "—you should know that whatever they decide, some of them are going to decide wrong. And you'll want to shake them. And you can't. That's the price of telling people true things. You don't get to make them believe."

"I know," I said. "I've been on the other end of that lesson."

"Yes," she said softly. "You have. And look how well you learned it."

* * *

The council met in the meetinghouse, which was also the school, which was also the place where dances happened and disputes were settled and the winter stores were counted. Forty-one adults showed up, which in Kestrel Ford constituted everyone who mattered and several who merely enjoyed mattering. Elias and I stood at the front near the woodstove, and I'll be honest: I've faced down rockfall, whiteout, and a client with a rifle and poor judgment, and none of it was worse than forty familiar-and-unfamiliar faces waiting for me to justify their worst day.

Elias spoke first, because it was his map, his sin, his home ground even if this specific village belonged to his aunt's people. He unrolled the survey copy on the trestle table and weighted the corners with stones, and there was a murmur when people saw the official seal, because seals still mean something in places like this, which is exactly why the state puts them on things.

He explained the basin. The heat under the rock. The dam site at the gorge mouth. The reservoir line—he traced it with one finger, slow, so everyone could watch it cross the fields, the mill, the churchyard, the island in the swimming bend. His voice held. Only his hand shook, and only slightly, and I was probably the only one who saw it.

Silence, when he finished. The particular silence of people reordering their entire future around a line on paper.

Old Sef, who farms the lowest terrace and has farmed it since before I was born, broke it first. "Water goes down," he said. "It always goes down. You're telling me it's going to come up."

"The dam holds the water back until it's deep enough to run the turbines," Elias said. "Then your valley is the bottom of a lake."

"My father's buried above the church." Sef's chin jutted. "My grandfather cleared that terrace with a mule and a crowbar. You want me to believe ink on a page means I leave?"

"I want you to believe it while leaving is still possible."

A woman near the back—I think her name was Petra, the ferryman's widow—said, "How much are they paying?"

"Petra," Maren said warningly.

"No, I'll ask it. Someone has to. The government doesn't build dams out of spite. There's compensation. Relocation money. New plots." Her voice cracked on *plots*, just barely, and I understood: she wasn't greedy, she was terrified, and money was the only version of safety anyone had ever offered her. "Because if there's money, some of us survive this. If there isn't—if we just run—we're refugees. I've been a widow. I won't be a refugee."

"There will be money," Elias said. "There's always money. It won't be enough, it'll come late, and it'll come with conditions that keep you from ever talking about what was under the water."

"That's cynical," someone said.

"That's experience," Elias said, and something in his tone made it clear he'd signed such documents himself, and the room shifted a degree against him.

Then Tam Voss—no relation to the captain, though the coincidence made my skin crawl the first time I heard the name—stood up. Tam is the closest thing Kestrel Ford has to a hothead, a big man with a forge and opinions heated daily. "Or," he said, "we stay. All of us. They can't drown a village full of people. They need us gone peaceful or gone quiet. If we stand on our own ground, what are they going to do—"

"They'll close the roads," I said. "They'll call it a geological hazard. They'll post patrols and stop letting food trucks through, and they'll wait. Winter does the rest. Nobody fires a shot. Nobody has to." I'd seen it done to a mining town once, years ago, from the outside, from the safe side of a checkpoint. "Standing your ground only works if the other side needs your permission. They don't. They need your absence, and they have every tool for manufacturing it except honesty."

"So we run," Tam said bitterly. "Forty families, on foot, into the storm season, because a cartographer and a blacklisted guide say so."

"Yes," I said.

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then we've lost a hard month and some pride." I looked around the room. "If you're wrong, you lose everything that can't be carried."

That should have settled it. It didn't, because rooms full of frightened people don't settle; they split. By midnight the village had sorted itself into thirds—not cleanly, not along family lines but along fault lines older than any of them: those whose land was highest and therefore safest-feeling, those with young children, those with graves. The graves were the hardest argument. You cannot reason a man off his father's hillside. I stopped trying around the second hour and just answered questions, honestly, one at a time, and hated every minute of it, because honesty was doing about as much good as shouting at scree.

Maren closed the meeting near one in the morning. She didn't take a vote. She said, "No one decides tonight. Sleep on it. Anyone who wants to leave, come to my house at first light and we'll organize. Anyone who wants to stay—" she paused, and I watched her choose mercy over persuasion, "—stay knowing what you know. That's all any of us can ask."

* * *

Afterward, in her kitchen, with the lamp turned low, Maren poured three cups of the plum brandy she saved for funerals and emergencies, which I suppose this was both.

"You handled them well," she said.

"I handled them terribly. Half of them think I'm a spy and the other half think I'm a prophet, and both halves are wrong."

"Both halves are frightened," she corrected. "Fear always looks like something else." She turned the cup in her hands. "Do you remember what you said to me, your first spring here? When I asked why you stayed in the low valleys instead of going back up?"

I remembered. I'd told her the mountains had taken enough from me and I wasn't going to stand in their doorway asking for more.

"And now look at you," she said. "Walked straight back up into the teeth of it. For us." Her eyes were bright and wet and fierce. "Lior. Why?"

Because of her, was the true answer, or partly—because a woman had fed a frozen stranger for a winter without asking a single question, and debts like that don't expire. But there was more, and I heard myself say it before I'd decided to: "Because somebody drew a line through your house and called it progress, and I know exactly what it feels like when powerful people edit the map and you're standing on the part they erased. I spent four years on the erased part. I'm not watching it happen to you."

Maren reached across the table and gripped my wrist, hard, the way you grip a rope. "Then hear me," she said. "Whatever happens next—whatever the village decides, whatever the soldiers do—you did not owe us this. Whatever it costs you, it was never owed. Do you understand? Love that's owed is just debt with better manners."

I understood. Understanding didn't help. Outside, the river ran dark and full toward the gorge, already practicing for the future it had been assigned, and somewhere downstream the diversion tunnels waited to swallow it.

Elias appeared in the doorway, gray-faced. "Riders on the road," he said. "Three of them. State markings. They stopped at the mill."

Maren blew out the lamp without hurrying, the way you do when hurrying is the most dangerous thing available.

"Well," she said into the dark. "Now we find out which third of the village was listening."
