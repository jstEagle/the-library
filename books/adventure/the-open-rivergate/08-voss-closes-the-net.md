## 8. Voss Closes the Net

I have closed a valley before. Twice, actually—once for a wildfire that killed eleven firefighters, once for a border dispute that killed no one but ruined three careers, mine briefly among them. You learn the choreography. Roads first, because roads are how stories get out. Then the telephone lines, or in a valley like this one, the single relay mast above Lowmark that carries every call down to the lowlands. Then the patrols, spaced like stitches across a wound. And last, always last, the announcement—the paper that tells people why their world has shrunk.

The announcement is the part I hate, because it is the part where I lie.

*Closed by order of the Ministry of Interior pending assessment of geological hazard.* Hazard. It was even technically true. There was a hazard in this valley. It was just not geological, and it was not pending, and it was me.

I stood on the porch of the Kestrel Ford waystation at six in the morning, watching mist come off the river in ropes, and read my own order aloud to the two corporals who would carry it door to door. My voice sounded reasonable. That was the trick of it—I had spent nineteen years in uniform learning to sound reasonable while doing arithmetic I didn't want to do out loud.

"Nobody leaves," Corporal Adeyemi said. Not a question. She was new to me, transferred in after the survey team went quiet, and she had the particular flatness of a soldier who has already decided what kind of officer I am and is waiting for evidence either way.

"Nobody leaves," I confirmed. "Anyone who asks why gets the hazard notice. Anyone who pushes gets logged. Anyone who tries to walk out through the hills—" I let it hang.

"We bring them in."

"You bring them in *alive*, Corporal." I said it more sharply than I meant to, and watched her eyebrows move a millimeter. "This is a containment operation, not a cull. The people in these villages are citizens of the Republic. They are not the problem."

"And the survey team, sir?"

"The survey team is missing." Which was true, as far as my orders went. What my orders did not say—and what I had pieced together from three weeks of intercepted radio traffic, a burned field report, and one very frightened junior cartographer's requisition records—was that *missing* was a generous word for whatever had happened upriver. Five men and women had walked into the Rivergate basin with a military escort of four and had not walked out. The escort had come back. Three of them, anyway. The fourth was in a hospital in the capital with a broken jaw he refused to explain, and when I'd visited him he'd looked at me the way you look at a dog that has been kicked by someone it trusted.

I had filed none of this. Filing it would have made it real, and real things require action, and action required me to decide which side of an equation I stood on.

Here was the arithmetic, the version I did at three in the morning staring at the waystation ceiling: the state intended to dam the Serran headwaters. The dam would light forty thousand homes, irrigate a drought-stricken province, and employ two thousand people for six years. It would also put three villages under sixty meters of water. Kestrel Ford. Lowmark. Ansel's Reach. Four hundred and eleven souls, per the last census—a number I knew because I had requested it myself, late at night, like a man checking whether the sum he feared was actually the sum on the page.

Four hundred and eleven people could be relocated. Relocation was ugly, expensive, and cruel in the specific way bureaucracies are cruel—generous compensation on paper, ruinous compensation in practice—but it was survivable. People survived relocation. People did not survive a war over water rights if the province went dark and the drought provinces rioted, and they did not survive what happened to officers who failed the Republic in strategic matters. I had seen that arithmetic done on other men. I had done pieces of it myself.

So: close the valley. Keep the villages quiet until the relocation order came down properly, with trucks and forms and a minister's signature, so that it happened in daylight instead of in some deniable dark. That was the story I told myself, and it was mostly true, and the part that wasn't true was the reason I couldn't sleep: somewhere upstream there were people who knew exactly what the dam would cost—not in megawatts but in poison—and someone with more rank than me had decided that knowledge itself was the hazard to be contained.

Adeyemi cleared her throat. "And the guide? Almeida?"

The name landed in my chest like a dropped tool. "What about her?"

"She's on your watch list. Cross-referenced twice. Once as a person of interest in the disappearance, once under her old file." Adeyemi said it carefully, the way soldiers hand you something sharp edge-first. "The blacklist file."

"Lior Almeida is dead," I said. "Officially. That's what the blacklist means—it means the person stops existing on paper. Whatever name she goes by now, she's a ghost, and ghosts don't concern this operation."

"Yes sir." A pause. "Only, the tracker says someone's been moving in the high country. Off-trail, no fire, cold camps. He says it reads like a professional."

"Then it reads like a ghost," I said, "and we have a valley to seal."

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the one currency I've never managed to spend wisely: I did not believe my own dismissal for a second. I had read Almeida's original file during the first week—everyone on the search had, eventually; her name kept surfacing in the survey team's route plans like a watermark. A guide who could read water the way priests read scripture. A client dead on a route that should not have killed anyone. A tribunal that found negligence where any mountaineer would have found falsified maps, and a silence from the expedition's own geologist, one Ilsa Brandt, that had always struck me as the loudest thing in the entire record.

If anyone in this valley could find the vanished surveyors, it was her. And if the surveyors had found what I suspected they'd found, then everything depended on who reached them first—me with handcuffs and a cover story, or her with whatever truth she was carrying.

I sealed the valley. I posted the patrols. I signed the hazard notice with a steady hand, and if my pen pressed a little too hard and tore the paper slightly at the flourish, well. Paper tears. Mountains do worse.

* * *

They came at dusk, which told me Voss was good at his work.

Dusk is when a village relaxes its eyes. Supper smoke, lamplight, children called in from the river shallows—that soft hour when a person sweeping the lane is just a person walking. Maren had drilled us on it without ever saying she was drilling us. *If soldiers come,* she'd say, mending a net or salting fish, *they'll come at dawn or dusk, because those are the hours you forgive yourself for being surprised.* Maren talked about soldiers the way other people talk about weather. She'd lived through enough fronts.

I was in her back room pretending to mend a net of my own when Dov came through the door without knocking, which was how Maren knew before he spoke.

"Roadblock at the ford," he said. "Two trucks. They're going house to house."

Maren set down her needle with the unhurried precision of a woman closing a shop. "Lior. Cellar."

"I can be gone over the ridge before—"

"You'll be in the cellar." She said it the way she said most things: as a fact about the world, like rain coming. "They'll have watchers on the trails—you taught me that much yourself, all those nights you spent studying the pass. You run now, you confirm every rumor in this village with your legs. You sit in my cellar, and by morning I'm just a widow whose root vegetables needed turning."

There was a third thing she wasn't saying, and I heard it anyway: *you running puts my son at risk, and my neighbors, and me.* Love, in Kestrel Ford, came with logistics.

The cellar was under the kitchen, behind a shelf of preserves that swung on a hinge Maren's late husband had built so cleverly I'd have missed it if she hadn't shown me. Inside: dirt floor, turnips in sand, a barrel of brined cabbage breathing its sour breath into the dark, and a stool. Someone had put a stool down there. Someone had thought, *she will need somewhere to sit while soldiers walk over her head,* and that someone was Maren, and I sat on that stool and understood that I would burn down the capital itself before I let anything happen to this valley.

Which was, I reflected in the dark, exactly the kind of thinking that got guides blacklisted. Attachment. The mountain teaches you to travel light, and I had never learned the lesson.

Boots on the floorboards above me. Not many—four, maybe five, moving in the economical pattern of men who had done sweeps before. A knock. Maren's voice, pitched perfectly between deference and deafness: "Coming, coming, my knees aren't what they were."

The front door. A voice I hadn't heard before, official and almost gentle. Captain Voss himself, then—he wouldn't send a lieutenant to work a village this size personally unless the village mattered, and Kestrel Ford mattered because it was the largest settlement below the basin and therefore the likeliest place for a ghost to shelter.

"—geological hazard, ma'am. Upstream instability. We're asking residents to remain indoors after nightfall and to report any strangers passing through."

"Strangers." Maren's laugh was a masterpiece, dry as August. "Captain, the last stranger through Kestrel Ford was a wool buyer in spring, and he overpaid so badly I half suspected him of being a spy. Nobody comes here. That's rather the point of here."

Someone laughed—a young soldier, quickly strangled. Voss didn't. I pictured him standing in that warm kitchen with his cap under his arm, doing his arithmetic: old woman, no husband, one grown son, net-mending income, nothing in the room suggesting a fugitive. Except that fugitives, in my experience, left exactly this kind of room behind them, because the best hiding places are always inside kindness, and kindness doesn't show on a checklist.

"Your son?" Voss asked.

"Ferrying the last of the nets in from the shallows. He'll be along." Dov was, in fact, thirty meters away sitting in a beached skiff with a lantern, performing the role of a man with no thoughts whatsoever. He'd rehearsed the vacant expression. It was terrible. It was also, apparently, convincing enough, because boots moved toward the back door, paused—

—and stopped, because Maren said, mildly, "Mind the preserves on your way past, Captain. Third shelf. If you break a jar of my quince I'll haunt you, and I'm told I'd be very good at it."

Another strangled laugh from the young one. And then—this is the part I keep returning to, the part that rearranged something in my chest—Voss laughed too. Briefly, genuinely, a man caught off guard by a grandmother threatening him with jam. "I'll mind the quince, ma'am. Thank you for your cooperation."

The boots receded. The door closed. And I sat in the dark with the cabbage smell and the turnips and the stool, listening to my own heart slow down, and I thought: *he minded the quince.*

You can tell a lot about a man from what he minds. A man who laughs at an old woman's joke while sealing her valley shut is not a monster. Monsters are easy. Monsters you fight. But a decent man doing indecent sums—who believes, truly believes, that he is protecting these people from something worse, that the trucks and the forms and the flood are mercy measured against some larger catastrophe only he can see—that man you cannot simply fight. You have to *out-argue* him, and he has the state's whole library behind his argument, and all you have is a waterproofed journal page and a scrawled line: *the map is wrong on purpose.*

Hours passed. The house settled. Eventually the shelf hinge whispered and Maren's face appeared in the gap, lamplight behind her, and she handed down bread and cheese and a cup of cider vinegar cut with honey, which is a vile drink that I would now defend with my life.

"Well?" she said.

"He's not what I expected," I admitted. "He apologized to your preserves."

"They do that, the ones who still think they're the good guys. Worse than the open ones." She sat down on the cellar steps, which she was too proud to admit hurt her knees. "Lior. Listen to me. That man upstairs is going to find you eventually, or Elias, or whoever else is carrying whatever it is you're all carrying. When he does, you need to have something better than running. Running is a young person's strategy and neither of us is getting younger."

"What would you do?"

Maren looked at me for a long moment, and in the lamplight from above her face was half shadow, half iron. "Me? I'd stop hiding the truth in cellars and start making them choke on it. But I'm an old woman with jars." She stood, wincing, and paused at the hinge. "Oh—one more thing. Dov heard the corporals talking at the roadblock. They're pulling the patrols off the lower gorge tomorrow. Consolidating upstream. Toward Ansel's Reach."

Toward the second journal. Toward whatever Elias had decoded and sewn and hidden, and toward the one place in this valley I could not reach before Voss's net drew tight.

"Get some sleep," Maren said, and the shelf swung shut, and I ate my bread in the dark doing arithmetic of my own: one night, one window of open trail, one chance to beat a believer to the truth he was trying to bury.
