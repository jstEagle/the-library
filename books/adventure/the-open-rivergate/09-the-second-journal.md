## 9. The Second Journal

I have spent my whole life making maps, which means I have spent my whole life deciding what not to draw. Every cartographer learns it in the first year: a map is an argument. You choose the rivers, the roads, the contours, and everything you leave off is a small mercy or a small crime, depending on who's paying.

I used to think of myself as a man of mercies. Small ones, anyway. I left off the bad drinking water at the Kestrel Ford well because it embarrassed the district office. I left off the old mine adit above Lowmark because children kept sneaking into it and I didn't want to make it easier for them. I told myself these omissions were kindnesses. It turns out they were just practice.

The missing half of the survey records was hidden in Ansel's Reach, and I knew it before anyone said so out loud, because I was the one who hid them.

* * *

Let me back up, because Lior keeps asking me to back up, and because honesty, I'm learning, is mostly a matter of getting the sequence right.

When the survey team voted to vanish — and yes, we voted, seven of us around a stove in a tent that smelled of paraffin and fear, with the escorts' radio mast humming on the ridge like a wasp in a jar — we split the records deliberately. That was my doing too. I'd spent three years learning how the state handles inconvenient documents: they don't burn them, burning leaves ash and questions; they file them, and filing is a kind of drowning. So we drowned our own work instead, scattered it, made it a thing that had to be found rather than seized.

Half went with Brandt to the high camp, sealed in the aluminum case she guarded like a relic. The other half came down the mountain with me, sewn into the lining of a surveyor's coat that I wore through two checkpoints by looking bored, which is the only disguise that has never once failed me.

The plan had been simple. I would reach Ansel's Reach — my village, my mother's kitchen, the one place in the Serran where every stone owed me a favor — and pass the records to a man named Oskar Vell, the schoolteacher, who had a dry cellar, a slow temper, and a reputation for being too dull-witted to be interesting. Dull-witted men live long lives in this country. I aspired to be mistaken for one.

But when I came down through the birch scrub above Ansel's Reach, I found the village already half-empty. The state had gotten there ahead of me, in its way — not with soldiers but with paper. Notices nailed to the meetinghouse door. COMPULSORY ACQUISITION NOTICE, PHASE ONE. RESIDENTS TO REGISTER FOR RESETTLEMENT ALLOTMENTS BY SEASON'S END. The nails were new. The paper was good stock. Somebody in the capital had chosen a font.

My mother's house had a notice on it too. My mother had been dead eleven years, and the house belonged to my cousin Petra now, and there was a notice on it all the same, addressed to OCCUPANT, as if the state couldn't be bothered to learn her name even while erasing it.

Petra let me in through the back, took one look at me, and said, "You look like your father after the bank took the mill."

"Thanks."

"You're welcome. Now tell me why the river smells wrong."

That stopped me. "It doesn't smell wrong."

"It does since the week your team went up. Sulfur, faint, under the frost. Old Halvard says the springs below the Reach are running warm. Warm in autumn." She folded her arms. "Elias. What did you find up there?"

And because I am weak, and because she had fed me, and because she was the last person alive who remembered me at nine years old falling into the millrace and being fished out blue, I told her some of it. Not the vote, not the vanishing, not the part where I was technically a ghost. Just the geology. The fissure lake. The heat under the rock.

She listened the way people in the Serran listen — completely, without interrupting, the way you listen to weather — and then she said, "So the dam will drown us and boil the river besides."

"That's not—" I started, and then stopped, because I hadn't actually run those numbers. I'd mapped the basin. I'd mapped the proposed reservoir. I had never once put the two drawings on top of each other. Cartographers do that, you know. We keep our arguments separate so we can sleep.

* * *

Lior found me at dawn on the third day, as I've already told her, raiding her supplies — though "raiding" flatters both of us. I was eating her oatmeal with my hands and crying a little, and she stood in the doorway of her own cabin with a rifle she didn't point and a face that said she was revising several theories about ghosts.

We've been circling each other ever since, she and I, like two animals that have decided the other one is probably not food. She wants the truth in the order it happened. I keep giving it to her in the order I can bear to say it. Last night, over a fire built small enough to hide from the sky, I finally gave her the piece I'd been sitting on — the piece about the second journal, the half-records in Ansel's Reach — and watched her face change from curiosity to something colder and more useful.

"You hid them," she said. Not a question.

"I hid them."

"And you know what's in them."

"I know what I put in them." I fed a stick to the fire. "What I didn't know until this week is what else got put in them. By other hands. After mine."

She waited. She's very good at waiting. It's the mountain guide in her, I think — patience as a professional skill, the same way I have neat handwriting.

"The survey had two phases," I said. "Phase one was resource mapping. Water, timber, mineral potential. That was the honest work. Phase two was feasibility — the dam study. And phase two wasn't done by us. It was done by a hydrological subcommittee whose reports came to us pre-written, and our job was to fold their numbers into our maps so the whole thing looked like one continuous, disinterested scientific process. Which it was not. It was a conclusion wearing a lab coat."

"And the second journal?"

"The phase-two field notes. The ones the escorts weren't supposed to know existed, because the escorts were supposed to believe the dam was safe. Brandt kept them. When we split the records, they went into her case at the high camp — except she'd already copied the critical pages, the ones she called 'the arithmetic,' and sent them down with me, because she said the high camp was the most dangerous place on the mountain." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "She was right, obviously. Look at me. I'm the proof."

"So they're in Ansel's Reach."

"In a flour tin, in Oskar Vell's cellar, under a false bottom, under forty kilos of winter wheat. Unless Oskar's dead or gone, in which case God knows. The village is emptying out, Lior. Registration deadline's in six weeks. People are selling things that can't be carried."

She stared into the fire for a long time. Then she said the thing that rearranged my insides:

"If the dam goes in, and the geothermal field ruptures, how far downstream does the poison go?"

And I said, "I don't know," and hated myself for it, and she said, "Then we find out," and that is how I ended up decoding my own buried work by lantern light in a blacklisted guide's cabin, with my hands shaking, doing the arithmetic I had spent two years refusing to do.

* * *

Here is what decoding looks like when the decoder is also the encoder and has had no sleep: humiliating. I'd written the cipher key into the margins of my own field atlas using a system based on contour intervals — elevation differences standing in for letter shifts, because no one audits contour lines, they're boring, boringness is armor. But I'd been exhausted when I did it, up at the high camp, writing by stove light while the escorts played cards twenty meters away, and my younger self had made two transcription errors that cost me four hours and most of my remaining dignity.

Lior sat across from me and watched me fail, and did not once say anything helpful, which I have come to understand is her highest form of respect.

Page by page, the phase-two notes came apart in my hands and reassembled into sentences, and the sentences were worse than silence.

The subcommittee's own hydrologist — a man named Ferenc, initials F.M., whom I met exactly once and remember as having the eyes of someone who reads his own reports last — had flagged the interaction in the third week of phase two. The reservoir's projected high-water line didn't just inundate the basin floor. It backed water into the fault-controlled fracture zone that fed the fissure lake. Increased hydrostatic head on a geothermal system. In plain language: drown the hot rock under sixty meters of cold water and pressure, and the system doesn't politely absorb it. It finds new openings. It always finds new openings. Steam bursts, lateral fractures, contaminated discharge into the main channel — and the main channel is the river, and the river runs through Kestrel Ford and Lowmark and Ansel's Reach and then through the lowland towns, the fishery at Marrow Bend, the intake pipes of three municipal water systems, a hundred miles of everything that drinks.

Ferenc had modeled it. There were numbers. There was a dilution curve showing arsenic and boron concentrations in the downstream water column exceeding safety thresholds for a minimum of nineteen years, probable range thirty to fifty, worst case — and here my hand stopped moving on the page, because the worst case was annotated in Ferenc's own pencil — *permanent alteration of watershed chemistry; recommend full project review.*

And beneath that, in different ink, in the tidy administrative hand I recognized from every memo that had ever ruined anyone's life:

*Recommendation noted. Project timeline unaffected. See Annex D for public communications strategy.*

Public communications strategy. For a poisoned river. They had a *strategy*. Someone had sat in a warm room and decided that the correct response to a hundred miles of poisoned water was communications.

"Lior," I said. My voice did something strange. "They knew. Before the dam was approved. Before the notices went up. They ran the model, they saw the curve, and they wrote a press plan."

She took the decoded page from me and read it twice, and I watched horror move through her face the way weather moves over a ridge — fast, total, and then settling into something that wasn't going anywhere. Resolve. Horror hardening into resolve, exactly as advertised, and I understood in that moment why she'd survived her disgrace and I'd nearly not survived mine: she metabolizes terrible news into motion. I metabolize it into shame, and shame sits still.

"Nineteen years minimum," she said quietly. "Fifty probable. Permanent possible."

"Yes."

"That's not three villages. That's the whole valley. Marrow Bend. The lowlands. Everyone downstream who ever trusted a tap."

"That's what I've been trying to tell you. It was never about land. Land you can grieve and move on from — my family's done it twice. This is about water. You can't carry water out of a valley. You can't resettle a river."

She was quiet a moment. Then, wryly, in that flat way of hers that I was beginning to recognize as the sound of her thinking: "You realize you drew the maps for this. Your name's in the survey credits. When this breaks, you're not a witness, Renner. You're evidence."

"I know."

"They could hang the whole scandal on the cartographer. The state's man inside the survey. It's almost elegant."

"It is elegant," I said. "I thought about that on the walk down. I've had a lot of time to admire it. If the project blows up publicly, they need a technical villain, and I'm the only member of the survey team who came down the mountain. I'm the fall guy with a signature." I turned the decoded page toward her, toward Ferenc's penciled annotation. "Which is why the second journal matters more than the first. The first records prove the state wants to flood three villages. Tragic, arguable, defensible — progress has a body count, they'll say, and half the country will nod. But this—" I tapped the dilution curve. "This proves they knew the project would poison a hundred miles of their own citizens' water and filed it under *communications*. That's not a tragedy. That's a decision. And decisions have authors, and authors can be named."

"Can they? From a flour tin?"

"From a flour tin with the subcommittee's distribution list, the model inputs, and Ferenc's initials on the annotations. Yes. If it reaches the right person intact." I rubbed my eyes. "If it reaches the wrong person first, it reaches a shredder, and I reach a courtroom."

* * *

We worked through what remained of the night. Lior copied the decoded pages in her own hand — she writes a hard, slanted script, all forward lean, like everything about her — while I cross-checked figures against the base maps I still carry in my head, because the originals went up the mountain with Brandt and I have never trusted my memory less than I do lately.

Somewhere past the coldest hour, she asked, without looking up: "Why didn't you run farther? You got out. You could have crossed the border, been a ghost properly. Instead you're hiding in a guide's woodshed eating her oatmeal."

I considered lying, briefly, the way you consider a warm coat you know won't fit.

"Because I checked the dilution curve against the villages," I said. "Nineteen years minimum. My cousin Petra will be seventy. Dov — Maren's boy, he can't be more than twelve — he'll be thirty-one, drinking boron with his breakfast. And I did that. Not with a gun. With a contour pen. I made the beautiful map that made the dam look sensible, and I signed it, and I took the fee." I looked at the fire. "You don't outrun a thing like that, Almeida. It walks faster than you do. The only way to slow it down is to turn around and face it with the paperwork."

She finished copying the page she was on, set her pen down, and looked at me across the fire with an expression I couldn't read — appraisal, maybe, or the beginning of something rarer.

"All right, cartographer," she said. "Ansel's Reach. Tomorrow, before first light, before the patrols thicken. We go get your flour tin."

"We?"

"You didn't think I'd let you walk into a sealed valley alone with the only copy of the only proof that matters." She kicked dirt over the coals, one precise motion, killing the fire like closing a book. "Besides, I owe a woman in Kestrel Ford a visit, and Ansel's Reach is on the way."

I should have felt afraid. A sealed valley, military patrols, a wanted man walking back toward the scene of his own disappearance — every survival instinct I owned cast its vote against it.

Instead I felt something I hadn't felt since the night of the vote around the cold stove: purpose, arriving like ballast, steadying the hull.

Outside, far downstream, the river ran dark and unknowing toward the sea, carrying its cargo of sulfur and snowmelt and everything we were about to risk our lives to say out loud.

One hundred miles of it. All of it counting on a flour tin.

* * *

We broke camp in the grey hour, and Lior paused at the treeline, looking north toward the Reach, and said the thing that became the hook everything afterward hung on:

"There's one problem with your plan, Renner. Oskar Vell's cellar is a day's walk into a valley the army closed yesterday — and somebody in Ansel's Reach already reported seeing you."
