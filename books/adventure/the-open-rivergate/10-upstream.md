## 10. Upstream

Maren packed us like a woman who had done this before, which she had—just never for people she loved. Dried fish wrapped in waxed cloth. Barley cakes dense enough to stop a bullet. A tin of rendered fat. Rope that smelled of her barn and would smell of us by the time we were done with it.

"You'll eat when I tell you," she said, pressing the last bundle into my hands and holding them there a moment longer than packing required. "You'll sleep when the ground is dry. And you will bring my son back down this river, Lior Almeida, or I will come up after you, and I am fifty-three years old and meaner than the mountain."

"I believe every word of that," I said.

"Good." She let go. Then, quieter, in the voice she used for things that frightened her: "He's not coming back down because you owe me. He's coming back down because he's nineteen and stubborn and thinks rivers are arguments he can win. Don't let him win one."

Dov was waiting at the woodline with his father's old pack on and a lantern hanging from it like an ornament, grinning the way only the young can grin at a thing they don't yet understand. Elias stood beside him looking like a man being walked to his own trial, which, in fairness, was more or less accurate. He'd spent three days arguing against bringing the boy and lost the argument to the boy, which is how most arguments with Dov ended.

"The patrol line runs here," Elias said for the fourth time, crouching over Maren's kitchen table with a charcoal map of the valley. "Two men at the ford, one on the ridge road, and a rotation past the mill at dusk. They're not hunting us specifically. They're sealing the valley. That's our advantage—they expect people trying to get *out*."

"And nobody's fool enough to go *up*," Dov said.

"Somebody has to be," I said. "That's the whole plan."

We left Kestrel Ford two hours before dawn, in that blue-black hour when even the dogs are embarrassed to bark. Maren didn't come to the edge of the village. She stood in her doorway with a lamp, and the lamp was the last thing we saw of her, a small yellow star shrinking behind us until the river bend took it, and then it was just the three of us and the water talking to itself in the dark.

I want to say something noble about how I felt leaving. The truth is I felt nothing but cold feet and a arithmetic running in my head: four days' food, six days' climb, storm season maybe nine days out, maybe five. Fear came later. Fear always arrives late, like a client who missed the start of the expedition and then wants to know why everyone else is ahead of him.

* * *

The patrol line was exactly where Elias said it would be, which should have comforted me and instead made me like him slightly less, because a man who can predict soldiers that precisely has spent too much time thinking like them.

We crossed above the ford, where the river braided through gravel shallows wide as a church floor. Cold came up through our boots in a way that made conversation unnecessary. On the far bank we crouched in the alders while a patrol lamp swept the water downstream, patient as a lighthouse, finding nothing but mist.

"They check the ford twice a night," Dov whispered. "My cousin watched them for a week."

"Your cousin should be less interested in soldiers," I whispered back.

"He says they're badly paid."

"He's right about that, at least."

Elias led us north along the true left bank, away from any trail, into country where the map—if there had been a legal map, which there wasn't—would have shown nothing but contour lines and apology. We climbed through birch that gave way to pine, pine that gave way to nothing but rock and lichen and wind. By midmorning the village was a rumor below us, and the river was a silver stitch pulling itself up through the valley toward the place no stitched map admitted existed.

That was the thing about the Rivergate, I was learning. Every other river in the Serrans announces itself. This one climbed in silence, as if it were embarrassed by its own destination.

We made camp that first night in a hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite, and I taught Dov the first rule of moving fast in bad country: you don't move fast. You move *continuously*. Speed is a sprint; continuity is a religion.

"My mother said you'd say something like that," he said.

"Your mother is a wise woman."

"She also said you'd make it sound like a sermon."

I gave him the look I used to give clients who talked too much on scree, and he had the good grace to laugh at it, which is more than most clients managed.

Elias sat apart, as he always did, working charcoal across a scrap of paper by lamplight—not mapping, I realized after watching him a while. Drawing. Little careful rectangles. Tents. He was drawing the high camp from memory, tent by tent, stove, gear lines, the arrangement of five sets of equipment laid out neat as a shop display. When he noticed me noticing, he folded the paper away.

"It helps me think," he said.

"What are you thinking?"

"That I drew the maps that brought them up here." He looked at the fire, not at me. "And that if we find anyone alive up there, they have every right to be angry at me, and I'd deserve it."

"Dov's mother fed you for three days," I said. "In her house. She doesn't feed people she thinks deserve punishment."

"Maren feeds everyone. It's not an endorsement, it's a doctrine."

It was the closest he'd come to humor since the scree field, and I decided to count it as progress.

* * *

The second day the river stopped being scenery and started being a character.

You could feel it change under your boots—a low vibration through the rock, a hum like a struck string that never quite finished its note. Dov heard it first, because nineteen-year-olds hear everything and understand none of it.

"Do you feel that?" he kept saying. "Do you feel that? It's like the ground's purring."

"It's the water," Elias said. "Deep water, moving fast, under the rock."

"How deep?"

"Deeper than we want to know."

By afternoon the river had narrowed into a gorge it had clearly been carving since the world was young, walls rising sheer on either side, and the sound stopped being a purr and became a voice. Not roaring—that would have been simple. It was a sustained, articulate pressure, the way a crowd sounds before it decides what it wants. Walking beside it felt like walking beside someone reading your mail aloud.

There was no trail. There didn't need to be. The gorge offered exactly one path, a shelf of broken stone along the eastern wall, and the river kept pace with us the whole way, sometimes below, sometimes suddenly level, once—for twenty terrible minutes—above us, hanging in a slot of white water overhead while we traversed beneath it with our necks craned and our hearts doing arithmetic of their own.

At one point the shelf simply ended. Forty feet of blank wall, then the shelf resumed. Below, the water ran green and deep and patient.

"We go around?" Dov asked.

"There is no around," Elias said. "The map shows—" He stopped himself. Old habit, reaching for a map that was a lie. "There's no around. There's up and over that rib, or there's swimming."

"Swimming's off the table," I said. "That water's snowmelt and something else. You'd have four minutes and no feeling in your arms after one."

So we went up and over the rib, which meant hauling packs, which meant rope work, which meant Dov going first because he was lightest and youngest and hadn't yet learned to be afraid of exposure, and me anchoring, and Elias climbing last with his eyes fixed on the rock and his jaw set like a man doing penance one hold at a time.

Halfway up the rib, Dov looked down at the green water and said, cheerfully, "If I drop, do you save the evidence or me?"

"Evidence," I said. "You're replaceable. Your mother would kill me otherwise."

He laughed so hard he nearly did drop, and I hauled the rope taut and swore at him with real feeling, and something shifted in the three of us right there on that wall—I felt it click, the way a rope comes tight, the way a team stops being three people and starts being one organism with three opinions.

Elias topped out last, lay flat on the stone breathing like a bellows, and said, "I hate you both."

"No, you don't," said Dov.

"No," Elias admitted. "I really don't."

* * *

On the third day we found the first sign that we weren't the first fools up this river.

A cairn—but wrong. Cairns are built by climbers, and climbers build them proud, stacked high, a little boast in stone. This one was low, flat, deliberate, the kind of pile a surveyor makes: functional, unloved, exact. Inside, waterproofed in oilcloth, a marker stake stamped with the state survey office's eagle, and scratched into the stake with a knife:

**R-7 / FLOW RISING / DO NOT TRUST GAUGE**

Elias read it three times. His face did something complicated.

"This is Torvald's marking style," he said. "Torvald Ostergaard. Instruments man. He wouldn't leave a note like this unless—" He turned the stake over. Nothing else. "Unless he thought someone might come up behind them."

"Did he think that?"

"I don't know. He voted with the half that wanted to stay hidden." Elias's thumb moved over the scratched letters. "*Flow rising.* The fissure lake is feeding harder than their instruments predicted. And the gauge—the state installed flow gauges on this river two years ago. Official readings. If those gauges are rigged to read low..." He didn't finish. He didn't have to. A rigged gauge is a document. It's the state signing its name to the lie in its own handwriting.

"So the proof isn't just in their journals," Dov said slowly. "It's bolted to the riverbed."

"If Torvald documented it." Elias pocketed the stake like a man pocketing a live coal. "Keep walking."

We kept walking. But the river had changed again—or I had changed toward it. All day it pulled at me, that hum through the boot soles, that crowd-sound in the gorge, and I caught myself thinking of it not as terrain but as a witness. Everything the state wanted drowned quietly had run down this water for years, carrying silt and heat and whatever else the source bled into it, and nobody upstream had ever been allowed to ask it a question.

Well. We were asking now.

* * *

The fourth day the weather turned, early, the way weather does when you've bet against it.

Clouds came over the western shoulders at dawn, low and bruised, and by noon they were spitting something between rain and sleet. Storm season wasn't due for days yet. Weather doesn't read schedules. I called a halt under an overhang and did the arithmetic again, and the arithmetic had gotten worse: food for two days, climb for three, and now a sky full of threat.

"We could turn back," Elias said. He said it carefully, testing the words, and I understood he was offering me the exit because neither of the others could take it without shame.

"No," Dov said, before I could.

"It wasn't your decision."

"It's my river." Dov had his chin up and his arms crossed and the sleet dripping off his nose, and he looked so much like Maren in that moment that it hurt. "Those villages are mine. Ansel's Reach is where my grandmother is buried. You two are guests on this climb. I'm the reason for it."

Silence, except the river, which had gotten louder as we gained altitude, as if it were leaning in to listen.

"He's right," I said.

"He's nineteen."

"He's right anyway. Those aren't mutually exclusive." I looked at Elias. "Besides, whoever's alive up there—your people, Torvald, all of them—they need to know the diversion tunnels close within the month. They need to know Voss is sealing the valley. Whatever we carry up is worth more than what we might lose turning back wet and empty-handed."

Elias looked at the clouds, at the river, at the boy. Then he laughed—one short, defeated bark—and started repacking his pack.

"You know what the worst part is?" he said. "I've done this math before. Up here. With better food. And I still keep choosing the hard answer."

"Then it's not the hard answer," I said. "It's just the answer."

* * *

We climbed into the cloud line that evening and camped above it, which is a thing I recommend to no one: the world below goes white and featureless, and you sit in a small island of rock surrounded by nothing, and the silence up there is different from the silence down there. Down in the valley, silence means nothing is happening. Up high, silence means everything is holding its breath.

Dov cooked barley cakes on a spirit flame and burned the first batch, and we ate the burnt ones anyway because calories don't care about texture. Elias got out his charcoal and, instead of tents this time, drew the river—all of it, from the ford to wherever it began, the whole spine of it, working upward stroke by stroke from memory. A cartographer redrawing a map honestly, perhaps for the first time in his career.

"That's not for the state," I said.

"No."

"Who's it for?"

He shaded in the gorge, the rib we'd climbed, the cairn at R-7. "For whoever comes next," he said. "Somebody should leave a map that tells the truth."

Above us, unseen in the cloud, the river went on rising toward its secret. I put my hand flat on the stone and felt it: that hum, deeper now, steadier, the pulse of something vast and warm and patient underneath the mountain's cold skin. The source. Pulling us up the way water pulls everything—without effort, without hurry, absolutely certain of its destination.

Dov yawned and curled against his pack. Elias folded his map away. I took first watch, because I always take first watch, and sat with my back to the warm stone listening to the mountain purr.

That was when I saw the light.

Far above us, up where the cloud broke against a shoulder of black rock, a flicker—orange, small, human. A fire, burning where no legal map permitted anything to burn. It showed for three heartbeats, then the cloud swallowed it.

"Elias," I said softly. "Dov. Wake up. Look."

They came to my side of the overhang and stared up into the murk with me, three faces lit by a dead flame, watching the place where a living one had been.

"Someone's up there," Dov breathed.

"Yes," I said. My heart was doing something that wasn't quite fear anymore. "And tomorrow, we knock."

The river hummed beneath us, all the way up into the dark, like it already knew who was waiting.
