## 11. The Scree Field

I have drawn maps for a living for eleven years, and in all that time I never once drew a rockslide. Not because I didn't know they existed—any cartographer worth his contour lines knows the mountains are held together by gossip and spite—but because a map is a promise about where the ground will be when you arrive. The scree field above the second bend of the Rivergate broke that promise at four minutes past noon on our third day out, and it did it with the enthusiasm of something that had been waiting all summer for an audience.

We had stopped to eat. That's the detail that keeps snagging in me like a burr in wool. We had *stopped*. Lior had said the words "we'll take ten minutes past this next shoulder," and Dov had groaned theatrically because he was sixteen and groaning was his native language, and I had sat down on a boulder the size of a kitchen table and gotten out the flatbread and the last of the smoked fish, and for ninety seconds the three of us were just people having lunch.

Then the mountain cleared its throat.

It doesn't sound like thunder. Everyone says it sounds like thunder and everyone is wrong. Thunder comes from the sky, from far away, from nothing personal. A rockslide starts with a click—a single stone somewhere above you deciding its relationship with the slope is over—and then a hundred more stones make the same decision in the same instant, and then the whole hillside files for divorce. It sounded like the mountain was unzipping itself from crown to toe, and the zipper was coming straight at us.

"Move!" Lior was already up, already shoving Dov toward the cliff face, toward the one patch of solid bedrock where the slide would split around them like water around a bridge piling. She'd told us about this on the first night, back when we still thought the worst thing ahead of us was weather: *when the slope goes, get against living rock.* I remember thinking it was the kind of thing guides say so they feel useful. I remember being wrong about that with great precision.

I went the other way. That's the other detail. While Lior hauled Dov into the lee of the cliff, I turned downhill—I want to say instinct, but instinct would have followed her; what I did was panic wearing instinct's coat—and got maybe six steps before the world tilted under my boots like a deck of cards being shuffled by a giant. The ground didn't move so much as *leave*. The entire slope, tons of it, gray and roaring, went sliding past me toward the river two hundred meters below, and I went with it, riding a wave of broken rock the way you'd ride a horse that hated you personally.

Here is what I learned about falling down a mountain: there is no strategy. There is no technique. There is only the animal arithmetic of keeping your head off things harder than your head, and even that is mostly luck. I remember the taste of dust. I remember my pack strap wrenching my shoulder half out of its socket and popping it back with a sound I felt in my teeth. I remember thinking, with a clarity that still embarrasses me, *the fish is going to be ruined.*

Then the slope ended.

Not gently. The slide ran out of mountain and dumped me over a lip of bedrock, and for one long, weightless, perfectly silent moment I was not touching anything at all. I was a cartographer in freefall over terrain I had mapped myself, and I remember looking down at the river—the same river we'd been following for days, green and patient and utterly indifferent—and thinking that from up here it looked exactly like it did on my own drawings. Blue line. Wiggly. Two-dimensional. Safe.

A hand closed on my wrist.

The jolt nearly took my arm off. My boots swung against the rock face and found no purchase, and suddenly all of my weight—all of me, every kilogram of failed cartographer—was hanging from Lior Almeida's grip, anchored somewhere above me in a crack I couldn't see, her body wedged behind a boulder the size of a coffin.

"Don't," she said through her teeth, "move."

"I wasn't planning to."

"You were planning to. You always plan. Stop."

She was right, which was worse than the fall. I could feel her recalibrating, redistributing, doing the quiet terrible math of two bodies and one anchor. Her breathing came in short controlled pulls, the way she did everything, as if even terror were a skill she'd practiced. Below my feet the river made its small indifferent noise. Above me, dust sifted down through the light like the mountain shedding dandruff over the whole affair.

"Dov!" she called upward. "Dov, talk to me!"

A pause. Then, from somewhere above and left, thin and cracked but unmistakably alive: "I'm here. I think my arm's—" A sound that was not words. "My arm's wrong."

*Wrong.* Sixteen years old and he used the word *wrong*, which meant he'd already looked at it, which meant it was bad enough that a boy raised around livestock and chainsaws couldn't find a better word for it.

"Hold on," Lior said—to him or to me or to the mountain, I couldn't tell. "Both of you hold on."

* * *

Getting me up took forty minutes. Getting to Dov took another hour, most of it spent traversing a ledge I would not have walked across on a bet in any other circumstance, following Lior's voice and her hands, because the dust hadn't settled enough to trust your eyes. She moved like someone reading a page only she could see. At one point she stopped, pressed her palm flat against the rock, and stood there for a full minute, and I understood she was listening—not hearing, *listening*, feeling for the tremor of more stone deciding whether to follow its brothers down. When she finally moved again she moved fast, and I learned that day that speed and hurry are different animals, and she only kept company with one of them.

Dov was sitting in the split of the cliff face with his left arm cradled against his chest at an angle arms are not issued at. His face was gray except for two bright spots of fever-color on his cheeks, and when he saw us he tried to stand and salute, or something adjacent to saluting, and Lior put him back down with one hand.

"Let me see."

"It's fine," Dov said, in the voice of every injured male since the invention of gravity. "It's just—it's fine."

"It's broken," Lior said. Not unkindly. Just true, the way she said most things. "Mid-forearm. Both bones, probably." She was already unpacking; her hands moved through the kit without her eyes, which is how you know someone has done a thing in bad places. "How did it happen?"

"A rock hit it." He paused. "A big rock."

"How big?"

He considered. "Rock-sized."

Lior almost smiled. Almost. It crossed her face like weather over a lake and was gone. "Good answer. This is going to hurt, and then it's going to stop hurting, and then you're going to be angry for about a week, and then it's going to itch for two weeks after that, and if you tell anyone I let you splint it with survey stakes I'll deny it."

"We don't have survey stakes," I said.

"We do now." She nodded at the wreckage scattered down the slope behind us. The slide had done us one favor, though it had taken some persuading to see it that way: the mountainside had excavated a section of the old survey line—wooden stakes, rusted flagging tape, a marker post stamped with the ministry's eagle. Evidence of the team that had come up here before us and not come down. Dov's arm would be set on government property, which felt appropriate. Most of the trouble in my life had arrived on government property.

Setting the bone was the worst ten minutes of the trip, and I include the rockslide in the comparison. Lior pulled, Dov screamed—a short, furious, astonished scream, the scream of a boy discovering a new room in the house of pain—and I held his shoulders and talked nonsense at him about rivers and maps and anything, everything, while sweat poured off both of us in the cold air. Then it was done, splinted and bound, and Dov lay back against the stone white as paper and started laughing, high and helpless, the laughter of the reprieved.

"That," he gasped, "was so much worse than the rockslide."

"The rockslide," Lior said, packing the kit, "had the decency to be quick."

* * *

We lost the trail. That's the sentence I keep trying to rewrite and can't. We lost the trail—the route Lior had planned, the line she'd walked twice before, the whole careful architecture of our ascent—because the architecture was now at the bottom of the valley in ten thousand pieces. What remained was a slope of fresh rubble, loose as ball bearings, that would take a sure-footed goat twenty minutes and take us, Lior judged, a full day, moving slow, testing every step, with a boy who had one working arm and a man who now flinched at gravel.

So we lost the trail, and then we lost the light, and then we lost the food.

The food deserves its own paragraph, because the food deserves a funeral. Our packs had been strapped tight when the slope went, but the slide had gone through our camp cache—Lior had staged supplies at the shoulder, the way she staged everything, methodically, a day's rations ahead of need—and when we finally picked our way around to where the cache should have been, there was a scar in the hillside and nothing else. Two days of food, wrapped and labeled in her precise hand, buried under four meters of new geology. We dug for an hour. We found one tin of oil, a bag of salt, and Lior's spare rope, coiled and intact, which she stared at for a long moment as though the rope had personally betrayed her by surviving.

"Two days' food," she said finally, "for three people, with five days to go."

Nobody said anything. There wasn't a version of saying anything that helped.

"We turn back?" I asked, and hated myself for asking, because I already knew the answer, and because part of me—the part that had hung over the river with my life dangling from a stranger's fist—wanted the answer to be yes.

Lior looked upstream. The gorge narrowed ahead into the dark throat of the high country, and somewhere beyond it, two hard days' climb away, was the source, and the truth, and whatever was left of the people who'd drawn the maps that ruined my life. Somewhere behind us was Voss, and the patrols, and a valley filling up with soldiers who had orders and no curiosity.

"No," she said.

One word. She didn't dress it up. She didn't explain. She just stood there with the wind pulling at her collar and the mountain smoking dust above her and said *no* the way other people say good morning, and I understood—for the first time, really understood, not the way you understand a thing in a briefing but the way you understand it in your spine—that this woman had decided what she was for, and the decision was not negotiable.

"Rationing starts now," she said. "Half portions. We move at first light. And nobody," she added, glancing at Dov, whose expression had already begun assembling an argument, "nobody gives me their share. I know how you two think. Eat your ration or I'll sit on you."

"She'd do it," Dov told me solemnly. "I've seen her sit on people."

"You have not."

"He has not," Lior confirmed. "But I've seen her eat a full ration while two hungry men watched," Dov said, "which is basically the same thing."

That night we camped in the split of the cliff, three bodies pressed into a hollow barely wide enough for two, because the alternative was the open slope and none of us trusted it anymore. The mountain grumbled occasionally—small settlements, stones finding new opinions—and each time, I felt Dov go rigid against my side, and each time Lior's voice came out of the dark, low and even: "Just settling. Sleep." Whether the mountain was settling or she was simply lying, none of us were in a position to check.

I lay awake doing sums instead. It's what I do instead of sleeping; it's why my maps are accurate and my mornings are grim. Three people, five days, half rations: the numbers didn't close. They didn't close by a lot. And somewhere in the middle of the arithmetic I realized I was calculating not in days but in body-weight, the way starving men and accountants do, and I made myself stop, and then I started again, because stopping felt like lying.

Every mile forward costs something. I'd written that once, years ago, in the margin of a field atlas, as a joke—a young man's joke, the kind of joke you make when the heaviest thing you've ever carried is a theodolite. Nobody laughed now. Every mile forward cost. It cost Dov a bone that would set crooked in the cold and ache every winter of his life, starting this winter, starting now. It cost two days we didn't have, while the storm season stacked its clouds behind the western peaks like a debt collector. It cost the food, the trail, the margin, the illusion—the whole comfortable fiction that we were travelers rather than gamblers, that the mountain was scenery rather than stakeholder.

And it cost me something I didn't have a name for yet. Something about hanging over that river and seeing my own blue wiggly line at the bottom of it. I'd spent eleven years drawing the world as if it would hold still. It doesn't hold still. It never held still. The map was wrong on purpose—I'd read those words in a dead man's cairn and thought they were about the state, about the dam, about the lies men draw over valleys they intend to drown. Lying on cold stone with a broken boy's breath whistling beside me, I understood they were about everything. About every line I'd ever inked. The map is wrong on purpose, because the truth is that the ground moves, and the river cuts, and the mountain slides, and the only honest map is the one that admits it's already out of date.

In the morning we ate half a ration each, and Lior gave Dov a third of hers anyway, cut into strips disguised inside his bread, and pretended not to notice him noticing, and Dov pretended not to notice her pretending, and I watched the two of them conduct this entire conspiracy in silence and thought: *this is what it costs, and this is what it buys,* and I could not for the life of me tell which column was bigger.

We climbed. The scree fought us step by step, giving way underfoot, erasing our path behind us as fast as we made it, so that by noon we were walking on a route that no longer existed anywhere except in Lior's head. She navigated by features that weren't there anymore—a notch, a leaning spire, a pale band of quartz—calling corrections to herself in a murmur, redrawing the mountain in real time.

By late afternoon we crested the shoulder, and the high country opened ahead of us, and there, tucked into a fold of the valley floor maybe two kilometers distant, half-swallowed by shadow—

Smoke.

A thin gray thread of it, rising straight up in the windless cold. A campfire. A stove. Somebody was up here, in the forbidden basin, past the legal limit, past the patrol line, past everything—somebody was warm.

Dov saw it first. He grabbed my sleeve with his good hand and pointed, and his face, which had been gray with pain for two days, lit up like a struck match.

Lior went very still. I watched her count the smoke the way she counted everything—carefully, suspiciously, twice.

"That," she said quietly, "is not on my map."

And then, softer, almost to herself, in a voice I'd never heard her use:

"But I think I know whose it is."
