## 13. The Source

The river told us we were close before we saw it. For three days it had been running cold and clear off the snowfields, the kind of water you could read a watch through. Then, on the morning of the fourth day, it went warm.

Not warm the way a stream gets warm in August. Warm the way a wound is warm. Elias crouched at the bank, dipped two fingers in, and held them up like a man testing whether he was still dreaming.

"Geothermal," he said. "We're inside the anomaly."

"We're inside a lot of things," Dov said. "Most of them bad."

The boy had earned the right to be sour. His arm hung in a sling Elias had fashioned from a tent fly and two tent poles, and he'd climbed four thousand feet with it, complaining at a volume calibrated to keep our spirits up and every animal in the valley informed of our position. He was sixteen and made of the same stubborn material as his mother, and I had stopped telling him to go back. Somewhere below us, past the scree field and the patrol line and the whole descending ladder of the world, Maren was waiting for news that would either save her village or get her son killed. I tried not to do that arithmetic. Voss did arithmetic. I was trying to be a different kind of animal.

We climbed out of the river gorge as the light went long and copper, and the trees gave up—not gradually, the way treeline usually surrenders, but all at once, as if the mountain had drawn a line and said *no further*. Above the line the ground steamed.

I had seen steam before. Fumaroles, hot springs, the ordinary plumbing of a young range. This was different. The whole slope above us breathed. Threads of vapor rose from a hundred cracks in the rock, drifting and braiding in the windless air, and the smell was sulfur and hot stone and something older underneath, something mineral and vast, like the breath of a thing asleep. The snow that should have covered this altitude lay in dirty, melting patches, and through the gaps the rock was stained yellow and white and rust-red, painted by water that had been rising through the mountain's bones for longer than any of our villages had existed.

"God," Elias said quietly.

"Don't," I said. "It's not God. It's geology. God would be less dangerous."

But I didn't believe myself, and I don't think he did either. There are places in the mountains where you understand, in your body, that you are standing on the skin of something, and that the skin is thin. This was one.

We came up the last shoulder in the grey hour before full dark, and there it was.

The lake sat in a basin like an eye in a face of rock. It was the color of old pewter, ringed with white mineral crust, and across its whole surface the steam rose in slow, continuous columns that caught the last light and turned to rose and gold. At its far end, where the basin wall split along a fissure you could have dropped a house into, the water glowed. Not reflected light. Glowed—a deep, sullen orange, like a forge door left ajar. And from that glowing fissure, the river was born. It poured out of the mountain's wound, steaming, and ran down the valley we had just climbed, gathering cold tributaries as it went, becoming by degrees the ordinary river that watered three villages and a hundred miles of country below.

The source of the Serran. The rivergate. Every map I'd ever owned drew this as blank paper and a dotted line labeled *unverified*.

The map wasn't wrong, I thought. The map was a lie with a straight face.

"Someone's been here," Dov whispered.

He'd seen it before I had. On the near shore, tucked into a hollow below a rock overhang: a tent. Then a second. A smoke-blackened stove pipe poking out from behind a boulder, its smoke so thin and well-banked you'd miss it from fifty feet. A line of laundry—actual laundry, socks and a shirt, grey with mineral dust—hung between two stones. And on the flat rock beside the water, arranged with the neatness of people who had been living with very little for a very long time: five tin plates, five spoons, and a kettle.

Four figures came out of the steam.

I had my hand on my knife before my brain caught up. Elias grabbed my wrist. "Wait," he breathed. "Wait. Look at them."

They were thin. That was the first thing—all four of them, thin in the way that people get thin when hunger has become a roommate rather than a visitor. Their clothes were government issue gone to rags, patched with sailcloth and birch bark and, in one case, what looked like the leg of someone else's trousers. They carried no rifles. One of them carried a fishing spear. They moved slowly, deliberately, the way people move when they have learned that sudden motion gets you shot, and they stopped at the edge of their camp and stood in a line, hands visible, and waited for us to decide what we were.

The one in front was a woman. She was gaunt and frost-burned and her hair had gone almost entirely white, and I was looking at a stranger.

Then she lifted her chin the way she used to when she was about to correct a student's bearing reading, and eleven years fell off her like a dropped coat, and it was Ilsa Brandt.

The world did something structural. I felt it in my knees before I felt it in my head.

"You," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "You're supposed to be dead. All of you are supposed to be dead. The whole country thinks you're dead."

"Not the whole country," Ilsa said. Her voice was rougher than I remembered, sanded down by cold and silence, but the cadence was the same—the cadence that had taught me everything I knew about reading a slope. "The right people know we're alive. That's rather the problem."

"You testified against me." The words came out of me like something I'd been carrying too long, a load I'd forgotten the weight of until I set it down. "Eleven years ago. You stood in a hearing room and you told them my route plan killed Aldous Vey. You looked at me across that table and you said, *the guide bears full responsibility.* I was twenty-six years old, Ilsa. You were the only person in that room who knew the route map was falsified, because you approved it, and you said nothing."

"I know what I said."

"You knew. You *knew* the map was wrong, because you told me so yourself, the week before, when you thought I hadn't noticed the switchback that wasn't there—"

"Lior." She said my name the way she used to in the field, one syllable, an order to hold position. "You're standing on the reason. If you want to shout at me, shout at me down there. Up here we keep our voices down, because there are men on the ridge behind you with rifles and orders, and they have been waiting four months for someone to lead them to this camp."

The steam moved. Somewhere in it, a kettle began to sing, thin and domestic and obscene.

Elias stepped forward into the space between us. "Ilsa. It's Renner. I came out in the spring. I—" He stopped, and I watched two guilty people find each other across a campfire's worth of distance, and I understood suddenly that I was the only one in this basin who hadn't known. The cartographer who walked out. The mentor who stayed. The team that voted to vanish. They had all been carrying pieces of the same secret, and I had been carrying the piece nobody wanted: the blacklist, the disgrace, the life cut in half, all of it resting on her testimony like a house on a foundation.

"You knew," I said to Elias. "In the cabin. When you told me the team voted to disappear. You knew who was up here."

"I knew the leader was someone you'd know," Elias said. "That's why I didn't say the name. I needed you to come, and I was afraid you wouldn't, if you knew."

He was right. That was the worst part. He was right, and I would have turned around at the trailhead, and I said so by saying nothing.

* * *

They fed us fish from the hot springs—fish that had never known a winter, fat and pale and tasting faintly of sulfur—and while we ate, the story came out in pieces, passed around the stove like a ration.

The survey had found the basin in the first week. Found the fissure, measured the outflow, run the numbers, and understood within days that they were sitting on enough heat to power half the province—and that the state's plan, already drafted, already budgeted, was not to use it but to bury it. Dam the gorge below. Flood the basin. The geothermal field, drowned under two hundred feet of impounded water, would be sealed, unmeasurable, deniable. Because the field, if it were ever measured honestly, came with a footnote the state could not afford: the same fault system that fed the fissure ran under the dam site. Fill the gorge, load the fault, and the engineers' own worst-case appendix said what the executive summary did not—that the reservoir could rupture the field, and a poisoned, scalding watershed would run a hundred miles downstream through every farm and fishery the province had.

"The flooding was the point," said the surveyor named Okonkwo, a small precise man with a beard gone to iron wool. "Not a side effect. You flood the basin, you destroy the evidence. Three villages are a rounding error. The watershed is an acceptable loss. The geothermal field becomes a rumor, and rumors don't have coordinates."

"So we voted," said a young woman I never learned the name of, because she never offered it and the others never used it, which told me something. "Eight of us. Four to come down and go public, four to stay and hold the data. And the escort—"

"The escort wasn't escorting," Ilsa said. "They were witnesses. Every man jack of them had orders we only understood later. Nobody was supposed to come down from this basin with a story. When we split, the ones who went down—" she looked at Elias, and something moved behind her eyes that might have been grief, "—were supposed to be met at the low bridge. You know why they weren't."

"Because Voss got the timing wrong," Elias said.

"Because Voss got a new order," Ilsa said. "And Voss, whatever else he is, is a man who follows the paper. The paper said no witnesses. The paper did not say what to do about four surveyors who refused to die, so the escort came up here to finish it, and we went to ground, and we have been going to ground ever since. Four months. We've eaten half our boots. We've watched them sweep this basin eleven times." She turned to me, and the firelight did something unkind to her face, hollowing it, making it older than it was. "And now you've led a boy with a broken arm and the world's most wanted cartographer straight up the river they're watching, and every one of those sweeps has just become a formality."

"I didn't lead them," I said. "I led us. There's a difference."

"Is there?" she said. "You were the best guide I ever trained. I told the hearing that too, if you'll recall. Right before I buried you."

* * *

I walked out of the camp after that. Not far—far enough to be out of the firelight, not far enough to be stupid. I sat on a rock above the fissure and watched the water come out of the mountain, glowing, endless, and I tried to do the thing I'd come four thousand feet to do, which was to feel one thing at a time.

It wouldn't sort. That was the truth of it. I had spent eleven years carrying her testimony the way you carry shrapnel—too deep to cut out, too close to the spine to ignore—and I had built a whole life around the wound. The false name. The low valleys. The hunters who never learned my real name, the checkpoints I mapped in my head like a man mapping a country that had annexed him. All of it, every day of it, had Ilsa Brandt's voice under it: *the guide bears full responsibility.* And now the voice was forty feet away, thin and frostbitten, drinking melted snow and eating spring fish, and it was the same voice that had taught me to read snow slopes and tie a tension hitch and walk away from an avalanche debris field without digging, because digging is for later and walking is for now.

She had been silent to save her career. That was the simple version, and I believed it, because I'd watched her ambition for six years and knew its weight. But the simple version didn't explain why she was here. Ambition doesn't climb to a steaming basin and eat its own boots for four months. Something else had brought her—something that had maybe been growing in her all along, the way the fault under the dam site had been growing, silent, loaded, waiting.

"Lior." Dov's voice, low, from the rocks behind me. "Elias says you need to see the ridge. Now."

I looked up. The steam was thinning in the evening cold, and through the clearing vapor, on the far shoulder of the basin, maybe a mile off, there was a light. Not a fire—a shuttered lamp, opening and closing. Once. Twice. A pause. Twice more.

Signal. Not to us. Across the basin, to a second light that answered from the rocks below the outflow, where the river left the mountain.

"They found our trail," Dov said. "Didn't they. The tracker."

"Voss's man read my boot prints off a rockslide," I said. "Of course he did." I stood, and my knees had stopped arguing, and everything in me had gone quiet and clear, the way it goes when the route ahead finally shows itself. "Go tell Ilsa she gets her wish. We keep our voices down. And tell her—" I stopped, because I didn't know how to finish the sentence, and Dov was already running back toward the firelight.

Below me the river came out of the mountain's wound, glowing, and ran down toward three villages that had maybe two weeks left before the diversion tunnels closed, and somewhere in the dark between here and there, men with rifles and orders were closing the last of the distance, and I understood that the mountain had brought me all this way to make me choose, at last, what I had spent eleven years refusing to choose: whose side the river was on.
