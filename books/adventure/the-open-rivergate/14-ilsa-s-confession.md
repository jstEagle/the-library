## 14. Ilsa's Confession

I have given a great many briefings in my life. I once lectured two hundred cadets on glacial moraine identification without a single note card. I have testified before tribunals with the calm of a woman reading a grocery list. But standing at the edge of the fissure lake, watching Lior Almeida's face do arithmetic it had clearly been doing since she saw me alive, I understood that none of that had prepared me for this.

The lake steamed behind me like a kettle left on a stove by someone who never intended to come back. The four of us had lived beside it for eleven weeks—Tomas, Priya, Okonkwo, and I—and in all that time I had not once said aloud what I was about to say now. Not because I lacked the words. I'd rehearsed them on the scree, in the tents, into the steam. Because saying them to the wind was free, and saying them to Lior would cost everything I had left.

She stood with her arms crossed, which was new. The girl I trained crossed her arms only when she was done listening and starting to decide. Elias hovered near her shoulder like a man watching two weather fronts collide. Behind them, Dov—the boy, Maren's boy, all elbows and stubbornness—had the decency to look away toward the water.

"You knew," Lior said. It wasn't a question. "When Voss came to my cabin offering amnesty. You knew they'd send someone."

"I knew they'd send someone," I said. "I didn't know it would be him, or when, or whether he'd shoot first. Knowing isn't the same as being able to warn you. There are four people in this basin whose survival depends on no one knowing where we are, and one of them is me."

"That's not what I'm asking."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

The wind came down off the saddle above the lake and pushed the steam sideways, and for a moment the whole basin smelled of sulfur and cold stone, the way it had smelled the day everything ended for both of us, six years ago and forty miles south.

"Sit down, Lior."

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will." I lowered myself onto the flat rock I'd been using as a chair since September. My knees announced their opinion of this. "Then stand there and let me talk, because if I stop halfway through this I will never start again, and you deserve the whole thing even if you hate every word."

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about the Serran Survey Authority, which no longer exists under that name because it was folded into the Ministry of Resources two years ago in a reorganization designed, I am convinced, specifically to lose paperwork: it ran on maps the way a church runs on scripture. And I was its cartographer-in-chief, which made me something worse than a priest. A priest can at least claim he believes what he says.

In the spring of the year Lior lost everything, I approved a route map for a client expedition into the Corren Narrows. I want to be precise here, because precision is the only currency I have left. The map showed the high traverse as rated Class Two. It was Class Four. The difference between those numbers is the difference between a walk and a death sentence, and I knew it, because I had walked it three years earlier with a survey team and filed an internal memo saying so—a memo that was returned to me by the directorate with a note suggesting that the tourism revenue projections for the northern valleys depended on a more optimistic assessment of access routes.

I redrew the map. That is the sentence I have carried for six years: *I redrew the map.* Not falsified—redrew, as though it were a matter of calligraphy. I moved a line. I changed a number. It took me perhaps twenty minutes, sitting at my desk with the afternoon sun coming through the blinds, and I remember thinking, with a clarity that still shames me, that the odds were long. That experienced parties climbed harder things than that every season. That the memo would eventually surface and the map would be corrected and no one would ever be hurt.

A party of four went up that route in June. One of them was a man named Aldous Ferro, fifty-one years old, a banker with more money than sense and a guide he trusted completely.

The guide was Lior Almeida.

* * *

"I've read the tribunal record," Lior said, when I paused. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of someone holding a door shut against a flood. "Twice. It's public. I've had six years to memorize it."

"The tribunal record contains what the tribunal was allowed to see." I looked up at her. "Do you know why your appeal failed? Not the politics—I mean mechanically, procedurally, why the second hearing went the way it did?"

"My route log was ruled inconsistent."

"Your route log was ruled inconsistent because the comparison document—the official survey map—was mine, and it was entered into evidence as authoritative without challenge. Nobody challenged it. Do you know why nobody challenged it?"

Lior said nothing. Dov had stopped pretending to look at the lake.

"Because the map bore my certification," I said. "Because Ilsa Brandt had never put her name on anything that wasn't true. Twenty-two years in the service. Every chart, every traverse rating, every hazard annotation—clean. My signature was worth more than your testimony, Lior. They didn't weigh the evidence. They weighed the reputations attached to it, and mine outmassed yours by several tons."

"And you could have—" Her voice cracked on the second word and she rebuilt it. "One letter. One statement. 'The map is wrong, I drew it wrong, under orders.' One page in your own hand and the whole thing comes apart differently."

"Yes."

"You were in the room. Third session. I saw you sitting in the gallery in a grey coat. You looked at me exactly once."

"I remember the coat," I said. "I still have it. I couldn't wear it again afterward. Isn't that a pathetic detail? I kept the coat and couldn't wear it, as though the fabric had absorbed some of what I'd done, and getting rid of it would have been too much like admitting—"

"Why?" It came out of her like a rockfall. "God help me, Ilsa, I've spent six years building theories. I decided you were a coward. Then I decided you were ambitious. Then I decided someone had something on you. Then I decided you simply never believed I was innocent, that you thought I'd misread the route, cut corners, killed him through incompetence, and your silence was just institutional loyalty to the institution over the individual. Which theory is it? Pick one. I've earned the right to hear the real one."

I made myself hold her eyes. This was the part I had never managed to rehearse past, the part where the words existed and refused to come out in order.

"All of them," I said. "At various hours of various nights, all of them. But the true one is smaller and uglier than any of those. Here it is: I was three years from my pension and my pension was the first financial security of my entire life. I grew up in a house where winter was a thing you survived rather than a season. I had a mother in a care facility in Lowmark whose fees came out of my salary. And the directorate made it very clear—not with threats, nothing so crude—with a tone of voice, with a returned memo, with a reassignment of my deputy to a posting in the eastern flats, that the institution would protect itself and I could choose which side of that protection I wanted to stand on."

"So you chose."

"I chose," I said, "and I told myself it wasn't really a choice, that anyone in my position would have done the same, that the system was the villain and I was just a gear inside it. And then I watched them take your certification, and your name, and your livelihood, and I told myself that at least you were alive, that Aldous Ferro's family had their settlement, that the truth would out someday because truths always do—which is a lie, Lior, in case you haven't learned it yet. Truths don't always out. Truths sit in sealed files in basement archives until someone burns the building or retires. I know because I helped seal some of them."

The steam drifted between us. Somewhere above the lake, a bird I couldn't name made a sound like a hinge.

* * *

"When the survey team came up here in August," I said, "we found the fissure field on the fourth day. Tomas flagged the thermal readings and I sat on that ridge—" I pointed, and my arm shook slightly, age or feeling, I couldn't tell which "—and I watched the river come out of the ground already warm, and I understood within an hour what the state would do with it. Dam the gorge. Flood the basin. Three villages. Kestrel Ford. Lowmark. Ansel's Reach."

"My mother," I said, and my voice finally broke, and I let it, because hiding it from her after everything else would have been obscene, "is in a care facility in Lowmark. The same facility. When I drew the flood projections, I drew the line that goes through her window. I sat there with a pencil and calculated the depth of water that will cover the bed she sleeps in, and I did it accurately, because accuracy is the only virtue I have ever actually possessed, and then I took the projections to the team and said: we go dark. We fake the disappearance. We refuse to certify. Because I had already sold one person's life for this institution, and I discovered, sitting on that ridge, that I could not sell three villages. There was a quantity of guilt beyond which the arithmetic stopped working, and I reached it."

Lior hadn't moved. She stood the way she used to stand at the top of a pitch, checking her anchor before committing to the next move, and I realized with a strange, distant clarity that she was doing exactly that—testing whether anything I said would hold her weight.

"Elias tells me you voted," she said quietly. "That half the team wanted to come down and go public."

"Four of us wanted to come down. Five wanted to stay hidden and build the case properly—get the second journal decoded, get independent verification, come down with something unanswerable instead of something arguable. Arguable gets buried; I've buried things myself, I know how it's done. The vote was five to four to stay. And then the escorts turned back early, and by nightfall we understood they hadn't turned back. They'd been withdrawn. Replaced. We heard the radio traffic on Okonkwo's set. There was a list of names and ours were on it, and the instruction wasn't *detain*."

"So you stayed dead."

"We stayed dead," I agreed. "It's the one honest thing I've done in six years, Lior. Disappearing. I recommend it. All my lies fell off me the moment my name did."

"Don't." Her voice was low and hard. "Don't do that. Don't package your silence as the beginning of your redemption. You stayed silent about *me* for six years while you collected your pension and your commendations and your directorship. You didn't grow a conscience when you vanished. You grew one when the flood line touched your own mother's bed. That's not courage, Ilsa. That's proximity."

The words went in the way an ice axe goes in—clean, deep, exactly where aimed. I nodded, because there was nothing else to do with them.

"You're right," I said. "I've had eleven weeks on this mountain to construct a better version of myself and I can't make it stick. Here is the version that survives inspection: I was a coward with a good reputation, and my reputation was the weapon they used against you, and I handed it to them loaded. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm telling you that Ilsa Brandt, certified cartographer, twenty-two years of clean charts, signed the map that killed Aldous Ferro and destroyed you, and then sat in a grey coat in the third session of your tribunal and said nothing, and I will sign whatever statement you want, testify wherever you want, name every name I know, whenever you ask. That's not forgiveness. That's inventory. Take all of it."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. Down at the water's edge, Dov had picked up a stone and was turning it over in his fingers, listening so hard I could feel it from thirty feet away.

"Do you know what the worst part is?" Lior said at last. Her voice had gone quiet, which from her was far more dangerous than loud. "Six years ago, in the tribunal, I needed you to be a coward. It was easier. If you were a coward, then the world was simple—bad people do bad things, I got crushed between them, fine. But you weren't a coward. You were tired, and careful, and you loved this work, and you did it anyway, one small line on one map, the way everyone does it—one small compromise, one reasonable afternoon. And that means there is no monster. There's just what people do when the cost lands on someone else." She looked at the lake, at the steam rising off it, at the country the state meant to drown. "And I've spent this whole climb wondering if I'm any different. Whether I'd redraw the line too, if my mother's bed were on the other side of it."

"What did you conclude?" I asked.

"I concluded I don't know," she said. "And that might be the most frightening answer there is."

She uncrossed her arms. It was such a small motion, and I read it the way I used to read her rope signals, and it was not forgiveness. It was not even absolution. It was something more conditional and more valuable: it was *noted*. Filed. Entered into the record of whatever tribunal she was conducting inside herself, where the verdict had not yet come down and might not for years.

"I need the rest of the journal," she said. "The second half. Elias says Ansel's Reach has it, and I need to know exactly what the state knew about the geothermal rupture before I decide what we do next. Can you decode it?"

"I wrote half the cipher myself," I said. "Yes."

"Then we leave tomorrow, first light, before the storm season closes the high passes." She turned toward camp, then stopped, and did not look back at me when she spoke. "Ilsa. The grey coat. Burn it."

She walked down toward the tents, and Elias followed, and Dov trailed after them, and I sat alone on my rock with the steam rising around me like the breath of something patient and enormous underneath the world.

I did burn the coat, that evening, in the stove. It smelled of wool and smoke and six wasted years.

And it was while I was feeding the last sleeve into the fire that Okonkwo came up from the watch point at a pace no man should run at his age, and said the words that ended our eleven weeks of dying honestly:

"There's movement on the southern ridge. A lot of it. And they're carrying lanterns."
