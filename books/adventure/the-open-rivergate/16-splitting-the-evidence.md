## 16. Splitting the Evidence

There is a particular kind of arithmetic you learn as a cartographer, and it has nothing to do with distances. It's the arithmetic of redundancy. You never draw one copy of a map that matters. You draw three, you hide two, and you memorize the third so thoroughly that even if they burn every sheet of paper in the province, you can still walk into a survey office with a stick of charcoal and a wall and reproduce it from memory. My old instructor called it "distributing your trust." He also died alone in a snow cave with all his copies in one satchel, which I have always felt was a pointed lesson delivered posthumously.

So when Lior spread the second journal's contents across the floor of Ilsa Brandt's high camp and said, "We need to split this up," I felt something I hadn't felt since before the scree field: professional satisfaction. Grim, cold-soaked, half-starving satisfaction. But satisfaction.

"Three copies," she said. "Minimum."

"Four," Dov said from the doorway, where he'd been standing guard with his arm in its sling and his chin set at an angle he had clearly inherited from his mother. "You're forgetting the one in my head."

Lior looked at him for a long moment. He was sixteen, frostbitten at the fingertips, down to his last dry pair of socks, and he had just volunteered his memory as archival storage for evidence that could get him shot. She didn't laugh at him, which I respected. I would have laughed. It's how I cope, and it's why nobody asks me to deliver eulogies.

"Four," she agreed. "But the head-copy is backup only. Paper travels."

* * *

The copying took most of a day, and I want to be honest about what we were working with, because the romance of this sort of thing tends to obscure the tedium. The original documents were: one surveyor's field journal, water-stained; a bundle of borehole readings on graph paper gone soft as cloth; two photographic plates of the fissure lake's thermal gradient, taken by a man named Ostrander who had since been shot on a fogbound ridge and deserved better than to be a footnote; and my own contribution — the master contour sheets I'd drawn of the basin, annotated in the margins with the corrections the state had ordered me to omit.

That last part is mine to carry, so let me carry it plainly. The dam plans were not mistaken about the geothermal field. They were not ignorant of it. The engineers' projections showed, in figures no one could misread, that impounding the river behind the Rivergate dam would raise pressure along the fissure system until it ruptured sideways into the watershed — and everything downstream of the rupture, for a hundred miles, would run hot and mineralized and dead. Kestrel Ford, Lowmark, Ansel's Reach were not collateral damage. They were the first line item in a cost-benefit analysis where the benefit column belonged to people who would never drink that water.

I had drawn the maps clean. I had left the margins blank because I was told to leave them blank, and I told myself it was just a job, and I was very good at telling myself things. So yes, when Lior handed me the pen and said "write it all out, three times," there was a penance in it that went beyond practicality. My hand cramped. Good. Let it cramp.

The method was Lior's idea, because of course it was. She thinks in systems the way other people think in sentences.

"No two copies travel together," she said, laying out the materials on Ostrander's camp table like a surgeon laying out instruments. "No two copies use the same medium. And no single person knows where all of them are — including us. Especially us."

"That last rule seems designed to frustrate me specifically," I said.

"It is," she said, without looking up. "You talk in your sleep. Dov told me."

"He's lying."

"He described your dream about the marmot in some detail."

I decided to let that go. There are hills worth dying on and hills worth dying near, and the marmot question was neither.

* * *

Copy one went onto oilcloth, written in pencil over graphite dust, rolled tight inside a section of bamboo Ilsa cut from the stand by the hot spring — the same stand, though none of us said it, where the survey team had once pitched their mess tent back when they still believed they were a team. The bamboo sealed with wax. It would float, if it came to floating. It would survive immersion, mild compression, and moderate dishonesty from customs inspectors. It could not survive fire, which is why it wasn't going anywhere near me.

"You take this one," Lior said, holding it out.

I didn't take it. "Give me the sewn one instead."

"The sewn one goes with Dov."

"Dov is sixteen and has one working arm."

"Dov is fast, quiet, knows the low country, and looks like exactly what he is — a boy walking home. Nobody searches a boy walking home. Whereas you—" she looked me over with the frank appraisal of someone pricing a horse, "—look like a man carrying something. You've looked like that since the day I met you. You raid a supply cache like you're smuggling state secrets."

"I *was* smuggling state secrets."

"And see how that worked out." She pushed the bamboo roll at me again. "Elias. If they stop you, they stop you. That's the whole design. You being caught can't be allowed to matter more than the paper arriving. Can you carry it like that? Like it's already lost?"

I thought about it honestly, which took longer than taking the roll would have. Because here is the thing about distributing trust: it means accepting, in advance, that you might be the copy that doesn't make it. Not the plan failing — just you. Your legs, your luck, your particular stretch of riverbank. The system survives precisely because it doesn't need any one of us, and a man has to be a certain kind of humble to sign on to that arrangement. I am not naturally that kind of humble. I became a cartographer because maps are the one place where being right is enough.

"I can carry it like that," I said finally. "Ask me again tomorrow, though. My answer improves after breakfast."

"There won't be breakfast."

"Then ask me anyway and I'll lie."

She almost smiled. Almost. With Lior, almost-smiles are a currency worth more than actual ones; actual ones she spends freely on Dov, but the almosts she saves for adults who've earned them. I counted it and put it away somewhere safe.

* * *

Copy two was the sewing project, and it fell to Ilsa, because Ilsa Brandt — my former mentor, the woman whose testimony had ended my career before Lior's ended hers, though that accounting can wait — turned out to be the only person among us who could put a stitch in waxed canvas without making it look like a fight. She sat by the stove with Dov's spare jacket across her knees, a double-edged needle flashing, and fed thin sheets of treated paper into a false lining she built along the left seam, panel by panel, while Dov watched her hands with an expression I recognized. It was the expression of a boy deciding whether to be afraid of someone.

"She taught me field drafting," I told him quietly. "Twenty years ago. Before either of our troubles."

"Did she teach you the margin rule?"

I blinked. "What margin rule?"

"Everything important goes in the margins." Dov nodded toward the contour sheets spread on the floor, where my marginal annotations crawled around the official linework like ivy around a fence. "She says the center of the map is what they let you publish. The margins are what's true."

I looked across the room at Ilsa, bent over her needlework, and felt the strange vertigo of realizing that a person had carried your own private doctrine further than you had — that she'd taught it onward, to a boy in a drowned valley, in the years while I was teaching myself to stop believing anything. Cartographers pass things down. It's the only immortality the trade offers. I just hadn't expected mine to arrive wearing a sling and defending my sleep-talk.

The jacket, when she finished it, looked exactly like a worn canvas jacket. She made Dov put it on, then made him take it off, then resewed one seam that had puckered, then made him put it on again. Perfectionism, in Ilsa, was never vanity. It was the discipline of people who know that small failures compound.

"If they take the jacket," she told him, "the papers burn or rot within a week. Don't defend it. Ever. You are not guarding the copy. You are one of three chances. Understood?"

"Yes," Dov said.

"Say what the jacket is."

"A chance," Dov said. "Not the truth. Just a chance."

Ilsa nodded once, satisfied, and I understood that she had given him the only gift that helps in this situation, which is permission to fail. I wished someone had given it to me at twenty-six. It might have saved everyone a decade.

* * *

Copy three stayed with Ilsa, and this was the one that generated argument, because Ilsa announced her intention to keep it in the high camp itself — sealed in the cairn where Lior had found the first journal page, under stones that had stood through forty winters of meltwater.

"They'll search the camp," Lior said. "When Voss's team comes through, they'll turn it inside out."

"They'll search our routes and our packs," Ilsa said. "They will not pull apart a trail cairn stone by stone. Cairns are sacred to people like Voss. Order lives in them."

"People like Voss dynamited a shrine on the Ansel road last spring because it blocked a sightline."

"People like Voss," Ilsa said, "are having doubts. I watched his face at the fog line. I've read a thousand men's faces on ridgelines and his was doing arithmetic he didn't like."

I kept out of that argument, mostly because both women were right, which is the worst kind of dispute to referee. Instead I spent the evening preparing the fourth copy — the one in heads — by making Dov recite the essentials back to me until he had them cold: the fissure coordinates, the pressure figures, the date of the engineers' report, the name of the deputy minister whose signature authorized proceeding despite the rupture projection. Names matter more than numbers, I told him. Numbers can be disputed. A signature is a confession.

He got it all by midnight, pale and stubborn, and then asked the question I'd been dreading.

"What happens to us? After. If it works."

I considered lying to him, in the gentle way adults lie to the young, and decided against it. He'd earned better, and besides, Lior was in earshot and she can smell a soft answer at fifty paces.

"If it works, the dam dies in committee, the inquiry takes two years, and everyone involved gets subpoenaed. Including me. Possibly especially me — I'm the one who drew the clean maps. I'll spend a season explaining myself to men in good coats." I shrugged. "And then, if I'm lucky, somebody hands me a permit for the upper Serran and I find out whether I still remember how to draw honest lines."

"And Lior?"

I glanced over. She was on watch at the tent flap, silhouette against the last grey light, and she had the stillness she gets when she's listening without admitting it.

"Lior," I said, "is going to have to decide what she wants for the first time in years. Being blacklisted makes your choices simple. Everything after is harder."

Dov nodded slowly, the way boys do when they understand more than they let on, and went to sleep.

* * *

We distributed the copies at dawn, because Lior believes — and I've come to agree — that anything done at dawn feels provisional, reversible, less like fate. The bamboo roll went into my inner pocket, against the chest, where it rode like a second heartbeat. The jacket went onto Dov's shoulders, and Maren's son walked out of the camp heading south along the ridge with his sling hidden under the canvas, looking for all the world like a boy going home late. Ilsa put her key to the cairn — a flat stone she'd chosen herself, weighted with a mark only she would know — and stood over it a moment with her bare hand flat on the rock, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

Then there were two of us at the camp, and the mountain around us, and the long descent ahead.

"One more distribution," Lior said, as we struck the tents.

"What's that?"

"Us. We don't walk down together." She folded the stove away with quick, economical movements. "Separate trails, a day apart, converging at Kestrel Ford. If they pick up one track, the other stays clean."

I looked at her. Fog was lifting off the fissure lake below, that slow white breathing the mountain does in the early morning, and beyond it the whole watershed lay waiting — the gorges, the patrol lines, the low bridge, the reed marshes, the capital somewhere past all of it where committees were, at this very hour, initialing diversion-tunnel schedules.

"You realize," I said, "that this is the part of the expedition where the cartographer traditionally gets separated from the group and presumed lost."

"I realize," she said, "that you've read too many of your own legends." She shouldered her pack and paused, and for a moment the almost-smile came all the way out — rare as a clear day in storm season, and worth the wait. "Kestrel Ford, Elias. Four days. Be the copy that arrives."

I watched her go down the eastern trail until the fog took her, and then I stood alone in the camp of the vanished with a bamboo tube against my heart and the whole river between me and the truth's next reader, and I confess I said a small, unprofessional word to the marmots, and started west.
