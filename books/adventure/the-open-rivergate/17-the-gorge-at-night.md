## 17. The Gorge at Night

There is a particular silence that comes before a river dies. I'd heard it once before, years ago, standing above a valley the state had drowned for a reservoir—the water rising so slowly that the village below had time to pack its churches and its photographs, but not its graves. That silence was patience. This one was a held breath.

The diversion tunnels were scheduled to close at dawn. Elias had gotten the schedule out of a patrol radio he was absolutely not supposed to have been within earshot of, and he'd come down to Kestrel Ford at a dead run with his coat flapping like a man being chased by his own guilt, which, to be fair, he was.

"Dawn," he said, bent double in Maren's kitchen, hands on knees. "They close at dawn."

Maren didn't look up from the kettle. "Then we leave tonight."

"Forty people," I said. "Through the gorge. In the dark."

"Thirty-eight," Maren said. "Old Sef went to bed an hour ago and informed me he intends to die in his own house, and I've decided arguing with him is a waste of good breath." She set two cups down without asking who wanted tea, because in Kestrel Ford tea simply happened to you. "So thirty-eight, plus whatever stragglers decide at the last minute that dying in their own houses is overrated."

I looked at Elias. He looked at me. Somewhere between us hung the arithmetic neither of us wanted to say aloud: forty kilometers of gorge, three crossings where the trail gave up and the water took over, one night, no moon worth mentioning, and a column of people whose average age was either nine or seventy with very little in between.

"I'll lead," I said, because if I said anything else my mouth would have betrayed me into saying something useful like *maybe we should wait*, and waiting was how valleys got drowned.

* * *

We gathered at the ford at midnight, and I will tell you what forty frightened people look like by lantern light: they look like a congregation. They stood on the shingle in their winter coats with bundles tied to their backs—grandmothers carrying sewing machines, which I want you to sit with for a moment, because when the state tells you that you have one night to become a refugee, you do not bring your grandmother's sewing machine unless you believe, with your whole stubborn heart, that you are coming back.

Dov was there with his arm strapped to his chest, scowling at anyone who looked at him like luggage. His mother wasn't. Maren had made her choice at the last minute with the same efficiency she applied to everything: she would stay with Old Sef and the four others who refused to go, keep the lamps lit in the windows, and give the valley someone left to be empty for.

"You come back," she told me, gripping my wrist hard enough to leave marks. "You come back and drink tea in my kitchen and complain about my biscuits."

"That's a terrible bargain," I said. "Your biscuits are famously bad."

"Then it's a bargain you can't refuse." She let go. "Go."

I turned to face them all, and here is the thing nobody tells you about leadership: it isn't the speech. The speech is easy. You say *follow me, stay between the lanterns, if the water takes you let it take the bundle first*—you say the practical things, and the practical things sound like courage. What's hard is the moment before the speech, when you look at forty faces and understand that every single one of them has decided, without evidence, that you know what you're doing. That is not trust. That is something heavier than trust. Trust can be earned. This was faith, and faith is just love wearing a backpack.

"Stay between the lanterns," I said. "If the water takes you, let it take the bundle first. Bundles float. People don't." A few of them laughed, which was the point. "And if you hear me shout *high ground*, you climb. Don't ask why. Don't look back. Climb."

An old woman near the front—Talia, who had taught me to salt fish the summer I hid in this valley with a false name and a broken reputation—raised her hand like a schoolgirl. "Lior. Girl. Are you scared?"

I considered lying. Talia had known me since before I knew how to lie well; she'd have smelled it.

"Yes," I said. "But I'm scared in the direction we're going, which is the only useful way to be scared."

She nodded as though I'd passed an exam. Behind her, thirty-seven other faces decided the same thing, and we stepped into the water.

* * *

The gorge does not narrow gradually. It narrows the way a fist closes—one moment you're walking on gravel with room to breathe, and the next the walls lean in and the river runs black and fast beneath a ledge barely wide enough for a determined goat. I'd run this stretch twice in daylight with clients who paid me to make danger feel like adventure. At night, with children, it felt like what it was: a throat, and we were going down it.

I carried the lead lantern. Dov carried the second, refusing help with the ferocity of a boy who had lost enough already and would not lose the job of holding light. Behind him came the column—Elias sweeping the rear with the third lantern and a length of rope coiled over his shoulder like a bandolier, calling softly every hundred meters: *count*. And the count came forward, person to person, a number passed hand to hand up the line like bread. Thirty-eight. Thirty-eight. Thirty-eight. As long as the number stayed thirty-eight, we were still a village. The moment it changed, we were a list of names.

The first crossing came an hour in, where the ledge crumbled into a chute and the only path was a shelf of wet stone angled against the current. I'd anchored a line there on my scouting run—Elias and I had come up two days earlier, and I'd driven pitons until my arms shook—and now I clipped the lantern to a rock spur so its light fell across the water and showed the villagers exactly what they were trusting me to get them through.

"Children first," I said. "On the rope. Hands on the line, feet where I put mine."

A girl named Petra—eight years old, missing a front tooth, carrying a cat in a basket that had opinions about the whole enterprise—went first because her mother's hands were full with a baby and her father was one of the ones who'd stayed. She took my hand at the lip of the crossing and looked up at me with the absolute, unblinking confidence of the young.

"Is it deep?"

"Not where we're stepping."

"Is it cold?"

"Yes."

"Okay." And she stepped.

That is the whole theology of childhood, right there. Not bravery—bravery is fear managed. What Petra had was better: she believed the adult holding her hand had already done the arithmetic, and she was free to spend her attention on the cat. Every step she took across that shelf, I felt the weight of that belief like a second pack. I have guided wealthy men through avalanches who trusted me less than an eight-year-old trusted me in the dark, and I will tell you which one kept me honest.

One by one they crossed. The old man Havel, seventy-three, who insisted on carrying his wife's ashes in a brass urn because he'd be damned if the state flooded her twice. The twins from Lowmark, who giggled the entire crossing in a way that made everyone behind them braver. A young mother who slipped at the far edge and I caught her by the collar, and for one suspended second she hung above the black water looking at me with total terror, and then her feet found stone and she said, "Sorry, sorry—" and I said, "You weigh nothing, next," because that was a lie she needed more than the truth.

Count. Thirty-eight. Onward.

* * *

The second crossing nearly broke us.

It came at the narrows, where the canyon pinches to six meters and the river—fed by snowmelt upstream, fed by whatever the mountain was doing with all that heat beneath our feet—ran high and loud enough that we had to shout. There was no shelf here. There was a fallen spruce, wedged between the walls at head height, slick with spray, spanning the worst of the current. Below it, the water folded white around a boulder the size of a chapel.

I tested the log myself, twice, crawling it end to end with the lantern hooked to my belt. It held. It would hold for one careful person. Whether it would hold for forty, and whether forty careful people could remain forty careful people at three in the morning with their legs shaking—I couldn't promise that. So I did the thing guides do instead of promising: I built redundancy. Elias anchored a rope along the log's spine, chest-height, and I stationed Dov at the near end with instructions that consisted mostly of *if anyone freezes, talk to them, don't touch them, a hand on a scared person's shoulder puts you both in the river.*

Then the crossing began, and I learned what terror looks like when it's been transmuted.

It looks like an old woman taking off her boots.

Talia sat down at the base of the log, unlaced her boots with fingers that trembled from age or cold or both, tied them together, slung them around her neck, and started across barefoot. When I caught up to her at the midpoint—because of course I did, I was crawling the log beside every single one of them whether they needed me or not—she glanced at me sideways.

"Wet boots are worse than wet feet," she said. "Feet dry. Boots rot."

"Talia, you're seventy-eight years old."

"And you're a child playing at mountains," she said, and kept moving, one bare foot, then the other, along a wet log above a killing current, humming. Actually humming. Some song from her girlhood, half-swallowed by the roar of the water.

I want to be honest about what that did to me. It didn't make me brave. It made me furious—furious at the men in the capital who had done their arithmetic in offices and concluded that these people were a rounding error, furious at Voss and his patrols and his hazard signs, furious at a world where a woman who could cross a flood-swollen gorge barefoot at seventy-eight while humming had to do it at all. But fury, I've found, is just tenderness that hasn't found its work yet. So I put it to work. I crawled that log forty times that night. My palms split. I didn't notice until later.

Halfway through the column, the cat got loose.

Petra's basket tipped on the log—the cat had expressed its views with escalating violence for an hour—and the animal shot out onto the span, yowling, claws finding the wet bark, and stopped dead in the exact center, frozen, tail bottlebrushed, directly above the white water. Petra, behind it, froze too. Dov started talking to her, low and steady, exactly as instructed, and I moved toward the cat with the slow idiocy of a woman who has spent her life around animals and never once learned better.

"Come here, you ridiculous creature," I murmured, and it looked at me, and it looked at the water, and it made a decision I respected: it leapt for my shoulder, dug every claw it owned through my jacket and into the meat beneath, and rode the rest of the crossing attached to me like a burr. Petra followed, crying and laughing at once. At the far side she tried to take the cat and it declined, so I walked the next kilometer with a cat on my shoulder and blood seeping down my arm, and I have never in my life felt more like exactly the right person in exactly the right place.

Count. Thirty-eight.

* * *

The third crossing was the one I hadn't solved.

I'll admit it plainly, because Lior Almeida has spent enough of her life being credited with competence she had to fake: I stood on the bank at the head of the lower gorge and looked at the water and did not have an answer. The trail ended at a pour-over—a smooth ramp of stone where the whole river slid down two meters into a churning pool, and beyond it the wall rose sheer. In summer, low water, you climbed down the ramp's left edge on ledges. Tonight the river covered the ledges entirely. The map—my map, the one in my head, drawn by a younger and more arrogant version of myself—said *possible at low water.* The river said otherwise.

Behind me, thirty-eight people waited in the dark with their bundles and their sewing machines and their urns of ash. Ahead of me, dawn was coming whether I liked it or not, and somewhere downstream the diversion tunnels were being readied to swallow this same water and redirect it, and every minute I spent staring at the pour-over was a minute stolen from the people behind me.

Elias came up beside me. He didn't say anything. He'd stopped saying anything helpful hours ago; he'd learned, somewhere on the log, that his job was to stand where I could see him and be calm, and he did it with the discipline of a man paying off a debt.

It was Dov who saw it. Of course it was.

"Lior." He pointed up. Above the pour-over, where the walls pinched tightest, an old pine had fallen across the canyon decades ago—high up, fifteen meters, wedged trunk-down between the walls like a picket stuck in a bottle. Its root plate was jammed against the upper rim. Its crown had rotted away, but the trunk itself ran down the wall at a shallow angle and vanished into the dark on the far side. A ramp. A rotten, moss-slick, insane ramp.

"No," I said.

"It's the only way."

"The only way is not automatically a way."

"Then find another one," Dov said, with the flat cruelty of a sixteen-year-old who has broken his arm and kept walking anyway, "before the sun comes up."

I hated him. I loved him. I tested the tree.

The root plate held—it was jammed under a lip of bedrock, and the tree had died in place rather than fallen, which meant its weight was already committed. The trunk was sound for the first eight meters, then soft. I went up it on my belly, distributing weight, testing every handhold, and at the soft section I traversed out along the wall on ledges the river had mercifully left exposed above the flood line, and came down on the far bank, and stood there breathing hard, and looked back up at forty faces—thirty-eight faces, plus mine and Elias's now on opposite sides of the water—waiting.

"Rope traverse," I called up. "Line runs along the trunk. One at a time. Children roped to me from below."

It took two hours. Two hours of sending people up a dead tree in the dark, of talking Petra across with the cat riding her basket this time, having apparently reconsidered its loyalties, of hauling Havel's urn across slung in a stuff sack because his hands wouldn't close anymore. Twice someone slipped and the rope caught them and the whole column below went silent as a held note, and twice I talked them back onto the wood, voice steady, heart hammering somewhere up around my teeth. Elias crossed last, after re-rigging everything twice, and when his boots hit the far bank he sat down right there in the gravel and laughed—one short, cracked bark of a laugh, the laugh of a cartographer who had spent his career drawing safe routes and had finally, at long last, walked one that wasn't.

Count. Thirty-eight. All of them. Every hand pulled across, every debt repaid—not debts I owed them, understand, but debts I owed to the version of myself who had once been carried through this valley half-dead and nameless, and had never figured out how to say thank you. This was the thank you. Forty crossings' worth.

* * *

We came out of the gorge at first light, into the wide grey valley below Ansel's Reach, and the people did what people do after a night like that: they sat down on the ground and laughed and cried and checked each other's hands and counted themselves again, out loud, thirty-eight, thirty-eight, as though the number might wander off if unattended.

I stood apart, wringing out my sleeves, and looked back up the gorge. The river ran quiet there in the early light, deceptively gentle, the way rivers always look after they've tried to kill you. Somewhere up that black slot, the water was already beginning its long obedience to gravity and engineering. By tomorrow night, the diversion tunnels would take it. By spring, the maps would change. By summer, Kestrel Ford would be a shape under brown water, and Maren's kitchen, and Old Sef's stubborn bed, and the church bell they'd buried in the sand because they refused to let the state ring it.

Dov came and stood beside me. His arm was filthy, his face was grey with exhaustion, and he was grinning like a lunatic.

"We made it," he said.

"We made it," I agreed.

"So why do you look like that?"

Because I was counting. Because thirty-eight people had come out of the gorge, and the count was supposed to stay thirty-eight, and it had—but the count had never included the ones who'd stayed behind, or the ones still up the mountain, or the man I'd sent downstream alone with a satchel of proof and a head start measured in hours. Elias had left us at the fork below Ansel's Reach an hour before dawn, slipping off toward the low bridge with his copy of the evidence sewn into his coat, and I had watched him go with the specific dread of a woman who knows exactly how bridges work and exactly how ambushes work and has just realized the two share a geography.

"He'll make it," Dov said, following my eyes. "He's sneaky. He's basically a marsh creature."

"Mm," I said.

Downstream, faint on the wind, came a sound I told myself was geese.

It was not geese.
