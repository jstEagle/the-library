## 19. The Bargain

The river at night sounds like a crowd that won't stop talking. Forty people moving through it sound like a riot.

I stood on the south bank with my lantern raised, counting heads as they came across the shallows in twos and threes, and I will tell you honestly what I was counting: not forty. Not yet. The water was high with snowmelt and the gorge had narrowed to a throat upstream, and every crossing cost us time we had been spending like drunkards for three days. Behind me, the lantern line snaked up the canyon wall toward the old shepherd track. Ahead of me, across the river, the flat ground where the patrol road met the water.

And somewhere on that road, headlights.

I had been watching them for an hour. Two lights, maybe three, moving with the patience of men who knew they had all the leverage in the world. Voss hadn't rushed us. He could have. That was the part that kept my stomach knotted — not the soldiers, but the courtesy. A man who surrounds you slowly is a man doing arithmetic.

"Lior." Maren's voice, low, from the water's edge. "Dov's group is at the narrows. Twenty minutes, maybe less. The elders can't go faster."

"Then we buy them twenty minutes," I said, and heard how calm I sounded, and was briefly proud of myself, the way you're proud of a stranger.

She looked at me. Maren has known me since the worst year of my life, when I came down out of the high country with nothing but a false name and a talent for not being seen, and she fed me anyway, and never once asked the questions that would have been her right to ask. She looked at me now the way she looked at weather coming over the pass.

"You have that face," she said.

"I don't have a face."

"You have a face like a woman about to do something stupid and necessary. It's the same face you had when you left for Ansel's Reach."

"It's my only face. I've grown attached to it."

She almost smiled. Then the headlights swung around the bend below the road and lit the river white, and neither of us smiled at anything again.

* * *

Here is what I had, going into that negotiation: one lantern, one river, forty lives in various states of wet and terror, a geological report that could end a government ministry, and the certain knowledge that Captain Edric Voss had stood at the edge of the fissure lake four days ago and seen what I saw.

Here is what he had: rifles.

I have never believed that arguments are won by the side with the better case. They're won by the side that can afford to lose. But there is a narrow category of man — I've met perhaps three in my life — who can be reached through the thing he believes in, and I had spent the whole climb down from the source trying to decide whether Voss was one of them. He had looked at the lake. I had watched him look. A soldier who sees a wound in the earth big enough to swallow a valley and keeps his orders anyway is a machine. A soldier who sees it and goes quiet is a man with a crack in him.

I walked out onto the shingle with the lantern held high, alone, and I heard Maren swear at my back in the old valley dialect, which I took as a blessing.

"Captain Voss," I called across the water. "You want to talk, or do you want to write a lot of paperwork?"

The lights stopped. A long moment. Then a figure came down the bank to the waterline, and behind it a half-dozen silhouettes that had the good manners to stay up on the road. Voss stopped where the spray reached his boots. He looked tired in the way that men look tired when the tiredness has passed through them and come out the other side as something structural.

"Almeida." My real name. He'd been saying it like that since the basin, deliberately, the way you'd set a bone. "You're standing in a river."

"I'm standing on a border. Your side of it has soldiers. Mine has grandmothers."

"Mine has orders."

"I know. I've read your arithmetic." I lifted the lantern so the light caught my face, which is a stupid theatrical gesture, and I did it anyway. "Forty-one people in that gorge. You've counted them. You know the dam's diversion gates close at dawn. You know that if your men fire into this canyon, the echo will do half your work for you, and the other half will be done by the river when forty bodies jam the narrows and the water backs up into the villages that haven't emptied yet. You've run those numbers. I've watched you run them. You didn't sleep last night either."

He didn't answer, which from Voss is a paragraph.

"Here's my offer," I said. "The column crosses. All of it. Elders, children, the lot. Your men watch them walk to the low road and you let them go — no arrests, no lists, no names taken down for later. In exchange, I come with you. Voluntarily. And Ilsa Brandt comes with me."

That landed. I saw it land. His head came up a fraction, the way a hound's does.

"Brandt is the leader of a conspiracy against the state."

"Brandt is a dying woman with a bad heart and the only complete copy of the second survey journal in her head. You want the truth about what the dam does to the watershed? It's in her. You can have her testimony or you can have a hole in the map and a poisoned river for a hundred miles and a commission of inquiry in three years asking why nobody told them. I'm telling you now. On the record." I tapped my own temple. "This is the record."

"You're offering yourself," he said slowly, "as a hostage."

"I'm offering myself as a witness. It's the same thing, the way your government uses words."

The river talked between us. Somewhere upstream a child cried and was hushed, and the hushing was worse than the crying, because it meant they were all listening, all forty of them, holding their breath in the dark for whatever I did next.

"Almeida." He said it quietly now. "Do you know what happens to people who walk into a detention facility voluntarily? They don't come out the way they went in. I've seen the process from the inside. There are men under my command who would prefer you didn't come out at all."

"Yes," I said. "I've met your tracker. He reads trails like scripture and I expect he prays too. But you're not him, Captain. You stood at the fissure lake and you didn't radio it in as a hazard to be buried. You misfiled the first report — don't look surprised, Elias reads your filing codes the way your tracker reads tracks. You've been quietly losing this war for a week and the only thing you haven't figured out is how to surrender with your career attached. I'm offering you the how."

"And what is it you think I want, exactly?"

"The same thing I want. For this to have been worth it." I lowered the lantern a little, because my arm was shaking and I didn't want it to show. "You saw the lake. You know the state's own engineers falsified the load calculations. You know that in nine years, when the dam fails or the field ruptures, some other poor devil will stand on a riverbank in the dark trying to buy time with the truth, and there'll be no one left to sell it to. Take me. Take Brandt. Let them go. And then go home and write the most boring report of your career."

He was silent for a long time. The headlights made a white wall of the mist, and in it his face was just a shape, and I thought: he's going to refuse, he's going to give the order, and I'm going to spend whatever seconds remain running upstream into the gorge with a lantern and no plan, which has been my entire method throughout this affair, so at least I'll die consistent.

Then, upstream, the first of Dov's group came around the bend — a lantern, then another, then the small wet sounds of people fording in the dark — and Voss turned toward the sound and I watched him make his decision the way you watch a man decide to jump.

"The column passes," he said. "Unmolested. No lists. And Almeida — if a single weapon is produced from that crowd, the arrangement dies where it stands."

"There's not a weapon in forty people. There's a fiddle, two sewing kits, and a rifle with no firing pin that old Ansel carries for the comfort of it."

"Ansel's Reach is under forty meters of scheduled water," he said, and there was something in his voice that might, in a better world, have been called grief.

"Then he'll want the comfort all the more."

He turned and walked back up to the road, and the headlights swung away from the river, and I stood in the spray with my lantern and my shaking arm and understood that I had just sold the future I'd been stupid enough to start wanting. There had been a morning, three days ago, at the high camp, when Elias had laughed at something — I don't even remember what, some terrible joke about contour lines — and I had looked at him with his frost-burned face and his guilt and his stubborn decency, and I had thought: after. After the villages, after the hearings, after. There was going to be an *after*, and it was going to have him in it.

I had just traded the after for the now. It was, I decided, the correct price. It was also the worst money I ever spent.

* * *

You will want Voss's side of it, and I can give it to you, because years later he gave it to me, in a hearing room with bad coffee, and I have his permission to tell it, which he expressed by saying "I don't care what you tell them," which from Voss is a love letter.

This is what he told me he was thinking, standing on the road above the river with his men behind him and his orders in his breast pocket like a stone:

He had been doing arithmetic since the basin. That was the whole of him, he said — a man who believed in the state the way other men believe in God, and who had discovered, standing at the edge of the fissure lake with the steam rising off it and the surveyor's frozen body four days behind him, that his God had been falsifying its own load calculations. He did not stop believing. That's the thing people got wrong about him afterward, when the scandal broke and the cartoons came out. He didn't convert. He audited. And the audit came back the same way every time: the project was unsound, the cover-up was criminal, and the only asset he possessed that could not be replaced by the next loyal man was his signature on a report.

So he signed the wrong thing. Quietly. He filed the basin reconnaissance as *geological hazard, unresolved, recommend seasonal closure* — which was true, in the way a locked door is true — and he attached no photographs, and he dated it so it would sit in a drawer until the hearings, and he wrote in the margin, in his small engineer's hand, a single sentence he later admitted he had rewritten eleven times: *The mountain was surveyed. The survey was not believed.*

He let forty-one people walk out of a gorge in the dark because the alternative was a massacre that would have been investigated by the same ministry he was lying to, and because — he said this flatly, without apology — the woman on the shingle had offered him the one currency he could still spend: a version of events in which he had chosen, rather than failed. He took it. He was not proud. He was not ashamed. He was, he said, a bookkeeper at the end of the world, and the books, for once, balanced.

The one thing he would not tell me — then or ever — was what he wrote in the private log that night. But his tracker, who reads everything the way scripture is read, once let slip that the log's last line for that date was four words long, and that they were not words a soldier chooses lightly.

I have spent some years guessing at them. I believe they were: *She bought them all.*

* * *

The column came down out of the narrows as the sky went the color of dishwater. I stood aside and watched them cross, and I did the thing you're not supposed to do, which is count them again, one by one, because each one was a debt. The cobbler's wife who had mended my boots my first winter in Kestrel Ford. The twins who used to follow me to the fishing holes. Old Ansel with his dead rifle and his living dignity. Maren last, because Maren would be last, carrying someone else's grandmother on her back across the shallows, and when she reached my side of the river she didn't stop, she just gripped my shoulder once, hard, the way you grip a rope, and said, "You come back. That's not a request, that's a debt, and you know how I feel about debts."

"I'll come back," I said, and we both knew I was lying, and we both knew I knew, and she went on up the low road without looking back, because looking back is a luxury of people with a future.

Elias was the last but one. He came out of the water white-lipped and furious, which was his way of being heartbroken, and he grabbed my lantern arm and said, "No. Whatever you did up there on that bank — undo it. We go together. There's a shepherd track, there's always a track, you taught me that—"

"There's no track out of this one, Renner."

"There's always a—"

"Elias." I said his name the way Voss said mine, deliberately, setting a bone. "The evidence goes downstream with Dov. The truth survives. That was always the plan — any one of us is enough, remember? You're enough. You've been enough since you walked into my cabin stealing my tea." I put my hand flat on his chest, over the jacket pocket where his copy of the report rode like a second heart. "Get this to the capital. That's your crossing. This one's mine."

"You're doing the thing," he said, and his voice cracked straight down the middle. "The stupid necessary thing."

"It's my only thing. I've grown attached to it."

And he laughed — God help us both, he laughed, wet and terrible, right there in the spray — and then he kissed me, brief and hard, like a signature on a document he intended to honor, and then he turned and walked up the road after Maren and did not look back either, because I had taught him that too.

Ilsa was waiting at the waterline where I'd told her to be, wrapped in two blankets, her bad heart ticking under them like a slow clock. She looked at me and didn't apologize — we'd done our apologizing at the source, or begun it; I don't think either of us believed it was finished — and she said only, "You didn't have to add me to the price."

"Nobody buys you, Ilsa. You're not for sale. You're a witness, and witnesses travel together." I picked up her pack. "Besides, you owe me a confession and I intend to collect it in a hearing room, under oath, with your whole career on the table. You don't get to die in a gorge owing me that."

"No," she said. "I don't suppose I do."

We walked up to the road together. The soldiers made a lane for us, and their faces were carefully blank in the way of men who had been told not to see what they were seeing. Voss stood at the head of the lane. He took my pack — my pack, the one with thirty years of mountain in it — and handed it to a corporal with an odd gentleness, and then he said, to me, quietly, under the sound of the river:

"The diversion gates close at dawn. Your people will be at the low bridge by then if they keep that pace." He paused. "The report I file today will say I found the gorge empty. Rocks, water, and a geological hazard. It will say nothing about lanterns."

"Thank you, Captain."

"Don't thank me. File nothing, sign nothing, and stay alive in there. Dead witnesses are a convenience to everyone, and I am done being convenient." He looked out at the river, at the light coming up gray behind the gorge, and something moved across his face that I recognized, because I'd been wearing it for years: the look of a person who has just found out what they're for. "Almeida. The fissure lake. When they ask you what it looked like — and they will ask — tell them it looked like the earth opening its eye. Tell them exactly that. It's the only sentence that ever made my superiors flinch."

They took us down the patrol road as the sun came up over the Serran range, me and Ilsa Brandt, in the backs of two trucks with our hands not quite tied. Behind us the river ran on toward the low bridge, toward the marshes, toward the sea, carrying Dov and his jacket and the truth at the speed of water.

I did not look back either. But I listened. All the way down the valley, I listened to it, the way you'd listen to a promise.

And in a marsh hamlet two days later, a man with a healing bullet wound and a burned copy of nothing would hear a radio crackle to life with the first word of the scandal — and that, as they say, is another chapter.
