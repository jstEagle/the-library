## 20. Downstream

The capital smelled like coal smoke and wet stone, and I hated it on principle before I'd been in it an hour. Kestrel Ford smells like river mud and woodsmoke and my mother's bread. The capital smells like a city that has decided it is better than weather.

I had sewn the evidence into my jacket lining myself, three nights before we split up at the gorge, because my mother said if you want a thing done right, do it yourself and don't tell anyone how. She was talking about bread at the time, but it holds for treason too. The jacket rode against my ribs like a second heartbeat the whole way down. Bus to Lowmark. Freight barge past Ansel's Reach — I watched our village slide by from the water and did not wave, because waving felt like goodbye and I wasn't ready to say that one yet. Then the long road south, thumb out, sleeping in hay barns where the farmers either didn't ask questions or asked them kindly.

The papers said the valley was closed for a geological hazard. The papers said a lot of things. My mother taught me that the first lie of any government is always printed in a font so boring you fall asleep before you doubt it.

I found the journalist the way Elias said I would: by being stubborn in a lobby until someone gave up trying to ignore me. Her name was Petra Vane, and she worked for *The Meridian Ledger*, which was the kind of paper that got read by people who signed things. She was maybe fifty, with ink stains on her fingers and the eyes of someone who had heard every story twice and believed half of them once.

"You're the boy from the valley," she said, not as a question.

"I'm a boy from a valley," I said. "There are several."

She almost smiled. "You have something for me."

"I have something for everyone," I said. "That's rather the point."

We went up to her office, which was small and cluttered and had a window overlooking a courtyard where pigeons conducted their endless gray parliament. I unpicked the stitches my mother would have approved of — tight, even, done under lamplight with a sailmaker's needle — and laid the contents on her desk: the survey plates, the correspondence memos, the second journal pages in Elias's careful hand, and the geological assessment with its fatal little footnote about watershed contamination downstream of the fissure field. A hundred miles of poisoned river, annotated in a bureaucrat's tidy pencil as *acceptable externality*.

Petra Vane read for a long time. The pigeons argued. My hands shook, and I put them in my pockets so she wouldn't see, because I wanted her to think I was the kind of person who carried treason calmly, even though inside I was sixteen years old and my whole village was underwater in a future that hadn't happened yet but was scheduled.

"This is real?" she said finally. "Not forged?"

"Check it," I said. "Please check it. Check everything. If you print this wrong, they'll use the mistake to bury it."

Now she did smile, properly. "Boy," she said, "you just described my entire career."

She checked everything. It took eleven days, during which I stayed in a boarding house run by a widow who charged me half rate because I fixed her roof gutter and reminded her of a son who had moved to the coast and written twice a year. Eleven days of walking past newsstands, of hearing strangers talk about dam schedules and grain prices while the truth sat in a locked drawer across town. Eleven days of learning what waiting does to a person. It turns you into a kettle. Everything in you wants to boil over and there is nothing to do but sit on the heat.

On the twelfth morning, the *Ledger* ran it across six columns.

**STATE KNEW FLOOD PLAN WOULD POISON WATERSHED, RECORDS SHOW.** Below it, smaller: **Three highland villages slated for inundation; internal memo calls contamination 'acceptable.'**

I bought nine copies. I couldn't help it. I stood on the corner outside the boarding house holding nine newspapers like they were live fish, and a woman going to work looked at me, looked at the headline, looked back at me, and said, "Is that your doing?"

"It's my doing," I said, "and about forty other people's, most of whom can't be named."

"Well," she said, "it's about time somebody drowned the right people for a change." And she walked on to work, and I stood there laughing in a way that was mostly crying, because that is what vindication feels like when it finally arrives: not triumph, exactly. More like a fever breaking. Like spring thaw, when the ice doesn't shatter all at once but groans and shifts and gives way overnight, and in the morning the river is running and you can't remember how you ever believed winter was permanent.

By noon it was everywhere. Other papers picked it up, some honestly and some spinning it, and by evening there were men on the radio using words like *suspended*, *inquiry*, *ministerial resignation*. The word I kept listening for came on the third day, in a dry announcer's voice between a report on wool tariffs and a concert announcement:

*"The Ministry of Works confirmed today that construction on the Serran diversion project has been suspended pending review. Asked whether the villages of Kestrel Ford, Lowmark, and Ansel's Reach would be removed from the inundation schedule, a ministry spokesman said the schedule was 'under active reconsideration.'"*

Under active reconsideration. Bureaucrat language for *we lost*. I sat down on the boarding house steps and laughed again, that same laugh-cry, and the widow brought me tea without asking why I was making noises at the sky.

They were spared. Not saved — spared, which is a smaller word, a word with conditions attached, a word that could be taken back. But the water was not coming. That night I wrote to my mother through the channel Elias had set up, four sentences, nothing that could hang anyone: *It printed. They stopped. We're still here. Come south when you can.*

Then I sat with the strange hollow feeling of having finished the most important thing I will probably ever do, at sixteen, carrying it sewn into a jacket, and realizing that the rest of my life now has to find some way to be worthy of the delivery.

Elias

The marsh hamlet was called Reedhollow, population forty-one, forty-two counting me, and it existed for two reasons: eels and silence. I fit in well, since I had brought plenty of the latter and, after the bridge, not much else.

Let me be honest about the bridge, because honesty is the only currency I have left and I've spent most of it anyway. They intercepted me at the low crossing with the copy of the records in a oilskin wrap inside my coat. Two of Voss's men, competent, tired, doing their jobs. And I burned it — soaked it in lamp oil from the ferryman's shed and put a match to it while they shouted, and stood there watching eleven months of cartography and the only uncorrupted duplicate of the watershed assessment curl into black lace on the wind off the water. They hit me with a rifle butt for it. Cracked two ribs and opened my scalp, and left me for the reeds, because dead cartographers attract inquiries and unconscious ones don't.

What they never understood — what Voss himself may not have understood, though I suspect he did, that man did arithmetic with lives but he could also do arithmetic with paper — was that burning my copy cost the truth nothing. There were three copies. That was the entire design. Lior called it ballast: distribute the weight so no single sinking takes the ship down. Mine was the decoy, the one meant to be caught, the one whose destruction would satisfy them while Dov's jacket and Ilsa's cache did the real work. I knew that when I struck the match. Knowing it made the fire no less terrible to watch. You do not spend years drawing maps of a place to watch them burn, even as a sacrifice, even knowing the original lives on somewhere safe. Every sheet of drafting paper I have ever touched has felt like a promise.

So: Reedhollow. The eel-fishers took me in because their matriarch, a woman named Sef who was missing two fingers and all patience, decided my ribs needed setting more than my story needed telling. I spent three weeks flat on a pallet in her smokehouse, breathing shallowly, listening to the marsh make its endless small sounds — water moving, birds arguing, wind combing the reeds — and learning that recovery is mostly boredom with occasional agony for variety.

Sef had a radio. A big cabinet thing, walnut-veneer, older than she was, that she switched on each evening for the shipping forecast and left running afterward out of what I took for superstition. For weeks it gave us nothing but weather and wool prices. I lay there with my ribs strapped and let the world stay safely far away.

Then, on an evening in late thaw, the announcer's voice changed key.

I knew before the headline finished. You spend enough years reading official documents and you develop an ear for the sound a government makes when it's been caught — a certain flattened tone, a careful choosing of words, the acoustic signature of a man reading statements he did not write. *"The Ministry of Works confirmed today that construction on the Serran diversion project has been suspended pending review—"*

"Sef," I said. "Turn it up."

She turned it up. We listened to the whole bulletin, and then the follow-up, and then the next evening's, and the one after that, as the story broke open like a river in spring: the *Ledger*'s investigation, the leaked memorandum, the phrase *acceptable externality* repeated in a hundred outraged mouths, the inquiry announced, the minister's spokesman resigning, the word *suspended* hardening within a week into the better word, *cancelled*, and then the best word of all, spoken almost casually on the ninth evening as though it were nothing:

*"—and the three highland settlements previously listed for relocation will remain, according to a ministry statement, 'in perpetuity.'"*

In perpetuity. I lay on the pallet in the smokehouse with my cracked ribs and my stitched scalp and I laughed until Sef threatened to fetch the doctor, and the laugh hurt so much I couldn't tell where the pain ended and the joy began, and I decided they were probably the same thing wearing different coats.

Kestrel Ford. Lowmark. Ansel's Reach. My birthplace among them — the village I had mapped for drowning, every lot line and wellhead drawn in my own hand because they ordered me to and I obeyed, because obedience seemed survivable and refusal didn't. I had told Lior once, on the scree, that guilt was just a map you keep redrawing and never finish. She'd said that was the stupidest thing a cartographer ever said, and that guilt was a river: you don't erase it, you learn which crossings hold your weight.

Well. Here was a crossing that held.

I thought about all of them. About Lior, wherever she was — and I knew where she was, or feared I did; the reports mentioned arrests on the riverbank, a guide in custody, hearings pending, and every mention landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. About Ilsa Brandt, who had traded her silence for a career and her career for the truth, and who was out there somewhere in the high country with her share of the evidence and her share of the grief. About Dov, sixteen years old, who had carried our future sewn into his jacket through checkpoints I still have nightmares about, and who was at this moment, presumably, being insufferable about it in the capital. About Maren, who fed me when I was a stranger raiding her stores and forgave me for the maps before I'd forgiven myself.

And I thought about the fire at the low bridge. The black lace of it on the wind. It had felt like the end of everything, lying face-down in the reeds with blood in my eyes. It had not been the end. It had been the middle. That's the thing nobody tells you about sacrifice: you don't get to see what it purchased. You strike the match in the dark and walk away wounded, and the harvest comes in fields you'll never stand in.

Except — vindication arrives like spring thaw, slow and then all at once, and sometimes, if you're lucky, it arrives close enough to touch.

Because on the eleventh evening, Sef came out to the smokehouse faster than usual, radio voice trailing behind her: hearings in the capital, testimony from a senior surveyor named Ilsa Brandt, the full falsification of route maps years ago admitted on the record, and — Sef squinted at the wireless as if it might be lying — *"the blacklist against the guide Lior Almeida has been formally struck down, with release expected within the week."*

I sat up too fast and my ribs filed a formal complaint.

Release expected within the week.

I lay back down and stared at the smokehouse beams and did the arithmetic I'm actually good at. Distance from Reedhollow to the capital: four days by road, three if the ferry was running. Time until release: seven days, give or take bureaucracy. Supplies required: whatever Sef would sell me and whatever my pride would let me beg.

"Sef," I said. "How much for the use of your boat to the ferry landing?"

"For you?" She looked at me with her two-fingered hand on her hip. "Nothing. But you'll take a crate of smoked eel to my cousin in Marshend, and you'll tell him Sef says the price of eel is criminal and he knows why."

"Done," I said.

"And boy." She paused in the doorway, silhouetted against the last marsh light. "Whoever's waiting at that gate. Don't be late. Things that wait too long start believing they shouldn't have waited at all."

I started packing that night. Two packs — one mine, one for a mountain guide who had once anchored me above a four-hundred-foot drop without hesitation and then lectured me the whole way down about anchor placement, as though saving my life were merely an opportunity to improve my technique. And a river permit for the upper Serran, obtained through channels I will not describe in writing, for water that was legal to travel now, officially, in perpetuity, open all the way to the source.

Open. The word kept catching in me like a hook. The rivergate, open. The map, corrected. The villages, standing. And somewhere behind a detention facility wall, a woman who had given up everything on a riverbank — her freedom, her future, whatever it was she'd begun, God help me, to want — about to walk out a gate into a life with nothing left in it but the beginning.

Seven days. Three days' travel. Four to spare.

I wrote the plan on the back of an old survey plate, because some habits are load-bearing: route, timing, supplies, contingencies. Then I looked at it, laughed at myself, and threw it in the stove. Some journeys you don't map. Some you just walk, toward the gate, trusting the person on the other side to recognize you coming.

I hoped she would. I'd recognize her anywhere — that walk, like the ground owes her money and she means to collect.

Four days to the capital. Seven days until spring, in every sense I knew how to count.
