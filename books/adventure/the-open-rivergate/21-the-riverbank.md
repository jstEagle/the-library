## 21. The Riverbank

Detention facilities are built by people who have never been cold. I decided this on my first morning at Halden Cross, staring at a radiator that clanked like a mule with opinions and a window set so high in the wall you could see sky but not ground—as if the architects understood that what breaks a mountain woman is not walls but horizon.

Four months now. Or five. The days here don't stack; they slide off each other like wet stones. I measure time the only way that still makes sense: by the river I can't hear. There's a river somewhere below the facility—I've caught its smell on certain winds, cold water over granite—and I've decided it's the Serran headwater, though the guards say otherwise. Let them say otherwise. A woman who has survived a blacklist, a manhunt, a gorge crossing at night with forty souls and one lantern, can believe whatever she likes about a river.

They took my name when they brought me in. Not Almeida—the false name I'd guided under for six years—but the real one, the one on the warrant. "Lior Almeida," the intake officer read, and looked up at me with the particular disappointment of a man who'd expected someone taller. "You're shorter than your file."

"Mountains flatten people," I said. "It's the altitude."

He didn't laugh. Nobody here laughs. That's the thing about detention: not the walls, not the rationed light, not even the boredom—it's the total absence of anyone finding anything funny. Humor requires an audience, and audiences require freedom of assembly.

So I make do. I run the corridors at dawn before the others wake—forty paces, turn, forty paces back—and in my head I climb. I've done the Serran traverse eleven times from memory, every switchback, every scree field where Dov broke his arm and Elias nearly went into the void and I learned exactly how much weight a piton will hold when you're praying with your whole body. Some nights I dream the gorge. Lantern light on black water, Maren's hand gripping mine, old Ansel's Reach women singing to keep the children calm while the canyon narrowed and the first seepage of the new dam came whispering through the rock like something learning to breathe. In the dreams I always get them through. I did get them through. That's the part I have to keep telling myself, because memory is a traitor in a place like this—it keeps trying to charge me interest on the cost.

The hearings happen in a room I'm sometimes trucked to, sometimes not. They sit me behind glass like a specimen and ask questions in the flat bureaucratic grammar of people building a case out of other people's lives. Did you enter the Rivergate basin knowingly? Did you distribute classified geological materials? Did you, on the night of October the ninth, lead approximately forty-one civilians through a restricted military corridor?

"Yes," I said to that last one. "Forty-one. The baby doesn't count as a civilian yet, apparently, but she crossed the gorge too."

The stenographer didn't look up. Somewhere in the transcript there is now a line reading *the accused smiled*, which I hope some clerk finds years from now and wonders about.

* * *

Ilsa comes to testify in the second month, and I watch her on the facility's grainy monitor because they won't let me into the chamber itself. She looks older. Not frail—Brandt was never frail, she was carved rather than born—but weathered in a way that six months ago would have made me furious and now just makes me sad. She wears her survey coat, the grey one with the agency crest half unpicked. Someone started removing the insignia and then stopped. I understand why. You can't unpick twenty years in an afternoon.

She tells them everything. I know because the guard assigned to my block, a young woman named Petra who pretends not to be on my side and fails completely, recaps it to me each evening in the supply closet where we're both technically forbidden to be.

"She named names," Petra says, hushed, delighted, passing me a smuggled orange. "Deputy Director Corvane. The route maps, the falsified approvals—all of it. The 411 expedition. Your client."

"Halev," I say. The name tastes like river silt. Tomas Halev, thirty-one years old, fell forty meters on a pitch that should never have been on our route, on a map Ilsa signed and I trusted because she was Ilsa Brandt and her signature was supposed to mean something. They blamed me. I was the guide; guides are where blame pools, like water in a low place. And Ilsa stood in the inquiry room with her hands folded and said she had no reason to doubt the field verification, and I watched my life close over my head like ice.

"She said—" Petra hesitates, checking her memory against something she heard secondhand, "she said, 'I knew the map was wrong. I approved it anyway. Everything that happened to Lior Almeida afterward happened because I chose my career over her life.' Direct quote. The room went very quiet."

I peel the orange slowly. Outside the supply closet, someone's radio murmurs the evening bulletin: dam project suspended pending review, three villages reprieved, a cartographer's evidence under parliamentary investigation. Elias's burned copy bought them nothing and everything; Dov's jacket-sewn duplicate did the work, along with Ilsa's cache. Three copies, three carriers—that was the plan, and the plan held the way plans rarely do, because we built it assuming we'd all fail.

"You're crying," Petra says, alarmed.

"I'm peeling an onion."

"There's no onion."

"Then I'm crying." I hand her the peel. "Go on. Tell me the rest tomorrow."

* * *

The blacklist falls in the third month, on a Tuesday, which feels right; bureaucracies strike their blows and lift their blows on Tuesdays, never on days that mean anything. Petra brings the notice to my cell printed on actual paper, which in this place is a ceremony. *Record expunged. Certification reinstated. All charges relating to the Rivergate incident referred to the parliamentary commission.* Below it, a line about compensation that made me laugh out loud—they offered me money for six years of my life, calculated at some daily rate, as if grief were billed hourly.

I pressed the paper flat against the wall above my bunk and read it until the words stopped being words. Expunged. Such a clinical word for a resurrection.

That night I dreamed of Halev for the first time since the gorge. He stood on the pitch below the false anchor point, whole and unhurt, and he didn't accuse me of anything. He just pointed up-mountain, the way clients do when they want you to keep moving, and I woke with my hands aching from grips that weren't there.

* * *

The waiting after that is its own country. Hearings continue; the commission wants me in person; the military wants me silent; Voss, I hear through Petra's cousin who works records, has filed a report so carefully misfiled that it may never surface, and has requested reassignment to a coastal posting where, presumably, no rivers will ask him hard questions. I think about him more than I expected to. A believer doing arithmetic with lives—he was that, exactly, and on the riverbank in the fog he'd finally gotten a sum he couldn't balance, and instead of forcing it he'd let the column pass and written the wrong answer down himself. I owe him. I hate owing him. Both things are true, and I've stopped trying to reconcile them.

Then, on a morning in the fifth month, Petra opens my cell door and says, "Today's the day," in the voice people use for weather and executions, and I understand that the machinery has finished grinding.

Release is anticlimactic by design. They walk me through corridors, take fingerprints again for reasons of symmetry, return a plastic bag containing everything I owned when I surrendered on the riverbank: one knife, one compass with a cracked bezel, a waterproof match case, and the lantern—Maren's lantern, dented, its glass smoked black on one side from the gorge. I'd forgotten they kept it. Holding it, I could smell that night again: pine smoke, wet wool, fear sweetened with song.

The gate is steel and painted government green, and beyond it is a gravel lot and a fence and, past the fence, the road down toward the valley town where the hearings were held.

Elias Renner is leaning against the fence.

Not pacing, not hovering—leaning, arms crossed, wearing a coat two sizes too big for him and an expression I recognize from a hundred trailheads: the studied casualness of a man pretending he hasn't been waiting long. His left arm hangs a little stiff. The marsh infection cost him some range of motion; he told me once, through the visiting glass, that he can't fully straighten his fingers anymore, and that he's decided to consider it a mapmaker's penance.

Two packs sit at his feet. One is mine—I know it instantly, the old alpine frame with the strap I stitched with fishing line the winter Maren sheltered me—and beside it, held loose in his good hand, a document folder gone soft at the corners from handling.

He sees me come through the gate and pushes off the fence, and for a moment neither of us moves. Four months of letters, censored into blandness, and a year before that of shared tents and shared secrets and a night in the high camp of the vanished when he told me about his birthplace and I told him about Halev, and none of it prepared either of us for gravel and daylight and the terrible simplicity of standing ten feet apart.

"You're shorter than your file," he says.

"So I'm told."

He holds up the folder. "River permit. Upper Serran, source to confluence. First legal expedition." His voice does something complicated on *legal*. "They're calling it the Rivergate Reserve now. Protected status. There's a plaque being ordered."

"A plaque."

"Granite, probably. Bureaucrats love granite; it makes them feel geological." He stops. The wry armor drops, and underneath it his face is just a face, tired and hopeful and afraid of the answer to the question he hasn't asked. "Ilsa's ashes went up last week. Dov carried them. He says the lake steams even in winter, and the river runs open all the way to the sea now. Open water, Lior. Through the whole gorge."

I look at the packs. Two of them. Deliberate, paired, unmistakable. He didn't bring one pack and an offer. He brought two packs and an assumption—which is either arrogance or faith, and with Elias it was always impossible to tell the difference, and lately I've stopped wanting to.

"The hearings are done?" I ask.

"Done. Testimony sealed. You're a free woman with a restored certification and a compensation check you should spend on better boots." He glances down at my issued shoes, and something in his expression cracks open—not the wry thing, the real thing. "You chose the riverbank," he says quietly. "You gave them yourself and Ilsa so forty-one people could cross. I've had four months to argue with that decision and I've lost every argument. So." He shoulders my pack and holds it out, straps forward, the way you offer a load to a climber you trust with your rope. "Come claim the rest of your life. The river's running open, and I've got a permit that says nobody can stop us from seeing where it starts."

Behind me the green gate clangs shut—a sound I've imagined for months as a tomb-lid and hear now, absurdly, as a starting pistol.

I take the pack. It weighs almost nothing and everything.

"Upper Serran," I say, testing it. "Source to sea."

"Source to sea." He smiles, and it's the smile from the tent in the high camp, from the fog on the riverbank, from every narrow place we ever passed through together. "Though I warn you—the tracker they hired to read your trail wrote a monograph about your route-finding. We may have a following."

"Let them follow," I say, and turn down the road, toward the valley, toward the smell of cold water over granite that I choose, today, to believe is the river. "It's an open rivergate now. Everyone's invited."

Above the tree line, faint as breath, the mountains wait—and somewhere past them, steaming in the morning cold, the fissure lake feeds the current that carries every secret, at last, to light.
