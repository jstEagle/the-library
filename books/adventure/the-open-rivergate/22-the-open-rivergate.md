## 22. The Open Rivergate

The permit was a single sheet of paper, and it took eleven months of hearings to earn it.

I keep it in the map case now, laminated against rain, because I have learned what paper is worth in this country and I am not taking chances with the only document that says my name is mine again. *Lior Almeida, licensed mountain guide, authorized to lead parties above the high falls.* Eleven months of sitting in a detention facility canteen while men in good coats argued about whether I had committed treason or performed a public service, and the answer, when it finally came, was neither: they simply struck the blacklist down and pretended it had never existed, which is how this country apologizes. It doesn't. It revises.

Elias was waiting at the gate that morning with two packs on his shoulder and a river permit for the upper Serran folded into his breast pocket like a love letter. He'd gotten his hair cut. He'd gotten new boots. He looked like a man who had decided to be alive about it, and when he saw me he didn't say anything clever or anything tender — he just held out the second pack and said, "You're carrying your own rope. I'm not your porter."

"I'd have been disappointed otherwise," I said.

That was fourteen months ago. Since then we've run the low valleys together, the routes I once walked under a false name, and every hunter who books us gets a guide who tells them exactly where they are at all times, because I know now what it costs when maps lie. But this trip is different. This trip is the one I've been waiting for since the night I stood on a riverbank and gave myself up.

This is the first legal expedition to the source.

* * *

There are nine clients, which is four too many for my taste and exactly right for Elias's, because Elias has never met a group he couldn't organize into something resembling a functioning unit within a day. He does it with clipboards. He does it with gentle, relentless questions about water intake and boot fit. He does it by learning everyone's name before breakfast on the first morning and using them relentlessly, so that by the second day nobody wants to be the one who lets the group down.

The party is a strange congregation. There's Dov, of course — Maren insisted, and Maren's insistence is a geological force you plan around rather than resist. He's nineteen now, taller than me, with his mother's stubborn jaw and a surveyor's eye he didn't get from anyone in Kestrel Ford. He carries the reserve's new boundary markers in his pack like they're made of glass, though they're steel, and handles them like they're made of glass anyway. Then there are two hydrologists from the capital, a young couple from Lowmark who got married in the spring and wanted their honeymoon somewhere meaningful, an old schoolteacher from Ansel's Reach named Petra who walks like a woman settling a debt with the mountain, and three government observers whose presence Elias negotiated down from seven and whose expense reports I intend to make as expensive as possible out of pure spite.

One of the observers is Captain Voss.

He told us at the trailhead, standing there in civilian gear that fooled no one, holding a pack that had clearly never been worn. "The ministry thought someone should see it," he said. "With their own eyes."

"You could have read the report," Elias said. "You wrote half of it."

"The report is numbers." Voss shouldered his pack wrong, then let Dov fix it without being asked twice, which from Voss constitutes a small revolution. "I spent eight years doing arithmetic with other people's lives and calling it duty. I'd like to stand where the arithmetic ends."

I looked at him for a long moment. The last time we'd been face to face, he was accepting my surrender on a riverbank while forty villagers crossed behind me in the dark, and I hated him with a clean, efficient hatred that required no maintenance. Now he was a middle-aged man with grey coming in at his temples, asking to walk beside the people he'd been sent to erase, and the strange thing — the thing I still haven't fully made peace with — is that I found I didn't mind.

The wilderness forgives nothing. People, given time, forgive almost anything. Ilsa taught me the first part. The river taught me the second.

"Stay in the middle of the line," I told him. "And if I say run, you run."

"Yes, ma'am," said the captain, and meant it.

* * *

The valley has changed, or I have changed, or both — the two possibilities being indistinguishable after enough seasons. The patrol posts are gone. The checkpoint at the low bridge is a fisherman's hut now, rented by a man who charges tourists too much for coffee and knows nothing and cares less. The diversion tunnels were sealed with concrete in the autumn, and the concrete is already growing its first skin of moss, because this country forgets fast when forgetting is convenient, and for once the convenience runs our way.

We make camp the first night below the scree field, and I sleep badly, the way I always do under that particular wall of broken stone. Elias notices. He always notices; it's the most annoying of his many virtues.

"It held," he says into the dark. "The slide. It's been two years. It's not waiting for you specifically."

"Everything on this mountain is waiting for me specifically. That's just physics with a grudge."

He laughs quietly, and across the fire Dov stirs and pretends he isn't listening, and fails, the way nineteen-year-olds fail at everything subtle. In the morning we cross the scree in fog, single file, my hands remembering each hold before my eyes find it, and nothing moves except a family of marmots who whistle us through like bored customs officials. On the far side Petra the schoolteacher stops, looks back at the field, and says, "My father crossed here in 'sixty-one with a mule and a bad attitude. He'd have laughed at our ropes."

"He'd have died on our ropes," I say. "Respectfully."

She considers this and nods, satisfied, and I understand her completely. Some people come back to these places to prove the mountain can't still hurt them. The honest ones come back knowing it can.

* * *

On the third day we reach the high camp, and I stop twenty meters out the way you stop at a grave you helped dig.

The tents are gone — the reserve authority cleared them last summer, catalogued everything, carried it all down on muleback with the solemnity of archaeologists. But the flat shelf of ground is still there, still level, still holding the faint rectangles where five sets of gear once lay arranged neatly, owners stepped out mid-meal, mountain keeping its own counsel. The cold stove ring. The stone they used as a table. Ilsa's tent spot, nearest the drop, because she never trusted herself to sleep far from an exit.

Voss stands at the edge of the shelf and takes off his hat, which surprises everyone including, I think, himself.

"One of my men died here," he says. Not an apology. A fact, laid down like a stone on a cairn. "Corporal Adeyemi. He was twenty-four. He put himself between the fog and the others because those were his orders and he believed orders meant something. I've read his file a hundred times looking for a reason it was necessary, and there isn't one. There never was one."

No one speaks. The hydrologists look at their boots. The honeymooners hold hands. And I think about how Voss stood on that same shelf two years ago with a rifle squad and a mandate, and how the fissure lake changed him before the paperwork did, and how change in men like him comes late and costs more than it would have early, but comes nonetheless.

"We'll camp here tonight," I say. "It's a good shelf. Flat, sheltered, defensible." I pause. "It was always a good camp. That was never the problem."

* * *

The source announces itself before you see it — a smell of warm stone and sulfur, a mist that beads on your eyelashes, a sound like the river breathing. Then the trees open and there it is: the fissure lake, steaming in the morning cold, a long tear in the earth's skin filled with water so blue it looks invented, fed from below by heat no map ever admitted to, overflowing into the stream that becomes the Serran that becomes the rivergate that becomes, eventually, the sea.

The legal expedition stands at its edge and does what legal expeditions do: measures things. The hydrologists unpack instruments and argue happily about flow rates. Dov drives the first boundary marker into the granite with a hand sledge, three swings, precise, and paints the reserve stripe on it in reserve blue. The observers take photographs for files that will, this time, tell the truth.

I walk the shoreline alone.

Ilsa died in February. Free, cleared, and furious about dying, which was the most Ilsa way possible to go. She'd testified for six days at the hearings — six days of flattening the ministry's lawyers with the patient contempt of a woman who had been right for twenty years and been paid in silence for it — and she'd walked out of the chamber on the last day, looked at the winter sky over the capital, and said to me, "Well. That's that. Now I can finally retire somewhere with worse weather." She lasted nine weeks. Her heart, which had carried her through blizzards, betrayals, and bureaucracy, chose the mildest February in recorded history to quit without notice.

She left me her ashes and a note that said, in full: *Take me up. Don't make a speech. You're terrible at speeches.*

So I don't make one. I open the tin at the lake's edge, where the steam rises off the water and the ground is warm underfoot even in the wind, and I tip it slowly into the air above the fissure. The ash catches the light, hangs, and goes down to the water, and the water takes it the way the river takes everything — without judgment, without ceremony, downstream toward the sea, carrying every secret to light.

"For the record," I say quietly, because I'm terrible at speeches but not at records, "you were right about the maps. You were wrong about me. We're even."

Behind me, footsteps in the gravel. Elias, giving me the distance of ten meters and no more, the exact distance a person needs and won't ask for. He doesn't say anything. He just stands there while the steam drifts between us, until I'm done, and then he says, "The hydrologists found something. They want you to see it."

"If it's another flow rate, tell them I care deeply."

"It's not a flow rate."

* * *

What they've found is a spring — a small one, seeping out of the rock fifty meters downstream of the lake, running clear and cold over a lip of stone into a natural basin the size of a bathtub. Except the basin isn't empty. The bottom is paved with coins.

Old ones. Green with age, fused into clusters, centuries of travelers throwing wishes into water that was already sacred to somebody before any of us drew a breath. The villagers' grandparents knew this spring. Their grandparents' grandparents knew it. It appears in none of the state surveys, none of the maps, none of the archives — because the maps were drawn by people measuring what could be taken, and a wishing spring has no yield.

Petra kneels at the edge and names the coin dates aloud like a litany. Dov photographs everything. One of the government observers — the youngest, a woman who has said perhaps forty words in three days — asks softly, "Should this be in the reserve report?"

"It should be in the reserve report," I say, "and nowhere else. Some things get mapped so they can be protected. This one gets protected by staying unmapped. Write it down as 'minor seep, no significance,' and then come back someday on your own time and throw a coin in like everyone else."

She looks at me, understands, and writes *minor seep* in her notebook with a perfectly straight face, and I think: there. That's the whole country in miniature. What survives is whatever people agree, quietly, together, to leave alone.

* * *

We camp that last night at the source, and Voss builds the fire, badly, and Dov fixes it, patiently, and the two of them end up talking until midnight in voices too low for me to follow — the soldier and the boy whose home he was sent to drown, finding whatever it is people find when they finally sit on the same side of a fire. I don't eavesdrop. Some reconciliations need privacy the way wounds need air.

Elias and I walk down to the outflow stream before bed. The moon is up and the water is loud and silver, pouring out of the fissure country toward the gorge, toward Kestrel Ford, toward the low bridge and the marshes and the sea.

"Do you ever think about the night we crossed?" he asks. "Forty people, lanterns, the water already rising?"

"Daily. Usually around four in the morning, which is when all my accounting happens."

"What's the balance?"

I consider it honestly, because he's earned honesty and I've learned to give it. "Maren's kitchen smells. Dov driving markers into stone that's going to stay stone. Petra walking her father's route. A captain doing penance with a badly built fire. You, with your clipboards." I nudge his shoulder with mine. "The cost was real. It doesn't cancel. But the ledger has more entries than it used to, and most of them are good ones."

"And you?" he says. "Where are you in the ledger?"

"Right here," I say. "Legally. Above the high falls. With my own name on the permit." I turn to look at him, moonlit, tired, happier than I have any procedural right to be. "Turns out survival was for something after all."

He takes my hand, and upstream the fissure lake steams gently against the stars, and the river runs open — no dam, no checkpoints, no lies in the margins — all the way to the sea, carrying everything with it: ash and coins and secrets and names, every one of them finally, permanently, in the light.

In the morning we break camp and start down. Nine days back to the trailhead, and Maren will be waiting at Kestrel Ford with a meal big enough for twelve, because Maren plans for two extra guests the way other people plan for weather. The clients will complain about their knees. Dov will finish the boundary line. Voss will write a report that tells the truth, which will be the strangest document the ministry has filed in a decade.

And somewhere below the falls, the river will keep moving, the way it always has, the way it always will — patient, unimpressed, and free.
