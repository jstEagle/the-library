## 1. Salt Flat Morning

The alkali cracked under Coral Joubert's boots like old crockery, and the sound carried farther than it should have in the flat white morning. She had learned to read that sound the way other people read weather — the thin plates of salt ringing hollow meant the crust was dry all the way down, which meant no rain in a week, which meant the man she was following had not stopped to water his horse anywhere on this side of the flats.

She crouched and touched the print again. Shod front hoof, off-side shoe thrown or pulled, so the near-side prints bit deeper and the off-side left only a faint crescent, like a thumb pressed into dough. The horse favored its right foreleg. It would be lame by noon if the rider pushed it, and men who stole horses always pushed them, because a stolen horse was a candle burning at both ends and every thief knew it.

Coral stood, worked her shoulders once against the weight of the rifle slung across her back, and looked out at the world.

The Salt Wells Flats ran white to every horizon, blinding under a sun that hadn't yet cleared the eastern mesa but already felt like a hand laid flat against her face. To the south, heat shimmer had begun to lift the distant mountains off the earth so they floated, blue and insubstantial, like islands in a sky-colored sea. There was nothing else. No tree, no rock taller than her knee, no bird. Just the crust, the light, and a line of hoofprints stitching away toward the shimmer like thread through cloth.

She had been following that thread since before dawn.

* * *

His name was Dade Pruitt, and he was not worth this. She had known that from the first hour, standing in the livery yard at Cane Springs with the owner's complaint folded in her pocket — a bay gelding, eight years old, gentle as a churchgoer, taken in the night along with a sack of grain and a pair of chaps that weren't even good chaps. A petty theft. The kind of thing a ranger handled with an afternoon and a form.

But the tracks had been clean, and the morning had been cool, and tracking was the one thing she still did without thinking about anything else, so she had followed them. That was how it always went now. Not duty. Not justice. Just the simple animal relief of having a line to walk and nothing to think about but the next print.

Three years since Tomas. Three years of lines.

She walked. The sun climbed. The salt crunched. Somewhere behind her, forty miles back, there was a cabin she paid rent on and never slept in, and ahead of her there was a man who thought he was getting away, and between those two facts her whole life fit with room to spare.

By midmorning the prints changed. The lame leg worsened — she could see it in the way the deep-side prints dragged a little tail of salt behind them now, the toe scraping on the lift-off. The horse was hurting. She found a place where Pruitt had dismounted, or tried to; the boot scuffs were shallow, hurried, and beside them a dark spot on the crust where the gelding had stood too long, head down. She knelt over the spot and smelled it. Salty, mineral, faintly sweet underneath.

Urine with glucose in it. The horse was sick as well as lame, probably foundering, and Pruitt was riding him into ruin because stopping wasn't in his nature any more than stillness was in hers.

She straightened up and felt something move in her chest that might have been pity, if she'd let it finish forming. She didn't. Pity was a door and doors were for houses and she didn't live in one.

Instead she did what the code required, which was the same thing it always required: keep moving, want nothing, finish the work.

* * *

She caught him at eleven o'clock, in the shade of a rock no bigger than a dinner table, the only shade on the entire face of the earth.

He'd stopped because the horse had finally refused to go on. When she came walking out of the glare — she never rushed the last hundred yards; rushing told a man you were afraid of him — Pruitt was sitting on the salt with his back against the rock, hat over his eyes, and the bay gelding stood beside him with its head hanging low and its breath coming in short, careful pulls.

"Pruitt," she said.

The hat came off fast. He was younger than she'd expected — twenty-four, maybe, with a sunburned neck and the kind of mustache a boy grows when he's copying his father. His hand went toward the pistol on his thigh, considered the matter, and thought better of it. Good. Men who thought better of it lived longer.

"Ranger." He said it like a curse and a surrender at once.

"You took a horse from the Cane Springs livery."

"I borrowed it."

"It'll be found where you left it, then."

He looked at the gelding. The gelding looked at nothing. Somewhere out on the flats the heat made a low sound like the earth itself breathing, and Pruitt's shoulders came down half an inch, and Coral understood that whatever story he'd been telling himself about being free was finished.

"There's water in my canteen," she said. "Give it to the horse, not yourself. Walk him slow when he can stand. I'll ride behind you to Cane Springs and we'll settle this with the owner and the magistrate, and you'll do a season of work instead of a stretch of jail, if the owner's agreeable. He usually is, for horses come back alive."

Pruitt stared at her. "That's it?"

"That's it."

"Why?"

Because the code said so, she almost said. Because a ranger doesn't punish; a ranger restores. Because wanting him to suffer would mean wanting something, and she had spent three years learning not to want things, because wanting things was how you ended up sitting in a dry creek bed outside Bitter Fork holding your brother's ledger and understanding that the world did not care what you wanted.

What she said was: "Because the horse didn't do anything wrong."

* * *

They made eight miles that afternoon, walking side by side, leading the gelding, who improved by degrees with water and rest the way horses do and men mostly don't. Pruitt talked. They always talked. She had learned that silence frightened people worse than a drawn gun, and that if she simply declined to fill the quiet, they filled it themselves, with everything.

He'd lost a claim, he said. Or never had one. Worked a season at the Consolidated smelter until the fumes got into his chest, then drifted. The horse had been standing there saddled and the gate had been open and something in him had just — gone. He used the word *gone* the way other men used *God*, with a helpless shrug at the end of it, as if to say, who can argue with the weather?

Coral listened the way she listened to wind: attentively, without hearing.

But somewhere past the fifth mile, one sentence snagged on her like a cocklebur.

"They say down in Dry Wells there's a broker buying up water rights all along the southern range," Pruitt said, wiping his mouth with the back of his hand. "Fellow named Calloway. Pays cash money, real cash, and folks are signing because a dry year's coming and cash is cash. My uncle signed over our spring last spring. Now we buy our own water back by the barrel." He laughed, short and ugly. "Man sells you your own river one cup at a time. That's not thieving. That's business."

The name went into her like a splinter going into a palm — small entry, deep travel.

*Renn Calloway.*

She kept her face doing nothing. That was the trick of it, the whole discipline of it: the face does nothing, the voice does nothing, the hands do nothing, and inside, where nobody can see, you put the name in the strongbox with the other names and turn the key and keep walking.

"How long has this Calloway been buying?" she asked, mild as milk.

"Year, two. Maybe more. He's got partners, they say. Big ones. Fellows with badges of their own, the kind you pin on, not the kind you wear on a shirt." Pruitt glanced at her sidelong. "You know him?"

"No," Coral said, and the lie came out smooth and level, and she hated how smooth it was, and kept walking.

* * *

They reached Cane Springs after dark. The liveryman, a wide soft-spoken man named Odell, looked at his gelding, looked at Pruitt, looked at Coral, and agreed to the season of work with the weary air of a man who had watched this particular play before and knew all his lines. Coral wrote it up in her book by lamplight in Odell's kitchen — date, offense, disposition — while a kettle muttered on the stove and a dog sighed in its sleep under the table.

It should have ended there. That was the design of it. Catch the man, close the book, ride on. Keep moving, want nothing. The code had held her together for three years the way a splint holds a broken bone, and you don't question a splint. You just don't lean on it.

She should have ridden north at dawn, or west, or anywhere, because there was always another complaint, another set of prints, another line to walk.

Instead she sat on Odell's porch after breakfast the next morning with her coffee going cold in her hands, looking south toward Dry Wells, thirty miles of greasewood and sand, and told herself she was only resting the horse.

The gelding needed a day. That was true. Its hooves were hot and its pulse at the fetlock still ran quick, and a good ranger rested a horse that had been ridden sick across alkali. Any competent person would rest it. Resting it was not wanting anything. Resting it was just husbandry.

So she stayed one day.

On the second day, the farrier at Cane Springs reset the gelding's shoes, and that took time, and time was a thing she could always find a use for. She cleaned her rifle. She mended the tear in her saddle skirt. She helped Odell's wife haul water from the spring and said perhaps forty words total, and Odell's wife thanked her twice for the silence, which people often did, mistaking it for courtesy rather than recognizing it as armor.

On the third day she had no reason left at all, and she stayed anyway, and sat on the porch with the cold coffee, and admitted it to herself the way you admit a wound is infected: quickly, without looking directly at it.

She wanted to go to Dry Wells. She wanted to stand in the street outside the land office and look at the man whose signature sat at the bottom of her brother's ledger like a fly in amber. She wanted it the way a thirsty woman wants water, which is to say her body wanted it whether her principles permitted it or not.

* * *

Tomas had been dead three years, and the ledger had outlived him the way paper outlives people, patient and indifferent.

He'd kept it in a canvas-backed notebook, his figures in his neat schoolteacher's hand — their father had sent both children to school in El Paso for four years, and it showed in everything Tomas wrote and nothing Coral wrote. Column after column of the springs and seeps and tanks on their section of the southern range: flow rates measured with a tin cup and a watch, dates, rainfall, notes on which pools went alkaline in drought and which held sweet. Eleven years of his life in pencil. Their water. Their inheritance, such as it was — a dry country ranch worth nothing but its water, and the water enough, barely, for cattle and a life.

And then Renn Calloway had come through with a leather portfolio and a smile like a well-oiled hinge, and there had been papers, and signatures — Tomas's signature, or something shaped like it — and when the dust settled, the Joubert water belonged to the Vermilion Land and Cattle Syndicate, and Tomas had ridden into the high country alone in the worst month of the year to try to reach the territorial recorder before the filing closed, and the high country in that month was a country of storms, and the storm had taken him, and the ledger had come home without him.

A trapper found the body in the spring thaw. Found the notebook too, wrapped in oilcloth inside Tomas's coat, because Tomas had known, at the end, what mattered. Not his rifle — the rifle was gone, traded or lost, maybe spent on a final desperate meal. Not his coat, his bedroll, his hat. The ledger. He had died protecting a book of numbers, because the numbers were the proof, and proof was the only weapon that had ever been any use against men like Calloway.

Coral had read it a hundred times. She had traced the forged signature with her fingertip until she knew its lies by heart — the loop of the *R* too round, the crossbar of the *t* placed by a right-handed man imitating a left-handed one, the ink too fresh for the date it claimed. She had taken it to the county clerk, who shrugged. To the deputy recorder, who studied it and grew pale and asked her please to take it elsewhere. To a lawyer in Yuma who wanted five hundred dollars in advance and offered, in exchange, a decade of depositions.

Nobody wanted the truth. The truth was expensive and the syndicate was rich, and Coral had learned what every grieving person eventually learns: that the world's machinery for justice runs on money and patience, and she had neither, and grief is not legal tender.

So she had become what she was instead. A ranger. A woman with a badge and a rifle and a rule — keep moving, want nothing — and a private war she fought one petty thief at a time, because the big thief was beyond her reach, and a person has to aim at something.

* * *

On the fourth morning, Odell found her in the yard checking the gelding's hooves, though the hooves had been sound for two days.

"He'll carry you fine now," Odell said. "Been ready since Tuesday."

"I know it."

Odell was a patient man; he kept slow animals and slower opinions. But he looked at her a moment longer than politeness required, the way people sometimes did when they'd decided she was a riddle worth one guess.

"You're headed south," he said. Not a question.

"Patrol route goes through Dry Wells."

"Does it." He nodded slowly, working a straw between his teeth. "Fair warning, then, since you've been decent to my boy here. There's men in Dry Wells lately who aren't from anywhere. Come in on the stage, four of them, city hats, city boots. They sit in the land office from opening to closing and they don't drink at the saloon and they don't talk to anybody. And yesterday the surveyor's office put up a notice — federal team heading into the Vermilion Range to run lines on the aquifer. High country. Early for it, too, with snow coming."

Coral kept her hands busy with the hoof pick. "Vermilion Range," she said.

"Tall country," Odell said. "Bad country, this time of year. My grandfather prospected up there. Said a man could freeze in July if the cloud came down low enough." He spat, thoughtful. "Seems like a strange season for a government to be curious about water."

Everything about it was strange, and Coral turned each strangeness over as she saddled up: a survey team in the high country weeks before the passes closed, and syndicate men sitting silent in the land office like spiders in a corner, and Calloway's name moving through the range towns on the tongues of drifters, buying water that wasn't his to buy.

Strange, and none of it hers. Her warrant covered stolen horses, not stolen aquifers. Her jurisdiction ended where the corruption began, which was to say it ended everywhere that mattered.

She swung up into the saddle. The gelding stood quiet under her, forgiving her the alkali crossing the way horses forgive everything, which was one of the reasons she trusted them more than people.

"Which way you riding?" Odell asked. "North road's faster to Dry Wells."

"South," Coral said. "I'll cut across the flats and come up the long way."

There was no reason for the long way. The long way added a day. But the long way passed the mouth of Alkali Wash, and Alkali Wash ran up under the shoulder of Redstone Mesa, and under Redstone Mesa was the spring her father had found, and her mother had buried her hairpin beside, and where Tomas had carved their initials into the cottonwood the summer before everything went wrong — *T.J.* and beneath it, smaller, shakier, *C.J.*, because he'd made her do it, laughing, saying a tree was better than a Bible for keeping names.

She hadn't been back in three years. She had arranged her entire life around not going back.

The gelding turned south onto the white crust, and the salt rang under his shoes like breaking dishes, and Coral Joubert rode toward the one place in the territory she had sworn never to see again, telling herself it was a patrol route, telling herself it was nothing, telling herself the code still held — keep moving, want nothing — while the tall blue shape of the Vermilion Range stood up on the horizon ahead of her like a wall between her and the rest of her life.
