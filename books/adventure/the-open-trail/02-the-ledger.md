## 2. The Ledger

The lamp at the Dry Wells boarding house burned low and yellow, the way lamps did when the wick needed trimming and nobody had trimmed it, and Coral Joubert sat beneath it with her brother's ledger open on her knees for what she knew without counting was past the hundredth time.

Outside, the town had gone to its bones. Dry Wells after dark was a sound more than a place — wind working the loose tin on the assay office roof, a dog somewhere near the livery making its slow complaint against nothing, the creak of the water tower's legs as it settled into the cold. The well itself, the one that gave the town its name, had gone dry eleven years back, and the town had stayed anyway, the way people did, out of habit or stubbornness or because leaving cost more than staying. Coral understood that arithmetic better than most.

She turned a page.

The ledger was Tomas's own hand, every line of it, the figures set down in his careful upright script with the little flourishes he never could quite kill — a habit from the year their mother had tried to make a schoolmaster of him before the desert reclaimed him for its own. *Claim No. 4471, Alkali Basin, registered 14 March.* The date of the filing. The date of the survey fee paid in silver coin, receipt pinned to the page with a straight pin gone brown with rust. The date of the amendment he'd filed to extend his ditch rights up-canyon. And then, three pages later, the entry that was not his hand at all:

*Claim No. 4471 — VOID. Non-payment of assessment. Reverted to territorial land pool per Statute 88.*

Signed: R. Calloway, Land Broker, authorized agent.

Coral had looked at that signature so many times she could have drawn it blindfolded, could have carved it into a plank with her eyes shut. The *R* with its long tail sweeping under the rest of the name like something dragging. The *C* opened too wide, a mouth about to swallow. She had studied it the way other men studied the faces of men they meant to kill, and she supposed that was exactly what she'd been doing, though three years of doing it had produced nothing but mileage.

Tomas had been dead two weeks when she first saw the ledger. It had come back to her by way of the county clerk's office in Bitter Springs, forwarded by a deputy who didn't include a note because there wasn't anything to say. The ledger, a tin box of receipts, and a letter in an envelope that Coral still hadn't finished reading even now, three years on. She kept the letter in the bottom of her saddlebag wrapped in oilcloth, and once or twice a year she took it out, read the first lines, and put it away again. Someday, she told herself. Someday when she stopped moving long enough to sit with it.

She hadn't stopped yet.

The assessment Tomas had supposedly failed to pay — she'd found the proof of payment inside this very ledger, the receipt pinned right there, dated nine days before the void order went through. Somebody had taken his money and erased his claim in the same month, and the somebody whose name sat at the bottom of the order was Renn Calloway, land broker, authorized agent, a man who by all accounts wore good boots and a better smile and left behind him a trail of signatures instead of tracks.

Three years. Fourteen counties. Two territories. Coral had chased that signature through courthouse basements and land office files and one memorable week in a tent outside a recorder's house in Cane Wash, waiting for him to come home, except he never came home because Calloway, it turned out, had moved on six months before she got there, promoted upward into some syndicate's business, his trail going cold the way trails did when a man had money behind him and rails under him and no reason in the world to look over his shoulder at a woman on a tired horse.

She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and pressed her palm flat against the leather cover. The spine was cracked through; she'd had it rebound once in Yuma and the bookbinder there had asked if it was a family Bible. Close enough, she'd said.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the dog had quit complaining and the wind had picked up, coming down off the Vermilions to the north with that edge it carried in late autumn, the smell of stone and coming cold. Coral rose, trimmed the wick, and stood a moment at the window looking north, where the range made its black serration against the last of the twilight. Snow already up there, most likely. There always was by now, in the high draws, in the shadowed couloirs where the sun didn't reach from October to April. Men died up there every year. Usually fools. Usually alone.

She thought of Tomas not in the desert where he'd died but in the kitchen of the home place, seventeen years old, drawing contour lines in the dust on the table because paper cost money. He'd had a gift for water. That was the thing people forgot about him, the thing the story of his death had buried — that before he was a victim he was a genius, a boy who could read a hillside like a page and tell you where the seep would be, who had found the spring in Alkali Basin that half the basin drank from now without knowing whose eye had found it first. Claim No. 4471. He'd been twenty-six. He'd had plans drawn for a reservoir.

And Renn Calloway had signed a piece of paper and made him nothing, and the desert had done the rest, and Coral had been four hundred miles away learning to be a ranger while her brother walked out into the basin looking for water that his own maps said should be there, except the maps were lies by then, or the springs had shifted, or God alone knew, because Tomas had left no note and the ledger was the only voice he'd left behind.

*Keep moving,* she told herself, which was the whole of the ranger code as she practiced it. *Want nothing.* A moving target was hard to hit and harder to grieve over. She'd been moving for three years and it had worked, mostly, in the sense that a man can say a fire has worked right up until the roof falls in.

A knock at the street door, two raps, unhurried.

Coral took the ledger off her knees and slid it into the drawer of the washstand, under a folded shirt, and crossed the room. Through the window's edge she could see the shape of it: broad hat, marshal's coat, the lamplight from the porch catching the star on his chest. Marshal Amos Greer. She'd met him yesterday when she rode in, the usual ritual — she showed her commission, he squinted at it, they exchanged the courtesies two professionals exchange when neither is sure yet whether the other is worth the trouble.

She opened the door.

"Miss Joubert." Greer held his hat in one hand, which meant either respect or bad news, and with Greer she suspected the two arrived together. "Didn't mean to keep you up."

"You didn't. I wasn't sleeping."

"No," he said, in a tone that suggested he'd guessed as much. "Mind if I come in? It's getting sharp out here."

She stepped aside. He came in, glanced once — professionally, not rudely — at the room: the bed unslept-in, the washstand drawer not quite closed, the chair angled toward the lamp. He was sixty if he was a day, gray to the collar, with hands like old rope and eyes that had watched a lot of country go wrong. He lowered himself into the chair she offered and set his hat on his knee and was quiet for a moment, which she recognized as a man arranging his words in order of damage.

"Telegram came in on the evening wire," he said. "Addressed to the ranger service, care of any officer in district. You're the only one in district."

"Go on."

"Federal survey team. Six souls, out of the Denver office. Been running section lines and aquifer work in the high Vermilions since August." He rubbed his jaw. "They're due to file their final reports by Friday. Permits expire Friday. After Friday, everything they've surveyed reverts to open claim, and anybody with a stake and a shovel can jump it."

"And?"

"And they've missed their last two scheduled check-ins. The wire office sent up a query three days ago, no answer. Sent another yesterday." He looked at her directly. "No answer."

Coral felt it start in her chest, that slow cold turn, the feeling she knew from years of tracking — the moment when a trail stops being a job and becomes a debt. "Six people don't miss check-ins," she said. "Not federal crews. They're drilled on it. Missing a check-in costs a man his rating."

"That's what the telegram says too. In fancier words." Greer leaned forward, elbows on knees. "There's a relief party being organized out of Bitter Springs, but Bitter Springs is two days' ride from the high country and the party they're organizing is four store clerks and a mule skinner. The wire asked for the nearest qualified officer." He paused. "That's you."

"I'm not on assignment. I'm passing through."

"Ma'am, I read your file yesterday after you checked in. You spent two winters running the Vermilion backcountry. You know those passes better than any living soul, including the ones who live there." He said it plainly, without flattery, which she appreciated. "I'm asking. Formally, if I have to make it formal."

Coral stood at the window again, looking north. The range had gone fully black now, just a jagged absence of stars along the horizon, and somewhere above it, in some draw or adit or snow-filled couloir, six people were either dead or waiting, and she found — and this was the part that surprised her, that sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone — that she didn't much care which.

That was the honest truth of it, the truth she'd have admitted to no one: three years of hunting a signature had worn something out of her. She was competent. She was reliable. She would go, because the code said go and because she had never in her life refused a duty, and she would do the work well, because she had never in her life done work badly. But somewhere between the wanting and the doing, the caring had thinned out like a creek in August. She could find them. Whether finding them meant anything to her — whether six strangers alive or dead would weigh on her the way one brother's empty grave weighed — that she genuinely did not know.

"When would I leave?" she said.

"First light, if you'll go. Sooner if you won't wait for light."

"The passes will be icing. Another week and the high route closes till spring."

"Then we're inside the week," Greer said. "Barely."

She turned from the window. "I'll want supplies. Grain for the horse, eight days of hard rations, and whatever the wire office knows about where the team was last posted."

"Cook will grumble about the hour. Let him." Greer rose, settling his hat. At the door he stopped, and for a moment the professional mask slipped and she saw the man underneath, tired, an old man carrying a young territory's troubles. "Miss Joubert. The telegram said something else. I held it back because it's secondhand and might be nothing."

"Say it."

"The last check-in they did send, three weeks back — the team wired that they'd hired a local guide. Somebody who knew the high country." He worked his jaw. "Name on the wire was Calloway. Renn Calloway."

The room went very quiet. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside the wind took the tin on the assay office roof and worried it, and the dog started up again, and Coral stood absolutely still with her hand on the back of the chair, and the ledger seemed to burn through the washstand drawer like a coal through wet canvas.

"That's not possible," she heard herself say, and her voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice.

"It's the name as received. Wires garble things. Could be Calhoun, could be Callahan." Greer watched her face, and whatever he saw there made him go careful. "You know the name."

"I know the name."

He waited. She didn't give him anything, because there was nothing to give — no proof, no warrant, nothing but a signature she'd memorized and a hatred she'd fed for three years like feeding a fire in a place with no wood. If Renn Calloway was on that mountain, then the hunt and the mission had just become the same road, and every professional instinct she owned told her that was the worst thing that could happen, and every other part of her, the part below instinct, the part that had kept the ledger a hundred times and slept in the saddle rather than stop moving — that part lit up like a struck match.

Greer put his hat on. "First light," he said. "I'll have the cook up."

The door closed behind him. Coral stood a long time without moving. Then she went to the washstand, drew out the ledger, and opened it one more time to the page with the signature, and set her finger beneath it, under the long tail of the *R*, under the wide-open mouth of the *C*.

"If it's you," she said quietly, to the ink, to the man, to the mountain dark beyond the glass, "then this time I'm not reading your name. I'm reading it off your boot prints."

She packed by lamplight, and the wind came down off the Vermilions all night, and she did not sleep, and in the morning there was frost on the inside of the window, and the high country to the north stood white to its waist, waiting.
