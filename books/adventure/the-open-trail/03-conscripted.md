## 3. Conscripted

The telegram had said *come at once*, and so Coral came — but she came the way water comes to a dry wash: slowly, grudgingly, taking the long way around every obstacle. She spent a night in the saddle outside Dry Wells rather than sleep in a bed, and she ate cold beans from the can rather than pay for a hot meal, and when morning finally made it impossible to delay any longer, she tied her horse in front of the territorial marshal's office and stood looking at the door for a long time before she went through it.

The office smelled of paper and lamp oil and old tobacco. Maps covered one wall — the whole territory rendered in faded ink, county lines drawn by men who had never seen the country they were dividing. Behind a desk scarred by boot heels and coffee rings sat Marshal Amos Teague, a broad, gray man with a mustache that had gone the color of dust and eyes that had not missed much in forty years of looking.

"Coral Joubert." He said it like he was confirming a delivery. "Sit down."

"I'd rather stand."

"Suit yourself." He pushed a telegram across the desk toward her, though she had already read its twin. "You got my wire."

"I got it. I'm here. Now tell me what you want, because I've got a horse that needs shoeing and a range that needs riding."

"What I want," Teague said, "is six federal surveyors back alive, and I want them back before their permits lapse and before the snow closes the Vermilion high country until June. What I have is a territory full of deputies who can't find their own hats in that country, and one woman who knows it better than anybody breathing."

Coral looked at the map on his wall. The Vermilion Range sat in the upper corner like a bruise — hatched lines for the peaks, blank white where nobody had bothered to survey the blankness. She knew that white space the way she knew the inside of her own hatband. Three summers she had ranged it. Two winters she had wintered in its shadow. She knew which draws held water in August and which passes drifted shut in October and which ridgelines lied to a man's eye about distance.

That was exactly why she did not want to go.

"Send somebody else."

"There isn't anybody else." Teague leaned back, chair creaking. "I sent two riders up last week. They turned back at the timberline with frostbitten ears and a story about weather coming in wrong. The army won't move until Washington says move, and Washington is three weeks and a railroad away. Those surveyors have families waiting on telegrams that don't come. You're the only person in this territory who can get up there fast enough to matter."

"Then they'll be dead by the time anyone matters," Coral said flatly. "That's arithmetic, Marshal, not cruelty. High country in late season eats people. It doesn't care if they're surveyors or saints."

"It cares if somebody knows what they're doing."

"And I know what I'm doing," she said. "That's my point. I know exactly what I'd be riding into, and I'm telling you it's a fool's errand with a flag on it."

Teague studied her for a long moment. Outside, Dry Wells went about its small business — a wagon rattling past, someone calling to a dog, the tin sign over the assay office ticking in the wind. He let the silence sit until it got uncomfortable, and Coral understood that this too was a tool, same as her standing was a tool, and that he was better with it than she was.

"You tracked a horse thief across the Salt Flats three weeks ago," he said. "Took you eleven hours to run him down over ground that eats tracks for breakfast. The judge who fined him told me you brought the man in walking, unmarked, with his horse calm as a churchgoer. That's not luck. That's craft."

"The flats are flat," Coral said. "It's in the name."

"Don't do that." His voice didn't rise. That was worse. "Don't play dumb with me, Miss Joubert. I've read your file. I know about your brother."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere behind the walls a clock ticked, patient as erosion.

"Tomas Joubert," Teague went on, gently now, which she hated more than if he'd shouted. "Water claim on the Alkali Fork. Erased out of the county ledger by a broker named Renn Calloway. Your brother rode into the desert to contest it and didn't ride out. You've spent three years—"

"You don't know what I've spent three years doing."

"I know you haven't stayed anywhere longer than a month since. I know you took the ranger commission because it comes with a reason to keep moving. And I know—" he tapped the desk once, "—that a woman running that hard from one thing will run just as hard toward another, if you point her right."

Coral felt something go cold and still in her chest, the way a spring goes still before it freezes over. "You think grief makes me good at my job."

"I think it makes you relentless," Teague said. "And right now relentless is the only supply those people have."

She turned away from him, toward the map, because looking at his face was costing her something she couldn't name. The Vermilion Range. Six strangers somewhere in all that white. A storm front building somewhere over the Great Basin that she could already feel in the ache of an old break in her wrist.

"I'll tell you what I know about missing parties in high country," she said, still facing the map. "After four days, you stop looking for survivors and start looking for remains. After seven, you stop looking at all. Your telegram came nine days after those surveyors were last heard from. Do your own arithmetic, Marshal."

"Our arithmetic says the storm hasn't hit yet. Our arithmetic says there's caves, mine adits, old prospect cabins up there — places a smart party could hole up. Our arithmetic says nobody's found so much as a glove, which means nobody's been found dead either." He paused. "And our arithmetic says the permits expire Friday. If those men are alive and we don't reach them, the land they surveyed reverts open, and there are men in this territory who've been waiting years for exactly that."

That pulled her around. "What kind of men?"

Teague's face did something complicated. "The kind I can't prove anything about yet. The kind who buy water rights cheap and sell them dear. The kind who'd just as soon a survey team wandered off into the snow." He shook his head. "I'm not asking you to investigate. I'm asking you to find people. But if finding people turns up something else — well. You've got eyes."

* * *

They argued for another hour. Coral laid out every reason: the season, the altitude, the size of the search area, the fact that she was one woman with one horse and no authority above a ranger's badge worth less than the tin it was stamped on. Teague met each one with the same maddening patience, and then, when she'd run out of reasons, he played his last card, and it wasn't the card she expected.

"I'm not asking," he said. "I'm telling you. Territorial statute, section twelve. In a case involving federal interests and probable loss of life, the marshal has emergency conscription power over any commissioned ranger within the district." He slid a paper across the desk. "I hoped it wouldn't come to that. I hoped you'd say yes like a human being. But I've got six names on a list and a window closing, and I'll use whatever the law gives me."

Coral stared at the paper. Deputization papers, already filled out in a clerk's careful hand, dated yesterday. He'd written them before he ever wired her. He had known.

"You son of a bitch," she said quietly.

"Probably," Teague agreed. "My wife said the same for thirty-one years." He dipped a pen and held it out to her. "Sign. Or I swear it onto you anyway and you ride north hating me twice as much. Either way you ride north."

For a moment she genuinely considered refusing — considered what it would cost, jail probably, or the loss of her commission, which was the only thing between her and having no reason at all to get up in the morning. Then she thought of Tomas, who had believed in papers and ledgers and the idea that the law was a fence built to keep the strong off the weak, and who had died because some men treated fences as suggestions. She thought of six strangers' families waiting on telegrams.

She took the pen. Her signature came out harder than she meant it to, the nib nearly tearing the paper.

"One week of supplies," she said. "I pick my own route. I answer to nobody on that mountain but myself. And when it's done — whatever 'done' means up there — you tear that conscription up and you never point me at anything again."

"When it's done," Teague said, "you can have my job."

* * *

The outfitter's store in Dry Wells was dim and smelled of canvas and bacon grease, and the proprietor, a stooped man named Ferro, filled her order without questions because she paid cash and cash asked nothing. Hardtack. Coffee. Salt pork. A block of pemmican she trusted more than any of it. A new candle lantern and three candles. A coil of good rope, fifty feet, because rope was the one tool that had never once lied to her. She stood at the counter doing the arithmetic of weight against days, discarding comfort item by item — the extra blanket first, then the second pair of gloves, then the tin of peaches she had wanted without admitting she wanted it.

A week of supplies. Nine days since the surveyors vanished. Four days past that before hope thinned to nothing. The numbers kept not working, and she kept working them anyway, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Outside, the light had gone long and amber. Her horse — a dun mare named Sorrel Foot because of a dark sock on her near hind leg — stood dozing at the rail, and Coral rested her forehead briefly against the animal's warm neck, the closest thing to prayer she practiced.

"We're going north," she told her. "Against my better judgment and everybody else's."

The mare blew air through her lips, which was as close to an opinion as she ever offered.

Coral saddled in the amber light, checking cinch and latigo twice, loading the panniers balanced left to right the way her father had taught her before he'd taught her anything else. The rifle went in the scabbard. The revolver went on her hip, though she caught herself weighing it and wondering — not for the first time and not for the last — what exactly she was carrying it for. Protection, she told herself. The mountain would decide otherwise if it wanted to.

Marshal Teague came down the boardwalk as she swung up. He carried a folded paper and an expression she distrusted on principle.

"Last thing," he said. "Description of the party. Six souls. Team lead is a Dr. Edmund Voss, geologist, gray beard, walks with a stick. His daughter-in-law Ada Quist is along as cartographer — twenty-six, dark hair, glasses. Four others: two chainmen, a levelman, a cook. Names are on the sheet." He handed it up to her. "And one more. They hired a local guide out of Cedar Draw to take them over the passes. Fellow named Calloway. Renn Calloway."

The world went narrow and bright, the way it did in the half-second before lightning.

Coral sat very still in the saddle. Somewhere behind her ribs her heart was doing something loud and stupid, and she made it stop with an act of will that cost more than the whole day's argument had. Renn Calloway. The name she had chased across three years and four ranges, through saloons and land offices and the memories of men who had sold him horses and never asked where he was going. The signature she had hunted through a hundred pages of forged ledger entries. The man who had erased her brother with ink and left the desert to finish the job.

"He's been up there a month," Teague was saying. "Good references, apparently. Knew the passes. Which is more than I can say for—"

"I know who he is," Coral said.

Teague stopped. Looked at her. Forty years of not missing much moved behind his eyes, and she watched him arrive at something without her giving him a word of help.

"Well," he said slowly. "That explains a few things. That explains why you fought me so hard, maybe. And maybe why you shouldn't have." He stepped back from the mare's stirrup. "Miss Joubert — I conscripted a ranger. Whatever else rides north with you today, that's between you and God. But those six people didn't choose any part of your history, and neither did the weather. Find them first. Settle accounts after. In that order, if you can manage it."

Coral looked down at him for a long moment. The amber light lay along the street like spilled honey. Somewhere north, past the edge of town, past the last fences, the Vermilions stood up blue and white against the sky, holding their secrets the way mountains hold everything: patiently, and until they chose otherwise.

"Order noted, Marshal," she said.

She turned Sorrel Foot north and rode out of Dry Wells without looking back, though she felt Teague watching her go, and felt the town thinning around her, and felt the old familiar pull of the empty country opening up ahead — the country where wanting nothing had always kept her safe.

But the arithmetic rode with her now, and it wouldn't stay quiet. Nine days missing. One guide whose boot prints she could identify blindfolded. A storm gathering somewhere over the basin like a debt coming due. And six strangers who might be alive, might be dead, might be neither — might be hidden, the way things get hidden when men with money need them hidden.

She rode north with a week of supplies and no desire to find anyone alive, and she told herself that was the truth, and the dun mare carried her up the valley road at a steady trot, and the mountains grew, and the afternoon aged, and by the time the sun went down behind her she had stopped lying to herself about one thing, at least:

She was going to find Renn Calloway. Whatever else happened on that mountain, whoever else she found or failed to find, she was going to stand in front of the man who killed her brother, and she was going to decide, at last, what justice looked like when it finally caught up.

The first stars came out. The trail climbed. Somewhere ahead, in the white silence under the peaks, the answer was already waiting — and Coral Joubert rode toward it with her jaw set and her brother's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, resentful as a storm front and twice as certain to arrive.
