## 4. Gear Without Ghosts

The Vermilion Range did not welcome her back.

Coral had felt it before on other returns — the sense that high country remembered a body the way sand remembered a boot heel, briefly and without affection. Three years she'd been away from these ridgelines, three years spent chasing small men across small crimes in country so flat a dropped hat could be found from horseback at a trot. The Vermilions had been Tomas's country more than hers. He'd run survey lines through these canyons as a young man, before the water ledger, before Renn Calloway's signature had turned his name into a debt. She had climbed here twice as a girl, once to fish an unnamed creek and once to bury a dog, and both times the mountain had taken something from her and given nothing back.

Now it took the sun.

By midafternoon the front she'd seen building over the western mesas had crawled up the sky like a lid closing, and the light went the color of old tin. Coral rode into the upper basin where the survey team had made its base camp, according to the marshal's papers, on the fourth of the month — eleven days gone now, six souls, permits expiring with the first snow. The basin opened ahead of her the way high basins do, all at once, a bowl of tawny grass and broken granite ringed by walls the color of dried blood, and she reined up at its lip and looked down and knew, before she was close enough to know anything, that it was wrong.

She couldn't have said how she knew. That was the thing about craft: it lived below language. A tracker reads country the way a banker reads a ledger, not line by line but in the whole weight of it, and the weight of this basin was off. Too still. Not the stillness of emptiness — emptiness has birds in it, has ground squirrels scolding, has the small busy noise of a place going about its business unwatched. This was the stillness of a room after people have left it in a hurry, or been made to.

She rode down into it slowly, rifle loose in its scabbard, eyes working.

The camp sat on a bench above the creek fork, four tents in a neat crescent, flylines strung between two piñons, a fire ring with a cook grate folded against a stone. Everything about it said *orderly*. Everything about it said *we'll be back by supper*. And everything about it said *eleven days ago*, because the flylines sagged with dew-weight, the canvas of the nearest tent wore a fine gray film of dust, and a tin plate on the cook table held the desiccated remains of what had been beans, curled at the edges like autumn leaves.

Coral dismounted and stood a moment with her hand on her mare's neck, letting the horse's unease tell her what it could. Sorrel Jane wasn't a nervous animal. She'd stood quiet through gunfire, through lightning walking the flats a quarter mile off, through a mountain lion screaming close enough to spit at. But she didn't want to come into this camp. Her ears worked like little radar dishes, sweeping, sweeping, and her nostrils flared at a wind that carried — Coral tipped her head and got it too — nothing. No smoke. No horse. No human smell at all, which in a camp that had held six people should have been impossible. Woodsmoke clings to a fire ring for weeks. Leather and sweat soak into tent canvas and stay. Even the latrine trench, when she walked to it, was freshly dug and utterly cold, as if it had been used once and then abandoned by men who had vanished into the air.

"Where'd you go," she said aloud, just to hear a voice, "and why didn't you take your boots?"

Because that was the heart of it, the thing her eye kept snagging on the way a sleeve snags on barbed wire. She circled the camp twice, then a third time, walking the perimeter the way she'd walk a track line, and there were no tracks. Not few tracks. Not faded tracks. *None.* The ground around the camp was the fine pale soil of the high basins, packed enough to hold a print for a week in dry weather, soft enough at the margins near the creek to hold one for a month, and it was blank. Six people had eaten, slept, worked, and dug a privy on this bench, and they had apparently levitated out of it.

Eleven days of wind would blur a trail. It would not erase it. Wind blurs edges; it doesn't lift two hundred pounds of man and horse off the earth. Rain would flatten sign, and there hadn't been rain — the marshal's telegram had said clear and cold, and the ground agreed, holding even the crisp edge of elk tracks along the creek, days old, sharp as if cut in wax. Elk had walked through this basin. Deer had walked through it. A coyote had trotted the length of the bench leaving a line of neat double prints, and every animal that lived had left its mark on this ground except the six human beings who had pitched their tents here and called it home.

Coral crouched at the edge of the fire ring and made herself look at the ashes. Cold, sifted by wind, but beneath the top layer they were fine and white, burned hot and complete. Someone had banked this fire well. Someone had intended to return to it. Beside the ring lay a hatchet driven neatly into a stump, blade buried to the eye — the kind of thing a man does when he means to come back in an hour and doesn't want to hunt for his tool.

She rose and went through the tents.

The first held bedrolls rolled tight, spare clothing folded, a canvas satchel of instruments — she recognized the transit case by its brass corner fittings, and when she lifted it, it was heavy, oiled, wrapped in its own leather straps. Nobody abandons a transit. A transit is the surveyor's spine; you carry it out of a burning building before you carry your coat. The second tent held paper: field notebooks in an oilcloth packet, tied with string, dry as bone. The third held food — this was the detail that put the cold in her wrists. Flour, bacon in cloth, coffee, a sack of dried apples, all cached on a raised platform against bears, all intact. Six people did not walk away from a hundred pounds of provisions into mountains already breathing winter. People in the high country guard food past the point of reason. They fight over it. They die near it.

The fourth tent was the guide's, and she knew it before she touched anything, because guides keep their gear different from scientists. Boots by the door instead of under the bedroll. Rope coiled in a professional's long lay, hung rather than piled. A slicker, a spare hat, a cleaning kit laid out with the bore rod parallel to the patches. And a rifle rack — empty. Two pegs, one rifle gone, one left behind, a cheap .30-30 with a cracked stock. A man takes the good rifle and leaves the bad one only if he expects to come back, or if he left in such a hurry he grabbed without choosing.

Or if he never meant to leave at all.

She stood in the doorway of that tent longer than she needed to, telling herself she was reading the gear, and what she was actually doing was feeling the shape of a presence. Camps hold their people the way shells hold their animals; you can feel the ghost of a habit in the way a bedroll is thrown back or a cup sits on a stone. This camp had six ghosts and none of them were dead ones, and that was worse. Dead ghosts lie still. These had been interrupted.

* * *

She made herself work.

That was the discipline, the code under the code: when a thing frightens you, you slow down and measure it, because fear moves fast and fast is how mistakes are made in country that kills you for them. So she built a small fire — she needed hot coffee the way a mule needs shoes, and besides, a fire was a statement, to the basin and to whatever had emptied it, that Coral Joubert did not skulk — and while the water heated she laid the camp out in her notebook the way she'd lay out a crime, page ruled into columns, each fact written plain.

Four tents. Instruments wrapped and cached. Notebooks secured. Food intact, roughly nine days' worth for six. One rifle missing, one left. Hatchet in stump. Fire banked hot. Latrine dug, lightly used, then unused. No tracks, human or shod, entering or leaving, within a radius she paced at two hundred yards in every direction. Creek running clear. No blood. No drag marks. No sign of struggle anywhere — and this was the strangest column of all, because six adults do not leave a camp together in silence. Somebody argues. Somebody forgets a pipe. Somebody writes a note and weights it with a stone. There was no note. There was nothing but the tidy, patient geometry of people who had stepped out of the world.

She drank her coffee and let the professional in her argue with the woman in her.

The professional said: *they walked out, they walked far, they walked on rock.* It happened. High country offered granite benches and bare slabs where a hundred men could pass and leave less than a deer. Maybe they'd moved camp in a hurry — a threat, a sickness, a decision — and taken the rock route down-canyon, and the wind had done the rest. The professional said this comfortably, the way a lawyer says *there's an innocent explanation*, and the woman in her, who had spent three years learning exactly how many innocent explanations a guilty world could manufacture, said: *then why is the food still here?*

You move camp in a hurry, you take the food. You take the transit. You take the notebooks or you burn them. You don't leave nine days of bacon sitting on a platform in bear country unless you left believing you'd be back by dark, all six of you, together, on foot, in boots that weighed nothing and printed nothing.

Unless you left in the night, without your gear, in company you hadn't chosen.

She looked west, where the front had finished its work. The sky over the Vermilions' high wall was the color of wet slate, and under it the peaks had gone a hard, featureless blue-gray, and the air had that flinty taste that meant snow within three days, maybe two. The marshal's arithmetic ran itself again in her head, unwelcome as a debt collector: permits expire Friday. First snows due Thursday, if the front held its line. Which gave her, generously, five days to find six people who had vanished from a camp without disturbing the dust.

And one other figure, unspoken, standing at the edge of the thought the way a wolf stands at the edge of firelight. Because a survey team in this country needed a guide, and the permit file the marshal had shown her had named the guide, and she had read the name and then read it again and then put the paper face-down on the marshal's desk as if it might bite. Renn Calloway. Three years of empty ranges and petty thieves and nights sleeping in arroyos, all of it aimed at a name, and the name had walked north ahead of her into the one piece of country she'd sworn she'd never need to see again.

Maybe he was dead in a ravine somewhere. The thought arrived wearing comfort's clothing, and she examined it and found it hollow. Men like Calloway didn't die in ravines. They died old, in beds, having cost everyone else the dying.

She kicked dirt over her fire, and as the steam of it rose she saw what she should have seen an hour ago.

Not a track. An absence arranged into a shape. On the bench's eastern margin, where the pale soil met the first scatter of granite slabs climbing toward the mine workings marked on her map — the old Consolidated, adits and tailings, abandoned forty years — the elk trail bent. It bent sharply, unnaturally, the way game trails bend around something they won't cross, and she walked to it and stood where the elk had stood and looked at what the elk had declined to step on: a swath of ground ten feet wide where the surface had been swept. Not eroded. Swept. Brushed smooth with branches or dragged sacks, the way a housekeeper sweeps a floor, the way a man covers a grave or a path.

Somebody had erased a trail here, carefully, recently enough that the elk — creatures of fixed habit — still detoured around the memory of whatever had frightened them.

Coral stood very still, coffee cooling in her hand, and felt the boredom of three years drain out of her body like water out of a cracked jug, and understood that what rushed in behind it was not relief. It was the old cold attention, the hunter's attention, the thing that had kept her moving across the empty ranges because stillness hurt — and it had followed her here, into the high country, and it had brought her brother's thief with it.

She went back to the guide's tent, took the cracked-stock .30-30 down from its peg, checked it — loaded, oiled, someone had cared for it — and slung it across her own back beside her rifle. If Renn Calloway came back to this camp for his gun, he'd find a ranger waiting, and she had questions that three years of distance had sharpened to a point.

Then she saddled Sorrel Jane, and pointed her east, toward the swept ground and the granite and the dark mouth of the old Consolidated workings showing on the map like a hole punched in the page — and at the edge of the swept swath, half-hidden where brush met stone, she saw the first footprint.

A boot heel, pressed deep, its scarred edge turned toward her like a face she knew.
