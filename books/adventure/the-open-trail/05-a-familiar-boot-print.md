## 5. A Familiar Boot Print

The wrongness of the camp did not diminish with daylight. If anything it grew sharper, the way a stone in a boot grows heavier the longer you walk on it.

Coral spent her first morning at the survey camp doing what she always did when a thing refused to make sense: she stopped trying to make sense of it and started cataloguing instead. She walked the perimeter twice, once slow and once slower, and by the second pass she had stopped seeing the camp as a mystery and started seeing it as a record — six people's habits written in canvas and tin and rope, waiting for someone literate enough to read them.

There were four tents, not six. Two of the larger ones had been struck and their poles lashed flat against a pack frame that still stood upright beside the cook fire, as if someone had meant to break camp and then changed their mind mid-motion. The fire itself was cold but orderly, stones ringed, ash raked, kindling stacked under a oilcloth lean-to the way a careful hand does it before turning in for the night rather than the way a panicked hand leaves it fleeing into the dark.

She counted the plates. Six tin plates hung from a ridge line strung between two junipers, washed and upside down to drain. Six cups nested inside one another on a crate lid. A Dutch oven sat buried to its handles in cool ash — the old trick for keeping bread warm through a cold night, and a trick nobody used if they expected to be gone by morning.

Nobody had fled this place. Nobody had been dragged from it either, so far as she could see. There was no sign of struggle, no spilled flour, no blood in the dust, no torn canvas. And yet there were no tracks leaving it either, and that was the part that kept snagging in her like a barbed hook.

Because people do not simply rise from their beds and evaporate. She had tracked men across ground that wanted to swallow every print — hardpan, wind-scoured slickrock, three days of blowing sand — and even then they left something. A scuffed lichen. A dropped button. A horse's droppings going cold in a shape that told you which way he'd stood. Ground remembered. That was the whole religion of her trade: the earth is honest even when men are not.

This ground was lying by omission.

* * *

By midday she widened her circles. She worked the way water works, downhill and patient, spiraling out from the camp in ever-larger loops, reading the terrain for the paths a body would naturally take — the line of least resistance toward water, toward shelter, toward the low saddle that led off the plateau. She found javelina sign, found a bighorn's old bedding ledge, found the crumbling stack of rocks some prospector fifty years dead had raised to mark nothing in particular. She did not find a single human track newer than the season before.

Except the ones at the camp itself. Those were everywhere and nowhere — scuffs around the tents, a churned patch near the instrument crates where feet had shuffled, the pressed ghost of knees in the dirt beside the fire where someone had knelt. But they went in circles. They arrived and milled and stopped, like the tracks of birds in a yard after the hawk has passed over. Nothing led away.

She crouched over the churned patch for a long time, letting her eyes go soft the way her father had taught her, not looking *for* anything but letting the ground offer up what it had. Wind moved through the junipers above her with a sound like slow breathing. Somewhere below, a raven said one flat syllable and thought better of it.

That was when she saw the heel mark.

It was half-effaced, pressed into the margin of the churned earth where the ground held a little more clay than sand, and most trackers would have read it as exactly what it appeared to be: another anonymous boot print among dozens, notable only for being deeper than the rest. A heavy man. A man carrying weight or standing long.

But Coral Joubert had spent three years reading one particular set of boot prints the way a widow reads letters, and she knew things about those boots that Renn Calloway would have been astonished to learn she knew.

The right heel carried a scar. Not a wear pattern — a scar, cut into the leather by a farrier's rasp that had slipped, a diagonal gouge about the length of her smallest finger, running from the outer edge of the heel toward the arch. She knew it because she had first seen it printed in the alkali outside Dry Wells three years ago, following the theft of Tomas's claim, and had seen it again since in eleven counties across two territories. She had lain awake in cheap rooms drawing it from memory in the margins of her brother's ledger until she could have reproduced it blind. A farrier's mistake, made once, in some shop whose name she would never know, and it had become the truest signature Renn Calloway possessed — truer than any name he'd signed, because a man can forge his name but he cannot forge the accident of his own boots.

Coral did not move for a full minute. She knelt there with her hand hovering an inch above the print, not touching it, the way you might hover a hand over a sleeping snake, and the whole high desert seemed to go quiet around her, the wind holding its breath, waiting to see what she would do.

Then she touched it. The clay was firm, edges still crisp, softened only at the very rim where two nights of dry air had begun to crumble it. Three days old. Maybe four. Fresh enough that whoever wore that boot had stood on this plateau within a day of the survey team's disappearance.

He had been here. He had been here *with them.*

* * *

She sat back on her heels and made herself think, because thinking was the one discipline she had never let grief take from her.

Renn Calloway was a water-rights broker's man, a paper man, a man who stole with ink and let other hands do the rougher work. In three years of chasing him she had built a portrait piece by piece from land office clerks, saloon keepers, farriers, and one frightened stable boy in Cane Springs: Calloway dressed well and drank little; Calloway could talk a county commissioner into anything and a sheriff into forgetting it; Calloway did not ride alone and did not ride hard and had never, in any account she'd gathered, pointed a gun at anyone himself. He was a man who arranged disappearances from behind desks in towns with telegraph offices.

And now his boot print sat in the middle of a vanished federal survey team's camp, deep, unhurried, mixed in with the milling prints of the missing six.

She rose and began working the camp again with new eyes, and everything rearranged itself around the print like iron filings around a magnet. The pack frame half-loaded — that wasn't panic, that was *departure interrupted*, someone breaking camp on schedule and stopping mid-task. The instruments wrapped and cached — that was a decision to come back for them. The plates washed and hung — that was routine, the last evening's routine, performed by people who believed tomorrow would be ordinary.

And threaded through all of it, the heavy right-heeled print, appearing near the tents, near the crates, near the fire. Walking among them. One of them, or accepted by them. A guide, maybe. A hired man to lead a survey crew into country they didn't know.

Coral stood in the center of the empty camp with the sun straight overhead and felt two currents in her chest run together into one river, and the confluence was so violent it was almost physical — a hot, narrowing feeling behind her sternum, her breath going shallow and quick. For three years the hunt and the duty had been separate animals. The hunt was hers, private, a debt she collected in miles. The duty was the marshal's, imposed, resented, a leash. Now the leash and the debt had braided into a single cord, and it ran north and east from this camp, up toward the Vermilion high country, and at the end of it stood Renn Calloway wearing a guide's job like a coat over his crimes.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them the way you notice weather — distantly, without ownership — and made fists until they stopped.

* * *

She gave herself the rest of the day, because discipline demanded it and because haste was how trackers died in high country. She ate cold beans and hardtack sitting on the crate lid, and while she chewed she took out Tomas's ledger — she carried it always, wrapped in oilcloth inside her bedroll, though she'd have denied under torture that it was a talisman — and turned to the page with the transfer of claim, the page bearing the signature that had ended her brother's life in every way short of burial.

*Renn Calloway, agent for the Consolidated Development Syndicate.*

The same syndicate whose abandoned mine workings honeycombed the ridge country forty miles northeast. She'd known that for two years and had told herself it was coincidence, because the alternative was a conclusion too large to carry alone. Now the print in the clay was telling her the conclusion had been waiting for her all along, patient as stone.

Tomas had been a water man the way monks are praying men — he'd kept his ledger of flows and seasons and seep springs with a devotion that embarrassed everyone who loved him, and Coral included herself in that number, back when loving him was a thing she did casually, in passing, the way you love a house you grew up in. When the claim transferred and the cisterns were padlocked by the new owners' agents, Tomas had ridden into the mountains to prove the spring flow that would void the transfer, and he'd come down off the Vermilion Plateau in October with fever in him, and by the time anyone found his camp he was past saving. The doctor in Dry Wells had used the word *pneumonia*. Coral had always heard a different word underneath it, a word shaped like *broken heart*, and she had decided that the men who broke it owed a debt payable in one currency only.

She closed the ledger. The wind had shifted while she sat, swinging around out of the northwest, and it carried a different temperature now, a thin metallic edge that hadn't been there yesterday. She looked up. Over the northern ramparts of the range, where the country climbed into the granite teeth, a cloud was building — white and innocent-looking at its crown, but gray-bellied underneath, stacking itself up with the slow confidence of weather that intends to become history.

Snow. Early snow, days ahead of any honest calendar. It would erase everything — tracks, camps, evidence, maybe people.

Which meant Calloway's trail was perishable. Which meant whatever had happened to six federal surveyors was perishable faster still.

* * *

She spent the last hour of light doing the arithmetic of pursuit, and the arithmetic was ugly.

One woman, one horse, seven days of supplies already drawn down to five by her detour through the flats. A storm front closing from the northwest on a timeline of two days, maybe three. A search area of several hundred square miles of the worst walking country in the territory — canyon country above timberline, where a body off the trail could lie ten feet from a path and never be found. And somewhere in it, six missing people and the one man in the world she had sworn to find, all tangled together like wire in a flood.

The marshal's orders had been simple enough: locate the survey party, ascertain survival, report. The marshal had not ordered her to bring anyone back, because the marshal was not a fool and knew what winter in the Vermilions did to rescue arithmetic. Find them, fix their position, get out ahead of the snow. That was the mission.

But the mission had changed shape in the clay beside a cold fire pit, and Coral knew it, and she sat with the knowing the way you sit with a hot coal passed hand to hand.

If she found the surveyors, she found Calloway. If she found Calloway, she found the surveyors — or what was left of the question of them. The two searches had collapsed into one, and there was no longer any version of this where she rode out early and reported from safety. Every road forward went through him.

She thought about what she would do when she reached him. She had rehearsed it for three years, in the honest dark of a thousand camps: the rifle coming level, the words she'd say, the way he'd look. In the rehearsals he always begged, or lied, or ran. In the rehearsals she always knew exactly what came next, and her hands never shook.

Tonight, looking at the cloud building over the high country, she noticed that the rehearsal had gone quiet. All she could see clearly was the print in the clay, and beyond it the empty trail climbing north, and the fact — arriving in her now with the weight of settled law — that she would follow that trail until the snow took it, and past the point where the snow took it, by memory and spite and whatever else she had.

She banked her small fire early and lay watching the stars wheel over the black comb of the range. Sleep came slowly and left quickly. Twice in the night she woke with her hand already closed around the ledger's oilcloth wrapping, and both times she lay listening to the wind, which had begun, very faintly, very far off, to make the sound that old high-country men called the mountain clearing its throat.

Before first light she was saddled. She took one last look at the camp — the washed plates hanging in their row, the cached instruments, the polite, terrible orderliness of it — and then she put it behind her and rode north and east, up the line of the drainage, following a scarred right heel into the teeth of the Vermilion Range.

Two miles along the trail, in the gray before sunrise, she found the first thing in nine days that was neither a print nor an absence: a strip of blue surveyor's ribbon, tied to a juniper limb, snapping in the rising wind. And beneath it, pressed into a seam of mud that the cold had stiffened overnight, a boot print facing uphill.

The right heel carried the scar.

He was ahead of her. He was moving. And for the first time in three years, Renn Calloway was running in a direction she could follow.
