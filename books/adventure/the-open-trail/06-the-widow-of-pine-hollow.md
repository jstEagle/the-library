## 6. The Widow of Pine Hollow

The mule died two miles below the old Consolidated workings, and Coral shot it out of mercy before the cold could do it slower. She stood a moment in the falling light with her hand on its warm neck, feeling the heat leave, and thought: that's one more thing this mountain has taken without asking.

Then she shouldered what she could carry and kept climbing, because the tracks said someone had climbed it before her — small boots, uneven gait, favoring the left leg, dragging now. The prints were hours old at most. Hours old meant alive. Alive was not something she'd let herself believe since the empty camp, but the snow didn't lie the way people did, and the snow said: one person, walking wounded, headed for the mine adits above Pine Hollow.

She found the first adit dark and empty, its timber lintel furred with frost. The second breathed.

Not wind — breath. The faintest fog of warmth rising off the black mouth of the tunnel, so slight she might have imagined it, except her hands had spent three years learning to read country the way other women read letters, and they did not imagine things. She stood at the entrance with her rifle low and said, in the flat carrying voice she used for stock and for liars:

"I'm coming in. I'm alone. I'm armed, but you'll hear me set it down."

Nothing answered. She went in sideways, one shoulder to the rock, letting her eyes give up the last of the daylight behind her.

The dark inside smelled of old timber and old water and, faintly, of a human body that had been afraid for a long time. Twenty feet in, where the adit widened into a collapsed stoping chamber, she saw the shape first — a huddle against the far wall, wrapped in what had been a survey tarp, and then the eyes, open, fixed on her with the terrible patience of someone too cold to be frightened anymore.

"Ada Quist?" Coral said.

The eyes blinked. A voice came out of the tarp like paper tearing. "You're not one of them."

"No."

"They said no one would come." A pause, long enough that Coral thought the woman had gone under. "I waited anyway. Isn't that stupid. I waited anyway."

Coral knelt, set the rifle against the wall where the woman could see it done, and began unpacking her bedroll with hands that moved fast and steady while her heart did neither. "Can you walk?"

"I can walk," Ada Quist said, and then, as Coral got a hand under her arm and felt how little of her there was left to lift, "I just couldn't decide where."

* * *

There was a thing about cold that Ada had learned in the adit, in the ninth hour or the nineteenth, when time stopped being a line and became a place you sat in: cold made you honest. Not brave — honest. It stripped everything down to the sentences underneath your sentences, and what was left was true whether you wanted it or not.

So when the ranger woman — Joubert, she'd said, Coral Joubert, like a name off a wanted poster — pressed a tin cup of melted snow into Ada's hands and told her to drink it slow, Ada heard herself say things she had been holding behind her teeth for four days.

"There were six of us," she said. "Then five. Then me."

The ranger didn't flinch. That was the first thing Ada noticed about her past the obvious things — the competence, the economy, the way she built the fire small and shielded because she knew smoke carried. She didn't flinch, and she didn't rush. She fed the fire a stick at a time and listened the way some people pray.

"Tell me from the start," the ranger said. "Only the parts you're sure of. We can come back for the rest."

"We came up to run lines for the aquifer survey. Six of us. Mr. Halloway, the two Brandt brothers, Miss Okafor, young Pell, and me." Ada held the cup in both hands and watched the steam. Her fingers wouldn't close all the way around it yet. "We were finding things we weren't supposed to find. Mr. Halloway said so on the fourth day. He said the maps we'd been issued didn't match the instruments, and instruments don't have opinions."

The ranger's head came up slightly. Just slightly. Like a dog scenting rain.

"Men came at night," Ada said. "Four days ago. Maybe five. They wore badges — concession badges, silver-and-black, the kind the land syndicates issue their own men so they don't shoot each other. They didn't rob us. That's the thing nobody will believe. They didn't take a thing. They took *us*."

* * *

Coral listened, and beneath the listening ran the older current, the three-year current, the one that never stopped moving even when she did. Concession badges. Silver-and-black. She had seen that badge once before, pinned to a broker's lapel in a Dry Wells saloon, the night Tomas's ledger went from being paper to being evidence.

"Where did they take them?" she asked.

Ada's eyes came up. In the firelight they were red-rimmed and clear, which was worse than either extreme. "Toward the Consolidated. There's an old haul road above the second switchback. They put the others on horses and made me watch, and then they argued about me." A sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter froze solid. "One of them said leave her. One said the widow talks too much. And the third one — he looked at me a long time and said, 'Cold does our work for free.' So they took my coat and pointed me downhill and told me to keep walking until I stopped."

Coral fed the fire another stick. Her hands were steady. Somewhere behind her sternum, something was not.

"You walked uphill instead," she said.

"I grew up in mining country." Ada's voice was going thin; Coral could hear the sleep coming for her, dragging at the ends of words. "My husband worked the Consolidated before it played out. Adits hold heat. Rock remembers the summer. I figured — if I was going to die, I'd rather die somewhere that had been warm once." Her eyes drifted half-closed. "That's a stupid thing to say."

"It's not."

"It is. It's exactly the kind of thing you say when—" But she was already going under, sliding sideways into the tarp, and Coral caught the cup before it spilled and sat back on her heels in the breathing dark of the mine and let herself feel, for one unguarded minute, the full weight of what she'd found.

Five people alive, maybe. Held at the Consolidated by men wearing syndicate badges. A witness who talked too much, spared by a coin-flip cruelty. And somewhere ahead of all of it, up that haul road, a boot heel scarred by a farrier's mistake that Coral would know in the dark, in the snow, in her sleep.

She banked the fire, checked Ada's wraps, and took first watch with her back to the rock and her face to the black mouth of the adit, where the night came down over Pine Hollow like a lid.

* * *

Ada woke to orange light and the smell of coffee, and for a confused moment she was twenty-six years old and her husband was shaking snow off his hat in the doorway, and then the moment closed over and she was forty-one and alone in a hole in a mountain with a stranger who had watched her all night without once closing her eyes.

"You slept four hours," the stranger said. "Eat."

The food was trail biscuit and salt pork, and Ada ate it the way you eat when your body has stopped consulting you. The woman — Joubert — moved around the little camp like weather, purposeful and impersonal, and Ada found herself studying her the way you study anything that shouldn't exist. Rangers were men. Rangers were stories. This one had a face like a closed ledger and hands that had already repacked both their packs, and when she caught Ada looking, she didn't smile or look away. She just waited, as if looking were a question and she had all day for the answer.

"The others," Ada said. "Are they—"

"Alive, if whoever took them wants them alive. Dead men don't hold land claims." Joubert said it flatly, factually, and then seemed to hear how it landed, because she added, quieter: "Permits expire Friday. If the syndicate wants that ground cleared of claimants, they need the survey team gone before the paperwork lapses. That means your people are worth more breathing than not. For now."

"For now," Ada repeated. The words tasted like the adit tasted.

Joubert crouched across the fire from her, forearms on knees. "I need to know what the survey found. All of it. Even the parts that don't seem important. Especially those."

And here was the thing Ada had turned over all night in the rock-warm dark, the thing she hadn't said yet because saying it would make it real: "It wasn't an accident, what we found. Mr. Halloway thought it was clerical at first. A misfiled plate, a swapped map. But it was every plate. Every map. The aquifer the syndicate sold rights to — the one my husband's claim folded into, before he died — it isn't there. It was never there. Dry rock, all of it, the whole southern parcel." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "The water runs north. Under unclaimed land. Land the syndicate has been trying to buy up cheap for two years, and couldn't, because homesteaders kept refusing. Because people like my Elias kept believing the maps."

The fire ticked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the sea, though there was no sea within eight hundred miles.

"So they forged the surveys," Joubert said slowly, "to sell water that doesn't exist —"

"To fund buying the land where it does." Ada nodded. "And when we found it, they couldn't just burn our notes. Notes get rewritten. Surveys get resubmitted. You have to make the *surveyors* disappear, and make it look like the mountain did it. Snow does our work for free." She heard herself quote the badged man and stopped, appalled, her hand at her mouth.

But the ranger wasn't looking at her anymore. She was looking at the fire, and her face had changed — not softened, nothing about that face softened, but something behind it had opened, the way a door opens onto a room you suspected was there all along.

"How long," Ada asked, "have you been chasing them?"

Joubert's eyes came up. For a moment Ada thought she wouldn't answer, and then she did, and her voice was so level it was clearly costing her.

"Three years. A man named Renn Calloway brokered the sale of my brother's water rights and left him on the range to die. His signature is on the transfer papers. His boot prints were at the survey camp." She stood, and began kicking dirt over the fire with short efficient strokes. "He was guiding your team."

Ada sat very still. "Then he knows where they are."

"He knows everything." The last coals died under the dirt. Joubert shouldered her pack and held out a hand — not tenderly, not roughly, just the way you offer a hand to someone on a ladder. "Can you climb?"

"I climbed out of a grave," Ada said, and took it. "I can climb."

* * *

They came out of the adit into morning light so clean it hurt, the whole of Pine Hollow washed white and blue, the peaks above standing up into a sky with storm written on its far edge — a high gray feathering off the northwest, still a day away, maybe two. Coral read it the way she read everything, automatically, and filed it under *not enough time*.

Ada Quist stood beside her blinking in the brightness, one hand on the adit timbers, a small gray woman in a borrowed coat, and Coral understood that she had just become responsible for her. Not by choice. By arithmetic. Five lives possibly waiting at the Consolidated, one witness who couldn't survive a hard climb alone, and a storm coming down like a debt collector.

Two speeds, then. Or one speed, chosen carefully.

"Stay behind me," Coral said. "Step where I step. If I say down, you're down before you ask why."

Ada looked at her — really looked, the way people only look at each other when politeness has frozen out of the world. "Why are you doing this? You didn't know any of us. You came up here because a telegram told you to."

Coral considered the question honestly, because the woman had earned honesty, standing four days in a hole in a mountain rather than die easy.

"I came up here because I'm good at finding people," she said. "What I do after I find them — that part I'm still working out."

She turned uphill, toward the haul road, toward the Consolidated, toward the scarred boot heel and whatever waited at the end of it, and heard Ada Quist fall in behind her, small boots finding her prints in the snow, one step at a time, trusting a stranger with nothing else left to trust.

Above the second switchback, the old haul road bent away north into the trees, and Coral's chest went tight, because there in the crusted margin of the track, sharp-edged and fresh, half a dozen horses had passed in the night — and beside them, unmistakable even after four days of weather, a man's boot print with a heel scarred wrong by a farrier's trembling hand.

Renn Calloway had walked this road recently. Walking *down*.

Coral stood over the print a long moment, breath smoking, rifle suddenly heavy in her hands, and behind her Ada asked quietly, "Is that him?" — because Coral's face must have shown it, must have shown everything.

"Yeah," Coral said. "That's him."

And the print led not away from the mine, but toward a thread of woodsmoke rising, thin and gray, from the timberline camp above.
