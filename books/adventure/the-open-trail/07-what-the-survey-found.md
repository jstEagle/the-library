## 7. What the Survey Found

The fever broke sometime in the gray hour before dawn, and Ada Quist woke to the smell of pine smoke and coffee and the particular silence of a high country morning — no wind, no birds, just the weight of snow-clouds pressing down on the world like a hand laid flat over a drum.

She was lying on a bedroll inside a mine adit, wrapped in a canvas tarp that smelled of horse and creosote, and for a long moment she did not remember where she was or how she had come to be there. Then she remembered everything at once, the way grief does — not in pieces but whole, a single blow: the tents standing empty, the instruments still wrapped in their oilcloth, Hollis's spectacles folded on his cot as if he'd only stepped out to check the barometer. The men with badges coming up the draw in the dark. Running. Falling. Crawling into this hole in the earth like an animal and waiting to die.

She was not dead. That was the first fact. The second was that someone had built a small fire near the adit mouth, behind a screen of stacked rock so its light wouldn't show, and someone else — a woman, tall, weathered, with a face like a closed door — sat beside her with a tin cup of coffee held out.

"Drink it slow," the woman said. "It'll hurt less going down than the water did."

Ada took the cup. Her hands shook so badly the coffee trembled in rings against the tin, but it was hot and bitter and real, and she drank it down in careful sips while the woman watched her with eyes the color of the alkali flats Ada had crossed once, years ago, on her way west.

"You're the ranger," Ada said. Her voice came out ruined, scraped raw by cold and screaming and three days of silence. "They said a ranger might come. Marshals send people, sometimes, when permits—"

"Coral Joubert." The woman did not offer her hand. She offered instead a strip of dried venison, and when Ada took it, she said, "Eat. Then talk. In whatever order you can manage."

* * *

Ada ate. It took longer than she expected, because her body had been living on meltwater and half a tin of condensed milk for three days, and it seized on the food with an animal urgency that embarrassed her. Coral Joubert did not watch her eat, exactly. She watched the adit mouth, and the gray light beyond it, and the snowline creeping down the western face of the Vermilions like a tide coming in over ground that had never learned to expect it. But Ada felt herself being counted anyway — weighed, measured, assessed for what she could carry and what she would cost. She had spent six weeks in the company of surveyors; she recognized the habit.

"I'm Ada Quist," she said, when she could. "Assistant surveyor. Chainman and level work. I was hired out of Denver in April."

"How many came up with you?"

"Six. Seven counting Mr. Calloway, our guide." She saw something move behind the ranger's face at that name — not surprise, something older and harder, a stone shifting in a riverbed — but the woman said nothing, so Ada went on. "Hollis Prentiss ran the party. Federal man, Geological Survey. There was Dr. Emmerich, geologist. Tomás Ruiz and Ben Okafor, instrument men. Sadie Voss, cook and packer. And me."

"And now there's you."

"Now there's me." Ada set down the empty cup. Her hands were steadier now, or maybe she had simply stopped noticing their shaking. "You want to know what happened."

"I want to know why five grown people walked out of a fully provisioned camp without taking a coat."

"They didn't walk," Ada said. "They were taken."

For the first time the ranger turned and looked at her directly, and Ada understood that this was a woman who had already guessed it, who had perhaps known since she found the camp, and who had needed to hear it said aloud anyway because a thing said aloud becomes a fact, and facts are what you build on.

"Tell me from the beginning," Coral Joubert said. "Not the night they came. Before that. What were you doing up here?"

And so Ada told her.

* * *

"We came up to verify the aquifer," she said. "That was the whole commission. The territorial land office has been selling water rights in this range for two years — deep rights, artesian claims, the kind of paper that makes men rich before a drop is ever pumped. Calloway's syndicate brokered most of them. Consolidated Desert Water & Land, they call themselves now. They sold parcels all through the eastern basin, and every parcel came with a map showing the aquifer running under it at sixty feet, eighty feet, a hundred and twenty — beautiful maps, blue-lined, stamped by the survey office." She paused. "Stamped by us, or by men using our name. That was the trouble."

The fire ticked. Somewhere above the adit, snow began to fall — Ada could hear it, a sound too fine to be called a sound, more a presence, a softening of the air itself.

"Hollis got suspicious in June," she said. "A rancher down in Dry Wells — a widow named Ferro — drilled on her parcel. Sixty feet, eighty, a hundred. Dry rock. Granite, mostly, some shale. Not even good granite. She'd paid four hundred dollars for the right to water that didn't exist, and when she wrote to complain, the land office told her the map was certified and the error must be in her drilling. Hollis pulled the original field sheets for the eastern basin out of the archive." Ada's voice caught, and she covered it with a cough. "He was a careful man. Meticulous. He used to say a contour line was a promise, and he never made one he couldn't keep."

"What did the sheets show?"

"That nobody had ever surveyed the eastern basin." Ada laughed, a dry, terrible sound with nothing in it. "That's the elegant part, if you can call it that. The aquifer maps — the ones sold to forty, fifty buyers — were drawn from a survey that was never run. The field sheets are blank. Dated, signed, stamped, and blank. Someone in the office drew the water where the syndicate wanted it, and everyone downstream just... believed the paper. Because it looked like paper always looks. Because we trust the line on the map more than the ground under our feet."

Coral Joubert had gone very still. It was a particular stillness, Ada thought — not the stillness of shock but of recognition, of a woman fitting a new piece into a puzzle she had been staring at for years.

"So the syndicate sold water that isn't there," the ranger said slowly.

"Sold it twice, really. First they sold the phantom rights — that's cash in hand, gone, untraceable. But that was only ever the opening move." Ada leaned forward, and the firelight moved on her face. "Because here is what the syndicate knows and the buyers don't: the real aquifer exists. It's bigger than anything on those forged maps. But it doesn't run under the parcels they sold. It runs under the open range north and east of here — unclaimed land, commons, the last free water in the territory. And as long as that land stays unclaimed, anyone can sink a well on it. Any homesteader, any town, any rancher the syndicate ever cheated. The moment the truth comes out — and it will come out, because widows keep drilling holes in granite — every defrauded buyer looks north and sees where the water actually is."

"So they need the commons gone."

"They need it *enclosed*," Ada said. "Claimed, fenced, bought up cheap or seized outright. And you can't claim land cleanly while a federal survey team is up in it making honest maps. So—" She stopped. She had not said it yet, not once, not even to herself in the dark of the adit, and saying it now made it true in a way thinking it never had.

"So they sent men up to stop the survey," Coral Joubert finished for her. "And to make sure the stopping looked permanent."

"The permits lapse Friday," Ada whispered. "If the team doesn't file its report — if we're listed as lost, delayed, dead — the basin reverts to provisional status. No certified survey, no protected status for the commons. The syndicate's lawyers can move on it within the month. They've probably already drafted the filings." She wrapped her arms around her knees. Inside the tarp her body was warm now, but some colder thing had gotten in and would not leave. "We weren't lost, Ranger. We were never lost. We were *in the way*."

* * *

The snow thickened through the morning. Coral Joubert fed the fire in small, disciplined increments, and Ada talked, because talking kept the dark of the adit at bay, and because she could see that every word was being taken down somewhere behind the ranger's flat gray eyes and filed with a precision that frightened her a little and comforted her more.

She told about the night the badged men came — but that, she sensed, was for later, for the next telling, and the ranger did not push. Instead Ada gave her the survey itself, the six weeks of it, the work that had been the best work of her life.

"I came up hating the mountains," she said. "Denver girl. Flatlander. I thought elevation was just distance with better views. But Hollis taught me to read the land the way you'd read a face — every fold and scar meaning something. And the Vermilions..." She shook her head. "There's water up here, Ranger. Real water. We traced it. Seeps at nine thousand feet, a spring-fed creek that runs in August, gravel beds that hum when you put your ear to them. The aquifer under the eastern basin isn't sixty feet down. It's a hundred and ninety, and it's wide as a valley, and it moves north — toward the commons. Hollis mapped it in three weeks. Honest lines, honest numbers. He said, and I remember it exactly, he said: 'This is the most important sheet anyone's filed in this territory in twenty years. This is the sheet that keeps the desert free.'"

Her voice failed her then, briefly. She let it.

"He knew," Ada said finally. "By August he knew what the maps were worth and who'd want them buried. He sent a duplicate south with the packer in July — did you know that? A copy of the aquifer sheets, sealed, addressed to the Survey office in Denver. If anything happens to us, he said, the duplicate files itself. Bureaucracy as insurance." She smiled, and it hurt. "I used to tease him about it. All that redundancy. Now I pray to God the packer made it down."

"Do you remember which packer? Name?"

"Voss sent him. A boy called Merrick — Danny Merrick, nineteen, rode a mule named Bishop. Left camp the ninth of August." Ada closed her eyes. "Three days before the badged men came."

Coral Joubert was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, flatter, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she did not like.

"If Merrick made Dry Wells, the duplicate's either filed or sitting in a mail sack. Either way it exists. That's one thread they can't cut." She looked at Ada. "But a duplicate is only proof the forgery happened. It doesn't prove what the syndicate intends. It doesn't name who ordered it. For that you need testimony — yours, and Hollis's, and the others'. Which is why they took them alive."

The words landed in the adit like stones dropped down a shaft. Ada heard them fall and fall and never quite hit bottom.

"Alive," she repeated.

"Dead men testify badly, but they also close the case. Missing men leave it open — permits lapse, land reverts, and five federal employees become a mystery nobody's obliged to solve until spring. By thaw, the commons is fenced and the filings are recorded and the whole thing is legal paper from top to bottom." The ranger's jaw tightened. "I've seen men killed for water. I've never seen it done with such tidy handwriting."

* * *

They rested through the middle of the day, because Coral Joubert insisted rest was discipline and not surrender, and Ada slept in patches, waking each time to the same calculation running under her sleep like groundwater: storm coming, permits expiring, five colleagues alive or not alive somewhere north of here, and one exhausted chainwoman and one silent ranger between them and the dark.

In the late afternoon the ranger boiled snow for tea and asked the question Ada had been dreading.

"The men who came. You saw them. How many, and what did they look like?"

"Four that I saw. Maybe more below in the draw." Ada wrapped both hands around the warm cup. "Big men. Quiet — they came up the trail without a sound, and the dogs we hadn't got, well, we hadn't got any, but the horses screamed. They wore badges. That's the part I can't make sit right in my head. Metal badges, star-shaped, pinned on coats that were otherwise ordinary — range coats, dusty. Like they wanted to be seen as law without being any law you could name."

"Concession badges," Coral Joubert said, and something in her tone made Ada look up. "Private police. Some of the big outfits carry them — land companies, cattle combines. Paper deputized by friendly justices. They have authority exactly as far as a judge's signature and not one inch further, and they count on nobody asking to see the signature."

"One of them had a ledger," Ada said. "A real ledger, bound in leather. He read names off it. Hollis Prentiss. Emmerich. Okafor, Ruiz, Voss. He read my name too." Her hands had begun to shake again, and she let them. "He read it like a manifest. Like freight. And then he said — I've gone over it a hundred times, trying to make it mean something else — he said, 'Five for the Consolidated, one loose end.' And they started up the slope toward us, and Hollis shouted *run*, and I ran."

"One loose end," the ranger repeated softly. "They counted you and missed you in the dark. Or they left you deliberately — a survivor tells the tale, and the tale serves them. Lost survey team, lone witness, tragic business, terrible weather." She stared into the fire. "Either way, you're evidence, Miss Quist. The only walking, talking evidence in the territory that this was planned. Which means the men with badges aren't finished looking for you."

Ada had known it, in the animal way the body knows things the mind refuses. Still, hearing it spoken made the adit feel smaller, the snow outside heavier, the distance to anywhere immense.

"Then we should go," she said. "Now. Tonight."

"Not tonight." The ranger nodded at the adit mouth, where the snow was falling so thickly the far wall of the canyon had dissolved entirely. "That's a whiteout building, and it's three days early. Nobody travels in that — not us, not them. We go when it breaks, and we go north, because there's a place I have to see before I trust any road south." She said it without explanation, and Ada, who had spent six weeks learning when a surveyor's silence meant *the data isn't clean yet*, recognized the register and did not ask.

Instead she asked the other question, the one that had been sitting in her chest since the ranger's face had shifted at the name.

"You know Renn Calloway," Ada said. "Our guide. Don't you."

The fire popped. Snow hissed faintly against the rock screen. For a moment Ada thought the woman simply wouldn't answer.

"He stole something from my family," Coral Joubert said at last. "Three years ago. Something that couldn't be replaced, and a man died because of it — not my blood, but mine all the same. I've tracked him across four ranges." She turned her gray eyes on Ada, and in the firelight they were not flat at all; they were full of weather. "And now I find him guiding a federal survey into the one basin where his own syndicate's fraud lives. So either he's deeper in it than I ever imagined—" she paused, and the pause had weight, "—or he's the only man in these mountains who knows exactly what that paper is worth, because he helped write it."

"You think he led them here on purpose."

"I think," Coral Joubert said, rising, gathering the coffee pot, moving with the economy of a woman who wasted nothing, not motion, not words, not feeling, "that tomorrow we follow the trail those badged men left north, toward the old Consolidated workings. And I think that on that trail, or at the end of it, Renn Calloway and I are going to settle three years of accounts." She looked back once, at Ada, at the fire, at the snow closing over the world. "Get your boots dry tonight, Miss Quist. Whatever he is, and whatever happens to him — five of your people are still breathing somewhere on this mountain, and Friday is closer than it looks."

Ada lay back on the bedroll after the ranger banked the fire, and listened to the snow, and did not sleep. Above her, faint through the rock, the mountain creaked and settled under its new white weight, like a great ship taking on cargo in the dark — and somewhere north of her, past the timberline, past the cornices and the cold, five voices she knew were waiting in the earth, and a man with a leather ledger was reading their names again by lamplight, deciding what they were worth, and to whom.
