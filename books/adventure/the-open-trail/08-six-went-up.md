## 8. Six Went Up

The adit breathed cold air against their backs like a thing sleeping. Coral had built the fire small — a handful of dry juniper from the mouth of the mine where some prospector had cached a bundle years ago, wrapped in oilcloth and forgotten — and she fed it stick by stick, because smoke in high country was a letter written to anyone watching. Ada Quist sat with her hands wrapped around Coral's tin cup, both of them around it, as if the cup were the only solid thing left in her world.

She had stopped shivering an hour ago, which worried Coral more than the shivering had. Shivering meant the body still had something to spend. Stillness this deep, this soon after cold, meant the account was nearly empty.

"Drink," Coral said. "All of it."

Ada drank. Her lips were cracked and gray-rimmed, and when she lowered the cup she looked at Coral with the particular clarity of a person who has decided that if she is going to die anyway, she may as well be understood first.

"You want to know what happened," Ada said. "You've been polite about it for two hours."

"I want you warm first."

"I'll never be warm again." She said it without drama, the way a surveyor reads an elevation off a dial. "Ask me now while I can still put the numbers in order. Numbers are how I think. You should know that about me if you're going to trust anything I say."

Coral set down the stick she'd been holding. Outside, the light through the adit's mouth had gone from white to pewter; the afternoon was aging fast at this altitude, and every hour of it felt like coin slipping out of a torn pocket.

"All right," she said. "Start with the camp. The morning of the day before last."

* * *

Ada told it the way she did everything — precisely, in sequence, with the dates and bearings recited like scripture, and beneath all of it a tremor she couldn't quite flatten out of her voice.

They had been eleven days on the mountain. Six of them: Ada Quist, party chief; Dr. Emory Sandoval, geologist; the Hollis brothers, Theo and Marcus, chainmen; Peter Vance, instrument man; and Ruth Okafor, who kept the notes and ran the level lines and, according to Ada, was the only one of them who could cook rice without burning it. Renn Calloway had been hired in Dry Wells as guide — contracted through the land office itself, which Ada now understood meant the syndicate had placed him among them, though whether he'd known what he was placing them into was a question she left hanging in the cold air between them.

"We were running a traverse up the east fork," Ada said. "Gravity readings, water table soundings. Standard reconnaissance for the aquifer adjudication. The permits cover three sections of unclaimed range north of the Vermilion divide, and our job was to establish whether the water the syndicate sold rights to actually existed where the maps said it did."

"And?"

Ada looked at her for a long moment. "And it doesn't."

Coral felt something shift under her ribs, old and familiar, like scree giving way.

"The formations are wrong," Ada went on. "Sandoval knew it by the fourth day. The strata the syndicate's maps show as saturated sandstone — we stood on it. It's shale. Dry as a sermon. There is no water under the Calloway holdings. There never was. Every man who bought those rights bought a hole in the ground."

The fire ticked. Somewhere above the mine, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a long exhale.

"But there is water on this mountain," Coral said slowly. Working it forward. "Real water."

"An aquifer the size of a small sea, running under the unclaimed sections. Under land nobody holds title to yet. Sandoval charted it in three days of soundings. It would make the syndicate's entire portfolio worthless overnight — unless the land itself passed into private hands before the federal adjudication closed. Which requires the current survey to fail." Ada's voice had gone very flat. "A failed survey. Expired permits. No findings filed. Then the sections revert to open claim, and the syndicate files first, with maps already drawn, and no one alive to contradict them."

Coral sat with that. Three years she had chased one man across one stolen claim, believing she was hunting a thief, a single snake in a single garden. And the garden was a continent. Tomas's ledger, his ruined name, his body in the alkali — one thread pulled from a fabric so large she couldn't see its edges.

"The men who came," she said. "Tell me about the men."

* * *

"They came at night," Ada said. "Or near enough to it. The moon was down behind the ridge and the sky was overcast, so it might have been nine o'clock, might have been ten. We were in the tents. I heard horses — not shod. That's what woke me, actually. Unshod horses moving quiet, six of them maybe, coming up the drainage. Nobody rides unshod stock in survey country unless they don't want to leave sign."

Coral nodded. She'd have woken at the same sound. Would have been on her feet with a rifle before the second horse cleared the willows. But then, Coral slept the way soldiers sleep, in slices, and surveyors slept like people who believed the world would hold its shape until morning.

"There were five riders," Ada said. "Big men, heavy coats, canvas covers over their hatbands. And badges." She held up her hand, fingers pinched together, indicating something small. "Tin stars, but not county stars. Not marshal's stars. Stamped metal, oval, with lettering around the rim. Concession badges. Like mining company police used to wear thirty years ago, before anyone decided that wasn't legal anymore."

"Did they give a name? A company?"

"They didn't give anything. They gave orders." Ada's hands tightened around the cup. "One of them — the tall one, gray in his beard, he seemed to be in charge — he read from a paper. Said the survey party was operating outside its permitted boundaries, that the section we were working was subject to a prior mineral concession, and that we were to cease operations and accompany him to the Consolidated office for verification of our credentials. He said resistance would be construed as claim jumping."

"That's nonsense. You'd have your permit papers—"

"We showed them. He barely looked. Folded them into his coat like he was pocketing a receipt." Ada's voice cracked, just once, and she pressed it flat again. "Ruth argued. She was the best of us, honestly, she had the law half-memorized — she started citing the territorial code, said they had no authority, said we'd file a complaint in Dry Wells the moment we got down. The gray-bearded man looked at her the way you look at weather coming. Then he took the field notebooks out of her hands and threw them in the fire."

Coral closed her eyes briefly. Three seasons of work. Soundings, traverses, level lines run at dawn in freezing mud — burned in a minute by men wearing tin that wasn't theirs.

"What happened to the six of you?"

"He separated us. That was the part I keep turning over — how organized it was. No shouting, no rope, nothing crude. They put me and Dr. Sandoval in one group and the other four in another, and the gray-beard said the doctor and I would come along to 'verify the instruments' and the rest would walk up to the Consolidated to wait for the magistrate. As if there were a magistrate. As if there were an office." She stared into the fire. "They marched us down the drainage in the dark. And at the fork below the talus slope, the gray-beard turned to me and said, very reasonable, very quiet — *you go back to camp, Miss Quist. Someone has to carry the instruments out when the weather breaks.*"

"Just you?"

"Just me. They took Sandoval. I said I wouldn't leave, and one of the younger ones took my arm — not rough, that was the horrible part, almost gentle — and walked me back up to the tree line and pointed me at the tents and said, *storm's coming, miss, you'll want to be low when it does.* And then they were gone. All six of them, and my whole crew, walking north toward the old Consolidated workings, and me standing alone in the dark listening to boots fade out."

Ada's composure finally bent. She turned her face away, toward the black throat of the adit, and her shoulders moved once, twice, and stilled.

"They knew the storm was coming," she said. "That's why they let me live, do you understand? Not mercy. Arithmetic. One survivor, half-frozen, with no notes and no witnesses, telling a story nobody in Dry Wells would believe — or worse, not surviving at all, and then it's six people lost to weather, a tragedy, permits lapse, land reverts, and everyone shakes their heads and files new claims by spring." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, angry at herself for the tears. "I'm not supposed to be alive. I'm a rounding error they were too hurried to correct."

Coral said nothing for a moment. The fire had burned down to a red eye, and she fed it another juniper twig and watched the flame stand up.

"You're wrong about one thing," she said. "About being a rounding error."

Ada looked at her.

"You're evidence," Coral said. "Living, breathing, testifying evidence. And evidence is the one thing men like that can't buy back once it walks into a courtroom. So we're going to keep you alive until Friday."

* * *

Ada frowned. "Friday?"

"Permits expire Friday at noon. That's what the telegram said in Dry Wells — the adjudication window closes and the sections revert to open claim. After that, even with testimony, the syndicate's lawyers start filing and the fight moves to paper, where they own every pen." Coral reached into her coat and drew out the little memorandum book she carried, pencil stub wedged in its spine, and opened it flat on her knee. "So we work backward from noon Friday."

She began to write, talking as she did, laying the arithmetic out loud because saying numbers made them real, made them weigh something.

"Today is Tuesday. We came up out of Dry Wells Monday morning, I made the abandoned camp by yesterday noon, found you at dusk. So." She tapped the page. "Storm front. I've watched that cloud bank building over the western divide since yesterday afternoon — lenticulars stacking like plates, wind swinging south-southwest, pressure falling hard enough that my joints have been complaining since midnight. In this country that front means snow inside three days. Could be Thursday night. Could be earlier. If it comes early, it erases the trail both ways and grounds everything, including rescue, including retreat, including us."

"Three days," Ada repeated.

"At most. Probably less." Coral wrote it: *SNOW — THURSDAY NIGHT, EARLIEST SAFE ASSUMPTION.* "Now the mine. The old Consolidated workings. How far?"

"From our camp?" Ada thought, and Coral could see her doing it, translating memory into distance. "We walked maybe four hours in the dark, downhill first, then up the drainage. Call it ten miles as the trail goes. From here—" she glanced toward the adit's mouth, toward the gray failing light beyond it, "—I don't know exactly where we are relative to it. North and east. Higher."

"Then here's what I know." Coral turned the book so Ada could see the rough sketch she was drawing — the drainage, the talus, the ridgeline climbing toward a notch she'd noted on her way in. "There's a miner's trail cutting up the east wall of this canyon. I crossed it yesterday tracking your sign. It climbs toward the divide, and the divide runs northeast toward the Vermilion headwall, and the Consolidated sits under the headwall — everybody in Dry Wells knows that much, it was the richest strike in the territory before it flooded and shut. A day's climb. Hard climb, with you the way you are. But a day."

"A day up," Ada said quietly. "And then what? You're one woman. They were six armed men, and there'll be more at the mine. Foreman, guards, whoever runs this operation. You can't take a fortified mine with a rifle and good intentions."

"No," Coral agreed. "I can't."

She looked at the fire. She thought about a boot print in the ash of the abandoned camp, a heel scarred by a farrier's slipped rasp, a crooked crescent she'd have known in the dark, in her sleep, in her grave. Renn Calloway was somewhere on this mountain, ahead of her or behind her, and whatever he was now — coward, tool, penitent, liar — he had been inside these men's employ. He knew their faces, their number, their habits. He knew the Consolidated's workings, maybe, if he'd guided survey parties through this range before the syndicate owned it outright.

That was a debt she could collect later. Right now it was an asset, and she hated herself a little for the bookkeeping.

"Five people are sitting in a flooded mine shaft waiting for a blizzard to do their killing for them," Coral said. "And every hour I sit here keeping you warm is an hour closer to the storm and an hour closer to Friday. Hesitation has a price tag now, and it isn't mine." She closed the book. "Here's how it has to be. We move at first light. Up the miner's trail, over the shoulder of the divide, down into the headwall basin. We reach the Consolidated tomorrow evening. I find the five of them, and I get them out, and we come down ahead of the snow or we don't come down at all."

"And me?" Ada asked. "Can you make that climb with me on your pace?"

Coral studied her. The woman was hollowed out, frost-nipped, three days past terror — but her eyes were steady, and she'd recited bearings and distances from memory with her teeth chattering, and she had walked out of the dark alone rather than sit down and die, which was more than most people managed.

"You'll make it," Coral said, "because the alternative is explaining to a judge why the aquifer evidence died on a mountainside. Fear of lawyers is a stronger medicine than brandy."

Something moved at the corner of Ada's mouth — not quite a smile, but the place where one would go if she ever got warm again.

Outside, the light in the adit's mouth died altogether, and the wind changed. Coral felt it before she heard it — a shift in the pressure against her eardrums, the smell of the air turning metallic and clean, the particular hush that came over high country when the sky began to load itself. She stepped to the mine entrance and looked north.

Over the divide, under the last bruised band of daylight, the clouds had begun to arrive — not drifting but advancing, a gray wall shouldering over the peaks, silent and total, like a tide deciding.

Three days. Maybe less. Maybe tonight.

Behind her, Ada asked softly, "What is it?"

Coral watched the wall of weather eat the last star above the headwall, directly over the old Consolidated, directly over five people who didn't know the clock had started.

"It's nothing," she said, and went back for her rifle. "Get some sleep. We climb at dawn."

But she lay awake most of the night with the memorandum book open on her chest, staring at the one line she hadn't shown Ada, penciled small at the bottom of the page:

*If Calloway is at the mine, decide before you see him what he is worth.*
