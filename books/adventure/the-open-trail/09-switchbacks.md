## 9. Switchbacks

The mountain did not want them on it.

Coral had known that from the first mile above Pine Hollow, where the trail gave up being a trail and became a suggestion — a thread of worn granite stitched through manzanita and pine duff, climbing in long diagonal strokes toward the spine of the Vermilions. By midmorning the suggestion had thinned to a rumor. By noon it was a switchback: cut into the mountainside by some prospector's stubbornness forty years dead, a shelf of stone no wider than a mule's shoulders, zigzagging up a face that shed rock the way old men shed teeth — one at a time, without warning, and always more than you expected.

She climbed it the way she climbed everything, which was to say without thinking about the top. The top was a lie you told yourself to keep your legs moving. What was real was the next handhold, the next flat place for her boot, the rhythm of breath and stone and the small animal arithmetic of her own body doing what it had been built to do since before she could name it. This was the country where she stopped being a woman with a dead brother and became simply a creature ascending. She loved it here and hated that she loved it, because loving it meant forgetting, and forgetting was its own kind of betrayal.

Behind her, Ada Quist made sounds Coral had never heard a human being make.

Not crying. Ada didn't cry. But every few steps there came a thin exhalation through clenched teeth — *hnn* — like air escaping a valve, and then the scrape of her boots finding purchase, and then silence, and then, ten feet higher, the sound again. Coral timed her own pace against it without meaning to. Ten feet of progress per breath. At that rate they would reach the mine by the turn of the season, or the turn of the century, whichever came first.

She did not look back. Looking back was an invitation to feel something, and feeling something up here, at eight thousand feet with three days of storm stacked behind the northern peaks like freight cars waiting on a siding, was a luxury with a body count.

"Ranger." Ada's voice, when it came, was scraped down to wire. "I need — a minute."

Coral stopped. Braced herself against the slope with one palm flat on sun-warmed granite, and let the minute happen. She counted it out honestly, sixty full seconds, because she had learned long ago that a lie told to a dying man about how much time he had left came back to you eventually wearing your own face. Below her, Ada leaned against the cut bank of the switchback, hat off, gray-blond hair plastered to her skull, chest working like a bellows with a split seam. Her lips had gone the color of the clay banks along the Salt Fork in August. Her hands, wrapped around a stick she'd insisted on bringing — a surveyor's stave, actually, brass-tipped, salvaged from the abandoned camp along with two days of food and the field notebook Coral now carried inside her coat against her heart — those hands were shaking.

Altitude. Cold nights in a mine adit. Two days of half-rations and fear. Any one of them would slow a healthy woman. Ada was carrying all three plus whatever the badged men had done to her before she ran.

"We're making poor time," Ada said. It wasn't an apology. It was a report, delivered in the flat professional cadence of a woman who had spent her life reducing landscapes to numbers, and Coral felt something twist under her ribs that she refused to examine.

"We're making time," Coral said. "That's all time asks."

Ada looked up at her. Her eyes were pale as weathered bone and twice as steady, and in them Coral saw the thing she'd been avoiding since the adit: trust. Raw, unsecured, extended on credit by a woman who had watched five colleagues marched into the dark by men with concession badges and had survived on nothing but the stubbornness of her own legs. Ada Quist trusted her. Not because Coral had earned it. Because there was nobody else.

Coral turned uphill and kept climbing.

* * *

The trouble with thin air, she thought an hour later, is that it takes away your excuses.

Down low, in the pines, a body runs on habit. You can hate someone with your whole chest and still walk beside them, because the walking happens below the hating, in the dumb loyal machinery of muscle and tendon. But up here the air got stingy. Every breath cost. And when the body started paying full price for oxygen, the mind started itemizing, and what Coral found on the ledger of her own thoughts, in her own handwriting, was this:

*Leave her.*

Not shouted. Not even spoken. Just there, the way a rattlesnake is there, coiled in the shade of the thought process, patient and cold-blooded. *Leave her at timberline with the bedroll and most of the food. She'll live. Probably. Storm's three days out; you could be to the Consolidated and back before it closes the passes. Five lives weigh more than six if the sixth is ballast.*

The snake never raised its voice. That was the horror of it. It sounded exactly like sense. It sounded like the ranger code itself — *keep moving, want nothing, finish the job* — dressed up in emergency clothing. And Coral knew, with the flat certainty of a woman who had spent three years listening to her own worst arguments, that the snake was lying. Not about the arithmetic. About the reason. The reason wasn't speed. The reason was that carrying another person meant carrying another possible death, and Coral Joubert had already buried one death she could have prevented, and she had sworn on the cracked earth over Tomas's empty grave that she would never again stand at the edge of someone else's dying and calculate whether wading in was worth the wet.

She had waded in for Tomas too late. She would not stand dry-shod again.

So she climbed, and she slowed her pace to Ada's pace, and she hated every yard of it, and the hatred was fine. Hatred was fuel. It burned clean enough at altitude.

The mountain changed around them as they rose. The pines thickened first, then twisted, then began to fail — great leaning bristlecones gnarled like arthritic fists, their bark stripped to bare white wood on the windward side, every one of them combed the same direction by forty years of prevailing gales. Then the trees quit altogether, surrendering the slope to krummholz and lichen and loose stone, and the sky opened overhead so wide and so blue it looked less like weather than like verdict. The air went thin and bright and cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. It tasted of iron and distance. Below them Pine Hollow shrank to a green wrinkle in a brown face, and the adit where Ada had nearly died became invisible, swallowed by the indifferent geology, and Coral thought: *that's how it goes. Everything gets swallowed. The trick is deciding what you carry up anyway.*

"Tell me about the maps again," she said.

It wasn't kindness, though it functioned as such. It was strategy. A talking climber keeps breathing in rhythm; a silent one starts rationing air and panicking quietly. But it was also hunger. Coral had spent two days turning Ada's fragments over like stones, looking for what lived underneath, and she wanted the whole shape of it now, while there was nothing to do but climb and listen.

Ada took the bait gratefully. "The aquifer survey," she said, between breaths. "Standard work. We were mapping depth-to-water across the eastern benchlands — the land Calloway's syndicate bought up last year. Twelve sections. They paid good money for it, which everyone thought was strange, because it's marginal grazing at best." Her boots scraped. *Hnn.* "Except it isn't marginal. Or it wasn't supposed to be. The original territorial assessment — 1879, old Hargrave's expedition — put the water table at forty feet under the eastern bench. Flowing sand and gravel, artesian pressure. That's why the syndicate bought it. Water rights in the high desert are worth more than gold and easier to fence."

"And?"

"And we drilled the test holes." Ada's voice flattened further, the way voices do around the truly obscene. "Forty feet. Sixty. Ninety. Dry rock. Solid granite basement, in two of the twelve sections. There is no aquifer under the syndicate land. There never was. Hargrave's map was real — but the copies in the land office, the ones the sales were based on—" She stopped to breathe. "We found Hargrave's original field notes in a Denver archive. The coordinates don't match. Someone redrew the aquifer boundary. Moved it east, onto the syndicate's sections, and moved the *real* water west—"

"—onto unclaimed land," Coral finished.

"Every drop of it." Ada laughed, a single dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "They forged the map to sell themselves worthless dirt, and the actual water sits under public domain, and the only way to take it is to make sure nobody surveys it before the claims revert. Which requires the current survey team to stop existing. Which required us to stop existing." She looked up the slope, at the dark notch of the pass still hours above them. "Five of my people are alive because they're worth more breathing than buried. For now. Until Friday."

Friday. Permit expiry. Coral turned the word over and felt it click into place with everything else — the ledger, the signature, the three years of dust. Renn Calloway hadn't stolen Tomas's water. He'd stolen a *line on a map*, and lines on maps were how the desert decided who lived. Somewhere below them, in offices with glass windows and spittoons, men were moving water with pencils, and men with badges were moving bodies with rifles, and the whole apparatus ran on paper that no ranger could arrest.

But paper could be carried. Paper could be walked out over eleven thousand feet of granite ahead of a storm. Coral touched the notebook inside her coat, and the touch steadied her the way prayer steadies other women.

*Hnn.* Scrape. Silence.

Then not silence.

* * *

It happened on the fourth false summit, on a stretch of scree where the old switchback had collapsed and the route funneled through a chute of loose talon-shaped rock the size of dinner plates, all of it angled at thirty degrees toward a drop that ended four hundred feet below in a fan of shattered stone.

Coral heard it before she understood it: the wrongness of footsteps. Ada's careful metronome rhythm broke — a slide, a scramble, the brass tip of the stave ringing off stone — and then the particular terrible silence of a person who has stopped touching the mountain.

Coral was moving before thought arrived. She dropped her pack where she stood, threw herself down the chute on her heels with both palms skimming the scree, and caught Ada's wrist at the exact moment the woman's boots went out from under her entirely. The jolt tore down Coral's arm and into her shoulder socket like a rifle shot. For one suspended second they were both falling — Coral's boots plowing furrows through the loose rock, gravel hissing past them downhill in a long whispering ribbon, four hundred feet of afternoon opening beneath Ada's swinging body —

—and then Coral's left heel found a buried slab, a fist of solid granite the mountain had hidden under the scree like a card sharp's ace, and the fall stopped.

They hung there. Ada's weight on Coral's arm, Coral's weight on the buried slab, the whole arrangement balanced on a margin thinner than the difference between Friday and Saturday. Below them the scree they'd dislodged went on sliding, ticking and rattling down the face, and the sound took a very long time to fade, and neither woman breathed until it did.

"Don't," Coral said. Her voice came out low and level, which surprised her, because everything under it was screaming. "Don't move. Don't look down. Give me your other hand."

Ada gave it. Her fingers were freezing and trembling and they closed around Coral's forearm with the desperate strength of a drowning woman, and Coral walked herself backward up the chute inch by inch, boots searching, heel-testing, hauling, until Ada's knees came up over the lip of the stable ground and she crawled onto it and lay there flat on her back, chest heaving, hat gone, hair fanned out on the stone, staring at the sky like a woman who had just been introduced to it.

Coral knelt beside her, arm throbbing, heart slamming against her ribs hard enough to hurt, and waited for the snake to speak.

It spoke. Of course it did. *One hundred and ninety pounds of gear between you and the mine,* it whispered, reasonable as a banker. *You've lost an hour. Your shoulder's compromised. The storm doesn't care about your feelings.*

And Coral looked down at Ada Quist — at the pulse hammering in her throat, at the scuffed knuckles, at the surveyor's stave lying three feet away where it had fallen, brass tip glinting — and she answered the snake the only way that mattered.

*I can't carry another death.*

Not wouldn't. Couldn't. She had discovered, hanging off that buried slab with another human being's life running through her fingers like water, that there was a load limit inside her, and Tomas had filled it, and there was nothing left. If Ada died on this mountain — if Coral stood over her body in the snow and calculated what it cost the mission — Coral would not survive the arithmetic. Whatever walked down off the Vermilions afterward would wear her coat and know her horse and be no one.

"You caught me," Ada said finally, to the sky.

"You slipped."

"You could have let go. It would have been — defensible. I'm slowing the operation. I read your reports, Ranger Joubert. I know how you think. Efficiency is a moral language for you."

Coral sat back on her heels. Her shoulder had settled into a deep hot ache that she catalogued and dismissed. Above them the pass glinted, still impossibly far. Somewhere beyond it, five people were breathing in a mine shaft because men with badges found them useful, and somewhere behind them the first outriders of the storm were combing their gray fingers through the far peaks.

"I don't leave people," Coral said. It came out rougher than she intended. "That's not efficiency. That's just the line."

Ada rolled her head sideways and studied her with those bone-pale eyes, and whatever she saw there made her nod once, slowly, the way one surveyor acknowledges another's benchmark. "Then we go slower," she said. "And we go together. Rope us together, if you have it. If I go over an edge, I'd rather take you with me than ask you to choose."

It was the most sensible thing anyone had said to Coral in three years, and it terrified her, and she dug the rope out of her pack anyway.

* * *

They roped up at the base of the final headwall, twenty feet of line between them, Coral leading. The sun had begun its long slide west and the shadows of the bristlecones stretched out across the slope like black rivers flowing uphill. The air was knife-thin now; Coral's breath came in visible rags and each switchback leg cost her a full minute of recovery she pretended not to need. Behind her, Ada climbed with a new economy, having spent her panic, rationing herself like a professional, and Coral hated how much respect that earned.

On the seventh leg, Ada spoke without being asked to.

"The man whose print you found. Calloway." Her voice floated up the rope. "You knew him before this."

Coral's boot paused on the ledge. "What makes you say that?"

"When I showed you the tracks at the camp, you went still. Not thinking-still. Hunting-still. I grew up around hunters." A pause, filled with wind. "He did something to you."

"He did something to my brother," Coral said. "Tomas homesteaded the eastern bench before the syndicate existed. Had a well, a windmill, a claim filed proper. Calloway brokered the paperwork that erased it. When Tomas rode to Dry Wells to contest it, somebody followed him home. They took his cistern apart board by board and drained his windmill tank and left him a hundred miles of desert from the nearest water, in July." The words came out with the smooth worn texture of things said often to no one. "My brother was the best navigator I ever knew. He misjudged one distance. One."

Below her, the rope drew taut and slack with Ada's climbing, a slow pulse, like something alive.

"And you've been tracking him ever since," Ada said.

"Three years. He moves like smoke. Every range I ride to, he's just left." Coral pulled herself over the lip of the switchback and stood a moment, hands on hips, looking up at the last pitch — a ramp of broken granite rising to the notch of the pass, and beyond the notch, faint as a pencil line, the timberline and the high basin where the Consolidated's tailings pile would show itself like a scar. "Until now. Now he's on my mountain, ahead of me, going where I'm going."

"What will you do?"

Coral considered the question honestly, which was a mistake, because honesty at altitude was dangerous. She thought of a boot heel scarred by a farrier's slip, printed in the ash of an abandoned camp. She thought of Tomas's ledger and the signature that wasn't quite a forgery, just a man signing his name to someone else's sin. She thought of the snake, and of the rope around her waist tied to another human being, and of the fact that she had not yet decided — could not yet decide — whether Renn Calloway at the end of this climb was a prisoner, a witness, or a corpse.

"I'll decide when I see him," she said.

Ada was quiet for three steps. Four. Then: "For what it's worth — the night the badged men came, Calloway wasn't with them. He'd been sent down to resupply. When he got back and found the camp empty, he tracked *them*. I saw his fire on the ridge two nights ago. He's up here somewhere, alone, following five hostages he has no legal reason to care about." She pulled herself onto Coral's ledge, breathing hard, and looked up. "Men don't do that unless something's eating them, Ranger. I've surveyed enough ground to know a sinkhole when I'm standing near one."

Coral didn't answer. She was looking at the pass.

The light had changed. The western sky behind them had gone the color of a bruise coming up under skin — a soft yellow-green band sitting on the horizon like a lid on a pot — and the wind, which had blown steadily upslope all day, had died. All of it. The mountain had gone silent in the way mountains go silent before they scream, and Coral had lived her whole life in country that talked this way, and she knew what the silence said.

The front wasn't three days out anymore.

It was tomorrow. Maybe tonight.

"Move," she said, and took the last pitch at a pace that allowed no conversation at all, rope singing between them, Ada scrambling and gasping and keeping up, both of them driven now by the same simple knowledge: whatever waited at the Consolidated mine, whatever waited on the ridge with a scarred boot heel, the storm would reach the pass before they reached anything, and every hour they'd budgeted had just been cut in half.

At the notch, wind hit them like a wall — cold, wet-smelling, wrong for September — and below them, in the blue shadow of the high basin, Coral saw it.

A thread of smoke. Thin, deliberate, signal-fire straight, rising from the black mouth of the old mine workings.

Someone down there wanted to be found.

Or wanted her to come.
