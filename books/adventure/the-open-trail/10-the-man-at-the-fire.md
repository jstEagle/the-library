## 10. The Man at the Fire

The smoke told them before the trees did.

Coral had smelled it for half an hour — woodsmoke with a green edge to it, pine boughs thrown on a fire by someone who wanted to be found or didn't care who came. That alone was wrong. In three years of high-country work she had learned that smoke at timberline meant one of two things: a fool or a trap. She had stopped believing in fools somewhere on the alkali.

"Off the trail," she said quietly, and swung Dandy into the trees.

Ada Quist followed without argument. Three days of climbing had taught the surveyor that Coral's instincts were instruments worth trusting, and her own legs were instruments worth rationing. She rode slumped now, hands hooked in the saddle horn, lips gray at the edges, but her eyes had sharpened instead of dulling as the air thinned — the look of a woman whose mind was the last part of her still running hot.

They tied off below a granite shoulder where the Engelmann spruce gave way to wind-bent krummholz, and Coral went up on foot, rifle unslung, Ada's protests dying behind her in the thin cold air.

* * *

The camp sat in a shallow bowl beneath the last stand of timber, fire blazing in the open like a signal tower, and the man beside it sat with his back against a fallen log and his hands flat on his knees where anyone could see them.

Coral knew him from forty yards. She knew the set of his shoulders, the way he held his head slightly left as if listening to something behind him — a habit he'd had even in Dry Wells, even across a ledger desk, even the day he signed away her brother's water with a flourish and a smile. Renn Calloway. Three years she had built him out of fragments: a boot print in alkali dust outside Las Uvas, a horse sold in Socorro matching his description, a barroom story of a man answering to his name who'd lost everything at cards in El Paso. She had assembled him piece by piece into something enormous, a figure made of her brother's last letter and the sound of Tomas coughing blood into a rag in a room with no water rights and no well.

He was smaller than she'd built him. Thinner. A beard gone half gray at the chin, though he couldn't have been past thirty-five. His coat was survey-issue canvas, patched at the elbow with thread that didn't match. His rifle lay six feet away against a stone, bolt removed, the bolt sitting in plain view on the log beside his hip like a confession.

She walked out of the trees with the Winchester level and did not stop walking until she stood ten feet from him, close enough to see the frost in his eyebrows and the raw, wind-split skin of his knuckles.

"Renn Calloway," she said.

His head came up. And here was the thing she would turn over for years afterward, the thing that would not fit any shape she'd made for it: he looked at her the way a man looks at weather he has been dreading for a long time and is almost relieved to finally stand in.

"Coral Joubert," he said. His voice was rough, underused. "You got my letter."

"I never got any letter."

Something moved through his face — not fear, worse than fear, the specific disappointment of a man whose one prepared speech has just been taken from him. He glanced at the bolt on the log, then deliberately left it there.

"There's coffee," he said. "And I'll answer whatever you ask. But you should sit down, because some of it takes telling."

"I'm not sitting down."

"No," he said. "I suppose you wouldn't." He reached — slowly, telegraphing every inch — toward the coffeepot balanced on a flat rock at the fire's edge, and poured a tin cup, and set it on the ground between them like an offering at a shrine neither of them believed in. Steam rose off it and shredded sideways in the wind coming down off the peaks.

Behind her, branches cracked. Ada Quist came up out of the timber leading both horses, moving with the grim economy of a woman who had decided that whatever was about to happen, she refused to experience it secondhand. She took in the scene — the armed ranger, the man with his empty rifle and visible hands — and her face did something complicated.

"You're the guide," she said. "You're Renn Calloway. You were our guide."

"Yes, ma'am."

"The others are dead because of you."

His jaw worked. "No," he said. "But I ran, and they're not dead yet, and those two things might be the same thing by Friday. So yes, ma'am. In every way that matters right now."

Coral felt the words land in her chest like stones dropped down a well. *Not dead yet.* She had climbed a day and a half on the assumption of corpses, had rationed her hope the way she rationed everything, and this ragged man by the fire had just handed her back a live number: five.

"Talk," she said. "Start with the night they were taken."

* * *

He talked. He kept his hands on his knees the whole time, as if he'd learned that hands were the first thing judged and the last thing forgiven.

"They came at moonset," he said. "Four men, maybe five — I counted four voices. Wearing concession badges, the brass ones with the Consolidated stamp. They had the camp surrounded before Hargrove's dog even woke, and the dog didn't wake, which tells you they'd done this before. They knew our watch rotation. They knew which tent was which. Somebody fed them all of it, and I'll get to that, because it was me."

Ada made a small sound. Coral said nothing. The fire popped and threw sparks up toward a sky going the color of gunmetal in the east.

"They took the five of them down-canyon toward the old mine. Tied, walking, not rough about it — that was the worst part, how businesslike it was. Like moving fence posts. Professor Aldous argued about the permits, legal language, jurisdiction, and the big one just said, 'Permits run out Friday, Professor,' like he was reading it off a schedule. Then Vess — the foreman, name of Vess, red beard, soft voice — turned around and looked straight at me and said, 'You come along too, Calloway. Or we send word ahead about who you used to be.'"

"And you ran," Coral said.

"I ran." No flinch. He met her eyes and let her put the whole weight of it on him. "I'd been waiting three years for somebody to say that sentence. 'Who you used to be.' I knew it would come someday, from somebody, and when it came I did exactly what I always do." He looked at his hands. "I've been running since before you started chasing me, Mrs. Joubert. Running is the one thing I'm good at."

"Then why the fire?" Coral jerked her chin at the blaze. "Running men don't build signals."

"Because I stopped." He said it simply, like reporting a change in the weather. "Got about four miles down the west fork before I understood what I was doing. Every time I've run, somebody pays for it. I ran out on a debt in Yuma and a man took the blame for a theft I committed and did two years for it. I ran out on—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I ran once before on a water deal, and a man died in a dry room in Dry Wells with his ledger in his lap, and his sister has spent three years riding circles in the desert looking for me, and I know that because I've watched her do it. I've seen you twice before tonight. Las Uvas. And the ford below San Andres, eighteen months back. You were asleep at your fire and I stood maybe sixty feet off with your name in my mouth and couldn't make myself use it."

The world went very narrow and very loud. Coral heard her own pulse. Somewhere above them a raven said one flat note and quit.

"You watched me," she said.

"For two days at Las Uvas. I told myself I was making sure you weren't following me, which was a lie, because I already knew you weren't. You were following the trail I'd left on purpose, the false camps, the burned ledgers. I kept leaving pieces of myself where you'd find them because—" His breath caught, and he mastered it the way a man masters a horse that wants to bolt. "Because a hunted thing starts to want the hunt to end, Mrs. Joubert. Even badly. Even at the end of a rope. Anything but another year of listening for your horse."

"You should have stayed asleep at that ford," Coral said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out, how cold, how much it sounded like her father. "You should have woken me. It would have been cleaner."

"It would have been easier," he agreed. "For you. Not for the five people in that mine, and not for the aquifer, and not for every ranch between here and the border that drinks free water. That's why the fire. Because I can't undo what I did to your brother by hanging. Hanging only undoes me. But I was inside the syndicate for four years. I forged the entries. I know the principals' names, I know where the real maps are, I know how Vess thinks and what he's afraid of, and I am the only man on this mountain who can hand you all of it while I'm still breathing." He leaned forward, and the firelight filled the hollows of his face. "So I stopped running. First time in my life. And then I built a fire big enough for you to find, because I knew you'd come. You always come. It's the one thing about you I've never doubted."

* * *

Ada had lowered herself onto a stone near the fire, and Coral realized distantly that the surveyor had positioned herself between the two of them — not blocking, exactly, but present, a witness with weight. Ada pulled off a glove and flexed her fingers toward the heat.

"The ledger," Coral said. "Tomas's ledger. You forged the entries."

"I copied them. From originals Vess supplied. Your brother's claim was valid — first filing, priority date, everything clean. The syndicate needed it to look like he'd defaulted on assessment work, so I wrote the assessment record showing failure to perform, and I wrote it in a hand close enough to the recorder's that nobody questioned it." He paused. "There's something else. About the ledger itself. Did you ever wonder why the pages after his claim were torn out?"

Coral's hand moved before she decided anything, and the ledger was in her coat, wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs where it had ridden for three years, warm from her body like a second heart. She unwrapped it and held it out, and watched Renn Calloway look at her brother's handwriting and go pale all the way to the lips.

"He kept two sets of books," Calloway said softly. "Everyone in the syndicate thought so. Vess sent me to steal the public one. But the private one — the one with the real flow measurements, the spring sources, the test wells — Tomas hid it. Vess tore the pages hunting for it and never found it, and it's the reason they need the survey team silenced, because Ada's crew independently measured the same aquifer Tomas did." He looked up at Ada. "Your professor figured it out, didn't he. That's why they took him alive."

Ada's voice came out thin. "Aldous said the syndicate maps show the aquifer under the east bench. Our instruments say it runs north-northwest, under the unclaimed section. Under commons." She looked at Coral. "If the land reverts Friday with their forged maps on file, they patent dry rock worth nothing — and then they file new claims over the true course using surveys they'll force Aldous to sign. Or bury him."

The wind rose, combed through the krummholz with a sound like surf, and died. Coral became aware that she was still holding the ledger out, and that her arm had not trembled once, and that this frightened her more than trembling would have. Three years she had imagined this moment — the pistol, the confession dragged out of him, the particular justice of the open range where a man who erased her brother answered to gravity and lead. She had rehearsed his begging. She had rehearsed his lying. She had not rehearsed this: a man who confessed freely, who named his own cowardice like pulling teeth from his own mouth, who sat by a signal fire waiting for her with his rifle in pieces because he had calculated, correctly, that she was more useful to the living than to the dead.

It felt like being robbed. Someone had stolen her vengeance and left her something heavier in its place.

"If I put a bullet in you right now," she said, "what happens Friday?"

"Vess holds the mine until the permits lapse. Land reverts. Forged maps stand. Five surveyors become five accidents in collapsed workings, and the syndicate owns the water under half the territory for a generation." He didn't blink. "And you spend the rest of your life knowing the shooting was the easy part and you chose it anyway."

"That's not an answer a guilty man gives."

"It's the only answer an honest one can." He picked up the tin cup of coffee he'd poured for her, still steaming on the ground between them, and held it out. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to be furious at me on Friday instead of today. One favor, Mrs. Joubert. Delay it. I'll carry your anger up that mountain and into that mine and through whatever Vess has waiting, and when it's done — when the names are filed and the warrants are signed and your brother's water belongs to nobody — then you can decide what I owe you, and I won't argue the bill."

Coral looked at the cup. Steam curled off it, bent sideways, straightened. Behind the man, the eastern sky had gone the color of a bruise, and the highest peaks had disappeared into cloud that hadn't been there yesterday, cloud with weight in it, cloud that meant snow early and snow hard.

She thought of Tomas in his dry room, and of Ada's gray lips, and of five strangers breathing in the dark under a mountain, and of the strange arithmetic grief had taught her: that the dead cannot be paid, only the living can, and payment comes due whether or not you feel like working.

She took the cup. Drank. Set it down on the stone between them, deliberate as a signature.

"Storm's coming in early," she said. "We move within the hour. And Calloway — you walk in front, where I can see your hands, and you remember that the reason I haven't shot you is strategy, not mercy. Don't make me confuse the two."

He nodded, and began, without being told, to break down the camp — and Coral stood watching the man she had chased across three years and a thousand miles fold a canvas tarp with careful, scarred hands, and understood that the hardest country she'd ever crossed wasn't behind her.

It was the next twenty-four hours, and every mile of it uphill.
