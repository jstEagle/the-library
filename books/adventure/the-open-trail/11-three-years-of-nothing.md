## 11. Three Years of Nothing

The fire was too small to be honest about. That was the first thing Coral noticed about it — a fire that size said *I am nothing, see me, I am nothing*, and Renn Calloway sat beside it the way a man sits beside a thing he has built to keep himself alive and no larger than it needs to be. His rifle lay across his knees with the action open, the bolt stowed in his coat pocket where she could see him reach for it if he reached at all. His hands were flat on his thighs, palms down, fingers spread, the way you hold them when you want another man's horse not to spook.

Coral did not sit. She stood at the edge of the light with her own rifle at the low ready and let the silence do its work on him while she did hers on her own breathing.

Ada Quist had gone down behind the windbreak of stone with Calloway's bedroll around her shoulders and Coral's canteen in her fist, and Coral had told her — plainly, without softness, because softness would have been a lie between them — *if you hear shooting, you keep walking south and you don't stop for anything that calls your name.* Ada had looked at her with those frost-cracked lips and said, "You're going to kill him," not as a question, and Coral had not answered, because she had spent three years answering that question in the dark of a hundred camps and had never once gotten the same answer twice.

Now here he was. Three years of ridgelines and dry camps and names spoken into barroom air, three years of waking with her hand already closed around a rifle stock, and the whole of it had come down to this: a small dishonest fire at timberline, and Renn Calloway waiting for her like a debt he'd finally decided to pay.

"You found my boot print," he said.

"Left heel. Farrier set the shoe a half-inch wide. He limped off your left side for a season after." She heard her own voice come out level, ranger-flat, and was grateful for it. "You were guiding the survey."

"I was."

"Six people went up that mountain. One came down."

"I know what it looks like." His voice cracked on the last word and he pressed his lips together until it steadied. Up close, in the orange jitter of the flames, he looked older than the man from the Dry Wells land office ledger — grayer at the temples, thinner through the face, with a new scar splitting one eyebrow that hadn't been there when Tomas Joubert was still alive to complain about the weather. But the eyes were the same. Quick, brown, always a little sorry for themselves. She had hated those eyes across three years and a thousand miles and they had not changed enough to make the hating easier or harder. They were just eyes.

"Sit down, Miss Joubert. Please. Your legs are shaking and you know it."

Her legs were shaking. Cold, she told herself. Altitude and cold. She sat anyway, ten feet out, on a rock with the fire between them, because a man who wanted her close was a man who wanted something, and a man who wanted something could be made to give.

* * *

He talked first. She had planned to make him wait, to let the quiet grind on him the way she'd ground on petty thieves across the alkali, but he started talking almost before her boots stopped moving, as though the words had been backed up behind his teeth for years and the sight of her had knocked the cork loose.

"I knew you'd come eventually," he said. "Not here. Not this fast. But eventually. You're the only person in the territory who never quit anything in her life, and I made sure you'd have reason."

"You flatter yourself."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "No, I don't. I've done the arithmetic, Miss Joubert. Every camp I broke before dawn, every alias, every town I rode through without stopping — I did all of it knowing you were somewhere behind me doing exactly what you do. You think I ran from the syndicate? The syndicate pays its people. I ran from you. Because you were the one thing in this world that remembered what I did."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, in the black timber, a branch gave under snow-load with a sound like a far-off shot, and both of them flinched, and Coral hated that they flinched together.

"Tell me about Tomas," she said.

And he did. Not the version she'd built over three years — not the smooth broker in the good coat who'd signed away a dying man's water rights with a gold nib and ridden east. He gave her the other version, the one that began two months earlier, in a back room of the Dry Wells land office with a man named Harrow breathing cigar smoke across the desk.

"They came to me because I was good with paper," Calloway said. "That's all I ever was, Miss Joubert. Good with paper. Harrow's outfit was buying up claims all along the eastern slope — cheap, quiet, fast. Most of them were fair deals, or fair enough. Men who wanted out. Men who'd failed. But your brother's claim—" He stopped. Looked into the fire. Started again. "Your brother's claim was the problem. It sat square on top of the only reliable water between the Vermilion headwaters and the flats. Harrow needed it. Tomas wouldn't sell. So Harrow told me to make him sell."

"How?"

"The ledger. The county abstract. A claim is only as real as its paper, and I was the one who kept the paper true. I entered a lien against the Joubert water rights — a debt Tomas never owed, to a lender who never existed. Interest compounding. Default clause. When the notice came, Tomas rode in to fight it, and by then the abstract showed what I'd written, and the clerk showed him what the abstract showed, and a man with fever in his lungs and no lawyer doesn't fight ink. Ink wins. Ink always wins." His hands had come up off his thighs without his seeming to notice; now he looked down at them as if they belonged to someone else. "He tried to pay. Did you know that? He came back three times with money that wasn't near enough, offering it anyway, asking for time. And each time I stamped the default deeper. The third time he coughed blood onto my desk and apologized for it."

Coral's rifle was in her hands. She didn't remember bringing it up. The muzzle wasn't quite pointed at Calloway — an inch or two of firelight between — but her finger had found the guard and her breath had gone short and thin.

"He walked out of Dry Wells," Calloway went on, quieter, watching the muzzle the way a man watches a snake, "and the fever took him on the trail home, alone, because by then there was no water left to him that the law allowed. And I signed the transfer the next morning and Harrow paid me four hundred dollars and told me I'd done honest work." He laughed then, one dry note with nothing in it. "Honest work. I rode out that same week and I haven't slept a full night since. That was the year you started coming."

"Three years," Coral said. Her voice sounded strange to her, sanded down. "Three years I chased you."

"I know every mile of it. I watched you do it. Santa Rosa, two winters back — you came into the saloon and I was dealing faro under another name and you stood not six feet from me asking after a man matching my description, and I dealt cards with my heart going like a drum and you never looked at my hands." He turned his palms up, showing them, as if the proof lived there. "You know why I never stopped running? Not fear of jail. Jail is a bed and meals. I kept running because the moment I stopped, the moment I let you take me, I'd have to say *why*. And saying why means saying Harrow. Saying Harrow means saying the men above Harrow. And those men—"

He stopped. His eyes went past her shoulder, up the dark shoulder of the mountain, toward the old Consolidated workings somewhere in that blackness, and Coral understood before he said it.

"That's who took them," she said. "Your five surveyors."

"Harrow's riders wear concession badges now. Legal paper, legal guns. They took the team up to the mine the night the storm front showed. I was meant to go with them. I was meant to stand there while Vess explained to Mr. Ostergaard how permits lapse and claims revert and how much simpler everyone's winter gets if the survey never files." His jaw worked. "I ran instead. Second time in my life I've run from something instead of finishing it. First time cost your brother. I told myself this time was different. This time I was running to get help, to get the law, to—" He spread his hands. "Then I heard a horse on the switchbacks and I knew it was you, and God help me, Miss Joubert, I was glad."

* * *

The fire had burned down to a red heart. Coral fed it two more sticks from the pile Calloway had gathered — small ones, keeping his dishonest little flame alive — and made herself think while she did it, because thinking was the one discipline she trusted more than her rifle arm.

She had rehearsed this. Not this fire, this mountain, but the shape of it: the moment she finally had him. In three years of rehearsal the scene had never once gone past the taking. She'd ride him down or corner him or catch him sleeping, and she'd put iron on him, and then — the rehearsals always frayed there, like film burned in the gate. Sometimes in the frayed white space she delivered him to a marshal. Sometimes she asked him questions first, a hundred questions, days of questions. Once or twice — in the worst hours, the hours she didn't examine — the frayed space was very short and very loud.

She had never once rehearsed him confessing freely. She had never budgeted for the way it would feel: not like winning. Like being handed a sack of wet sand when you'd trained your shoulders for a fight.

"You could have written it down," she said. "Any time in three years. A letter to the marshal. A sworn statement. You could have ended yourself and Harrow both with a pen."

"And died in a cell before the ink dried." He said it flatly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Harrow's principals own judges, Miss Joubert. Own two that I know by name. A letter from Renn Calloway, forger, gets read by a man on their payroll and burned, and then I'm dead and the paper trail dies with me and your brother stays robbed forever. No. If it was going to be said, it had to be said to someone they couldn't buy. Someone with a badge and a reputation and a stubbornness they couldn't price." He looked at her across the coals. "It had to be you. It was always going to be you. I just needed to be sure you'd listen before you shot."

"And am I listening?"

"You tell me."

She was. That was the humiliating truth of it. She was listening the way she tracked — every word laid down like a print, weighed, matched against the others. And the prints all agreed. The lien, the dates, the amounts — they fit the ledger page she'd read a hundred times, the one she carried in her coat like a splinter worked too deep to draw. Only a man who'd held the pen could know that the default notice bore the file stamp of the fourth of March and not the first, the clerk's error that had haunted her for three years because it meant the paper had been handled twice.

"You'll swear it," she said. "All of it. Names. Dates. Harrow, and whoever stands above Harrow. Sworn before a federal judge, on the record, where burning it means burning the court."

"Yes."

"And you'll go to prison for it."

"For forgery, fraud, and whatever else they pin on me. Yes." He almost smiled. "Miss Joubert, I've been in prison for three years. It just didn't have walls. Walls would be a relief."

"And the five surveyors." She leaned forward, and the heat of the coals touched her face. "They're the reason any of this happens tonight. Not your conscience. Not me. Five strangers freezing in the Consolidated because your employers needed the land empty. They come down off that mountain alive, all five, before the permits lapse Friday — or I take your confession and feed it to the fire and we settle Tomas's account the old way. Do you understand me?"

For the first time since she'd stepped into the light, Renn Calloway looked afraid — not of her rifle, she thought, but of the size of what she was asking, the weight of it, eleven lives balanced on a forger's nerve.

"I understand," he said. "Vess keeps them in the old hoist house. Four guards on rotation, one lantern, and the shaft's the only way in or out that isn't a day's climb. There's a ventilation raise on the north face — timbered, half-collapsed, but I helped cut it when I was nineteen and I know which sections will hold. Storm closes the passes tomorrow night. After that nobody goes anywhere for a week, and Friday the permits die and the land reverts and Harrow's men walk away rich and legal." He swallowed. "We'd have to move tonight. Both of us. Together."

"Together," Coral repeated. The word sat in her mouth like a stone from a riverbed — smooth, cold, wrong-shaped.

"I'm the only one who knows the raise. You're the only one who can shoot straight enough to matter. And the woman — Quist — she can't climb that face alone, so she comes too, or she freezes, and either way she's your witness and mine." He raised his open hands a little higher, offering them. "Three years you've hunted me for what I did to your brother. Here's the only payment I've got that's worth anything: help me spend it."

Coral looked at him for a long time. The fire had gone down to embers, and in that dying light the man from the ledger became just a tired man with a scarred eyebrow and shaking hands, and the hatred she had carried like a canteen for three years suddenly felt heavy in a new way — not heavier, but *fuller*, sloshing with things she hadn't put in it herself. Nothing about this was clean. Nothing about this was the reckoning she'd ridden a thousand miles to collect. Justice, it turned out, was not a bullet; it was a negotiation conducted over a too-small fire with the man who had killed her brother's future, and the terms were five strangers' lives, and the currency was trust.

She should hate that. She did hate that. And underneath the hating, steady as groundwater, was the plain ranger arithmetic she could not argue with: one gun on this mountain was a funeral; two was a chance.

"Get your gear," she said. "You break trail. I walk behind you with the rifle, and the first print you lay that isn't straight toward the Consolidated is the last thing you ever do. We wake Ada. We move inside the hour."

Calloway let out a breath that seemed to empty him of three years, and reached for his pack — and froze, head tilting, listening.

Coral heard it a heartbeat later. Down-slope, in the black timber, faint but unmistakable on the frozen air: the ring of shod iron on stone. More than one horse. Coming up the switchbacks.

The syndicate hadn't waited for the storm after all.
