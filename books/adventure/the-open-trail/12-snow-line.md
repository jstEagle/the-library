## 12. Snow Line

The snow came the way bad news comes — early, and all at once.

Coral felt it before she saw it, a change in the pressure of the air against her face, a hush falling over the pines below them like a congregation settling. She reined up on the shoulder of the ridge and looked north, where the sky had gone the color of old pewter, flat and heavy from horizon to horizon. No wind yet. That was worse. Wind meant the storm was still deciding. Stillness meant it had already decided.

"That's not supposed to come for another week." Ada Quist sat her borrowed mule badly, hands knotted in the mane, her face still carrying the gray undertone of the mine adit, of three days half-frozen in the dark. "The survey schedule had the front arriving the fourteenth. I checked the barometric readings myself."

"Mountains don't read schedules." Coral turned her horse south, back the way they'd climbed, and stopped. The trail behind them — two days of switchbacks, scree fields, their own tracks stitched across the talus like clumsy handwriting — was already filling with white. Fat flakes drifted down through the timber, unhurried, indifferent, each one landing soft as an apology nobody had asked for. In an hour there would be nothing to show they'd ever passed this way. In two hours, no way at all.

She'd known it could happen. She'd told the marshal it might happen. She'd ridden into the high country anyway because permits expired Friday and five people were somewhere ahead of her in the dark, and because Renn Calloway's boot print had fused two separate hungers into one road. Now the mountain was closing its hand around all of them — her, Ada, Calloway trailing behind on the bay he'd been given at the fire, his wrists still raw from the rope she'd kept on him until yesterday — and the hand was tightening.

"We can't go down," Ada said. It wasn't a question. She'd been reading contour lines since she was old enough to hold a pencil, and even she could see what the snow was doing to their retreat.

"No."

"Then we go up?"

"We go sideways." Coral pointed east along the ridgeline, where the timber thinned toward a granite shoulder she remembered from a summer eleven years ago, when she'd run stock through these passes with Tomas and their father. "There's an old prospector's cabin on the far side of that shoulder. Stone walls, sod roof. My father called it the Hollis cabin, after a man who froze to death in it, which tells you everything you need to know about its hospitality. But stone holds heat, and it's out of the drift line."

"How far?"

"Two hours. Three if the storm closes first."

Ada looked at the sky, then at Coral, and something passed over her face that Coral recognized because she'd worn it herself for three years: the look of a person doing arithmetic with lives instead of numbers. "The other five," Ada said. "If we're sheltering, they're sheltering too. Whoever took them would need shelter."

"Then whoever took them is sitting still until this breaks," Coral said. "And so are we. Nobody climbs anything in a whiteout. Not them, not us. The storm buys everyone time. The question is who spends it better."

Behind them, Calloway said nothing. He'd said very little since the fire at timberline, since his confession spilled out between them like water from a kicked-over bucket — *I forged the entries, I forged them under orders, I've been running from you because stopping means naming names* — and Coral hadn't decided yet what to do with any of it. He rode with his shoulders hunched against the snow, and every time she glanced back she found him watching her with an expression she refused to name, because naming it would mean admitting she'd seen it.

She turned her horse east and led them into the white.

* * *

The cabin was exactly as her memory had kept it, which was to say: barely standing, and stubborn about it.

Stone walls to the eaves, chinked with moss that some dead man had packed by hand a lifetime ago. A sod roof sagging under its own weight but intact. One door of split planks hanging on leather hinges gone stiff as rawhide. Inside: a stone hearth, a bunk frame without straw, a table made of a door laid across two barrels, and a smell of cold ash and mouse droppings and decades of empty.

It was, Coral thought, swinging down and pulling the horses under the lean-to of leaning spruce beside it, the finest palace in the Vermilion Range.

They worked without being told to work. That was the thing about people who'd been cold long enough — they understood the liturgy of warmth without needing a preacher. Ada gathered wood while Coral cleared the flue with a length of green branch, dislodging a decade of bird nests in a smoky avalanche. Calloway carried water from the creek, already slushing at its edges, and stacked it near the fire in dented tins. Within an hour the cabin held a small orange light and the particular blessed roar of green pine burning fast, and the three of them sat around it steaming, thawing, alive in a place named after a frozen corpse.

Ada slept first. She simply folded, mid-sentence, her head tipping against the wall beside the hearth, and Coral caught her before she slid and eased her down onto the bunk, pulling her own blanket over the surveyor's thin frame. Ada's hand stayed curled around her notebook even in sleep, fingers white-knuckled on the cover, as though the forged aquifer maps and her testimony were the only possessions she trusted anyone not to steal.

Calloway sat across the fire, drying his boots, and watched Coral watch Ada.

"You should sleep too," he said.

"I will."

"You won't." He turned a boot toward the flames. His hands were steady now; they hadn't been at the fire, when he'd held them out palms-up like a man showing he carried no weapon. "You haven't slept more than two hours at a stretch since I met you on this mountain, and I'd wager it's been longer than that. Years longer."

"Don't wager on me, Calloway. You'd lose."

"I've been losing bets on you for three years." He said it quietly, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Every time I crossed a county line, I figured the next one would have you behind it. Some nights I slowed my horse on purpose. Did you know that? Some nights I wanted you to catch me."

"I caught you."

"Not all of me." He looked up. Firelight moved in his eyes. "The part that signed Tomas Joubert's ledger — that part you'll never catch, because it doesn't exist anymore. I burned it out of myself somewhere on the Salt Fork, alone, the winter after. What's left is just a man who wants to hand you the names and take whatever you decide he deserves."

Coral stood, abruptly, because sitting across a fire from Renn Calloway while he talked about deserving things was more than the day required. She went to her saddlebags and dug past the spare socks, the ammunition, the dried apples, down to the oilcloth packet at the bottom that she had carried for three years and opened perhaps six times.

Tomas's last letter.

She didn't take it out. She just stood there with her hand on it, feeling the paper's edges through the oilcloth, and hated herself for the ritual of it, and performed the ritual anyway.

"He wrote it the week before," Calloway said behind her. Softly. "Didn't he. The week before I came to Dry Wells with the syndicate's papers."

"You don't get to talk about him."

"No," he agreed. "I don't."

But the words were already in the room, and the fire was warm, and Ada was asleep, and outside the snow fell thicker now, erasing the world one silent inch at a time until there was nothing left anywhere but this stone box, this heat, these two men who had broken her life in different ways and were both, impossibly, asking her for something.

Coral sat back down. She unwrapped the oilcloth.

* * *

The letter was four pages, written in pencil on the backs of assay reports, because Tomas never wasted good paper on words. His handwriting slanted hard to the right, urgent, the letters of a man who wrote the way he rode — fast, leaning forward, certain the destination mattered more than the road.

She hadn't read it aloud since the day it reached her, in a boarding house in Las Animas, three years and a season ago. She'd read it silently a hundred times since. She knew it the way she knew the switchbacks of this range. But knowing a thing and saying it were different countries, and she had never once crossed between them.

"Why tonight?" Calloway asked, when she'd smoothed the pages on her knee.

"Because tomorrow we climb to a mine full of men who kill for paper," Coral said. "And because if the storm takes the cabin, or the mine takes me, or Friday takes us all, this letter dies with me, and it shouldn't. It wasn't written for me alone. He just never got the chance to send it to anyone else." She looked at the fire rather than at Calloway. "So you're going to sit there and listen, and you're going to hear all of it, and that's your punishment starting now. Not a bullet. This."

He nodded once. He did not look away, and she hated him a little for that, and respected him a little for it too, and filed both feelings away where she kept everything she couldn't afford to carry.

She read.

"*Dear Cora —*" and her voice caught on the word, because nobody living had called her that since, "*— by the time you read this I'll likely be a fool in one direction or another, so pick whichever fool you prefer and love me accordingly.*"

She read about the water. About the seep Tomas had found in the red rock country south of Dry Wells, the one that ran cold in August when every other spring within forty miles went to mud. About how he'd filed his claim, sunk his shaft, built the little stone headgate he was so proud of, and how the broker Renn Calloway had come with papers so official-looking that Tomas had signed without reading past the second line — because that's what honest men did, Coral, they believed the government's ink meant something.

"*They tell me I sold my rights fair and square,*" she read, and her voice went flat and hard as the alkali flats, "*but Cora, I never sold the water. I sold a piece of paper I didn't understand. There's a difference, and I think they're counting on men like me never learning it.*"

She read about the ledger — how Tomas had started keeping his own record of every transaction, every signature, every date, because a man with nothing left but a grievance ought to at least keep it accurate. How he'd traced the forged entry to Calloway's hand. How he'd written to the territorial land office and gotten back a form letter.

"*If you come looking for me,*" she read, and here her voice finally broke, the way it always broke, the way it had broken alone in a hundred rooms, "*don't come angry. Come careful. And Cora — if it turns out I'm wrong about all this, if I'm just a man who lost his water fair, then bury me at the ridge above the seep anyway, because it's the only piece of this desert that ever gave anything back.*"

The fire popped. Snow hissed faintly against the stones of the wall, a sound like the mountain breathing.

"*Love, Tomás. Tell the desert I tried.*"

Coral folded the pages along their worn creases. Her hands were steady. Her eyes were wet, and she let them be wet, because Ada was asleep and Calloway was watching and three years of keeping her grief locked in a box smaller than a letter had bought her exactly nothing — not justice, not peace, not even the comfort of believing she'd been strong. Strength, it turned out, was just another kind of running.

"He knew," Calloway said at last. His voice was rough. "When I came to his claim. He knew something was wrong with the papers. He asked me — God help me, he asked me straight out, *Mister, is this honest?* And I told him yes, because the syndicate's man was standing behind me with a shotgun, and because telling the truth would have cost me everything I thought I wanted." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "He believed me. That's the part I can't — he *believed* me, Joubert. He shook my hand."

"I know," Coral said. "He wrote about the handshake."

"I've thought about that handshake every day for three years."

"And I've thought about the letter every day for three years, and neither of us has done a damn thing worth the thinking." She stood, and crossed to him, and held out the folded pages. "Take it."

He flinched as if she'd offered him a snake. "No."

"You'll testify Friday. You'll say all of this in front of a federal judge, with your name attached, and when you falter — and you will falter, Calloway, everyone falters — I want you holding this letter. I want you to remember that the man whose future you destroyed wrote *tell the desert I tried*, and I want you to try harder than he got the chance to." She pushed the pages into his chest until his hands came up to take them. "That's not forgiveness. Don't confuse it. That's ammunition."

He held the letter like it weighed more than the rifle he'd surrendered at the fire. "What happens to me after Friday?" he asked. "After the testimony. After the warrants."

"That depends on whether the judge believes you."

"And you? What do you believe?"

Coral went to the window — a gap in the stonework glazed with ice — and looked out at the storm. The snow had buried the world completely. Somewhere south, their back-trail was gone. Somewhere north, five strangers waited in a mine, and men with concession badges waited with them, and Friday came on regardless, patient as gravity.

"I believe," she said slowly, "that I spent three years wanting to put a bullet in you, and now I'm trusting you to help me save five people I've never met, and I don't know which version of me is the fool. Maybe both. Maybe that's what survival costs — you stop being sure of your own heart, and you pay in honesty instead." She turned back to him. "Sleep, Calloway. Tomorrow we count heads at the Consolidated, and I need your eyes fresh and your nerve steady, and if either fails me, the letter goes back in the oilcloth and you go back to being the man I hunted."

He lay down by the fire with Tomas's letter tucked inside his coat, over his heart, like a man wearing armor made of someone else's grief.

Coral took the first watch. Outside, the snow fell and fell, deep and silent and total, closing over the high country like a page turning. Whatever tracks they'd left, whatever trails led home — erased. There was only forward now. Only the mine, and the storm, and Friday coming on like a debt collector.

Near dawn, Ada woke screaming the names of the five who'd been taken, and Coral held her until the shaking stopped, and outside the window the snow kept falling, and somewhere below them in the dark, the Consolidated mine waited with its single shaft and its badged men and its stolen surveyors, counting the same hours they were counting, waiting for Friday to make the killing legal.
