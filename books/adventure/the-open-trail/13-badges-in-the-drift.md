## 13. Badges in the Drift

The snow stopped an hour before dawn, and Coral woke to a silence so complete it felt like pressure against the eardrums. She lay still on the bunk she'd claimed, listening past the tick of the cooling stove, past Calloway's even breathing on the floor by the hearth, past Ada's shallow sleep in the corner — listening for what was missing.

Wind. The wind had stopped.

In three years of riding empty country she had learned that silence after a storm was never empty. It was a held breath. Somewhere out under that white ceiling, anything that had been moving all night would now be standing still too, and both of them would be listening.

She rose without sound, dressed in the dark by touch, and went to the window. The shutter opened onto a world remade. The cabin's yard, the woodpile, the dead corral fence — all of it buried under two feet of new snow, smooth and blue-gray in the pre-dawn light, unbroken as fresh paper. Behind her, up-slope toward the pass, their back-trail was gone. Ahead, down-canyon toward the valley, the same.

They were sealed in. Or sealed off. She hadn't decided which yet.

Calloway stirred when she fed the stove. He'd learned to wake quietly over the years, she supposed, the way any hunted thing did. He looked at her face and then at the window and asked nothing, which she appreciated. Ada woke slower, blinking, her frostbitten fingers curled into her sleeves, and Coral put coffee into her hands and watched her hold them around the tin cup like something living.

"We're out of time to be careful," Coral said finally. "Permits lapse Friday. That's four days. Mine's a day's climb down and east if the drifts let us through. But we can't move eleven people" — she caught herself, corrected it — "we can't move anyone until I know whether those riders doubled back last night."

"You think they'll come," Ada said. Not a question.

"I'd come." Coral turned her cup slowly on the table. "Two men rode up this canyon asking questions at every shanty between here and the timberline. They didn't find us because we were already inside when they passed. They'll figure the storm drove whoever's up here somewhere, and there's exactly one roof on this side of the ridge. They'll come back."

* * *

They came back at mid-morning.

Coral heard the horses before she saw them — the particular muffled crunch of hooves in deep snow, arrhythmic, laboring. She was at the window with the shutter cracked to a finger's width, and Calloway was beside her without being told, and Ada had gone gray-faced and still against the far wall with her hands pressed flat to the logs as if she could push herself through them.

Two riders. Dark coats, faces half-lost behind wool scarves, but the badges glinting at their collars — small brass rectangles stamped with the Consolidated's winged pick. Concession men. The same kind that had walked into the survey camp at night, according to Ada's fragments, wearing the same metal.

One of them dismounted at the edge of the yard and stood looking at the cabin for a long moment. The other stayed mounted, hand resting easy near his hip. Not drawing. Just near. Everything about them said men who expected compliance rather than resistance, and that expectation, Coral thought, was the only weapon she had.

"They'll knock," Calloway breathed. "They always knock first. Badges are theater — they want you to open the door yourself."

"Then we let them."

"Coral." His voice dropped lower. "If they see my face—"

"They won't see your face." She turned from the window and looked at him, and something in her expression must have settled the matter, because he nodded once and moved to the space behind the door, rifle in his hands though she'd made him swear it would stay unloaded until she said otherwise. To Ada: "You lie on the bunk. Blankets to your chin. You've been sick for days and you look it, which is the truth, and the truth is the best lie we have."

Ada swallowed hard and climbed onto the bunk.

The knock came — three blows, unhurried, knuckles not fists. A man who knocked like that had done it a hundred times and been answered a hundred times.

Coral opened the door to the width of her own shoulders and no more, letting the cold pour through, letting them see her: a woman alone in a high cabin, rifle leaning against the jamb where a rifle ought to lean, eyes flat as the alkali flats she'd crossed a lifetime ago.

The man on the ground was broad, weathered, with a beard going iron at the jaw and the badge pinned crooked above his heart. He smiled the way men smile at bank tellers.

"Afternoon, ma'am. Sorry to trouble you. We're riding for the Consolidated, checking on strays from the survey outfit that got caught in this blow." He tipped his head toward the white silence behind him. "You seen anybody? Six of 'em, government men, come through maybe a week back."

"One woman," Coral said. "Sick. That's all."

The rider's gaze moved past her shoulder into the dim interior, and Coral let him look. Let him see the single bunk occupied, the second bunk stripped to its ropes, the floor clear of gear because she'd spent the hour before dawn hiding everything that multiplied people — extra cups, spare blankets, the third set of tracks in the ash. Let him see a cabin that held one life and no more.

"Sick, is she." His voice stayed pleasant. "Alone up here?"

"My husband went down to Dry Wells for the doctor eight days ago." She let just enough weariness into it. "Storm closed the pass behind him. So now it's me and her and whatever's in the cache, and I'd take it kindly if you kept moving, because I don't have warmth or food to spare for strangers."

It came out of her smooth and cold and perfectly shaped, and some detached part of Coral — the part that had spent three years reading sign and running arithmetic on other people's lies — stood back and marveled at it. She had planned to kill these men if it came to that. She had rehearsed it in the dark: two shots, two bodies, the arithmetic of survival. And instead here she was doing *this*, and it was working, and she could feel it working, the way you feel a horse settle under you.

The mounted man shifted his weight. "Ma'am," he called past his partner, "mind if we water our horses? There's a spring—"

"There isn't. It froze solid a week ago. You can melt snow if you want to sit around waiting on it, but I'd sooner you didn't, with sickness in the house."

The bearded one laughed, easy and dismissive. "Fair enough, ma'am. Say — the survey party. Government men travel heavy. Instruments, tents, mules. If they got caught out in this—" he gestured at the canyon walls, the drifts piled against the dead trees like frozen surf "—well. You know how it is up here. White comes down and takes its due."

And there it was. Underneath the pleasantness, offered like a coin flipped, the shape of what he wanted her to say. *Took its due.* He wasn't asking if she'd seen them. He was offering her a story, the way you offer a dog a scrap, to see if she'd swallow it whole or sniff it first.

Coral felt the moment balance on its point. One wrong note and the pleasantness would drop away and there'd be two rifles and a sick woman and a man hiding behind a door, and none of her shooting would change what happened after.

So she gave them the story back, but bent — bent just enough to make it hers.

"If you mean the ones camped at the basin below the switchbacks," she said, "I saw their fire go out night before last. Never came back. Storm took the whole slope off the cornice that same night — you'd have heard it if you were anywhere on this side. Sounded like the mountain coughing." She shook her head slowly. "I figured that's what happened to them. This country doesn't leave much to find when it does that. Come spring, maybe. Spring tells everything."

She watched the words land. Watched the bearded man exchange a glance with his partner — a quick flicker, there and gone, but she'd spent three years learning to read men across distances longer than this, and she read it now: relief. Not suspicion. *Relief.* They wanted the story to be true. They needed it to be true, because five dead surveyors in a snowslide explained everything — the permits lapsing, the land reverting, the syndicate holding clean paper on ground that would otherwise carry federal witnesses.

They wanted to believe her more than they wanted to check her.

"Snowslide, huh," the mounted one said. He wasn't smiling anymore, but neither was he frowning. He was doing arithmetic, and Coral could almost hear the numbers coming out right. "That basin's bad ground. Cornice above it the whole length."

"Worst on the range," Coral agreed. "I told my husband the government should post a warning. Nobody listens to women about mountains."

The bearded man chuckled at that, tipped his hat brim, and turned back toward his horse. "Well. Sorry for your trouble, ma'am. Hope your husband makes it back safe. We'll leave you to your patient."

"Much obliged."

Coral stood in the doorway and watched them wheel their horses down-canyon, watched the two dark shapes shrink against the white, and did not close the door until they were specks. Then she shut it, leaned her forehead against the cold log wall, and discovered that her hands were shaking — not from fear, she realized, but from something closer to vertigo.

She had talked two armed men out of a killing without spending a bullet. Three years she had carried a gun the way other people carried grief, and it had never once done what her voice had just done in the space of ten minutes.

"What did you tell them?" Calloway asked from behind the door, low.

"The truth, mostly." She straightened. "Bent where it needed bending. They wanted a snowslide, so I sold them one."

"And they bought it?"

"They *wanted* to buy it. That's different, and it's better." She turned to face him and Ada, who had sat up on the bunk with her blanket pooled in her lap, watching Coral with an expression Coral couldn't name — something between awe and unease, as if she'd just seen a familiar animal do an unfamiliar trick.

"It won't hold forever," Coral went on. "A day, maybe two, until someone walks that basin and finds no slide debris where I said it was. So we move tomorrow at first light, while they're still telling each other the story I gave them. Down-canyon, along their own tracks — the drifts will hide ours inside theirs. And then—"

She stopped. Because she heard herself saying the next part and understood what it meant.

"Then we follow them," she said. "All the way to the Consolidated."

* * *

They left at gray light, three figures moving down a canyon the color of bone.

Coral led, breaking trail to the depth of her knees in places, following the churned line the two riders had cut the morning before. Their horses' tracks ran down the canyon floor like a stitched seam, and Coral kept them just inside it, her own prints falling into the disturbed snow where they'd vanish among hoof-churn. Behind her came Calloway with the sled rope over his shoulder hauling their cached supplies, and behind him Ada, moving better than Coral had dared hope, her breath hanging in ragged flags.

By midday the canyon widened, the walls fell back, and the tracks they followed joined a broader road — packed, used, running east toward a black scar on the mountainside that Coral could see from six miles off. Timber frames. A headframe silhouetted against the sky. Waste rock spilling down the slope like the mountain had been gutted and left open.

The Consolidated.

They halted in the shadow of a granite spur, and Coral glassed the mine with Calloway's binoculars while the other two waited in the lee of the rock, steam rising off their shoulders. Smoke from the cookhouse. Mules in a pole corral. Men moving between buildings — she counted seven, eight, nine, and stopped counting because the number kept growing. And at the mouth of the main adit, a timbered structure with a heavy door and, unmistakable even at this distance, lamplight behind the ventilator grate.

Lamplight meant occupancy. Occupancy meant the surveyors might still be breathing.

"There," Calloway said quietly beside her, and pointed down-slope. Not at the mine. At the tracks.

The two riders had descended toward the complex, but fifty yards short of the cookhouse they'd been met by a third man on foot, and the three of them stood together in the snow for a long while — long enough that their boots had trampled a dark patch — and then all three had turned and walked, not ridden, toward the office building at the mine's edge.

Reporting. Coral felt her pulse slow and sharpen, the old hunting rhythm settling over her. They were reporting that the trail was cold, that the mountain had taken its due, that the paper would fall clean on Friday.

Which meant that right now, somewhere in that office, men were relaxing.

"Tonight," she said. "While they believe their own story. Tomorrow they start wanting proof, and proof is the one thing we can't survive them finding."

She passed the glasses to Calloway and studied the ground instead — the approach, the cover, the line of the tramway trestle that ran from the headframe down toward the ore bins, its shadow a long dark stripe across the snow. A route. There was always a route; the country offered them to anyone patient enough to read for them.

But as she traced it, her eye snagged on something else. Near the adit, half-buried by drifting waste rock, a secondary opening — a ventilation raise, by the angle of it, timbered and grated, climbing the slope above the main entrance. And beside it, half-hidden in the shadow of the timbers—

Boot prints. Many. Going in. None coming out.

Coral lowered the glasses and found that her hands were steady now, steadier than they'd been in three years, and that the steadiness frightened her more than the shaking ever had. Because she understood suddenly what she was looking at, and what she was looking at was not a rescue problem.

It was a ledger. Five lives held in the dark against a Friday deadline, and every hour she spent planning was an hour the interest accrued.

"Eleven of us," she said aloud, to the rock, to the cold, to whatever part of herself was still listening. "Five down there, three up here, and a mountain full of men paid to keep it that way."

Behind her, Ada spoke, very quietly. "My brother-in-law worked a claim outside Silverton. He always said the desert gives back exactly what you bring to it." A pause. "What are you bringing, Mrs. Joubert?"

Coral didn't answer. She was watching the lamplight behind the ventilator grate, and thinking about a boot heel scarred by a farrier's mistake, and about a brother who had believed water belonged to everyone, and about the strange, unsettling discovery of the past two days — that she could win with words, that she could bend men with nothing but a story shaped right, and that this power frightened her worse than any gunfight, because guns only killed once.

Words, she thought, watching the smoke rise thin and straight from the cookhouse. Words could build things that lasted.

Or burn them down.

She pulled the glasses back to her eyes and began, methodically, to count guards.
