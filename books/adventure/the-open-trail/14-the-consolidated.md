## 14. The Consolidated

The mine announced itself before they saw it. A smell came down the canyon on the morning wind — coal smoke and mule dung and the particular sourness of men who had lived too long in one place — and Coral reined up on the last bench of the trail and let her horse blow while she studied what the badged riders' tracks had promised.

The Consolidated sat in the crook of the canyon like something that had tried to crawl out of the mountain and been beaten back. A headframe of gray timber, its sheave wheel long since seized with rust. A tramway of rotting ore cars on rails that vanished into a tunnel mouth boarded over with fresh planks — fresh, that was the thing, fresh lumber pale as a scar in all that gray. A bunkhouse with a stovepipe smoking. A corral with eight horses standing hipshot in the wind. And strung along the canyon rim above it all, catching the flat light, a line of wire with tin cans rattling on it.

An alarm line. Someone had strung cans on wire like a woman protecting a henhouse, and Coral found she respected the impulse even as she catalogued its failures.

"You're smiling," Ada said beside her. The surveyor's lips were still cracked from the adit, her voice still rough, but three days of Coral's coffee and Calloway's trail-finding had put something back in her spine. She sat a horse like a woman who had learned in the last week that sitting a horse was a skill worth learning.

"Counting," Coral said. "Eight horses. That means eight men, maybe nine if one's walking. One entrance that I can see, and they've boarded it, which means they're not working the mine. They're keeping something in it."

"Or someone."

"Or someone." She turned in the saddle. Calloway sat his mule ten feet back, the way he'd learned to — close enough to be useful, far enough that her hand couldn't reach him without her deciding to reach. Three days of that geometry. Three days of him talking when talk was needed and going quiet when it wasn't, and Coral still could not decide which she hated more: the man who'd signed Tomas's claim away, or the man who was trying so carefully to be something else in front of her.

"The ventilation raise," he said. "I told you. Every deep mine has one. The Consolidated flooded in '79 and they drove a raise up the east wall to drain bad air. It comes out on the bench above the tunnel."

"And you know this because?"

"Because I surveyed it." He said it flat, no apology in it. "Before I learned there was more money in moving paper than moving rock. I was twenty-two. I thought the mountain was the most honest thing I'd ever met."

Coral looked at him a moment longer than she meant to. Then she looked back at the mine.

* * *

They left the horses in a draw a mile out, hobbled, and came in on foot along the creek bottom where the willows were thick enough to hide a crawling woman. Coral went first. She always went first; it was not bravery, it was arithmetic — if the lead person was shot, only one person was shot.

The canyon narrowed. The creek ran shallow over red gravel, loud enough to cover their movement, and Coral moved through the willow shadow with her boots finding the quiet places between stones, the way her father had taught her before her father had taught her anything about grief. Behind her, Ada's breathing was controlled and deliberate, a woman managing fear like a budget. Behind Ada, Calloway's moccasined feet — he'd wrapped his boots with strips of blanket two days ago, without being asked — made almost no sound at all. A man who had spent three years being followed knew how to follow.

The tunnel mouth came up on their left. Coral lay in the willow stubble and watched it through an hour of nothing.

What she saw: two men at the mouth, rifles across their knees, playing cards on an upturned crate. A third who walked a circuit — corral, bunkhouse, headframe, tunnel — with the bored regularity of a man doing a job he'd stopped thinking about. Smoke from the bunkhouse chimney. A cook clattering pans. And from somewhere inside the boarded tunnel, when the wind died and the creek quieted, a sound.

A voice. Muffled, distant, human. Then another answering it.

Alive. Five someones, alive, behind fresh planks and two men with rifles.

Coral let out a breath she'd been holding since the adit. Then she made herself stop feeling it, because feeling it would make her stupid, and she lay back in the willows and did the rest of the arithmetic.

Two at the door. One walking. That left four or five unaccounted for — the foreman, whoever else. Eight horses, eight or nine men. The boarded door was the only way in she could see from the creek, which meant the syndicate had chosen their prison well: one mouth, timbered shut, guarded. The ventilation raise on the east wall would be the way the guards themselves came and went, or it was choked, or it was watched. She wouldn't know until she climbed it.

And above all of it, ticking like a clock in a quiet room: the permits. Friday. Two days. The syndicate didn't need to kill anyone. They only needed to keep five men breathing behind a plank wall until the paperwork expired and the land reverted, and then the water under it — the real water, the water Ada's instruments had found — would belong to whoever held the concession badges. The murder, if it came to murder, would be a formality committed by a calendar.

Coral had spent three years believing that the worst thing a man could do was sign a paper that killed someone slowly. Lying in the willow stubble, she understood she had been naive. The paper was only ever the first instrument. The rest was men with rifles and tin cans on wire.

* * *

They were halfway back to the horses when the rider found them.

Not found them — found the trail, or thought he did, because the man came down the canyon trail at a trot with his collar up against the wind and his eyes on the creek bottom, and Coral had exactly enough time to pull Ada and Calloway off the trail and into the rocks before he drew level.

He was young. That was the first thing. Twenty, maybe, with a jaw that hadn't finished deciding what it was, and a rifle that sat in his scabbard like a tool rather than a weapon. He reined up where their trail crossed his, frowned at the gravel, and Coral had her hand on her Colt and her breath stopped and three years of arithmetic running — one man, close range, but the shot would bring nine —

"Rider!" The call came from up the canyon. "Vess wants you back — they're moving tonight!"

The young man straightened. Whatever he'd half-seen in the gravel, he decided against it, and he put his horse up the trail at a canter and was gone.

Coral let her breath out slowly. Ada's hand was on her arm, gripping hard. Calloway hadn't moved at all.

"They're moving tonight," Ada whispered.

"Or they want someone to think so." Coral stood out of the rocks, brushing gravel from her knees. "Either way, we're out of time for being careful. I'm going to walk in the front and see what they'll sell me."

"You can't —"

"I can." She looked at Ada, then at Calloway. "Because they don't know your face yet, Ada, and Calloway —" she paused, and the pause cost her something — "Calloway, they know yours. If Vess is who I think he is, he's been paying you. Which means when I walk in alone, I'm a stranger with a ranger's star and no backup, and the first thing a foreman does with a stranger is try to buy her. I want to hear the number."

"And if the number's good?" Calloway asked quietly.

Coral looked at him. "Then I'll know exactly how small a man thinks my brother's life is. That's worth the walk."

* * *

She went in the way she'd decided to go in: down the middle of the canyon trail, on foot, hands empty and visible, star on her coat, at a pace that said she had all the time in the world and knew something they didn't.

The card players saw her at two hundred yards. One stood, rifle coming up, and Coral stopped walking and let him look at her. Let him count her hands. Let him do the arithmetic she wanted him to do: a woman alone, unarmed, walking toward nine rifles, is either a fool or has already done the thing you'd do it to stop her from doing.

"Territorial ranger," she said, when she was close enough not to shout. "Coral Joubert. I'm here about five surveyors."

The standing man looked at his partner. His partner looked at the bunkhouse. And from the bunkhouse doorway came a man Coral knew at once would be Vess, because he was built like the mine itself — squat, timbered, load-bearing — and because he was wiping his hands on a rag as he came, the way a man does when he wants you to think he's just finished an honest day's work.

"Ranger." Vess's voice was gravel over gravel. "Long way from anywhere. You lost?"

"Looking for the federal survey team. Six permits, filed out of Dry Wells in September." She let her eyes travel the canyon — the headframe, the boarded tunnel, the corral, the tin cans on the wire — slowly enough that he'd know she'd counted everything. "Their instruments are cached at their high camp. Their tents are still pitched. Their tracks stop at a mine adit. And their guide's boot prints come down off this mountain and end at your corral."

Something moved behind Vess's face — not guilt, nothing so useful as guilt. Calculation. She watched him weigh her, and she watched him arrive at the same number she'd arrived at: one woman, and Friday coming.

"You'd be the Joubert woman," he said. "The one that's been hunting Calloway."

The name in his mouth did something to her spine, but she kept her hands loose and her face like the alkali flats: flat, white, giving nothing back. "You've met him."

"Met him, paid him, and been double-crossed by him, if you can believe it." Vess spat, expertly, into the creek. "Man took syndicate money to lose that survey in the high country and then got religion, or got scared, or got something. Left us holding five mouths we got to feed." He smiled, and it was a good smile, a practiced one, the smile of a man who had sold things to people who didn't want them for thirty years. "But that's not your trouble, Ranger. Your trouble is jurisdiction. This is private claim land. You got no paper that says you can be standing here."

"I've got a star and a missing-persons report filed by a federal marshal. That's paper enough to walk this canyon. Whether it's paper enough to open that tunnel depends on what I find behind the planks."

"Behind the planks is mine workings. Flooded in '79, condemned twice, full of bad air. Be a fool's errand."

"Then you won't mind if I'm a fool."

They looked at each other across ten feet of red gravel, and Coral understood that they were not talking about the tunnel at all. They were talking about Friday. He knew she knew. She knew he knew she knew. The whole canyon was balanced on two days of paperwork, and both of them were pretending the rifles were about something else.

Vess sighed, and the sigh was the most honest thing he'd done. He folded the rag, tucked it in his belt, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into something almost gentle, the register of a man making an offer he'd made before, to other people, in other canyons.

"Ranger. You've been on this mountain a long time. You look tired, and you look like a woman who's been chasing something for years and hasn't caught it." He let that sit. He knew about her. Of course he knew about her; Calloway's flight had been syndicate business, and syndicate business kept files. "Now, I'm not going to pretend I don't know why. And I'm not going to insult you by pretending those surveyors matter to you more than your own account does."

"They don't matter to me more than anything," Coral said. "They matter to the law."

"The law." Vess nodded slowly, as if she'd said something wise. "Here's the law as it actually runs, Ranger. Friday, those permits lapse. Saturday, this canyon belongs to the Consolidated Concession, all of it, everything under it and everything on it, and any persons found on it are trespassers, and trespassers are my problem to manage. Now —" he raised one hand, forestalling her — "I know what you're thinking. I know what you came down that mountain for. So let me make it simple."

He named a number.

It was a good number. It was, Coral thought with a strange, floating clarity, more money than she had earned in three years of ranger's pay, more than Tomas's water rights had ever been worth on paper, more than the ledger in her saddlebag represented in a lifetime of Tomas's careful, doomed arithmetic. It would buy a ranch. It would buy a life with fences and a roof and no reason ever again to keep moving. It would buy the stillness she'd been fleeing, gift-wrapped.

And it was nothing. It was insultingly, obscenely nothing. It was five men behind planks and one woman's brother dead of thirst in a canyon because a signature moved, and all of it priced out at a number a foreman could authorize without telegraphing his principals, a number that said: this is what your grief costs at retail.

Coral found, to her surprise, that she wasn't angry. She was something colder and more useful than angry. She was a woman reading a ledger, and the ledger told her everything: that they feared her enough to pay, that they feared her alone, that they did not know Ada was alive in the rocks above the canyon, that they did not know Calloway was with her, and that they thought the whole weight of the law was one tired woman who could be bought for less than a season's ore.

"That's your number," she said.

"That's my number."

"For five men."

"For five men, their gear, your silence, and your ride south. And Ranger —" the gentleness again, and beneath it, for the first time, a hairline of threat, like quartz in granite — "I'll make it once. Men who come down a mountain alone to bargain don't usually have anybody waiting on 'em. But men who go back up a mountain after refusing money —" he shrugged, a small, load-bearing shrug — "mountains are hard on people this time of year."

Coral looked at him for a long moment. Then she did the thing she had decided to do in the willows, the thing that had been forming in her since the alkali flats three years ago, the thing that was not the gun and was not the grave and was not the road: she smiled.

"Mr. Vess," she said, "you've made me a counteroffer I'd like to consider overnight. I'll camp at the mouth of the canyon. I'll give you my answer in the morning."

Vess studied her, hunting the trick in it, and finding none that he could see — because there wasn't one, or not the kind he knew how to hunt. "Morning," he said finally. "Sunup. And Ranger — the offer dies with the sun."

"Everything dies with the sun," Coral said. "That's why we count the days."

She turned and walked back down the canyon the way she'd come, hands empty, back straight, past the card players and the corral and the boarded tunnel mouth — and as she passed the planks, close enough to touch them, she heard it again, faint through the timber: a voice, a man's voice, hoarse but carrying, singing something low and tuneless and stubborn, and another voice telling it to shut up, and a third laughing.

Alive. All of them, still singing behind the wood.

Coral did not turn her head. She walked on, past the tin cans rattling on their wire, out of rifle range, up the creek bottom into the willows where Ada and Calloway lay watching, and only when the canyon bent and the mine was gone did she let her face change.

"Tonight," she said quietly, crouching between them, pulling the map of the canyon out of her own memory and laying it down in words. "He gave us tonight. He thinks he bought until morning, and men who think they've bought something sleep heavy." She looked at Calloway. "The ventilation raise. How far from the tunnel to the bench above it, and how bad is the air?"

Calloway was already unbuckling his bedroll, already moving, the hunted man and the useful man occupying the same body without visible strain. "Six hundred feet, near vertical, timbered every twenty. Air's bad below the hundred-foot mark — you'll smell it before it hurts you. We go up with candles. When the candles gutter, we climb faster." He looked at her, and for once he didn't look away. "There's a timber at the top they'd have wedged shut. I can open it. I built the wedges."

"You built the wedges," Coral repeated. Three years of hunting this man, and here he was, offering her the key to his own architecture. She should have felt triumph. What she felt was the weight of the rope she'd have to trust him at the end of.

"Ada." She turned to the surveyor. "When we open that door, five men are going to come up a raise into freezing air, half of them half-starved, and every one of them is going to want to know what's happening. You're the only one they'll believe. You're the one who has to get them up that mountain before Vess wakes up and counts his prisoners."

Ada nodded once. Her hands were shaking, and she looked at them with distaste and made them stop.

"Then we go at moonset," Coral said. "Two hours. Eat, check your rope ends, and don't talk past that."

She sat back against the willow trunk with her rifle across her knees and looked up the canyon at the mine, where the bunkhouse windows glowed yellow and the smoke stood up straight in the still air, and she thought about the number. She would carry that number a long time, she suspected — not the figure itself but the smallness of it, the ease of it, the way Vess had reached for it without even rising from his chair. That was the true ledger, the one Tomas had died trying to keep: not what things cost, but what men believed they could get them for.

The moon went down. The mine lights went out one by one, the way lights do when men sleep heavy on money already spent. And in the black willows, Coral Joubert checked her rope, checked her candles, and started toward the mountain.
