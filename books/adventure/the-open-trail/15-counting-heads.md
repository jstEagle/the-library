## 15. Counting Heads

The Consolidated mine had been dead for twenty years before anyone decided to make it useful again.

Renn Calloway knew that the way he knew his own hands — by having helped kill it. Not with powder or picks, but with paper. In the spring of '79 he had sat in a Phoenix office with a fountain pen and a stack of assay reports and written the numbers that made the Consolidated's last investors walk away. The ore was thin. The water was worse. He remembered the surveyor who'd shown him the maps, a stooped man with ink-stained cuffs, pointing at the cross-sections and saying, *There's nothing down there but rock and rust, Mr. Calloway. You'd be doing everyone a kindness to let it die.*

He had done exactly that. And now he lay on his belly in the snow-dusted scree above the mine, watching five armed men guard a hole in the earth where nothing but rock and rust had ever been, and understood that he had spent his whole life mistaking the shape of things for their substance. Paper killed the mine. Paper had killed Tomas Joubert. Paper, if it went wrong in the next three days, would kill six more people whose only crime was counting water honestly.

Below him, the shaft house squatted against the mountainside like a rotten tooth. Its timber frame had gone silver-gray with weather, boards sprung, the hoist tower leaning at an angle that would have been picturesque anywhere else. Snow had begun to gather in the lee of the walls, and the men had cleared a path from the bunkhouse annex to the shaft collar — which told him they expected to move people through that path, and soon, and not gently.

He counted them again. It was the fourth time, and the number refused to change.

Two at the shaft collar, one smoking, one pacing. One at the bunkhouse door. One walking the tramway grade that switchbacked up from the valley — the obvious approach, the one any rescuer would take, which meant it was watched because it was obvious and not because it was good. And the fifth man moved between all of them on no schedule Renn could fix, drifting like smoke, appearing at the collar when you'd just marked him at the grade. That one worried him most. A sentry with a pattern could be timed. A sentry without one could only be hoped about.

*Five,* he thought. *And Vess inside, probably, and whoever else. Six, seven. Against Coral and me and a woman who can barely stand.*

He rolled his head sideways, careful, careful, the way a man moves when the ground under him is made of loose stone and older mistakes. Ada Quist lay beside him in the shadow of a granite rib, wrapped in both their blankets, her breathing shallow but steady. She had insisted on coming. He had argued against it and lost, which was becoming a familiar sensation where Coral Joubert was concerned.

Coral herself was twenty feet further along the ridge, flat against a shelf of rock so dark she might have been part of it. Even now, knowing where to look, he kept losing her. Three years he had felt her behind him like weather — a pressure, a change in the air — and he had never once seen her unless she wanted to be seen. There was a lesson in that, he supposed, about the difference between being hunted and being watched. He had spent three years running from the first. He was beginning to understand what it cost to earn the second.

She raised two fingers, then pointed down-slope. *Fall back.* He gathered Ada with the patience of a man handling stolen goods, and they withdrew along the granite spine until the mine dropped out of sight behind them, into a fold of the mountain where a seep had frozen into a curtain of blue ice and the wind couldn't find them.

Coral crouched, pulled a stub of pencil and the back of an old survey sheet from her coat, and began to draw.

Renn watched her do it. Her hand moved without hesitation — the shaft house as a rectangle, the collar as a notch, the tramway grade as a dashed line climbing off the page. She marked the guards' positions with small crosses and then, after a moment's thought, crossed out the fifth man's last known spot and wrote something beside it. He leaned close enough to read it upside down: *drifter. no pattern.*

"You saw him too," he said.

"Everyone sees him. Nobody catches him." She didn't look up. "That's the dangerous kind."

"He'll be the one who hears us."

"He'll be the one who hears *you*," Coral said, and finally looked at him, and there was nothing cruel in it, which somehow made it worse. "You know I'm right."

He did know. He was the largest thing on the mountain that wasn't wearing a badge, and he had never learned to move quietly because he had never needed to — quiet was for men with something to lose, and he had burned through everything losable years ago. Except now, kneeling in the frozen seep-shadow with a woman who had every reason to put a bullet in him and hadn't, he found he had acquired a whole inventory of things to lose and no idea how to protect any of them.

Ada spoke from inside the blankets, her voice cracked and small. "The lower workings flood. Every spring. The old timers said the Consolidated drowned three mules and a man in '74." She had been staring at the ice curtain while they talked, and now she turned her head, and her eyes were clear in a way they hadn't been in the adit, clear the way people get when fear burns off everything else. "If you're thinking about the lower levels — don't. The timbers are rotten. The whole mountain is holding its breath down there."

"Thank you, Miss Quist," Renn said quietly.

"Don't thank me. Just don't get us drowned."

Coral folded the map and put it away. For a long moment she said nothing at all, and Renn had learned enough of her silences by now to know this one wasn't empty. It was load-bearing. She was weighing something, and whatever it was, it had lives stacked on both sides of the scale.

"Say it," he said. "Whatever you're not saying."

Her jaw worked once. "Eleven people," she said. "Six in that hole, five of us out here. One entrance. Five guns we can see and maybe more we can't. Permits lapse Friday. First real storm tonight or tomorrow." She ticked each item off against her knee like she was pricing stock. "Every plan I've ever made started with cutting losses. Leave the slow ones. Take the ones who can move. Come back for the rest if there's a rest to come back for."

"And?"

"And I'm tired of plans like that." She said it flatly, without drama, the way a person reports the weather. "I left a man behind once. Not like that — he was already gone, there was nothing to leave. But I've been leaving people ever since. Whole towns. Whole years." She looked at the ice curtain, at the light going through it blue and bent. "So the question isn't whether we can save six people. The question is how many of us it costs, and whether I'm willing to pay it, and I don't know yet. That's what I'm not saying."

It was the most honest thing anyone had said to him since his mother died, and it came from the woman whose brother he had ruined, and Renn Calloway found that he could not look at her while she said it, so he looked at the mountain instead, at the white line of the snowfield creeping down toward timberline like something patient.

"I have a plan," he heard himself say.

Coral's eyes came around sharp. "We're not doing anything stupid."

"It's stupid," he admitted. "But it's the kind of stupid that only needs one volunteer."

* * *

They walked the perimeter through the afternoon, or Coral did, moving from cover to cover with Ada and Renn trailing in the paths she broke, and by the time the sun slid behind the western ridge they had the geometry of the place fixed in their heads. One road in, the tramway grade, watched. One ventilation raise on the north face, half-collapsed, opening above the second level — Ada had worked from its memory of the old maps, and her memory was good, though the raise would take blasting powder or a miracle to open from outside. One shaft collar, timbered, with the hoist long stripped but the ladder ways reportedly intact below the first landing.

One way in that mattered. One way out that mattered. Everything else was geology.

They made cold camp in a hollow beneath a fallen ponderosa, no fire, sharing strips of dried meat and the last of the coffee brewed that morning. Above them the sky was going the color of a bruise, and the wind had begun to carry that mineral smell that meant snow was thinking seriously about it.

Renn sat with his back against the log and laid it out, because there was no point waiting for courage. Courage was a currency he'd stopped carrying.

"The guards rotate at dusk and dawn," he said. "Vess runs a tight enough operation for that much — I watched him run cattle camps before he went bad, and he always rotated at dusk and dawn. Men are sleepy and hungry at those hours. Sloppy." He drew in the dirt with a stick: the collar, the bunkhouse, the grade. "At dusk tomorrow, the drifter will be somewhere between stations. The other four will be converging on the fire for the changeover. There'll be maybe four minutes where every gun on that hillside is looking at coffee instead of the dark."

"And in those four minutes," Coral said, "we do what."

"We go in loud."

The silence that followed had weight. Ada stopped chewing.

"Loud," Coral repeated.

"There's dynamite in the old magazine. Half a case, maybe more — the syndicate restocked it when they reoccupied the place, I saw the crates through the glass when I was still guiding for them, before I ran. Blasting caps too." His mouth had gone dry and he pushed through it anyway. "The lower workings are rotten. Ada said it herself. You set a charge at the right timber, on the second level, and you bring down enough rubble to seal the lower stair — trap the guards below, cut them off from the collar. Then you take the surveyors up the ventilation raise from inside. It opens onto the north face. From there it's a traverse to the ridge, and from the ridge it's home."

"That leaves the guards sealed underground," Coral said. "Alive or dead?"

"Alive, if the charge goes where I tell it to go. Sealed, not buried. Angry, cold, and digging for a week." He met her eyes. "I'm not asking you to kill five men, Coral. I'm asking you to trust me with powder, which I understand is a longer list."

"Why you?" It wasn't hostile. It was genuinely a question, the way she asked everything — stripped of decoration, wanting only the load-bearing answer. "Why does it have to be you setting the charge?"

"Because I know the Consolidated's timbers better than any man alive. I helped write its death certificate; I read every survey, every cross-section, every engineer's complaint about green wood and rushed shoring. I know which supports are decorative and which are structural, and I know the difference well enough to drop half a staircase and not the whole mountain." He paused. "Also because if it goes wrong, the man it kills should be me. That's arithmetic even I can do."

"No," Coral said.

"It's not a request."

"I didn't say it was. I said no." She shifted, and the firelight — no, there was no firelight, just the last gray slide of day through the branches — caught the lines of her face and made them look carved rather than drawn. "Nobody on this mountain dies to balance your ledger. If you set that charge, you set it and then you climb out with the rest of us, and you stand in front of a federal judge on Friday and name names until he's sick of hearing them. That's your payment. That's the only payment there is. Dying in a hole in the Consolidated doesn't undo Tomas. It doesn't undo anything. It's just another man leaving someone behind."

Renn looked at his hands. They were shaking, a little, and he wasn't sure from cold.

"You could still shoot me," he said. "It's not too late. It's never been too late. You've had three years and a hundred chances and I've never once made it hard for you."

"I know," Coral said. "That's the problem."

Ada made a sound that might have been a laugh or a cough. "You two are the worst rescue party in the history of the territory," she said, "and I include the time my cousin went after a stray burro in the Superstitions and got lost for nine days."

* * *

Night came down fully, and with it the temperature, and with that the sound of the mountain changing — rock contracting, ice growing, the far-off groan of the snowfields settling into their winter shapes. They took turns watching the mine's lights. There were lights now, lantern-glow leaking from the bunkhouse windows, and the shadows of men crossing them, and once the sound of laughter, ugly and casual, carried up the slope on the wind.

Somewhere down there were five people who had done nothing wrong, eating bad food under guard, counting hours toward Friday the way prisoners count toward a sentence they haven't been given yet.

Renn sat his watch and thought about Tomas Joubert.

Not the abstract Tomas, the grievance, the entry in the ledger with the forged signature — he had lived inside that abstraction for three years and it had fit him like a coat that shrank every season. He thought about the actual man. The actual man had bought him a beer in Dry Wells once, years before any of it, and talked for an hour about contour lines the way other men talked about horses, with a love so plain it embarrassed the room. *Water doesn't lie,* Tomas had said, tapping the bar with one finger. *Men lie. Maps lie. Water just runs downhill and tells the truth about the country.* And Renn had nodded and finished his beer and gone home, and eighteen months later he had sat at a desk and written lies over that man's name for money, and then kept the money, and then kept running.

He wondered if Tomas had known, at the end. Out there in the heat with his rights erased and his water gone, watching the horizon for help that paperwork had already intercepted — had he known it was paper that killed him? Had he died understanding that he'd been murdered by a fountain pen?

Renn found he was crying, quietly, the way men cry on watch when nobody's supposed to see, and he let it happen because there was no one to perform for and no strength left to spend on pretending. The tears froze in the wind almost before they fell.

When Coral relieved him she looked at his face for a long moment in the starlight. He braced for contempt. What he saw instead was harder to name — recognition, maybe. The face of someone who had also cried on watch, also alone, also for a man the desert had taken.

"Calloway," she said.

"Yes."

"Tell me the plan again. All of it. Every step. And if there's a single piece you've been hiding because you think I'll refuse it, tell me that piece twice."

So he told her. The magazine, the charge, the timber on the second level, the timing, the four minutes at dusk, the ventilation raise, the traverse, the ridge. He told her the part he'd been carrying like a stone: that the raise's upper lip was choked with rockfall, and someone would have to break it open from below with hand steel while the others waited in the dark, and that someone would be in the worst position if the charge brought down more than intended. He told her he intended to be that someone too.

She listened without interrupting. When he finished she was silent for the length of ten breaths, twenty, and the wind moved through the branches overhead and the mine glowed faintly below like a coal refusing to die.

Then she reached into her coat and took out Tomas's ledger — the original, the thing he had forged, the thing she had carried across three years and a thousand miles of emptiness — and held it out to him.

"What's this?"

"Tomorrow, when you set the charge, you set it next to this. On the timber. Where the mountain can have it." Her voice was steady and very quiet. "My brother believed water tells the truth. This book is where the lying happened. It belongs down there, under the collapse, with the rest of what the Consolidated buried." She pressed it into his hands. "And you come up the raise with us, and you testify, and you live with what you did in the open air where living with it actually means something. Those are my terms. Take them or I find another way in and you stay tied to that tree until Friday."

Renn Calloway held the ledger. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the mountain.

"I take them," he said.

Coral nodded once, and turned to wake Ada, and began, in a voice pitched low and even, to explain the plan — the whole plan, every step, the kind of plan that requires trusting a man completely, which was the kind Coral Joubert had sworn she'd never make again, and here she was making it, and the strange thing, the thing Renn would remember for the rest of his life, was that she never once looked at him to check whether he was worth it.

Below them, the lanterns in the bunkhouse went out one by one. Somewhere in the dark, the drifter began his rounds.

Dusk tomorrow. Four minutes. One charge, one ledger, eleven lives, and a mountain full of rotten timber between them and Friday.
