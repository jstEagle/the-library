## 16. Blown Timber

The Consolidated mine breathed.

Coral felt it the moment they came down off the ridge in the blue hour before dawn — a slow exhalation of warm air from the shaft mouth, carrying the smell of candle smoke and old timber and men who had lived too long underground. The mountain was alive with other people's heartbeats, and every one of them was between her and eleven lives that were not her own but had somehow become so.

She lay on her belly at the lip of the dump slope, field glasses pressed to her eyes, and counted.

Two guards at the shaft mouth, wrapped in blankets against the cold, a fire burning low between them. One more walking the tram rails toward the ore house, rifle slung, boots ringing on iron. A lantern moving inside the black of the adit — she could see its glow pulse when it passed the timbers, like a slow lamp behind ribs. That made four she could see. Vess would not run his operation on four men. There would be two more below, maybe three, sitting cards and coffee in the hoist house where the surveyors were kept.

Seven. Maybe eight. Against herself, Calloway, Ada Quist, and five half-starved surveyors who hadn't walked a mile in three weeks.

The arithmetic was ugly, and she ran it twice hoping it would come out different. It didn't.

* * *

They held their council in the shadow of an old prospect pit two hundred yards above the workings, voices down to breath, faces close enough to share warmth.

"The shaft mouth is the only way they know," Coral said, scratching a map in the dirt with a stick. "One way in, one way out. That's how Vess wants it. He's not guarding prisoners, he's guarding a door."

"Then we don't use his door." Calloway crouched across from her, and even in the dark she could see the change in him since the cabin — since the letter, since the snow. He sat like a man who had set something heavy down and found his spine again. "I worked this district before I worked for the syndicate. Before you knew my name, Coral."

"I knew your name before you ever earned it," she said, and did not soften it.

He took that too. He'd been taking things from her for three years; one more cut wouldn't kill him. "There's a ventilation raise," he went on. "Old workings, pre-Consolidated. They drove a raise up to the surface on the north face to clear blast smoke — it comes out above the second bench, under that overhang of rock. It's choked with rubble partway, but a man could climb it. A woman could climb it. Five weak ones, maybe, if they're willing."

"And the guards?"

"That's the part you're not going to like."

Coral looked at him across the little map scratched in dirt, and waited.

"There's a main crosscut below the hoist station," Calloway said. "Where the badged men are sleeping, most likely, because it's warm and out of the wind. The oldtimers called it the Sump. When they took out the lower pillar in '89 there was nothing holding the roof up but those timbers. Good timbers — Consolidated bought the best. But timber is timber." He turned his hand over, palm up, as if weighing something invisible. "A charge at the right stull, placed right, and the whole lower working comes down behind us. Seals them in. Not forever — a crew could dig through in a day or two. But long enough."

Ada Quist spoke for the first time. Her voice was still rough from the cold, from the weeks in the adit, but her mind had never stopped working; Coral had learned that about her on the switchbacks, and admired it the way you admire a blade that keeps its edge. "You're proposing to bury six men alive."

"Bury them slow," Calloway said. "In air. With light. Where they can be dug out."

"You don't know that anyone will dig them out," Ada said. "Vess will write them off before he writes off the claim. Men like that always do."

The silence stretched. Somewhere downslope, the tram rails ticked as they cooled.

Coral thought about Tomas. She thought about the ledger, and the signature, and the three years of empty ranges, and the way grief had taught her that the world did not balance its accounts — that you could not put a wrong man in the ground and get a right brother back. She thought about what she'd told herself at the ledge, hauling Calloway up over the cornice: *justice cannot be administered by gravity.*

But these weren't men falling. These were men she would be putting under the rock herself.

"They came at night," Ada said quietly, watching her. "Wearing badges. They took five people who had done nothing but count water. Whatever you decide, decide it knowing what they are."

"That's exactly why I have to decide it slow," Coral said.

She looked at Calloway. "If it goes wrong — if the roof doesn't fall clean, if someone's above the Sump when it goes —"

"Then it goes wrong," he said. "And it's mine. Every grain of powder, every pound of rock. Mine." He met her eyes and didn't flinch, and she hated how much that cost her to accept. "You lead them up the raise. That's the work only you can do. Let me do the work only I can do."

It was trust, and he was asking for all of it at once, and there was no version of the night ahead that required less.

"All right," Coral said. "Blow your timber."

* * *

They went down in the last dark, and the mountain swallowed them by degrees.

First the dump slope, loose rock talking under their boots until Coral taught them the ranger's walk — heel first, weight rolled, slow as water. Then the tram line, where they moved along the ties in single file while the sentry's lantern bobbed somewhere ahead in the black. Then the adit itself, and the dark closed around them like a hand.

Calloway carried the powder in a canvas satchel against his chest, wrapped in his coat to keep it dry and quiet. He'd taken it from the magazine behind the ore house while the sentry walked his far leg — twenty minutes of Coral's life spent flat against frozen rock, counting the man's footsteps the way she'd once counted stars to stay sane on the alkali. Twenty minutes, and then Calloway was beside her again, breathing hard, grinning like a man reborn, and she had wanted to strike him for the grin and hadn't known why.

Inside, the mine was warmer than the night, and that was the worst thing about it. The warmth meant the mountain had been keeping men alive down here, feeding them, letting them sleep — men who had taken her brother's ledger apart entry by entry, men who were holding five strangers as collateral against a piece of paper expiring Friday.

Coral led by touch. Her fingers read the walls the way they read contour lines: the smooth machine-cut stone of the newer drives, then older pick marks, then the sag of a rotten lagging board that told her they'd crossed into the pre-Consolidated workings, the labyrinth Calloway had promised. Behind her came the five surveyors, roped together wrist to wrist, moving on legs that shook but moved. Ada came last, a compass in her pocket and her jaw set like a woman taking minutes of her own rescue.

Twice they froze as lamplight swung through a crosscut ahead. Once a guard passed within ten feet, humming to himself, oblivious, and Coral lay in the drainage ditch with her cheek in cold muck and her hand nowhere near her gun, because a shot down here would echo through every tunnel in the mountain and bring the whole operation down on them like a cave-in.

Then the raise.

It opened off the left wall like a throat — a steep climb of ladders and foot pegs and broken rock, breathing cold air down from the surface. Coral went up first, testing each rung, each peg, feeling the old wood flex and hold. Halfway, the rubble choke Calloway had warned of: a jammed mass of slabs and gravel they climbed through on hands and knees, Ada passing the weakest of the surveyors' packs up ahead of them, everyone breathing in small careful sips.

Above the choke, the raise narrowed and the air turned knife-cold, and then there was no roof, only sky — a slot of gray-black dawn between the overhang rocks, snow crystals drifting down through it like ash.

Coral came out onto the north face of the mountain and stood in the falling snow and let herself breathe.

Five surveyors followed her out, blinking, staggering, catching at the rock. Ada came last, and when she surfaced she looked back down into the hole they'd climbed out of, and then at Coral, and said nothing, because there was nothing yet to say. Below them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, the mine went on breathing.

Now it was Calloway's turn.

* * *

They heard it rather than saw it — a single sharp crack, like the mountain clearing its throat, rolling up through the rock under their feet.

Then a longer sound. A deep, grinding exhalation, stone settling on stone, dust sighing up the shaft they'd just left and pluming out of the slot above them in a gray ghost-cloud. The overhang shed a skin of frost. Somewhere far below, faint as memory, men began to shout.

Ada grabbed Coral's arm. "The timbers."

"It's done." Coral's voice came out flatter than she intended. "Lower workings are sealed."

She should have felt triumph. She catalogued what she actually felt instead: the shout still echoing, thin and human, down in the dark. Dust on her lips. Calloway somewhere beneath her feet in the collapsing dark, alone now, committed, coming up the raise after them with the roar of his own work still in his ears. She found she couldn't pray and didn't try. She just listened, the way she'd listened for horses on the flats, for weather in the pines, for everything the desert ever told her — and after four minutes that lasted a season, the rubble in the raise shifted, and a hand came up through the slot, and then Calloway's head, white with rock flour, eyes bright as a boy's.

"Clean fall," he rasped. "They've got air and light and a week of grub down there. Vess will have them out by Sunday or he'll answer to the territorial engineer." He pulled himself onto the bench and sat, shaking, laughing a little, alive. "It's done, Coral. It's done."

It wasn't.

* * *

Because the mountain had one more thing to say.

They were crossing the second bench toward the tree line when the sentry found them — not the sleepy man from the tram line but another one entirely, a big man with a beard full of ice whom Coral had never counted, coming around the shoulder of the overhang with his rifle already off his shoulder, and the whole morning compressed into a single terrible instant.

He wasn't aiming at Coral. He was aiming at the crowd of stumbling, rope-scarred surveyors, the evidence itself, and his face had the flat certainty of a man who has been paid to make a problem disappear and intends to earn his wage.

"No!" Ada stepped between — stepped *forward*, this woman who owed Coral nothing, arms spread, voice cracking across the snow. "Don't—"

His left hand shot out and caught her by the collar, wrenching her off her feet, dragging her in front of him as a shield, rifle swinging up over her shoulder — and time went strange and slow the way it does when the body decides faster than the soul.

Coral had her gun out. She registered that distantly, the way you register weather. She had the shot — she could see it, the triangle of the man's forehead past Ada's ear, forty feet, downhill light, a shot she'd practiced ten thousand times on bottles and tin cans and the kind of men who steal water rights and leave brothers to die. She had the shot, and she had the choice, and both arrived together.

*Take it,* said three years of empty range. *Take it and be done.*

But Ada's eyes were wide over the man's shoulder, and they were not afraid for herself. They were afraid of what Coral would become if she fired through that narrow margin of air. And Coral understood, in the space of a heartbeat, that the shot was not the question. The question was whether she would spend the rest of her life the way she'd spent the last three years — moving, wanting nothing, carrying the dead — or whether she would stop here, on this bench, and pay a different price.

She didn't shoot.

She closed the distance. Four strides through knee-deep snow, faster than the man could swing his muzzle clear of his hostage, and she went in low and hard with the pistol itself — butt-first, a ranger's ugly close work, driving it into the hinge of his jaw with everything grief and guilt and three winters of riding had built into her arm. His head snapped sideways. Ada tore free and fell. The rifle went off into the sky, a flat crack that rolled away down the valley and woke every echo in the Vermilions, and then Coral was on top of him in the snow, and the gun came down once, and again —

— and stopped.

Her arm hung in the air above his face. The man's eyes were rolling, unfocused, blood sheeting from his scalp into the snow, his chest rising in ruined hitches. Alive. He was going to live, and some part of her that had wanted very badly to keep swinging screamed at the mercy, and a quieter part, a part that sounded like Tomas, said: *this is the one you carry home.*

"Coral." Ada's hand on her shoulder. "Coral. He's done. Look at me. He's done."

The surveyors stood in a silent row in the falling snow, five pale faces, witnesses to whatever she had just decided to be.

Calloway took the man's rifle and broke it over a rock and threw the pieces into the trees. Nobody spoke about the blood. There would be time for the blood later, or there wouldn't; either way it was hers now, priced and paid, a debt she'd feel in her hands for the rest of her life. She wiped the pistol on her coat leg without looking at it and got to her feet, and her knees nearly went, and Ada caught her elbow and held it, and that — being held up by a stranger — undid her worse than the killing almost had.

"Tree line," Coral managed. "We move for the tree line. Now."

"Why?" one of the surveyors asked, young, hollow-eyed. "We're free. Aren't we free?"

Coral looked east, where the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and felt the wind change against her cheek — felt it the way she'd felt it a hundred times, the pressure dropping, the smell of the high country turning to iron.

That shot had rolled down the whole valley. And the storm she'd been racing for three days had just come over the divide early, a white wall swallowing the peaks one ridge at a time, and it was coming for them at a dead run.

"No," Coral said. "We're not free. We're above treeline in an open whiteout with a wounded man and no trail." She pulled Ada upright, turned them all into the wind, and raised her voice over the rising scream of it. "Rope up. All of you. We cross now — or the mountain keeps us."

Behind them, far below, the mine's whistle began to blow — long, furious, unanswered — calling its buried men and its lost prisoners home through the snow.

The first flakes hit like feathers, then like sand, then like nothing at all, because the world had gone white.
