## 17. Whiteout

The storm did not arrive. It assembled.

Coral felt it coming the way she felt a liar coming — a pressure behind the ears, a wrongness in the light. For an hour above the last stunted pines the sky had been the color of a whetstone, and the wind had been talking to itself in a low voice, and then all at once the world simply closed, like a hand folding shut, and there was nothing left but white and the sound of it coming down.

"Rope!" she shouted, and the word was torn sideways out of her mouth before it finished.

Eleven of them on the shoulder of the Vermilion Range, strung out along a spine of rock that in clear weather she could have walked blind drunk. In clear weather. That was the joke of it. She had spent three years telling herself she knew this country better than anyone alive, and the mountain had waited until she had ten other lives tied to her waist to prove the difference between knowing a place and owning it.

She got the rope out with fingers she could not feel and tied them the way her father had taught her, twelve years old, on the lava fields south of Dry Wells: eight feet between climbers, knots checked twice, the strongest hands at the ends. Calloway at the front. That had been her decision and she had made it without letting herself think about it, because thinking about it meant admitting what it was — a trust, extended to a man she had planned to put a rope around for entirely different reasons.

"Front's mine," he'd said, back at the cabin, before the badges came and went. "I've crossed this shoulder in weather. Give me that much."

She'd given it. She was still deciding what that made her.

Behind Calloway: Ada, with the survey instruments lashed to her back because she would not leave them and Coral had not argued, because the maps in that case were the whole reason any of them were still breathing. Then the five surveyors — Halloway, the Prue twins, old Maren, and the boy, Oskar, who could not have been twenty and who had not stopped shivering since the mine. Then Coral herself, last, where she could see them all and none of them could see her face.

"Listen!" she bellowed into the wind. "Nobody lets the rope go slack. Nobody passes anybody. When I stop, you stop, and you stop *now*, not when you finish your thought. We move on my whistle — two short, we go. One long, we drop and hold. Anybody falls, the rest of you go flat and dig in and *hold*, and you don't look, and you don't think. You hold. Am I understood?"

A ragged chorus of yeses. Oskar's was barely a sound.

They went.

* * *

There is a kind of blindness the high country gives you that is worse than night. Night has edges. This had none. The snow came not as flakes but as a solid grinding whiteness, a thing with weight and intent, and after twenty minutes Coral could not see her own boots. She could not see Calloway eight feet ahead. She could see the rope — sometimes — a pale ghost-line jerking through the murk, and she could hear him, a scrape and crunch and once, when the wind gapped, the low sound of him talking to himself, or to the mountain, or to her.

She navigated by memory and by the braille of wind against her face.

That was the truth of it and she held onto it like a rung: the wind on this shoulder came out of the northwest and hit a climber's left cheek going up and the right cheek going down, and there was a place — she had camped there once, seven years ago, tracking a rustler in October — where the wind doubled because the ridge kinked east around a granite nose, and if she kept the doubled wind on her left she would be on the spine and not on the corniced east flank where the snow lay over nothing at all.

So she walked with her face turned into the storm like a woman leaning into a door, counting her own steps, reading the wind's pressure against her cheekbone the way a blind man reads a page. Two hundred steps of steady wind. Then the kink. Then the doubled wind, cold and doubled, on her left. Good. Good. She was a compass. She was nothing but a compass with ten lives tied to it, and there was a strange mercy in that, because a compass doesn't grieve, doesn't hate, doesn't count the years it spent wanting a man dead. A compass only points.

The whistle went twice. They moved.

* * *

It was somewhere in the second hour that Calloway's trail-breaking began to change.

She heard it before she understood it. Trail-breaking has a rhythm — the lunge, the sink, the drag, the lunge — and Calloway's rhythm had been steady as a pump for two hours, and then it stuttered. Then it shortened. Then there was a long interval where the only sound was wind, and the rope in Coral's mittened hand went slack, and her heart went off like a gun.

She blew one long blast and plunged forward along the rope, hand over hand, until she hit him.

He was down on one knee, then both, and when she hauled him up by the collar he came up wrong, loose, his head lolling, and she got his face close to hers and saw what the storm allowed her to see: his cheeks gray-white, waxy, the color of tallow. His eyes trying to focus on her and failing, sliding off.

His hands. She looked at his hands. The right glove was gone — she never learned when he'd lost it, whether in the mine or on the climb — and the bare hand was the color of old candle wax and did not look like a hand anymore. It looked like something a farrier would nail to a post as a warning.

"Calloway." She shook him. "Renn." His name in her mouth, first time in three years, and it came out flat as a stone. "Look at me. Can you walk?"

"I can—" His voice was a slurred ruin. "I can break. I'll break trail."

"You'll break nothing." She got his ruined hand and stuffed it inside her own coat, against her body, because the arithmetic was simple and she refused to look at it too closely: his hands were the price, and the price was already paid, and the only question was whether she spent the rest of him too. She turned him around, put him behind Ada, ahead of herself, and took the front.

The wind hit her full in the face and erased everything she'd been reading. She stood in it for a moment, blind, empty, the compass needle spinning — and then she planted her feet, turned her left cheek to the doubled wind, and began to count.

One. Two. Three.

Behind her, ten people breathing. That was the whole of it. That was the entire world: ten breaths and a rope and a number in her head.

* * *

She did not know how long the crossing took. Time went the way the trail went — under the snow, gone.

There was a stretch where the ridge narrowed and she felt the ground change under her boots, a hollowness, a drum-sound, and she knew without seeing that the cornice had grown out over the east face and she was walking on its root, and she turned them west, into the wind's teeth, because the wind's teeth were at least standing on stone. There was a stretch where Oskar fell — she heard the rope snap taut, heard the twins shout — and she called "Hold, hold, hold" and the line went flat and dug in and held, and she walked back down the rope and found the boy on his side in the white with his eyes open and empty, and she got him up, and she said, "You're doing fine," which was a lie, and he said, "I know," which meant he understood it was a lie, and somehow that was better. There was a stretch — she would remember this later, in the thaw, when she had time to be afraid retroactively — where the wind stopped entirely for perhaps a minute, and the silence was so enormous that Maren began to weep, quietly, without breaking stride, and nobody said a word, because they all understood that the silence was worse than the storm, the way the eye of a thing is worse than its teeth.

And through all of it, the thought that would not leave her alone:

*This is the whole book. This is the whole of it.*

Three years of moving. Three years of empty ranges and cracked alkali and a ledger read a hundred times and a name said like a curse — and here at the end of it she was moving again, moving harder than she had ever moved, but the moving had changed. Before, she had moved to outrun a stillness she couldn't bear. Now she moved because ten people breathed behind her, and every step was a thing done *for* and not *against*, and the difference between those two kinds of motion was the difference, she understood suddenly, between a hunt and a crossing. A hunt ends when something dies. A crossing ends when something arrives.

She wanted, with a ferocity that frightened her, to arrive.

* * *

The cabin — Maren's old prospecting shack, two miles shy of the descent, the one place in this white hell she could promise herself — did not appear. It simply resolved, the way a photograph develops: first a darkness that might be shadow, then a shape that might be a wall, and then, all at once, a door, and she was standing in front of it with her hand on the latch and no memory of the last quarter mile.

She got them inside the way she'd do anything now, by counting. One through ten through the door, Calloway last because he'd stopped answering his name. She got the door shut against the storm and the sound of the world cut off so completely that for a moment the silence rang.

Then: work. Nobody had to be told. Ada had the stove going before Coral had the last knot undone; the surveyors were stripping wet wool, chafing each other's hands, and the Prue twins got Oskar wrapped in every blanket the shack owned. The cabin filled with steam and breath and the small animal sounds of people coming back from the edge, and Coral stood in the middle of it with her hands on her hips, swaying, and let herself feel, for exactly three seconds, nothing at all.

Then she turned to Calloway.

He was sitting against the wall with his knees up, and he had not taken off his pack, and when she crouched in front of him she saw why. He couldn't. His hands would not close. The bare right one she had warmed against her body for two hours was blackening at the fingertips, and the left, gloved the whole way, was barely better — swollen, clumsy, the fingers curling like a dead bird's feet.

"Stove," she said. "Now."

"I can't feel them," he said. He said it the way a man reports weather. "Both of them. I can't feel them at all."

She got his pack off. She got his boots off, because his boots mattered more than his hands and she wasn't going to say so. She put his hands as close to the stove as he could bear, which was not close, which was a foot away and trembling, and she sat beside him on the floor of Maren's shack with the storm screaming at the door like something locked out, and she took his ruined right hand in both of hers.

Not gently. She worked it. Thumbs into the palm, along the tendons, the way you'd work a frozen pump, forcing blood into tissue that had stopped asking for it. He hissed. Then he made a sound he probably didn't know he was making, low and continuous, an animal sound of pain, and he did not pull away.

"Talk," she said. "Keep talking. If you go quiet I'll think you're going under."

"About what?"

"Anything. The syndicate. Vess. The names." She worked the thumb down his index finger and he shuddered. "You owe me names. Start paying."

He laughed — a cracked, wheezing thing, half delirium. "You're extorting a man with frostbite."

"I'm a ranger. It's what we do."

So he talked. Names came out of him in the cadence of pain — a land commissioner in the territorial capital, a banker in Dry Wells whose name made Coral's teeth ache with its smallness, a judge, a judge — and Ada, wrapped in a blanket across the room, wrote them in a survey notebook with a pencil stub, her handwriting steady as contour lines. And Coral worked the frozen hands and listened, and understood that she was holding the end of something in her lap: three years of pursuit, and it had come down to this, her thumbs in the palm of the man she'd sworn to ruin, trading warmth for truth, and it did not feel like vengeance. It felt like arithmetic. It felt, God help her, like enough.

"Your brother," Calloway said, suddenly, his eyes half-closed, deep in the gray country between pain and sleep. "Tomas. He signed his claim in a hand like a schoolteacher's. I copied it four times before I got it right. I want you to know I practiced."

"Why would you tell me that?"

"Because you should know I knew what I was doing." His head tipped back against the wall. "It wasn't carelessness, Coral. It never was."

She worked his hand and said nothing, and outside the storm ground on toward morning, and somewhere below them, two days' climb down, a mine shaft held a foreman who thought the snow had done his work for him — and Coral Joubert sat on a cold floor, holding her enemy's hand warm, and began, for the first time in three years, to plan a descent.
