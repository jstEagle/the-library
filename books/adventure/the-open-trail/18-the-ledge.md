## 18. The Ledge

The mountain had been trying to kill them for six hours, and Coral Joubert was tired of letting it think it might succeed.

The whiteout had swallowed the world somewhere above the last stand of wind-bent pines, and since then there had been nothing but rope, wind, and the small animal sounds of eleven people breathing through wool scarves. She could not see them. She could feel them — a living chain running back from her gloved fist to Calloway breaking trail ahead of her, then Ada behind her, then the five surveyors, then Vess's two men who had surrendered their rifles at the mine mouth rather than be left in the collapsed workings with their fellows. Eleven souls strung out on a hundred feet of manila like beads on a thread, and Coral was the knot that held the string.

She navigated by memory. That was all there was. The compass had become a joke hours ago, its needle swinging drunk in the magnetic chaos of the Vermilion Range, and her eyes were useless against a world the color of unbleached linen. But she had crossed this shoulder of the mountain twice before, once in summer and once in a November much like this one, and she carried the route in her body the way other people carried grief: constantly, involuntarily, in a place too deep to set down.

Fifty paces of climbing traverse. Then the slope eased into the long bench below the cornice. Then the notch between the twin towers where the wind funneled through with a sound like a freight train falling off a cliff forever.

*Fifty paces,* she told herself, though she could not have said what a pace meant anymore when each step might land on air or stone or nothing at all. *Then the bench. Then the notch.*

Calloway broke trail because his hands still worked. Barely. She had watched them failing all day — the way he'd stopped being able to work the knots at the last rest stop, how Ada had tied him off instead, her frost-nipped fingers clumsy but willing. He swung his arms now in great exaggerated arcs as he waded through drifts to his chest, driving blood into fingers that had gone the color of candle wax. He did not complain. In three days he had not complained once, and Coral had decided not to decide what that meant.

The wind came again, a gust that leaned on her like a hand pressing her face away, and she turned her shoulder into it and counted steps. Twelve. Thirteen. Somewhere behind her a surveyor — young Pell, she thought, the one who wept without noise — made a sound that might have been a sob or might have been the wind finding a gap in his collar. She could do nothing about either. The rope said they were moving. Moving was everything. Moving was the whole of the law.

Twenty-six. Twenty-seven.

Her boot found the bench. She knew it before she knew it — the sudden flattening of the grade underfoot, the way the wind's voice changed from a scream to a moan as the slope above began to shelter them. The cornice. She remembered it from summer: a great white eyebrow of overhanging snow, curled out from the ridge like a wave frozen at the moment of breaking, hanging above the bench for thirty yards. In July you walked beneath it and never thought about it. In November, after three days of storm loading—

*Don't walk beneath it.* The thought arrived whole and cold. *Cut high. Go over the top of the ridge before the cornice, not under its lip.*

But going over the ridge meant exposing the whole rope team to the full fury of the wind on the crest, and behind her were people who had spent four days in a mine and three days climbing on half rations, people whose legs shook when they stood still, people who would be plucked off the crest like leaves. Under the lip was sheltered. Under the lip was fast. And the permits expired tomorrow morning, and the evidence in the oilskin packet against her chest would rot into worthless paper if they came down the mountain a week late and found the land office already signed away.

Under the lip. Fast. She chose it, and she would carry that choice too, along with all the others.

"Close up!" she shouted backward, her voice torn away before it finished leaving her mouth. She tugged the rope twice — *tighten, come close* — and felt the line go taut as the column bunched. "Rope tight! Nobody stops! Move!"

She moved. Calloway moved ahead of her, a gray ghost shape in the gray world, and beneath them the bench ran flat and true toward the notch, and above them hung thirty yards of snow that had been falling for ten thousand winters and waiting, perhaps, for this one.

She was twenty feet under the lip when the mountain exhaled.

There was no warning she could have used. Later she would understand that the cornice had been failing since the first hour of the storm, sagging under its own tonnage, and that no choice she made would have changed the arithmetic of snow and gravity. But in the moment there was only a sound — a deep, structural groan, like a ship's timbers letting go — and then the world tilted, and the bench itself seemed to lean, and Calloway's ghost shape ahead of her dropped out of existence as if the mountain had opened a door and taken him through it.

The rope snapped taut around her waist and hauled her forward onto her knees.

She slid. The broken cornice was avalanching down the far side in a white thunder, tons and tons of it, and Calloway was inside that thunder, and the rope was dragging her across the bench toward the edge with the terrible patient strength of something that weighed more than she did. She jammed her axe — Ada's axe, actually; everything was everyone's now — into the crust and it held for one second and skated free, and she slid another yard, and behind her the rope team was screaming, and she could hear Ada's voice cutting through all of it, clear as a bell: *"ANCHOR! GET AN ANCHOR!"*

Pell anchored. She never learned exactly how — later he said he'd wrapped the rope around a buried boulder he'd felt with his boot, and that he'd done it without thinking, and that he'd cried the whole time he did it. But suddenly the line stopped paying out, and Coral stopped sliding with two boots and one elbow hanging over a drop that the wind filled with white silence, and the only thing between Renn Calloway and the bottom of the Vermilion Range was her grip on a rope and a boy's improvised anchor and the arithmetic of friction.

She got her other axe in. She got her heels in. She crawled back from the edge, one inch at a time, until she could kneel on solid bench, and then she looked down.

He was there. Of course he was there. Thirty feet down, dangling at the end of the rope against a wall of blue ice and black rock, turning slowly, his arms over his head, his ruined hands hooked into the line. Snow-dust still sifted past him. Below him the slope fell away in a series of cliffs and ramps for eight hundred feet, and beyond that the clouds simply ended, and beyond the clouds there was nothing worth naming.

"Calloway." Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "Can you hear me?"

His head tipped back. The hood fell away from his face, and even at thirty feet, even through the sift of snow, she could see him clearly — see the frostbitten ruin of his cheeks, the eyes squinting against the wind, the mouth working.

"I hear you," he called up. His voice was almost calm. That was the thing she would remember longest: how calm. As if he'd been expecting the mountain to collect him eventually, and here it was, right on schedule.

"The rope holds," she said. "I'm going to haul you up."

"No."

The word floated up through the snow-dust, small and definite.

"You don't get to say no," she said. "You're coming up."

"Coral." He said her name carefully, the way a man handles something breakable. "Look at your hands. Look at the rope. There are nine people up there depending on that line, and every minute I hang here is a minute the cold is eating through your gloves and theirs. You know the math. You've known the math your whole life."

She did know the math. It was the oldest math in the desert, older than water rights, older than fences: one rope, many lives, and a man on the end of it who had earned his fall fair and square. Three years ago Tomas Joubert had died of thirst in a box canyon forty miles south of here because the water in his well had been sold out from under him, and the man dangling at the end of her rope had held the pen that did it. Every mile she'd ridden, every empty range she'd crossed, every night she'd slept sitting up with her rifle across her knees — all of it had been aimed at this moment, at this man, at this exact geometry of rope and gravity and consequence. The desert had finally delivered him to justice. All she had to do was open her hands.

It would be so easy. It would be so easy that it frightened her — how easy, how quiet, how the wind would take the sound of his fall and scatter it, and the rope would come up light, and she would stand there holding an ending three years in the making, and it would feel like nothing at all. That was the trap of it. Not the temptation to kill him. The temptation to *let go*, which wore the clean white robes of nature, of accident, of the mountain's own verdict, and left her hands as innocent as fresh snow.

Nobody would ever know. Except she would know. She would know the way she knew the route through the notch, in the body, in the deep place, forever.

"Coral." His voice again, thinner now. "Let go. Please. I'm the reason your brother is dead. I've known it since Dry Wells, since the telegram, since I saw your name on the marshal's orders and understood that God has a sense of humor after all. I ran from you for three years because I couldn't say the names. Let me give you this instead. It's the only honest thing I have left to give."

Below him the wind gusted and he swung, gently, like a bell, like a hanged man, and Coral Joubert knelt at the lip of a ledge in a whiteout and discovered the true shape of the thing she had been carrying for three years.

It was not hatred. Hatred would have been simple. It was the want — the fierce, starving, three-year want — to make the ledger balance. To have someone pay what Tomas had paid. To stand at the end of the accounting and watch the debt collected, in full, with interest. And here it was, offered to her freely, delivered by gravity itself, requiring nothing from her but the release of her grip.

And it would not balance. That was the truth of it, the truth she had ridden three thousand miles to avoid: Tomas was dead, and hauling this man up would not change that, and dropping him would not change that either. The ledger did not care. The ledger stayed unbalanced no matter what happened on this ledge, because some entries could never be settled, only carried. Her brother's name would weigh the same in her chest tomorrow whether Renn Calloway's body lay at the bottom of this cliff or sat shivering beside a fire testifying to a federal judge.

But there was a difference. That was the other truth, the one underneath, the one she'd been circling for twenty-two chapters of her own stubborn life: there was a difference between a debt collected and a debt *carried honestly*. One of them made the carrier smaller. She had watched herself getting smaller for three years, range by range, and she was tired of it. Tired of the moving, tired of the wanting, tired of the arithmetic that always ended with somebody at the bottom of something.

"Ada!" she shouted over her shoulder. "Get the team off the lip! Back from the edge, fifty feet, dig in! Pell, hold that anchor like it's your mother's grave!"

She turned back to the edge. She took the rope in both hands, above the axes, and she began to pull.

Not fast. Never fast. A hand over hand, a foot braced, a lean back into the load, the way you'd raise water from a deep well — steady, mechanical, a rhythm that asked nothing of her heart and everything of her back. Thirty feet of rope with a grown man on the end. The wind tried to help and hinder in equal measure. Her shoulders burned. Her frost-cracked palms split somewhere in the second ten feet and she felt the rope go slick and warm and she pulled anyway.

He helped when he could. Halfway up he found a ramp with his boots and walked himself higher, hands over hers on the line, and she hated that too — hated the competence of him, the way he made even his rescue efficient — and she kept pulling.

When his head came over the lip she grabbed his collar and dragged him onto the bench like a landed fish, and then she did a strange thing: she rolled him away from the edge, ten feet, roughly, and put herself between him and the drop, and only then did she let go of him entirely.

They lay side by side in the snow, breathing. The wind moaned over the broken cornice. Somewhere behind them, muffled, the team waited.

"You should have let go," he said.

"I know."

"It would have been easier."

"Easier isn't the same as cheaper." She sat up. Her hands were shaking now that the work was done, shaking the way they always did after, and she looked at them — split, bleeding, frost-white — and thought: *these are the hands that didn't.* "Tomas wanted to be buried on the ridge above our father's place. Ashes. He wrote it in a letter I haven't been able to finish reading for three years. I'm going to read it. And I'm going to bury him. And you—" she pointed at him, and her finger was steady, which surprised them both, "—you're going to be alive for it. Alive and in irons and talking. Because if you die on this mountain, the syndicate wins, and Tomas loses twice, and I will not permit it. Do you understand me? Your death is not yours to spend. It belongs to the judge now."

He looked at her for a long time. Snow gathered on his eyelashes. When he spoke, his voice cracked down the middle.

"Yes, ma'am," he said.

She got up. She got the team moving. They crossed the notch an hour later, single file through the funnel of wind, and the sun — a pale smear behind the cloud ceiling, nothing more — touched the western horizon as they came down off the shoulder into the black timber, and Coral Joubert led them downward through the darkening forest toward Dry Wells and the morning and the land office, carrying her evidence, her prisoner, her split and bleeding hands, and the knowledge, new and heavy and strangely light, that she had held on.

Behind them the mountain kept its silence, keeping what it keeps.

Ahead, down through the timber, a single light showed in the valley — the land office lamp, or a ranch lantern, or only a star low on the horizon. It did not matter which. Morning was coming, and the permits with it, and for the first time in three years Coral Joubert was not riding away from anything at all.
