## 19. Permit Expiry

They came down out of the Vermilions the way water comes down — finding the path of least resistance, wearing it smooth as they went.

Coral rode point on a horse that had stopped trusting its own footing two days ago and trusted her instead, which was more than she could say for most of the eleven souls strung out behind her on the rope of their own survival. Ada Quist rode second, wrapped in Calloway's coat over her own, her frostbitten fingers tucked into her armpits like a bird's folded wings. Behind her came the five surveyors from the Consolidated mine — Hollis, Brandt, the Okafor twins, young Mercer with his cracked ribs bound in a surveyor's plumb line because it was all they'd had — and behind them, leading three horses loaded with evidence instead of ore, Renn Calloway walked.

He had not asked to ride. She had not offered. Some arithmetic between them had settled that without a word spoken, and Coral found she preferred it that way. A man on foot at the rear of a column was either a prisoner or a penitent, and she hadn't decided yet which one he was, and she suspected he hadn't either.

The snow had followed them down to eight thousand feet and then given up, turning to rain, then to that fine cold mist that isn't weather so much as the mountain's breath on your neck. By the time they struck the wagon road above Dry Wells, the mist had burned off into a morning so clean and hard-edged it looked cut from tin. The desert lay below them the way it always did — vast, indifferent, beautiful in the way a ledger is beautiful if you understand what the columns mean.

Coral reined up where the road crested the last ridge and looked down at the town.

Dry Wells sat in its bowl of dust like a handful of dropped coins: the assay office, the mercantile, the saloon with its false front leaning east after forty years of wind, the church whose bell had been cracked since before Tomas was born. And there, at the head of the street, squat and gray and certain of itself, the territorial land office, with the American flag hanging dead on its pole in the windless morning.

Beneath the flag, men were already gathering. She could see them even at this distance — a knot of dark coats on the office steps, and among them, unmistakable even at half a mile, the pale gleam of a buggy that cost more than every horse in town combined. The syndicate had come to watch the permits lapse. They had come to collect.

"Ranger." Ada's voice beside her, scraped thin by six days of smoke and cold. "How long?"

"Noon," Coral said. "Permits expire at noon today. After that, every claim those men hold goes back to open range unless the filings are contested."

"And we're the contest."

"We're the contest."

Ada looked down at the town for a long moment. Her face was still marked where the mine timber had grazed her, a purple-yellow bloom along the jaw that would go green before it went away. When she spoke again her voice had changed — steadier, the voice of a woman who had spent three weeks keeping instruments true in country that wanted them crooked.

"I've been thinking about what I'll say. In there." She nodded toward the land office. "I've said it to myself maybe two hundred times since the cabin. Every version I try comes out wrong."

"They mostly do," Coral said. "Say it anyway. Wrong is admissible. Silence isn't."

* * *

They came into Dry Wells at nine minutes past ten in the morning, and the town turned out to look at them the way towns turn out for anything — hungry for spectacle, disappointed by mud.

Eleven people and eight horses, roped together in spirit if not in fact, riding single file down the one street with the mountains standing up blue and white behind them like witnesses who'd come all this way and refused to leave until it was done. Word had traveled ahead of them somehow — it always did, faster than any rider, carried on some frequency the desert owned outright — and by the time they reached the well at the street's center, half of Dry Wells stood along the boardwalks with hats in hand or hands in pockets, saying nothing, watching everything.

Coral dismounted at the land office steps and handed her reins to Mercer, who took them with the careful reverence of a man handling something breakable. Then she stood a moment in the street and let herself be seen.

She was not a sight worth seeing, and she knew it. Six days of storm had written themselves across her: chapped lips split at both corners, cheeks scoured raw by wind, hair stiff with sweat-salt under a hat brim crusted white. Her coat was torn at the left shoulder where the cornice rock had caught her hauling Calloway up. Her hands, when she looked at them, belonged to someone older — knuckles split and scabbed, nails blackened, the rope burns across both palms already going the deep red that meant they'd scar.

But she was upright, and she was here, and behind her stood ten more who shouldn't have been.

The knot of dark coats on the steps parted slightly as she approached. At its center stood a man she knew by reputation and photograph both: Ambrose Vess's principal, the syndicate's territorial man, a Mr. Aldous Crane — though nobody called him mister twice. He wore a coat of dove-gray wool that had never known rain, and he held his hat at an angle suggesting the world existed at his convenience.

"Miss Joubert." His voice carried the warmth of a stove in an empty room. "The territory owes you a debt. Those people lost in the high country — a terrible business. We'd begun organizing a recovery party ourselves. Very glad it won't be necessary."

"Was it," Coral said.

It wasn't a question, but he answered it anyway, smoothly, the way men like him answered everything. "Excuse me?"

"Glad. Was it. That it won't be necessary." She climbed the steps past him, close enough to smell bay rum and wool. "Because I count five survivors you told the marshal died in an avalanche. I count a guide you let this territory believe ran off with the payroll. And I count a mine full of badged men holding federal surveyors hostage against permit expiry, which is a word I'd look up the definition of before noon if I were you."

She didn't wait for his answer. She pushed through the land office door with the whole ragged column of them at her back, and the door swung wide and stayed wide, because no one in Dry Wells was going to be the one to close it.

* * *

The land office smelled of ink, lamp oil, and the particular staleness of paper that has been argued over too long. Inside, behind a rail of varnished pine, sat Registrar Emmett Loudermilk — a small round man with spectacles thick as bottle bottoms and the expression of a church deacon watching a funeral procession arrive early.

Behind him, the walls were drawers. Hundreds of them, floor to ceiling, each labeled in faded copperplate: *Claims, A–D. Claims, E–H.* The entire desert, filed away in pine boxes. Coral had spent three years hating that wall without ever once seeing it. Now she saw it plainly: every drawer a throat, every filing a held breath.

"Registrar Loudermilk." She laid her hat on the rail. Water dripped off its brim onto the clean floorboards, and she found she didn't care. "Coral Joubert, district ranger. These are the missing members of the federal survey party, Section Nine, Vermilion Range. All five accounted for, plus the party's surviving surveyor, Miss Ada Quist. And this—" she stepped aside, letting the light fall on Renn Calloway, who stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands and his eyes down, "—is Renn Calloway, who will be making a sworn statement this morning, and who is surrendering himself to whatever custody applies."

Loudermilk's mouth opened and closed twice. "Miss Joubert, I — the permits, the hour — there are procedures—"

"There are," Coral agreed. "Here's how we're going to follow them."

She unslung the oilskin packet from her shoulder — the one she'd carried over the pass, through the whiteout, down the switchbacks, sleeping with it inside her coat against her chest like a second heartbeat — and set it on the rail with a sound like a gavel coming down.

"In this packet: the original aquifer surveys, Section Nine, signed and dated by the party's chief instrument man, Mr. Hollis, who is standing right there and can authenticate them. You'll find they do not match the copies filed with this office in the names of the Meridian Water and Land Consortium. You'll find, in fact, that the consortium's claims sit on dry rock, and that the water those claims purport to control runs under unpatented ground — ground the consortium has spent this season trying to clear of witnesses."

She drew out the maps one at a time and spread them on the rail, weighting the corners with her hat, a canteen, a surveyor's brass transit case that Ada passed forward without being asked. The forged lines and the true ones lay side by side, and even Loudermilk, blinking through his bottle-bottoms, could see where the blue ink of the real aquifer bent away from the black ink of the claim like a river refusing a fence.

"Second item." Coral's voice was steady. Somewhere beneath the steadiness, three years of empty ranges and cheap hotel rooms and a brother's name she hadn't spoken aloud in months were all leaning on it, and she let them lean. "The sworn statement of Renn Calloway, taken before witnesses last night at the Ketchum cabin, attesting that the ledger entries transferring water rights from independent holders — including one Tomás Joubert, deceased — to the consortium were forged at the direction of named principals of that consortium. The names are in the statement. There are eleven of us in this room who heard him make it."

"Third item." She nodded to Ada, who came forward on legs that shook and did not stop. "The direct testimony of Miss Ada Quist regarding the night of the twenty-third, when armed men wearing consortium concession badges removed five federal surveyors to the old Consolidated workings and held them there, with intent, as stated to their faces by the mine foreman, to keep them until the permits lapsed and the land reverted."

Ada set her own deposition on the rail — six pages, written by lamplight in the prospector's cabin in a hand that wobbled and never once broke off. Then she looked up at Loudermilk, and her voice, when it came, was quiet and absolutely level.

"I watched my colleagues marched into that mine at gunpoint, sir. I lay in a hole in the ground for nine days listening for boots. I am prepared to repeat all of it under oath, in any court, at any hour, for as long as it takes. But I would take it as a professional courtesy if we did not have to wait past noon, because at noon the permits lapse, and I have read the statute, and I believe what happens after noon gets considerably more complicated for everyone in this room except me."

Silence. The stove ticked. Outside, through the fly-specked window, Coral could see the crowd on the boardwalk growing, and see Aldous Crane standing at the edge of it, very still, doing arithmetic of his own.

Loudermilk took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, put them back on, and looked at the maps for a long time. When he spoke, his voice had shed its deacon's hesitancy entirely.

"The consortium's filings," he said slowly, "were accepted on the basis of survey documents furnished by — I recall now — a subcontracted guide. A Mr. Calloway, I believe the signature read." His eyes lifted to the doorway. "Is that the same gentleman?"

"It is," Calloway said. His voice was rough, and he cleared his throat and started again. "It is. And I'm the one who wrote the figures on those copies, Registrar. The originals in that packet are the true ones. I've signed a statement saying so, and I'll sign whatever else you put in front of me, and I'll stand in front of Judge Marsham himself next week and say it out loud. I'm done running." He glanced once at Coral — one glance, quickly away — "Running's the only thing I was ever good at, and it turns out it isn't good enough."

Loudermilk nodded, slow, like a man accepting the weight of something heavy. Then he turned to the wall of drawers, and Coral watched him pull the key from his vest, and watched his hand hover a moment over the drawer marked *M — Meridian Consortium* — and in that hovering instant she understood that this small round clerk had probably suspected for years, the way clerks suspect, in the privacy of their ledgers, and had waited all that time for someone to walk through his door with proof enough to make suspicion safe.

His hand came down. The lock clicked. The drawer rolled open.

"What is the time?" he asked.

Ada checked the transit case's chronometer. "Eleven fifty-one."

"Then we have nine minutes." Loudermilk drew out the consortium's claim filings — a fat folder, ribbon-tied, smelling of the syndicate's money — and laid them on the rail beside the true maps. From another drawer he brought the contest forms, blank, and a pen, and an inkwell, and set them down with the ceremony of a man laying out communion.

"Contestants step forward."

* * *

They filed the contests at four minutes to noon.

Ada first, for the survey party, her signature pressed deep enough to show through the page. Then Hollis for the instrument record. Then, when Loudermilk asked whether anyone present wished to contest on grounds of fraudulent transfer, Coral stepped up to the rail and picked up the pen.

Her hand did not shake. She had expected it to. Three years she had imagined this — not this desk, exactly; she had imagined a different kind of reckoning entirely, conducted at closer range and higher altitude, with a rifle and a long sightline — but some version of it, some final stroke that closed the account Tomas had opened. And now here it was, and it was a fountain pen and a government form, and her hand was steady as a transit.

She wrote her name. Under it, in the space marked *grounds*, she wrote: *Fraudulent conveyance of water rights, Tomás Joubert, deceased, per attached affidavit.*

The ink needed a moment to dry. She stood there while it dried, looking down at the two words side by side on the form — *Joubert, deceased* — and felt nothing at all for the space of three heartbeats, and then felt everything, all of it at once, so that she had to grip the rail.

Outside, the church bell rang noon. It rang cracked and flat, the way it had rung all her life, and it sounded, just this once, like a verdict.

Loudermilk blotted the pages, tied the folder, and stamped it — a sound like a single gunshot, clean and final. "Filed and entered," he said. "The claims of the Meridian Water and Land Consortium are hereby contested pending judicial review. This matter goes before Federal Judge Marsham at the sitting of court, Tuesday next." He looked up over his spectacles at the room full of ruined, frozen, living people. "Ladies and gentlemen. I'd suggest you all get something to eat."

* * *

The street outside had changed while the ink dried.

The gray buggy was gone. Aldous Crane was gone with it — but two of his dark-coated men remained, standing at the corner by the assay office with the studied idleness of men under orders to stay visible, and Coral marked them the way she marked everything now: distance, cover, hands, escape routes. Old habits. They'd serve a few more days yet.

The crowd, though — the crowd had not dispersed. It had thickened. Word of what had happened behind that door moved through Dry Wells the way water moves through alkali, soaking into everything, and when Coral came down the steps the people didn't cheer, because desert people don't cheer; they just took off their hats, one by one, up and down the boardwalk, until the whole street stood bareheaded in the noon sun like a congregation.

An old woman near the well — Coral recognized her distantly, the widow Reyes, who had sold Tomas eggs the summer before he died — reached up and touched Coral's sleeve as she passed, and said only, "Your brother's water," and patted her arm, and let her go.

Calloway stood at the hitching rail with his hat in his hands, waiting to be arrested or thanked or shot, and receiving none of the three. The territorial marshal was somewhere south with a warrant that wouldn't arrive for days. So for now he simply stood there, a man suspended between identities, and when Coral passed him he said, quietly, not looking at her:

"Tuesday. I'll be there."

"You'll be there," she agreed. "In custody, if the marshal's quick. On your honor, if he isn't. Either way — you'll be there."

He nodded. And then, because the day had already cost more honesty than most years, he added: "The letter. The one you read in the cabin. What he asked for — the ridge, the ashes." His jaw worked. "When this is done, if you want company for the ride up, I'd consider it an honor I haven't earned. And if you don't, I'll understand that too."

Coral looked at him a long moment. The sun was full on the street now, burning off the last of the storm's damp, and somewhere a door banged, and the ordinary noise of a town resuming itself rose around them like water filling a footprint.

"I ride alone," she said. "That part isn't yours."

"No," he said. "It isn't."

She gathered her reins from Mercer and swung up, and the horse stood steady under her, and for the first time in six days — for the first time, if she was honest, in three years — Coral Joubert sat a saddle with nowhere to go.

Tuesday, the judge. Tuesday, the testimony. Tuesday, Calloway in the witness chair saying aloud the names he'd swallowed for a decade, and warrants going out like weather toward men in gray coats who had never once gotten wet.

And after Tuesday —

After Tuesday, a ridge. An urn. A name spoken into the wind.

She turned the horse toward the Dry Wells Hotel, where a bed waited, and a bath, and the first meal in six days that didn't come out of a tin, and behind her the mountains stood white and silent, already letting go of what had happened there, the way mountains do — keeping only the shape of it, never the story.

The story, for once, was coming down to the valley. And on Tuesday, it would be told in a courtroom, under oath, by everyone who'd survived to tell it.
