## 20. Testimony

The courthouse in Dry Wells had been a saloon before it was a courthouse, and it still smelled faintly of spilled whiskey and old smoke, as though the walls remembered what the town preferred to forget. Coral Joubert sat in the third row of benches — not the front, because the front was for people who wanted to be seen, and not the back, because the back was for people who wanted to leave — and watched the morning light come through the tall windows in long dusty columns that moved with the wind outside.

She had not slept. None of them had, really, since the descent — eleven days ago now, though it felt like a season. Ada Quist sat two rows ahead of her with a blanket over her shoulders despite the stove's heat, her hands still wrapped where the frostbite had taken some feeling and left the rest tender. The other five surveyors sat together like a choir without a hymn, cleaned up, bearded, blinking at ceilings as if they'd forgotten buildings had them. Calloway was not in the room yet. Calloway was being held.

Judge Aldous Merrick had come out from the territorial capital on the same train that brought the warrants, a small dry man with spectacles that magnified his eyes into something owl-like and patient. He had listened to Coral's first statement the evening she walked into the land office — mud-frozen, snow-burned, eleven days on the trail behind her — and he had not interrupted once. That was how she'd known he was serious. Men who intended to dismiss you interrupted.

The room filled slowly. Land men from the syndicate's offices sat near the front wearing good coats and bad expressions. Ranchers stood along the walls because there weren't enough benches, hats in their hands, faces Coral knew from three years of riding a circuit that never stayed anywhere long enough to know her back. She recognized the widow Harkness from Pine Hollow. She recognized the schoolteacher who had once asked her if rangers ever got lonely, and she had answered yes, and then ridden away before the woman could ask anything else.

Today she could not ride away. Today the trail ended at a desk, and she had to sit still while justice did its work without her hands.

* * *

They brought Calloway in at nine o'clock through the side door, in irons, flanked by two deputies who looked embarrassed about the whole arrangement, as though escorting a man to save a valley were somehow against the natural order. He'd been given a shave and a clean shirt — someone in Dry Wells had decided a man testifying against the syndicate ought to look respectable doing it — but the mountain was still on him. It was in the way he held his left hand slightly apart from his body, the fingers stiff and half-curled where the cold had gotten into them on the whiteout crossing. It would be months before those hands worked right, if they ever did. He'd broken trail for six hours above treeline until they stopped working at all, and Coral had taken the point from him without a word, and neither of them had mentioned it since.

He caught her eye when the deputies seated him. She gave him nothing. He nodded anyway, as if nothing were an answer he'd learned to read.

Judge Merrick opened proceedings the way a man opens a gate in high wind — braced, deliberate, no wasted motion. There were lawyers present for the syndicate, two of them, arrived on the same train as the judge, and they spent the first hour objecting to everything: to the admissibility of maps carried off a mountain by a ranger with no warrant, to the testimony of a surveyor whose frostbitten hands made her signature unreliable, to the very jurisdiction of the hearing itself. Merrick overruled them each time in a voice like gravel poured slow, and each overruling landed in the room like a stone dropped down a shaft — you heard it fall a long way before it stopped.

Then they called Renn Calloway to the stand.

Coral had imagined this moment for three years. She had imagined it in a hundred shapes: him running, him cornered, him begging, him lying, him dying with her name in his mouth or Tomas's. She had rehearsed what she would say, what she would do, how her hands would feel around the truth of him. What she had never imagined was this — sitting thirty feet away with her boots flat on a plank floor, watching him swear an oath in a voice roughened by altitude and frost, and feeling almost nothing in her trigger finger. Almost. Not quite nothing. But close enough to notice the distance.

He told it plainly. That was the thing she hadn't prepared for either — the plainness. No performance. He described how he'd come west broke at twenty-two and gone to work for a land broker named Harlan Vess — the same Vess, though the room didn't know it yet, whose foreman's badge Coral had seen at the Consolidated mine — and how Vess had taught him that water rights were just paper until somebody made them true, and that making paper true was a trade like farriering or surveying, a craft you got better at. He described the ledger entries. He described Tomas Joubert's claim, the spring box, the filing dates, the way a signature could be shifted a season forward on a copy and a claim could simply evaporate, legally, cleanly, leaving a man standing on ground that belonged to someone else.

"He came to me," Calloway said, and his voice cracked on it, and he let it crack. "Tomas Joubert came to my office in Dry Wells with his ledger and his receipts and he asked me to help him find where the error was. I shook his hand. I told him I'd look into it. And that night I burned the original filings."

The room made a sound — not loud, but collective, the sound of forty people inhaling at once.

"I didn't know he'd die," Calloway said. "I want that understood. I want it written down that I didn't know. But I also want it understood that I didn't check. I took his money — whatever the syndicate paid me to make the problem go away — and I rode south and I let the desert do the rest. And when word came that a man had been found dead of thirst twelve miles from a spring he owned on paper, I told myself it wasn't mine. Three years I told myself that." He looked down at his ruined hands. "It was mine. Every mile of it."

Coral found that she was gripping the bench in front of her hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and that the grip was not rage. Or not only rage. It was the grip of a woman holding something heavy in place so it wouldn't shift while it settled — the way you hold a fence post plumb while the concrete sets.

* * *

They broke at noon. The syndicate's lawyers spent the break in a huddle by the stove, talking low and fast, and Coral noticed that one of them kept looking at the door, and that the door kept not opening, and that whatever reinforcement they'd expected from the capital was not coming. Word traveled fast on a train line. Warrants traveled faster.

Ada Quist testified in the afternoon. She did it sitting upright despite the blanket, despite the hands, in a voice that started thin and gained iron with every sentence. She walked the court through the aquifer surveys — the real ones, the ones the syndicate's forged maps had buried. She explained, patiently, the way she must have explained things to students once, how a contour line could be moved a hundred feet and turn wet rock into dry rock on paper, and how the land the syndicate had bought cheap — the land they'd needed cleared of claims, cleared of surveyors, cleared of witnesses — sat directly over the deepest artesian basin in the territory.

"They weren't buying water," Ada said. "They were buying the absence of it. Every dry claim they sold was a claim they'd emptied first. And every acre over real water was left unclaimed, waiting for the permits to lapse, waiting for the land to revert, waiting for nobody to be alive who knew the difference." She lifted her bandaged hands slightly, an offering. "We knew the difference. That's why five of us are alive and why we nearly weren't."

Merrick asked her how she'd survived. She looked, for just a moment, at Coral — a look that carried the whole of the mine adit, the shared body heat, the terrible arithmetic of two women keeping each other warm enough to see morning — and said, "Someone found me who shouldn't have been able to. I've stopped asking how."

* * *

The names came last.

Calloway gave them the way a man gives up a map he's memorized — slowly, precisely, in order, so that nothing could be lost between the telling and the writing. Harlan Vess, principal. Judge's clerk Emory Satt, who had laundered the filings through the territorial recorder's office. A mining investor named Ochs whose money had bought the badged riders. Two more names Coral didn't know, eastern men, names that meant railroads and banks and distance. Merrick's clerk wrote each one down and read each one back, and Calloway confirmed each one, and when it was done the judge removed his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve with great care, and put them back on.

"Warrants will issue this afternoon," he said. "For all named principals, on charges of fraud, conspiracy, assault, and murder." He let the word *murder* sit in the room for a while. "Mr. Calloway. You understand that your cooperation does not purchase your freedom."

"Yes, Your Honor."

"You understand that you will answer for Tomas Joubert regardless of what becomes of the men above you."

"Yes, Your Honor." Calloway's voice was steady. His ruined hands were not; they trembled in his lap where he'd folded them, and he let them tremble, hiding nothing. "I'd have answered for him three years ago if I'd had the courage. This is just the version of it where I'm useful first."

The deputies took him up. As they passed the third row he turned his head, and for a moment their eyes met again, and this time he didn't nod. He just looked at her — a look that said everything he'd already said aloud and one thing more, which was *thank you for not letting the mountain have me*, or maybe *thank you for letting the mountain have me a little*. She couldn't have translated it under oath. She only knew that watching him go, in irons, toward a cell instead of a grave, felt less like losing than she had expected and more like setting down weight than she had earned.

* * *

They came to her at the end. Merrick had saved her for last, he said, because beginnings were easy and endings were work.

"Ranger Joubert. The territory is prepared to offer a reward. The syndicate's frozen assets include a discretionary fund, and the federal attorney has authorized payment for recovery of the survey team and delivery of evidence leading to conviction. The figure discussed is four thousand dollars."

Four thousand dollars. Coral turned the number over the way she'd turn a strange rock on the trail, looking at what lived underneath it. Four thousand dollars was a ranch. Was ten years of not moving. Was every debt she'd never let herself owe anyone, paid off at once, a door closing gently behind her into a life where she wanted things again and could afford to keep them.

She thought about Tomas. Not the death — she was done carrying the death in front of everything else — but the ledger itself, the careful hand, the columns of gallons and seasons, the way he'd believed the desert's water belonged to whoever needed it next and had only written the numbers down to prove it. He'd have laughed at four thousand dollars. He'd have laughed the way you laugh at a man offering to sell you the sky by the yard.

"No, sir," she said.

Merrick waited. The room waited. Somewhere near the front, one of the syndicate's lawyers stopped writing.

"Then state what you want," the judge said. "Within reason."

"The aquifer," Coral said. "All of it. The land over the real water — the unclaimed acres, the reverted parcels, the commons along the Vermilion drainage. I want it entered as unclaimable. Public water. No filings accepted, no sales, no leases, not to the syndicate, not to honest men, not to the territory itself. Water under that ground stays where it is and belongs to whoever's thirsty." She paused, and heard her own voice steady, and was briefly surprised by it. "My brother kept a ledger because he believed numbers could protect a thing. They can't. Only law can, and only if the law says *no* loudly enough. So say no, Judge. Say it in ink. That's my reward."

Merrick regarded her for a long moment over the owl-spectacles. Then he made a note — a short one, unhurried — and said, "So ordered, pending ratification. Ranger Joubert, the territory owes you thanks it will express badly and remember longer than you expect."

"That's fine," Coral said. "I'm not good at being thanked either."

There was laughter in the room — small, tired, real — and the gavel came down, and it was over. Just like that. Three years ended in the sound of wood on wood, and the desert went on outside the windows exactly as it had before, indifferent and shining, and Coral Joubert stood up from the third row and discovered that her legs were shaking and that she was, for the first time in three years, in no hurry whatsoever to be anywhere else.

* * *

Evening found her at the corral behind the assay office, checking hooves that didn't need checking, because a woman has to put her hands somewhere after a day like that. The light was going amber and long across the flats. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing. The mountains to the north wore their new snow like a debt paid.

Ada found her there, walking carefully on the packed dirt, her bandaged hands tucked in her coat.

"They're saying the marshals leave for the capital tomorrow," Ada said. "With him."

"I know."

"You could go with them. See it finished."

Coral ran a thumb down the mare's fetlock and found nothing wrong, as expected. "It is finished," she said. "That's the strange part. I spent three years thinking finishing would feel like something breaking. It feels like—" She searched for it. "Like coming over a rise and finding the camp already pitched."

Ada stood beside her a while, watching the light fail. Then, quietly: "What will you do now? When the thaw comes. When there's no trail."

And Coral, who had asked herself that question every night for three years and always answered it with distance, found that the answer had changed shape somewhere on the mountain without her permission.

"There's a ridge east of here," she said. "Above the spring box. Come spring, I've got ashes to carry up it." She looked north, at the white peaks going pink and then gray, and something in her chest that had been clenched since a dry well and a dead man's ledger finally, cautiously, began to loosen. "After that — I reckon I'll stay long enough to find out what staying feels like."

Behind them, in the window of the assay office, lamplight came on, and someone inside was already talking about spring.
