## 21. What Stopping Costs

The thaw came to Dry Wells the way grief came to Coral Joubert — slowly, and then all at once.

One morning in late February the icicles on the eaves of the boarding house began to weep, and by noon they were dripping steady as a metronome, and by the following week the street outside was a brown river running downhill past the assay office, carrying winter's grit and horse dung and one lost mitten out toward the flats. The mountains to the north still wore their white, but down here the desert remembered itself. Creosote smell rose up soft and resinous from the wet sand. Somewhere a meadowlark sang its rusty-hinge song, the first Coral had heard since autumn, and she stood in the doorway of her rented room with a cup of coffee going cold in her hand and realized she had been in Dry Wells for eleven weeks.

Eleven weeks. She had not stayed anywhere that long since before Tomas died.

She kept waiting for it to itch. That was how it had always been — three days in a town and her hands started looking at the road, four and her horse grew restless under her, five and she'd be gone before light with some errand invented to justify the leaving. Moving was the trick she'd learned. Motion kept the grief spread thin, like butter over too much bread, so no single moment held enough of it to break through. Stop moving and it all pooled, deep and black, right where you stood.

But here she stood, eleven weeks, and the pool had not drowned her. It had simply been there, the way the mountains were there, and she had learned — slowly, clumsily, the way Ada learned her contour lines — to stand beside it without falling in.

* * *

The fence work started by accident.

Old Man Pruett's place sat on the south edge of town, forty acres of alfalfa and a windmill that complained all day like a child denied something, and the winter had taken his east fence apart in three places. Coral rode past it one gray morning on her way back from checking a stock tank and saw him standing at the break with a bundle of wire and a hammer and eighty-one years in his shoulders, staring at the job the way a man stares at a river he means to swim.

She didn't decide anything. Her hands decided. She was off the horse and tying it to his gatepost before any argument reached her brain, and when she held out her palm for the pliers he looked at her a long time over his white whiskers and then handed them over without a word, which was the correct amount of talk for that kind of morning.

They worked until the sun stood high and warm. Post-hole digging in thawing ground was honest misery, and Coral found she liked the misery, liked the way it filled her arms and emptied her head, liked the simple arithmetic of a fence: this post is broken, set a new post, the fence stands again. Nothing about it required her to think about the Consolidated mine or the sound the timber made when Calloway blew it, or the guard who grabbed Ada's arm in the dark and what Coral had done about it — the weight of that man going down into the lower workings, the sound he'd made, the fact that she had not dreamed of it once in eleven weeks and then dreamed of nothing else for six nights running.

"You're the ranger," Pruett said around midday, sharing out cold biscuits from a cloth. "The one brought those survey people down off the mountain."

"Some of them."

"All of 'em that could come." He chewed a while. "My wife's buried up past the north fence. Forty years I've told myself I'd move her down to the cemetery where I'll be, and forty years I haven't done it. You know why?"

Coral shook her head.

"Because doing it means admitting there's nobody left to do it after me." He brushed crumbs from his whiskers. "Some jobs you put off because they're hard. Some you put off because finishing them ends something."

She thought about the small tin box in her saddlebag, riding with her now for three years, holding what Tomas had asked her to hold. She ate her biscuit and said nothing, and Pruett nodded as if she'd said plenty.

She came back the next day and fixed the west fence too. There was no reason to. The fence wasn't hers. But the work was good and the old man made bad coffee and told stories about the range before the railroads, and when the week ended she'd repaired every strand on his property and he'd paid her in eggs and a jar of his dead wife's peach preserves that he clearly couldn't bring himself to open, and Coral understood that she had just been given something, though she couldn't have named what.

* * *

Ada Quist came to the boarding house on a Tuesday, standing on the step with her collar up against the wind and a roll of paper under her arm like a woman arriving for an appointment she half hoped would be cancelled.

"I can't read them," she said, without greeting. "The maps. My own maps."

Coral stepped back to let her in.

It had been two months since the mountain, and Ada had filled out some, color in her face where the frost and the fear had scraped it away, flesh on her wrists where the mine had starved it off. But her hands still had a tremor in cold weather, and she still slept with her boots by the bed, laced and ready, and Coral recognized both habits the way you recognize your own face in a stranger.

"They're my drawings," Ada went on, unrolling the sheets on Coral's table and weighting the corners with the salt cellar and a tin cup. "I drew every line of these. And now I look at them and it's like reading a letter in a language I used to speak. The doctor says it's the head injury. The doctor says a great many things." Her finger hovered over the sheet without touching it. "I look at this and I see lines. I don't see *ground*."

Coral leaned over the map. The Vermilion Range in section lines and elevations, the aquifer contours she and Ada had carried down through the whiteout to a federal judge's desk — the document that had unmade the syndicate's claims, that had put warrants out for three men whose names Coral had learned only in a courtroom, men she'd never meet, justice administered at such a distance it hardly felt like hers.

"This is a hill," Coral said, touching a closed loop. "And these numbers are how high it is. And these lines bunched close together mean it rises steep, and lines far apart mean it slopes easy."

"I know that," Ada said, sharp, and then shut her eyes. "I know that. I knew it well enough to draw it. I just can't —" Her hand opened and closed. "It's like the knowing went somewhere and won't come home."

So Coral did what she'd watched no one ever do for her: she sat down across the table and started from the beginning, as if Ada knew nothing, as if they were both beginners, and found that beginning again was its own kind of mercy.

They met twice a week, then three times. Coral would take her out past the town wells to the low hills where the land itself was the textbook, walk her up a slope and have her feel the steepness in her legs, then show her the same steepness drawn as crowded lines on paper. They'd sit on a rock with the whole basin spread below them and Coral would say, *where does the water go*, and make Ada trace it with her finger, down the draws, along the fall of the land, pooling where the contours cupped like hands.

"It runs to the unclaimed section," Ada said one afternoon, sudden, her finger stopping on the flat below the ridge. "That's why the syndicate wanted it cleared. That's why they took us." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and alive. "I remember. God help me, Elodie — Coral — I *remember*."

"I know," Coral said. "You remembered it in the adit, half-frozen, when remembering could've got you killed. Memory comes back the same way spring does. Can't hurry it. But it comes."

Ada wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way women did when they refused to make a ceremony of crying. Then she pointed at another contour and said, "All right. Where does *that* water go?" and they went back to work.

What Coral never told her — what she barely told herself — was that the lessons were not entirely for Ada. Every hour Coral spent explaining the land, she was re-reading it herself, and finding that she still loved it. Not the fleeing-across-it love she'd known for three years, the love of a woman using distance as a drug, but the older love, the one from before, when the desert had been home instead of escape. The land hadn't changed. Only her reasons for riding it had.

* * *

In March the judge's clerk came through on the stage with papers, and among them was a check.

Reward money. Territorial funds disbursed for the recovery of the survey party, less expenses, signed and sealed. Two hundred and forty dollars, which was more money than Coral had seen at one time since her father sold the home place, and she stood in the land office holding it and felt nothing but a kind of cold arithmetic.

Two hundred forty dollars. The guard in the mine had weighed less than that, she supposed, in somebody's ledger. Five surveyors' terror had weighed less. Tomas's whole life, his water rights, his claim, his grave-less death in a dry wash — the syndicate had priced all of it, and the price had been small, insultingly small, and Vess had offered her a cut of exactly that logic on a mountainside and she had refused it, and now the government offered her the same coin wearing better clothes.

She carried the check to the bank and endorsed it over — not to herself. To the territorial fund for maintaining the aquifer surveys, earmarked, per her written instruction, for keeping the unclaimed sections surveyed and mapped and free. Let the water stay commons. Let no one ever buy what ran beneath it. It was the only monument she cared to raise, and it wasn't stone, and it wouldn't need her to visit it.

The banker, a round man named Fetterman who had processed her brother's stolen claim paperwork years ago without ever once looking up from his desk, counted the endorsement twice and looked at her strangely.

"Most folks," he said, "would keep it."

"Most folks weren't there," Coral said, and walked out into the sun.

* * *

April came in like a rumor and then like a fact. The alfalfa on Pruett's place greened up. The willows along the dry wash went gold-green with pollen. And Coral noticed, one morning while tightening a hinge on the boarding house gate, that she had stopped checking the northern horizon.

For three years she had read the north the way other people read weather — scanning for movement, for dust, for the shape of a rider who might be Renn Calloway, though after the ledge and the trial she'd known he was in custody, known it rationally, the way you know a tooth is pulled and still flinch at the socket. The habit had outlived its reason. Now, catching herself facing south, working, unhurried, she understood the habit had quietly died sometime during the thaw, and she hadn't even attended the funeral.

There were other things she noticed. That she knew the names of the boarding house cat's kittens. That she'd eaten supper at the Pruett table four times and had opinions about salt. That when the marshal stopped her in the street and asked, casual, whether she'd want her commission renewed come summer — seasonal patrol work, the high country, good pay — she heard herself say, "Ask me in June," and meant it as neither yes nor no, and discovered she was a person who could hold a question open without it killing her.

Stillness hurt. That was the truth of it, and she wouldn't lie to herself about it anymore than she'd lie on a trail report. Stillness hurt like a healing break hurts — that deep-set ache that means the bone is knitting, that you can't rub out because the rubbing *is* the mending. Some nights it hurt enough that she lay awake listening to the windmill complaining across town and rehearsed, out of pure old habit, the route she'd take if she left tomorrow. Santa Fe, maybe. Or clear south to the border country where nobody asked questions.

But then morning came, and the fence needed fixing, or Ada needed a lesson, or the peaches needed eating, and the road was still there — the road was always there, that was the whole point of roads — and she found she could let it wait. Wanting nothing had been her code. Wanting *these things* — eggs and bad coffee and a tremor-handed friend tracing water across a map — was turning out to be survivable after all.

* * *

On the last Sunday of April, Coral took the tin box down from the top of her saddlebag, where it had lived for three years and eleven weeks, and carried it out past the north fence of the Pruett place, where the ground rose toward a ridge with a view of the whole valley.

She didn't dig. Not yet. She sat on the ridge with the box in her lap until the shadows went long, reading the land the way Ada was learning to read it — where the water went, where the frost sat latest, where the soil was deep enough to hold what she meant to give it. Tomas had wanted the high ground, she remembered, wanted to look out over everything he'd tried to build and lose and try again. This ridge wasn't his ridge. His ridge was a hundred miles north, in country she might not ride again this year, and that was all right. Ashes didn't need the exact spot. They needed the stopping.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she'd borrow Pruett's spade.

She was halfway back to the gate when she saw the rider coming up the county road from the south, raising a long tail of dust, and something in her chest did the old clench — three years of habit firing at a silhouette — before her eye sorted the details: the dun horse, the canvas-wrapped instrument case strapped behind the saddle, the easy seat of someone who rode for a living and liked it.

The rider came on and halted at the gate, and pushed back a hat, and Coral saw it was one of the junior surveyors from the party — Halloran, the young one with the burned forearms who'd wept openly on the descent and pretended afterward he hadn't.

"Miss Joubert." He swung down, stiff-legged, and dug in his coat. "I was in Dry Wells on supply business and the clerk at the land office said I'd find you out this direction." He handed her an envelope, official cream, stamped with the federal eagle. "From Judge Marsham's office. He said — well, the note inside explains. He also said to tell you the hearings resume in Phoenix in three weeks, and that the principals named in Mr. Calloway's statement are contesting the aquifer findings, and the judge would —" Halloran glanced at the envelope, then at her face, and lowered his voice as if the valley might be listening. "He'd take it as a personal favor if the woman who carried the maps down the mountain were in the room when they tried."

Coral took the envelope and didn't open it. The wind moved the willows down by the wash. Behind her, faint across the fields, the windmill complained on, patient as ever, like something that intended to keep asking until it got an answer.

Three weeks. Phoenix was four days' ride. The hearings would run however long crooked men with lawyers could stretch them.

She looked south, down the long open road she'd ridden away down a hundred times, and for the first time in her life the road looked less like an exit than a door — one she could walk through and, what's more, walk back through.

"Well," she said to Halloran, and tucked the envelope unopened into her shirt, next to where the tin box would ride tomorrow, going into the ground at last. "I reckon I'd best see about a horse."
