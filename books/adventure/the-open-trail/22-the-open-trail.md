## 22. The Open Trail

The thaw came late that year, and when it came it came all at once, the way water always did in the desert — grudging for months and then reckless in a week. Coral watched it happen from the porch of the Dry Wells boarding house, coffee going cold in her hands, as the Vermilion Range shrugged off its white and ran brown with it, every gully and arroyo talking at once. Three years she had listened for hoofbeats behind her. Now she listened to meltwater, and could not make herself move until the cup was empty and the sun had cleared the water tower.

Ada Quist left on a Thursday, riding the stage east with a satchel of corrected aquifer maps and a federal subpoena in her coat pocket, and before she climbed aboard she took Coral's hand in both of hers and held it the way you hold something you are not sure will still be there when you look again.

"You could come," Ada said. "Washington. They'd give you a desk with your name on it."

"I'd set fire to it inside a month."

"I know," Ada said, and smiled, and it was the first smile Coral had seen on her that reached all the way up. "I told them you would. I told them that was the recommendation."

The stage pulled out in a fan of dust, and Coral stood in the road until the dust settled, and then she stood there a while longer, because the road went somewhere and she was not on it, and some old part of her — the part that had kept her saddle wet and her bedrolls cold for three years — raised its head and howled.

She let it howl. She went back inside and mended a fence.

* * *

The fences had been Vess's doing, mostly. The syndicate's riders had cut wire wherever it suited them in the years they'd been quietly strangling the range, and now that the warrants had gone out — three principals named, two already in custody in Tucson, one fled to Mexico with federal marshals a week behind him — nobody was left to maintain the damage. So Coral maintained it. She strung wire in the mornings and read contour lines with Ada in the afternoons until Ada left, and after that she read them alone, sitting on her porch with the ledger open on her knees, Tomas's cramped handwriting marching up the margins the way it always had, patient and exact.

*Water is not property. Water is the range itself, awake.*

He had written that when he was nineteen, full of certainty, before the world showed him what brokers did with certainty. Coral had read the line a hundred times in a hundred camps and it had always sounded to her like a boy's naivety. Sitting still, it sounded like something else. It sounded like a man who had understood his country better than the men who killed him, and who had died not because he was wrong but because he was right in a way that could be forged around.

Judge Harrow had asked her, in the cool of his chambers after the testimony, whether she wanted the reward. Five hundred dollars. Her name in the territorial gazette. She had looked at the paper he slid across the desk and thought of Renn Calloway in a cell in Prescott, hands folded, waiting to be moved, waiting to testify again, waiting out whatever sentence the court decided a man earned for forging a ledger and then spending three years running from the sister of the man it destroyed.

"Keep the money," she had said. "Put it in the aquifer. Keep the land unclaimed. That's the whole reward."

Harrow had studied her for a long moment. "That's not how rewards work, Ranger."

"Then don't give it to anybody." She had stood, and put on her hat, and paused at the door. "Judge. The water under the Vermilion — it doesn't belong to the syndicate, and it doesn't belong to the survey office, and it doesn't belong to me. It belongs to whatever's thirsty. Write it down however the law lets you write that."

He had written something. She never learned exactly what. But in March a survey crew came through Dry Wells with new instruments and new maps, and they staked nothing, and they left the springs alone, and one of the young men — a boy with soft hands and a good horse — asked her at the saloon whether she was the ranger who'd brought them down off the mountain.

"Eleven people came off that mountain," she said. "I was one of them."

He bought her a drink anyway. She drank it, and went home, and slept the whole night through without dreaming of snow.

* * *

She knew it was time when the lupine came in.

The ridge above the old Joubert homestead turned purple every April — had turned purple every April of her life, and of Tomas's, and of their father's before them. Tomas had asked for it in the letter, the last letter, the one Coral had read aloud by lamplight in a prospector's cabin while the snow came down like a curtain drawn across the world. *Not in the churchyard,* he'd written. *Up on the lupine ridge, where you can see the whole east fork. I want to be where the water starts.*

She'd carried the small tin box for three years. Through the alkali flats and the town of Dry Wells and the Vermilion high country and the whiteout and the ledge where a man hung by her rope and she had held on. The box had ridden in her saddlebag through everything, through the coldest night and the worst decision, and it had never weighed anything, and it had weighed everything, and she had not been able to set it down, and she had not been able to open it, and so she had kept moving, because a body in motion does not have to decide.

On the last morning of April she saddled the dun at first light, wrapped a week's supplies behind the cantle out of pure habit, and then, standing there with the cinch in her hands, she took the supplies back off and put them in the tack room. Two days' ride. She'd be home by Sunday.

*Home.* The word arrived without permission and sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

She rode out of Dry Wells with the sun coming up behind the water tower, and nobody asked her where she was going, because everybody knew.

* * *

The east fork was running high.

She heard it a mile out — that low continuous conversation of water over stone, the sound Tomas had loved more than any sound on earth, the sound he'd said was the desert counting its money. The winter's snow, the storm that had nearly killed eleven people and had instead delivered them, was in that water now. All of it. Every flake that had erased their back-trail and sealed the mine and turned the world to white blindness had melted and gone home to the river, and the river carried it down through the range, past the springs the syndicate had wanted to own, past the land that stayed unclaimed, past everything they had tried to buy and could not.

The lupine was thick on the ridge. She rode up through it with the blossoms brushing her stirrups, purple so deep it looked wet, and the smell of it — green and cold and faintly sweet — rose up around her like something with arms.

At the top she stopped and did not dismount for a long time.

The whole east fork lay below her, silver-threaded in the morning light, running the way it had run before men ever put a number on it. The Vermilion Range stood up behind it, still wearing its high snow, and the desert opened out south of it, vast and patient and indifferent to every claim ever filed against it. Wind moved across the ridge in long slow pushes, bending the lupine in waves, and somewhere below a meadowlark was singing the same four notes over and over, the way they did, as if the song were a fence being built note by note around a piece of the morning.

Coral got down. She unsaddled the dun and hobbled him in the grass, because she was not going to be quick about this, and then she stood at the highest point of the ridge with the tin box in both hands and did not open it.

Three years. Three years of tracking a horse thief across the alkali and staying four days too long. Three years of a ledger read to rags. Three years of a boot print in the high country that had fused two hunts into one obsession, and a mine, and a rope, and a man's weight on the end of it, and the moment — she would carry that moment until she died — the moment when holding on had cost more than she had and she had held on anyway, because letting go would have made her a different person, a person who administered justice by gravity, a person Tomas would not have recognized.

She had thought for a long time that the box was the reason she couldn't stop. That if she could just get him up here, she'd be free to ride on.

Standing in the lupine with the wind in her face, she understood it had been the other way around. She hadn't been unable to stop because of the box. She had been unable to open the box because stopping was where he was, and where he was was where it hurt, and she had arranged her whole life around the principle that a person could outrun a hurt if she never slept two nights in the same place. The open trail. Keep moving, want nothing. It had kept her alive. It had also kept her from this — from the four notes of the meadowlark, from the smell of the lupine, from the sound of her brother's river, from every good thing that only exists in a place you agree to stay.

"Tommy," she said.

The wind took it. The wind took everything, that was its nature; that was why the desert was honest and the towns were not. She crouched, and worked the lid off the tin, and the ashes inside were pale gray, fine as the alkali she had once tracked a thief across, and her hands did not shake, and she was grateful for that, and then she decided it didn't matter whether they shook or not, and was grateful for that too.

"I brought you the whole story," she said. "You'll want it in order."

She told him. She told him about the telegram and the marshal and the camp with no footprints, and Ada Quist in the mine adit with frost in her eyelashes, and the forged maps, and the badged men, and the switchbacks where she had caught a stranger's arm on the scree and understood in one lurching instant that she could not carry another death. She told him about the fire at timberline and the man sitting beside it with his hands open, and the confession, and the snow, and the letter read aloud — *your letter, Tommy, I read it out loud, Ada cried, I didn't, I was too busy being furious at how much I didn't* — and the standoff in the cabin where she had won with words instead of a gun and it had unsettled her more than any ambush ever had. She told him about Vess's offer, insultingly small, and the blown timber, and the guard who had grabbed Ada and the choice Coral had made, the one she would carry forever, the one she did not dress up or explain away, because he was her brother and he deserved the whole ugly weight of it.

"And then the ledge," she said. Her voice went quiet. The river kept on below, patient, unimpressed. "You know who it was. You've known since the boot print. He went over and I held him. I want you to understand — I want *me* to understand — it wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't even mercy, not exactly. It was that letting go would have cost more than holding on, and I finally know the difference between a cost and a price. A cost is what you lose. A price is what you pay to become who you're going to be. I paid it. I'm still paying it. Some mornings I wake up in the boarding house and my hands are around a rope that isn't there."

She stood. The wind came up the face of the ridge and bent the lupine flat and straightened it again, and high overhead a pair of ravens turned on the updraft without a single wingbeat, riding the air the way she had once ridden the trail, going nowhere on purpose.

"He's in Prescott," she said. "He'll testify again in the fall. He asked me — through his lawyer, can you imagine, through a *lawyer* — whether I'd come watch. I haven't answered. I might. I might not. It's the first thing in three years I've let myself not decide today."

She tipped the tin.

The ashes went out in a long gray ribbon, and the wind took them the way the wind takes everything — out over the east fork, out over the water starting, out over the range that had made him and unmade her and was, this morning, doing neither, just being itself, purple and green and loud with meltwater. Some of the ash came back on a backdraft and dusted her sleeves and she didn't brush it off.

"Water is not property," she said. "Water is the range itself, awake. You were right, Tommy. You were right and they killed you for it and the water doesn't care and the water is right too. I'm going to stop being angry at the water for not caring. That's the last piece. That's what's left."

She sat down in the lupine, then, properly sat, cross-legged like when they were children watching the fork run high, and she stayed until the sun stood straight overhead, and she ate the lunch she'd brought, and she let the meadowlark finish its fence, and when the ravens finally tilted and slid away south she watched them go all the way out of sight and felt — not nothing. Not the nothing she had chased for three years, the clean empty of the open trail. Something heavier and better. Something with weight to it, the way a door has weight.

* * *

She came down off the ridge in the long gold light of late afternoon and the dun was waiting at the hobble, hipshot in the grass, and she saddled him with hands that moved slow on purpose.

The trail forked at the bottom of the ridge, the way it always had. South, the open trail: the alkali flats, the empty ranges, the next town and the one after, the whole enormous indifferent country where a person could keep moving until moving was all she was. She looked down it for a while. It looked the same as it always had. It would always be there. That was the thing about the open trail nobody told you — it wasn't an answer, it was just a road, and roads don't hold you, they only carry you past.

She turned the dun north. Toward the river. Toward Dry Wells, where the boarding house had a room she'd paid through the month, and the tack room had her gear in it, and Judge Harrow had mentioned — offhand, the way careful men mention things — that the new survey office would be needing someone who knew the high passes better than anyone alive, someone to walk the range and make sure the springs stayed unclaimed and the water stayed awake. It wasn't a badge, exactly. It wasn't the trail, either. It was something in between, something that had never existed before, something she'd have to invent as she went.

The dun picked his way down through the lupine and up the near bank of the east fork, and the water talked beside them the whole way, counting its money, and Coral rode with her hat low and her face turned toward home, and behind her the ridge held its purple in the last of the light, and the wind off it carried a little gray dust of ashes out over the water starting, going wherever the wind went, which was everywhere, which was, she thought, exactly right.

At the first bend of the river she stopped the dun and looked back once — not at the ridge, but down the south fork, at the open trail unspooling away into the desert, patient as ever, asking nothing.

"Another time," she said, and it wasn't goodbye. It was the way you speak to a friend you'll see again when there's a reason.

Then she put her heels to the horse, and the river ran beside her all the way home.
