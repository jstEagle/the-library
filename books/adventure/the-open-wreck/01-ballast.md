## 1. Ballast

The winch cable sang its one flat note all morning, and Zephyrine Yarrow had learned to read it the way other people read weather. Today it said: *nothing down there but rust and patience.* She stood at the rail of the *Magpie's Debt* with a mug of tar-black coffee going cold in her hand, watching the crane swing the third lift of the day out of the green water — a tangle of hull plating from a fishing trawler that had been drunk on reef for eleven years.

"More ballast," Marisol called up from the deck, wiping her hands on a rag that had once been white. "You want me to cut it?"

Zephyrine considered the plate. Corroded through in three places, worth maybe forty credits as scrap, less than the diesel it cost to raise. But she'd learned a long time ago that salvage wasn't about what things were worth. It was about what you could carry.

"Cut it," she said. "We're done here."

The trawler job was the kind of work she took when the accounts got thin: unglamorous, low-risk, a harbor authority contract with paperwork instead of adventure. Three days of lifting scrap off a known wreck in twelve meters of warm, boring water. The kind of diving where nothing went wrong because nothing was there to go wrong. She should have felt relief. Instead she felt the old itch under her sternum, the one that said *keep moving* even when moving made no sense.

She finished her coffee and tipped the dregs over the side, watching them bloom and vanish.

*Twenty years,* she thought, not for the first time, not for the thousandth. *Twenty years since the* Vespera *went down.*

Her father's boat hadn't been a wreck anyone salvaged. The navy had cordoned the site within a day, called it a structural failure during a storm survey, paid out a settlement that covered the funeral and six months of rent, and closed the file. Zephyrine had been nine. She remembered mostly the smell of the naval officer's uniform — machine oil and starch — and how he'd shaken her mother's hand like he was passing over something heavy and wanted to be rid of it quickly.

The file stayed closed. The waters stayed open, technically, but no charter would take divers out there, and after a while nobody asked why. That was the coast's way. Questions were ballast you dropped at the first sign of weather.

"Boss." Teodor's voice came up from the engine hatch, gravel over gravel. He emerged into the light squinting, a smear of grease across his jaw like war paint. "Compressor's running hot again. Third time this week."

"You said you fixed it."

"I said I *improved* it. Fixing costs money." He held up his hands, palms out, fingers webbed with black. "I can keep it alive till we're paid. After that, it wants a new impeller housing or it wants a funeral."

"Then we get paid." She checked the manifest on her slate. Harbor authority transfer cleared in two days, minus fees, minus Teodor's parts budget, which left — she did the arithmetic without wanting to — enough for the marina slip, the fuel bill, and roughly one week of being afloat. "Barely."

"Barely is my favorite number," Teodor said, and dropped back into the hatch.

That was the crew, more or less. Teodor who kept the dead engine breathing through stubbornness and theft of parts from his own past jobs. Marisol, nineteen, deckhand, who'd come aboard two seasons ago with a duffel bag and no last name she'd admit to and turned out to be the best line-handler Zephyrine had ever watched. And Ash, the navigator, who was currently nowhere visible, which meant she was below with the charts, which meant she was fine. Ash was always fine. Ash was always exactly where she said she'd be, which Zephyrine had privately decided was either a virtue or a very well-kept secret.

They were four people on a forty-year-old salvage tender with a compressor held together by argument. It wasn't much. It was hers.

* * *

The message came at dusk, while the harbor lights were coming on in strings along the breakwater and the air smelled of rain that hadn't committed yet.

Marisol brought the tablet up to the wheelhouse, where Zephyrine was pretending to review invoices. "Contract came through. The one you flagged last month — the freighter *Corvane*, sunk off Point Meridian. Insurance wants cargo location confirmed before they'll authorize recovery." She hesitated, which Marisol almost never did. "There's an addendum."

"Read it."

"'Advisory: the contracted zone has been designated restricted by the Coastal Naval Authority as of this date. Salvage operations may proceed only under escort and observation. All questions regarding the restriction are to be directed to—'" Marisol looked up. "'—are to be directed to no one.'"

"They actually wrote that?"

"They wrote that."

Zephyrine took the tablet. The chart overlay loaded slowly, the way it did when the signal was bad, drawing the coastline in pieces — headland, bay, reef shelf — and then the restricted zone bloomed red across the water like a bruise.

She knew the shape of that water before the label finished rendering. She knew it the way you know a scar: by feel, in the dark, without looking.

Point Meridian. The *Vespera*'s grave sat eleven kilometers inside that red circle.

For a long moment the wheelhouse was very quiet. Somewhere below, Teodor's compressor coughed and caught. The tide slapped the pilings in its patient, indifferent rhythm.

"Pull it up," Marisol said quietly. "There's other contracts. There's always other contracts."

There were. There were always other contracts, and they were always somewhere else, and Zephyrine had spent twenty years making sure they were always somewhere else. She had a map in her head — she'd never drawn it, never spoken it aloud — of everywhere her father's boat wasn't. She'd worked the northern shelf, the far atolls, the drowned river towns two hundred kilometers upcoast. She had built a whole life as a shape drawn around an absence.

And now the navy had gone and painted the absence red, which meant they were still watching it, which meant — twenty years later — there was still something there to watch.

"Rent's due on the slip," she said.

"We can anchor out. We've anchored out before."

"The compressor needs parts. Teo needs parts. You need—" she gestured vaguely at Marisol, who needed nothing she'd ever admit to, "—things. I need things. Stopping costs more than going."

"That's not true and you know it. Stopping is free. Going is what costs."

Zephyrine almost smiled. Nineteen years old and wise as a barnacle. "Going is what keeps us honest."

"Going is what keeps us *moving,*" Marisol said. "Those aren't the same thing, boss. I've been watching."

Zephyrine set the tablet down on the chart table, faceup, the red zone glowing between them like a coal. Outside, the last light was draining off the water, and the harbor bells rang the change of watch, and everything was ordinary, everything was fine, everything was exactly as it had been every night for twenty years.

Except that her hands weren't steady. She put them flat on the table until they were.

*Say no,* said the part of her that had kept her alive this long. *Say no, take the trawler money, run north, forget the red circle exists.*

But another voice — smaller, older, buried deeper than any wreck — said: *they're still guarding it.*

If it were just a grave, you didn't guard a grave for twenty years. Graves got markers and flowers and eventually everyone's forgetting. This was something else. This was a cordon around a question, and questions guarded that carefully were questions someone was afraid of.

Her father had died in those waters, officially, of structural failure during a storm survey. Her father had been many things — careful, methodical, the kind of diver who checked his gauges twice and his knots three times — but he had not been careless, and the *Vespera* had been surveyed sound not six months before. Zephyrine had been a child, and children accept the story they're handed. She was not a child anymore. She was a salvage diver, and salvage divers knew better than anyone that wrecks told the truth the sea had been keeping for them.

"Accept the contract," she said.

Marisol stared at her. "Under escort? With navy observers on our deck, watching everything we do?"

"Under escort. With observers." Zephyrine heard her own voice come out level and was faintly proud of it. "We do the job clean, we say yes sir and no sir, we find their cargo, we get paid, and we leave. That's all it is."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then it isn't, and we deal with that too." She picked up the tablet and signed. The confirmation chirped back instantly, as if the navy had been waiting — which, some cold part of her noted, it probably had.

Below decks, a hatch opened and closed. Ash appeared in the wheelhouse doorway with a mug of tea she'd made from water hot enough to be dangerous, and looked at the tablet, and looked at Zephyrine, and said nothing at all. Ash was very good at saying nothing. Zephyrine noticed, distantly, that Ash had already pulled the Point Meridian charts before anyone mentioned the contract — they were lying squared on the nav desk, weighted at the corners, like they'd been waiting there for years.

Maybe they had. Zephyrine had certainly been waiting somewhere herself.

"Course when we're ready?" Ash asked, mild as milk.

"Day after tomorrow. Tide window at oh-six-hundred." Zephyrine rolled up the invoices she'd been failing to read and tucked them away. "Point Meridian."

Ash nodded once and went back below, and Marisol lingered a moment longer, chewing the inside of her cheek, wanting to argue and knowing it wouldn't land. In the end she just said, "My dad used to say the sea gives back what it takes, eventually. Never said what condition it'd be in."

"No," Zephyrine agreed, looking out at the darkening water. "It never does."

That night she slept badly, which was normal, and dreamed of a boat she couldn't quite see, which was also normal. What wasn't normal was the dream's ending. For twenty years the dream had ended the same way: the *Vespera* slipping under gray water, her father's hand raised in a wave that might have been goodbye, the surface closing like a book.

Tonight the water didn't close. Tonight the tide kept going out — impossibly far out, the seabed rising naked into the air, fish gasping in the light — and the dream walked her down across the exposed sand toward a cave mouth in the cliffs, black and breathing, with something stamped on the rock above it in letters she couldn't quite read.

She woke before dawn with salt drying on her lips, though the porthole was shut tight, and lay listening to the harbor wake up around her.

Two days to Point Meridian. Two days to the water she'd spent half her life sailing around.

She got up, put on the coffee, and started the long business of getting underway — because rent was due, and stopping, whatever Marisol said, had always cost her more.
