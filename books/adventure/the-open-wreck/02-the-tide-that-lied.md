## 2. The Tide That Lied

Marisol had worked the water long enough to know when it was lying to her.

She'd grown up on a stilt village where the tide was a second mother — it fed you, drowned your neighbors, and never, ever broke its promises. It came in twice a day like a debt collector. It went out twice a day like a confession. So when she stepped onto the deck of the *Vela Oscura* at dawn and saw the bay drained down to its ribs, her first instinct wasn't wonder. It was suspicion.

"Teo," she called down into the engine hatch. "You seeing this?"

The engineer's head emerged, gray stubble and welding goggles pushed up on his forehead. He squinted across the flats the sea had abandoned overnight. "Seeing what?"

"The bay's gone."

He climbed up beside her and stood at the rail, and for a long moment neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say that the silence wasn't already saying better.

The water had pulled back maybe four hundred meters. Further. The channel buoys sat upright in wet sand like drunk men leaning on nothing. The *Vela* itself, moored in six meters of water yesterday evening, now listed against the mud with her keel exposed to the air, barnacles drying white along the hull. And beyond all of it, where the charts showed only open water and a depth reading of thirty-one meters, stood a wall of black limestone — and cut into that wall, low and dark and dripping, a cave mouth wide enough to drive a truck through.

"No chart has that," Marisol said.

"No chart has any of this." Teodor spat over the rail out of habit, though they were looking at mud. "Tides don't do this. Not here. Not anywhere. A king tide drops you two meters, three if the moon's showing off. This is—" he gestured at the whole ruined geography of it, "—this is the sea leaving town."

Zephyrine came up from below decks last, as she always did, moving like someone who'd been awake for hours before anyone else. She stopped at the rail. Marisol watched her face do something complicated — watched the captain take in the drained bay, the impossible cave, and file each one away behind those flat gray eyes without giving anything back.

"Soundings said thirty-one meters," Zephyrine said.

"Soundings are liars too, apparently," Teodor said.

"Ash?"

The navigator appeared at the wheelhouse door with her tablet hugged to her chest like a schoolbook. She always held it that way, Marisol had noticed. Like it might be taken from her. "I ran it three times," Ash said. "Barometric pressure, lunar cycle, seiche resonance off the storm front. Nothing accounts for it. The nearest model says we should have a normal neap tide this morning. Instead the whole bay is—" She hesitated, hunting for the word.

"Empty," Marisol supplied.

"I was going to say wrong."

They took the dinghy across the flats because there was no other way to go, and because none of them — not one of them, though they'd have denied it later — could bear not to.

* * *

Walking on the seabed is a strange sacrament. Marisol felt it with every step: the crunch of dead coral under her boots, the stranded jellyfish pulsing feebly in shallow pools like dropped lanterns, the smell of everything the ocean usually kept private — sulfur and salt rot and old iron. Fish flopped in the shrinking lagoons behind them. Somewhere a heron stalked the wreckage of a fishing skiff that must have gone down decades ago, picking at it with proprietary interest.

The cave mouth grew as they approached until it swallowed half the sky. Black stone streaked with mineral run-off, orange and green, like the rock was bleeding rust. The water dripped from its lip in slow ropes, and inside, past the threshold, Marisol could hear it — the hollow boom of an inner chamber still holding water, breathing in and out with some tide far deeper than the one that had failed outside.

"Careful," Zephyrine said, and it was the first full sentence she'd spoken since dawn.

They went in single file, Teodor's flashlight cutting a cone through the gloom. The floor sloped gently upward, dry for the first ten meters, then slick with algae. The walls narrowed. And then the light found it, and Marisol heard Teodor stop breathing.

Gear. Dive gear. Rusted into the rock shelf along the left wall like it had grown there — twin tanks fused with corrosion, a harness rotted down to its webbing, a weight belt whose lead had gone white with oxidation. Twenty years in salt water, easy. Thirty. But the thing that made Marisol's skin crawl wasn't the age of it.

It was the stamp.

On the tank shoulder, half-eaten by rust but unmistakable, a logo: a circle broken by a horizontal wave-line, with letters beneath that time had chewed but not destroyed.

**YARROW DEEPWORKS.**

Nobody moved. The inner chamber boomed its slow tide. Water dripped somewhere in the dark, patient as a clock.

"That's not possible," Teodor said finally. His voice came out strange, flattened. "Yarrow Deepworks folded twenty years ago. Before the accident. Before—"

He stopped. Everyone heard him stop.

Marisol looked at Zephyrine. She had to; she couldn't help it. The captain stood very still, one hand resting on the rusted tank, not reading the logo so much as touching it, the way you'd touch a gravestone to confirm it was real. Her face gave nothing away, which was how Marisol knew it cost her something enormous.

"My father's company," Zephyrine said quietly. "His stamps were hand-cut dies. No two identical. I'd know this mark anywhere." She lifted her hand. There was rust on her palm, orange as dried blood. "This gear didn't wash in here. Look at the silt line. It's been sitting above every high tide for two decades. Which means for two decades, this cave has been underwater — and dry inside."

"Caves breathe," Teodor offered weakly. "Air pockets, tidal siphons, there are systems in Borneo that—"

"Not at thirty-one meters under open ocean, Teo. Not sealed like this." Zephyrine turned her flashlight deeper into the passage, and the beam caught something else: a shape further in, angular, man-made, half-buried in flowstone. A door. An actual steel door set into the living rock, its surface furred with mineral deposit, a stencil barely legible across it.

Marisol read it aloud before she could stop herself. "*Property of the Hydrographic Survey Directorate.*"

"Navy," Ash said from the back. Her voice was thin. "That's a navy directorate. Disbanded in—" She paused. "Disbanded quietly."

The word *quietly* sat in the air between them like a third person.

* * *

They argued about it on the way back, and then they argued about it on the boat, and the argument had teeth.

"We report it," Teodor said. He'd planted himself at the galley table with his arms crossed, the posture of a man building a wall out of his own body. "A restricted zone, an uncharted cave, government property sitting in plain sight. We call the harbor office, we collect whatever finder's fee doesn't get us arrested, and we forget we ever grew a conscience."

"And say what?" Marisol leaned against the bulkhead. "That the tide lied? They'll laugh us off the radio. Or worse — they won't laugh. They'll send people."

"People with questions," Ash murmured.

"People with answers they already wrote," Zephyrine said.

Everyone looked at her. She stood at the chart table with her arms braced on it, head down, and Marisol realized she hadn't touched the charts since they'd come back aboard. Zephyrine Yarrow, who navigated by paper the way other people prayed, couldn't bring herself to look at a map of that bay.

"The zone gets flagged restricted tomorrow, probably," the captain went on. "Maybe tonight. Whatever pulled the water out of that bay wanted that cave seen or wanted it buried, and either way the navy will close it like a wound. If we report it, we're witnesses. Witnesses get managed." She straightened. "If we stay quiet, we're just another salvage crew that never got paid."

"There's gear in there worth money," Teodor said, but his heart wasn't in it and everyone knew it. "There's a steel door worth more. Salvage rights on a government vault—"

"You want to put a claim on a navy grave?" Marisol said. "Because that's what's behind that door. You can smell it. That's not a storage room, Teo. That's a tomb somebody welded shut."

Silence. The *Vela Oscura* creaked against her mooring lines, keel still grounded, and outside the porthole the sea was already creeping back — Marisol had been watching it all through the argument, the water sliding up the flats with a patience that felt deliberate, reclaiming its territory inch by inch. By nightfall the cave mouth would be drowned again. By morning it would be a rumor.

Ash spoke from the doorway, quiet enough that they almost missed it. "My grandmother used to tell a story about this coast. About a cave that shows itself once in a generation." She hugged the tablet tighter. "She said the cave charges admission. Not money. It takes something from everyone who walks in, and it keeps it."

"Folklore," Teodor said.

"Sure," Ash said, and didn't smile.

Zephyrine finally looked up, and Marisol saw it happen — saw the decision move through her captain's face like weather, arriving from somewhere much deeper than thought. Grief and hunger wearing the same expression.

"We dive it," Zephyrine said. "Tomorrow, at the next low. Just me and Marisol on the line, Teo on surface, Ash on the radio. In and out before the water comes back. We look, we log, we touch nothing we can't carry, and nobody reports anything to anybody. Agreed?"

Teodor opened his mouth. Closed it. "Agreed," he said, in the tone of a man agreeing to his own funeral.

Marisol nodded. But standing at the porthole afterward, watching the sea climb the flats in the failing light — watching it erase, meter by meter, the black mouth of the cave and everything sleeping inside it — she felt the wonder of the morning curdle slowly into dread, cold and specific as ballast in her chest.

Because she'd remembered something else her grandmother used to say about the water.

The tide never lies, the old woman told her once. It only tells the truth early.

And Marisol couldn't shake the feeling that today, the sea had shown them exactly what was coming — and that the truth it had dragged into daylight was going to drown someone before the monsoon arrived to bury the evidence.
