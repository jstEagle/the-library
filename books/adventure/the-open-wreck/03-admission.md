## 3. Admission

The legends came with the territory. Every drowned coast had them — the sea that takes a toll, the cave that charges admission. Old divers said it like a joke with teeth in it: *the water always wants something back.* Zephyrine had spent twenty years paying it in small installments — fingers scarred white from cold, a marriage dissolved by distance, a father folded into the floor of a bay she'd sworn never to work again.

And now here she was, standing at the lip of that bay at dawn, watching the impossible tide hold itself open like a throat.

"You're sure about this," Marisol said behind her. Not a question. Marisol didn't ask questions she didn't already know the answers to; she asked them so you had to say them out loud.

"The tide won't last." Zephyrine checked her gauges without looking at them, a habit, a rosary. "Four hours, maybe five before the swell turns. If we're going in, we go now."

"I didn't ask if we *could*. I asked if you were sure."

Zephyrine looked out at the cave mouth. It sat low in the cliff face where no chart admitted anything but rock, a black arch maybe three meters across, its base still slicked and dripping from the retreat of the sea. The water beyond it lay flat as poured lead. Yesterday there had been ten fathoms over this spot. This morning you could walk to the entrance on wet stone, and the whole crew had stood on the exposed shelf in silence, listening to the bay breathe.

Legends said the cave charged admission. Fine. She'd been paying her whole life.

"I'm sure," she said.

* * *

They went in on foot first, gear hauled in dry bags, because the passage above the waterline ran high enough for an hour before it narrowed into flooded dark. Teodor's lamps carved the tunnel into slices of amber and shadow. The rock was volcanic, black-green, veined with quartz that caught their beams and threw it back in cold sparks — and everywhere, everywhere, the marks of human hands.

Rust blooms the size of dinner plates. A collapsed scaffolding of galvanized pipe, furred orange. Cable sheathing split open like rotten fruit. And stenciled on a supply crate wedged into a crevice, half legible under two decades of mineral crust:

**MERIDIAN SALVAGE & MARINE — PROPERTY OF — DO NOT OFFLOAD WITHOUT AUTHORIZATION**

Zephyrine stopped walking.

She knew that stencil. She'd grown up tracing it with her finger on her father's hard hats, on his coffee thermos, on the side of the family truck. Meridian Salvage. Her father's company. Her father's *letters*, in her father's blocky hand, because he'd painted every stencil himself — he used to say a man should mark his own work the way God marked the days.

"That's not possible," Marisol said quietly. "Meridian folded before you were born."

"Before I was *born*, no." Zephyrine's voice came out flat and strange, like someone else speaking through her mouth. "It folded when I was nine. After the accident."

Nobody said *after your father died*. They were kind that way, or cowardly. She couldn't tell anymore which kindness was which.

Teodor crouched by the crate, worked a pry bar under the lid. Inside: dive cylinders, green with corrosion, their valve stamps crisp enough to read. Test dates. Batch numbers. And on each one, punched into the metal shoulder, the same inspector's code.

Zephyrine knew that code too. She'd known it since she was six years old, sitting on a workbench swinging her legs while her father taught her to read hydro stamps. **Y-7.** Yarrow, station seven. His personal mark. He'd joked that someday she'd have her own letter, and she'd said she wanted Z, and he'd laughed and said nobody trusts a tank stamped with a letter that sounds like sleep.

Her father's tanks. In a cave that did not exist on any chart. In waters the navy had flagged restricted two weeks ago, in the same bay where the official record said the *Corvina* went down with all hands in a routine salvage mishap, twenty years ago this autumn.

"Boss." Ash's voice, from deeper in the tunnel. Steady as ever, which was its own kind of eerie. "You need to see this."

* * *

The tunnel opened into a chamber the size of a chapel, and like a chapel it made you lower your voice. Their lamps swept across a vault door set directly into the living rock — not installed, Zephyrine realized, but *built around*: the chamber had been excavated to receive it. Two meters of riveted steel, government gray, a wheel-lock the size of a ship's helm. Above it, a brass plate rubbed nearly blank by time, but the numerals survived:

**EXPEDITION 91-C / AUTHORITY: MINISTRY OF MARINE SURVEY / ACCESS RESTRICTED**

Nineteen ninety-one. The year before the Corvina sank. The year her father started coming home late with salt crusted in eyebrows he swore he hadn't been diving.

"There'll be a lock mechanism," Teodor said, already circling the wheel, tapping, listening. "Probably failsafe — spring bolts, salt-water trigger. If they sealed it expecting never to come back—"

"Then how do we know it's not rigged?" Marisol asked.

"We don't," Zephyrine said. "That's the admission price."

She said it lightly and hated herself for it. But her hands were steady when she took the wheel beside Teodor, and together they threw their weight against it. The wheel fought them for one full revolution, shrieking like something alive — then gave all at once, and the bolts withdrew with a sound like a swallowed word, and the vault exhaled twenty years of held breath past their faces. Cold air, mineral and old, tasting faintly of diesel and paper.

Inside: dry. Impossibly, deliberately dry. Desiccant drums stacked along the walls, most of them split and spent, but the inner room they'd guarded remained intact. Steel shelving. Document cases with rubber gaskets still seated. A bank of equipment under tarps. And on a desk bolted to the floor, centered, deliberate, left where someone would find it —

—a manifest.

Ash found the lamp switch, and the chamber lit up in harsh institutional white, and everything got worse, because light is honest.

The manifest was typed on Ministry letterhead. EXPEDITION 91-C, PERSONNEL ROSTER. Fourteen names. Zephyrine read down the column of ranks and ratings — survey officers, a photographer, two navy liaisons, dive team — and her eyes snagged on the seventh line the way a hull snags on a reef.

**DIVER, SENIOR — D. E. YARROW — MERIDIAN SALVAGE (CONTRACT) — STATUS: ACTIVE**

Active. Not lost. Not deceased. *Active.*

The vault hummed with silence. Somewhere far off, down the flooded throat of the cave, the sea shifted against the rock — the tide beginning to think about turning.

"Zeph." Marisol's voice, very gentle. "You okay?"

"No," Zephyrine said. It was the truest thing she'd said in years, and it came out almost grateful.

Because here was the vertigo of it, the free-fall: she had built her entire life inside the shape of that accident. The nomadism. The refusal to sign leases. The way she chose contracts the way other people self-medicate — always moving, always new water, never the same coast twice. She'd told herself it was freedom. She'd told herself a lot of things. And the whole architecture of it had been raised on a single foundation stone: *her father died by mischance, in open water, doing honest work.*

This document said otherwise. This document said her father had stood in this chamber, in this light, and signed a roster. That he'd been crew on something with a ministry seal on it. That whatever happened to him afterward happened *inside* the story, not outside it — that he hadn't fallen off the edge of his own life, he'd walked into the deep end of someone else's.

Every memory she owned suddenly had two versions. The beach picnics where he taught her knots — was that the summer before 91-C, or after? The night he came home smelling of another man's cigarettes and wouldn't say whose. The last time she saw him, waving from the dock, and how he'd held the wave a beat too long, the way you hold a wave when you're memorizing someone.

"His name's on the equipment manifests too," Ash said from the shelving. She'd moved without anyone noticing, the way she did. She held up a clipboard, its pages swollen with damp but legible. "Signed delivery receipts. He was here at least six times. Last entry—" she turned it toward the light "—eleven days before the Corvina went down."

"Put it down," Zephyrine said.

"Boss—"

"*Put it down.*"

Ash set the clipboard on the desk, precisely, aligned with the manifest's edge. Her face gave away nothing, which Zephyrine noted, distantly, the way you note a current pulling at your fins.

Marisol put a hand on her shoulder. Teodor had gone very still by the tarps, and when Zephyrine glanced at him he was looking at the equipment beneath — sonar rigs, sediment cores, sealed sample canisters — with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

"This isn't salvage gear," he said. "This is *survey* gear. Whatever they found down here, they weren't salvaging it. They were measuring it. Cataloguing it. Deciding what to do with it." He looked up. "And then somebody decided the answer was *nothing*. Seal it, sink the approach, lose the paperwork."

"And my father signed the roster," Zephyrine said.

"Yes."

"He wasn't a casualty of an accident. He was crew." The words tasted like pressure change, like the wrong gas mix, like depth coming on too fast. "He was *crew*, and then he was dead eleven days later, and the navy called it mischance, and I believed them for twenty years."

Nobody answered. The lamp buzzed. Down the tunnel, the sea knocked once against the rock, patient as a landlord.

Zephyrine crossed to the desk. Under the manifest lay a second document, thinner, a carbon copy, its header half-torn away. Most of the text had been redacted — black bars like the rungs of a ladder going down into nothing. But the final line had escaped the censor's pen, typed in a clerk's tired hand:

*Final disposition of recovered materials to be determined by [REDACTED] following completion of disposal operation, ref. CORVINA.*

Ref. Corvina.

Her father's boat. Her father's grave. Referenced in a government file as a *disposal operation*.

The chamber seemed to tilt. Zephyrine gripped the desk's edge and breathed the way you breathe at thirty meters — slow, deliberate, counting — because panic at depth kills faster than water does, and she was, she understood suddenly, at a very great depth indeed.

"Okay," she said, when she could trust her voice. "New plan. We log everything. Photographs, positions, condition reports — proper salvage documentation, because if we're going to rob a grave, we do it like professionals. We take nothing out yet except copies. The originals stay until I've read every page in this room."

"And the tide?" Marisol asked.

Zephyrine looked at the vault, at the roster, at the seventh line. *Status: Active.* Her father, alive in ink, waiting for her.

"The tide can wait for us," she said. "We're not leaving until I know what he was part of."

Behind her, unnoticed by everyone but Ash — who noticed everything, and filed it, and said nothing — the radio in her jacket pocket clicked once, softly, a transmission burst too short to carry words.

Just long enough to carry coordinates.
