## 4. Company Man

Teodor Vasko heard the patrol boat before he saw it — a change in the harbor's breathing, the slap of water against the *Magpie's* hull going from lazy to agitated. He was elbow-deep in the bilge pump at the time, sleeves rolled past his elbows, forearms slick with a mixture of diesel and salt that no soap on this coast could cut. He froze with his hand on the impeller housing and listened.

Engines. Two of them, running slow and deliberate, the way a man walks through a house he owns.

He wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being clean years ago and climbed the ladder into grey morning light. The monsoon sky hung low over the bay like a tarpaulin someone had forgotten to tie down, and there, cutting a long white seam across the water, came the navy boat — gunmetal grey, hull number stenciled sharp as a scar, a man at the bow rail with binoculars raised.

Teodor did what any sensible engineer does when authority comes calling: he picked up a wrench and made himself busy.

* * *

The patrol boat made three circuits of the bay before it committed. Teodor counted them from the engine hatch, pretending to service the winch motor while his eyes tracked the vessel over the top of the cowling. First pass wide, along the headland. Second pass closer, close enough to read the wake pattern, close enough to see the deck officer's cap. Third pass — slow, almost insolent — right down the middle of the anchorage, between the *Magpie* and the two fishing boats whose crews had come out to gawk at the drained bay.

The tide was still wrong. That was the thing nobody could stop talking about. Three days now since the sea had pulled back like a curtain and shown them all something they weren't supposed to see, and the water still hadn't come back to its proper line. The charts said high tide at 0640. The rocks below the cave mouth sat dry as bone at noon. Old men on the fishing boats muttered about omens. Marisol called it *the tide that lied*, and the name had stuck the way names do when they're true.

And now the navy had noticed.

"Engineer." The voice came from below the gangway side, flat and official. "Vessel registration. Now."

Teodor descended the ladder slowly, buying seconds, arranging his face into the particular expression of a man who is mildly inconvenienced by bureaucracy and nothing more. It was an expression he'd practiced. He'd needed it before.

The officer who boarded them was young — thirty maybe, with the kind of jaw that hadn't yet learned what compromise looked like — flanked by two enlisted men who stood very still and looked at everything. Not at Teodor. At everything else. The dive gear racked along the rail. The cable spools. The open hatch to the hold, where somebody — Zephyrine, probably — had left the crate from the cave sitting half-covered under a tarp.

Teodor stepped sideways without appearing to step sideways. Just enough to put his body between the hatch and the officer's eyeline.

"*Magpie*, salvage registry out of Port Calloway," he said. "Papers are below."

"They'll do." The officer didn't move toward the papers. His eyes went past Teodor's shoulder instead, up to the wheelhouse, where a figure stood silhouetted against the fogged glass. "Who's your navigator?"

The question landed wrong. Teodor felt it land wrong the way you feel a mooring line go slack — nothing visible, everything changed. Officers asked about cargo, about permits, about last port of call. They didn't ask about crew by function.

"Ash," he said. "Why?"

"No reason." The officer smiled, and it was the worst smile Teodor had ever seen, because it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Routine survey of the bay. Restricted waters notice went out yesterday. You'll have seen it."

"I don't read notices," Teodor said. "I read pressure gauges."

"You should start reading notices." The officer turned, then paused at the rail, one hand on the wire. "Your employer. Yarrow. She has family history in these waters, doesn't she?"

The bay went very quiet around them. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"Everyone on this coast has family history somewhere," Teodor said. "Most of it drowned."

The officer held his gaze for three seconds too long, then dropped over the side to the waiting boat. The engines woke with a snarl, and the grey hull swung away toward the cave mouth, where it slowed, and loitered, and began — Teodor watched this with his stomach tightening like a drum skin — to lower a small boat into the water.

* * *

He found Zephyrine in the galley, drinking coffee that had gone cold hours ago because she never remembered to drink it hot. She had the expedition manifest spread across the table, her father's name circled in grease pencil so many times the paper had torn through. She looked up when he came in, and whatever she saw in his face made her set the cup down.

"Navy just boarded us," Teodor said. "No search warrant, no inspection. Asked for papers they didn't look at. Asked about you by name. Asked about your father."

Zephyrine was quiet for a moment. Outside, through the galley's salt-hazed window, the patrol boat sat off the cave mouth like a dog chained to a door.

"How did they know my name?" she said finally.

"That's the question I've been chewing since they left." Teodor lowered himself onto the bench across from her, and the bench complained, and he ignored it. "Registry filings have the vessel name. They don't have crew rosters unless somebody filed one. And nobody files crew rosters for a salvage contract in restricted water unless they want the contract refused."

"So somebody told them."

"Somebody told them." He rubbed his jaw. His beard rasped against his palm, three days past shaving, the texture of a man coming apart at the seams. "Zeph, I need to tell you something first, because if this goes where I think it goes, you're going to hear it from someone worse than me."

She waited. She was good at waiting. It was one of the things that made her hard to work for and harder to lie to.

"I owe money," Teodor said. "Not fishing-boat money. Real money. To people who keep ledgers in red ink and collect in person. I took this contract partly because the pay clears my debt and partly because Port Calloway is a long way from the men holding the ledger." He held up a hand before she could speak. "I'm not telling you this to confess. I'm telling you because debt makes a man a target, and a target can be bought, and I want you to know that I know how that works from the inside. So when I say what I'm about to say, understand I've been checking myself against it for two days."

"Say it anyway."

"Someone on this boat is transmitting."

Her eyes narrowed. "Transmitting what?"

"Coordinates. Burst transmissions, short ones, encrypted or compressed or both — I can't crack them, but I can count them." He pulled a folded page from his shirt pocket, grease-stained, covered in his own cramped handwriting. "First one night before the tide dropped. Second one the morning we entered the cave. Third one yesterday, twenty minutes after the navy's first flyover. Every transmission, within hours, the navy knows exactly where we are and exactly what we're doing. That's not coincidence. That's a schedule."

Zephyrine looked at the page. Then at him. Then out the window at the grey ship squatting on their horizon.

"Could it be you?" she asked. No accusation in it. Just the question, laid out flat, the way she laid everything out.

"It could be me," Teodor admitted. "That's why I showed you the debts. Anyone with a radio access code and a reason could do it. Marisol's got reasons — she's got a mother in a care home and a brother in prison. I've got creditors. You—" he paused, chose honesty, "—you've got a wreck you'd burn the world to open. Any of us could be the leak. But there's one of us who's been quiet in a way that isn't natural."

"Ash."

The name came out of both of them at once, and the silence afterward had weight, like water pressure.

"She's the navigator," Teodor said. "She plots every course, logs every position, handles the radio watch on night shifts alone. If anyone aboard had clean access to transmit our coordinates without anyone noticing, it's her. And she's been—" he searched for the word, "—*watchful*. Since the cave. Standing at the rail at odd hours. Looking at the navy boat like she's reading it, not fearing it. Everybody else looks at that ship and sees a threat. Ash looks at it like she's checking whether a letter arrived."

Zephyrine pushed back from the table. For a long moment she said nothing, and Teodor watched her do the arithmetic he'd already done — the boarding, the questions, the timing, the girl at the rail — and arrive at the same ugly sum.

"We don't accuse anyone," she said at last. "Not yet. If we're wrong, we gut this crew for nothing. If we're right, we spook them and lose the only advantage we have, which is that whoever it is doesn't know we know." Her jaw set, and Teodor saw her father in it suddenly, some old photograph of the man, the same stubborn architecture. "We watch. We lock the radio room. We rotate the night watch so nobody's alone with the transmitter. And Teodor—"

"Yeah."

"When we find out who it is," she said quietly, "I want to be the one who asks them why."

* * *

He couldn't sleep that night. The bilge pump was fixed, the winch serviced, the generator humming its one good note, and still sleep wouldn't take him. So he did what engineers do with insomnia: he walked his ship.

The *Magpie* at midnight was a different animal — cables creaking, halyards tapping, the whole hull murmuring in a dialect of stress and settlement that Teodor had spent eleven years learning to translate. He moved through her in the dark without a lamp, hand on bulkheads, feet finding the known squeaks and avoiding them out of habit.

He was passing the aft companionway when he saw the light.

A thin blade of it under the radio room door. Faint, moving — the flicker of a screen, not a steady bulb. The radio room should have been dark. The radio room should have been locked; he'd locked it himself after dinner, per Zephyrine's order.

Teodor stood in the passage with his heart going like a piston and made the calculation every paranoid man eventually makes: knock, or listen?

He listened.

Through the door, low and careful, a voice. Ash's voice. He knew it from a thousand navigation reports — that level, unhurried tone, the accent from nowhere in particular. But this wasn't a position report. The words came clipped, spaced, half-swallowed, the cadence of someone talking to a machine or a handler, not a friend.

"...negative on recovery... they've opened the manifest... no, he doesn't suspect... I need more time. I'm asking you for *time*..."

A pause. Static breath.

"If you move on them now, everyone dies in this storm and you get nothing. Give me until the tide turns. I can deliver the vault intact. I swear it on—"

Another pause. Longer. When the voice came again it was smaller.

"Yes. Understood. Yarrow stays alive. I understand what that costs."

Teodor backed away from the door one silent step at a time, his mind roaring louder than the generator, because he had heard enough to hang her and not nearly enough to understand her. *Deliver the vault.* *Yarrow stays alive.* A traitor negotiating terms — but terms that kept Zephyrine breathing, terms that asked for time instead of action, terms that sounded less like betrayal and more like a woman standing in a doorway trying to hold it shut with her body.

He made it back to the engine room before his legs decided they were done. He sat down on his tool chest in the dark, among the smells of oil and iron that had been home longer than any address, and stared at the bulkhead.

Winch cables didn't snap all at once. That was the thing people didn't understand. They frayed strand by strand, quietly, under load, and the load never stopped increasing until the moment they parted — and by then everything hanging from them was already falling.

Above him, faint through the deck plates, he heard soft footsteps cross the aft deck. The radio room light went out.

And out in the black bay, right on schedule, the navy's searchlight swept the cave mouth once, twice — patient as a creditor — and went dark.
