## 5. What the Water Kept

The tide came back at 0340 and the cave swallowed itself whole.

Zephyrine stood on the deck of the *Magpie's Debt* and watched the water climb the rock like it was reclaiming stolen property. Yesterday the sea had pulled back forty meters, baring a throat of black stone no chart admitted to, and the crew had gone down into it and come back up with rusted dive gear stamped with a logo she'd known before she knew her own name: YARROW & SONS SALVAGE, stenciled in flaking white on a tank that should have been at the bottom of the ocean twenty years ago.

Her father had one son. He'd been six when the boat went down.

She hadn't slept. She'd sat in the wheelhouse with the vault manifest open on her knees, reading his name over and over — *T. Yarrow, salvage crew* — listed under EXPEDITION PERSONNEL, not under CASUALTIES, as if the navy had filed him alive right up until the moment they needed him dead. The paper was brittle as moth wing. She'd held it so long the damp from her hands had begun to soften the corners, and she'd caught herself doing it — worrying the edge like she used to worry the hem of his shirt when he carried her up the dock road — and she'd put it down before she destroyed the only proof she had.

Now the sun was up, gray through monsoon haze, and the bay lay flat and green as bottle glass, keeping its secret again.

"We going down or are we going to keep staring at it?" Marisol said, coiling line beside her. The deckhand's hands were steady but her voice wasn't quite.

"Teodor says the surge window is narrow," Zephyrine said. "Four hours of slack inside the cave before the tide starts breathing again. After that the current in there will fold you in half."

"And you're going anyway."

"I'm going anyway."

Marisol looked at her for a long moment. Whatever she saw made her nod once and turn away, which was Marisol's way of saying *I think this is a mistake and I'll help you make it.*

* * *

The cave at depth was a cathedral with the lights out.

Zephyrine dropped through the entrance on her own line, past the shelf where yesterday's discovery still hung — the old rigs, the company-stamped tanks, arranged almost neatly, like a shrine someone had meant to come back for. Below them the passage narrowed and turned, and her torch beam swept across walls furred with white bacterial mats that glowed faintly where the light touched, ghost-lace in the black.

Forty-one meters. Fifty. Her computer ticked against the silence. The water here was cold in a way tropical water shouldn't be, cold rising from somewhere deeper, and she thought: *the cave breathes cold because it goes down further than anyone admits.* She thought about legends — the ones the harbor kids told, the ones her father used to laugh at — that the cave charged admission. That it took something from everyone who entered and gave back something else.

It had taken her father. She was here to collect what it owed.

At sixty-two meters the passage opened into a flooded chamber, and her beam found the vault door.

They'd seen it yesterday from the threshold — the steel face, the expedition stencil, the seal still intact after thirty years. Now she swam to it alone. The wheel was crusted but not seized; the navy built things to last, even the things they wanted forgotten. It took both hands and most of her air reserve discipline to break it free. The sound traveled through the water like a bell rung underwater, felt more than heard, and something in the dark above her — a fish, a current, her imagination — shifted.

Inside: shelving. Cases. And on a desk bolted to the floor, weighted under a brass bar, a logbook bound in oilcloth.

She knew the handwriting before she touched it.

* * *

Decompression is a strange place to read your father's last words.

She did it hanging on the line at nine meters, then six, then three, the logbook sealed in a sample bag pressed against her mask plate, reading by torchlight in the blue-gray purgatory between the world below and the world above. Teodor would have screamed at her — task loading at deco, splitting attention when nitrogen was stacking in her blood like sandbags — but she could not have waited. Not for the boat. Not for the surface. Not for another hour of not knowing.

The first pages were ordinary. Depths, times, salvage notes in a hand she'd watched form letters on grocery lists and birthday cards. Then, dated eleven days before the official sinking:

*They found it in the trench section. I won't write what it is because I don't trust the paper either. Callis took photographs. The Captain has gone quiet. We were told this was a hydrographic survey.*

Then, four days later:

*Navy liaison aboard now. Two of them. They smile too much. Callis asked what happens if we refuse to sign the nondisclosure and the taller one laughed like it was a joke. Nobody laughed with him.*

Then, two days before the end:

*I understand now why they hired a salvage crew instead of using their own divers. Salvage men disappear clean. No next of kin who matter, no union, no press. They told us tonight the survey equipment is "compromised" and must be scuttled. All of it. The Captain signed. I have not signed yet. Zeph turns eight next month. I promised her a shell from this coast.*

Zephyrine hung on the line and the ocean held her up and she shook.

Not grief, or not only grief. Fury — a full-body fury with nowhere to go, burning off the nitrogen haze like a flare. They had written him into a cover story the way you'd write a figure into an insurance claim. *Equipment compromised. Scuttle all of it.* And the word *all* sat there on the page like a hole in the hull, because she knew what else was on that boat besides equipment. She knew the casualty list by heart. She'd recited it at every memorial, every anniversary, every year the navy sent its polite letter of regret.

*All of it.*

The last entry was dated the final day. The handwriting had changed — smaller, faster, pressed hard enough to tear the page in places.

*They came at night. Ordered us to sink the evidence ourselves — our own gear, our own hands, so the blame would sit on civilian negligence if it ever surfaced. The Captain refused to let them do it remotely. He said a man should know what he's burying. So they made us take the boat out loaded, and they stayed aboard to watch, and I understood at the dock that none of us were coming back. Witnesses are also evidence.*

*I'm writing this in the head, fast. If anyone ever reads it: the thing in the trench is real, it is not ours, and they were afraid of it. Not afraid of what it does. Afraid of what it proves. There is a difference and the difference is everything.*

*To my daughter. If it's you somehow, little fish — I didn't leave you. I was taken. Be angry at the right people. And don't dive alone.*

*— T.Y.*

The safety stop timer counted down and Zephyrine Yarrow broke every rule she owned and stayed until it hit zero, and then some, crying into her regulator where nobody could see, salt water joining salt water, furious and tender in the same breath the way only the dead can make you.

*Don't dive alone.* She'd broken that one for twenty years. She broke it now, gently, one last time, for him.

* * *

On deck, the crew gathered around the sample bag like it was a fire in winter.

Teodor read it twice and said nothing, which from Teodor was a sermon. Marisol cried without noise, wiping her face with the heel of her hand like she was mad at it. Ash stood at the rail, half-turned away, and Zephyrine watched her — watched the way the navigator's eyes moved over the handwriting, the way her jaw set, the way she checked the horizon twice in a minute, scanning for a silhouette that wasn't there yet.

Or maybe Zephyrine was seeing ghosts everywhere now. Maybe paranoia was just love with nowhere safe to land.

"He signed nothing," Zephyrine said finally. Her voice came out scraped raw. "The last entry. Everyone else signed the scuttle order. He wrote this instead."

"That's your father," Teodor said quietly. "That's the man."

"The thing in the trench." Marisol frowned. "What does that mean? What did they find?"

"I don't know." Zephyrine looked down at the bag, at the oilcloth binding, at the words *afraid of what it proves.* "But the vault had more than his logbook. There were cases down there. Sealed. Stenciled EVIDENCE — HOLD FOR REVIEW. And there was a cabinet at the back with a camera housing, film canisters, and a data case with a naval serial number."

"You want to go back down," Teodor said.

"I need to go back down."

"It's sixty-two meters with a cave penetration and a decompression ceiling, and the tide turns in—" he checked his watch "—ninety minutes. Tomorrow. We plan it properly. Mix gas, staged bottles, two divers minimum. You are not diving alone again; I read the last page too."

"Tomorrow," she agreed, and hated the delay and loved him for imposing it.

Ash spoke from the rail without turning around. "The navy patrol boat changed course an hour ago. It's holding station outside the headland now. Not leaving. Not approaching." A pause. "Like it's waiting for something."

"For what?" Marisol asked.

Zephyrine looked at the flat green water, at the place where the cave breathed beneath it, and felt the answer settle over her like cold water down the back of a collar.

"For the storm," she said. "They're waiting for the storm. Because when it comes, nobody asks questions, and whatever's down there gets buried a second time — permanent this time." She picked up the sample bag and held it against her chest, over the heart, the way you'd hold a child. "We have days. Maybe less. Tomorrow we go back down and we take everything."

Above the headland, far off, the first thunder of the season rolled across the water like a door closing somewhere very large.

And beneath their keel, patient as it had been for thirty years, the cave kept what the water kept — and waited to see what they'd dare carry out.
