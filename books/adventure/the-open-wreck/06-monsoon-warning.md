## 6. Monsoon Warning

The storm had a name before it had a face — *Vesna*, the trackers called it, a woman's name stitched onto something that would not behave like one. Marisol heard it first on the shortwave at four in the morning, huddled in the wheelhouse with a mug of tea gone cold against her palm, listening to a voice from the coastal station recite coordinates and barometric readings like a priest reading last rites.

*Landfall projected in five days. All vessels advised to clear the shelf waters by Thursday.*

Five days. She did the math without wanting to. Five days was fuel and food and luck, and they were short on all three.

Behind her, the bay lay flat as poured tin, holding its breath. The cave mouth gaped at the tideline where the impossible low tide had exposed it three days ago — a black seam in the cliff that no chart admitted to, breathing its cold mineral smell out over the water. Marisol had stopped looking at it directly. Every time she did, she thought of the rusted dive gear stamped with Yarrow Salvage's old logo, and of Zephyrine's face when she'd seen it: not surprise, exactly. Something worse. Recognition.

The wheelhouse door banged open and Teodor came in carrying the weather fax like it was evidence at a trial.

"Confirmed," he said. "Category three when it makes landfall. Maybe four if the water stays warm." He spread the printout across the chart table, his thick fingers pinning the corners. "Storm surge models put six meters on this coast. Six meters, Marisol. That cave won't be a cave anymore. It'll be a throat."

Marisol looked at the isobars crowding together like a fist closing. "Does Zeph know?"

"She's in the hold with Ash, going through the manifest again. Third time today." Teodor rubbed his jaw. "She knows. She's just not saying it yet."

* * *

They gathered at dusk in the galley, which was too small for five people and their silences. Zephyrine sat at the head of the table with her father's name still wet in the air between them — *crew, not casualty*, the vault manifest had said, and none of them had found a way to say it aloud since. The lantern swung gently overhead, throwing shadows that climbed the bulkheads like slow animals.

Teodor laid the evacuation order on the table. It had come in an hour before, printed on navy letterhead, unsigned by anyone willing to put a name to it:

*FINAL NOTICE — ALL CIVILIAN VESSELS TO CLEAR RESTRICTED WATERS BY 0600 THURSDAY. NO EXCEPTIONS WILL BE GRANTED. THE ZONE WILL BE CLOSED PENDING POST-STORM ASSESSMENT.*

"Post-storm assessment," Ash repeated quietly. She sat with her arms folded, her navigation charts pushed away from her like something she didn't want touching her skin. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning they'll seal it," Zephyrine said. Her voice was level, but Marisol had learned to hear the current under it. "Blast the cave mouth shut, call it storm damage, and whatever's down there becomes a rumor nobody can prove. They've had twenty years to do it clean. The monsoon is doing them a favor."

"They've been circling since yesterday," Teodor said. "Patrol boat, gray hull, running dark except for that searchlight. Asking questions on channel sixteen every few hours."

"And someone's answering," Marisol said, before she could stop herself.

The galley went very still. She hadn't meant to say it — hadn't meant to admit she'd seen the faint double-blink on the radio traffic log, the transmissions that didn't match anything inbound or outbound, timed too regular to be accident. But it was out now, sitting on the table between the coffee pot and the evacuation order like a third document.

Zephyrine looked at her. "You're sure?"

"I'm not sure of anything," Marisol said. "But I know our radio, and I know what it sounds like when it's talking to somebody we're not talking to."

Ash's face did nothing at all, which was itself a kind of answer, and Marisol watched Zephyrine file it away — not confronting, not yet, just storing it somewhere behind her eyes where she kept everything sharp enough to cut.

"We'll deal with that later," Zephyrine said. "Right now there's only one question that matters." She turned the evacuation order over, facedown. "Thursday morning they close the zone. Friday night, maybe Saturday, Vesna comes ashore. That gives us—" she glanced at Ash, who answered without being asked.

"One dive window. Tomorrow. High slack tide, maybe ninety minutes of usable bottom time inside the cave before the surge starts building."

"Ninety minutes," Teodor said. "To do what? We already pulled the logs. We already have the manifest. What's left down there worth dying for?"

"The final record," Zephyrine said. "The vault had a sealed section — you saw it. Pressure-door, expedition stamp, no key on-site. My father's logbook says the expedition recorded everything. Video, sonar surveys, testimony. Whatever the navy wanted buried badly enough to sink its own people for — it's behind that door." She paused. "And my father put it there. He was ordered to sink the evidence, and he chose to seal it instead of destroying it. He bought us twenty years. I'm not going to let the tide take the last of it."

Teodor exhaled through his nose, long and slow. "You're asking us to stay inside a restricted zone under naval patrol, with a category three bearing down on us, to crack a sealed vault that the government of this country has killed to keep shut."

"Yes," Zephyrine said.

Silence. The lantern swung. Outside, the first real wind of the change came off the water and rattled the rigging, a long low note like a held breath finally starting to break.

Marisol realized she was waiting — waiting to see which way each of them would jump, the way you wait on a swell to see if it'll carry you or fold over your head. She thought about rent, about the engine parts they couldn't afford, about the easy out sitting right there in the shape of a full fuel tank and a heading south. Nobody would blame them. Nobody would even know.

"I'm in," she said.

Every head turned. She shrugged, feeling her ears go hot. "I've been running from bad weather my whole life. It never once worked. And—" she looked at Zephyrine, and made herself finish the sentence— "your father kept that door shut for twenty years so somebody could open it. Might as well be us."

Teodor grunted, which from him was a speech. "I'm in. But we prep the boat tonight. Storm lashings, spare air, everything stowed by dawn. If that dive goes wrong, I want us able to run with ten minutes' notice."

Ash was last. She stared at her charts for a long moment, and Marisol saw her thumb move once across the paper — tracing, maybe, some route only she could see. When she looked up, her voice was steady.

"There's a gap in the patrol pattern. Between 0300 and 0500 they fall back to the harbor mouth — refueling rotation. If we run the dive at first light tomorrow, we get ninety minutes before they're back on station, and the tide gives us cover coming out." A beat. "I can get us in and out. You have my word."

Something passed across Zephyrine's face — not quite trust, not quite its opposite. "Then it's settled. One dive. Tomorrow at dawn."

* * *

Marisol couldn't sleep. She came up on deck around two and found the boat rocking on a swell that had started arriving ahead of the storm — long, slow, deep swells from far offshore, the sea clearing its throat. The stars were still out, but they had a smeared look, like breath on glass.

Zephyrine was at the bow, sitting on the anchor windlass with her father's recovered logbook open on her knees, reading by the light of a red-lens flashlight. Marisol lowered herself onto the rail nearby and neither of them spoke for a while. That was the thing Marisol loved about boats — they taught you how to be quiet next to another person.

"Do you think he knew?" Marisol asked finally. "Your father. That someday someone would come back for it?"

Zephyrine turned a page. "He wrote like he knew. Listen to this." She read aloud, her voice low against the wind: *'If anyone finds this, I could not destroy what they asked me to destroy. Some things are heavier than orders. I am sorry for what it costs my family. Tell my daughter the sea keeps honest accounts.'*

Marisol swallowed. "Honest accounts," she repeated.

"He sank the truth to keep it safe," Zephyrine said. "Everyone thinks he went down with a wreck. Really he built one. Twenty years underwater, holding the door." She closed the logbook gently, the way you'd close a hand over something living. "Tomorrow we open it."

The wind picked up, and far to the southeast, below the horizon, something vast was turning toward them. Marisol felt it in her teeth before she heard it in the rigging — pressure dropping, the whole world leaning in.

"Dawn, then," she said.

"Dawn," Zephyrine agreed.

Neither of them mentioned the double-blink on the radio log. Neither of them mentioned Ash, alone in the wheelhouse at that hour, her silhouette bent over the transmitter console, her hands moving with the practiced calm of someone who had done this many times before.

But Marisol saw. And she said nothing — because tomorrow, everyone aboard would need every ally they had, and the sea, as the dead man wrote, keeps honest accounts.

It always collected in the end.
