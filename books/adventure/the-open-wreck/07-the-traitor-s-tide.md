## 7. The Traitor's Tide

The radio had a voice like wet gravel, and for eleven days it had been saying her name.

*Ash. Ash. Confirm position of the salvage vessel.*

She answered it the way she always did, hunched in the chart room with the door latched and the fan turning its slow, useless circles overhead: late, vague, and wrong. *Position, uh — holding off the north shoal. Visibility poor.* Every lie cost her something. She could feel them accumulating in her chest like silt, layer over layer, until one day she would not be able to draw a clean breath on this boat again.

The transmitter sat under the floor panel by the bilge pump, small as a hymnal, warm as a held bird. Lieutenant Okonjo's voice came through the earpiece she kept hidden inside her spare glove.

"You're stalling me, Ash."

"I'm giving you what I have." Her own voice sounded foreign — flattened, official, the voice of a woman who had never eaten dinner at that scarred table with those four people, who had never laughed until her ribs hurt when Teodor tried to teach Marisol to play dominoes and lost every round on purpose. "The bay's a maze. The tide tables are useless here. I can't give you coordinates I don't trust."

A long silence. Somewhere behind the lieutenant's breathing, she could hear rain on metal — the navy cutter riding out the same weather they were, ten miles east, close enough to reach them in forty minutes if he ever decided to stop asking and start coming.

"Your sister's tuition is due in March," Okonjo said at last. Not cruelly. That was the worst part. He said it the way a man reads a shipping manifest. "Your mother's clinic renews its license in April. These are things I know because knowing them is my job. You understand."

"I understand."

"The vault contents. The manifest. The name Yarrow. I need all of it before landfall, Ash, or the arrangement changes. Do you hear what I'm telling you?"

She looked at the floor panel. Under it, the little machine blinked its patient green eye, recording nothing now, transmitting only when she pressed her thumb to it — a detail she had never mentioned to him, letting him believe she was live twenty-four hours a day so that he would call less often, ask smaller questions, wait.

Buying time. That was all she had left to sell. Time, measured out in half-truths, drip by drip, like blood from a wound she'd opened herself.

"They haven't opened the manifest," she said. "Zephyrine keeps it locked. She's — careful. She doesn't trust anyone."

"She's right not to," Okonjo said, and rang off.

Ash sat in the dark of the chart room for a long while after that, listening to the rain thicken against the hull, and let herself feel, fully and without anesthesia, what she was. A traitor. The word didn't cut anymore; it had gone dull from use. What cut was the memory of Zephyrine two nights ago, standing at the rail in the phosphorescent wake, saying quietly, almost to herself: *My father's name is on that manifest, Ash. Listed as crew. Twenty years they told us he drowned by accident, and he was working for them.* And then Zephyrine had turned and gripped Ash's shoulder — her hand cold from the sea air, her grip anything but — and said, "I'm glad you're here. I don't say that enough. This crew is the only thing that ever made sense to me."

Ash had smiled and said something easy, and gone below, and been sick into the head.

* * *

They came at dawn, three hours before the storm trackers said the outer bands would arrive, which meant Okonjo had decided not to wait for her permission or her lies any longer.

Ash heard the diesel first — a deep, wrong note under the rain, an engine bigger than anything that belonged in this bay. Then Marisol's shout from the deck, cracked with sleep: "Boat! Navy boat, coming in fast!"

By the time Ash reached the wheelhouse, the cutter was already swinging alongside, gray and barnacled and bristling, six figures in black rain gear stacking up at its rail with boarding hooks. Zephyrine stood at the helm in her oilskins, hair plastered to her face, and Ash watched her run the arithmetic — no outrunning them, no sea room, monsoon swell building — and arrive at the only answer there was.

"Kill the engines," Zephyrine said. "Nobody does anything stupid. We're salvors under contract, we've got papers—"

The first boot hit their deck before she finished.

There were five of them who boarded, plus Okonjo himself, whom Ash recognized from a photograph she'd once been shown in a windowless office in a city whose name she still refused to say aloud. He was shorter than his voice, broader, with the calm of a man who had already read the ending of this story. Two of his sailors went aft and put their hands on Teodor and Marisol. One covered the wheelhouse. The last one — young, nervous, rifle too high — covered Ash, though he couldn't have known why that was funny.

Okonjo walked the deck slowly, rain streaming off his cap brim, and stopped in front of Zephyrine.

"Captain Yarrow." He said it like a toast. "We meet at last. Your navigator has told me a great deal about you."

And there it was. He said it deliberately, Ash knew — a knife laid flat on the table between them all, so everyone could see it. Marisol's head came up. Teodor went very still. Zephyrine didn't move at all, but Ash saw her eyes flick sideways, just once, toward Ash, and the look in them wasn't hatred. It was worse. It was grief arriving ahead of understanding.

"Ash," Zephyrine said quietly. "Is that true?"

Every version of the truth she had rehearsed for eleven days — the threats, the photographs of her mother's clinic, the letter about her sister's school, the polite men who explained how easily a small family far from anywhere could come apart — all of it jammed in her throat like a fist.

"Yes," she said. "Since before we took this contract. They have my family, Zeph. Not — not literally. But they might as well."

"Where's your transmitter?" Okonjo asked pleasantly. "Show the captain your little radio, Ash. Show her how good you've been."

Ash knelt, lifted the floor panel, and held the device up in both hands like an offering. The green light blinked. Rain drummed on the deck. Behind Okonjo, the cutter's searchlight swept the cave mouth across the water — the low tide had begun to drain the bay again, earlier than any table predicted, the sea pulling back like a held breath, exposing the black arch of stone that no chart admitted existed.

"There's something you should know before you take us in," Ash said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "I've been reporting to you for eleven days. And in eleven days, Lieutenant, what have you actually gotten? Nothing. Because I gave you nothing. I stalled you. Every call, every day, I bought them another day. If you'd moved when you wanted to, they'd have had the vault open and the evidence copied before you ever got here. Instead you waited on me. So whatever I am—" her thumb found the transmitter's casing, and she turned it over, showing him the seam, "—I'm the reason you're too late."

Okonjo's face did something complicated. "That device is supposed to transmit continuously."

"It transmits when I press it. Which means everything you think you've heard from this boat for eleven days—" she let him do the math himself, watched it land, "—was whatever I chose to record. Including, Lieutenant, the last three conversations you and I had. From the other side."

The young sailor's rifle wavered. Okonjo's jaw tightened, and for one naked second Ash saw the thing underneath the calm: a man realizing he had been played by the smallest piece on his board.

"Take it from her," he said. "Restrain all of them. We'll sort identities aboard the cutter—"

It happened fast after that, and Ash would spend the rest of her life replaying it in fragments. Teodor moving — huge, slow-looking Teodor moving like a dropped crane load, putting his body between the sailors and Marisol. The searchlight swinging. Zephyrine's hand closing around Ash's wrist, not gently, and her voice in Ash's ear, low and furious and alive:

"Cave. Now. Can you get us in?"

"I've mapped it," Ash said. "From the soundings. I know where the water goes."

"Then you're not a traitor, you're our pilot. Move."

They went over the port side together — Zephyrine first, then Ash, into water cold enough to stop thought, swimming hard through the rain-hammered chop toward the black arch as shouts crackled behind them and a searchlight blade sliced the surface inches from Ash's kicking feet. The cave swallowed them by degrees: first the shoulders, then the noise of the storm, then the light, until there was only the slap of their own strokes echoing off wet stone and the thin gray oval of the entrance shrinking behind them like a closing eye.

Inside, they dragged themselves onto a shelf of rock above the draining waterline, gasping. Somewhere deeper in the dark, the vault waited with everything in it — the logs, the footage, the proof of what the navy had done to its own expedition, to Zephyrine's father, to the truth.

Behind them, muffled by stone, came the sound of the cutter's launch grinding ashore, and boots, and voices. Okonjo wouldn't leave without what he came for. He had five sailors, rifles, and a deadline called landfall.

Ash pressed her back to the cold rock, lungs burning, and looked at Zephyrine — dripping, shaking with rage or cold or both, alive.

"He'll seal the entrance behind him if he has to," Ash said. "Once the surge comes, this whole system floods. We have maybe two hours before the tide turns and the cave becomes a grave."

Zephyrine was already pulling her dive mask down off her forehead, eyes fixed on the darkness ahead, where the vault lay waiting with her father's name in it.

"Then we'd better not waste a minute of the time you bought us," she said, and stepped off the ledge into the black water.
