## 8. Pressure

The sea came for the cave the way a debt collector comes at midnight — no warning, no negotiation, just sudden and total claim.

Zephyrine felt it before she heard it: a change in the water's temper against her skin, a pressure pulse rolling through the flooded passage like a fist closing. Twenty years of diving had taught her body to read the ocean the way sailors read cloud banks, and every instinct she owned was screaming the same word.

*Run.*

She didn't run. She was thirty meters inside the cave system with her dive light pinned on the vault's final compartment, and in her hands — sealed in the waterproof case she'd pried from its bracket four minutes ago — was a hard drive the size of a hymnal.

The navy sank its own expedition. The footage existed. Her father hadn't been a casualty of bad weather; he'd been a line item in an erasure.

And now the ocean wanted it back.

* * *

It had started well. That was the thing she'd remember later, how deceptively gentle the morning had been — the monsoon still two tides out, the bay holding that bruised green calm that divers call *false glass*. Marisol had run the surface comms from the skiff. Teodor had rigged the guide lines double. Even Ash had been steady on the plot, calling depths off the chart they'd drawn themselves because no official chart would ever admit this cave existed.

"Bottom time's your master," Marisol had said through the regulator link, her voice tinny and close. "Whatever you find down there, the cave doesn't get a vote."

"The cave always gets a vote," Zephyrine had answered. "It just usually votes no."

The vault's last compartment had been behind a bulkhead plate stamped with the expedition crest — the same crest that appeared on her father's manifest, listed under *crew*, not *casualty*. The plate had come away with a groan of corroded fasteners, and behind it, dry as a sermon in a sealed jar, sat the drive and a single sheet of laminated orders.

She'd read the orders by dive light. Three sentences. Authorization to dispose of expedition materials in situ. Signature block redacted. Date: eleven days before her father drowned.

Her hands had not shaken. That surprised her. She'd expected fury to hit like a narcotic hit, warm and blinding. Instead there was only a terrible clarity, cold as the water itself, as if twenty years of grief had been ballast and someone had finally cut it loose.

Then the surge came.

* * *

The first pulse knocked her sideways into the bulkhead. Her shoulder took the impact; her light spun wild across the rock, painting strobing shadows of stalactites like teeth. Through the comm came Marisol's voice, sharp with adrenaline:

"Zeph, tide gauge just jumped forty centimeters in ninety seconds. That's not the storm yet — that's the bay *breathing wrong*. Get out. Get out now."

"I have it," Zephyrine said. "I have the record."

"Then swim."

She swam. Or she tried to. The passage back toward the entrance chamber had been a generous corridor on the way in — wide enough for two divers abreast, Teodor's guide line humming taut along the left wall. Now the water in it moved like something alive and angry, surging in, dragging back, surging in, each cycle stronger than the last. The storm was still offshore, but the monsoon's leading edge was already pumping the bay like a bellows, and the cave was exhaling through the only throat it had.

Zephyrine clipped the drive case to her harness with a locking carabiner and followed the line.

Fifty meters from the entrance chamber, the ceiling spoke.

Not a sound exactly — more a feeling transmitted through the water, a deep structural complaint, and then grit began sifting down through her light beam like brown snow. She stopped. In a cave-in, stopping is death and moving is also death, but one of them is a decision.

"Marisol. Tell me what you see from outside."

A pause too long. Then: "The mouth — Zeph, the mouth is half closed. There's a rockfall shelf forming at the lip. I can see daylight through maybe two meters of gap and it's *shrinking*."

Teodor's voice cut in, low and steady the way he got when things were worst: "Surge cycle's about ninety seconds. Between pulses there's a slack window. It won't be clean, but it'll be real. Count them, Zeph. Ride the exhale."

She counted. One. Two. On the fourth cycle she found the rhythm — the water rushing *out* of the cave like the whole system inhaling through its teeth, and in that dragged-back moment the current in the corridor went almost still, a held breath between blows.

She moved on the exhales. Kick, kick, kick, hand over hand up the guide line while the water held its breath, then flatten against the wall and let the surge hammer past her like freight. Grit rained. Somewhere behind her, deeper in, something large let go of the ceiling with a boom she felt in her molars.

Thirty meters out. Twenty.

At fifteen meters from the entrance chamber, the guide line went slack.

Not cut — *buried*. A slab had come down across the corridor ahead, and the line ran under it and did not come out the other side. Her light found the blockage: a fall of rock the width of a bus, wedged floor to ceiling, leaving a gap at the top no wider than her shoulders. Water was forcing through it in a white rope of current, screaming through the gap.

Through the comm, distant and distorted, Marisol was saying her name. Saying it again. Saying it the way you say a name when you're starting to be afraid the person attached to it has stopped being reachable.

* * *

Here is what the arithmetic looked like, hanging in the dark at fifteen meters from daylight:

Her gauge read sixty bar. Enough for perhaps twelve minutes of easy swimming, less with exertion, far less if she fought current. The gap above the rockfall would take everything she had to force — a minute, maybe two, of full-throttle effort against flow that wanted her dead. Doable. Probably doable. If nothing else fell. If the slack windows kept their rhythm. If the drive case didn't snag.

But the drive case was the problem, wasn't it. Because the case was bulky, and the gap was narrow, and there were two ways through it: clip the case ahead of her and push it through first, which risked losing it to the current roaring through the gap — or hold it against her chest and go through together, which meant her profile was bigger, her margin thinner, her odds worse by a factor she could feel in her chest rather than calculate.

Twelve minutes. One narrow door. Two lives in her hands, and only one of them hers.

She thought about her father's logbook, recovered two days ago from the deep shelf where he'd hidden it, where he'd known — God help him, he'd *known* — that someone would come looking someday. His handwriting, cramped and urgent in the final pages: *They will call it weather. It was not weather.* And beneath that, the sentence she'd read forty times and still couldn't carry calmly: *If my girl ever reads this, tell her the sea keeps honest books even when men don't.*

The drive in its case held the footage. Camera records from the expedition's final week. Whatever the navy had needed buried badly enough to sink a ship and write a man into the ledger as weather — it was on this drive. Proof. The kind of proof that topples careers, opens inquests, drags the dead out of unmarked ledgers and gives them names.

The kind of proof that gets people killed. Had gotten people killed. Would, very possibly, get her killed in the next ten minutes if she loved it too much to let it go.

"Zeph." Marisol's voice, stripped raw. "Talk to me. Your transmitter's pinging but you're quiet and I need you to be loud right now."

"I'm at the rockfall," Zephyrine said. "There's a gap. It's tight."

"How tight?"

"Tight enough that I'm doing math."

Silence on the line. Then Teodor, gently, the engineer's mercy of a man who understood load calculations: "Zeph. Whatever you're carrying — the numbers are the numbers. Don't let the cargo make the decision."

* * *

That was the moment. She knew it even as she hung there in the brown snowfall of silt, breathing compressed air that tasted of metal and ending. This was the moment the whole wrecked arc of her life had been bending toward — the salvage diver who never stayed anywhere longer than a lease, daughter of a man erased by his own government, holding in her arms the one object that could un-erase him.

Letting go of it would mean her father stayed a line item forever. *Casualty, cause: weather.* Staying with it would mean joining him, and the proof going dark anyway, sealed in a flooded cave with both of them.

Unless—

Unless she made it through with it. Unless the slack window held. Unless the ceiling held. Unless.

Her father had been ordered to sink the evidence, and he'd sunk it so deep and so carefully that it had taken twenty years and a lying tide to find it. He'd paid with himself. And the last page of his logbook — the page she'd copied out and kept folded against her heart since the night she read it — ended with a line she'd taken for a farewell and now heard differently:

*Some things should surface. Some things should stay down. Wisdom is knowing which is which, and love is paying either price without flinching.*

He'd chosen to bury it. Not from cowardice. From judgment — the terrible, adult judgment that some truths cost more alive than dead, that the men who signed those orders would still be in their offices long after the inquest files yellowed, and the only people who'd pay fresh interest on the revelation were people like her crew, people like Ash's family, people with no armor.

He'd paid the price without flinching. The question turning slowly in the dark in front of her was whether she could.

The surge hit. She flattened. Silt blinded her light to a brown murk. Through the roar she heard the ceiling groan again, closer now, and her gauge needle lay at fifty-five bar and falling, and the gap above the rockfall glimmered faintly with refracted daylight — daylight, actual daylight, the ordinary miracle of it — two meters of shrinking freedom directly overhead.

"Fifty-five bar," she told the comm. "I'm going through on the next exhale. With the drive."

"Zeph—" Marisol started.

"With the drive," Zephyrine said, and found that her voice didn't shake. "My father bought this truth with his life. I'm not spending it twice."

She clipped the case high and tight against her chest plate, checked her backup light, and watched the water. Exhale coming — she could feel the corridor beginning to drain, the current slackening, the whole cave drawing breath.

One. Two. Three.

She kicked for the gap.

The rock bit her tanks with a screech of steel on stone. The current grabbed the drive case and hauled at it like a living thing. Her regulator hose snagged, wrenched her head sideways, and for one white-hot second she was blind, deaf, breathless, pinned in the earth's clenched fist with the ocean trying to take back everything it had ever lent her —

— and then she was through.

Through, and tumbling into the entrance chamber, and the daylight gap ahead was a knife-slit of green-gray storm light barely wider than a coffin lid, and behind her, deep in the dark, the ceiling finally finished its argument. The boom rolled through the water like a verdict. The vault, the corridor, the rockfall, the last twenty years of silence — all of it collapsed into rubble and black water in a single convulsion that shoved her forward on its own shockwave like the cave itself deciding, at the end, to vote yes.

She shot out of the mouth into open bay and chaos — rain needling the surface, the skiff wallowing gray in the swell, Marisol hauling on the tether line with both fists, screaming words the wind stole.

Zephyrine broke the surface gasping, one arm locked around the drive case, and looked back at the cliff face in time to see the last of the cave mouth swallow itself. Stone ground against stone. The sea poured in. By the time the next wave lifted her, there was nothing there but wet rock and foam — no mouth, no vault, no admission.

Just the drive against her ribs, and five days of monsoon between them and home, and somewhere out in that boiling gray horizon, a navy patrol boat turning toward the bay.

"Get us out of here," Zephyrine said.

Marisol was already throwing the throttle.
