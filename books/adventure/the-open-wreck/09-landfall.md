## 9. Landfall

**Marisol**

The rain came sideways, like it had somewhere better to be and no patience for the harbor.

Marisol hauled the last fender aboard with her arms burning and her hair plastered to her skull, and she thought: *this is the stupidest thing we have ever done, and I once watched Teodor weld a fuel line while smoking.* The *Vela Oscura* groaned against the dock, the whole hull shuddering as if the boat itself understood what was coming and wanted no part of it.

"Lines off!" Zephyrine's voice cut through the wind, raw from shouting but steady underneath, the way a keel is steady under a deck that's pitching. "Marisol, get below and secure the forward locker — if we take water over the bow I don't want tools flying around like shrapnel."

"Aye." She didn't argue. Arguing with Zeph in weather was like arguing with the tide; you'd lose, and you'd be tired afterward.

Below, the boat was a drum being beaten by the storm. She wedged herself against the bulkhead and cinched straps, and through the hull she could feel it — a deep, rhythmic concussion rolling in from the point where the cave mouth had been. Not thunder. Thunder didn't keep time.

The tide was coming home. And it was bringing the whole monsoon with it, pouring down into that limestone throat like water down a drain, sealing it shut behind them.

She'd stood at the rail an hour ago and watched the sea climb back over the rocks that had been dry as bone two days before. Watched the cave mouth swallow itself. Somewhere in there was a vault full of government paper and a dead man's logbook pages and twenty years of lies, all of it going dark and drowned and gone.

*Or so they'll believe,* Ash had said, and wouldn't explain, and now Ash was standing on the dock in the rain with a duffel bag and no rain jacket, waiting to be collected by people who owned her.

Marisol's chest did something complicated about that. She shoved it down with the locker straps and climbed back up into the wet.

* * *

**Teodor**

Teodor had the engine room lit like an operating theater because he refused to do surgery in the dark, even when the patient was a forty-year-old marine diesel and the surgeon was a man whose hands were shaking from caffeine and fear in roughly equal measure.

The navy patrol boat was still out there. He'd seen it on the scope before the rain closed the world down — holding position past the breakwater, riding the building swell like it had nothing better to do than watch them leave. Which, he supposed, it did.

"They're not following," Ash had told them, an hour ago, her voice flat as slate. "I stalled them. Told my handler the vault flooded on the spring tide, evidence unrecoverable, dive team withdrawing ahead of the storm. They bought it because it's what they wanted to buy." A pause. "They'll want me ashore for debrief. If I'm not on your boat when you clear the harbor, they won't look for one."

Zephyrine had looked at her for a long moment — the kind of look Teodor had only seen her give the sea, the kind that measured depth without touching bottom. Then she'd said, "You come back from this. That's not a request."

Ash had almost smiled. Almost.

Now Teodor fed the engine fuel and listened to its heartbeat and tried not to think about the transmission log he'd found three days ago, the little encrypted bursts ticking out of the nav station every six hours like a clock counting down his own paranoia. He'd assumed the worst. He'd sharpened a wrench, for God's sake, and slept with it under his bunk like a man in a prison novel.

It had been Ash. It had always been Ash. And it had never, not once, been what he'd imagined.

"Engine's good," he told himself, because there was no one else down here to tell. "Everything's good."

The hull took a wave broadside and the whole compartment lurched, a wrench skittering across the deck plates, and Teodor grabbed the rail and laughed — a short, cracked sound the storm ate instantly. Nothing was good. Everything was terrible. But the engine was good, and the crew was aboard, and the truth was in a waterproof case lashed under the floor plates where no navy diver would ever think to look, because as far as the navy knew, there was no truth left to find.

He throttled up when the bell rang from above. The *Vela Oscura* turned her nose toward the open harbor mouth and the black screaming wall of weather beyond it.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

The harbor mouth was a door slamming.

Zephyrine had run bars in weather before — she'd run worse than this, once, off a coast she didn't name anymore — but she had never run one with a navy cutter sitting fat and patient on her flank, watching. Every instinct said the watchful ones were the dangerous ones. Stillness was what predators did before the strike.

But the strike didn't come. The cutter sat. The rain fell. And the *Vela Oscura* went through the gap between the breakwater arms with six feet of clearance on either side and a sea state that turned the world into a washing machine set to destroy.

"Hard to port!" she screamed, and Marisol threw the wheel, and the boat climbed a wave the size of a chapel and surfed down its face with the propeller singing and every plank in the hull praying aloud.

For eleven minutes there was nothing but water and noise and the animal work of keeping a small vessel alive inside a large catastrophe. Zephyrine stopped being a person with a dead father and a stolen archive and became hands and eyes and the next correct decision, over and over, the way she'd trained herself to be since she was nineteen years old and the navy had handed her a folded flag and a lie.

Then they were through. Then the swell opened up long and rolling instead of steep and murderous, and the rain thinned just enough to show her the coastline falling away astern, the harbor lights smeared into smears, the point of rock where the cave had been already invisible beneath the chaos of the surge.

Gone. Sealed. Twenty years of silence poured back over the wound like a bandage made of ocean.

Her father had known this would happen. She understood it now, fully, the way you understand a thing only after you've lived it: he had chosen the water as the keeper of secrets because the water never talks, never leaks, never gets subpoenaed. It just holds. And holds. And holds.

Except it hadn't held everything. It had given her back a logbook. It had given her one page — the page she'd taken, the page in her breast pocket right now, dry inside three layers of plastic, pressed against her heartbeat like a second heart:

*I did what they asked. Tell my girl the sea is honest, even when men aren't.*

Zephyrine put the wheel over and pointed the boat south, away from the restricted zone, away from the cutter, away from everything, and let the monsoon push them along like a hand on their backs.

"We made it," Marisol shouted beside her, half question, half prayer.

Zephyrine looked at the gray horizon, at the wall of rain marching toward them like a country invading another country, and felt something in her chest unclench for the first time in twenty years. Exhaustion, maybe. Or the strange lightness of a woman who has finally decided what she's carrying and what she's leaving behind.

"We made it," she said.

Behind them, the cave finished closing. Ahead of them, Ash stood alone on the dock getting smaller and smaller, until the rain took her too.

* * *

**Ash**

The navy jeep smelled of mildew and gun oil, which struck Ash as an honest combination, at least.

She sat in the back seat with her duffel between her knees and her hands folded on top of it, and Major Okonkwo's man — a lieutenant with a jaw like a filing cabinet — drove her through streets already flooding, wipers losing their fight against the rain, streetlights stuttering in the gusts.

"You were supposed to call in daily," the lieutenant said, eyes on the road.

"The storm took the relay tower," Ash said. "You can verify that."

"I will."

"Please do."

He glanced at her in the mirror then, briefly, and she gave him nothing — the flat, competent blankness she'd spent four years perfecting, the face of a woman with no interior. Inside, she was running calculations faster than any nav computer on the *Vela*: how much they believed, how much they suspected, whether Zeph had cleared the harbor mouth yet, whether the waterproof case under the floor plates would survive the crossing, whether the copy she'd mailed from the post office in Port Cael — postage paid, address handwritten, three days ago, the single most dangerous act of her life — was already moving north through a postal system too slow and too indifferent to stop it.

They thought the vault was drowned. They thought the evidence was gone. They thought their informant was coming in from the cold, chastened and useful.

Let them think it. Thinking was free. Believing would cost them.

The jeep swung onto the coastal road and the sea appeared on the left, enormous and white-capped and furious, and Ash looked at it the way other people looked at old photographs. Her family was inland, safe, paid for with four years of her soul. Her crew was southbound, alive, paid for with whatever was left.

That was the whole ledger. She'd balanced it. Whatever happened in the debrief room tonight — and something was going to happen, because Major Okonkwo hadn't risen to major by believing convenient stories — the ledger balanced.

"Almost there," the lieutenant said.

The naval station rose out of the rain ahead, floodlit and grim, barbed wire glittering like tinsel. Ash straightened her collar. In her pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, was the one thing they hadn't searched for because they'd never thought to: a single page of her own handwriting, dated today, naming names, listing dates, describing exactly what the expedition of 1996 had found and exactly who had ordered it sunk.

Insurance. For her family. For the crew. For a salvage diver she'd betrayed and saved in the same breath.

If she walked out of this station, the letter would go in the mail tomorrow. If she didn't walk out, well. The navy had taught her that dead drops were a solved problem.

The jeep stopped. A guard opened her door into the hammering rain.

"After you," the lieutenant said.

Ash stepped out into the storm, lifted her face to it for one unguarded second — letting it wash her clean of the boat, the bay, the cave, the whole drowned year — and walked toward the lights.

Behind her, far to the south, a small steel boat ran hard for open water, carrying a secret the size of a man's life.

And in Zephyrine Yarrow's breast pocket, dry as a promise, a dead man's handwriting waited to be read one more time — and then, at last, let go.
