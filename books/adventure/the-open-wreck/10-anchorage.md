## 10. Anchorage

The harbor town had a name nobody used. Everyone just called it the harbor, as if there were only one in the world, and for three weeks now it had been mine.

I noticed it first in small ways. I stopped sleeping with my boots by the bed. I let the salt crust on the *Magpie's* rails go two days before scraping it, because there was no departure to prepare for, no tide window to beat. I bought a kettle. A kettle is a promise you make to a place — it says *I will be here tomorrow morning to boil this again.*

Marisol noticed before anyone. She came up the gangway with a crate of limes balanced on one hip and found me sitting in the wheelhouse doing nothing at all, which for me was like finding a shark sunbathing.

"You're sick," she said.

"I'm resting."

"Zeph. You don't rest. You refit. You provision. You pace." She set the crate down and studied me the way she'd study a hull for hairline cracks. "You've been in port nineteen days."

"Nineteen days isn't a record."

"It is for you. Teo keeps a ledger." She grinned, but her eyes stayed serious. "He says you're either dying or nesting."

Maybe both. The monsoon had taken something out of me that I hadn't known was load-bearing. We'd come through it — barely, the *Magpie* holed twice, Ash gone ashore into the arms of the people who owned her fear, the cave drowned under forty feet of new water and a hundred years of silt to come. The navy believed they'd won. Let them. Winning against an empty sea is its own kind of drowning.

"Paysheets," I said, and pulled them from under the chart table where they'd been waiting, signed and sealed, since Tuesday. "Everyone's paid off. Full shares plus hazard. Tell them."

Marisol didn't take the papers right away. "And you?"

"What about me?"

"What does Zephyrine Yarrow do when the crew's paid and the boat's fueled and the horizon's sitting right there being free?"

I looked out the wheelhouse glass at the harbor — the fishing boats knocking gently together, the gulls arguing over the fish market, the hills behind town going green from all the rain. Somewhere out past the headland, past the restricted buoys the navy still hadn't removed, my father lay inside a vault that no longer existed, holding secrets that no longer existed, and for the first time in twenty years the thought of it didn't feel like a debt.

"I don't know," I said, and discovered I meant it as good news.

* * *

Teodor was the last one to sign. He came down to the quay in his good shirt, the one without hydraulic fluid on the cuffs, and stood over the paysheet for a long time before he touched the pen.

"This is severance," he said. "Not wages."

"It's both."

"You're selling the *Magpie*."

The broker's offer had come in that morning, generous enough to be insulting, which is how you know it's real. "I'm buying an apartment above the chandlery," I said. "First floor. There's a window that faces the water."

Teodor sat down heavily on a bollard, which for a man his size was an act of surrender. "Twenty-two years I've turned wrenches next to you. You've never once said the word *apartment*. You've said *bunk*, *berth*, *rack*, *hammock once, in Borneo, never again*—"

"Teo."

"—and now you're telling me you have a *window*."

"Two windows."

He laughed, then it caught somewhere in his chest and became something else, and he pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes for a moment before signing. His signature came out crooked. Everything about him was crooked; it was why we'd lasted.

"The others?" he asked.

"Gone or going. Marisol's taking a deck job on a research vessel — legitimate science, can you imagine. She'll hate it by March and love it by June. Petey's got a cousin with a tug outfit upcoast."

"And Ash?"

I'd gotten one letter from her, forwarded through three ports, unsigned except for a single drawn anchor. It said: *They believe what I told them. I believe what I told myself. One of those is survivable.* It didn't say where she was. It didn't need to. People like Ash don't surface until they choose to, and when she did, she'd know the harbor, and the harbor would know her.

"Ash is ashoring," I said. "Her word, not mine."

Teodor folded his copy of the paysheet with the care of a man folding a flag. Then he stood, and instead of shaking my hand he put his fist lightly against my sternum, the old engine-room salute, knuckles over the heart.

"Keep the windows clean," he said, and walked away up the quay without looking back, because looking back was for people who intended to return, and we were both pretending this wasn't goodbye so hard that it almost worked.

* * *

The lease was four pages long and I read every line twice, which made the notary nervous. My hand hovered over the signature block longer than it had ever hovered over anything. Twenty years of contracts — salvage agreements, insurance waivers, articles of engagement signed in eleven ports in six alphabets — and every one of them had been a way of leaving. This one was a way of staying. The pen felt heavier than any dive gear I'd ever worn.

I signed. The ink dried. Nothing collapsed. No alarm sounded. The notary stamped it and asked if I wanted a copy and I said yes, please, and she looked faintly disappointed, as if she'd been hoping for a story.

Upstairs, the apartment smelled of fresh paint and someone else's cooking history. I opened both windows and let the harbor in — diesel and brine and frying onions and the bell buoy counting the swell like a patient heart. I hung nothing on the walls. There would be time. That was the whole point, the strange luxurious terrifying point: there would be time.

Then I went back down to the *Magpie* one last time, because there was a fire to build and a book to feed it.

* * *

My father's logbook had survived the monsoon, the cave, the navy, and my own cowardice and courage in equal measure, because I had kept it wrapped in oilcloth inside the spare-air locker, where even I had pretended not to know it was. Most of it was proof — coordinates, orders, the expedition manifest with his name printed among the living, the final entry in a hand gone shaky: *They want it on the bottom. They want me on the bottom with it. If you're reading this, Zephyrie, the water lied to you too.*

I had read that page maybe forty times. I could recite it. I could recite the way the paper felt, soft as skin at the corners, the way the ink had bloomed where seawater had kissed it and retreated.

The rest — the navy correspondence, the survey charts, the page proving whose order had sent a hundred men into the dark — I carried up onto the *Magpie*'s foredeck in a steel bucket, along with a bottle of lamp oil and my father's old brass lighter, the one engraved with a diving helmet and the motto of a company that had never really existed: *WE GO DOWN SO OTHERS NEED NOT.*

Marisol appeared at the rail as I crouched over the bucket. She didn't ask. She'd earned the right not to ask, and I loved her for spending it.

"You sure?" was all she said.

"No," I said. "That's why it has to be tonight, while I'm still sure enough."

I fed the pages in one at a time. They burned fast and blue-edged, the truth about the expedition curling into ash that the wind took out over the water and scattered across the same bay that had tried to keep it. I watched each page go. I grieved each page. And underneath the grief, quiet as ballast, something else settled: relief. Not the relief of forgetting — I would never forget — but the relief of putting down a weight that was never mine to carry. The dead don't need lawyers. They need witnesses, and I had witnessed, and now the sea could hold what it held.

The last sheet I saved was the final entry. *If you're reading this, Zephyrie.* I read it once more by the light of the burning rest, then folded it small and put it inside my shirt, over my heart, where Teodor's fist had landed, where it would stay.

When the bucket held nothing but warm ash, I stood at the rail a long time. The harbor lights came on one by one along the quay. Somewhere a radio played something with a steel drum in it. The bell buoy counted. Counted. Counted.

"Fair winds, Papa," I said. "I'm anchoring."

* * *

Morning found me in the chandlery downstairs, buying rope I didn't strictly need, because a woman has to start somewhere and rope is as good a place as any. Old Ferro behind the counter rang it up and squinted at me.

"Heard you sold the *Magpie*."

"Heard right."

"Heard you signed a lease." He said it the way other men say *heard you joined a cult*.

"Two windows," I said.

"Huh." He bagged the rope, then paused, and his voice dropped into the register fishermen reserve for weather and wrecks. "Fella came through yesterday asking after you. Navy type, civvies, bad shoes. Wanted to know if the Yarrow woman was settling in or passing through."

The old cold moved through me, quick and familiar — and then, strangely, passed. I thought of Ash's letter. *One of those is survivable.* I thought of a bucket of ash riding the tide. Whatever the navy came looking for, it was on the bottom now, and so was the version of me who ran.

"What did you tell him?" I asked.

Ferro shrugged. "Told him the Yarrow woman rents the flat with the two windows. Told him if he wants to ask her anything, he can knock on her door like anybody else." He slid the rope across the counter. "You expecting trouble?"

I picked up the rope, heavy and honest in my arms, and looked out through the chandlery glass at my harbor, my street, my door with the brass number I now knew by heart.

"No," I said. "But I'm home if it finds me."
