## 1. Miles, Not Years

Three years, four months, and roughly eleven thousand miles. That's what my life added up to, if you measured it the way I did — not in birthdays or seasons or any of the units normal people used, but in distance put between me and a man who thought he could map everything except his own daughter.

I was measuring it that morning too, standing on the salvage dock at Port Amara with a rusted winch cable in my hands and salt crusted in my eyebrows, watching the harbor do its slow gray business under a sky the color of old tin. Port Amara in the off-season is not a place so much as an argument between the sea and everything people have built to keep it out. The pilings groan. The water slaps back. Nobody wins.

"Paloma." Dez, the dock boss, had a voice like gravel poured through a funnel and a talent for finding me exactly when I was trying to disappear into work. "You're doing it wrong."

"I'm doing it fine."

"You're doing it angry. Different thing." He took the cable from me with the gentleness of a man handling something that might bite, which, to be fair, it might. Frayed wire will take a finger off you before you finish saying *oops*. "Barnacles on the Merrow's chain. Go scrape."

Scraping barnacles. The great adventure of Paloma Pemberton, twenty-six years old, formerly of the Pemberton Institute of Coastal Archaeology by way of her father's kitchen table, currently of nowhere in particular. I'd been in Port Amara six weeks, which was two weeks longer than my average, and I could feel the itch starting under my skin — the one that said *move along, girl, nothing here but another set of walls you'll eventually walk out of.*

I walked down the dock toward the Merrow, a half-sunk ketch they were stripping for scrap, and I did the thing I always did when the itch got loud: I counted miles.

Port Amara to Cape Reckoning, up the coast: two hundred and ten nautical, give or take. I knew because I'd memorized the coastal charts when I was nine years old, curled up on the floor of my father's study while he drew survey lines and I pretended not to be listening. He'd caught me once, tested me on soundings like other fathers quizzed their kids on multiplication tables, and been so delighted when I got them right that I'd felt — for about a week — like the most important person in the world.

Then he'd gone back to his maps, and I'd learned that being important to Elias Pemberton was a part-time position with no benefits.

The barnacles came off in ugly white chunks. I let my hands work while my head wandered, because that was the arrangement we had, my head and my hands. My head went where it wanted; my hands kept me fed.

Eleven thousand miles. I'd actually tried to calculate it once, properly, sitting in a bunkhouse outside Saltmere with a borrowed pencil and a chart torn from an almanac. Every job, every port, every desperate bus ride and deckhand hitch since the night I'd walked out of my father's house with one bag and a speech I'd rehearsed for a month. The speech had been good. It had ended with something like *you'll never even notice I'm gone,* which was the cruelest thing I could think to say, because it was also the thing I most feared was true.

He hadn't come after me. That was the part I never said out loud, even to myself, even at three in the morning in whatever rented room I was bleeding rent into. Three years, and not one letter, not one telegram, not one embarrassed relative sent as envoy. Nothing. And I had built myself a whole philosophy on top of that silence, load-bearing and everything: *he doesn't need me, therefore I don't need him, therefore freedom.* A beautiful structure. You could live in it, if you didn't mind the drafts.

"Hey. Salvage girl." One of the Merrow's owners, a squat man named Ferro whose idea of conversation was commands, jerked his chin at the water. "Careful on the port side. Deck's soft."

"Everything's soft if you stand on it long enough," I said, and scraped.

* * *

Lunch was bread and sardines on the breakwater, legs dangling over the harbor mouth, watching the fishing fleet come and go. This was my favorite hour of any day in any port, and I want to be honest about why: it was the only hour that belonged entirely to me. No boss, no crew, no pretense that I was building anything. Just me and the water and the small honest business of eating.

A mail boat was working its way up the channel, the little steamer that brought letters and parcels twice a week from the rail head inland. I watched it without thinking anything in particular, which is how the worst thoughts get you.

Because here's the thing about my father's silence: I checked for mail anyway. Every port, every bunkhouse, every general delivery window in forty towns along this coast, some part of me asked the question — *anything for Pemberton?* — and every time the answer was no, I told myself I'd only asked because I was expecting a parts catalog or a wage dispute letter or literally anything else. Eleven thousand miles of asking-by-not-asking.

The mail boat tied up. A boy in a rubber coat started distributing envelopes to the handful of people who waited on the quay, and I finished my sardines and stood up and made myself walk away, because I had barnacles to scrape and a philosophy to maintain.

Except.

Except that evening, back at my camp — I camped, mostly, because rooms cost money and money was the one thing my freedom reliably failed to produce — I found the envelope.

It was in my kit. My actual kit, the canvas duffel I'd carried through eleven thousand miles, the bag that held everything I owned, which fit the description of *everything I own* precisely because it contained almost nothing. I'd opened it that night looking for my spare shirt, and there it was, wedged down between a coil of rope and a tin of waterproof matches, face-up, like it had been waiting.

An envelope. Heavy paper, good stock, the kind you don't see often out here. Addressed in handwriting I would have known blindfolded, upside down, underwater, dead — the sharp precise capitals of Elias Pemberton, who lettered his field notes like an engraver because he believed sloppy notation was a moral failing.

*P. PEMBERTON.*

No endearment. No address beyond my initial. Even now, even across three years of silence, he couldn't bring himself to write *my daughter* or *Paloma* or anything that admitted we were people to each other rather than entries in a ledger.

And postmarked — I turned it over, and my stomach did something complicated — postmarked fourteen months ago. Fourteen months. It had been riding around in my kit for over a year. Which meant I'd carried it through at least three ports without knowing, which meant at some point I'd packed that duffel with my fingers touching this envelope and I hadn't felt it, hadn't known, hadn't —

No. That wasn't possible. I'd have remembered. I'd have felt it. Unless I'd found it before and buried it deeper and forgotten, and honestly, with my track record of self-deception, I couldn't rule it out.

I sat down on my bedroll with the envelope in both hands and did not open it.

Here's what I want to explain, because it probably looks insane from the outside: I wanted to open it more than I had wanted anything in years. My hands shook. My throat did that tight hot thing. Fourteen months ago my father had sat down somewhere — his study, maybe, or a ship's cabin, or God knows where — and written to me, and I had been carrying his words around unopened like a stone in my chest without even knowing it.

But opening it meant answering it. Opening it meant the silence was over, and the silence, terrible as it was, was mine. It was the one thing I'd built that couldn't leave me. If I read his words, then he was back in my life whether he stayed or not, and if he wrote *come home* I would have to decide, and if he wrote nothing much at all — a forwarding note, a formality, *enclosed please find* — that would be worse, that would be eleven thousand miles of exile justified by a man who couldn't even be bothered to miss me properly.

So I didn't open it. I told myself I'd open it tomorrow, when I was calmer, when I'd had time to prepare, knowing full well that I had already had fourteen months to prepare and hadn't managed it.

I wrapped the envelope in my spare shirt — the one I'd originally been hunting for, irony noted — and pushed it down to the bottom of the kit, under the rope, under the matches, under everything.

Then I lay awake in my tent listening to the harbor slap its slow argument against the seawall, and I did the arithmetic I always did, the only arithmetic that had never once comforted me.

Eleven thousand miles. Three years, four months. Zero letters answered, zero letters received — except, apparently, one, carried unknowing against my spine through half the coast like a splinter working toward the heart.

Tomorrow I'd scrape more barnacles. Tomorrow I'd tell Dez I was thinking of moving on, and he'd grunt and say the Merrow needed two more days, and I'd say maybe I'd stay for them, and neither of us would believe it, because we both knew the pattern by now. Six weeks was already a record. The itch was already talking.

*Move along, girl.*

And I would move along. That was the whole architecture of my life: motion mistaken for meaning, distance mistaken for direction, freedom worn like a coat that had never quite fit but that I'd tailored myself, so what was I going to do, admit it?

The lamp guttered. Outside, the tide turned, and the harbor's argument changed key.

I slept badly, and dreamed of charts, and woke before dawn with salt on my lips and the itch under my skin louder than ever.

It was two days later — two days of scraping, hauling, and pointedly not thinking about the weight at the bottom of my kit — that the old man came stumbling out of the dark into the light of my fire.
