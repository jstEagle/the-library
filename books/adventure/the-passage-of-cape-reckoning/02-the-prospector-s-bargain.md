## 2. The Prospector's Bargain

There are three kinds of people who wander into a salvage camp after dark: the lost, the lying, and the dying. I'd learned to sort them by the way they walked. The lost walk like they're apologizing to the ground. The liars walk like they own it. The dying don't really walk at all — they arrive, the way weather arrives, and you just have to decide what to do about them.

Hollis was the third kind.

I heard him before I saw him, which should have been impossible given how much noise the surf was making that night, but there's a particular sound a man makes when his body is quitting on him — a wet, dragging shuffle, like someone hauling a sack of wet rope through gravel. It carried over the waves the way a scream carries over music. You can't unhear it.

I had a fire going at the edge of the wrecking yard, a pot of fish stew I'd stretched across three days, and a half-mended net in my lap that I was mending mostly to avoid thinking about the letter sitting in my kit. Three years of letters, actually, all unopened, all forwarded from port to port by my aunt Marisol, who apparently believed persistence was a virtue rather than a symptom. The newest one had arrived that morning, and I'd done what I always did with them: put it with the others, unopened, in the oilskin pouch at the bottom of my bag, where it could quietly judge me.

So when the old man came staggering out of the dark toward my fire, my first thought — and I'm not proud of this — was *please don't let this be complicated.*

"Fire," he said. Just that. "Fire."

"You found one," I said.

He collapsed next to it anyway, which told me he hadn't been looking for fire so much as permission. He was sixty if he was a day, seventy if the sea had been hard on him, and it clearly had. His coat was salt-stiff canvas gone soft as cloth at the elbows, patched with tar and what looked like pieces of a different, better coat. His hands were a prospector's hands — knuckles swollen like galls on a branch, nails black-rimmed, a web of old coral cuts across both palms that had healed into white lace. He smelled of bilge and fever sweat and, underneath it, faintly, of lamp oil.

"Stew," I said, because it seemed rude not to offer, and because feeding strangers is cheaper than talking to them.

He ate like a man who'd forgotten food was optional, then remembered it wasn't. Between spoonfuls he shivered so hard the pot rattled against his knee, and I noticed his lips had a grayish cast that I didn't love. Fever. The bad kind, probably — the kind that comes from a cut on a reef or water held too long in a canteen, and doesn't negotiate.

"You're off the *Kestrel*," I said, nodding at the salvage barge anchored a mile up the channel. "Crew's been diving the shallows all week."

His eyes came up. They were pale, almost colorless, and very clear for a dying man's eyes. "You know boats."

"I know barges." I tied off a knot in the net. "I work the dock."

"Not dock work." He said it with strange certainty, the way drunk men say things. "Dock work is a woman who watches her hands. You watch the water. You've been watching the water since you sat down."

I didn't tell him he was wrong, because he wasn't. Watching the water is the closest thing I have to a prayer, and I do it more than I admit.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Paloma."

"Hollis." He said it like he was checking whether the name still fit. Then, without any transition at all: "How far to Cape Reckoning?"

The question landed strangely, the way a stone lands in still water — small splash, long ripples. Cape Reckoning was four hundred miles north, past the storm line, past the last honest chart, into the stretch of coast where maps go vague and ships go missing. Nobody asked about the Cape casually. People either fled from it or dreamed about it, and neither kind ended up shivering at a stranger's fire in Port Amara.

"Why would you want to know that?"

"Didn't say I wanted to know." A ghost of a smile moved through the fever-sweat on his face. "Said I wanted to know how far. Different thing."

"Four hundred miles," I said. "Five hundred by sea, if you hug the coast like a sensible person."

"And if you don't?"

"Then it depends on how much of you the Storm Gate leaves."

At the word *Storm Gate* something went through him — not a flinch exactly, more like a bell being struck somewhere inside his chest. "You know the Gate."

"Everyone knows the Gate. Knowing it and running it are different animals."

"Yes," he said softly. "They are."

I should have sent him up to the *Kestrel*'s camp then. They had a medic, such as she was, and a stove, and eight men who owed him whatever crew loyalty amounts to, which is usually nothing but is at least company. Instead I threw another stick on the fire and refilled his bowl, and I've spent enough nights since then taking that decision apart to know there was no good reason for it except this: he watched the water too, and lonely recognizes lonely the way two strays recognize each other across a yard.

We sat while the fire worked through its wood. The surf kept up its argument with the breakwater. Somewhere behind us the wrecking yard clinked and settled as the tide turned, all that rusted iron talking to itself in the dark.

"I was diving the Sable shoals," Hollis said eventually, as if continuing a conversation we'd been having for years. "Thirty years I've worked these waters. Found things. Lost things. Mostly lost things." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that went on long enough that I leaned forward and put a hand on his shoulder blade, and felt the heat coming off him like a stove door left open. "Last month I found something that wasn't mine to find."

"That happens out here."

"Not like this." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "I need you to hold something for me."

"I don't hold things for strangers."

"You will." He said it without arrogance, which somehow made it worse. "Because in an hour I'll be dead or close to it, and you'll be the only person on this coast I trust, and trust at that point isn't a feeling, girl. It's arithmetic."

"I'm not your arithmetic."

"No," he agreed. "You're worse. You're a woman sitting alone at a fire watching the water like it owes her something. That's a person with nowhere else to be. Nowhere-else-to-be people are the only reliable ones left."

I opened my mouth to tell him exactly where he could file his assessment, and that's when the shaking took him properly. Not shivering — convulsing, his jaw chattering hard enough that I heard his teeth click, his hands spasming against his knees. I got him flat on the sand, got the coat off him, got water into him between spasms. His skin was burning and clammy at once, which is the combination you don't want, and his eyes had rolled back to show white.

"Hollis. Hey. Stay with me."

"There's a pouch," he said, when he could speak again. Each word cost him visibly, like coins counted out of a nearly empty purse. "Inside my coat. Left side. Take it out. Take it *out*, girl, before anyone else comes looking for it."

I want to be able to say I hesitated for noble reasons. What I actually did was glance up the beach — empty, dark, the *Kestrel*'s lights a smudge a mile off — and then dig into his coat like a thief, because some part of me had already decided this story was going to involve me whether I liked it or not.

The pouch was oilskin, tied with waxed cord, and heavy in a way that wasn't coin-heavy. Inside was a bundle wrapped in sailcloth, and inside that, a map.

Not a printed chart. A hand-drawn map, on paper gone soft and yellow as old skin, folded and refolded so many times the creases had started to fray into lace. It was stained — grease, salt, something darker along one edge — and covered in ink lines fine as spiderwork: coastlines, soundings, a reef system rendered in obsessive detail, and at the center, beneath a headland drawn like a clenched fist, a shape I couldn't make sense of in the firelight. Angular. Deliberate. Like architecture, though no architecture I knew stood underwater.

And margins. The margins were crowded with annotations in a small, slanted hand — notes on tides, on currents, on depths, arrows pointing to features with labels like *approach at slack only* and *the arch has fallen since the survey.* Someone had loved this map. Someone had argued with it, corrected it, lived inside it.

"Who drew this?" I asked.

"Doesn't matter who drew it." Hollis had propped himself on one elbow, watching me with those colorless eyes, and there was something terrible in how steady they were. "Matters where it goes. Cape Reckoning. There's a place under the water there — you'll see it plain enough when you're standing over it. The map goes somewhere safe. That's all. That's the whole job."

"Safe from what? Safe *where*? Hollis—"

"Tell no one." His hand shot out and closed around my wrist with a strength that shocked me, fever or no fever. His grip was a ratchet. "Swear it. Tell no one. Not the harbormaster, not the magistrates, not your sweetest friend. Men have died for less than what's on this paper, and men are *dying now* for it, and the ones doing the killing wear clean coats and call themselves officials." He pulled me closer, close enough that I could feel his breath, hot and wrong-smelling. "Promise me, Paloma Pemberton."

I hadn't told him my surname.

I want that on the record, wherever records get kept. I never gave him my last name. My kit sits ten feet away with a bundle of unopened letters in it, every one addressed *P. Pemberton*, and I have turned that fact over so many times the edges have worn smooth, and I still don't know how he knew. Maybe he'd asked at the dock. Maybe the fever made him a mind-reader. Maybe some things you're not meant to understand until later, and some things never.

"Say it," he whispered. "Say you'll carry it to the Cape and tell no one."

Here is what I should have said: *I don't know you. I don't know what this is. I'm not getting dragged into whatever killed you.* Here is what I should have done: wrapped the map back up, set it on his chest, and walked to fetch the *Kestrel*'s medic, letting the world sort itself out without my fingerprints on it.

Instead I looked down at that dying man's face, lit orange by my fire, and I thought about promises — how few I'd ever kept, how the biggest one I'd ever broken was walking out on my father three years ago and never looking back, how a promise is just a debt you agree to feel later. And I thought, with the specific stupidity of the young and restless: *it's a piece of paper. How much trouble can a piece of paper be?*

"I promise," I said. "I'll carry it to the Cape. And I won't tell anyone."

Something went out of him when I said it — not the fever, unfortunately, but the tension, the purpose that had been holding his spine straight. He sagged back onto the sand like a puppet with its strings cut, and the smile that spread across his face was the worst thing I saw that night, worse than the convulsions. It was relief. Pure, finished, relieved-of-duty relief, the look of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long his shoulders had grown around it.

"Good girl," he murmured. "Knew you would. Saw you on the dock Tuesday. Watched you three days." His eyes were closing. "Pemberton. Had to be a Pemberton."

"Hollis — wait. What does that mean? Who else knows about this? Hollis!"

But he was sliding under, and by the time I'd gotten more water into him and propped him near the fire, he was past answering. I sat up with him the rest of the night, because what else was I going to do — sleep? I fed him sips of water. I mopped his forehead with a wet rag. I listened to his breathing change character hour by hour, from ragged to rattling to something thin and whistling, like wind through a cracked window, and I told myself each new sound was improvement because the alternative was admitting I was keeping vigil for a stranger whose whole life I knew as three sentences.

Somewhere past midnight, lucidity surfaced in him one last time, brief as a fish rising.

"The margins," he said, eyes still shut. "Read the margins. He wrote everything down. He always—" A breath. "He always wrote everything down."

"*He* who?"

No answer. The tide turned. The fire burned down to a red eye and I fed it, mechanically, one-handed, the other hand resting on the map in my lap. At some point I realized I'd been holding it the entire night, and that I'd moved my kit — with its judging bundle of letters — to sit on top of it, as if paper could guard paper.

Hollis died an hour before dawn, in that gray light when the sea goes the color of unpolished silver and even the gulls haven't decided to start yet. No drama to it. His breathing simply spaced itself further and further apart, like footsteps slowing, and then stopped, and the silence afterward was enormous. I checked for a pulse twice because I didn't believe it, and then a third time because I still didn't, and then I sat back on my heels in the cold sand and understood, all at once and completely, what I had done.

I had promised a dying man I'd carry his map four hundred miles up a coast that kills ships, to a cape that eats them, and tell no one — and I'd made the promise in under a minute, the way you'd promise to water someone's plants.

That's the thing nobody tells you about promises. They're weightless when you make them. They weigh nothing at all going in. It's only afterward, carrying them, that you discover what you agreed to lift.

I buried Hollis above the tideline where the sand was soft, digging with a wrecking-yard spade while the sun came up blood-orange over the breakwater. I marked the grave with a length of spar and his coil of rope, because a man should have something of his own over him. I went through his pockets first — a habit from the docks, practical and ugly — and found a tin of matches, a bent knife, and nothing else. Whatever else Hollis had been, he'd traveled light. Everything he had left in the world fit in a grave and an oilskin pouch, and the pouch was in my bag.

I told the *Kestrel*'s crew at midmorning. Their bosun, a gray-whiskered Eel Islander named Tabb, crossed himself with a fist instead of an open hand, the old way, and said Hollis had signed aboard six weeks back, quiet, kept to himself, paid cash. "Dived the Sable shoals on his own time, odd hours. We figured treasure-hunter." He shrugged. "Aren't they all?"

"Aren't we all," I said, and Tabb laughed, and I laughed, and both of us were lying.

That afternoon I sat on the dock with my feet hanging over black water and the map in my hands, open to the light for the first time. In daylight it was worse. Better and worse. The central shape resolved into unmistakable architecture — walls, courts, colonnades rendered in section, drowned and labeled with depth figures in that same slanted marginal hand. And the handwriting itself did something to me that I wasn't ready for, because I knew it. Of course I knew it. You spend seventeen years watching a man fill notebooks at the dinner table, annotating charts, arguing with other people's surveys in tiny furious script, and you don't forget the hand. Even after three years of not opening his letters, I knew it the way you know a voice through a wall.

My father had written in these margins. Years ago, by the fading of the ink. My father had held this map, studied this map, believed in this map — and the destination at its center, beneath that clenched-fist headland, was the place he'd chased his whole life and ruined our family chasing: the drowned temple of Cape Reckoning. The obsession that had taken him away from every dinner, every birthday, every year of my childhood, and finally, three years ago, taken me too — because I'd rather leave than keep waiting to matter more than a legend.

The prospector had known my name. He'd said *had to be a Pemberton.* He'd said *he always wrote everything down.*

I looked at the map. The map, grease-stained and fraying, looked back at me with my father's handwriting crawling all over it, and the tide slapped the pilings below my feet like slow applause, and somewhere behind me in the town a harbor bell rang the hour, indifferent as ever.

I had promised to take it to the Cape. I had promised to tell no one.

I had also, I realized with a sensation like stepping off a dock in the dark, absolutely no idea how I was going to get there — because the only boat I could afford was the one in my imagination, and the monsoon was six weeks out, and every pilot worth the name had already stopped answering questions about the northern coast.

Except, the dockhands said that evening, maybe one. Some disgraced charter man up the channel, banned from the legitimate runs, who'd take coin and ask nothing. A boat called *Mercy*.

It figured.
