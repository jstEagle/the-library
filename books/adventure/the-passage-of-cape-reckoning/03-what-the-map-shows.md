## 3. What the Map Shows

I didn't sleep. That's the thing nobody tells you about promises — they don't come with instructions, and they don't let you rest.

Hollis's body had been gone since before dawn. The harbor watch came for him with a canvas stretcher and the bored efficiency of men who collect the dead the way other men collect driftwood, and by seven I was standing on the dock at Port Amara watching gulls argue over something unspeakable, trying to remember exactly what I'd sworn to a dying stranger.

*Carry it to the Cape. Tell no one.*

I'd said yes because he was dying and because saying yes to dying men is cheap. It costs nothing in the moment. It only costs everything afterward, when you're alone with the thing they gave you and it starts to feel less like an object and more like a hand on your shoulder.

The map was rolled inside a length of oilcloth, tied with fishing line, and it had spent the night under my bedroll where I could feel it through the canvas like a second heartbeat. Now I sat cross-legged on the dock with my back against a bollard, my tin cup of harbor coffee going cold beside me, and untied the line.

The oilcloth smelled of kerosene and old sweat and, underneath that, something mineral — like the inside of a cave. I unrolled the map across my knees.

It was grease-stained, as promised. It was also beautiful, in the way that things made by obsessed hands are beautiful. Someone had drawn the coastline from memory or from long study, and they'd drawn it *well* — every headland and anchorage rendered in careful ink, soundings marked along the approaches in a neat, patient hand. Cape Reckoning sat at the northern edge of the parchment like a clenched fist, and around it the artist had sketched the reef lines in fine, nervous strokes, as if even drawing them from a distance required courage.

But it was the margins that stopped me.

There were annotations. Dozens of them. Tiny script crowding the edges of the map, climbing into the empty sea-space, filling the gaps between islands with questions and calculations and — this was the part that made my skin prickle — arguments. Whoever had annotated this map hadn't agreed with it. They'd *debated* it. In one corner, next to a cluster of reefs off the Cape's southern shoulder, someone had written: *Not a reef. Too regular. Check against tidal tables.* And beneath that, in slightly different ink: *Checked. Still too regular.*

I knew that handwriting.

You spend eighteen years of your life watching a man write, and you learn his letters the way you learn his face. My father wrote his lowercase e's like little locked doors. He crossed his t's with a single impatient slash that always overshot to the right, as if the letter itself were running late. He had a habit of writing *cf.* before everything, as though every observation needed a citation to be taken seriously, even observations he'd made himself.

*Cf. tidal tables. Cf. survey of 1187. Cf. the fisher songs — ask at Low Anchor.*

Low Anchor. He'd written *ask at Low Anchor*, underlined twice, and then, smaller, almost apologetic: *Marta will remember me.*

My coffee went cold in my hand and I didn't notice. The gulls went on screaming their argument over whatever it was, and the dock creaked beneath me with the tide, and I sat there reading my father's handwriting on a map given to me by a dead man, three years after I'd walked out on him without leaving so much as a forwarding address.

Here is what I knew about my father, Elias Pemberton: he was a man who loved the past more than the present, which is another way of saying he loved everything except the people actually in the room with him. He was brilliant and absent in equal measure. When my mother died, he grieved the way archaeologists grieve — carefully, methodically, cataloguing the loss, dusting it, labeling it, shelving it somewhere he wouldn't have to look at it every day. I was fourteen. I got shelved too.

And when I was twenty-two, after the third expedition he chose over my graduation, after the fourth grant application he discussed with more passion than he'd ever discussed my life, I walked out. I told him he loved dead civilizations more than living daughters, and I told him I hoped his temples kept him warm, and I left. Three years of coastal odd jobs since. Three years of measuring my life in miles instead of years, because miles you can count and years just accumulate.

His obsession — the one that had cost him appointments, marriages of convenience with wealthy patrons, and finally any institutional credibility he might have had left — was a drowned temple. Not metaphorical. An actual temple, actual drowned, somewhere off the northern cape, belonging to a civilization he insisted had existed and flourished and vanished without leaving a single respectable ruin above the waterline. Every conference he'd ever been laughed out of circled back to it eventually. *The Gift-Givers,* he called them. A culture, he claimed, that measured wealth not by what you kept but by what you gave away — whose kings were buried, if they were buried at all, in structures designed to be flooded, surrendered to the sea on purpose.

The academic world called it fantasy. My father called it the work of his life. And now here was its coastline, drawn in a dead prospector's careful hand, annotated in my father's impatient slashes, arguing with itself about reefs that were too regular.

I turned the map over with fingers that didn't feel entirely like mine.

The reverse held more. A sketch of the Cape itself, seen from seaward, with a collapsed sea arch marked at its base — and beneath the arch, a series of descending channels drawn in cross-section, like the throat of some enormous creature. At the bottom, a chamber. Hollis — or whoever had first drawn this — had labeled it in blocky capitals: *THE HOLLOW PLACE.* And beside it, in my father's hand, small enough that I had to hold the map close: *Cf. flood-channels. Deliberate? Cf. the gift economy — a treasury built to be given away. If the water rises, the wealth returns to the sea. Ask: who would build a vault that drowns itself?*

*Who would build a vault that drowns itself.*

I read it four times. Each time, the question landed somewhere new. First it landed as scholarship — the kind of elegant, half-mad hypothesis my father used to deliver across the dinner table while I pushed food around my plate. Then it landed as grief, because the handwriting was his and the man who wrote it had been missing for eleven months, and everyone who still bothered to ask had stopped expecting answers. Then it landed as fear, cold and specific, because if my father had found this place — if these annotations meant he'd stood where I was being asked to stand —

Then it landed as something I didn't have a name for yet. Something that felt like the past reaching up out of the water with both hands.

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape: a dying prospector gives me a map. The map leads to my father's obsession. The map carries my father's handwriting. There are no coincidences at this density. Either Hollis had known my father — worked with him, followed him, been part of whatever expedition had swallowed him whole — or Hollis had been given this map by someone who did. Either way, the thread ran through my hands now, and pulling on it meant pulling on everything I'd spent three years pretending wasn't attached to the other end.

"Bad news?" said a voice above me.

One of the dockhands — Tomas, who I'd worked salvage with off and on for two seasons — stood looking down at me, wiping his hands on a rag. I realized how I must look: sitting on the planks at dawn, hunched over a scrap of oilcloth like a fortune-teller, white-knuckled.

"It's old," I said. "That's all."

"Old maps mean wrecks," Tomas said, with the cheerful pragmatism of a man who thought of the ocean purely as a storage facility for things that had gone wrong. "Wrecks mean salvage. Salvage means pay." He squinted at the coastline drawing. "That the north cape?"

I rolled the map up so fast I nearly tore it. "Just junk."

He shrugged and wandered off, and I sat there feeling the lie sit in my stomach like ballast. It was the first lie I'd tell about this map, but I already understood, with the clarity that comes at dawn after a sleepless night, that it wouldn't be the last. Maps like this weren't junk. Maps like this were worth killing for — I'd seen men on this coast go to knives over a rumored cargo manifest. And I had just shown it, however briefly, to a dockhand on a public pier, in full daylight, three feet from a dead man's stretcher.

Tell no one, Hollis had said. I'd lasted four hours.

I tucked the map inside my jacket, against my ribs, where I could feel it with every breath. And that's when I noticed the last annotation — the one I'd missed, because it wasn't in the margins at all. It was written along the very edge of the parchment, vertical, cramped, as if whoever wrote it had run out of room and out of time:

*If you are reading this, P., then Hollis found you. I'm sorry for what that means. Don't trust the port. Come by water. Come quietly. And P. — I was wrong about what matters. Come find out why.*

*P.*

Paloma. He'd signed it with a single letter, the way he used to sign notes left on the kitchen table when I was small — *P., eat something, — Dad* — as if I were a colleague he was fond of rather than a child he was responsible for. Except this note wasn't fond. This note was afraid. I could feel the fear coming off the ink the way you feel cold coming off stone.

*Don't trust the port.*

I looked up. Port Amara spread out around me in its morning industry — cranes swinging, fish carts rattling, the magistrate's flag hanging limp and gray above the customs house. Ordinary. Sleepy. The same port it had been yesterday, when it was just the place where I happened to be broke.

Except now I knew my father had written *don't trust the port* on a treasure map, and a man had died delivering it to me, and somewhere in all that gray morning routine there might be eyes that had been watching Hollis watch me.

*I was wrong about what matters.*

Eleven months missing. Presumed dead by everyone except me, who had refused to presume anything because presuming meant grieving and I'd done enough grieving for one lifetime. And now, in eight cramped words on the edge of a dead man's map, my father had managed to say the one thing I'd waited three years to hear — and wrapped it in the one situation guaranteed to make hearing it dangerous.

I should have burned the map. I want that on the record, wherever records get kept. I stood there on the dock with the oilcloth in my hands and the harbor breathing around me, and I genuinely considered feeding it to the water, keeping the promise by destroying the promise, going back to odd jobs and miles. It would have been safer. It would have been sane.

But he'd said *come find out why*. And whatever else my father was — careless, obsessive, a man who shelved his daughter alongside his grief — he had never once lied to me about the work. His maps were true. His theories were honest, even when they were mad. If Elias Pemberton wrote *come find out why*, then there was a why, and it was waiting under the Cape, and no amount of sanity on my end was going to change that.

Besides. I owed a dead man a delivery.

I finished the cold coffee, folded the map back into its oilcloth, and stood up. The morning had warmed by a degree or two. Out past the breakwater, far to the north, the sky over the open water had gone a strange bruised color that the older fishermen call *monsoon weather* and the younger ones don't call anything, because they're busy doubling their mooring lines.

I had maybe two weeks before that weather closed the coast. Less, if the sky was lying generously.

What I didn't have was a boat, a crew, or a plan. What I had was a map annotated by a missing man, a promise to a dead one, and the growing suspicion that the port I was standing in was the last place I ought to be standing.

I started walking down the row of moored charters, looking for a pilot desperate enough to say yes.

* * *

I found him by his reputation before I found him by his face.

Every port has one — a pilot whose name comes up in dockside conversation the way bad weather does: frequently, reluctantly, and with a certain grim respect. Idris Vale. Banned from legitimate charters two years back over a sinking nobody would discuss in detail, working the gray edge of the trade ever since. The stories disagreed on the details but agreed on the shape: he'd lost a boat, lost passengers, lost his license, and somehow — this was the part everyone said with a slight frown, as if it didn't fit — never lost the respect of the actual sailors. The men who worked water trusted him. The men who worked paperwork didn't.

I found him at the far end of the mooring rows, aboard a boat that had seen better decades — a converted survey cutter with patched rails and a name painted on the stern in flaking gold: *Mercy*. He was doing engine work the way surgeons do surgery: sleeves rolled, forearms black to the elbow, swearing at the machinery in a low, conversational tone that suggested a long and complicated relationship.

"I need to go north," I said. "Up the coast. Past Sable Key."

He didn't look up. "Passenger rates are posted."

"I saw. I'm not asking for a passenger run. I need a charter. Quiet, flexible, and fast."

That got his attention. He straightened up slowly, wiped his hands on a rag that only redistributed the grease, and looked at me with the flat, unhurried assessment of a man who has learned that trouble announces itself honestly if you give it a moment. I gave it a moment. He took it.

"Quiet, flexible, and fast," he repeated. "That's three adjectives. Usually when somebody wants all three, there's a fourth word they're not saying."

"There's always a fourth word."

"What is it?"

"Mine," I said. "For now."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. He glanced past my shoulder, down the mooring rows, and I watched his eyes do a slow circuit of the docks behind me. Checking. I didn't know yet what he was checking for. I'd find out.

"Two hundred up front," he said. "Half again on delivery. You provide your own food. I provide the boat, the fuel, and the discretion." He paused. "Where exactly north?"

"Sable Key first. After that—" I hesitated, and he caught it, filed it away. "After that depends on what I find."

"Vague destinations cost extra."

"Then bill me."

He studied me a moment longer, and I had the distinct impression of being read like a document — quickly, thoroughly, and with more accuracy than I'd have liked. Whatever he concluded, he kept it behind his eyes.

"Slack tide tomorrow," he said, turning back to his engine. "Be here at first light. Don't be late, and don't be followed."

"I'll do my best on both counts."

"And lady—" I stopped halfway down the plank. He still hadn't fully turned around. "Whatever the fourth word is. When you decide to say it, say it early. Boats sink slower than secrets, but not by much."

I walked back up the dock with the map warm against my ribs and the bruised sky pressing down from the north, and I told myself the tightness in my chest was just the coffee, or the lack of sleep, or the ordinary fear of spending money I barely had.

It wasn't. It was the past, reaching out with cold fingers, checking my pulse.

And somewhere behind me, back along the mooring rows, I had the sudden, certain feeling of being watched — the way you feel a draft before you find the window. I didn't turn around. Turning around tells watchers that they've been seen, and I had a delivery to make, and eleven months of silence to answer for, and a father's handwriting burning a hole against my ribs.

*Come find out why.*

I was coming, Dad. God help us both.
