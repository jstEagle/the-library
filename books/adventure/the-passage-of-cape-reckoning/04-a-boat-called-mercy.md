## 4. A Boat Called Mercy

There are three kinds of trouble that walk down a harbor pier at dusk, and I've learned to tell them apart the way a doctor reads a fever. The first kind is drunk — loud, generous, forgettable by morning. The second is desperate — quiet, watchful, willing to pay too much. The third is dangerous, and it never looks like trouble at all. It looks like a woman in a salt-stiffened jacket counting coins on her palm like she's deciding whether she can afford to be here.

She stopped at the end of Pier Six where the *Mercy* sat tied up looking exactly like what she was: forty feet of patched teak and stubbornness, a fishing boat that had been everything else first. I was mending net in the wheelhouse doorway because mending net is what you do when you want to look busy and harmless, and I'd gotten very good at both.

"You're Vale," she said. Not a question.

"Depends who's asking."

"Paloma Pemberton." She said it like a name she'd been carrying too long, set down carefully so it wouldn't spill. "They say you'll take anyone anywhere if the coin's right."

"They say a lot of things about me." I set down the net. "Half of them are even true. Which one did you hear?"

"That you don't ask questions."

That one was true, mostly. It had been true for two years now, ever since the inquiry, since the name Idris Vale went from being something people said with respect at the pilots' guild to something they said quietly, the way you mention weather coming. *Vale? Oh. Ah.* A pause where respect used to live.

"Coin's right?" I asked.

She opened her hand. Silver, and more of it than a dock worker should have, which told me either she'd been saving for something or she'd come into it recently from someone who couldn't use it anymore. Both possibilities interested me. Neither was my business. That was the arrangement I had with myself these days: nothing was my business except the water, the boat, and getting paid.

"Up the coast," she said. "Past Sable Key. To Cape Reckoning."

I didn't let anything move on my face. Cape Reckoning. The Cape had a reputation the way drowned men have reputations — spoken of often, visited rarely, and then only by fools or the grieving. The reef there ate boats. The strait closed with the monsoon like a door slamming, and everyone on this coast knew the season was turning; you could smell it in the mornings now, that green metallic edge under the salt.

"That's a long run," I said. "Into bad water. In a closing season."

"I know."

"And you're going alone?"

"Yes."

Lies, all three of us were trading — her lying about why, me pretending I believed her, the harbor itself lying flat and pretty over the fact that half its pilots would sell your route to the magistrate's men before you cleared the breakwater. But her eyes went past my shoulder when she said *alone*, quick as a fish turning, and I filed that away.

* * *

Here's what I'll say about disgrace: it's cheaper than honor. Honor costs you every day — you have to keep paying it, keep choosing it, keep refusing the easy exits. Disgrace you pay once, up front, and then it just sits on you like ballast. You get used to the weight. You even get fond of it, some nights, because it explains things. Why the charters dried up. Why old friends cross the street. Why a man with twenty years of coastal water in his hands ends up running cargo nobody asks about for coin nobody counts.

The scandal — and I won't explain it, not yet, maybe not ever, though the shape of it will leak out of me eventually the way all truths do on small boats — the scandal wasn't mine. That's the part that eats at me. I steered the *Coriander* honest through a gale my employer's shortcuts had no business surviving, and when she went down off the shallows with four souls aboard, the company needed someone to blame who couldn't afford lawyers. They found me. Twenty years of clean seamanship weighed less than a director's signature. The inquiry took my license, the guild took my name, and the sea — the sea didn't take anything, because the sea doesn't care, and there's a strange comfort in that. The only judge who never lied to me was the water.

So now I buy back my honor the way a man pays off a debt in installments. Small jobs done straight. Cargo delivered without questions. Wrecks salvaged honestly, shares split fairly, promises kept even the ones made cheaply at midnight to strangers with silver in their palms. Every job is a coin dropped back into a well I can't see the bottom of. I don't expect to hear it land. I do it anyway.

Which is why, when Paloma Pemberton counted out the passage price without haggling — and a person who doesn't haggle is either rich or frightened, usually both — I said yes before I'd fully decided to.

"We leave at slack tide," I told her. "Dawn tomorrow. Be here early. If you're late, I go without you, and I keep the coin."

"I won't be late."

They always say that.

* * *

I saw the watchers an hour after she left, and I'm telling you this now because it matters to how the next few days went, and because a pilot who doesn't notice he's being watched isn't a pilot, he's cargo.

Two men, working the far side of the harbor basin like they had business there, which they didn't. One leaned against a bollard reading nothing. The other bought a meat skewer from a cart and ate it without tasting it, his eyes doing laps of Pier Six. They wore their coats wrong for the evening air — buttoned, purposeful — and they had the particular stillness of men being paid to wait rather than men waiting for pleasure.

I'd seen that stillness before. Two years ago, outside the inquiry hall, men with the same coat problem, same patient hunger. Magistrate Corvane's colors weren't on them, but Corvane's men never wore their colors when they were hunting; they wore them when they wanted you to know they'd already won.

So. A stranger with too much silver, bound for Cape Reckoning alone, watched by hired patience. I stood in my wheelhouse doorway and did the arithmetic every disgraced man does: walk away, or lean in.

Here's the thing about walking away. I'd been walking away for two years. Away from the guild, away from proper charters, away from every chance to stand in front of a room and say *this is what actually happened*. Walking away keeps you safe and it keeps you small, and I was tired of small. Tired of mending net in doorways. Tired of being a man whose word meant nothing because nobody asked him for it anymore.

A woman was going to sail into Corvane's reach with a purse full of silver and a lie about traveling alone, and whatever she was really doing at the Cape, it was worth watching and worth money and worth risk. All three of those together usually means one thing: it means the truth is nearby, wearing a disguise.

I went below and pulled out my charts. The run to the Cape was three days in fair weather, five in foul, impossible once the monsoon closed the strait. The monsoon was due within the week. So whoever watched her wasn't worried about her leaving. They were worried about where she'd stop along the way — and that meant they knew roughly what she was doing, which meant someone had talked, which meant her secret had a leak in it somewhere, and leaks sink faster than hulls.

I spent the evening checking the *Mercy* the way I check her before any serious run: bilge, fuel, water, the spare prop, the patch kit, the flare locker. She's an old girl, the *Mercy*, named by a previous owner who won her in a card game and swore she'd saved his life, and I kept the name because it seemed like the sort of thing a boat ought to live up to. Forty feet of former trawler with a rebuilt engine I'd traded six months of labor for, a hull that had kissed more reefs than I'd kissed women, and a wheel that answered my hands like it missed them.

By midnight I'd talked myself into it three times and out of it twice, and the fifth time I stayed talked-in, which is how decisions actually get made on the water. Not bravely. Accumulatively.

* * *

She came early. Of course she came early — the desperate ones always do, standing on the pier in the gray before dawn with their bag already packed, like arriving first might make the leaving safer.

I threw off the lines without ceremony. "Below or topside, your choice. Rules are simple: I steer, you stay clear of the lines, and if I say take the wheel, you take the wheel and hold her heading no matter what you see."

"What will I see?"

"If I knew that, I'd charge more."

She almost smiled. It was a good almost-smile, the kind that suggests a full one exists somewhere underneath, rationed. She went forward and settled against the rail with her bag between her knees like a shield, and I brought the *Mercy* out past the breakwater into a morning the color of unpolished pewter.

Port Amara fell away behind us — the cranes, the magistrate's white customs house, the hills with their shanty lights going out one by one as the day took over. I felt the usual loosening in my chest that open water brings, that feeling like a held breath finally released. Two years I'd been leaving that harbor. It never got old. Leaving is the one part of my life that still works perfectly.

We made good time through the morning. The swell was long and lazy out of the southeast, a pre-monsoon sea, the ocean clearing its throat. Paloma stayed forward mostly, watching the coast slide by, and I noticed things about her the way you notice things about passengers when you've got hours and nothing but horizon: she watched the water like she was reading it, not admiring it. Her hands were work-roughened but she held them still, folded, controlled. And every hour or so she'd touch the bag in her lap — not open it, just touch it, the way you'd touch a wound to confirm it's still there.

Around noon I brought her a cup of tea, because a pilot who feeds his passengers gets better answers than a pilot who interrogates them.

"Dive much?" I asked, casual as I could manage.

"Why?"

"Because you've got diver's shoulders and you flinch when the hull slaps a swell, like you're listening to what's underneath it." I handed her the cup. "Also you checked my anchor line twice without being asked. Salvage folk do that."

She looked at me for a long moment over the steam. Deciding, I could see, how much of herself to spend.

"My father was an archaeologist," she said finally. "I grew up around digs and dives. It doesn't mean I'm diving anything."

"Never said you were."

"Everyone wants to know what's in the bag."

"Everyone wants to know what's in everyone's bag. That's the whole trouble with people." I turned back for the wheelhouse. "I don't. Bag stays shut, that's fine by me. I'm paid for the miles, not the mystery."

And that was true, right up until mid-afternoon, when I spotted the sail.

* * *

It came up behind us out of the haze, a ketch-rigged salvage vessel running heavy and fast, and I knew her before I could read her name — I knew her lines, her trim, the way she carried too much canvas for the wind because her captain liked speed more than sense. *Sable's Daughter*. Oren Blackwood's boat. Blackwood ran salvage out of Port Amara, legitimate on paper, and everyone on the waterfront knew what legit meant when the paper was signed in the magistrate's office.

He wasn't following me. He was following her. Or following the route, or following the rumor, or simply sweeping the coast ahead of the monsoon the way Blackwood swept it every season, hoovering up wrecks before the storms scattered them. Any of those explanations was possible. None of them was comforting.

I altered course casually, a lazy ten degrees, as if making way for a tide line. Watched. Ten minutes. The ketch altered too.

I altered back. So did she.

Well, then.

I went forward and stood beside Paloma at the rail. "Don't turn around," I said quietly. "There's a salvage ketch astern that's taken an interest in our wake. Could be coincidence. Could be nothing. Could be Oren Blackwood, who does favors for Magistrate Corvane."

Her knuckles whitened on the rail. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Who's watching you, Pemberton?"

"No one."

"See, that's the second lie you've told me today, and I let the first one go because it was cheap. This one costs. Men don't burn coal chasing a woman carrying laundry."

The ketch grew behind us, patient, unhurried. Blackwood had the legs on us — heavier boat, more canvas — but I had the local water and I intended to use it. Ahead lay the shoal ground off Gullwing Point, a maze of sandbars and tide rips that no outsider would thread at speed, and I put the *Mercy*'s bow at it without slowing.

"Inside the bars," I said. "Hold on to something."

"What are you—"

"Hold on."

I took her through the Gullwing rips at full throttle, the *Mercy* slewing across tide lines, green water coming aboard once, twice, the shoals showing their teeth off either beam in long brown smiles. Behind us the ketch hesitated — I watched it in the mirror, watched Blackwood decide whether his keel and his employer's patience were worth the gamble — and then fell off, standing away, giving us the sea room we'd stolen.

For a while neither of us spoke. The engine thudded. My heart came down off the rigging slowly.

"He'll report it," Paloma said at last. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't. "Whoever sent him."

"Probably." I eased the throttle back to cruising. "So here's where we are, you and me. Somebody with power wants to know what you're carrying, badly enough to put a fast boat on the water. You're headed to the most dangerous anchorage on this coast during the week the sea closes for the season. And you've lied to me twice, which I'd respect more if it weren't going to get us both killed."

She stared at the water churning past the hull.

"I'm not asking what's in the bag," I said. "Not yet. But I am asking this much: is whatever you're doing worth dying for? Because I need to know whether I'm piloting a passenger or a fuse."

The question hung there in the spray and the engine noise. She looked at me then, really looked, and I saw her weighing something — not whether to trust me, but whether trusting anyone was survivable. I recognized the expression. I wore it for two years.

"It's worth more than that," she said finally. "But not to me. To someone else. I made a promise."

"A promise." I nodded slowly. Promises were currency I understood. "To whom?"

"A dead man," she said. "As of three days ago."

I looked at her a long moment, the wind pulling at her hair, the coast sliding past, the afternoon light going bronze. A dead man's errand. A purse of silver that wasn't hers. A map, probably — it was always a map, on this coast, in this trade; half the wrecks in these waters were sunk by maps, and the other half by the men who drew them.

"Well," I said, and turned back toward the wheelhouse. "Then I suppose we'd better not waste the trip."

She followed me in. "What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means," I said, settling my hands on the wheel, watching the darkening line of cloud building on the southern horizon like a wall being raised, "that a man who died trusting you with something important deserves better than a pilot who asks too many questions. So I'll steer. You keep your secrets. And when whatever's coming for us catches up — and it will, Miss Pemberton, because Blackwood knows these waters nearly as well as I do, and Corvane owns everything else — when it catches up, you and I are going to need each other more than we need the truth."

Ahead of us, the storm built. Behind us, the ketch's sails dipped below the horizon, gathering itself for another run. And between those two pressures, the *Mercy* pushed north with her patched hull and her borrowed name, carrying a disgraced pilot, a stranger with a dead man's promise, and whatever waited beneath the waters of Cape Reckoning — waiting, as it had waited for centuries, for someone foolish enough to come asking.

I only hoped, watching that storm wall rise, that whatever we found there would be worth the finding.

Somehow, I already suspected it wouldn't be.
