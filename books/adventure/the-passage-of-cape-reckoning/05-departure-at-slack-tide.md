## 5. Departure at Slack Tide

There is a particular hour in Port Amara when the town pretends to be honest. Just before dawn, when the fish sellers haven't yet arrived to argue over prices and the harbor master's office is still dark, the waterfront belongs to gulls and liars. Idris Vale and I were both of those things that morning, though only one of us was being paid for it.

"Slack tide's in forty minutes," he said, not looking up from the engine he was murdering with a wrench. "You want to tell me again why we're leaving before sunrise like a couple of smugglers?"

"Because I don't like crowds."

"You don't like crowds." He straightened up, wiped his hands on a rag that had given up being white sometime last year, and looked at me the way a man looks at a crate he suspects contains something other than what's written on the manifest. "Nobody pays passage to beat the crowds out of Port Amara. Port Amara doesn't *have* crowds before noon."

"It has them after," I said.

He let that sit between us for a moment, then went back to the engine. That was the thing about Idris Vale I was already learning: he collected questions instead of asking them. He'd stack them somewhere behind his eyes and bring them out later, one at a time, when you least wanted company for your own thoughts.

The *Mercy* was a coastal pilot boat gone slightly to seed — thirty feet of stubborn oak and patched steel, her name repainted so many times the letters stood up off the hull in little ridges, like scar tissue. She smelled of diesel and old rain and, faintly, of someone else's better years. I liked her immediately. Wrecks always recognize each other.

I spent the forty minutes doing what I do best, which is looking busy while my stomach chews on itself. I coiled lines that didn't need coiling. I checked the fresh water twice. I sat on the gunwale with the map rolled inside my jacket and tried not to think about how heavy a piece of oiled cloth could get when you'd promised a dying man you'd carry it three hundred miles north.

Hollis had died at dawn two days ago, curled against my cold fire pit like a man finally comfortable. Before that he'd gripped my wrist with a strength that surprised us both and made me swear — hand on the map, his clouded eyes fixed on mine — *carry it to the Cape, girl, tell no one what it shows.* I'd said yes because saying yes to dying men costs nothing at the time. It turns out the interest compounds fast.

*Tell no one.* Which meant, as of this morning, lying to the man whose boat I was borrowing, whose water I was drinking, whose life I might very soon need. Guilt makes a fine ballast, my father used to say — keeps the ship steady right up until it sinks her. Three years since I'd heard his voice and still his sayings lived in my head rent-free, sassing me at inconvenient hours.

"There." Idris nodded toward the harbor mouth without lifting his chin. "Company."

Two men had come down the quay road at a walk that was trying very hard to be casual. Big men. The kind of big that comes from lifting cargo or breaking things, and they had the look of men who'd been told to find somebody and hadn't been told why, which made them dangerous in the boring way. One wore a sash of deep green with a gold thread running through it — the magistrate's colors, Corvane's colors, the same sash I'd seen on the customs shed yesterday, the same sash that had suddenly developed an interest in strangers asking questions at the harbormaster's office.

I hadn't asked questions at the harbormaster's office. But Hollis had staggered through half of Port Amara's taverns in his last feverish days, waving that map and talking about temples, and towns like this have ears the way nets have holes.

"They looking for us?" Idris asked quietly.

"They're looking for somebody."

"That's usually the same thing by lunchtime."

He didn't panic. I'll give him that. He just finished seating the last fitting, closed the engine hatch, and said, "Get the stern line. We leave now, tide or no tide. Slack's a suggestion; the sea doesn't keep appointments any better than magistrates do."

We cast off with the light barely gray and the storm front stacked up on the southern horizon like a debt coming due — a long bruise-colored wall of cloud with lightning ticking inside it, patient as arithmetic. The wind ahead of it was already working, shoving little gusts down the harbor streets, rattling awnings, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

The two men in green reached the end of the quay just as the *Mercy* gathered way. One of them raised an arm — not waving, more like hailing, the gesture of a man accustomed to being obeyed at distances. The other put two fingers to his mouth and whistled, sharp, and somewhere back up the street a whistle answered.

"Don't look at them," Idris said. "Looking is answering."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. Everyone does. Keep your eyes on the channel like it owes you money."

A launch came off the moorings near the customs shed — a hard-chined patrol skiff with a green pennant snapping at its staff and an outboard that sounded healthier than anything had a right to sound at that hour. It swung out into the channel maybe four hundred yards ahead of us, then turned, idling, blocking the fairway like a hand laid flat across a table.

Idris exhaled through his nose. "Well. That's unfriendly."

"What are our options?"

"We could stop and chat. I'm told I'm charming when I'm being arrested." He eased the throttle forward a hair, watching the skiff, reading the water between us the way other men read faces. "Or we could go around the outside of the mole, where the swell's already building and their skipper would have to be either brave or stupid to follow."

"Which is he?"

"Ask me in ten minutes."

He put the wheel over and we ran for the gap between the breakwater's arm and the open sea, where the groundswell from the storm was already standing up in long gray shoulders. Behind us the whistle blew again, angrier, and the patrol skiff's engine note climbed — they'd made their choice, brave or stupid, and were coming to collect.

What followed lasted perhaps six minutes and aged me roughly a decade. Idris drove the *Mercy* like a man who knew every plank of her personally and trusted none of them individually. We took the swell quartering-on, buried the bow once in foam, shipped a little green water over the rail, and came out the far side of the mole into open water with the skiff falling astern, its skipper having discovered, as predicted, that courage and seaworthiness are different subscriptions. The last I saw of Port Amara that morning was the green pennant shrinking against the bruised sky, and the last thing I felt was my own heartbeat slowly agreeing to rejoin the rest of my body.

"Well," Idris said at last, easing off the throttle to a long easy pound-and-swing. "That was a cheerful start to a simple passenger run."

"I did warn you I don't like crowds."

"Mm." He handed me a thermos without ceremony. Coffee, black enough to stand a spoon in, tasting faintly of rust and decisions. "Here's what I know," he said, settling against the wheel. "A woman takes a berth north with no stated destination. She pays too much and haggles too little. At dawn she leaves ahead of a storm, pursued by men wearing Magistrate Corvane's colors, who apparently consider her worth a wet chase. And she watches that pennant go with the face of a woman doing sums she doesn't like." He took a sip of his own coffee. "So. Where exactly are we going, Miss Pemberton?"

And there it was. The question he'd been stacking behind his eyes since the dock, brought out at last, one at a time, when I least wanted company for my thoughts.

I had a lie ready. I'd built it carefully, the way you build anything in a hurry — a salvage survey, a wreck chart my father left, routine work, nothing worth a magistrate's attention. It was a good lie. It had load-bearing walls.

"Cape Reckoning," I said, which was true, and then, because truth is a slippery deck, I kept walking: "My father was a salvage man. Charted wrecks all along this coast. He left notes — sites nobody's worked. There's a freighter off the Cape he marked but never dove. I want to see if anything's left worth taking."

Every word of it was almost true, which is the best kind of lie, the kind that floats. My father *had* charted wrecks. He *had* left notes. He simply hadn't left those notes, and the site at the end of them wasn't a freighter, and the man himself hadn't been seen in three years by anyone who'd admit to it.

Idris watched me for a long moment. The wind pulled at his collar. Somewhere under the hull the sea went on having opinions.

"A wreck chart," he said.

"That's right."

"And Corvane's men chase passengers with wreck charts."

"Corvane's men chase anybody who looks like they're leaving in a hurry. You saw them. They didn't even follow past the mole." I shrugged, and I confess the shrug was good too, loose and bored, a shrug I'd practiced on harbormasters and landlords. "Maybe they like the exercise."

Another silence. Then, to my considerable relief, he laughed — short, dry, not entirely convinced, but filed away. "Fine. A wreck chart. Cape Reckoning's four days north in this weather, five if the monsoon gets ideas. There's a cay halfway called Sable Key where we can shelter and dive for whatever supplies we're short of, because I notice you boarded this vessel with one bag and no sense." He turned his face into the wind. "One condition."

"Name it."

"When you're ready to tell me the real version, don't dress it up as something else. I've had enough people lie to me well. I can't afford another one badly."

Something in my chest did a slow, uncomfortable roll, like ballast shifting. "It's a wreck chart," I said.

"Sure," said Idris Vale, and steered us north.

* * *

We ran north all day with the storm chewing at our heels. By afternoon the sky had gone the color of wet slate and the swells had organized themselves into something with intent. Idris handled the boat with a competence that was almost insolent — he barely seemed to steer, more to negotiate, trading small corrections with the sea like a man haggling in a market he knew stall by stall. Twice I caught myself watching his hands on the wheel and thinking that whoever had taken his license from him had taken it for reasons that had nothing to do with how he drove a boat.

I didn't ask. Fair's fair. He wasn't asking me.

Instead I went below, into the little cabin that smelled of diesel and canvas, and unwrapped the map where no one could see it. Lamplight, the floor pitching, Hollis's grease-stained coastline crawling under my thumb toward the Cape. And there, in the margins, in handwriting I would have known anywhere — even blindfolded, even dead — the annotations. Depth soundings in my father's cramped engineer's script. A note beside the reef line: *approach only at slack, spring low.* Beside the drowned arch: *the door is where the water goes in.*

Three years of silence, and here he was, talking to me in pencil.

I sat with that until the lamp swung shadows across the bulkhead and my eyes stung for reasons I attributed, firmly, to the diesel fumes. Then I rolled the map up, wrapped it back in oilcloth, and — because Idris's words had gotten under my skin like a splinter, *don't dress it up as something else* — I did the coward's compromise. I took my father's unopened letter, the one that had ridden in my kit for three years through every port and every bad night, and I slid the map inside it. Folded together. Sealed as one.

If anyone searched the *Mercy*, they'd find a letter. Old, private, worthless to anyone but me. Nobody robs a woman of her mail.

It was a clever hiding place. I want that recorded. It was genuinely clever.

It was also, I understood even as I smoothed the flap shut, the most dishonest thing I'd done in years — tucking the truth inside the one envelope I'd never had the nerve to open, binding my promise to a dead man onto the ghost of the man who'd drawn the margins. Two pieces of my past pressed flat together like flowers in a book, both of them waiting.

Above me, boots crossed the deck. I climbed the companionway into the rising wind.

Idris stood at the wheel with his chin tucked against the weather, and he didn't turn around, and he didn't ask what I'd been doing below for an hour. He just said, "Storm's going to make the strait ugly by tomorrow night. If we mean to reach the Cape before it closes, we'll want shelter and supplies by midday. Sable Key's on our line."

"Sable Key's fine," I said.

"And Pemberton." Now he glanced at me, one eye, salt spray drying white along his jaw. "Whatever's in that letter you keep touching like a wound — you don't have to tell me. But if it's the reason men in green sashes chase boats at dawn, I'd take it kindly if you told me before they catch up. Not after."

The wind took my answer, if I'd had one, and tore it into the rigging. Ahead, the coast ran north into the murk, and behind us the storm front leaned closer, and somewhere in my jacket a letter weighed more than paper had any right to weigh.

Guilt, my father would have said, makes excellent ballast.

Right up until it sinks you.
