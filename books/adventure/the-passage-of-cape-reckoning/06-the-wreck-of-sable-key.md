## 6. The Wreck of Sable Key

The storm we outran caught up with us anyway, the way debts do.

By midmorning of the second day the Mercy was running ahead of a gray wall of rain with the sea behind her standing up on its hind legs like a horse that had decided it was done being ridden. I'd been through worse weather in worse boats, but I'd been younger then, and stupid in ways I could afford. Now I was just stupid, which paid worse.

"We need to get off this water," I said, not looking at her. Paloma Pemberton had spent the last hour wedged in the companionway with her arms wrapped around her knees, watching the horizon like it owed her an apology. She hadn't been sick. I'll give her that. Most landfolk who talk a big game about the sea turn green by the second swell and spend the rest of the voyage decorating my deck. She just sat there, gray around the mouth, jaw set, taking it.

"I thought you were supposed to be a good pilot," she said.

"I am a good pilot. The sea is a bad sea. There's a cay about six miles north — Sable Key. Used to be a coaling station. Now it's a graveyard with good holding ground." I jerked my chin at the chart. "If we're going to get hammered, I'd rather get hammered at anchor."

She looked at the chart, then at me, and I watched her do the arithmetic — how much she trusted me, how much she had left to trust with. It came up short. It always comes up short, the first week.

"Anchor," she said finally. "Fine."

* * *

Sable Key was exactly as charming as I remembered, which is to say it looked like the sea had tried to eat it and given up halfway. A hook of black coral and guano-white rock, barely a mile long, littered with the bones of ships. There was a three-masted barque on the eastern shoal with her ribs sticking out of the water like the fingers of something that had drowned reaching for shore. Farther in, half a dozen smaller wrecks lay tumbled along the tideline, bleached and split, their names long since flaked away.

And in the anchorage itself, listing against a spit of sand with her stern half under, sat a freighter.

She was maybe a hundred and twenty feet of rusted iron, grounded at some point in the last twenty years and never pulled off. Her bow was high and dry enough to walk on; her stern flooded to the deck at high tide. Somebody had painted a name on her once — I could just make out the ghost of letters under the rust bloom: *CORMORANT*. Or maybe *CORMORANT'S END*, which would have been a pilot's idea of a joke.

"She's been picked over," Paloma said, standing at the rail with the wind flattening her hair against her skull.

"By beachcombers, maybe. Nobody dives out here." I killed the engine and let the anchor chain run. "Which means whatever's in her engine room — spares, fuel lines, a working bilge pump if the saints are generous — is still there. And I'd very much like a working bilge pump before we meet the back side of this storm."

"You think we'll need one?"

"I think a pilot who doesn't assume his boat will leak is a pilot who dies surprised." I was already pulling the dive gear out of the locker. "You dive?"

She hesitated a half-second too long. "Some."

"Some." I handed her a tank anyway. "Well. 'Some' is about what the visibility will be, so you'll fit right in."

* * *

The water around Sable Key was the color of strong tea, all the storm-churned silt of the coast rolled up in it, and the freighter came out of the murk like a whale surfacing — first a shadow, then a shape, then all at once an enormous rusted fact. I went down the anchor line and along her port side, and Paloma kept pace beside me, and I'll admit I watched her more than I watched the wreck for the first few minutes. Because "some," it turned out, meant something. Her kicks were even. Her buoyancy was clean. She checked her gauge without being told and she didn't grab at the wreck, didn't claw her way along the hull like a frightened tourist.

She moved like somebody who'd done this before, in a place where doing it badly got you killed.

I flashed her the okay sign. She returned it, and even through the mask I could see she was annoyed that I'd checked.

Fine. Fair. I'd have been annoyed too.

The stern section was flooded to the deckhouse, and we dropped through a cargo hatch into a cathedral of rust. Light came down in broken columns from the hatches above, and the silt hung in it like the inside of a pearl. I found the engine room by counting frames the way my father taught me — he'd been a wrecker before he was anything else, and he used to say a ship's bones tell you where her heart was — and there it was: two diesels gone to orange lace, but the storeroom aft still had its door half-shut, and inside, miracle of miracles, a boxed spare impeller, a coil of copper pipe, and — I actually laughed into my regulator — a bilge pump, still in its grease paper, the crate stenciled *SPARE — DO NOT REMOVE*.

"Sorry, Cormorant," I said to nobody. "Removing."

That was when I saw the other crate.

It was newer. That was the thing that stopped me. Everything else down there wore twenty years of rust like a uniform, but this crate was pine, pale, its edges still sharp, its stenciling crisp: a shipping mark I didn't recognize, a port code for Port Amara, and a date — three weeks old. And it wasn't alone. There were drag marks in the silt leading from the crate to the hatch, fresh ones, and tied off to a stanchion near the hatch was a length of new polypropylene line with a lift bag, half-deflated, still dripping when I touched it.

Somebody had been down here recently. Recently enough that they'd be back.

I looked up and Paloma was hovering at the storeroom door, and I watched her see the same things I'd seen, in the same order, and I watched her face go carefully, deliberately blank — the blankness of a person deciding very fast what to do with information.

She was good at that. Too good.

I pointed at the pump. She nodded. We took what we'd come for and left the pine crate where it sat, and neither of us said a word about it, because you don't talk underwater, and because we were both, I think, deciding whether the other one was going to mention it first.

* * *

The storm front hit Sable Key an hour before dark, and we spent the evening in the Mercy's cabin with the rain hammering the deck overhead like a crowd that wanted in. I got the new pump installed by lamplight while Paloma cooked something out of cans that was, by the standards of boat food, nearly a cuisine. We ate in a silence that wasn't comfortable but wasn't hostile either. It was the silence of two people circling the same question.

She circled it first. "The crate," she said.

"The crate," I agreed.

"You saw the date on it."

"I saw the date. Three weeks. And a lift bag, and fresh line, and drag marks in the silt." I set down my fork. "Somebody's working these waters, Pemberton. Working them properly, with proper gear, out of a proper port. And they're not after the Cormorant — there's nothing in her worth a dive crew's insurance premium. So they're using her as a base. A cache. A place to stage from."

"Stage for what?"

"That's the question, isn't it." I leaned back against the bulkhead. "Here's what I know. Somebody's been diving the wrecks along this stretch — not salvaging, *surveying*. I've seen their markers. Little brass pins driven into the coral on three of the cays we passed yesterday, each one with a number stamped on it. That's not treasure hunting. That's charting. Somebody is making a map."

I watched her hands. People's hands tell you more than their faces, if you're patient. Hers went still — not relaxed, still. Held.

"Maybe they're archaeologists," she said.

"Maybe. Archaeologists file surveys with the coastal authority. These markers aren't filed. I checked before we left Amara — the survey register's public, it's how I used to pick up chart work." I turned my cup slowly on the table. "So either they're archaeologists who don't want anyone knowing where they're looking, or they're not archaeologists. And in my experience, the second one pays better and ends worse."

"And which are we?"

The question landed harder than she probably meant it to. I let it sit for a moment, because I deserved it, honestly. A disgraced pilot and a woman with a map she won't show me, running up the coast ahead of a storm and pretending we're just two honest people going somewhere honest.

"We're the honest version," I said. "God help us."

She almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost — a crack in the careful blankness — and I found I'd been waiting for it without knowing.

"You could just tell me what's on that map," I said. "You know. As one honest person to another."

"I could," she said. "And you could tell me what you did to get banned from charter work. As one honest person to another."

Touché. The rain filled the pause for us, drumming on the deck, running in ropes down the ports.

"Fair," I said. "Not tonight, though. Tonight I'm tired, and the story's not a short one, and if I tell it badly it'll sound worse than it is."

"Is it bad?"

"It's a story where I was the only one who survived to be blamed for it. That's most of what you need to know." I looked at her across the lamplight. "Your turn. Is yours bad?"

She was quiet a long moment. Outside, the wind came around and the Mercy tugged at her anchor, restless, like she could smell the open water and didn't like being tied to a graveyard.

"It's a story about my father," she said finally. "That's most of what you need to know."

And there it was — the first true thing either of us had said since Port Amara, offered not as payment but as a down payment. I understood the currency. I'd been trading in it myself for three years: fragments, carefully rationed, just enough honesty to keep the other person from walking.

But sitting there in the lamplight with the storm doing its best to tear the roof off, I found I didn't want fragments from her. Which was a dangerous way to feel about a woman I'd known four days.

"Sable Key," I said instead, because the safe thing and the true thing weren't the same and I was out of practice with the true ones. "You know why it's called that? The old coaling station men used to say the island was sable because of the black coral. It wasn't. It's called Sable because the first survey ship that charted it, the *Sable*, wrecked on the approach, and the cartographers had a sense of humor." I nodded at the dark ports, toward the barque's ribs standing out of the shoal. "Half the wrecks out there are ships that came to look at other wrecks."

"That's grim."

"That's the coast." I banked the lamp down to a coal. "Get some sleep, Pemberton. Tomorrow we run the inside passage while the weather holds, and after that it's two days of open water to the Cape, and I want us both sharp for it."

She lay down in the forward berth, and I took the pilot seat by the window out of habit, and for a long time I watched the rain and listened to her breathing even out, and I thought about pine crates and brass pins and a woman whose hands went still when I said the word *map*.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about honor: it doesn't come back all at once. It comes back in installments, the way a debt gets paid. A good anchorage in bad weather. A spare pump pulled from a dead ship's heart. Keeping your word when keeping it is expensive. I'd been making payments for three years, small ones, to an account nobody was keeping but me.

But tonight, watching her sleep and knowing she was lying to me about something that mattered, I made a bigger payment than usual. Because I'd decided — somewhere between the drag marks in the silt and the almost-smile in the lamplight — that whatever she was running from, or toward, she wasn't running *at* anybody. And a man who's lost everything can afford to bet on that.

I was almost asleep when she spoke from the dark, so quiet I might have dreamed it.

"Idris. The markers you saw. The numbers — were any of them under thirty?"

I sat up. "How did you know there were numbers?"

A long silence. The rain. The anchor chain creaking.

"Because I've seen one before," Paloma said. "Three weeks ago. On a crate being loaded onto a salvage ship in Port Amara. The ship was called the *Vulture's Debt*." A pause. "And the man signing for it was named Blackwood."

I stared into the dark toward her berth, and every honest instinct I had left stood up at once and started shouting, because I knew that name. Everybody on this coast knew that name. Oren Blackwood didn't survey wrecks. He stripped them — and lately, the rumor in every port bar from Amara to the Cape was that Blackwood had new backing. Deep pockets. Port authority pockets.

Somebody was mapping my coast, and they were doing it with the magistrate's money.

"Get some sleep," I said again, and this time neither of us believed it.
