## 7. The Magistrate's Men

The ship came out of the haze an hour after dawn, and I knew she was trouble before I could read her name.

Trouble has a silhouette. It's the way a vessel sits in the water — too much freeboard for a working hull, too little rust on her rails, a wake that comes at you straight and unhurried because whoever's steering has never once had to run from anything. The Mercy, by contrast, sits like a dog that's been kicked by several different owners and has opinions about all of them. I'd learned to read ships the way other people read faces, and this one was smiling the way customs officers smile.

"Company," I said.

Idris was already at the wheel, squinting into the glare off the water. He didn't ask how I knew. He'd been watching the same horizon I had, and I was starting to understand that Idris Vale processed bad news about four seconds faster than most people, which probably explained both his survival and his disgrace.

"Corvane's colors," he said. "Blue pennant, gold device. That's not a patrol boat. That's somebody's private idea of law."

"How long?"

"Twenty minutes if they're polite. Ten if they're not." He glanced at me, then at the deck where my kit sat open beside the coil of line I'd been pretending to mend since sunrise. "Whatever you're carrying that you lied to me about — now would be the time to have a plan for it."

I want it noted, for the record of history or whatever future reader might be keeping score, that my first instinct was to lie again. It rose in me smooth and ready, the way a good liar's lies always do: *It's nothing, it's papers, it's a grocery list.* Three years of drifting teaches you that the truth is heavy cargo and nobody pays freight on it.

But Idris had taken me aboard when every legitimate pilot in Port Amara had turned me away, and he hadn't asked questions I could see were costing him. And there was something else — something about the way he said *whatever you're carrying* without reaching for it himself. A man with less honor would have simply grabbed it. A man with more sense would have thrown it overboard.

"The map," I said. "Hollis's map. It leads somewhere my father spent his life looking for."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise exactly. Recalculation. "And Corvane wants it."

"I don't know what Corvane wants. But men don't chase a stranger up two hundred miles of coast over nothing."

"No," Idris said. "They don't." He turned back to the wheel and began, very calmly, to do nothing useful whatsoever. "Then you've got twenty minutes to make it disappear."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about terror: it sharpens. Everyone talks about the shaking hands and the dry mouth, and yes, fine, those come too, but underneath all that the mind goes strange and clear, like water over glass. Every object on the Mercy's deck suddenly revealed its true nature. The bilge pump became a hiding place. The flour tin became a decoy. My father's letter — three years unopened, riding in my kit through every port and storm like a stone I couldn't bring myself to throw away — became, of all things, armor.

I went below while Idris slowed us to a drift that looked convincingly idle. The cabin smelled of diesel and old canvas and the particular staleness of a man who lives alone with his regrets. I pulled out the letter.

It was thicker than I remembered. That sounds like a small thing, but it stopped me cold for a second — the weight of it, the way the paper had swelled slightly with damp years, as if the words inside had been breathing. My father's handwriting on the front, my name in those careful archaeologist's capitals: PALOMA. As if I were a find. An artifact. *Pemberton, P., undated, coastal region, condition uncertain.*

I hadn't opened it in three years. I wasn't going to open it now, with ten minutes left and a magistrate's boarding party closing in. But I held it, and I thought about Hollis dying at my fire with his hand around my wrist, and I thought about the map — grease-stained, soft as cloth from handling, covered in my father's marginal handwriting that I still hadn't told Idris about — and I did the arithmetic of concealment.

The map was roughly letter-sized. Folded, it was maybe half an inch thick. The letter was a packet, not a page — my father had never been capable of writing anything short; even his field reports ran to forty pages and three appendices. If I folded the map along its existing creases, wetted the back edge just enough to make it cling—

No. Wetting it risked the ink. Think. Think like someone smarter than you are, which in moments like this is the only kind of thinking available.

I unfolded the map once, flat against the chart table, and looked at it properly for the first time since the fire. The coastline of Cape Reckoning ran down its right side in Hollis's shaky hand. And there, in the margins, in a script so familiar it hurt — tight, slanted, annotated with survey numbers and depth soundings — my father's notes. Dated. Eleven years old. He had held this map before Hollis ever gave it to me. Which meant Hollis had known him. Which meant the dying prospector hadn't chosen me at random, or if he had, chance had a crueler sense of humor than I'd credited.

There was no time for the feelings this produced. There is never time for feelings when men with guns are approaching; that's their great tactical advantage.

I refolded the map along its creases, slid it inside the letter's fold — the envelope was actually a folded wrapper, sealed with a blob of wax my father had apparently stamped with some symbol I'd never bothered to examine — and pressed the whole thing flat under the chart weights. Then I took the letter and the two books I owned and stacked them in the small shelf above the bunk, spine out, ordinary as laundry. Then I made myself stop touching them, because the surest way to mark a hiding place is to keep checking it.

Up top, Idris had arranged us beautifully. The Mercy lay ahull, sails slack, looking precisely like what we were supposed to be: a broke pilot running light cargo up the coast ahead of weather. He'd even dug out a manifest — blank forms he kept for exactly this purpose, which told me something about how often he'd been boarded before — and was filling it out in his blocky, unapologetic hand.

"What are we carrying?" I asked.

"Salt fish, rope, and a broken windlass I'm transporting for repair, allegedly." He didn't look up. "Boring is armor. Be boring."

"I'm not good at boring."

"Noted. Try anyway."

The other ship came alongside with a competence that annoyed me — no fenders dropped late, no shouted confusion, lines across our rail before I'd finished counting her crew. Eight men. Two of them carrying the particular short-barreled confidence of hired muscle, the rest dressed in the gray-and-blue of the port authority, which in Port Amara's jurisdiction meant they answered to the man whose pennant flew at their masthead.

The man who came over the rail last was not what I expected, and I've learned to distrust that feeling, because expectation is usually the trap you set for yourself. I'd imagined Corvane's men as thugs. This one was broad without being heavy, silver at the temples, with a face like a cliff that had decided to be friendly about it. His coat was better than his crew's and worse than his ship, which meant he understood exactly how much wealth to display and to whom.

"Oren Blackwood," he said, and offered Idris a hand. "Salvage master, licensed out of Port Amara. Riding with Magistrate Corvane's commission. Routine inspection — weather's coming, and we're checking that every hull between here and the Cape is sound and accounted for."

"Idris Vale." They shook. I watched Blackwood's face do a small, satisfied thing at the name, and I understood that the name was the point. Idris Vale was known. Idris Vale was flagged. We hadn't been stopped at random any more than Hollis had picked me at random. The coast was smaller than it looked.

"Vale." Blackwood let the syllable sit there like bait on a hook. "You're a long way from your charter waters."

"Fish don't respect boundaries."

"No," Blackwood agreed pleasantly. "They certainly don't." He turned to me, and his eyes did the thing that experienced searchers' eyes do — a quick inventory, hat to boots, cataloguing weight and bulk and where a person's hands wanted to go. "And your passenger?"

"Crew," Idris said.

"Deckhand," I said, and touched my rope calluses toward him like credentials. "Paloma Marsh."

Blackwood smiled at the lie the way you smile at a child's drawing of a horse — charmed by the effort, unmoved by the accuracy. "Well, Deckhand Marsh. We'll be quick. Storm front's building behind us and I'd as soon be through it tied to a dock." He snapped his fingers at his men. "Two below, two on deck, rest of you keep to your own boards. Standard sweep."

Standard sweep. I stood at the rail with my hands loose at my sides and my heart going like a drum in a parade, and I watched four strangers move through the only home I currently had, looking for the thing that would get us both hanged or drowned or worse — because I was beginning to suspect, from the cut of Blackwood's coat and the patience of his smile, that whatever Corvane wanted from that map, he preferred his witnesses processed rather than merely silenced.

One of them found my kit within ninety seconds. Of course he did; it was sitting in plain sight because Idris had advised me that the boldest hiding spot reads as innocence, and I'd believed him right up until a man with forearms like mooring lines started lifting my spare shirt out of it with two fingers, the way you'd handle evidence.

He went through everything. He shook the shirts. He unrolled the oilskin. He weighed my knife in his palm and looked at me, and I looked back with the flat boredom of a woman who has been searched before by less polite people, which was even true. He set the kit down. Moved on.

Below, I heard drawers opening. I heard Idris saying, cheerfully, "Careful, that windlass part's worth more than your boat," and someone answering, "It's our boat now if the manifest doesn't match," and Idris laughing like a man with nothing in the world to hide, and I marveled at him. Terror had gone through me like a keel through kelp. Terror seemed to have gone through Idris and come out the far side as performance.

Then one of them came up the companionway holding my books.

My stomach performed a maneuver I felt in my teeth. Two books and a letter, carried in his hands like a tray of drinks, and Blackwood turning from the rail to look at them, and time going thick as cold tar.

"Reader," Blackwood said, to me.

"When the work allows."

He took the top book — a battered collection of coastal charts and sailing directions, genuinely mine, genuinely boring — and thumbed it. Set it down. Took the second, a novel with a broken spine I'd traded a night's watch for in Port Amara. Fanned it. Nothing fluttered out, because I had put nothing in it, because I was not stupid, because terror had sharpened me into someone briefly cleverer than myself.

Then he picked up the letter.

I want to say I breathed normally. What I actually did was something adjacent to breathing that I hope passed for it at six feet. My father's handwriting faced the sky. *PALOMA.* Blackwood turned it over. The wax seal caught the light — unbroken, thank every god I didn't believe in, unbroken — and he held it up and studied the stamp in it, and something flickered across his cliff of a face.

"This seal," he said. "You know whose mark that is?"

"It came to me in the post," I said. "From a man I don't intend to write back to."

"Mm." Blackwood weighed it in his hand. I could see him deciding things. I could see the machinery behind the friendly face running its quiet arithmetic: a woman alone, a disgraced pilot, a letter from — and here I watched him arrive at it, watched the conclusion land behind his eyes like a diver hitting water — *a Pemberton*. The name would mean something to him. It would mean something to anyone Corvane had briefed, because a man who has spent years hunting another man's obsession learns to recognize the obsession's next of kin.

"Private correspondence," Blackwood said slowly, "is protected under maritime custom."

"So it is," I said.

"And yet." He turned the letter once more, and I understood with terrible clarity that he was going to open it, custom or no custom, and that the only question was whether he'd do it politely or otherwise, and that behind the wax seal, folded flat against three years of my father's unread words, lay the map that would deliver everything Corvane wanted directly into his ringed and patient hands.

Idris saved it. Or luck did, wearing Idris's face.

"Captain Blackwood," Idris called from the helm, mild as morning. "Your lookout's signaling. Squall line's come forward — it'll be on us inside the hour, and your ship draws too much to beat east of the shoals. You want my advice, you'll conclude your inspection and run for Low Anchor anchorage while the running's good."

Blackwood didn't move for a long moment. The letter hung in his hand. The wind had begun to carry the smell of rain.

Then, with the discipline of a man who had survived this long by knowing which appetites to defer, he set the letter back on the shelf. Spine out. Ordinary as laundry. He patted it once — a gesture I will feel on my skin for the rest of my life, the pat of a man marking a debt he intended to collect.

"Everything appears in order," he said. "Salt fish, rope, a broken windlass, a silent pilot, and a deckhand who reads her father's mail." He stepped to the rail, then paused, and gave me the smile again, the customs-officer smile, the one that isn't a smile at all. "Safe passage, Miss Pemberton."

He used the name deliberately. He let it fall between us like a coin he knew I'd have to pick up.

They crossed back to their ship in under a minute, cast off clean, and turned east toward Low Anchor with the unhurried confidence of hunters who had already decided the quarry wasn't going anywhere. Idris brought the Mercy's head around north, laid the sails on, and didn't say a word until the blue pennant was a smudge against the building clouds.

"He knows who you are."

"He knows who my father is. It's not the same thing." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. Inside, I was still standing on that deck watching a stranger hold three years of silence in his hand.

"It's close enough to be dangerous." Idris looked at me, and there was no anger in it, which was somehow worse. "That seal. He recognized it. Which means Corvane's been hunting my father's trail longer than I've been alive to notice it, and I led us straight into his net because I trusted a stranger with a story full of holes."

"You did trust a stranger with a story full of holes," I said. "And she's about to fix that."

I went below and came back up with the letter and the map, and I laid them both on the chart table where the wind could worry their edges, and I watched Idris look from my father's handwriting on the margins to my face and back again, doing his own arithmetic, four seconds faster than grief.

"Start talking," he said. "All of it this time. And then explain to me why a man smart enough to hide a map inside a letter waited three years to open the letter."

"That," I said, as the first drops of the squall hit the deck like gravel thrown by an angry god, "is going to take longer than the weather allows."

Behind us, low and black and patient, the monsoon began to eat the horizon.
