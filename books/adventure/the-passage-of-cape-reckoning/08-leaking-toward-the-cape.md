## 8. Leaking Toward the Cape

The Mercy told me she was hurt before I ever saw the water.

Forty years of boats will teach you their language if you're willing to listen, and a boat in pain speaks plainly — a new note in the engine's drone, a shiver through the deck plates that isn't the sea's doing. I was at the wheel with the dawn still an hour off and the horizon a bruise-colored rumor when I felt it: a softness in her stride, like a woman favoring one leg.

I cut the throttle and listened. Beneath the diesel's grumble, faint but unmistakable, came the sound no captain wants to know by name — water where water shouldn't be, sloshing in the bilge with a rhythm too cheerful for anyone's good.

"Well," I said to the dark, "that's not ideal."

The Pemberton girl appeared at the cabin door before I'd finished saying it. She had a way of materializing when things went wrong that I was beginning to suspect wasn't coincidence so much as instinct — the same instinct that makes sharks find blood. Her hair was flattened on one side from sleeping against the chart table, and she looked, in the gray half-light, young enough to worry me and old enough to pretend she didn't.

"What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong," I said. "Something's *leaking*. Wrong implies intent."

She came up beside me and put her palm flat on the deck, the way dockhands do, reading vibration like braille. "How bad?"

"That depends entirely on how honest you want me to be."

"Honest."

"Bad." I killed the running lights and let my eyes adjust to the dark. Behind us, far back along our wake, the sky had begun to organize itself into something with opinions. The monsoon front had been building for two days — a wall of cloud stacked up like bad debt, purple-black at its base, occasionally flickering from within as though something enormous were turning over in its sleep. We were running ahead of it because running ahead of it was the only option that didn't involve being inside it. "We've got water coming in somewhere aft of the keel bolt. Could be a seam let go, could be we kissed something in the dark last night. Either way, she's taking on faster than she likes."

"How fast is that?"

"Faster than nothing. Slower than panic. For now."

Paloma didn't say anything for a moment. Then she rolled up her sleeves, which I had learned was her version of a battle standard going up a flagpole.

"Bilge pump?" she asked.

"Aft locker. There's a hand pump too — the electric one has moods. You work the handle, I'll find the hole."

She went aft without another word, and I'll confess something here that I wouldn't say aloud on any dock between Port Amara and the Cape: watching her go, sleeves rolled, jaw set, I felt something I hadn't felt in three years of solo runs and cheap cargo and sleeping aboard because sleeping ashore meant questions. I felt like part of a crew again. It lasted about four seconds, which was long enough to be dangerous.

* * *

Finding a leak in a hull at night is a particular kind of misery. It's hide-and-seek played with a partner who's actively trying to drown you. I got down into the bilge with a flashlight clenched in my teeth and started working forward from the stern, feeling along the frames the way you'd check ribs for breaks.

The Mercy is thirty-two feet of teak and stubbornness, built the year I was born by a boatwright in Sable Key who believed a hull should be able to take a hit and forgive it. She'd forgiven plenty over the years. Apparently her patience had limits, and last night — somewhere in the long black stretch past the shoals, when I'd been running on coffee and confidence — we'd found them.

There. Aft of the fuel tank, low on the starboard side, a seam had opened maybe eight inches, weeping seawater through caulking that had finally decided retirement sounded nice. Not gushing. Insisting.

"Pemberton!" I shouted up through the deck plate. "How's our guest?"

"Waist-deep in the bilge well and rising slow!"

"That's the spirit. Kill the pump a minute — I need it dry to work!"

I hauled myself out, dripping, and started pulling together what a man needs to stop the ocean from moving into his house: a length of canvas, a wooden wedge, the mallet, a tin of marine epoxy that was older than my last honest paycheck, and a rag I'd been meaning to wash since Port Amara. Paloma crouched beside me at the hatch, breathing hard, her hands slick with bilgewater.

"Talk to me," she said.

"Seam opened up, starboard quarter. Caulking gave out. It's fixable — temporary fixable, anyway. I wedge it, pack it, epoxy over it, and she holds until we can careen her somewhere with a beach and fewer waves."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

I looked at her. In the light of the work lamp her face was steady, and I realized she genuinely wanted the answer, not comfort. There are two kinds of passengers in this world — the kind who ask how bad it is hoping you'll lie, and the kind who ask because they intend to help. I'd spent three years carrying the first kind.

"Then we get very friendly with the radio," I said, "and hope someone answers before the monsoon does. But it'll hold. The Mercy's held worse than me patching her."

"You talk about her like she's a person."

"She's better company than most people I've known." I wedged the canvas into the gap, drove the taper home with three hard strikes, and packed the seam tight. Water slowed to a sullen trickle. "Hand me the epoxy."

She did, and then — without being asked, without making a ceremony of it — she took the second brush and started working the compound alongside me, matching my strokes, learning the rhythm in about thirty seconds flat. We worked in the lamplight with the boat rolling under us and the storm stacking up behind us like a creditor, and neither of us said anything for a long while, because there are moments when talking would just be noise getting in the way of the actual conversation, which was happening in elbows and timing and trust.

When the patch was done — ugly as sin, solid as a promise — we sat back on the deck plates, soaked to the elbows, and shared the last of the coffee from the thermos. It had gone bitter hours ago. Neither of us mentioned it.

* * *

The trouble with nights like that one is they make people honest. There's something about bailing seawater alongside another human being that strips away the usual paint. By the time the sky went from bruise to ash, we were sitting in the cockpit with the autopilot holding a course I didn't fully trust, and Paloma asked the question I'd been waiting three days for her to ask.

"Why did they ban you?"

I should have deflected. I'm good at deflecting; it's practically a trade skill. Instead I watched the gray line of the horizon and heard myself answer.

"There was a sinking," I said. "Four years ago. A charter out of Port Amara — the *Cormorant Queen*, forty feet, steel-hulled, owned by a company called Meridian Marine. I was pilot."

"I heard the name," she said quietly. "Everyone heard the name."

"Eleven souls aboard. Tourists, mostly — a wedding party, if you can believe it. We went down in weather half as bad as what's chasing us now. Lost the hull integrity off the Sable shallows and went down by the bow in ninety seconds." I turned the empty cup in my hands. "Nobody died. Cold water, close to shore, fishing boats out that night. Every soul pulled off. But a boat's a boat, and a pilot's a pilot, and somebody has to be guilty."

"Was it you?"

"The inquiry said yes. The inquiry was paid for by Meridian." I laughed, and it came out with edges on it. "Here's the truth, Pemberton, and you can believe it or file it with the rest of what you don't believe about me. That hull had rust in her frames you could put your fist through. I flagged it twice in writing. Twice. My employer signed off on both inspections — his inspector, his paperwork, his signature. When she went down, those papers grew legs. Walked right out of the record. And Idris Vale, disgraced pilot, was standing closest to the fire, so he got handed the whole thing to hold."

Paloma was quiet for a while. The wind had come around and was starting to carry the smell of the storm — that green, metallic smell like a struck bell.

"So why won't you say it? Why let everyone think—"

"Because saying it costs money I don't have, and lawyers I can't afford, and witnesses who've all gone suspiciously deaf. Because 'the pilot sank the boat' is a story people already bought, and nobody refunds a story." I shrugged, and it cost more than a shrug usually does. "So I run cargo nobody respectable wants carried, and I keep the Mercy floating, and every once in a while somebody like Corvane's people looks at me like I'm a tool they might use, and I say no, and that's my whole life. Buying back my honor one job at a time. Interest rates are terrible."

She looked at me across the cockpit, and there was something in her face I recognized because I'd seen it in mirrors — the look of a person deciding whether to hand over a piece of themselves they'd been guarding.

"My father used to say," she said slowly, "that the sea keeps honest books. Everything gets entered eventually. Even the things people lie about."

"He sounds like a man who spent a lot of time on water."

"Too much." Something closed in her voice, a door shutting on a room I wasn't invited into yet. "He's missing. Three years now. That's — that's part of why I need the Cape. Why the map matters."

I nodded and didn't push. There'd be time for the whole of it or there wouldn't; you can't force a confession out of a person any more than you can force a tide. But I filed it away, next to the way she'd lied to me about what the map was back in Port Amara, and the way her eyes had gone to the horizon when she said *missing*, like she was checking whether the sea had returned anything.

"Vale," she said, after a bit. "Your boat. If the inquiry took your license — how do you still charter at all?"

"Limited coastal permit. Day runs, fair weather, no passengers for hire. Enough to eat, not enough to live." I patted the deck beneath us. "And she's registered under my mother's name, which kept her out of the bankruptcy. Best legal advice I never paid for."

Ahead of us, the water had changed color — gone from slate to a deep, oily green, the color it turns when the bottom starts thinking about rising up to meet you. I checked the chart, then checked it again, because charts deserve suspicion but not twice in ten minutes.

"We're coming up on the shallows off Gannet Rock," I said. "After that, open water to the Cape, two days if the weather holds its temper. Which it won't. So we'll be running the edge of the front, dodging squalls, and praying this patch holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we'll have a very memorable swim." I glanced at her. "You swim?"

"Since before I could walk. My father threw me off a dock when I was four. Said the sea should meet me on its own terms before I met it on mine."

"Your father sounds like he was either very wise or very tired."

"Both," she said, and looked away, and I understood that I'd just been shown the edge of something large and submerged, the way you see a reef's shadow from a masthead — enough to know it's there, not enough to chart it.

* * *

By midmorning the monsoon had stopped being a rumor. It filled the southern sky wall to wall, black and boiling, lightning walking inside it like prisoners pacing a cell. The wind came in gusts now, warm and wet, shoving the Mercy's shoulder whenever it felt like it. I ran her hard, keeping the swells on our quarter, riding the long rollers toward the Cape with the patched seam groaning underneath us like an old man climbing stairs.

Paloma worked the deck like she'd been born on one — coiling lines, lashing the dinghy, clearing the scuppers — and I caught myself more than once just watching her instead of the water, which is how pilots die, so I made myself stop. Mostly.

Around noon the electric bilge pump kicked on by itself.

It ran longer than it should have. Then it ran again twenty minutes later. Then again, sooner.

I went below with the flashlight and found the truth waiting for me in the bilge well, patient as arithmetic: the patch was holding, but the strain of the building sea had opened a second seam forward of the first, thinner, slower, meaner. Two holes in a boat is not twice the problem of one. It's a different order of problem entirely, the way two creditors are different from one.

I stood there in the reeking dark with water lapping at my boots and did the math I didn't want to do. Pump capacity versus inflow, distance remaining versus fuel remaining, the Cape ahead and the monsoon behind and nowhere between them but water that wanted us.

Up top, Paloma read my face before I said a word. She was getting frighteningly good at that.

"It's worse," she said.

"It's worse."

"How much worse?"

"We can stay ahead of it if we run flat-out and the sea stays civil. It won't stay civil. There's a place—" I stopped, because the place I was thinking of had a reputation, and reputations exist for reasons. "There's a passage through the outer reef, maybe six hours ahead. Locals call it the Storm Gate. It cuts two days off the run to the Cape."

"But?"

"But it's called the Storm Gate for the same reason some dogs are called Killer." I looked south at the wall of weather, then north at the thin line of coast, and felt the old familiar weight settle onto my shoulders — the weight of a decision that belongs entirely to you and will follow you forever, whichever way you make it. "Running it in this swell is the kind of thing that gets your name spoken quietly in harbors. Assuming your name gets spoken at all."

Paloma followed my gaze to the storm, then looked back at me, and I saw her do the same math I'd just done, and arrive at the same answer, and hate it exactly as much.

"How well do you know it?" she asked.

That was the question, wasn't it. How well does any man know the door he's only been through once, years ago, in daylight, with a mentor shouting in his ear? I thought about lying. I thought about the safe route and the slow leak and the monsoon catching us in open water like a debt collector with our address.

"I know it," I said, "well enough to be terrified properly. Which is more than most can say."

Behind us, the first outrider squall came down off the front like something dropped from a great height, and the rain hit the deck in fat, hammering drops, and the Mercy leaned into the wind as if she'd already made up her mind.

"Then steer," Paloma said, tying down the last line. "I'll bail."
