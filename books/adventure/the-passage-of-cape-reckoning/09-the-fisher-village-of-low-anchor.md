## 9. The Fisher Village of Low Anchor

The Mercy started taking on water the way an honest man starts lying — slowly, apologetically, and then all at once.

Idris heard it before I did. He was below deck checking the bilge, and the sound he made when he came back up wasn't a word so much as a decision. "We're putting in," he said. "There's a village on the leeward side of that headland. Low Anchor. They'll have a careenage and a man who can stitch a hull."

"How far off course?"

"Half a day, if the wind forgives us. A week, if it doesn't." He glanced at the sky, where the monsoon was stacking itself on the horizon like furniture being moved into a room we'd have to live in. "We don't have a week."

So we didn't argue. There's a particular intimacy in shared catastrophe — you learn very quickly which arguments are worth having, and "should my boat stop leaking" is not one of them.

Low Anchor announced itself before we saw it: the smell of woodsmoke and fish guts and tar, that perfume every working coast wears like a uniform. Then the village itself, strung along a shingle beach like beads on a crooked wire — stilt houses, drying racks hung with silver fish, a breakwater of black volcanic stone that some stubborn generation had built by hand and every generation since had maintained out of respect or guilt.

The careenage was a slipway and a winch and a man named Brannoc with forearms like anchor chain, who walked around the Mercy's hull sucking air through his teeth in the universal dialect of boat repair. "She's been patched before," he said, "badly, and by someone in a hurry." He looked at Idris. "That you?"

"That was me," Idris admitted.

"Then you can hold the light while I do it properly."

I expected Idris to bristle. Instead he nodded like a man accepting a sentence, and something in my chest went soft and strange, watching him hand over his boat — his whole reputation, his whole redemption project — to a stranger with a mallet. That's the thing about people carrying shame. They'll hand over anything if you ask for it in the right tone.

The repairs would take two days. The caulking had to cure. There was nothing to do but wait, and I have never been good at waiting in places where the past has fingerprints.

* * *

I felt it first at the drying racks, where an old woman was turning fish with a wooden paddle, each one flipped with a rhythm so practiced it had stopped being motion and become music. She looked up at me, looked past me, and frowned.

"You're not from here," she said. "But your face is."

"I'm passing through."

"Nobody passes through Low Anchor. They arrive, or they don't." She flipped another fish. "Who were your people?"

"Pemberton," I said, before my caution could catch up with my mouth. "Edmund Pemberton."

The paddle stopped.

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain and a faint smell of salt. Around us, the village went on — children chasing a dog, nets being mended, a gull conducting its usual extortion racket — but our little circle of air went still.

"You're his daughter," the old woman said. It wasn't a question. "You have his chin. He had a chin like a decision."

I laughed, and it came out wrong, somewhere between a bark and something wetter. "You knew him?"

"Everyone knew him. He came the year of the red tide. Dug in the middens, asked a thousand questions, drew everything." She set down the paddle and studied me with eyes gone pale as sea glass. "He was a good man. A strange man. He paid for his lodging in stories, and the children followed him like gulls follow a trawler." She paused. "You didn't come with him this time."

"He's not — " I started, and found I didn't know how to finish it. *He's not with me. He's not anywhere. He's a letter I haven't opened.* "We're not close," I said instead.

"Ah." The old woman nodded, in the way of people who have lived long enough to know that *not close* is a country with many districts. "I'm Maren. Come. If you're his blood, you'll eat at my table, and you'll tell me what he was like when he was young, and I'll tell you what he was like when he was ours."

* * *

Maren's house smelled of cedar smoke and dried kelp, and the stew she made had a fish in it I couldn't identify and didn't dare ask about. She fed me the way old women on working coasts feed people: relentlessly, as if malnutrition were a personal insult.

"He came for the middens," she said, "but really he came for the stories. Every village has them. The ones that sound like nonsense until you map them." She smiled. "He used to say the old ones didn't build temples to keep gods in. They built them to keep promises in."

I went very still. On my bunk aboard the Mercy, folded into the unopened envelope of my father's letter, was a map that said exactly that — in his handwriting, in his marginalia, in his obsessive looping script. *They gave. That was the wealth. They gave until giving was the architecture.*

"You knew about the temple," I said carefully. "The drowned one. Off Cape Reckoning."

Maren's smile faded by degrees, like a tide going out.

"We knew of it the way you know of a grave," she said. "You don't dig in it. You don't build on it. You leave flowers, sometimes, if the season's been cruel." She looked at me. "Your father understood that better than any outsider I ever met. That's why we helped him. That's why — " She stopped. Her hands, which had been steady all afternoon, developed a small tremor.

"That's why what?"

"That's why we never forgave ourselves for the second expedition."

* * *

Here is what I learned over the next hour, in Maren's low voice, while the light went amber and then bruised-purple through her one window:

Three years ago — the same season I walked out, though I didn't tell her that, though I couldn't — my father had come back to Low Anchor. Not with students and notebooks this time, but with six hired divers and a salvager's contract, and a hunger on him that the villagers didn't recognize.

"He was different," Maren said. "Faster. Angrier, almost. He said someone else was coming for the site — a magistrate's survey, salvage permits, men with machines. He said if he didn't reach it first, they'd strip it to the bones and sell the bones." Her mouth twisted. "We told him the sea would decide. He said the sea had already been outvoted."

They provisioned him. They gave him the safe approach through the outer shoals — knowledge their families had carried for two hundred years, given freely, because he'd earned it once and they believed he still was that man. Six boats went out with his expedition. One boat came back, four days later, with two divers aboard and no expedition leader.

"What happened to the divers?"

"One had drowned. The other wouldn't speak of it. He stayed a month in the village, staring at the water, and then he walked inland and we never saw him again." Maren's voice was very quiet now. "Your father never surfaced. They searched for nine days. The monsoon came early that year and closed the strait, and after that, the sea keeps what it takes."

The stew had gone cold in front of me. I don't remember eating any of it. I remember the fire popping, and Maren's granddaughter asleep in the corner, and the enormous, unbearable arithmetic of it: *three years*. Three years I'd spent being angry at him for loving his work more than me, for missing my mother's memorial, for choosing a drowned civilization over his living daughter — and he'd been dead the whole time. Drowned off Cape Reckoning, chasing the temple that was chasing me, while I carried his letter around like ballast and told myself I was free.

Grief, it turns out, has a schedule of its own. It doesn't arrive when the news does. It waits — sometimes years — until it can find you somewhere quiet, somewhere with cedar smoke and cold stew and an old woman's sea-glass eyes, and then it comes in all at once, like water through a breached hull.

"I'm sorry," I said, and it was such a small, stupid thing to say. "I didn't know. Nobody told me."

"Who was there to tell?" Maren asked gently. "He wrote to you, didn't he? Men like that always write. It's how they say the things they can't say out loud."

I nodded, and couldn't speak, and Maren pretended not to notice, which is the highest form of kindness available to the old.

* * *

Idris found me on the breakwater after midnight.

I'd gone there to be alone with it — the grief, the guilt, the whole sodden cargo of it — but the black stones were cold and the wind was coming off the water in long shoves, and after an hour of trying to feel nothing I settled for feeling it in the presence of someone who wouldn't ask.

He sat down without a word. We watched the monsoon lightning work the far horizon, silent and patient, like a debt collector walking up the drive.

"Brannoc says two days," Idris said finally. "Maybe three, if the caulking needs a second cure."

"Okay."

"Paloma." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "Maren's granddaughter told me. About the expedition. About your father." A pause. "I'm sorry."

"Everyone keeps saying that. Sorry. Like it's a door I can walk through."

"Isn't it?"

I laughed, or something near it. "I spent three years being furious at him. Do you understand that? I built my whole — I made *distance* my career. Miles instead of years. Every job further from him than the last, and I told myself it was freedom, and the entire time — " My voice did something humiliating. I let it. There was no one left alive to be strong for. "The entire time he was at the bottom of the sea, and the last thing I ever gave him was a slammed door."

"You don't know that's the last thing."

"He never came after me, Idris. He never — "

"He wrote to you." Idris's voice was quiet. "That's a man coming after you the only way he knew how. Slow. In ink."

I looked at him. The lightning lit his face in stutters, and there was nothing in it but an awful, useful honesty. He'd said the true thing, the thing I'd been circling for three years and one unopened letter, and it hurt exactly as much as truth is supposed to.

"The map," I said. My voice was steadier than I felt. "It's his. The annotations — I told you it was a prospector's chart, and it is, but my father wrote all over it. Years ago. Before Hollis ever had it." I pulled my knees up. "And now I find out he died trying to reach the place on it. And now I find out someone else — Corvane, Blackwood, whoever — is still hunting it, and the only thing my father left on this earth is a piece of grease-stained cloth pointing at his own grave."

"Then we should be careful with it," Idris said. "And careful with you."

"I don't need — "

"Everyone needs something. I needed a boat. You needed a pilot. Now you need two days and a grave to sit with." He stood, brushing off his trousers. "Get some sleep, Pemberton. We run at first light after the cure. Storm Gate's still ahead of us, and the monsoon isn't waiting for your grief to catch up."

He walked back toward the careenage, and I stayed on the breakwater a while longer, doing the arithmetic again, but differently now. Not *three years lost* but *three years kept* — kept in a letter, kept in a map, kept in an old woman's stories about a man with a chin like a decision who paid for his lodging in tales.

* * *

We were up before dawn on the third morning, loading the last of Maren's provisions — salt fish, hard bread, a jar of something fermented that Idris eyed like it might be armed. Brannoc pronounced the hull sound and charged Idris a price that made them both smile, which I suspect was the point.

Maren walked me to the boat. At the gunwale she caught my wrist, her grip still strong, her eyes still pale.

"He told us once," she said, "that the temple wasn't a treasure. It was a *test*. That whoever found it would be asked a question, and the question was the treasure." She pressed something into my hand — small, wrapped in oilcloth. "He left this with us. He said, *if my daughter ever comes, give it to her. If she never comes, burn it.*"

My heart was slamming. "What is it?"

"I don't know. He said you'd know what to do with it." She stepped back, and her face was the face of every elder on every coast, carrying everyone's griefs in a net too small for them. "The second expedition went out on a falling tide, against our advice. The old ones say the temple only opens for those who arrive *with* the water, not against it." She almost smiled. "Your father argued with that for a week. He was wrong, of course. But he was magnificent being wrong."

I climbed aboard the Mercy with the oilcloth packet burning a hole through my pocket like a second heart. Idris cast off, Brannoc shouted something unrepeatable and encouraging, and the village of Low Anchor shrank behind us into the morning haze.

Out past the breakwater, Idris set our heading and looked over. "Well?"

I unwrapped the packet on my knee.

Inside was a diver's slate — a small black rectangle, the kind my father had used a thousand times underwater, scratched with messages when a pencil wouldn't survive the wet. The writing on it was his. Recent. The chalk still legible, protected all these years by the oilcloth.

It read:

*Paloma — if Maren gave you this, then you came, and I am sorry for every mile I put between us. The temple is real. So is the danger. Do not trust the magistrate. Do not dive alone. And whatever they promise you —*

The slate was scratched through at the bottom edge, gouged, as if it had been written in a hurry, or dropped, or held by a hand that was already underwater.

Idris read it over my shoulder. Then he looked up at the horizon, where the monsoon had begun, at last, to move.

"Whatever they promise us," he said quietly, "looks like it's going to be a very short list."
