## 10. Corvane's Offer

The ship came out of the haze at midmorning like a debt collector.

I'd been watching that stretch of water for two days, ever since we cleared Sable Key, and I'll tell you what I know about ships: a merchantman runs fat and lazy, a navy cutter runs mean and straight, and a salvage tug runs like it's apologizing for existing. This one ran none of those ways. It ran like it owned the water beneath it, the wind above it, and every man who'd ever looked at either. White hull, black trim, no colors flying, which on this coast is its own kind of flag — the flag of a man so important he doesn't need to announce himself.

"Idris." Paloma's voice from below, tight. "There's a boat coming off her. Fast one."

"I see it."

"What do we do?"

"We do nothing interesting," I said, and put the Mercy's helm over just enough to look bored.

Here's the thing about being disgraced: you develop a nose for trouble the way a beggar develops a nose for bread. Three years of watching charters dry up, of harbormasters finding sudden reasons to inspect my papers, of dockside conversations going quiet when I walked up — you learn that trouble doesn't usually come at you head-on. It comes alongside, friendly, with its hand already in your pocket.

The launch was white too, with an awning aft and six rowers moving like they were paid by the stroke. Nobody pays for awnings and matched rowers unless they want you to know the cost of disagreeing with them. I counted rifles under the canvas — three, maybe four — and then stopped counting, because counting doesn't change arithmetic.

Paloma came up on deck with the map's oilskin bundle nowhere visible, which meant she'd hidden it well, which meant she'd been hiding things from me longer than I'd known about them. I filed that away. There'd been a weight between us since Blackwood boarded us outside Port Amara — she thought I hadn't noticed her slipping something into her father's letter, and I let her think it, because a lie you can see coming is almost as good as the truth.

"They're hailing us," she said.

"I have ears."

"They're hailing *you*. By name."

That landed colder than the spray. The launch was still half a mile out. A voice carried across the water through a speaking trumpet, unhurried, almost pleasant:

"*Mercy of Port Amara. Captain Idris Vale. Heave to and receive the Magistrate's courtesy.*"

"He knows your name," Paloma said quietly.

"Everybody knows my name," I said. "That's what disgrace is. It's not anonymity. It's fame with the sign turned around."

* * *

They came aboard without asking, which told me everything before anyone opened their mouth. You don't board a stranger's vessel uninvited unless you've already decided whose vessel it is.

He came up the ladder last, and I'll give him this — he didn't look like a villain. Corvane looked like a man who'd been carved by someone very skilled who'd run out of time for the eyes. Silver at the temples, a magistrate's coat cut close enough to count ribs through, hands soft as a clerk's but steady as a surgeon's. He looked around my deck — the patched rail, the salt-stained canvas, the pump handle lashed by the mast where it had lived for two nights now — the way a man looks at a room he's deciding whether to buy or burn.

"Captain Vale." His voice was mild. That was the worst part. "You've fallen a long way."

"Falling's easy," I said. "It's the landing that costs."

Something flickered behind his eyes — amusement, maybe, or its taxidermied cousin. He gestured, and his men spread along the rail with the loose professionalism of people who'd done this before. Paloma stood by the mainmast with her arms crossed, and I watched Corvane look at her exactly once, cataloguing her, and then dismiss her as furniture. I've made that mistake myself. It's how you end up surprised.

"You're wondering how I found you," he said.

"Not really wondering. Dreading knowing."

"A salvage tender reported your heading after Sable Key. A fisherman sold your description for two silver. And Oren Blackwood," — the name came out with the flat distaste of a man naming a tool he finds crude — "is persistent to the point of embarrassment. You were never hard to find, Captain. You were simply not worth finding before."

"And now?"

"Now you are carrying something worth more than this boat, this coast, and possibly your life. And I am offering you a chance to be paid for it instead of drowned for it."

The wind shifted. Somewhere below, the bilge pump knocked its tired rhythm — the Mercy had taken a wound in open water and I'd patched her with prayer and pine tar, and both were running thin. Corvane heard it too. I watched his gaze drop toward the sound, and I understood suddenly that he knew everything. The hull damage. The route. Probably what we'd eaten for breakfast.

"Let's say I'm carrying something," I said. "Say it plainly, then. Men like you don't cross open water for maybes."

"Men like me." He smiled without warmth. "Very well. Plainly." He stepped closer, boots quiet on my deck. "Three years ago you were the finest pilot on this coast. The *Vespera* sinking was not your failure — I have read the inquiry. I know Harrow & Sons cut her plating and overloaded her manifest, and I know they bought the tribunal that blamed you. Your license can be restored. Your name can be cleared. Publicly. Formally. With reparations that would buy you ten boats better than this one."

My hands went still on the wheel.

I want to tell you I felt nothing. I want to tell you the offer slid off me like rain off oilskin. But I'm an honest man in the only place I have left to be honest, which is inside my own skull, and inside my own skull there was a moment — long, hot, shameful — when I saw it all laid out: the harbor office door opening, the tribunal's verdict read aloud and reversed, my name said in Port Amara without the spit-polish of pity. Standing at a charter desk again with clean papers. Sleeping nights without hearing forty-one people scream through a hull I'd begged my employers to reinforce.

Forty-one. You don't forget a number like that. You carry it in your chest like ballast, and every port that turned me away added another stone.

"All of that," Corvane said softly, watching my face the way a card player watches yours, "for one delivery. Bring me the woman and the map she carries. That's all. No violence required. She steps onto my ship, you step into your old life, and everyone profits according to their station."

"The map," I said. My voice came out flatter than I intended. "Not the temple. Just paper."

"The paper is the temple, Captain. But yes. Paper first."

* * *

Paloma hadn't moved. I could feel her listening the way you feel weather changing — pressure, not sound. And I caught myself doing something I hated: weighing her. Not her safety. Her *weight*. What she was against what I'd lost. A woman I'd known two weeks against a name I'd buried three years ago.

And here's what saved me, if anything did: I looked at her properly. Really looked. At the salt in her hair and the burn on her knuckles from the pump work she'd done half the night without waking me, taking my watch because she'd seen I was dead on my feet and never said a word about it. At the way she stood at that mast like she'd stand there through a hurricane if standing was what the boat needed. Two weeks, and she'd already bailed my bilge, held my helm in the Storm Gate approach swells, and lied to me badly enough that I knew she was terrified of something bigger than any lie.

You don't sell that. Not for a pardon. Not for a fleet.

Because here's the arithmetic Corvane couldn't do: he thought he was offering me my old life back. But my old life was the one where I trusted men like him, and it cost forty-one souls and everything I had. My new life was a leaking boat and a lying passenger and a promise I hadn't even been told the shape of yet — and it was, God help me, the first honest work I'd done since the *Vespera* went down.

"No," I said.

Corvane tilted his head, genuinely curious. "No?"

"No. I'll say it slower if it helps."

"Captain Vale." He sighed, and for a moment the civility thinned and I saw the machinery underneath, cold and oiled and enormous. "Do you understand what refusal costs? Blackwood has orders. The monsoon closes this coast in nine days. Every harbor from here to the Cape will be closed to you — I need only send word. You will die out here fixing leaks with tar, and no tribunal will ever clear your name, because tribunals require a living defendant."

"That's the second-best offer I've had today," I said. "The best was the fisherman who offered me rum for my boots."

"Why?" And there it was — real curiosity, naked and brief, the first honest thing on his whole ship. "You owe this woman nothing. You've known her a fortnight. What is she to you?"

I thought about lying. It would've been easier. But some questions deserve true answers, if only because true answers are rare enough to be disorienting.

"She's the first person in three years who needed me for something I'm actually good at," I said. "And she hasn't asked me what happened to the *Vespera*. Everyone else asks. She just gets in the boat."

Corvane studied me for a long moment. Then he did something worse than threatening me.

He laughed. Short, genuine, almost fond.

"Oh, Captain. You're exactly what the reports said. Sentimental, competent, and doomed." He straightened his coat. "Keep her, then. Keep the map. Sail to the Cape. I'll be there when you arrive — I'm always there when things arrive. And when you've seen what's down there, when you understand what it can build for a man like me—" he paused at the ladder, hand on the rail, and looked back with something almost like kindness— "you'll wish you'd taken today's price. It only goes up from here."

"Corvane." He stopped. "Why let us go? You've got four rifles and I've got a pump."

"Because forced men guard secrets poorly," he said, "and broken pilots sail carelessly. I'd rather you arrive at the temple alive, willing, and followed." He smiled. "Besides — I want you to choose this, Captain. In the end. Everyone chooses. It's simply a matter of what the sea takes from them first."

He went down the ladder. The launch pulled away, white and orderly, rowers in rhythm, and the big ship wore off slowly toward the southeast without hurry, without hiding, a statement rather than a pursuit.

* * *

We stood at the wheel together for a while after they were gone. Neither of us spoke. The pump knocked. The Mercy limped north on a wind that smelled like rain that hadn't decided yet.

"He knew about the hull," Paloma finally said. "About the patch."

"He knew about my breakfast."

She looked at me sideways. "What did he offer you?"

And there it was. The question I'd been dreading, arriving dressed as a simple thing. I could lie. She'd lied to me; fair was fair. But I was tired of fair, and I was tireder of lies, and there was a limit to how much weight a leaking boat should carry.

"A full pardon," I said. "License restored. Name cleared. Reparations. For delivering you and the map to him."

Paloma went very still. "And you said no."

"I said no."

"Why?"

"Because he wanted me to sell the first honest thing I've done in three years," I said, "and I've learned what dishonest cargo does to a hull. It sinks you. Slow, then all at once."

She stared at me, and something moved across her face — guilt, recognition, the look of a person holding a secret that has just gotten heavier. Her hand drifted toward the breast of her jacket, where the letter lived, where the map lived inside the letter, and I watched her decide, again, not to tell me.

"Idris," she started.

"Don't," I said, not unkindly. "Whatever it is — you'll tell me when telling me costs less than hiding it. Or when it costs more. Either way, I'll still be at this wheel."

She looked at me for a long time. The wind gusted, cool now, smelling properly of rain. Behind us, far off to the southeast, Corvane's white ship sat on the horizon like a splinter under the skin — not chasing, not leaving. Waiting. Keeping pace with our future.

"We should make for Low Anchor," she said at last, quietly. "The hull needs real repair. And there are people there who—" She stopped. Swallowed. "People who knew my father."

So. There it was, or the edge of it. I nodded like it was nothing, like I hadn't just been handed the corner of a map I'd been sailing blind.

"Low Anchor, then," I said, and put the helm over.

But I'll tell you what kept me up that night, more than the pump, more than the white ship on our wake: Corvane hadn't threatened us. He'd *scheduled* us. He let us go the way a fisherman lets a line run — because a hooked fish fights less than a netted one, and because he knew something I was only beginning to suspect.

We weren't sailing to the Cape.

We were being sailed there.

And somewhere south of us, in a village I'd never seen, a man named Hollis had died believing a map could outrun a magistrate — and every mile north we made, I liked our odds less.
