## 11. Halfway Truths

Idris didn't speak to me for the first hour after we cleared Low Anchor's breakwater. He steered with his jaw set like a man chewing on a word he couldn't decide whether to swallow, and I stood at the rail pretending to study a horizon that had nothing to teach me.

The village was still a smudge of brown and green behind us when he finally said it.

"You cried at that woman's table."

"I didn't cry."

"Your eyes leaked. That's crying, Pemberton. Sailors' definition." He adjusted the wheel a degree. "You cried because they told you your father walked into the water and never came back. Which means the map in your kit isn't salvage paper. It means you've known since Port Amara what we're chasing, and you let me run my boat — *my* boat — up this coast on half a story."

I turned to face him. There was no way around it now. Low Anchor had taken the lie out of my hands and broken it open on their kitchen table.

"You're right," I said.

He blinked. I think he'd been braced for an argument, maybe even a knife. Honesty seemed to disappoint him for a second before it settled into something warier.

"Say the rest."

I went below, dug through my kit past the flares and the spare line, and came back up with two things: the grease-stained map Hollis had pressed into my hands while his fever ate him alive, and the letter from my father that I'd carried unopened for three years, through four towns and a dozen jobs, like a stone I'd grown fond of carrying.

I spread them both on the chart table between us.

"Three years ago I walked out on him," I said. "We fought about — it doesn't matter what we fought about. It matters that I told him I'd rather scrape barnacles in Port Amara than spend one more season being the daughter of a man who loved dead civilizations more than living people. And then I did exactly that. Scraped barnacles. Measured my life in miles put between us." I tapped the letter. "He wrote me anyway. I never opened it. Couldn't. Then a dying prospector fell into my campfire outside Amara and traded me this map for a promise — carry it somewhere safe, tell no one. And when I looked at it, I recognized the handwriting in the margins."

Idris looked down. His eyes moved across the map the way a pilot reads water — fast, hungry, cataloguing. The coastline. The reef teeth. The little arch drawn in careful ink off Cape Reckoning, annotated in a hand that slanted left and pressed too hard on its descenders.

"That's him," Idris said quietly. "That's the father the elders talked about."

"The same. He annotated this years ago. Before Hollis ever owned it, maybe. They must have crossed paths — two men hunting the same drowned thing." My throat tightened. "Hollis made me promise to take it safe. He didn't say safe from whom. But men have been watching me since Port Amara, Idris. Men flying Corvane's colors. So no, I didn't tell you the whole truth. I told you enough to buy your wheel and not one word more, because every person who knows is a person Blackwood can squeeze."

"And because if you said it out loud," Idris said, "it stopped being a job and became your father."

"Yes," I said. "That too."

He was quiet a long moment. The Mercy rolled under us, patient as a dog waiting out a storm.

"Corvane offered me a pardon yesterday," he said. "Reinstatement. Clean papers. All I had to do was hand you over."

My stomach dropped through the deck. "And?"

"And here we are." He said it flatly, but there was a flicker under it — the look of a man who has just set down something heavy and isn't sure yet whether his arms miss the weight. "You want to know why? It's not honor. Don't go painting me with that brush. It's that I've been bought before, and I know exactly what the merchandise feels like afterward."

"What happened to you, Vale?" I asked. "You've been dodging that question since Amara. Everyone on this coast knows the name Idris Vale and everyone says it the same way — quiet, like it might bite."

He laughed, but there was no humor in it. He killed the engine to an idle, and the sudden silence felt like a door closing.

"Four years ago I ran cargo for Meridian Coastal. Big outfit, big contracts, big reputation. I was their youngest charter master. Golden boy." He leaned against the wheel housing, arms folded. "One autumn I flagged a load — ferry run, forty-one passengers, weather turning. The hull plating below the waterline was corroded through in three places. I filed the report. Maintenance logged it. Management signed off on the crossing anyway, because the schedule was worth more than the steel. I refused to take her out. They gave her to another pilot."

"Idris—"

"She went down off Sable Key. Eleven dead." His voice didn't crack. That was somehow worse — the terrible evenness of a man who has rehearsed this so many times it's gone smooth as sea glass. "The inquiry needed someone to blame. The company had lawyers; the other pilot had a family to feed and kept his mouth shut. I had a clean record and a conscience, which made me the perfect villain. They said I'd falsified my inspection report to cover my own negligence. That I'd waved her through. My license revoked, my name blacklisted from every legitimate charter from here to the capital."

"The report," I said slowly. "You filed a report. There would be copies—"

"There were. Buried inside a week. The maintenance chief who countersigned it drowned with the ship, conveniently for everyone above him." He shrugged, a small bitter motion. "So now I buy back my honor one risky job at a time, for people who can't afford honest pilots. And none of it adds up to anything. You can't bail out a name, Pemberton. Believe me. I've tried."

The wind shifted. Somewhere behind us, far south, the sky had begun assembling the monsoon — a bruised purple wall sitting on the water like a verdict.

"Why tell me?" I asked. "Why now?"

"Because you just handed me the truest thing you own." He nodded at the map, at the letter. "Seemed rude not to match it." He paused, and then, grudgingly, almost against his will: "Also because I watched you lie to Oren Blackwood with a straight face while he searched my boat, and I thought — there's someone worth telling the truth to."

* * *

We ran north through the afternoon with the truth finally loose among us, and it changed the shape of everything, the way pulling a splinter changes the ache.

Idris spread the map fully open for the first time and studied my father's marginal notes with a professional's eye, and I watched him read another man's obsession and recognize it.

"He knew these waters," Idris murmured, tracing a line of ink along the reef shelf. "Look — current timings, tide windows. This note here: 'Gate opens four days before monsoon closure, no longer.' He wasn't guessing. He'd been through the Storm Gate."

"Can you do it?"

"I can try it. Whether I can survive it is a separate question." He glanced up. "These annotations are dated. Nine years old. If he found the temple entrance nine years ago, why did the elders say he vanished only recently?"

That snagged in me like a fishhook. "A second expedition," I said. "The elders mentioned it. One that never returned."

"So he found it young, lost it or left it, and came back at the end of his life to finish the work." Idris tapped the little inked arch. "And whatever he found the second time, he didn't come back from it. Or didn't want to."

"Don't say that."

"I'm saying all of it," he said gently. "Because you deserve all of it, and because sailing on half-truths nearly got us boarded by a magistrate's salvage crew. We don't get to pick which truths are heavy anymore. Not if we're doing this together."

*Together.* The word sat in the cockpit between us, unremarkable and enormous.

"I have the letter," I heard myself say. "His letter. Three years unopened."

Idris said nothing. He just let the Mercy run, and the silence he gave me was the kindest thing anyone had handed me in years — room without pressure, space without judgment.

I picked up the envelope. My thumb found the flap, sealed with wax that had gone brittle as old coral. My hands were steadier than I expected them to be.

"Not yet," I said. "When we're anchored off the Cape. When I know how this ends. I'll read it there."

"That's not avoidance," Idris said. "That's ballast placement. Smart sailor."

I laughed — actually laughed — and something in his face loosened, like he'd been holding his own breath for four years and hadn't noticed until now.

* * *

Dusk came down violet and restless. I took the wheel so Idris could sleep, and the coast slid by dark and glittering on our port side, and I thought about wreckage.

Everyone I'd met on this water was wreckage of one kind or another. Hollis, burned out by fever and obsession. The elders of Low Anchor, keeping faith with a missing man because grief needs somewhere to live. Idris, hull breached by a lie that wasn't his, pumping ever since. Me — I'd spent three years calling my wreckage freedom.

But here was the thing about wreckage that I was only beginning to learn: it makes reef. Broken ships become places where things grow. Coral doesn't care that the hull was scuttled dishonestly. Fish school in the ribs of the guilty and the innocent alike, and divers come from miles around to see what beauty built itself out of the disaster.

Maybe that's what trust was. Not two intact people agreeing to sail together, but two wrecks close enough together that something could grow in the gap.

Near midnight I woke him for his watch, and he took the wheel, and before I went below he caught my sleeve.

"Pemberton. The Storm Gate — I'll need you on the bow tomorrow calling the water. I can't read the swells and steer at the same time, not through that passage. Whatever happens, whatever you see coming at us — call it straight. No halfway truths on the bow."

"Straight calls," I promised. "All the way down."

He nodded and turned to the wheel, and I went below to my bunk, where the map lay folded beside the unopened letter, two documents of the same man, one read and one waiting.

Through the hull I could hear the sea change its mind — a long low swell rolling in from the southeast, deeper-voiced than anything we'd felt since Amara. The monsoon, clearing its throat.

In the morning, the Storm Gate.
