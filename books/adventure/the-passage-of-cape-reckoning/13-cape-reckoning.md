## 13. Cape Reckoning

The Cape announced itself the way bad news does — quietly at first, then all at once.

We'd been running since dawn with the monsoon breathing down our necks like a debt collector, and I'd spent most of those hours below decks pretending to sleep while actually cataloguing every new sound the Mercy made. There was the groan from the patched section of hull that Idris had named "the Complaint" and refused to discuss further. There was the pump, which had developed a rhythm somewhere between a heartbeat and a hiccup. And there was my own pulse, which had been doing a fine impression of a drumline ever since we'd cleared the Storm Gate with our lives and most of our dignity intact.

Then Idris called down from the wheelhouse, and his voice had a strange hush in it, like a man in a church he didn't believe in.

"Paloma. You'll want to see this."

I came up into light gone strange — green-gold, filtered through cloud, the sea flat as hammered tin. And there it was: Cape Reckoning, a fist of black basalt punching up out of the water, crowned with wind-stripped pines that leaned inland as if permanently flinching from the sea. The cliffs fell away in sheer faces streaked white with guano, and at their base, where the rock met the water, the surf moved wrong — too smooth, too deliberate, sliding over something vast and submerged with the tenderness of a hand over a sleeping face.

I'd seen photographs of this place. I'd grown up with them, actually — pinned above my father's desk, taped inside his field notebooks, one of them laminated and carried so long its edges had gone soft as cloth. In the photos it looked imposing but inert. A rock was a rock.

In person it felt less like geography and more like attention.

"There," Idris said, pointing to the leeward side.

The cove opened between two arms of the cliff like a held breath. The water inside was dark and unnervingly still, sheltered from the swell, stained the color of old tea by tannins leaching off the pines. It was beautiful in the way of things that don't care whether you live or die.

"Depth?" I asked, because asking technical questions made me feel like a professional instead of a woman chasing her father's ghost along a coastline he'd loved more than her.

"Forty feet at the shelf, dropping fast past the arch." He brought us in slow, engine barely ticking over, reading the bottom through the clear patches the way other men read weather. "You're sure about this."

It wasn't a question, exactly. It was an invitation to be sure out loud, which after everything — the lie, the truth, the night we'd spent bailing seawater side by side while he told me about the *Coriander* going down with forty souls aboard because a shipping company's spreadsheet mattered more than a weld inspection — felt like what it was: trust, extended again, with visible effort.

"I'm sure," I said. And then, because he'd earned honesty and I'd apparently decided to make a habit of it now: "I'm terrified. But I'm sure."

"Good." He killed the engine and dropped anchor, and the chain's rattle echoed off the cliffs like applause from an empty house. "Terrified and sure is the only combination that's ever gotten me anywhere worth being."

* * *

The collapsed sea arch wasn't hard to find once you knew to look for it. My father's map showed it as a broken circle just off the southern point, and the reality matched the drawing with a fidelity that made my chest ache — Hollis had drawn it from memory decades ago, and the sea hadn't shifted it so much as a fathom. Half the arch still stood, a natural bridge of stone spanning maybe thirty feet of water, thick with barnacles and draped in kelp that swayed in the surge like the hair of something drowned. The other half lay where it had fallen, no one knew how long ago, forming a rubble slope that descended into blue-black nothing.

And beneath the fallen half, if the map's annotations were right — my father's annotations, his cramped, slanting hand filling every margin — the entrance waited. A fissure in the seabed. A doorway.

We geared up on deck in a silence that had its own vocabulary. I checked Idris's regulator; he checked mine. We'd fallen into these rituals somewhere around the third day without discussing them, the way people who've been through weather together do. His hands were steady. Mine were mostly steady. The dive lights hung from our harnesses, and the spare tank sat clipped to the Mercy's rail like a promise we'd both pretended not to notice making.

"Fifty minutes bottom time, no heroics," Idris said. "If either of us gets separated, we go straight up, slow, and meet at the surface. No searching. No being brave."

"You've given this speech before."

"I've needed this speech before." He settled his mask over his face, and for a second his eyes behind the glass were very serious. "Paloma. Whatever's down there — your father's been gone three years. Whatever we find, it's not going to be him waiting."

I nodded, because arguing would have required breath I wanted to save, and because some part of me — the part that had walked out on him, the part that had let a letter sit unopened in my kit through three years and two thousand miles — already knew that. Knew it the way you know a storm is coming from the smell of the air.

But knowing a thing and believing it are different animals, and only one of them drowns.

We went down together.

* * *

The water took us the way deep water always does — gently, then completely. The light thinned from gold to green to a bruised twilight, and the temperature dropped until I could feel it pressing through the wetsuit like a cold palm laid flat against my spine. Below us the rubble slope unspooled into the dark, and I followed Idris's fins down past shelves of rock furred with sponge and coral the color of dried blood, past a school of silver fish that parted around us and reformed behind like we were a thought they'd decided against.

At thirty feet, I saw the first carving.

It was small — a square of dressed stone set into the natural rock, no bigger than a dinner plate, worn nearly smooth. But what remained was unmistakable: two hands, open, palms up. Giving. Not taking. Even eroded almost to suggestion, even furred with a century of marine growth, the gesture read clearly as language.

My father had written about this. In the papers I'd read as a teenager, half to mock him and half because mocking required knowing the material — the coastal civilization he'd theorized, the one the academies had laughed out of every journal that would print him. *A culture that measured wealth in what it released,* he'd written. *Not what it accumulated.* Peer reviewers had used words like "romantic" and "unfalsifiable." One had suggested, with the delicacy academics reserve for cruelty, that Dr. Pemberton might benefit from distinguishing between archaeology and wish fulfillment.

I hovered in front of the carved hands, my exhaust bubbles rising past my face in silver strings, and wished every one of those reviewers could be strapped to a dive line beside me.

Idris touched my arm and pointed. Below us, wedged in the shadow of the fallen arch, there was a darkness that wasn't the water's darkness. It had edges. Structure. A doorway's patient geometry.

We descended the last few meters side by side, lights sweeping ahead of us, and the entrance resolved out of the gloom: a fissure in the seabed perhaps eight feet wide, framed by stones cut so precisely that after five hundred years underwater the joints still wouldn't admit a knife blade. The flood had taken the temple's upper stories whole — that much was obvious even from outside — but the foundations had been built to last, and last they had, sunk into the bedrock like roots.

The doorway breathed. That's the only word I have for it. The surge pushed water in and pulled it back out through the flooded halls beyond, a slow tidal respiration, and swimming into that current felt like being swallowed by something that was, impossibly, still alive.

Inside, we rose.

* * *

The great hall had been designed for air. You could tell immediately — the ceiling vaulted high overhead in stepped courses of stone, and though the sea had claimed everything below the twenty-foot line, the upper reaches of the chamber stood dry, echoing, hung with stalactites of mineral deposit that had begun growing the moment the waters broke through. Our lights found columns, friezes, the shadowed suggestion of stairways climbing into blackness. The acoustics were extraordinary; every bubble-scratch and fin-kick came back to us doubled and tripled, as if the hall itself were whispering commentary.

We surfaced in the largest of the air pockets, and the sound of our regulators cutting off rang through the space like a bell struck in a cathedral.

For a long moment neither of us spoke. There are places that impose silence, and this was one — not the dead silence of abandonment but the attentive silence of a room that has been listening for a very long time.

"Well," Idris said finally, and his voice came back to him from four directions. "Your father wasn't romantic."

"No," I said. My voice did something embarrassing on the second syllable and I let it. "He wasn't."

We swam to the nearest column, hauled ourselves half out of the water onto a ledge of stone, and got our lights working properly. And then the hall opened up around us in earnest.

The friezes ran the full circumference at shoulder height, and they told a story in procession: figures carved in low relief, moving left to right through scenes of giving. A figure pressing grain into another's hands. Figures lowering a boat laden with goods into the sea — deliberately, it seemed, ballasted to sink, an offering. A circle of figures, each holding out what they had to the next, a closed loop of open hands. Above them all, repeated like a refrain, the symbol from the stone outside: palms upturned, empty, generous.

There was no king in any panel. I looked for one — looked for the central figure, the ruler, the one larger than the rest, because every civilization I'd ever read about put one there. There wasn't. The largest figures in the entire frieze were the ones mid-gift, caught in the act of letting go.

"They buried their rich," I murmured, half to myself, remembering my father's wildest claim. "They sank their treasure on purpose. Wealth only counted if it left your hands—"

"Paloma." Idris's light had stopped moving. "Come look at this."

I turned, and the wonder went out of the water like heat out of an open door.

* * *

It was a dive tank. A modern aluminum scuba cylinder, standing upright against the base of the column, furred with the beginnings of algae but unmistakably, appallingly recent. Next to it: a plastic crate, its lid cracked, containing a coil of rope still bright with factory whiteness, a dive slate with writing on it, and — my light found it and my stomach dropped through the floor of the world — a carabiner clip of the kind sold in every chandlery from Port Amara north. Stainless steel. Unrusted.

Someone had been here within weeks. Maybe days.

Idris crouched beside the gear, and I watched the ease drain out of his posture degree by degree, replaced by the coiled readiness of a man who'd learned the hard way what it meant when other people's equipment turned up where it shouldn't. He picked up the dive slate carefully, by its edge, and angled it into his light.

Coordinates. Depth readings. A sketch of the hall layout — crude but accurate, the columns marked, the frieze noted, and at the far end of the chamber, past a colonnade the sketch showed but our lights couldn't yet reach, a single word underlined twice:

**VAULT**

Below the sketch, smaller, in handwriting that pressed hard enough to score the slate:

*Sealed. Do not force. Report to O.B.*

O.B.

Oren Blackwood.

The name landed in me like a fishhook. I heard myself breathe out, long and slow, and in the cathedral acoustics of the drowned hall it sounded like a confession.

"He's been here," I said. "Blackwood's crew. They found it first — or they found enough of it to know what's here, and then they went back for Corvane's orders, or heavier equipment, or—" I stopped, because the arithmetic was finishing itself in my head with terrible efficiency. "They know about the vault. And they know we're coming, Idris. Corvane let us go at Low Anchor because he never needed to stop us. He just needed to follow us."

Idris set the slate down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you handle a thing that might go off. When he spoke, his voice was flat and quiet, which I'd learned was worse than shouting.

"The Mercy's anchored in an open cove with one exit," he said. "And we've got maybe forty minutes of air apiece, and the only way out of this hall is through sixty feet of water."

Above us, faint but unmistakable, filtering down through the stone and the sea: the sound of an engine. Not distant. Approaching.

We clicked off our lights and stood in the dark of a five-hundred-year-old temple, listening to it come.
