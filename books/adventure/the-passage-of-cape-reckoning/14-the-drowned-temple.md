## 14. The Drowned Temple

The sea off Cape Reckoning was the color of a bruise healing — deep violet shading to green where the light could still be bothered to reach. I floated at the surface, regulator between my teeth, watching the shadow of the collapsed sea arch stretch away beneath me like a doorway someone had knocked down in a fit of temper.

Idris hovered beside me, and even through the mask I could read his expression: *this is a bad idea, and I'm going to do it anyway because you'll do it without me otherwise.* We'd had that conversation three times already, once with words.

"Bottom's at forty feet," he'd said back on the Mercy, tapping the depth soundings on Hollis's map like they might apologize. "Forty feet is a working dive. Forty feet inside a flooded ruin with no survey, no backup, and a monsoon building behind us is a different animal."

"And yet."

"And yet," he'd agreed, in the tone of a man who had already lost the argument and was just waiting for the paperwork.

Now we descended.

The water took us gently, which I hadn't expected. I'd braced for cold, for the shove and pull of current, but the cove held the sea the way a cupped hand holds rain — sheltered, patient. Light came down in long slanted columns, and the silt hung in them like dust in a sunlit room, the whole seafloor lit like the inside of some enormous, forgotten house.

The arch's rubble came up to meet us. Blocks the size of ox-carts lay tumbled across each other, furred with coral, and between them the entrance gaped: a corridor of fitted stone, wide enough for two, descending at a shallow angle into the dark. The edges of the blocks were carved. Even furred over with growth, the pattern was unmistakable — not decoration, not gods or kings or battles. Hands. Hundreds of hands, open, palm-up, carved in procession down both walls like a crowd waiting to receive something.

My father had a word for that. He'd had a lot of words, most of them delivered at dinner with his hands moving and his food going cold. *Iconography of reciprocity*, he'd called it once, pointing at a shard from a dig I was supposed to be helping with and wasn't. *When a culture puts open hands on its thresholds, Pemberton, it's telling you what it thinks a door is for.*

I'd been fourteen and furious about something I no longer remember. I'd told him nobody cared.

Somebody cared. I was looking at it.

* * *

The corridor sloped down and the light followed us partway and then gave up, and we switched on our torches. The beams carved tunnels through the green-black water, and the temple opened around us like a throat.

I don't know what I expected. Tombs, maybe. The drowned places in stories are always tombs — dead kings hoarding their gold under the waterline, waiting for divers with more courage than sense. My father had spent thirty years arguing that Cape Reckoning was something else, and the academic world had spent thirty years patting him on the head and going back to their shipwrecks and their coin hoards, because a drowned *treasury* was a story you could fund and a drowned *argument* was not.

He was right. God help me, he was right, and I was swimming through the proof.

The first hall was vast — my torch beam couldn't find its far wall — and it was not a treasury. There were no chests. No stacks of coin fused into coral. Instead there were alcoves, dozens of them, cut into the walls in careful rows, and every alcove held the same thing: a stone basin, empty, its lip worn smooth.

"Offering bowls," I said into my regulator, and the word came out as a fog of bubbles, and I didn't care. Idris looked at me. I pointed, and he swam the row with me, torch sweeping basin after basin, all of them empty, all of them *open* — turned outward, toward the entrance, toward whoever might come in needing one.

A treasury hoards. This place had been built to empty itself.

The carvings on the hall's columns told the rest, or enough of it that my chest went tight inside the dive vest. Processions of figures, and in each scene the figures carried something — grain, cloth, a jar, a child's toy rendered in loving miniature — and in each scene they were *giving it away*. Not to kings. Not to gods on thrones. To other figures, smaller, plainer, carved with the same care and the same dignity: the ones with nothing. And here was the thing that would have made my father weep into his notes, the thing he'd predicted in a paper that got him laughed off a conference stage in 2009: the figures doing the giving were carved *larger*. Not because they were more important.

Because the culture had understood that the act enlarged you. That wealth was measured in what left your hands.

*The Reckoning civilization did not accumulate,* my father had written, in the paper that ended his funding and started his obsession. *It circulated. Its temples were not storehouses but engines of redistribution, and its elite status was achieved through dispossession — the deliberate, ceremonial giving-away of everything. If the site at Cape Reckoning exists as I believe it does, it will contain no vaults. It will contain the machinery of generosity.*

I hung in the water in the middle of that first hall, torch beam trembling slightly, and felt something I couldn't name for a long moment. It was grief and it was vindication and it was fury — fury at every committee that had defunded him, every colleague who'd smiled the way you smile at a charming old man who's lost the thread. He'd been right in the specific, detailed, unglamorous way that nobody ever thanks you for.

Idris touched my arm. Pointed deeper. The hall had a far end after all, and a doorway in it, flanked by more open hands, and beyond it the green dark went on.

* * *

We went down through the temple the way water would have — finding the low places, following the slope. A second hall, smaller, its ceiling collapsed into graceful ruin, a school of silver fish parting around our lights like a living curtain. A stairway, drowned, each step carved with a single word in a script I half-recognized from my father's notebooks — the Reckoning glyph for *gift*, if his transliterations were right, repeated down the whole flight like a heartbeat.

*Gift. Gift. Gift.*

And then the shrine.

It sat at the end of a short passage where the temple narrowed, a niche cut into the living stone, and it was different from everything else we'd seen — smaller, humbler, the kind of place you'd duck into on your way past, not a destination. Inside it stood a stone figure: a person seated, arms extended, palms up, and the basin of the offering bowl set in the lap. The face had been worn away by water and time, which meant the figure could be anyone. Which meant, I suppose, it could be everyone.

And wedged into the basin — wedged into the lap of that patient, faceless, giving figure — was a waterproof case. Modern. Black plastic. The kind divers use.

Idris saw it at the same moment I did, and I felt him go rigid beside me. We'd found modern gear at the entrance — a scuffed tank boot, a fresh anchor scar on the rock, a ration wrapper that couldn't have been in the water more than a few weeks. Someone had been here. Someone recent. And now someone's equipment, sitting in a three-thousand-year-old shrine, in the exact spot a person would put something they wanted found — or something they wanted protected.

I should have been afraid. I registered that I should have been afraid the way you register weather reports from far away. Mostly I was reaching.

My hands shook — cold, I told myself, it's the cold, forty feet of seawater is cold — as I worked the case free of the basin. It was heavy. It was sealed. And stenciled on the lid, faded but legible, in handwriting I would have known in the dark, in a crowd, at the bottom of the sea:

**E. PEMBERTON — PROPERTY OF.**

I made a sound into my regulator that I'm glad no one will ever transcribe.

My father. My father had been *here*. Not decades ago, not in some early expedition I'd never heard about. The case was modern. The case was *recent*. The wrapper at the entrance, the anchor scar — I'd been walking through the evidence of him for an hour and calling it dread.

Idris's hand closed on my shoulder — *steady, slow your breathing, don't bolt for the surface* — and I did the thing you're trained to do, which is the opposite of everything you feel. I breathed. Four counts in, six counts out. The bubbles rose past my face like small pale ghosts leaving.

We ascended to the arch rubble and surfaced inside a pocket of air the temple had trapped beneath the collapsed arch — a low stone chamber, half above the waterline, echoing with the slap and suck of the sea. We pulled our regulators out. The air tasted of salt and stone and something mineral and old.

"Paloma." Idris's voice was careful. "Whatever's in that case—"

"It's his." My voice came out wrecked. "It's his handwriting. It's— he *was here*, Idris. Three years. Three years I thought he was off chasing ghosts and not calling, and then he was gone, and everyone said the sea took him, and—" I stopped, because my throat had closed. "He was *here*."

I got the case open with fingers that wouldn't behave.

Inside: a dive slate and chalk. A small, waterproofed notebook. A photograph, sealed in plastic, gone soft and wavy with damp — my father, grayer than I remembered, squinting into the sun on the deck of some boat, and I could not tell you the last time I'd looked at his face on purpose. And the notebook.

I opened it to the last written page, because that's what you do, because you're human, and the date at the top hit me like a hull breach.

It was dated *eleven months ago*.

Eleven months after the expedition that never returned. Eleven months after the coast guard closed the file and the university sent a condolence letter to a daughter who couldn't make herself open it. Eleven months after I'd stood at a memorial service where people said *lost to the sea* in the gentle past tense, and I'd sat in the back row and thought: *he chose it over me, and the sea just finished the job.*

He was alive here. Writing. Working. *Eleven months ago.*

"Paloma." Idris again, softer. "Read it to me or don't. But read it."

I read it. My voice cracked on the first line and steadied after, the way a boat does.

*Day 214. The flood-channels are real — I've mapped three of them, and there's a fourth behind the vault chamber I can't reach alone. The builders designed this place to be drowned. Deliberately. A failsafe, or a ritual, or both — I can't decide if that's faith or engineering, and I suspect they wouldn't have understood the question.*

*The vault is the heart of it. Everything they gave away, everything they measured themselves by, is recorded there — the ledgers of a civilization that kept accounts of generosity. Corvane's people are getting closer. I've moved the journal. If you're reading this, and if you're who I think you are, then I'm sorry I wasn't brave enough to say this to your face, and I'm proud of you in ways I never learned to say out loud.*

*Some things must stay buried to stay sacred. I understand that now. I think you always did.*

*The channels can be opened from inside. Remember that. Whatever happens — remember that.*

I stopped reading. The chamber echoed. Somewhere below us, the temple sat in its green silence with its open hands and its empty bowls and its heart of recorded generosity, and somewhere above us, out past the arch, the monsoon was stacking itself against the horizon like a debt coming due.

Corvane's people. Getting closer. The gear at the entrance, the anchor scar — I'd assumed they were Corvane's. Now I wasn't sure of anything except that my father had been here, and afraid, and alone, and had thought of me at the end of a long entry in a drowned shrine, and had been *proud of me in ways he never learned to say out loud*.

I held the notebook against my chest, over the dive vest, over the heartbeat, and I cried into the trapped air of a three-thousand-year-old temple, and Idris Vale — disgraced pilot, stranger, three weeks ago a man I'd lied to about a map — sat on the rubble beside me in the half-dark and said nothing, which was the exactly correct thing to say.

It took me a while to notice what he was looking at.

He'd gone still in the particular way he went still when something was wrong — the way he'd gone still in Port Amara when he first spotted the magistrate's watchers, the way he'd gone still on the Mercy's deck the night before the Storm Gate. He was staring at the water filling the lower half of our air pocket. At something pale drifting in it.

I followed his gaze.

A ration wrapper. The same brand as the one at the entrance. Except this one hadn't been in the water long at all — the print was crisp, the plastic unclouded, and it turned slowly in the lantern-light of the trapped air like a small white flag.

Fresh. *Hours* fresh, maybe less.

"Paloma," Idris said quietly, "someone's in the temple with us. Right now."

And from somewhere below — down the stairway carved *gift, gift, gift*, down in the dark where the vault waited and the flood-channels slept — came the muffled, unmistakable clank of a tank knocking against stone.
