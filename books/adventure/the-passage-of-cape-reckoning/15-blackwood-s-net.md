## 15. Blackwood's Net

The first thing I saw when I broke the surface was the Mercy, and the second thing I saw was that she wasn't ours anymore.

There were men on her deck. Four of them, maybe five, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had already won whatever argument they'd come to have. One sat on my cabin roof with his boots dangling over the companionway like he owned the view. Another was at the rail, and he was holding my spare anchor line in his hands the way a man holds a leash.

I trod water beside Paloma, salt stinging the cut on my palm from the temple stones, and felt something cold settle into my chest that had nothing to do with the water temperature.

"Idris." Her voice came out low through the regulator hanging around her neck. "That's—"

"I see it."

"Is that Blackwood?"

I spat seawater and squinted against the afternoon glare. It was. Oren Blackwood stood at my helm — *my* helm, with his big square hands resting on *my* wheel like he was remembering how it felt to steer something worth steering — and he was watching us float in his cove with the patient expression of a fisherman whose net has finally closed.

"How long do you think they've been sitting there?" Paloma asked.

"Long enough to make themselves lunch in my galley, from the smell of it."

She almost laughed. Almost. Then the laugh died somewhere between her ribs and her throat, because we both understood what this was. Everything we'd run from since Port Amara had been swimming behind us the whole way, patient as tide, and now it had caught up while we were forty feet down, poking through a drowned civilization with our hearts in our mouths.

"We could dive back down," she said. "The entrance channel. We could stay under until—"

"Until what? Until they get bored? Until the air runs out?" I shook my head. "They've got the boat, Paloma. The boat is the exit. There is no other exit."

The cove at Cape Reckoning is a horseshoe of black stone, walled on three sides by cliffs that drop straight into deep water. Beautiful, in the way that beautiful things are often traps. The only way out by sea is the mouth of the cove, and even from here I could see why: anchored beyond it, fat and gray and flying the magistrate's colors, sat Corvane's ship. A coastal enforcement vessel, re-rigged for salvage, its crane arm folded like a sleeping mantis.

Blackwood lifted one hand off the wheel and beckoned. Just that. Two fingers, twice, the way you'd call a dog across a yard.

"He's not even going to pretend to negotiate," Paloma said bitterly.

"No," I said. "He's going to pretend it's an inspection. Watch."

We swam in. There wasn't anything else to do, and I've learned in my years on the water that the difference between surrender and strategy is mostly a matter of what you tell yourself on the way in. So I told myself: *strategy*. I told myself: *he wants the map, not us dead — dead men can't hand over maps.* I told myself: *the Mercy has gotten me out of worse.*

She hadn't, actually. But hope doesn't require an accurate record.

* * *

They hauled us aboard with all the courtesy of customs officers and all the warmth of undertakers. Somebody threw a blanket over Paloma's shoulders; nobody offered me one, which I took as a small professional courtesy — Blackwood remembered I didn't scare easy, and he wanted me to know he remembered.

The deck of my own boat smelled wrong. Coffee, yes — somebody had used my pot — but underneath it, engine grease and unfamiliar sweat, and worst of all the faint chemical tang of someone who'd been through my stores. I glanced at the open hatch to the hold and saw my spare line coiled wrong, my flares moved, my whole carefully ordered world rearranged by strangers' hands.

"You're bleeding on my deck," Blackwood said.

"It's your deck now, apparently."

"Don't be dramatic, Vale. It never suited you." He came down the helm steps slowly, a big man gone soft in the middle but hard everywhere else, with a face like a dock piling — weathered, splintered, impossible to argue with. He nodded at one of his men. "Get her below. Dry clothes. She looks half-drowned."

"I'm fine," Paloma said.

"You look like something the temple spat back out." His eyes flicked to hers, sharp and interested. "Which, I'm guessing, is more or less what happened."

Nobody answered him. He smiled like a man who enjoys being right without confirmation.

"Here's where we are," he said, settling onto my bench by the transom like it had been built for him, which was the kind of thing Oren Blackwood did — sit in other men's places until they became his. "Magistrate Corvane's compliments. He's been tracking you up the coast since Low Anchor — don't bother asking how; you already know the answer, or you should. You found the site. Congratulations. The Magistrate is very pleased. Now here's what happens next."

He held out his hand, palm up.

"The map. The journal, if you found it. Any notes, sketches, coordinates, or clever little copies either of you made thinking you were being smart. And then your silence — which means you forget the temple exists, you forget this cove exists, and when anyone asks, you two spent the monsoon season fishing for mackerel and catching nothing, as usual."

"And if we say no?" Paloma asked from inside her blanket.

Blackwood looked at her with something that might have been respect in a better man. In him it was just arithmetic.

"Then the Magistrate takes the site anyway, because there are eleven armed men on that ship offshore and two of you standing wet on a thirty-foot sloop. But before that happens, things get unpleasant in ways I'd rather skip, honestly. I've got no appetite for hurting women and I've got less for hurting pilots. Pilots are a brotherhood, Vale, even disgraced ones." He spread his hands. "So let's all be reasonable. Boat stays with me as collateral until the Magistrate says otherwise. You two go ashore at Low Anchor with your lives and your silence. Everybody walks away."

"Except you keep the boat," I said.

"Collateral."

"That's a hostage with paperwork."

Blackwood grinned. It was the first honest thing about him. "It is, yeah. And she's a good boat, Vale. Better than you deserve, some say." He ran his palm along my rail with an intimacy that made my jaw tighten. "I remember her. Before your scandal. She was the fastest thing on this coast, and everyone said so, and everyone also said the man who sailed her must be half part fish." He looked up at me. "Funny thing, that scandal. Funny timing. Right after your employer started cutting corners on hull inspections, right before the *Andromeda* went down with all hands but you."

The deck went very quiet. Even his crew stopped pretending to check lines.

Paloma turned to look at me. I kept my eyes on Blackwood.

"You want to finish that thought?" I said.

"No thought to finish. Just making conversation." He shrugged, vast and easy. "Point is, I know boats, and I know what losing one does to a man. So understand me when I say: cooperate, and you'll get her back eventually, probably. Don't cooperate, and the Magistrate will keep her, and I promise you he won't love her. That man breaks things he owns. Ask his wives."

One of his men snorted. Blackwood didn't smile this time.

"So." He held out the hand again. Palm up. Patient. "The map."

* * *

Here's the thing about being robbed on your own deck: you learn exactly what you're willing to bleed for.

I watched Paloma's face while Blackwood waited. She was doing the arithmetic too — I could see it running behind her eyes, fast and desperate. The map was in the waterproof roll inside her jacket, along with her father's journal, and both of them were soaked through with cove water and temple dust and everything we'd learned in the last six hours. Everything her father had died protecting. Everything Hollis had trusted to a stranger at a campfire.

And I watched her start to reach for it.

"No," I said.

Everyone looked at me. Blackwood's eyebrows went up, genuinely curious.

"Excuse me?"

"You heard me." My heart was going like a drum in a storm, but my voice came out flat, which is the trick of it — you can be terrified as long as you sound bored. "You don't get the map, Blackwood. Not because I care about treasure. Because I've seen what's down there, and so have you now, and we both know what Corvane wants it for. That's not scholarship. That's a man building himself a crown out of somebody else's god."

Blackwood studied me for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the cove mouth, Corvane's ship fired up a generator, a thin mechanical whine rolling across the water.

"You're a romantic," Blackwood said finally. "That's the saddest thing I've heard all year, and I heard a lot of sad things working for the Magistrate." He sighed and stood, and the deck seemed to tilt toward his weight. "Vale. Paloma. Let me explain the situation in terms a pilot can chart."

He pointed at the Mercy.

"This boat is worth maybe four hundred crowns in her current condition, and she's leaking, and I've got men who can sail her anywhere I tell them to. She is also, currently, the only thing keeping either of you afloat in a cove surrounded by cliff, in the teeth of a building monsoon, with a hostile ship between you and home." He ticked the points off on thick fingers. "Now. The Magistrate gave me simple instructions: retrieve the map, retrieve the divers, deliver both intact. *Intact*, he said, because apparently dead archaeologists make poor witnesses. But instructions have flexibility. Accidents happen at sea. Boats leak. People fall. Weather comes."

He let that sit.

"So here's my last offer, and it's better than you deserve. Give me the map and the journal. Come aboard Corvane's ship tonight, eat a hot meal, sleep in a dry bunk. In the morning the Magistrate puts you ashore at Low Anchor with your silence bought and paid for, and — this is the part I'd listen to, Vale — I put in a word about your pardon. Your reinstatement. The *Andromeda* business cleared off your name. You'd pilot again. Legitimate charters. Your own boat, even, someday, if you play it straight."

I felt Paloma go still beside me. Not tempted — I'd seen her tempted, at the offer of dry clothes and answers, and this wasn't that. This was the stillness of a woman watching a trap being described in loving detail.

"You'd do that," I said. "Give up the boat, take the pardon."

"I'd do it in a heartbeat," Blackwood said. "Because the boat's just wood and canvas, Vale, and the pardon is forever. Only a fool trades forever for wood."

"Only a man who's never loved a boat says that."

Something moved behind Blackwood's eyes — not anger, something older and tireder. For half a second I thought I'd reached him. Then it closed over, smooth as water over a sinking stone.

"The map," he said again. "Last time I ask nice."

* * *

What happened next, I want to be clear, was not planned. I'm a decent liar but a terrible improviser, and what came out of my mouth surprised me most of all.

"The map's not on the boat," I said.

Blackwood's patience thinned visibly. "Where is it, then?"

"In the temple."

Silence. One of his men laughed, then stopped laughing when nobody joined him.

"Think about it," I said, warming to it, lying the way I used to lie to harbor inspectors — fast, specific, technically verifiable. "Waterproof roll, weighted, sunk in the shrine chamber. You think we'd carry the only copy of the only map onto an open deck with your crew crawling all over it? We dove it down this morning before you arrived. Coordinates are in my head. The roll is in a stone niche forty feet down, in a chamber that floods at high tide, and the tide's coming in." I checked the sky theatrically. "You've got maybe three hours before that entrance channel closes for the day."

Blackwood stared at me. I stared back with the serene confidence of a man who has absolutely buried a priceless artifact in a drowning cave, which was true in spirit if not in fact — the roll was currently pressed flat against Paloma's ribs under a blanket, and had been the entire conversation.

"You're bluffing," he said.

"Probably. Want to bet your Magistrate's goodwill on it?"

He turned to Paloma. "Is he bluffing?"

She looked at him with wide, exhausted, utterly convincing eyes. "He's insane," she said. "But he's usually right about tides."

Another silence. Out on the water, the light was starting to go bronze and long, and the swell at the cove mouth had picked up a new voice — deeper, meaner, the monsoon announcing itself from beyond the horizon like thunder clearing its throat.

Blackwood rubbed his jaw. I watched him weigh it: send a diver down now, on his own gear, into an unknown flooded temple on a stranger's word, with the tide running and his employer waiting — or call my bluff and risk the map staying lost, and having to explain to Corvane how the salvage captain lost a piece of paper to a disgraced pilot.

Oren Blackwood was many things, but he hadn't risen to command Corvane's salvage fleet by gambling when he could wait.

"Fine," he said at last. "Then nobody goes anywhere. You two stay aboard — my men will make sure of it. At slack water tomorrow morning, you'll dive down and fetch that roll yourself, with my diver on your tether, and if there's no map in it, the Magistrate will find out exactly how much of your boat floats." He jerked his chin at his crew. "Lock them in the cabin. Post two on deck. Nobody touches the wheel but me."

"And the boat?" I asked.

"Is collateral," Blackwood said, climbing back toward the helm, "until tomorrow proves whether you're a liar or just stupid. Frankly, Vale, I'm hoping for stupid. Liars I have to watch. Stupid I can work with."

They shoved us into my own cabin and barred the door from outside, and I heard the bolt slide home with a sound like a full stop.

* * *

The cabin was dark, close, smelling of our own lives — Paloma's charts, my father's brass compass on its hook, two mugs in the sink from a breakfast that belonged to different people. Through the little port I could see Corvane's ship riding the swell beyond the cove mouth, lights coming on along her deck as evening fell, patient and enormous.

Paloma sat on the berth and pulled the waterproof roll out from under the blanket, holding it in both hands like something rescued from a fire.

"That was the stupidest brave thing I've ever seen," she whispered.

"Thank you. I practice."

"Idris." Her voice cracked on it. "He's going to figure it out. Tomorrow morning, when there's no map in that niche —"

"There will be."

She looked up.

"We dive tonight," I said. "Through the cabin port, swim the hull, take the tender. Or we dive down and move the roll to a niche they'll never find, and tomorrow I hand Blackwood an empty waterproof bag and watch his face. Either way, we're not waiting for his schedule. Tide turns at two in the morning. Slack water. Their sentries will be cold, bored, and watching the cove mouth — because that's where the threat is supposed to come from." I tapped the port glass, out at Corvane's lights. "Nobody watches the water underneath them. That's the whole history of drowning, Paloma. Nobody ever watches the water underneath."

She was quiet a moment. Outside, the first real wind of the monsoon found the cove and began testing the Mercy's rigging note by note, like a musician tuning up before a performance.

"They'll take the boat if we're caught," she said.

"They'll take the boat anyway. They've taken it now." I sat down beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and looked at the compass on its hook — my father's, pointing its stubborn north at a wall instead of the sea. "Everything we love is leverage tonight. The boat. The map. Your father's name. Mine. The only question left is what we do with the hours before they turn all of it against us."

Paloma looked down at the roll in her hands, then at the journal beside it, her father's handwriting ghosting through the wet pages. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — steadier, colder, the voice of someone who had stopped calculating escape and started choosing ground.

"Then we don't run," she said. "There's something in the journal I didn't show you. Something my father wrote about the flood-channels. About what the temple was *built* to do." She met my eyes, and in the dim cabin light I saw the shape of the next twenty-four hours arrive all at once, enormous and unavoidable. "If Corvane wants what's down there, Idris, we need to be the ones who decide whether he ever gets it. Tonight. Before the tide turns."

Beyond the hull, the monsoon wind rose another notch, and somewhere out in the dark, Corvane's ship answered with a single blast of her horn — long, low, and certain, like a man clearing his throat before he speaks.
