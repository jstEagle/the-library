## 16. The Price of Power

They brought me aboard the *Corvane's Grace* with a rope around my wrists and a hand on my collar, as if I were cargo that might bolt over the side. Which, to be fair, I was considering.

The magistrate's flagship made the *Mercy* look like what she was — a working boat held together by prayer and paint. The *Grace* was white-hulled and gleaming, brass fittings polished to a shine you could check your teeth in, and everything about her said: *I have money, and I want you to know it before I say a word.* Even the gangway had been scrubbed. Even the sailors looked laundered.

Idris came up beside me, hands bound too, though he walked like a man being escorted rather than captured. There's a difference. Captives hunch. Idris rolled his shoulders back like the rope was an insult he intended to survive out of spite.

"Don't say anything clever," he murmured.

"That's your job?"

"My job is keeping us alive. Cleverness is how people die on boats."

"Noted." I'd already thought of six clever things. I filed them away like contraband.

* * *

They took us below, to a cabin that was less a cabin than a statement. Mahogany paneling, charts framed like paintings, a decanter of something amber sitting on a tray that probably cost more than the *Mercy*. And at the center of it, behind a desk built from a single slab of dark wood, sat Magistrate Corvane.

I'd imagined him a hundred ways since Port Amara — fat and sweating, or cruel and twitchy, some cartoon villain twirling his mustache over stolen artifacts. What I got instead was a man in his fifties with silver at his temples and reading glasses pushed up into his hair, dressed not in uniform but in plain linen, the way powerful men dress when they want you to forget they're powerful. He had kind eyes. That was the worst part. He had genuinely, sincerely kind eyes, and I understood immediately that they meant nothing, the way a lure means nothing to the shape of the fish it imitates.

"Miss Pemberton," he said, and my name landed in the room like a dropped anchor. "Please sit."

"I'll stand."

"Of course." He gestured anyway, and one of his men pressed me into a chair regardless. Corvane watched this happen without expression, which told me everything: the courtesy was decoration; the force was structure. "You've had a long swim. Some tea, perhaps?"

"I've had better hospitality from jellyfish."

Idris made a small sound beside me, half cough, half prayer.

Corvane smiled like I'd passed a test. "Your father had the same mouth on him."

The words went through me sideways. Not the fact of them — I'd known, since the journal, since the camp in the cliffs, that my father had stood in this man's orbit — but the ease of them. Corvane deployed my father's memory the way other men deployed knives.

"You knew him," I said.

"I tried to." He poured two glasses of the amber liquor and set one in front of me uninvited. "Edmund Pemberton was the finest field archaeologist I ever encountered, and the most stubborn man I ever failed to buy. I offered him everything. Funding, ships, a permanent post, his name on every discovery for the rest of his life. He asked me one question in return." Corvane removed his glasses and cleaned them on his sleeve, unhurried, a man who owned time itself. "He asked me what I intended to *do* with what we found. And when I answered honestly, he laughed at me. Actually laughed. Then he walked off my ship in a tender and vanished into the fog, and I spent three years learning where he'd gone."

"And now you know."

"Now I know." He set the glasses down and folded his hands, and here it came — the civilized face settling into place like a mask fitted by a good tailor. "Do you know what your father found down there, Miss Pemberton? Do you understand what's sleeping under Cape Reckoning?"

"A temple. A civilization that measured wealth in giving."

"So he told you." Something flickered across his face — envy, maybe, or grief wearing envy's clothes. "Yes. A gift economy. Three thousand years old. A people who built their entire order around the principle that power flows *from* what you surrender, not what you seize. Their kings were elected annually and required to give away their fortunes to keep the crown. Their temples stored not gold but *obligations* — carved records of every debt of gratitude between families, generations deep. A ledger of kindness." He shook his head slowly, admiring it, the way a butcher might admire a living animal. "Remarkable. Utopian. And utterly useless to anyone who wants to *govern*."

"There it is," I said. "I was wondering when the mask would slip."

"It hasn't slipped. It's been off this whole conversation; you simply weren't looking." He leaned forward, and the lamplight caught his face at an angle that showed me the lines around his eyes weren't from smiling. "You think I'm a tomb robber. Blackwood certainly thinks so — he's simple enough to want the gold. But I don't want the gold. Gold spends. Gold runs out. What I want is in those shrine halls. The regalia. The records. The *symbols* of a civilization that ruled itself for eight hundred years through consent and gratitude. Do you understand what that would mean in my hands?"

"Loot with better branding?"

"It would mean legitimacy." The word came out of him quiet and reverent, and that was the frightening thing — he wasn't performing. He believed this. Somewhere inside the corruption there was a cathedral, and he prayed in it daily. "This coast is a patchwork of fishing villages and smugglers' coves held together by my patience and my gunboats. Every year I hold it costs me more. But if I return from Cape Reckoning bearing the regalia of the Giver-Kings — if I stand in the harbor at Port Amara crowned in the symbols of the coast's own ancient covenant — then I am no longer a magistrate appointed by a distant capital. I am the *restored heir* of something older. The villages won't need garrisons. They'll kneel because kneeling will feel like memory." He spread his hands. "I don't want to steal their past, Miss Pemberton. I want to *become* it."

"And my father said no to this."

"Your father said—" Corvane laughed, short and genuine, and for one strange moment he sounded almost fond. "Your father called it 'the most articulate tyranny I've ever been offered.' He said a gift economy dies the moment someone owns its gifts. He was right, of course. That's why he had to be managed rather than persuaded."

"*Managed.*"

"He's gone now. Let's speak of the living." He rose and came around the desk, and his men tensed, and he waved them off with irritation, as if even his own muscle was an impediment to the performance. He stopped in front of my chair. Up close he smelled of cedar and ink. "Here is what I offer you, and I offer it once. Your boat, your pilot, your freedom, full pardons all around. In exchange: you help me read the shrine records. You're your father's daughter; you can do it. And when I am crowned in the old covenant, you take your place beside me as keeper of the archive. Scholar. Advisor. The woman who gave the coast back its own history." His voice softened into something almost gentle. "You've spent three years drifting, Miss Pemberton. I'm offering you a purpose with marble under it."

And here's the thing I'll never tell Idris, or anyone: for about four seconds, I wanted it.

Not the crown part. Not the gunboats. But *purpose with marble under it* — a desk, a calling, a reason my father's blood in me meant something besides restlessness. Four seconds of standing in a warm lit room while the monsoon hammered the deck above, being seen by a man who had read my whole life in a file, being offered a shape for all the formless wanting I'd carried since I walked out on the last man who'd offered me one.

Then I thought of Hollis dying at my fire with a grease-stained map pressed into his hands like a sacrament. *Carry it somewhere safe. Tell no one.* A promise made too easily and paid for in full. Hollis hadn't given me that map so a magistrate could wear it as a hat.

Then I thought of my father, laughing at this man, walking off into fog toward a drowned temple and a death he chose over this chair. He hadn't left me a journal wedged in a shrine so I could curate a tyrant's costume collection.

"No," I said.

Corvane sighed, and it was the sigh of a man closing a ledger. "I confess I hoped for more resistance. Resistance is useful. It gives the story a villain." He returned to his desk and rang a small bell, and the guards reappeared. "Blackwood believes you have a map. I no longer require it — I have your dive site, your entrance arch, and your cooperation is optional. But the map has annotations in Edmund's hand that exist nowhere else, and sentiment makes me thorough. So you'll provide it, or Mr. Vale will spend the night reconsidering his loyalties in a hold that floods at high tide. I'm told he's had experience with sinking ships. It would be poetic."

My stomach dropped through the hull.

"The map," Corvane said pleasantly, "or the pilot. You have until the tide turns."

* * *

They kept us apart after that — Idris marched aft, me locked in a guest cabin that was luxurious the way a display case is luxurious. Beautiful wood, a real bed, a porthole showing nothing but rain. A gilded box with a lock on the outside.

I sat on the bed and did arithmetic.

The map was hidden inside my father's letter, sewn into the lining of my jacket, which was currently in a barrel of confiscated gear on deck, which meant it was either safe or already in Blackwood's meaty hands. If it was found, Corvane got everything and Idris drowned on schedule. If it wasn't found, I had until slack water to figure out how a woman with bound wrists and no boat negotiated with a man who owned the weather.

There was a third option. There's always a third option; my father taught me that much, at least, before I stopped listening to him. The temple had flood-channels — I'd seen the channels myself, down in the dark, great stone throats cut into the bedrock that the old engineers had used to raise and lower the water level with the tides. My father's journal had diagrams. I'd skimmed them by lantern light in the cliff camp, thinking they were academic.

They weren't academic. Nothing my father ever drew was academic.

If the channels could be opened deliberately — if the sea could be invited in on our schedule instead of the tide's — then the vault, the shrines, the regalia Corvane wanted to wear like a borrowed soul... all of it could go to the water. Permanently. Sealed. Sacred in the only way that word still worked: buried.

It would kill whoever was inside when it happened.

I turned that over the way you turn a knife, examining the edge. Corvane. Blackwood. Their divers. Maybe Idris. Maybe me. Everyone in the dark when the ocean came home.

A civilized man had offered me marble and I'd said no. Now the alternative was arithmetic with bodies in it, and I hated that my father's handwriting had led me here, hated that the last lesson he'd leave me was a choice between a tyrant's crown and a drowning.

Through the porthole, the rain came sideways. Beyond it, faint under the storm, I could just make out the black shoulder of Cape Reckoning, and beneath it, somewhere, the green-lit halls where my father had drawn his last diagrams and written his last words: *some things must stay buried to stay sacred.*

He'd known. Of course he'd known. He'd stood in Corvane's cabin years ago and seen this exact moment coming, and he'd left me the maps and the channels and the terrible math, trusting me to do what he couldn't ask.

The lock rattled. Boots in the passage. A key turning.

The door opened, and Oren Blackwood filled it, holding my jacket by the collar like a dead seagull, and grinning like a man who'd found the letter.

"Well, well," he said. "Little bird's been sewing secrets into her feathers."
