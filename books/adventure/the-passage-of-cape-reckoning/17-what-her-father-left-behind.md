## 17. What Her Father Left Behind

The journal said *north face, above the tide line, where the rock wears a crown of salt.*

My father wrote like a man who assumed the world was listening and might be persuaded. Even his directions were half poetry, half field notes, as if he couldn't decide whether he was leaving instructions or composing an epitaph. Which, it turned out, he was.

Idris wanted to come with me. I could see it in the way he stood at the rail of the Mercy, arms crossed, watching me coil rope with more attention than the task deserved.

"You shouldn't go alone," he said.

"I went alone for three years," I said. "It's the one skill I've kept sharp."

"That's not the flex you think it is."

"Everything's a flex if you commit."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't. "Paloma. Blackwood's people are on that water. Corvane's ship is sitting out there like a fat black spider waiting for something to land in its web. You're going to walk up into cliffs your father knew better than anyone, following a dead man's handwriting, and I'm supposed to stand here and *wait*?"

"Yes," I said. "Because if they're watching this cove, two people climbing is a story. One person gathering firewood is Tuesday." I slung the pack over my shoulder — journal, knife, water skin, and the letter I still hadn't opened, riding against my spine like a small warm stone. "Keep the engine warm. If I'm not back by dark, be gone by dawn."

"And do what?"

I didn't have an answer for that. Neither did he. We let the silence hold it, which was becoming our habit — letting silences carry what neither of us had learned to say out loud yet.

"Firewood," I repeated, and started up the scree.

* * *

The north face of Cape Reckoning does not so much rise as *refuse*. The rock goes up in shelves and ledges, black basalt streaked white with guano and salt, and the wind comes off the strait with the specific intention of removing you from it. I climbed the way my father had taught me when I was nine and furious about it — three points of contact, test every hold, trust nothing that looks generous.

*Trust nothing that looks generous.* God. He'd been writing my biography since before I was born.

I found the first marker an hour up: a cairn no bigger than a fist, wedged into a crack, topped with a piece of driftwood bleached bone-white. It was the kind of thing you'd never see unless you were looking, and I only saw it because the journal had told me to look — *the old builders marked their pilgrim paths, and I have followed their habit.* My father, taking lessons in secrecy from a civilization four thousand years drowned. There was something funny in that, and something that made my throat hurt, and I'd learned by now those were usually the same thing.

The cairns led me along a ledge system that no goat would choose, around a shoulder of cliff, and then — abruptly, the way the coast does everything — into shelter. A fold in the rock, a hollow maybe twenty feet deep, walled on three sides, floored with fine pale sand that had blown in over years and never left. Out of the wind entirely. From its mouth you could see the whole cove below: the Mercy at anchor, the black shape of Corvane's ship beyond her, the collapsed sea arch and the dark water over the temple like a lid on a pot someone was always threatening to lift.

He'd been able to watch all of it. Every day. For however long he'd lived up here.

The camp was still there.

Not intact — time and weather had done their patient work — but there. A lean-to frame of driftwood and salvaged line, sagging. A fire ring with ash gone to cement. Two crates stenciled with the logo of the Amara Institute of Antiquities, which meant he'd planned this expedition officially before it became unofficial, before whatever happened happened. And against the back wall, wrapped in oilcloth tied with a knot I recognized because he'd taught it to me alongside the climbing —

A sea chest.

I stood there longer than I should have. Long enough for the wind to change twice. Because standing in front of that chest was standing in front of the last three years, and the last three years were a door I'd nailed shut from my side, and now here was the handle.

"You walked out," I said to the empty camp, to him, to nobody. "You don't get to leave me homework."

But my hands were already working the knot.

Inside: a brass lamp, dry matches in a tin, a coil of good rope. A tin plate and cup. A second journal — thinner, the binding cracked, the cover warped with damp that had gotten in despite everything. And beneath it, flat and wrapped in waxed canvas, a bundle of papers, and on top of the bundle, in handwriting I would have known blindfolded, upside down, underwater:

*For Paloma. If she ever comes. She'll come stubborn.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and I sat down hard in the sand with the bundle in my lap like it weighed forty pounds instead of two.

*She'll come stubborn.* He'd known. He'd sat in this hole in the rock, watching the cove, watching the water that held his life's work, and he'd written *she'll come stubborn* the way other men write *hopefully.* Not hope. Certainty. About me. The one thing we'd never managed to give each other while he was alive to spend it.

I opened the last journal first. Cowardice and courage wearing each other's clothes, as usual.

* * *

The entries began eleven months before his disappearance. I read them by the light through the camp mouth, hunched over pages gone soft at the edges, and the man who emerged from them was not the man I'd walked out on.

That man had been granite. Certain. A voice like a lecture hall even at dinner, even at Christmas, even — especially — when I'd told him I wasn't coming on the Cape expedition, that I was done being the junior assistant to his obsession, done measuring my worth in artifacts catalogued. He'd looked at me like I'd announced I was done breathing. He hadn't said anything cruel. He'd said something worse: *"You'll understand later."*

The man in this journal doubted everything.

*Day 14. The carvings in the lower gallery contradict the gift-economy model as I published it. Either the civilization gave away wealth, or it gave away power, and I am no longer sure those are different things. I have been wrong in print for fifteen years and called it scholarship.*

*Day 31. Met a prospector named Hollis at Low Anchor. Half mad, wholly honest, which is rarer than either virtue alone. He has a map. Old grease-stained thing. Says it came down to him from a diver who never came back up. I did not tell him what I think it shows. Some knowledge is a burden best carried by few.*

Hollis. The dying man who'd put that same map in my hands on a beach three hundred miles south. The circle closing so quietly I hadn't heard it start.

*Day 40. Visitors. A salvage vessel flying port colors, asking questions at the village. Their captain's name is Blackwood. They are not scholars. I watched them buy information from a boy with fish money and understood the shape of what's coming. Corvane wants the temple. Not the truth of it — the *use* of it. A drowned people who measured greatness in giving, repurposed by a man who measures everything in taking. There is an irony there the old builders would have appreciated.*

*Day 52. I found the flood-channels today. The builders engineered them deliberately — sluices cut into the bedrock, sealed with stone plugs, designed to drown their own sanctuary rather than let it be taken. They built a temple with a self-destruct and called it devotion. I have spent my life studying them and I am only beginning to understand them.*

*Day 60. Corvane's offer came through an intermediary, as such things do. Reinstatement. Funding. A ship. Everything I lost when the Institute cut me loose — in exchange for the location, and my silence about the channels. I told him what I told my daughter: some things must stay buried to stay sacred. He did not find it amusing. Neither, I think, did she.*

I stopped reading. Put the journal facedown in the sand. Looked at the cove, at the water, at nothing.

He'd quoted me. In his secret camp, alone, hunted by a magistrate, he'd quoted the words I'd thrown at him across a kitchen table the night I left — words I'd meant as a weapon, *your precious buried things, Dad, some things must stay buried*, flung at his obsession — and he'd carried them up a cliff and made them true. Made them armor. Made them the reason he died.

I picked the journal back up. My hands weren't steady. I read anyway.

*Day 61. They know I'm here. I moved the camp higher. The channels are the whole game now. As long as the plugs hold, the vault can be reached — and it will be reached, by them, if not by me. But if the plugs can be pulled... the sea will keep what the sea was given. I have been thinking about my daughter again. I have been thinking about her constantly, which is new, and I suspect it is what happens to a man when he finally understands that understanding-later is a debt you may not live to pay.*

*Day 63. Last entry, I think. Blackwood's divers found the arch. I have one move left and it is not a clever one. I am going to pull the outer plug myself and collapse the approach tunnel. It will seal the upper galleries — not forever, nothing is forever, the sea sees to that — but for years. Long enough. Long enough for the right people to find it, or for no one to, which the builders would say amounts to the same thing.*

*I am too old for the swim back through the surge channel and I know it. So. Final inventory: one theory, vindicated. One temple, defended. One daughter, unforgiven by herself if not by me — Paloma, if these words ever reach you, hear this part clearly: there was nothing to forgive. You were right to leave. I was wrong to make leaving feel like betrayal. That was my excavation, and I dug it deep, and I am sorry I made you climb down after me.*

*Some things must stay buried to stay sacred. You taught me that. I only wish I'd learned it while you were still shouting it across the table.*

*Come stubborn. Come safe. Come home to the water, if nowhere else.*

*— Your father, who measured his wealth in what he gave away, and gave it all to you.*

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. The sun moved. The wind changed. Somewhere below, gulls argued over something neither would share. I cried the way you cry when you've been carrying something so long you've forgotten its weight until you set it down — ugly, graceless, alone in a hole in a cliff, laughing once in the middle of it because *come stubborn* was the most him thing anyone had ever written and I hated him for it and I loved him for it and both of those were finally allowed to be true at the same time.

Three years. Three years of miles, of dock work and deck work and sleeping in places with names instead of homes, of telling myself freedom felt like this, of the unopened letter riding in my kit like ballast I refused to jettison because jettisoning it would mean admitting it mattered. And all that time he'd been up here, fifty feet above the sea, quoting me back to myself, dying to keep a promise to a civilization that couldn't thank him, and writing my name in the margin of his last days.

I unwrapped the rest of the bundle. Under the journal: charts, annotated in his hand. A copy of the original map — Hollis's map, the grease stains reproduced in careful pencil. And a single sheet, folded twice, addressed in the same steady script: *Paloma.*

Not the letter in my kit. Another one. Newer. Written up here, in this sand-floored hollow, by a man who knew exactly how many days he had left and spent one of them on me.

I held it for a long time.

Then I folded it away, unopened, next to the first one. Not because I didn't want to read it. Because I suddenly, fiercely did want to — and I wanted to read it in the right place, at the right time, with the right person waiting below, and I wanted it to be a beginning instead of an ending. He'd waited three years to be heard. He could wait one more day. Some things, it turns out, are worth doing properly.

I took the journals, the charts, the lamp. I rebuilt the cairn at the camp mouth and added a second stone beside it, the way the old builders marked a path someone had walked and would walk again.

And then I looked down at the cove, and the laugh died in my chest.

Corvane's longboat was in the water. Four oars, pulling steady, making directly for the Mercy — and Idris was alone aboard her, and the distance between the ship and my boat was closing with the calm, unhurried certainty of a thing that has already decided how it ends.

I was moving before I finished being afraid, down the ledge path with the journals hammering against my back, running toward the water with my father's last words strapped between my shoulder blades — *some things must stay buried* — and the sudden, sickening understanding that Corvane had just decided to start digging.
