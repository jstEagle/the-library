## 18. The Choice Below

There are two kinds of fear, I've learned. The kind that makes you fast — the kind that gets a man through a collapsing swell or a burning engine room — and the kind that makes you slow. Deliberate. The kind that sits in your chest like ballast and makes every movement feel like it's happening underwater.

The second kind had me by the throat the moment Corvane's divers clipped our harnesses to his guide line and pointed down.

"Stay close to me," I told Paloma, my voice a strange flattened thing through the regulator. "If anything goes wrong, you go up. You don't wait. You don't look for me. You go *up*."

Her eyes behind the mask were furious and bright. She didn't answer, which meant she'd heard me and intended to do the exact opposite. Three weeks of knowing this woman had taught me her silences better than most people's speeches.

* * *

The descent through the temple's throat was a journey through a drowned cathedral. Corvane's crew had strung work lights along the entrance passage, and they turned the water into a green-gold haze, shadows swimming at the edges of everything. The walls closed in around us, carved with processions of figures — I'd seen them on the way in, an hour ago, when this had still been an archaeological site instead of a robbery.

Now we were forty feet down, moving through halls that had waited three thousand years for the sea, and Magistrate Aldous Corvane was swimming among them like a man touring his future palace.

He'd brought eight divers. Two were his personal guard — big men with rebreathers and knives they made no effort to hide. The rest were salvage hands from Blackwood's crew, and I'll say this for Blackwood: his people looked ashamed. All except the man himself, who swam at the rear of the column with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like the answer to.

Corvane himself moved through the water with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once been told no by the ocean. He'd hired a diving instructor, apparently, sometime in the last two days. Money does that. Money buys you the sea.

The central vault lay at the heart of the complex, past a gallery of pillars and through a doorway that had been sealed with a stone slab — until Corvane's crew had levered it open that morning. The chamber beyond was round, maybe sixty feet across, its domed ceiling lost in darkness above the reach of the lights. And along its walls, in recessed niches carved like open hands, sat the treasure.

I'm not a man who cares much for gold. It's heavy, it's soft, and it buys you boats that sink. But even I stopped moving when the lights swept across that chamber.

Vessels. Crowns. Blades with hilts of woven wire. Tablets dense with script. And everywhere, everywhere, the same motif: a figure with open palms, giving something away. The giving figure. The civilization that had measured wealth in what it surrendered — Paloma's father's theory, carved in stone by people who'd been dead three millennia and had apparently meant it.

Corvane hung in the middle of the chamber, turning slowly, and even through the mask I could see the expression on his face. It wasn't wonder. Wonder would have been almost forgivable. It was appetite. A man looking at a warehouse.

"Load the transport sleds," he said, and his voice came through the comm units crackling and intimate, a father's voice at bedtime. "Start with the tablets. The metalwork after. We have the window until the tide turns, and I intend to use all of it."

* * *

Here's what nobody in that chamber knew except me — and, a few feet away, the woman whose face I could read even through neoprene and glass.

The night before, anchored in the cove with Blackwood's men swaggering across the Mercy's deck, Paloma had sat beside me in the dark of the hold with her father's journal open on her knees, and she'd been tracing the temple's floor plan with one finger. Over and over. The same circuit.

"What is it?" I'd asked.

"Channels," she'd said. "The builders cut flood-channels through the whole complex. My father thought they were ceremonial — the temple was designed to be flooded and drained, seasonally, like a tide pool. A ritual of giving the site back to the sea." Her finger had stopped on the vault. "But look. The channels converge here. Under the vault floor. There are gates — stone gates, counterweighted, the same mechanism as the entrance slab. They're meant to be opened from inside."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning this temple isn't just *in* the water, Idris. It's a machine for letting the water in. And someone standing in the vault could open every gate at once."

I'd looked at her for a long moment. "That would drown the vault."

"That would drown the vault," she'd agreed quietly. "And everyone in it."

"And you're telling me this because—"

"Because I need you to know. In case it comes to that." She'd closed the journal. "In case *I* decide it comes to that."

I hadn't slept after that. I'd lain in the dark listening to Blackwood's men playing cards on my deck — *my* deck, the bastards — and I'd thought about the shape of the choice she was carrying. And I'd realized something that frightened me more than the drowning did: I already knew what she'd choose. I'd known since Low Anchor, since the way she'd held the old fisherwoman's hands. Paloma Pemberton was her father's daughter, and her father had died rather than sell this place.

The only question was whether I could live in the same current she was about to swim in.

* * *

Now we were in the vault, and the sleds were filling, and the arithmetic was getting worse by the minute.

I watched her work. That's what I did for the first hour — watched her, because watching her was the only useful thing I was allowed. Corvane had assigned her to catalog the finds. "You have the scholarship," he'd told her, with that smile of his that made you want to check your pockets. "Consider it a family inheritance, Miss Pemberton. Your father located this site. You get to name its contents."

He'd said it kindly. That was the worst of it. Corvane's cruelty always came dressed as generosity, and half his victims thanked him on the way to the gallows.

Paloma catalogued. She handled each artifact with a gentleness that made my chest hurt, recording descriptions in a waterproof slate book, and I knew — I *knew* — that she wasn't recording for Corvane. She was recording for the record. For whoever came after. So that if the temple vanished, the knowing of it wouldn't vanish with it. That's what her father would have done. That's what she was doing, in the belly of the thing, while men with knives stacked three thousand years of grace onto salvage sleds.

And all the while, her eyes were moving. Casual glances. The recesses in the walls. The floor joints. The dark slot at the chamber's far side where the counterweight channel ran — I'd spotted it myself, a vertical fissure with a stone lip worn smooth, and I'd watched her find it too, watched her look away from it too quickly, and my stomach had dropped through the floor of the world.

Because I'd seen the other thing. The thing she hadn't seen yet, or had seen and hadn't weighed.

The vault had one exit. The doorway we'd come through. If the gates opened, the water wouldn't come in gently. It would come in like a verdict. The chamber would fill in seconds — I'd felt the pressure in the walls on the way down, the whole complex holding its breath — and everyone inside would be slammed against stone by a current no diver could fight. Corvane's men. Blackwood's crew. Her.

Me.

* * *

It went wrong the way things go wrong — slowly, then all at once.

A salvage hand named Petros — young, nervous, hands that shook — fumbled a tablet into its cradle on the sled and it slipped. Stone hit stone with a sound like a struck bell, and the whole chamber seemed to listen.

Corvane didn't shout. He never shouted. He swam over, took the boy's harness in one fist, and held him there, face to face, for a long ten seconds.

"Care," he said, "is a form of respect. I expect respect in my house." Then he let him go and turned, and his eyes found Paloma across the chamber.

"You've been looking at the walls, Miss Pemberton."

The water went very still. Even the work lights seemed to dim.

"I'm cataloguing," she said.

"You're cataloguing *and* you've been looking at the walls. The channels." He drifted closer, unhurried, a man closing a ledger. "I've read your father's journal. The one you so helpfully recovered. Did you know he made the same discovery? The flood mechanism. He stood where you're standing and worked out the same arithmetic." He tilted his head. "And do you know what he said when I offered him a place at my side? He said, *some things must stay buried to stay sacred.*" Corvane's voice softened with something that might have been genuine regret. "Sentiment. It's a disease in men of learning. I'd hoped you'd inherited his mind without his affliction."

"And if I didn't?" Paloma said. Her voice was steady. I marveled at it. I was holding my regulator with both hands and she sounded like she was ordering tea.

"Then I'll have you surface with the first sled load, and you'll stay on the boat, and my people will finish the work without the distraction of your conscience." He smiled. "I'm not a monster, Miss Pemberton. I'm a builder. Monsters break things. I *use* them."

He turned away. Dismissed. And in that moment — in the half-second of his turned back — Paloma looked at me.

I've been a lot of things in my life. Pilot, coward, scapegoat, drunk, and once, briefly, a man who told the truth at the right time and ruined himself for it. But I had never been looked at the way she looked at me just then. It wasn't a plea. She wasn't asking permission. She was telling me the time had come, and asking me one question only: *are you with me?*

And here's the thing about love. I'd spent three weeks refusing to call it that, because calling it that felt like painting a target on it. But there in the drowned vault, with Corvane's men hauling a civilization into crates, I finally understood that love isn't a feeling you have. It's a current you swim in. You don't get to choose the water. You only choose whether you fight it or let it carry you.

I nodded. Once.

* * *

What happened next happened in the space of about ninety seconds, and I'll tell it honestly, including the parts that shame me, because a man who won't tell those parts is a man who's still lying.

Paloma drifted toward the counterweight channel. Casual. Cataloguing. She'd left her slate book on a sled — deliberately, I realized later, so her hands would be free and her attention would look idle. I positioned myself between her and the nearest of Corvane's guards, a big man called Rennick, and I did the one thing I'm actually good at: I talked.

"Rennick," I said on the comm, pitching it low and easy, "that sled's rigged wrong. The lift points are going to shear the cradle when you bring it up the shaft. Look at the starboard strap."

It was even true — I'd noticed it on the way down; sloppy rigging was the story of Blackwood's whole operation — and Rennick was enough of a salvage man that he couldn't *not* look. He swam to the sled. I drifted after him, pointing, complaining, being the annoying expert. Behind us, at the chamber's edge, Paloma's hands found the stone lip of the counterweight channel.

Then Petros ruined everything, because Petros was the only honest man in Corvane's crew. He looked up from his sled, across the chamber, and saw her.

"Magistrate—" he started.

And Paloma pulled.

I heard it more than saw it. A deep grinding note through the stone, felt in the teeth and the chest, the sound of a three-thousand-year-old mechanism waking up angry. Somewhere below and around us, gates that had been closed since before the first pharaoh were swinging open, and the sea — patient, enormous, owed this for thirty centuries — began to collect.

The water moved. That's the only way I can say it. It didn't rush in; it *decided*, all at once, the way a wave decides, and every light in the chamber swung on their lines like hanged men.

"OUT!" I screamed into the comm. "Everybody out, NOW, she's flooding—"

What Corvane did, I'll grant him this: he didn't freeze. He grabbed the nearest sled line and hauled for the doorway, shouting his men after him, because Corvane was many rotten things but he was not a man who died for anyone's convenience. Rennick bolted. Blackwood grabbed Petros. The chamber emptied toward the exit in a tangle of harnesses and bubbles and greed.

Paloma didn't move.

She was still at the channel, both hands on the counterweight, driving it home, and the current was already taking the chamber apart around her — sleds lifting, tablets tumbling, the work lights dying one by one like a city going dark. I looked at the doorway. The doorway was thirty feet away and filling with bodies. I looked at her.

I'd told her on the descent: if anything goes wrong, you go up, you don't look for me.

She'd heard me. She'd intended the exact opposite. And God help me, so had I.

I swam *in*.

The current hit me like a door slammed by a giant. It took my fins sideways, dragged me across the chamber floor, and I let it — rode it, the way you ride the Storm Gate, not fighting the water but reading it, finding the seam where it ran slack along the wall. My shoulder hit stone. I reached blind, caught a harness strap, hauled — her, she was *her*, she was fighting me, actually fighting me, pointing back at the channel, at the gates, at the work not finished — and I put my face an inch from her mask and shouted through the comm, through the water, through everything:

"You've done it. It's DONE. Now we live, and we live *together*, and that's the whole job — HOLD ON TO ME—"

And she stopped fighting.

Whether it was the words or the water or something in my face she finally believed, I'll never know. But her hand closed on my harness and the chamber gave its last great shudder, and the vault — the treasure, the crowns, the tablets, the open-handed figures giving everything away — went dark behind us as the sea poured through the halls of the people who had built it to be given back.

We came up the entrance shaft in a rising column of churning green, past the last of Corvane's work lights, past the carved processions flickering by like a procession of ghosts waving goodbye. Above us, far above, the cove's surface was a hammered silver disc, and the Mercy sat at anchor on it, and Idris Vale — disgraced, drowned-hearted, half a man for eleven years — kicked for that light with everything he had, with her hand locked in his, with the whole drowned world collapsing at his heels.

Behind us, deep below, I heard the vault floor give way. A sound like the sea clearing its throat.

And then, crackling through the comm on an open channel, Corvane's voice — thin, furious, disbelieving — counting his men aloud as they surfaced, and coming up one short, and then two, and then going silent altogether as somewhere in the dark below, the man who would not let go of his treasure kept his grip all the way down.

The cove boiled around us. We broke the surface gasping, and I got an arm around Paloma, and the Mercy's rail was forty yards off through the chop, and Blackwood was already hauling people aboard, shouting names.

Paloma coughed, spat, and looked back at the cove's dark center, where the temple had gone quiet beneath us forever.

"It's done," she said. Not to me. To her father. To the sea. Maybe to herself.

"Yeah," I said, and started swimming. "Now let's see if we're done too."

Because Corvane's launch was still out there, beyond the headland, and a man like that doesn't drown his empire in one vault. He comes back for the people who cost it to him.

The first shot cracked across the water before we'd swum ten yards.
