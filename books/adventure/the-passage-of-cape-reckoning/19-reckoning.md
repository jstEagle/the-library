## 19. Reckoning

The vault door was bronze, and it was beautiful, and I hated it.

That's a strange thing to feel at the bottom of the sea, in a drowned temple lit by dive torches and the pale green ghost-light filtering down through flood-channels two thousand years old. But hate it I did. Because I knew what was behind that door — my father had told me, in ink, in his careful archaeologist's hand, in the journal I'd carried up from the shrine like it was made of glass. *The Vault of the Giving Hand.* The heart of everything he'd spent his life chasing. And now Magistrate Corvane stood in front of it with a crowbar and six armed men and the patient smile of a man who believed the world was a warehouse waiting for inventory.

"Stand back," Corvane said. He'd come down himself. Of course he had. Men like him don't send others to do their crowning.

The water in the great hall was chest-deep, still and cold as held breath. Our torch beams swung across columns carved with figures — men and women handing things to each other: grain, shells, children, fire. A civilization that measured wealth by what it gave away. My father's theory, carved in stone older than any nation on the coast, and the man about to break into its treasury thought it was a stockroom.

I hung back near the eastern wall, where the journal said the channels were. The maintenance channels, my father had called them. *The builders knew the sea would come for them eventually,* he'd written. *So they gave it doors.*

Idris drifted close to me, close enough that our shoulders touched through the water. His mask was pushed up. "Tell me you have a plan," he murmured, "and not just the look of a woman doing arithmetic."

"I'm always doing arithmetic."

"That's what frightens me."

Across the hall, Blackwood's divers had rigged lines to the bronze doors. Corvane watched them work with his hands clasped behind his back like a man at an opera. One of his men — the big one, Rask, who'd boarded us at Sable Key and put a boot through Idris's chart case — hauled on a winch line, and the first hinge shrieked through the water like something dying.

*Not yet,* I told myself. *Wait until they're committed. Wait until they're inside.* Because here was the terrible arithmetic: once those doors opened, Corvane would never leave. Not willingly. Not while there was gold in reach. And every second I waited was a second the temple held its breath around us, walls groaning under two monsoons' worth of pressure, the storm above hammering the strait closed behind us.

My father had died for this place. Not dramatically — quietly, in a cliff camp, alone, leaving me a letter I hadn't opened because opening it meant admitting he was gone. *Some things must stay buried to stay sacred.* I'd read those words three days ago and they'd felt like a burden. Standing in his life's work, watching a magistrate pry it open with hardware, they felt like instructions.

The second hinge went. The doors groaned inward, and black water — older water, denser, water that had been waiting in the dark since before anyone's grandmother was born — breathed out past us all.

Corvane laughed. Actually laughed, a short delighted bark that echoed off the columns. "Lamp," he said, and one of his men swam forward with a diver's lantern, and together they crossed the threshold into the vault.

Inside: I saw it for maybe four seconds before I turned away, and I'll be honest, part of me wanted to keep looking. Stacked along the walls, tiered like amphitheater seating, were offerings. Gold, yes — cups, masks, sheets of it beaten thin as leaf — but also things worth nothing on any market. A child's carved boat. Jars of seeds, sealed two thousand years ago. Bundles of cloth gone to lace. The whole hoard of a people whose treasure was the act of giving, catalogued and preserved so no one would ever forget what they'd been.

Corvane's lantern light swept over it and I heard him say, softly, reverently, the way other men say prayers: "There you are."

"Now," I said.

Idris didn't ask what. That's the thing about Idris Vale — he'd spent three weeks learning when not to ask. He turned, kicked hard for the entrance passage, and shouted back over his shoulder, "Everyone out! She's going!"

Rask looked up from the winch. "Going? Going wh—"

I reached the eastern wall and found it where the journal said it would be: a seam in the stone shaped like a hand, fingers spread, palm open. The Giving Hand. My father had found it too — there were fresh scrape marks beside it, months old, and a chisel left leaning in a crevice. He'd stood exactly where I was standing. He'd put his hand exactly where I was putting mine.

The stone was warm. That should have been impossible, forty feet under the sea, but the temple sat on a vent field — Idris had noticed the shimmer in the water on our descent, the way heat bled up through cracks in the floor — and the builders had used it. Geothermal pressure, my father wrote, held back by clay seals and stone gates. *They did not fear the flood. They scheduled it.*

I pressed my palm flat against the carving and pushed.

Nothing.

I pushed harder, threw my weight into it, screamed into my regulator, and the stone didn't move, and behind me Corvane's men were hauling offering after offering out of the vault into net bags, and I thought, *no, no, this can't be how it ends, him winning in a room full of my father's ghosts—*

Then I stopped pushing and started reading. There were marks around the hand-carving. Not wear marks. *Instructions.* Small drilled holes in a pattern, and beside them, worn nearly smooth, a line of glyphs I recognized from the entrance lintel because I'd spent an hour photographing them while Idris complained about air. The same glyph appeared on the flood-channel grates we'd swum past on the way in.

It wasn't a lever. It was a lock — and locks want keys, and the key wasn't strength.

The grates. The channel grates had the same glyph, and they were iron, and iron rusts, and rusted iron bends. Two thousand years of salt water does half your work for you if you're willing to do the other half with a wrecking bar.

I spun in the water. "Idris!" My voice came out garbled through the regulator, so I grabbed his arm and pointed, and thank God for three weeks of pointing — he read me in a heartbeat, saw which grate I meant, the wide one in the northwest wall behind the columns, and swam for the pry bar clipped to his harness.

Corvane saw us too. Of course he did. "What are they doing?" His voice cracked across the hall, all the opera-house calm gone. "Stop them. *Stop them.*"

Rask came at us through the water like a torpedo, and I did the only useful thing I've ever done with my body, which was get between him and Idris and kick off a column to meet him head-on. We collided. It was graceless and painful and I lost a fin, and none of it mattered, because it bought Idris five seconds, and five seconds was enough.

The pry bar bit into the grate. Idris put his whole back into it — a man who'd been blamed for a sinking he didn't cause, hauling against the sea itself — and the corroded iron shrieked, bent, and tore free at one corner with a sound like the world's largest nail coming out of the world's largest coffin.

Behind the grate: darkness, and a low rushing hum, and warmth rolling out of it like the breath of something enormous that had been sleeping.

"The channels are open," I said, though nobody could hear me. "All of them. It's been building since the moment we came in."

Because that was the truth I'd understood with my palm on the Giving Hand. The seals weren't holding the sea out anymore. They were holding the vent pressure *in*, and the temple had been ticking like a kettle since Corvane's crew anchored overhead and started drilling anchor points into the roof. My father's chisel. His scrapes at the wall. He hadn't been trying to open the channels.

He'd been trying to make sure no one else could seal them shut again.

The first channel let go with a boom I felt in my teeth. Water came out of the northwest wall not like a current but like a decision — a brown-green column of it, boiling with silt, slamming across the hall and taking two of Corvane's net bags with it. Offerings tumbled end over end through the murk. A golden mask spun past my face, empty eyes staring, and I swear it looked disappointed in everyone.

The hall tilted. Or the water did. Everything became the same roaring direction.

"*Out!*" Idris grabbed my harness strap and hauled, and we went with the current toward the entrance passage, because the only way to survive a drowning building is to be ahead of the water, not beside it. Behind us I heard shouting — Corvane's voice, high and furious: "The gold! Get the gold! Leave the equipment, *get the gold!*"

He was still in the vault. I looked back once — one look, I promised myself, one look and then never again — and through the churning silt I saw lantern light in the bronze doorway, and men swimming *inward*, against the flood, toward the stacked wealth, because Corvane was pointing at it, because for him the arithmetic had never once included the word *enough*.

A column cracked somewhere above us. Then another. The temple had held the sea for two millennia by being willing to die slowly; we had asked it to die quickly, and it obliged the way old things oblige — all at once, without complaint.

The entrance passage was chaos. Blackwood's divers were ahead of us, and I expected them to fight us for the line, for the surface, for air. Instead Oren Blackwood — who'd held a gun on Idris three days ago, who'd searched my father's letter with gloved hands — caught my wrist as the current tried to peel me off the guide rope, and shoved me upward toward the light.

"Move, girl!" he bellowed through his regulator. "Climb!"

We climbed. The shaft above the temple was a chimney of collapsing bubbles, and we rode it, Idris below me, Blackwood above, the roar of the drowning halls fading beneath the white noise of our own escape. My lungs burned. My ears screamed. Somewhere in the last ten feet I ran out of anything resembling technique and simply clawed at the water like a woman climbing out of her own grave.

Then night air. Rain. The Storm Gate's swells crashing beyond the cove, and the Mercy's riding lamp swinging at anchor, and I was lying on wet rock gasping like a landed fish while rain hammered my face, and Idris collapsed beside me, and for a long moment neither of us could do anything but breathe and exist.

The cove floor shuddered. Once. Twice. Then a deep, grinding groan rolled up through the rock, and the sea over the collapsed sea arch bulged, foamed white, and sank — sucked down into the throat of the temple as the halls below filled with the ocean they'd been built to receive.

"It's done," I said. I don't know if I said it aloud or just thought it very loudly. "It's done. It's over."

Idris pulled his hood back and looked at me, rain streaming down his face, and there was something in his expression I couldn't read — relief, grief, awe, all fighting for the same real estate. "Paloma." He nodded past me, toward the water. "Look."

The surface of the cove was littered. Nets. Air tanks. A golden cup turning slowly in the swell. And debris rising in ones and twos — and then, horribly, a man. Rask, alive somehow, coughing and clinging to a buoyancy bag, hauled out by Blackwood's remaining divers. Then another of Corvane's men. Then a third.

But not Corvane.

Blackwood stood at the water's edge counting his people, and I watched the count come up short, and I watched him understand it, and I watched a man who had spent years being someone's instrument realize the hand holding him had just been cut off at the wrist.

"He wouldn't leave it," Blackwood said. Not to me. To the rain. "I told him the structure was failing. I told him." He turned, and his face was gray under the lamplight. "He was still in the vault when the second channel blew. He had the mask in his hands. The gold burial mask, the centerpiece. He was *laughing*."

The sea over the arch heaved once more, and settled, and went quiet, and somewhere down in the dark the Vault of the Giving Hand closed itself around the last man who ever mistook having for keeping.

I sat down on the rock. I'm not proud of what I felt. It wasn't triumph. It wasn't even grief, not yet — grief would come later, in installments, the way it does. What I felt was clarity, terrible and clean, the kind you get on the far side of a choice that cannot be unmade. My father had given his life to keep this place sacred. I had given it the sea instead. And between those two gifts lay a magistrate who'd had every chance — my father's chance, my chance, the fisherfolk's warnings, the plain evidence of a civilization wiser than his — and had chosen a mask.

Idris sat down next to me. His shoulder found mine and stayed there.

"You okay?" he asked, which was absurd, and kind, and exactly right.

"No," I said. "Ask me tomorrow."

Rain drummed on the rocks. Out in the cove, Blackwood's surviving men were dragging their own aboard their launch, and Corvane's ship — the *Vigilant*, all guns and gilt — sat dark at anchor, its master gone, its crew beginning to realize that authority is a story, and theirs had just run out of pages.

Then Idris stiffened. His hand closed on my arm.

"Paloma. The Mercy."

I followed his gaze. Our boat — Idris's boat, the *Mercy*, patched and re-patched, the closest thing either of us had to a home — was swinging at her mooring, lamp burning, exactly where we'd left her.

And standing on her deck, silhouetted against the riding lamp, rifle slung, waiting with the patience of a man who has already sent word ahead, was a figure I recognized from Low Anchor's dockside gossip and Corvane's own introduction at the parley.

The Vigilant's first officer. Corvane's right hand. Come to collect whatever was left.

He raised a lantern, slow and deliberate, so we could see him. So we'd know he'd seen us.

"Well," Idris said quietly, getting to his feet, "tomorrow might have to wait."
