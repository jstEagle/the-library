## 20. Aftermath on Open Water

The sea does not care whose side you were on an hour ago.

I learned that lesson twice in my life — once the hard way, years back, when the *Kestrel's Pride* went down with her cargo and my name went down with her, and once now, bobbing in a tender that had no business staying afloat, surrounded by men who'd held a knife to everything I loved not six hours past.

The temple was gone. I'd felt it go — a groan through the water like a whale mourning, then a silence so complete it pressed against your eardrums. Paloma had opened the flood-channels, and Cape Reckoning had swallowed its own secret whole. Corvane had gone down refusing to let go of it. His last sight, as far as anyone could tell, was gold glinting through green water, reaching for it even as the sea reached for him.

There was a poetry in that. There was also a body count, and bodies don't read poetry.

* * *

We were eleven souls in a boat built for six. Four of mine — me, Paloma, and two of Blackwood's deckhands who'd decided mid-catastrophe that loyalty to a drowning man's ambition weighed less than loyalty to their own lungs. Seven of his. Oren Blackwood himself sat at the stern with his arm bound in a strip of sailcloth, watching me with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like the answer to.

"Vale." His voice was gravel soaked in seawater. "How far to Low Anchor?"

"Two days, if the wind holds kind. Three if it doesn't."

"And if we run out of water before then?"

"Then we'll have a much shorter conversation about priorities."

One of his men laughed — a short, startled bark, quickly smothered, like laughter wasn't authorized aboard. But it broke something open. Blackwood's mouth twitched, and for one strange moment we were just two sailors calculating survival instead of enemies calculating advantage.

"We ration," Blackwood said. "Half measures. And we rig a still from the tarp and the copper line in my kit. I've done it before."

"So have I," said Paloma, who had been silent since the temple, since the water, since whatever she'd seen down there that she hadn't yet found words for. She'd barely spoken since surfacing. Her hands hadn't stopped shaking until an hour ago, and even now they moved with a careful deliberateness, like she was relearning them. "My father taught me. Condensation off the tarp, drip into the bailer. It's slow but it works."

Blackwood looked at her a long moment. Something passed over his face — not quite grief, not quite respect. "Your father," he said. "Pemberton. I met him once. Years ago. Port Amara, before any of this."

Paloma went very still. "You knew him?"

"Everyone knew him. Man would talk your ear off about gift economies and drowned civilizations while you were trying to drink your beer in peace." Blackwood shifted, wincing at his arm. "He was decent. That's rare, out here. Decent people usually drown first."

"He didn't drown," Paloma said quietly. "Not the way you mean."

* * *

Night came down gray and close, the monsoon grumbling somewhere behind us like a dog told to wait. We rigged the still under Paloma's direction, and it worked — barely, stubbornly, a teaspoon of hope condensing every few minutes into the bailer. Eleven people taking turns sipping warm, plastic-tasting water from a shared cup. There's a democracy in thirst that no magistrate ever managed.

I took the tiller through the middle watch because nobody else could read these waters, and because steering gave my hands something to do besides remember what the temple had felt like collapsing around us. The storm gate behind us. The vault flooding. Corvane's face in the torchlight, going from fury to disbelief to something worse — understanding. He'd understood, right at the end, that Paloma had beaten him not with force but with a choice. You can't loot what isn't there. You can't rule by symbols when the symbols are silt.

A shape settled onto the thwart beside me. Blackwood. Of course.

"You could have let us drown," he said. Not accusatory. Curious, almost.

"The thought occurred."

"When the channels opened. When the halls started coming down. You had your tender, your woman, your exit. You went back for Deng and Ferro anyway. My men." He shook his head slowly. "Why?"

Because Idris Vale had spent seven years buying back his honor one risky job at a time, and here, apparently, was the final installment. But I didn't say that. What I said was: "Because the sea already had enough of us today."

Blackwood was quiet for a long time. The bow slapped water. Somewhere aft, one of his men snored with the boneless exhaustion of the nearly-drowned.

"I followed Corvane for nine years," he said finally. "He pulled me out of a debtor's cell in Port Amara. Gave me a ship, a crew, a purpose. Told me we weren't thieves — we were historians. Salvage with papers." A bitter sound, half laugh. "And I believed him, because believing was easier than asking why a historian needed armed men and sealed orders. Tonight I watched him send three of my crew into a flood-channel to clear debris ahead of himself. Watched him weigh their lives against minutes of head start on some vault." He stared out at the black water. "They were worth more than the vault, Vale. That's the arithmetic I got wrong for nine years."

"It's the arithmetic everyone gets wrong," I said. "Until the water comes in."

* * *

Dawn brought wind, and the wind brought rain, and the rain brought misery, which at least was familiar territory. The tender shipped water faster than bailing could fully answer, and we fell into shifts — Blackwood's men and mine trading places at the buckets without being asked, without ceremony, the way sailors do. There's a language of shared labor that outranks every flag ever flown.

Paloma sat wrapped in the tarp beside me, watching the horizon where Low Anchor should be.

"They'll know," she said. "The village. They'll know what happened at the Cape."

"Probably."

"Corvane's men will come asking questions. If he survived—"

"He didn't." I'd seen the water take the vault entrance. Nobody walked away from that. "But his regime doesn't need him alive to function. It needs his prize. And his prize is currently a silted-over hole full of very disappointed fish." I glanced at her. "Magistrates fall, Paloma. Slowly, usually, and ugly, but they fall. A man whose power rests on owning a miracle loses everything the morning the miracle turns out to be a hole in the ocean."

"And the fisherfolk? The people who actually guarded that place for generations?"

"They keep guarding it. Now they just guard an absence." I shrugged. "Sometimes that's stronger. Hard to steal a story when the story is *don't ask*."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then: "Idris. In the temple. Before the water. You could have taken something. Anyone would have. A coin. A shard. Nobody would have known."

"I would have known."

"That's it? That's the whole answer?"

"That's the whole answer." I turned back to the tiller. "I spent years being blamed for a sinking I didn't cause because other people put profit above seaworthiness. I'm not going to spend the rest of my life proving them right."

She reached over and put her hand on my arm — brief, warm, gone again. Some transactions don't need words.

* * *

It was near dusk on the second day that we saw the sails.

Three of them, running up the coast road of the shipping lanes, flying no colors I recognized — which, in these waters, meant either pirates or reformers, and lately the difference had gotten thin. Blackwood tensed beside me.

"Corvane's patrol?" Paloma asked.

"No," Blackwood said slowly. "Corvane's patrol flew the magistrate's pennant. These are... nothing. Unmarked." He squinted. "That's Port Amara registry on the lead ship. Merchant hulls. Someone's mustering private ships without a warrant."

"Against Corvane?" I asked.

"Or for whoever comes after him." Blackwood's jaw worked. "Word travels fast when a magistrate's flagship sinks at Cape Reckoning with him aboard. Half the coast owes him grudges. The other half owes him money, and money gets nervous when the debtor stops answering letters."

The ships passed us at a distance — close enough to see figures at the rails, far enough that hails wouldn't carry. One dipped a flag in something like salute, or maybe curiosity, and ran on north. Toward Port Amara. Toward the seat of a government that had just lost its strongest argument.

Blackwood watched them go with an expression I couldn't read. "Nine years I enforced that man's order," he said. "And it takes one tide to start unmaking it."

"Tides are like that," I said. "Patient, then all at once."

* * *

We raised Low Anchor at dawn on the third day, running before a kinder wind, the village's drying racks and stilt-houses emerging from the morning haze like something remembered rather than seen. The fisherfolk came down to the water in numbers — they'd seen us coming, of course; villages like this see everything — and there was no hostility in the crowd, only a kind of grave attention, as if they'd been waiting for us specifically.

An old woman stood at the head of the landing, silver-haired, straight-backed, with a walking stick that looked older than the village. Maren. I remembered her from our stop here weeks ago — the one who'd spoken of Pemberton with such fondness, and of the second expedition that never returned.

Her eyes found Paloma first, then me, then Blackwood and his battered crew, and she took the measure of all of us in about four seconds flat.

"Eleven came out," she said. "How many went in?"

"More than eleven," Paloma said.

Maren nodded slowly, unsurprised, unsurprisable. "And the temple?"

"Gone," Paloma said. "Sealed. Flooded. It stays buried now."

Something moved through the crowd — not celebration exactly. Relief, maybe, wearing celebration's clothes. Several of the elders touched their foreheads in an old gesture I'd seen carved once, in a flooded hall, on a wall depicting people giving things away.

Maren's gaze landed on Blackwood, and her voice cooled several degrees. "And this one?"

"This one," Blackwood said, before I could answer, "is a man who spent nine years on the wrong side of a promise, and who'd like to spend what's left of his life on the right one. Starting with—" he hesitated, and I watched pride die visibly in a man who'd fed on it for a decade, "—starting with telling the truth. To whoever needs to hear it. About Corvane. About what he wanted. About what it cost."

Maren studied him the way she'd study weather coming in. Then she stepped aside from the landing.

"The truth is welcome here," she said. "So are the tired. Get them ashore, Vale. There's fish soup and there's dry blankets, and there's a letter—" she paused, and her eyes softened as they returned to Paloma, "—there's a letter someone left in my keeping, years ago, saying it should find its way to a girl named Pemberton, whenever she came looking."

Paloma made a sound I'll never forget — small, cracked, half breath.

"Whenever she came looking," Maren repeated gently. "Well. She came looking."

* * *

I stayed at the landing while they went up, watching the tender rock against the pilings, watching Blackwood's crew and mine carry each other's wounded up the planks without distinction, watching the village absorb eleven salt-soaked strangers like the tide absorbing rain.

Somewhere south, a regime was cracking along fault lines that had been there all along — Corvane's prize dissolved, his flagship sunk, his creditors and enemies smelling blood in the water. Somewhere north, unmarked ships were running toward Port Amara with intentions. And here, on a splintered dock in a village too small for most maps, a dying prospector's promise was about to come home, and a dead man's letter was about to reach the daughter he'd never stopped writing to.

I'd spent seven years measuring myself against a disgrace. Standing on that dock, watching Paloma climb toward a letter with her father's hand inside it, I realized the debt had been paid somewhere back in the dark water off Cape Reckoning — paid not in coin or salvage or reinstatement, but in the only currency that had ever mattered on this coast.

The sea keeps its own ledger. That morning, for the first time in years, I wasn't afraid to read it.

Behind us, far to the south, thunder rolled over open water — the monsoon arriving at last to seal the Cape, right on schedule, closing the door behind us.

And ahead, up the path, Paloma had stopped walking. She'd turned around, letter clutched to her chest, looking for me in the crowd.

I went to her.
