## 22. Anchored

*Paloma*

Three weeks after the sea tried to kill us, I found myself sanding a hull in the hot sun of Low Anchor, and for the first time in my adult life, I wasn't thinking about leaving.

That deserves some context. For three years I measured my life the way a sailor measures a voyage — in miles put behind me, in ports that blurred together, in the growing distance between me and a man I'd loved and left in the same breath. Miles, not years. That was my arithmetic. A thousand miles from Port Amara meant freedom. Two thousand meant I'd won. It was a stupid system, because the one distance I was actually tracking — the one between me and my father — never got shorter no matter how far I walked. You can't outpace a thing you're carrying.

Now the arithmetic had changed. Now I measured my days in coats of paint applied, in planks replaced, in the slow resurrection of a boat called Mercy, and in the number of times Maren Osei's grandchildren had come down to the beach to pester us with questions. The answer to the last one was eleven, if you're wondering, and one of them — a girl named Tavi with a gap in her smile you could sail a dinghy through — had appointed herself our unofficial forewoman and paid herself in dried fish, whether we wanted it or not.

"Paloma." Tavi stood at the edge of the careening ground, hands on hips, in the universal posture of supervisors everywhere. "Idris says you're doing the seams wrong."

"Idris," I called over my shoulder, without looking up from the caulking iron, "is a liar and a disgrace to his profession."

"I heard that," said Idris Vale, from inside the boat, where he was elbow-deep in the bilge and had been for two hours. His voice carried the particular cheer of a man who has found his level. "And for the record, she *is* doing the seams wrong. She's doing them the Port Amara way, which is to say fast and badly."

"The Port Amara way kept me fed."

"The Port Amara way is why we're re-caulking them now."

Tavi looked back and forth between us like she was watching a tennis match played with hammers, delighted. This was, I had learned, the villagers' favorite entertainment: the two of us bickering over the Mercy like an old married couple who'd only just met. Which, in fairness, was roughly what we were.

Here's what nobody tells you about surviving something terrible with another person: afterward, you don't get to be strangers anymore. The sea had stripped us down to load-bearing walls — fear, stubbornness, a kind of blunt honesty that would have been rude anywhere on land — and what was left had turned out to fit together. Not neatly. There were still mornings I woke up in Maren's guest room with salt-crusted dreams and the phantom roar of water in my ears, mornings when Idris went silent as ballast stone and I had to learn not to ask, just to sit next to him on the beach until the silence turned back into a man. But the fit was real. You don't argue about caulking seams with someone you're planning to leave.

The Mercy sat on the careening ground like a patient on a table, stripped to her bones, and I'll be honest — when we'd first dragged her up onto the rollers after the temple, after the storm, after everything, I'd thought she was done. The monsoon had chewed her transom. The reef at the Storm Gate had opened a seam along her port side like a surgeon with opinions. Blackwood's men had stripped half her rigging during the boarding, and Corvane's ship had clipped her anchor line on its way to the bottom of history.

But Idris had looked at her ruined hull with an expression I recognized, because I'd seen it in my own face looking at my father's map. The expression of a person who has decided that a thing is worth saving, and who will not be reasoned with.

"She's sound," he'd said, running his hand along a plank that was very obviously not sound. "Timber remembers its shape. You just have to give it time."

I'd wanted to say something wry about men and their boats. I hadn't, because I'd understood, suddenly and completely, that he wasn't talking about the boat.

* * *

*Idris*

There's a thing that happens when you've been disgraced long enough: you stop expecting to be trusted, and then trust arrives anyway, and you have no idea what to do with it.

For four years after the *Cormorant* went down — after Halstead's shortcuts put forty-one souls in the water and put my name on the mud at the bottom of it — I'd worked the edges of the trade. Illegitimate charters, cargo runs with paperwork that was more suggestion than fact, the kind of jobs where the coin was good and the company was worse. I told myself I was buying my honor back one run at a time, but that was a lie I told at anchor with a bottle. You don't buy honor back. You build it, plank by plank, in front of witnesses, and the witnesses have to be willing to say what they saw.

Paloma was my witness. That's the whole of it.

She'd stood in front of the fisherfolk of Low Anchor and told them what happened at the temple — all of it, including the parts that made her look like a fool and the parts that made her look like a hero, and the parts where she was both at once, which is most parts, if you're honest. She told them about the flood-channels, and Corvane's men breaching the vault, and the water coming down through the halls of a dead civilization like the sea taking back what was lent to it. She told them her father had died in the cliffs above the cove keeping the temple's location out of the wrong hands, and that she'd finished the job he started. She told them about Hollis, the prospector who'd died with a promise on his lips and a map in his fist, and how that map had ended where it ended.

And then — this is the part I keep turning over — she'd told them I was worth chartering.

"A boat and a pilot," she'd said, standing in front of the whole village with her arms crossed like she was daring anyone to object. "For honest work. Supply runs, mail, passengers, salvage that's actually salvage. If anyone on this coast needs something moved and wants it moved by someone who won't rob you, lie to you, or sell you to a magistrate — I know where you can find both."

Maren Osei, who is the closest thing Low Anchor has to a government, had looked at me for a long moment. I'd braced for the question everyone always asks — *the Cormorant, was that you?* — the question I'd spent four years dreading and rehearsing answers to.

Instead Maren had said: "Can you fix an engine?"

"I can fix anything," I'd said, "if you give me time and don't talk to me while I'm doing it."

"Then you're hired," she'd said, and that was that. Four years of exile, ended by a woman who hadn't even asked.

I asked Paloma about it later, on the beach, while we were burning the old antifouling off the keel. "You vouched for me. You didn't even know my whole story yet."

"I knew enough," she'd said, feeding the fire. "I knew you ran the Storm Gate to save a stranger. I knew you went back into a flooding temple for me. I knew you gave Blackwood your boat instead of giving him the map, and then when it didn't matter anymore, you still went back and got the boat, because it was *yours* and you don't leave your own behind." She'd shrugged. "People tell you who they are by what they refuse to do. The rest is paperwork."

I've thought about that a lot. *The rest is paperwork.* The Cormorant inquiry, the license revocation, the blacklist — paperwork. What I'd actually done, in the water, in the years after — that was the record that counted, if anyone cared to read it. And someone had.

So when Maren offered us the village's supply contract — monthly runs down to Port Amara for goods, mail and passengers both directions — I said yes before she'd finished the sentence. And when Paloma looked at me sideways, the question in her face, I said, "You're not getting rid of me by signing a contract, if that's what you're worried about."

"I wasn't worried," she said.

"You were a little worried."

"I was a little worried," she admitted, and grinned, and that was the moment, if I'm pinning it down, that the sea stopped being the thing I was hiding in and started being the thing I lived on. It happened that fast. Four years of running, ended by a contract and a grin on a beach that smelled of burning paint.

* * *

*Paloma*

The Mercy went back into the water on a morning so calm it felt like the coast was holding its breath for us.

The whole village came down. That's the kind of place Low Anchor is — nobody has anything better to do than watch a boat float, and nobody pretends otherwise. Maren stood at the front with her arms folded. Tavi had commandeered a spot on the sea wall with her brothers. Old Sena, who remembered my father from the survey years, had brought a jar of something homemade and unlabeled that she pressed into Idris's hands with the instruction to "drink it when something good happens, or when something bad happens. It works for both."

The boat slid off the rollers and took the water like she'd been waiting for it. No weeping seams. No list. She sat in the swell and *bobbed*, light and sure, and I heard Idris exhale beside me — a long breath, four years long, maybe longer.

"Well?" I said.

"Well what?"

"Aren't you going to say something sentimental about timber remembering its shape?"

"I already said that." He was watching the boat with his whole face. "You don't repeat the good lines. That's how you know they're good."

We took her out for a trial run that afternoon — just the two of us, up the coast and back, the engine turning over sweet and the hull quiet as a held note. The Cape sat on the horizon behind us, blue and indifferent, and I found I could look at it now. That was new. For weeks after the temple, I hadn't been able to turn my head in that direction without my chest going tight — grief arriving years late, right on schedule, the way it does. But grief, it turns out, is like a tide. It goes out. It leaves things on the sand that you didn't know were there.

My father's letter was one of those things. I'd finally read it — properly, at Maren's kitchen table, with tea going cold in front of me — and I'm not going to recite it here, because some things are yours and not the world's. But I'll say this much: he'd written it the week before he left for the Cape, and it wasn't an apology and it wasn't an explanation. It was a map. Not of the temple — of *him*. The places he'd been afraid. The choices he'd made wrong. The daughter he'd loved badly and loved anyway, and the wish, written out in his cramped surveyor's hand, that I'd find something in my life worth anchoring to, and that I'd forgive him for never showing me how.

I'd sat with that letter for a long time. And then I'd folded it up and put it in the same oilcloth sleeve where Hollis's map had lived, and I'd realized I was smiling, which surprised me, because I hadn't cried, which surprised me more. Some reconciliations are loud. Mine was quiet — a letter, a cup of tea, a village going about its evening outside the window. He'd spent his life looking for a drowned temple. I'd spent mine looking for a door out. We'd both been looking in the wrong water.

"You're doing it again," Idris said.

"Doing what?"

"The face. The one where you're a thousand miles away."

"I'm exactly zero miles away," I said. "That's the whole point."

He laughed, and the Mercy ran on, and the coast unspooled beside us — green headlands, white sand, the little smudge of Low Anchor's smoke coming up over the trees like a welcome sign. My life, measured now in something other than distance. Measured in seams caulked and contracts signed, in a girl named Tavi who'd decided we belonged to her, in a boat that floated, in a man at the tiller who'd seen me at my worst — terrified, lying, drowning a temple — and had stayed anyway.

* * *

*Idris*

We took our first paying charter a week later. A family, if you can believe it — the Osei cousins, four of them, and a cargo of dried fish and one extremely displeased goat, bound for the market at Port Amara and back. Honest work. The kind I'd been banned from, and had run from, and had finally, at the bottom of a flooded temple and the end of a monsoon, been handed back.

Paloma worked the deck like she'd been born to it, which she hadn't — she'd been born to a surveyor's tent and a man's obsession — but she'd earned it, the hard way, the only way it sticks. The cousins loved her by the second hour. The goat did not love anyone. We came back up the coast with an empty hold, a lighter purse of coin than we'd have liked, and a standing order for two more runs, and when we tied off at Low Anchor's dock in the gold end of the day, Paloma sat down on the planks with her back against the mast and said, "I could do this forever."

"Forever's a long charter," I said.

"I've had longer ones that paid worse."

Maren came down the dock with the evening mail sack and a look on her face that I'd learned to read as *news, and I'm deciding how to deliver it.* She handed Paloma the sack — there was a letter in it, addressed to *The Pilot and Keeper of the Boat Mercy, Low Anchor*, which meant word was traveling, which meant we had a reputation now, the good kind — and said, "There's a man in Port Amara asking after you two. A surveyor. Says the coastal authority wants to chart the reef roads properly, after the storms, and he's heard there's a crew that knows those waters better than anyone alive." She paused. "He also says they're offering proper rates."

"Proper rates," Paloma repeated, in the tone of someone who'd spent her life being paid improper ones.

"Proper rates," Maren confirmed. "And — this is the part I thought you'd want to hear — the survey office is the one your father helped found. They've got his old charts. They asked, very politely, whether the daughter of Emrys Pemberton might be willing to consult."

The dock went quiet. Somewhere behind us the goat complained about the general state of existence.

Paloma looked out at the water for a long moment. I watched her do the arithmetic — I could see her doing it, the old habit, miles and distances — and then I watched her stop. Watched her put the numbers down. She looked at the letter in her hand, and the boat under her, and the village lights coming on up the beach one by one, like the coast itself was counting us in.

"Tell them," she said, "that we'll take the job. Both of us. And tell them—" she glanced at me, and I nodded, because I already knew what she was going to say, had known it since the day the Mercy came off the rollers, "—tell them we're not going anywhere."

Maren smiled and went back up the dock. The tide turned. The Mercy tugged at her lines, gentle, the way a sleeping thing tugs at a blanket, and I sat down next to Paloma on the planks, and we watched the water carry the last of the light out toward the Cape.

Not running toward it. Not running from it. Just — here. Anchored.

It was, we both agreed later, over Sena's jar, which turned out to work for good moments too: the best chart we'd ever made.
