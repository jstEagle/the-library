## 1. The Map That Lies

The Kestrel River took what it wanted and gave back nothing, and August Okafor had spent eleven years learning to live on the difference.

He stood on the deck of the *Maggie B* while his crew winched the last of the dredging gear aboard, watching the brown water slide past the pilings at Halloway Bend. Three weeks they'd spent clearing a logjam for a timber outfit that paid late and complained early. Before that, a bridge survey two counties down. Before that, something smaller he'd already forgotten. That was the shape of his life now: contracts like stepping stones, each one placed just far enough from the last that he never had to stand still long enough to look back upstream.

Upstream was Devil's Throat. It was always upstream.

"Boss." Naledi came across the deck with her boots ringing on the planking, a folded telegram in her fist. She was the only foreman he'd ever met who could read water and ledgers with equal fluency, and she didn't waste either. "Wire for you. Came up on the morning packet."

He took it without much interest. Wires meant work, and work meant another stone to step on.

**OKAFOR STOP HAVE ROUTE SURVEY UPRIVER THROAT SOURCE STOP GENEROUS TERMS STOP VANE**

Naledi watched him read it twice. "Elias Vane," she said. Not a question. Everyone in the valley knew the name; half of them owed him money and the other half wished they did.

"He knows I won't take it."

"Then why wire?"

August looked at the paper until the letters blurred. The Throat. Twenty years since the collapse, twenty years since the company sealed the tunnel mouth with concrete and official grief and called the eleven men inside a cost of progress. His father had been there when they poured it. Had come home gray as ash and never said one word about it that August could remember, except once, drunk, weeping into his hands: *the map, boy, mind the map.* Then he'd died, and left August the map, and August had carried it from town to town in a waterproof tube like a splinter working its way toward his heart.

"Tell the packet man: no."

"You want me to write it or just say it loud?"

"Loud is fine."

She grinned and went aft, and August went below to count money that wouldn't be enough.

* * *

The debt collector arrived on a Thursday, which was fitting, because Thursdays were when the *Maggie B* took on coal and August had nowhere to hide.

The man's name was Cratch, and he wore a suit too fine for the riverfront and shoes that had never touched mud. He sat in the cramped cabin across from August with his knees together and an envelope on the table between them, and he smiled the way bankers smile — professionally, and only with the mouth.

"Mr. Okafor. Eleven hundred and forty dollars, owed to the estate of Harlan Vane Investments, secured against this vessel." He patted the bulkhead fondly, as if the *Maggie B* were a dog that might bite. "Payment is ninety days past due."

"I know what I owe."

"Of course you do. And Mr. Vane — Elias, not the estate, though the estate holds your paper now — has asked me to convey a proposal before matters become unpleasant." Cratch slid the envelope forward. Inside was a contract, drafted in a lawyer's hand, terms generous enough to make August's teeth ache. Full crew wages for six months. The debt forgiven on completion. A bonus figure that would have bought the *Maggie B* outright and the dock she tied to.

Survey a route upriver to the source of the Kestrel. Through Devil's Throat.

"No," August said.

Cratch nodded as if he'd expected it, and produced a second document from his coat. "Then I'm instructed to begin forfeiture proceedings Monday morning. The boat, the gear, and — forgive me — the wages owed to your crew, which I understand you've been carrying personally since the Halloway contract underpaid by a third."

August said nothing. There was nothing to say that Cratch hadn't already counted.

"The offer stands through Sunday," Cratch said, rising. He paused at the cabin door, and for just a moment the banker's polish slipped and something almost human looked out. "For what it's worth, Mr. Okafor, Mr. Vane doesn't strike me as a man who gives up. I've worked for creditors twenty years. The ones who keep asking are the ones who want something more than money."

Then he was gone, and August sat alone with the lamp and the contract and the sound of the river going by, going by, always going somewhere he wasn't.

* * *

That night he opened the tube.

He told himself it was practical — if he was going to refuse Vane properly, he ought to know what the man thought the map was worth. But his hands knew better. They unrolled the old linen sheet on the chart table with the tenderness of men handling a dead man's face, weighting the corners with brass instruments, and there it was: Devil's Throat, drawn in his father's precise engineer's hand, every contour and cut graded like scripture.

Jonas Okafor had been the best surveyor in three counties. That was the cruelty of it. The map was beautiful. The man had been beautiful, in his way, before the Throat got into him like rot into timber.

August traced the line of the old right-of-way downstream to where the gorge pinched shut and the ink simply stopped — the place they called the sealed mile, where eleven men and a bore tunnel went into the mountain and the mountain kept them. Below the seal, the map resumed, confident as ever, as if nothing had happened in between.

He'd studied this map a thousand times. He knew its every mark.

Which was why, at two in the morning, with the lamp low and the river muttering against the hull, the wrongness finally caught him.

A junction. Where the old spur line branched toward the quarry cut, the ink was different. Not faded — *layered*. Someone had inked over an original line, carefully, patiently, the way a man lies with a pen when he thinks no one will ever hold the page to lamplight. August tilted the sheet. Held it against the flame's glow from behind.

And there it was, ghosting through the blacked-out ink: a second junction, a branch his father had buried under a lie, running not toward the quarry but *into* the gorge wall itself. Toward the sealed mile. Toward whatever Jonas Okafor had helped bury and then spent the rest of his life refusing to name.

*The map, boy. Mind the map.*

His father hadn't left him a survey. He'd left him a confession with one word scratched out, waiting twenty years for someone brave enough or desperate enough to read it.

August sat back. Above him, footsteps crossed the deck — Naledi, doing her rounds, pausing a moment over his hatch before moving on. She'd take the refusal well. She always did. They'd run another contract, another stretch of river, another year of stones to step on.

Or.

He looked at the contract on the table. Six months' wages. The debt gone. The crew paid. And at the end of it, the source of the Kestrel, and the door his father had inked shut.

The past had been knocking for twenty years. Tonight, finally, August understood that it wasn't going away. It was only getting louder, the way water gets louder behind a bad dam.

He dipped his pen.

Outside, far upriver, thunder rolled off the high country — rain coming, the Kestrel rising, the season turning against them before they'd even started. Naledi would call it a bad omen. August signed his name anyway, and felt the boat shift beneath him as if the river had already begun pulling them upstream.
