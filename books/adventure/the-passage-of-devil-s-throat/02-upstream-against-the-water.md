## 2. Upstream Against the Water

The river turned against us on the third morning.

August felt it before he saw it — a change in the boat's voice, the hull going from a low conversation to a mutter, then to a snarl. He stood at the bow with his father's map rolled in his fist like a baton and watched the gorge walls close in around them, black stone streaked white where twenty years of waterfalls had written their signatures.

"Soundings," he called back.

Naledi's answer came over the engine's growl: "Six feet. Five. August, it's dropping fast."

The *Meridian* was a converted survey barge, shallow-drafted and stubborn, and she was the last thing August had ever bought with money he'd earned honestly. She carried eleven people now: himself, Naledi, six crew, a cook who'd stopped cooking the moment the walls narrowed, and Elias Vane, who stood at the stern rail every morning in a coat too fine for the water and watched the country go by like a man reading a ledger.

The rapids came at noon.

August had run water all his working life — had learned to read a river the way other men read timetables — but nothing in his experience had prepared him for the Throat's upper reaches. The channel braided and unbraided around drowned timber, and the current came at them sideways, shoving the *Meridian* toward a shelf of rock that hadn't been on any chart because no chart had ever dared come this far.

"Hard to port!" Naledi was at the wheel, sleeves rolled, her forearms corded as she fought the helm. "Port, damn you — *port* —"

The boat struck something soft and gave a shudder August felt in his teeth. A drowned tree, its roots a cage of black branches, and then they were through, into slack water behind a gravel bar, the engine coughing, everyone shouting at once.

"Damage?" August asked.

Naledi ran the length of the deck, checking seams with her palms the way a doctor checks ribs. "We're holding. Barely. But August—" She stopped, and he watched her decide whether to say the rest. "There's fresh cut timber on that bar. Fresh. Somebody's been through here recently, and they weren't drifting."

He looked. She was right: pale saw-marks on the stumps, not yet weathered gray.

Nobody had been through the upper Throat in twenty years. That was the whole point of the Throat.

* * *

Naledi counted heads at dusk, the way she did every evening since the second night, when the flare signal had answered them from the south bank — three white stars where no camp should be, in country where no camp was permitted.

"Eleven," she said, and wrote it in her log.

She had a foreman's suspicion of arithmetic. Eleven souls aboard meant eleven sets of footprints, eleven rations, eleven explanations. Anything more was a leak. Anything less was a body.

The cook, Ferro, was missing from the fire.

She found him at the waterline, crouched over a tin box, and she watched from the shadow of the cargo net for a long moment before she let her boot scrape the gravel. Ferro went rigid, then stood too fast, the box already kicked half into the shallows.

"Evening," Naledi said.

"Evening, ma'am. Just — washing up."

"In the dark."

"I like the dark."

She retrieved the box from the water. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth: a signal lamp, a shutter key, and a page torn from a river gazetteer with their route penciled in — tomorrow's route, the route she'd only decided on that afternoon and spoken aloud to no one but August.

"Who's paying you?" she asked.

Ferro's face did something complicated. "You don't understand what these people are. Vane's people. They bought my sister's house debt, they—"

"I asked who."

He didn't answer. She didn't make him. She lashed the box to the mast where everyone could see it and put Ferro on double watch, and she told August only this: *someone aboard is selling us, and it isn't over.* August's jaw went tight and he said, "Then we feed them lies worth buying," and Naledi, who had worked for men who lied to their crews, decided in that moment she'd follow this one into the gorge itself.

* * *

The trestle at Mile Forty-One had been a marvel once. August could see it in the bones: the cut stone piers, the iron spine, the engineering pride of a company that had believed it could staple a railroad to the throat of a living gorge. Now the spans hung like broken ribs, and the middle hundred feet were simply gone — washed out, or taken out, the girders twisted into shapes that made his stomach turn, because iron doesn't tear like that unless something *very* large has argued with it.

They portaged around it, hauling gear up a goat path for half a day. It was Naledi, scrambling under the last intact span to rig a hoist line, who found the crates.

"August." Her voice carried flat along the stone. "You want to see this."

Six crates, stacked in the dry shadow of a pier, tarped and lashed against the decades. She'd pried one open. Inside, packed in sawdust gone brown and soft: dynamite. Old, sweating, weeping nitroglycerin stains through the paper like the sticks were crying.

And stamped on every crate, in faded ink, the same mark.

The Devil's Throat Mining & Transport Company. The seal his father had helped bury.

"These were never recovered," August said quietly. "The company reported the whole magazine lost in the collapse. Insurance paid on it."

"Then either the river moved them," Naledi said, "or somebody put them here after. And August — the tarps. Look at the tarps."

He looked. The tarps were newer than the crates. Replaced within the last few years. Someone had been maintaining this cache. Someone had been *keeping* it ready.

Behind them, boots on stone. Vane stood at the mouth of the pier's shadow, hat in hand, and for once the ledger-keeper's face was unreadable.

"Well," Vane said. "So it wasn't only a collapse. They armed the grave."

"You knew," August said. It wasn't a question. "You knew these were here. That's why the route runs this bank."

"I knew the company filed strange claims," Vane said. "I've spent twenty years and most of my fortune learning the shape of the strange. This—" he touched the crate's seal with one finger, almost gently, "—is the first piece I've ever held. The rest is under the water, at the source. Where your father put it."

"My father *sealed* it. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Vane's voice stayed soft. "You've seen the map, August. You've seen the junction he inked over. Ask yourself what a man seals, and what a man hides, and why he'd spend his last lucid year burning every paper he owned but that one."

August had no answer. He'd been asking himself since the night the debt collector came, and every answer he found had his father's handwriting on it.

* * *

That night the gorge narrowed to a hundred yards, then eighty, and the water changed its mind about being a river and became something older. The *Meridian* tied off against a shelf of rock, engines cold, and the crew sat around a small fire with the walls rising on either side of them like the inside of a throat — and August finally understood why the old maps had named it that.

Naledi took the middle watch. From the bow she could see the channel ahead: black water, white stone, and far off, where the gorge bent north, a faint seam of lighter rock running through the cliff face. Man-cut. Regular. The old bore.

The tunnel. They were close enough to see its scar from outside.

She was still watching when the flare went up — not from the south bank this time, but ahead, above the tunnel scar itself. One star, red, burning against the black stone. Then a second. Then a third.

A signal. An answer. Someone at the source, telling whoever held the shutter key that the expedition was coming — and that they were expected.

Naledi didn't shout. She climbed down to August's bunk and woke him with a hand on his shoulder, and by the light of the banked lantern she watched him read her log entry, the one she'd written in a foreman's flat hand: *Three red over the bore. They know we're here. They've known since the bar.*

August rolled his father's map open across the chart table and held the lantern low over the inked-over junction, and for the first time he noticed what exhaustion and grief had kept him from seeing: the over-inking wasn't hiding a junction at all.

It was hiding a date. Faint, beneath the black, in his father's own hand: a day, a month — and a year that was *four months before* the collapse.

His father had known. Before the mountain ever came down, his father had drawn this line and buried it, and whatever waited at the flooded source had been waiting for twenty years for someone to come and dig it out.

Ahead, in the dark, the river ran toward the bore, and the bore ran toward the truth, and the water between them was rising.
