## 3. The Source

The tunnel mouth was smaller than August remembered it, though he had never actually seen it — only the photograph on his father's desk, taken the week before the collapse, when Devil's Throat was still a boast and not a wound. The real thing was a black arch cut into grey limestone, half-drowned already, the river sliding into it like a tongue into a closed mouth. Someone had built a wall across the opening once. Most of that wall was gone now, tumbled into the water, and what remained was furred with twenty years of moss.

Naledi stood beside him at the bow of the survey boat, saying nothing. She'd said nothing for two days, ever since they'd found the dynamite crates stenciled with the old company seal hidden under the ballast in the hold. She hadn't accused anyone. She hadn't needed to. The whole crew knew what a crate like that meant in a gorge this narrow: someone aboard wanted them stopped, or wanted them dead, and either way the river would do the work.

"Map," she said finally.

August unfolded his father's map on the thwart, weighting the corners with his knife, a coil of rope, Naledi's brass compass. In the daylight it looked almost innocent — contour lines, the river's old course, the bore of the tunnel drawn as a confident straight stroke through the mountain's root. But he'd spent three nights with it since the inked-over junction, and he no longer trusted a single line of it.

The junction was there if you knew to look. His father had laid new paper over the old drawing and redrawn everything except one spur — a maintenance adit, a side gallery running off the main bore toward the river's edge. Under lamplight, held at an angle, you could see the ghost of it beneath the overpainting, and you could see something else too: a sequence of small crosses, five of them, marching down the spur like stations of some private crucifixion.

Five crosses. Five men sealed in the Throat. The company's report had called it seven dead, all lost in the main collapse. But the map said five were somewhere else entirely.

"He drew them," Naledi said quietly. "Your father marked where they are."

August didn't answer. He couldn't. His throat had gone tight as wire.

The boat scraped stone. They were at the mouth.

* * *

He went in alone. That was the argument, and he won it by refusing to have it — by simply taking the lamp and the map case and stepping from the boat onto the ledge inside the arch while Naledi was still listing reasons. She called him a fool in three languages. He heard her start rigging a second lamp line anyway, because Naledi Okafor-adjacent loyalty worked like that: she'd curse you and save you in the same breath.

The air inside was cold and mineral, tasting of iron and old rot. Water came up past his knees within thirty meters, then his waist, then — where the floor dropped toward the old drainage grade — his chest. He moved along the left wall where the footing was rubble but solid rubble, hand over hand along a cable that had been galvanized once and was now more rust than metal. The lamp threw his shadow huge against the rock, and the rock gave it back multiplied, a dozen Augusts staggering through the dark.

The first cross was carved into the wall at the mouth of the spur. Not painted. Carved, deep, by someone with time and a chisel and no hope of being found. Below it, letters: J. MBEKI. August put his palm flat against the stone. It was like touching a grave marker in a church no one visited, and he supposed that was exactly what it was.

The water rose. He could hear it rising — a low, patient sound behind him, the river finding its way into places it had been denied for twenty years. Or being helped. He thought of the dynamite crates and pushed the thought down.

The spur opened after forty meters into a chamber, and the chamber held the truth.

It wasn't a collapse. Collapses were chaotic; he'd seen enough of them to know the signature — shattered timber, rock flour, the random violence of a mountain shrugging. This was orderly. The charges had been set in a line, evenly spaced, drilled into the crown of the bore at professional intervals, and fired together. The roof had come down as a single deliberate slab. And beneath it, wedged in the wreckage where the water hadn't reached, lay the things the company report had never mentioned: tool rolls still buckled shut. A lunch pail. A ledger, swollen fat with water but legible, its columns recording — day after day, month after month — concrete pours signed off below specification. Sand-heavy mix. Thin rebar. A foreman's initials approving each batch.

His father's initials.

August stood chest-deep in the dark with the ledger dripping in his hands and felt twenty years of inherited pride die quietly in his chest. His father had not sealed the tunnel to protect the valley from a dangerous bore. He had sealed it to bury the evidence — the shoddy work, the cost-cutting, the men who'd gone back inside to shore up a tunnel their own reports said was failing, and who had been written off as casualties of an accident when the charges went off.

There was a letter folded into the back of the ledger. He almost didn't open it. He opened it.

*If you are reading this, then I did not have the courage to do it myself,* his father had written. *The order came from above me. I signed the pours because I was told to sign them, and I set the charges because I was told to set them, and I have told myself for twenty years that a man who obeys is different from a man who decides. The five are in the spur. Say their names.*

August read the names twice. Then the water surged — a wall of it, sudden, coming down the main bore behind him with a sound like a freight train entering a station — and he understood, with the cold clarity of a man whose lamp has just guttered, that someone had opened the river's path on purpose.

* * *

"You always were the sentimental one."

Vane's voice carried down the spur ahead of the lamplight, and August turned, ledger clutched to his chest, water at his collarbones now. Vane stood on the higher ledge near the chamber's mouth, dry, immaculate even here, two of his hired men behind him with lamps and coils of fuse.

"The sluice gate upstream," Vane said. "My men opened it an hour ago. The river will finish filling these workings by morning." He smiled, and it was the smile August had hated across a hundred contract tables — patient, proprietary, certain. "I needed you to find it first. I needed it witnessed. You, specifically. The son of the man who did it."

"You knew," August said. "You've known for years."

"I suspected. Your father's map confirmed it — the part he thought he'd hidden." Vane crouched, careless of the spray, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse. "Seven families got settlement money and a lie, August. Seven graves with nothing under them. I am going to put the truth in every newspaper from here to the coast. The company, the engineers, the government men who looked away — all of it. Your father's name first."

"And the route?" August said. "The survey? The railroad?"

Vane spread his hands. "There is no route. There never was. The gorge can't be bored — the rock won't hold. I needed the expedition to get us here, and I needed you to open what your father closed. The scandal is worth more than any line through this valley. It will break the company that ruined my brother."

August's arms ached around the ledger. Somewhere above, faint through fifty feet of stone, he could hear the river climbing.

"So drown it," Vane said. "Drown the evidence, keep your father's name clean, and we both walk out rich men. Or carry it out, and burn every bridge you have left — including mine, including the valley's, including your own." He glanced at the rising black water, then back, and his smile thinned to something honest at last. "Choose quickly. The river already has."

Behind Vane, at the lip of the ledge, a third lamp appeared — Naledi, soaked to the skin, a cutting torch in her hand, her eyes finding August's across the chamber with a question in them he couldn't yet answer.

The water touched his chin.
