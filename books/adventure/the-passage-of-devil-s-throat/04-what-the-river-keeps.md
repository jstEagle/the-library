## 4. What the River Keeps

The water did the work faster than August could have.

He had planned it carefully — the charge set against the last intact bulkhead, the fuse run back along the flooded walkway to higher ground where Naledi waited with the lamp. But when he touched flame to cord and felt the river surge through the old workings behind him, he understood that he had only ever been giving permission. The Throat had been waiting twenty years to finish what it started.

The blast came as a sound he felt in his teeth more than heard. Then the corridor behind him filled with a roar like a train taking a curve too fast, and August ran, boots slipping on wet stone, the documents pressed flat against his chest inside an oilskin wallet. He did not look back. Looking back was how men died in tunnels; his father had taught him that, in the one lesson of his life that had turned out to be true.

Naledi's lamp found him at the junction chamber, and her hand found his arm, and together they climbed the ladder into gray morning light while the tunnel breathed its last below them.

* * *

They sat on the rocks above the gorge until noon, watching the mouth of the Devil's Throat swallow itself. The water rose through the bore in a slow brown tongue, filling the workings floor by floor, and by midday there was nothing left but a whirlpool and the smell of wet stone. Whatever Vane wanted from down there — ledgers, correspondence, proof written in his father's hand — it belonged to the river now.

"You knew," Naledi said. It wasn't a question. She'd known since the dynamite crates, since the sabotage, since the night she'd caught Corrigan passing signals across the water and August had told her to let him go.

"I knew Vane wanted a scandal," August said. "I didn't know I wanted the same thing."

"And now?"

August opened the oilskin wallet. Inside were eleven pages, water-stained at the edges but legible: the payroll manifest from the final week, the crew roster, the telegrams between the company office and the resident engineer — his father — ordering the bore sealed before inspection. Eleven names. Eleven families who had spent twenty years being told their men died in an act of God.

"Now they get what they're owed," August said. "Not the money. The money's gone with everything else. The truth. That's all that's left to give."

Naledi looked at the pages a long time. "It won't bring them back."

"No. But it stops the lie from outliving everyone who could have ended it."

She nodded once, and that was enough. Naledi never needed two words where one would do.

* * *

Vane was waiting at the downstream camp, and he knew before August spoke. He stood at the edge of the water with his coat unbuttoned and his hands empty, watching the whirlpool with the expression of a man reading his own obituary.

"You destroyed it," he said. "Twenty years of digging, and you drowned it in an afternoon."

"I drowned the argument," August said. "The truth is up here." He held out the wallet. "Eleven names. Your father signed the order to seal the bore. So did mine. Take it or leave it — either way, it goes to the families tomorrow."

Vane took the wallet as if it might burn him. For a long moment he said nothing, and August watched him do the arithmetic: the route upriver dead, the salvage claim worthless, the fortune he'd mortgaged against this expedition gone into the same water as the tunnel. A lesser man would have wept. Vane just went very still, the way he'd gone still across a contract table fifteen years ago, calculating what a thing cost and whether he could afford it.

"My father ruined yours," Vane said finally. "Or yours ruined mine. I've never been able to decide which."

"Neither," August said. "They ruined eleven other men, and then they ruined themselves. That's the whole story. I'm done telling it any other way."

"You could have kept the map. Kept the secret. Lived comfortable on the lie like every other man in this valley."

"I tried that," August said. "I spent twenty years drifting between contract jobs because I couldn't sit still anywhere long enough for the past to find me. It found me anyway." He shouldered his pack. "There's no forgiveness coming, Elias. Not from me. You brought dynamite onto my boat and put my people in the water to get your scandal. We're square, and we're through."

Vane looked at him for a long moment — and something in his face shifted, some load set down or picked up, August couldn't tell which. "For what it's worth," Vane said, "you did what I never could. You chose."

Then he turned and walked downriver toward whatever was left of his life, and August did not watch him go.

* * *

The deliveries took three weeks.

He went alone, by rail and foot, to eleven doors. Some of the families had scattered; he tracked them through parish records and old union rolls, through cousins of cousins. At each door he handed over a copy of the pages and kept the originals, and at each door he watched the same thing happen: the long silence, the hand trembling over a name, the question — *why now? why after twenty years?* — and no answer he could give that wasn't the true one.

"Because the man who helped bury it was my father," he said, again and again. "And I helped bury it a second time by staying quiet. I'm not staying quiet anymore."

An old woman in Kestrel Hollow, widow of the powder man, held the pages under her lamp for ten minutes without speaking. Then she looked up and said, "Your father's boy. I remember you. You used to hide behind his legs at the company dances." And she made him tea, and she did not forgive him, and she did not need to. She asked him to read the names aloud, all eleven, and he did, and when he finished she said, "Now it's said out loud. Now it counts."

That was the closest thing to absolution the valley offered, and August took it.

* * *

The permanent post came looking for him instead of the other way around. The valley line — the live line, the one that actually carried freight and people through the mountains — had a stretch near Miller's Bend that ate signal relays and shrugged off ballast every spring thaw, and the company wanted a man who understood old construction well enough to know where new construction had cut corners. They wanted, though nobody said it plainly, a man with something to prove to the ground itself.

August took the post in October. He rented a room above the depot in a town called Harrow's Crossing, hung his father's map — the corrected version, the junction inked back in, the lie amended in his own hand — above the desk, and began the slow work of keeping trains running through country that wanted them dead.

Naledi stayed on as foreman of the maintenance gang. On his first morning she handed him a coffee and a work order and said, "You look terrible."

"I slept," August said.

"First time in twenty years, by the look of you."

He drank the coffee. Below the trestle, the river ran high and fast with autumn rain, and somewhere upstream, behind forty feet of stone and water, the Devil's Throat kept its dead. He thought about them sometimes — not with dread anymore, but with a kind of companionship, the way you think of men whose shift you've taken over. Somebody had to stay and mind the line. Somebody had to be the one who didn't run.

The 7:40 freight came through on schedule, whistle singing off the canyon walls, and August Okafor stood on the platform of the town he'd finally stopped leaving, and watched it pass, and went to work.
