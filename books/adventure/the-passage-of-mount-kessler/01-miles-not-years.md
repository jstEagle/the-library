## 1. Miles, Not Years

The engine coughed twice before it caught, the way it always did on cold mornings, and Felix Joubert let it cough because a man who argued with his airplane before sunrise was a man who'd be arguing with it all day. The de Havilland Beaver sat on the gravel strip at Tidewater like an old dog that had learned to sleep through anything — frost furring the struts, dew silvering the fabric of the wings, the floats resting in puddles left over from last week's rain. He ran his palm along the cowling as he walked the preflight, feeling for soft spots in the aluminum the way a doctor might feel for a fever. There was a dent above the third rib on the port side he could have told stories about, if anyone asked, and nobody ever asked, which was the way he liked it.

Ten years he'd been flying this coast. Long enough that the Beaver felt less like a machine than a marriage — familiar, temperamental, forgiving in exactly the ways he needed forgiveness. Not long enough that any town on it had gotten around to naming a street after him, or a bench, or a kid. That was deliberate. Felix kept a mental ledger of his own, and the only entry he allowed himself was this: *owes nothing, owns nothing, stays nowhere.* It wasn't freedom exactly. But it was shaped like freedom from the outside, and from the outside was where he did most of his looking.

He loaded the morning's cargo by feel: two crates of penicillin bound for Sourdough Inlet, a box of dental supplies somebody up there had been waiting three months for, mail sacks heavy with catalog orders and letters people probably shouldn't have written but had anyway. And under the mail, wrapped in oilcloth like everything else, the thing that actually paid for the fuel: gossip. Not literally — though there were weeks it might as well have been. What he carried was the small freight of connection: photographs of grandchildren, a spare carburetor part someone had scavenged in Kessler Point for a widow's generator, a wedding dress ordered from Vancouver and folded into a garment bag that took up half the cabin. On this coast, the airplane wasn't transportation. It was the thread itself. Cut it, and the settlements came apart into islands.

Felix understood threads. He also understood what happened when you tied one around your own wrist.

He swung up into the pilot's seat, settled the headset over his ears, and let the Beaver walk itself down the gravel into the gray water of the sound. The hulls slapped flat and then bit, and then they were flying, the way they always flew — suddenly, completely, the whole coastline dropping away beneath him like something he'd dreamed rather than lived.

* * *

The Kessler coast in October was a country made of weather and rumor. Fog sat in the valleys like a tenant who'd stopped paying rent; the mountains showed themselves in pieces, a shoulder here, a glacier's blue lip there, and you learned to navigate by memory and nerve. Felix flew the route the way other men drove a commute: Tidewater to Sourdough Inlet, Sourdough to Halvorsen's Landing, Halvorsen's down to Kessler Point if the manifest asked, and home again with whatever the day had shaken loose. Four hundred miles of salt water and spruce, and he knew every mile of it the way you know a scar — intimately, without affection.

Sourdough Inlet came up out of the fog at nine-forty, its single street running along a boardwalk that had been sinking into the tide flats since before the war. He set the Beaver down against the current, taxied to the community dock, and killed the engine to the usual reception: a dog barking from a woodpile, old Marta Vukovich standing at the end of the planks with her arms crossed like she'd been sent to collect him personally.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm early," Felix said. "You're just used to miracles."

She snorted, but she took the penicillin crates herself, refusing help the way she refused most things, and while she signed the manifest her eyes flicked up at him — quick, appraising, the look of a woman who had buried a husband and two sons to this water and had stopped being impressed by men who wouldn't sit still. "There's coffee on," she said. "And Ruthie Calloway's been asking after you."

"Ruthie always asks after me."

"Maybe someday you'll ask back."

He smiled the smile he kept for occasions like this, the one that agreed to nothing, and climbed back into the plane. Behind him, faint through the open window, he heard Marta say something to the dog about men who treated their lives like layovers. He didn't catch all of it. He didn't need to. He'd written the sentence himself, in a hundred towns, in a voice that sounded more like hers every year.

* * *

By noon he was down at Halvorsen's Landing, delivering the wedding dress to a bride who cried on the dock, and by two he was standing in the Kessler Point air office listening to the radio crackle out the marine forecast while he waited on a part for the widow's generator.

"—advisory remains in effect for the outer coast," the voice said, patient and official, the kind of voice that expected not to be listened to. "Astronomical low tides combined with sustained offshore winds are producing water levels not seen in the instrumental record. Harbormasters should expect exposed foreshore, stranded vessels, and unusual bottom conditions through Thursday. Repeat—"

"Record lows," said the man behind the counter, whose name was Denny and whose function in life was to know things slightly before everyone else. "They're saying lowest in sixty years. Maybe ever, for some stations."

"Mm," Felix said.

"My cousin fishes off the point. Says the kelp's lying flat as a rug out there. Says you can see bottom where nobody's seen bottom since his grandfather's day." Denny leaned on the counter, lowering his voice as though the ocean might overhear. "Old-timers are talking about it. My grandfather used to tell a story about a low tide like this, back — oh, must've been the fifties. Whole beach stripped bare. Things come up out of the mud nobody wanted to see." He paused, enjoying himself. "Bones, mostly. Fish bones. Nothing to it."

"But?"

Denny shrugged, expansive. "But old-timers don't tell stories about nothing."

The radio crackled on behind them, repeating itself to an empty room. Felix signed for the part, tucked it under his arm, and stepped out into the hard clean light of the afternoon. Kessler Point sat below him in its shallow bowl — the cannery long dead, its skeleton of rusted pilings standing in the shallows like the ribs of something that had died standing up; the church with its white paint gone chalky; and above it all, on the shoulder of the mountain, Kessler House, big as a courthouse, its windows catching the sun. The family name was on the sawmill road, the school gymnasium, the plaque by the dock commemorating some benefaction or another. Half the town worked for the family trust in one form or another, and the other half worked for people who did.

Felix had flown charters for the Keslers twice. Both times he'd been paid in crisp bills and thanked by a man with a handshake like a door closing. He hadn't asked questions either time. Asking questions was how you ended up staying somewhere long enough to owe somebody an answer.

He walked down to the shore because the part delivery wasn't until four and because his legs got restless on the ground, and that was when he saw it — or almost saw it, the way you almost see a face in wallpaper. The tide was out past anything the town seemed prepared for. The beach ran naked for a hundred yards beyond its usual line, kelp flattened, barnacle beds drying in the air, and the water stood far off in the sound like a guest who'd stepped outside and forgotten to come back. People were wandering down onto the exposed flats with their hands in their pockets, looking at starfish and old bottles and the foundations of a wharf nobody living had known was there.

And at the base of the headland, where the cliffs met the shingle, there was a dark opening in the rock that Felix was fairly sure — not certain, but fairly — had not been there the last forty times he'd stood on this beach.

It might have been there. That was the thing about a coast like this: it kept its own counsel. Caves opened and closed with storms; rockfalls happened; memory was a poor surveyor. But the opening sat too square in the cliff face, too deliberate, its edges straightened as if by intent, and even from fifty yards he could see that the light inside it didn't fall off the way light falls into a hollow. It went *in*. It kept going.

"Felix!" Denny's cousin, maybe, or somebody's cousin — a fisherman in rubber boots, waving from the flats. "You seeing this? Tide's supposed to drop another foot tonight!"

Another foot. The thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome: *whatever that opening is, tomorrow it'll show more of itself.*

He looked at it a moment longer than he meant to. There was something about the shape of the dark in there — not menacing, exactly. Patient. Like a room with the door left open by someone who intended to come back.

Then he turned uphill, because the part was due at the widow's place at four, and because a man with a schedule has no business standing on a beach wondering about holes in the world. The radio advisory played itself again somewhere behind him, patient and official, addressed to harbormasters and to anyone else who believed the sea kept records.

Felix Joubert had spent ten years making sure he wasn't on any record worth keeping. He collected his truck keys, checked his watch, and drove up out of the bowl of the town without once looking back at the water.

* * *

That night he slept in the hangar bunk at Tidewater, as he did most nights, in a room with a cot, a stove, and nothing on the walls — nothing personal, nothing permanent, nothing that would take longer than ten minutes to pack. He'd lived worse places and better ones and had left both. The stove ticked. Wind came down off the glacier field and moved around the hangar like something looking for a way in.

He was nearly asleep when the radio on the shelf beside the cot hissed awake — the coast station doing its evening round, reading notices to mariners in that same patient voice. Most of it washed over him. Then:

"—vessels in the vicinity of Kessler Point are advised that extreme low tides will continue through Thursday. Exposed foreshore may reveal submerged hazards and historical structures. Persons are cautioned against entering caves or excavations exposed by the receding waterline. Repeat: persons are cautioned against entering—"

He reached over and turned the dial until the voice dissolved into static.

It wasn't fear. He'd like to say it was fear, because fear would at least have been interesting. It was simpler than that, and older. Somewhere in the back of his chest there was a door he'd shut ten years ago, and he had learned — the hard way, the expensive way, the way that still woke him some nights in places he wouldn't name — that doors like that stayed shut only if you never stood next to them. Never touched the handle. Never, under any circumstances, wondered what was on the other side.

A cave full of something the government bothered to warn people about was a handle.

He lay in the dark and listened to the wind work at the hangar door, and told himself the things he always told himself: *you've got a schedule. You've got a fuel bill. You've got a widow's generator running on borrowed parts and a bride's grandmother expecting photographs by Sunday. Whatever the tide left on that beach belongs to the tide.*

Outside, far down the coast, the sea was pulling back farther than it had pulled back in sixty years, and the mountain at Kessler Point stood over its emptying shoreline with the patience of stone.

Tomorrow, Felix told himself, he'd fly north to Sourdough and back, and none of it would matter.

He was almost right.
