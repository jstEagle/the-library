## 2. What the Tide Left

The tide was wrong before Felix Joubert ever saw it.

He felt it first in the light — a strange pewter sheen on the water south of Kessler Point where there should have been the ordinary gray-green chop of a working coast. Then he felt it in the air itself, a smell like wet stone and old kelp lifted up through the floats' open windows, mineral and cold, the smell of things that had been underwater for a long time being asked to breathe again.

"Radio said minus four," Ines Okafor called from the right seat, her voice pitched over the de Havilland's engine drone. She had her notebook out already, because she always had her notebook out, its cover soft as cloth from ten years of salt and handling. "Minus *four*, Felix. The broadcast said they'd never recorded anything close."

"They say a lot of things on the radio." Felix eased the yoke forward a degree, letting the Beaver settle down the long shallow gradient of morning toward the point. "Half of it's weather, half of it's gossip, and the two sound exactly alike if you're not paying attention."

"I'm paying attention." She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the water, at the coastline unspooling beneath them, and her stillness had the particular quality he'd learned to notice in scientists — the quality of a person watching something happen that only happens once.

Felix had been flying medicine and mail and gossip between the coastal settlements for eleven years now, and he had a private taxonomy of passengers. There were the ones who slept. There were the ones who talked. There were the ones who gripped the doorframe through every bump and apologized for it. And then there were the rare ones who went quiet in a way that made the whole airplane quieter, who looked down at the world with an attention so total it seemed almost rude, as though the landscape ought to be embarrassed to be caught looking like this.

Ines Okafor was one of those. Three weeks running samples between the research stations, and she had not once been bored by the view.

Below them, Kessler Point came into its full geography: the town huddled behind its breakwater, the cannery's rusted spine, the great gray shoulder of Mount Kessler rising inland with its glacier hanging off it like a slack sail. And beyond the point itself, exposed along the shore where the water should have been lapping black and deep against the basalt —

Felix took his hand off the throttle without meaning to.

The sea had pulled back. Not just low — *pulled back*, the way a man pulls back a blanket. A quarter mile of seabed lay naked in the morning light: ribbed sand, boulder fields furred with weed, tide pools steaming faintly in the cold air like the breath of something exhaled after a very long time. Channels that no living person had ever seen dry ran silver between the rocks. And set into the base of the headland, where the charts showed nothing but cliff —

A mouth.

It was a cave opening, tall as a hangar door, black as a held breath, framed in rock so dark it looked cut rather than carved. Water still dripped from its lip in long slow threads, catching the light like wire.

"Felix." Ines's voice had changed. "Felix, look at the headland. Look at the — is that a cave? The charts don't show a cave there."

"The charts don't show half of what's here." But his own voice had gone careful, and he heard it, and hated that he heard it. "That shoreline's all undercut. Sea caves everywhere. Probably opens and closes with the tides most years."

"Not like that." She was leaning against the window now, palm flat on the glass. "Not *dry* like that. Felix, a minus tide like this might come once in a century. Maybe once ever, with the way the pressure systems have been sitting. That cave has been sealed by the sea for — we don't know how long. Nobody alive has seen inside it."

"Then nobody alive is missing much."

She turned and looked at him, and her eyes behind their salt-flecked glasses were bright with a hunger he recognized and distrusted on sight, because he'd spent eleven years making sure he never felt it himself. "Land."

"No."

"Felix."

"It's a supply run. I've got insulin and engine oil and Mrs. Adeyemi's rheumatism pills in the back, and the strip at Kessler Point is a mud puddle with ambitions. I'm not beaching my airplane on a seabed that's going to fill back up with the Pacific Ocean the moment the moon changes its mind."

"We'll take the skiff. You've got a skiff strapped to the float, I've watched you use it twice." She was already unbuckling. "Two hours. The tide tables give us four before it turns, and I want two of them for margin. Please. Ten years from now there'll be a paper about this and everyone will say, imagine if someone had landed."

"And if the paper says *and then the tide ate them*?"

"Then it'll be a very well-cited paper."

He should have laughed. He did laugh, a little, the way you laugh when you're losing an argument you'd already lost three sentences ago. But underneath the laugh there was that other thing, the old thing, the thing that had kept him moving town to town for a decade: the instinct that anything worth stopping for was worth running from. Caves. Secrets. Things the sea had decided to keep hidden and had, for reasons of its own, briefly unhidden. He had built a whole life on the principle that you flew over such things. You didn't land in them.

But she was already climbing into the skiff, and the truth — which he would examine later, turning it over like a stone — was that some part of him wanted to see it too. Wanted it the way a starving man wants bread and distrusts the hand offering it.

"Two hours," he said. "I'm timing it. And if the water starts coming back, we leave whatever we're holding and we run."

* * *

The seabed was stranger up close than it had been from the air.

They walked it in rubber boots, Ines setting a pace that was nearly a jog, Felix carrying the coil of rope and the flashlight he'd grabbed from under the seat without quite admitting to himself why. Around their feet the exposed floor of the ocean lay littered with its own history: crab shells split like dropped plates, a boot sole gone to leather lace, the ribs of some boat dead so long it had become geology. Anemones hung closed and glistening in the drained pools, waiting, patient as monks. Everything smelled of iodine and iron.

"You know what strikes me?" Ines said, hopping a channel of trapped seawater. "Nobody's here. The whole town can see this shore. Half of Kessler Point should be down here gawking."

Felix glanced back toward the town. Smoke rose thin from the cannery chimneys; a truck crawled the coast road, unhurried. "Maybe they see it every day."

"At minus four? No." She shook her head. "Something's off. Even the birds are wrong — look, they're not feeding on the flats. They're all sitting on the headland, facing the cave. Like they're waiting for it to be over."

He looked. She was right. Gulls by the hundred lined the basalt above the cave mouth in silent ranks, and not one of them was hunting the richest tide flat any of them would ever see.

"That's not eerie at all," Felix said, and walked faster.

The cave swallowed sound before it swallowed light. Twenty feet in, the wash of the surf behind them went dull and distant, as though they'd stepped through a curtain, and the air changed — colder, drier than any sea cave had a right to be, with a faint movement in it, a slow breathing draft that pushed past them from deeper in. Ines stopped dead.

"Do you feel that?"

"Airflow." Felix played the flashlight beam along the walls. "Cave breathes. Means there's another way in somewhere. Higher up, probably. These things usually —"

He stopped talking, because the light had found the marks.

They covered the walls. Both walls, from the drip line down to the silted floor, and up above the drip line too, marching away into the dark farther than the beam could follow. Not graffiti. Not the random scrawl of tide or ice or bored sailors. They were cut into the stone deliberately, evenly, in vertical columns, each mark a short deep stroke with a slight hook at the bottom, row upon row upon row, thousands of them, tens of thousands, the rock face worked over so thoroughly it looked like fabric, like knitting, like the pages of some enormous book written in a language made of one word repeated until the word broke.

Ines made a small sound. She had both hands pressed to her mouth.

"Tally," she said through her fingers. "Felix. It's a tally. Someone stood in this cave and counted something. Counted *for* something. This is — these are cut marks, look at the regularity, look at the —" She moved along the wall, tracing columns with a fingertip held just above the stone, not touching, a scientist's discipline warring visibly with the need to touch. "This isn't hours or fish or days. Who counts *this* many of anything?"

"Miners count shifts," Felix said quietly. His voice sounded wrong in the cave, flattened, close. "Sailors count days. Prisoners count everything."

"Prisoners," Ines repeated.

The draft moved again, that slow tidal breath from the dark, and the flashlight beam trembled in his grip — just slightly, just enough that he knew she noticed and was kind enough not to say so.

They went deeper. He told himself it was to keep her from going alone, which was true, and also that he wanted to see the end of the wall, which was also true, and neither truth explained why his heart was working like this, hard and shallow, the way it used to work years ago in another life when doors opened onto rooms he shouldn't enter. The floor sloped gently down. The tally marks continued, column after column, and here and there among them were other signs: a date chiseled in a formal old-fashioned hand — *NOV 1911* — and beside it, smaller, almost shy, a single letter repeated like a signature. *K.*

"Kessler," Ines breathed. "That's a K. The family initial. Felix, the Keslers built the mine up-valley in — what, 1908? 1909? This could be connected to the mine. This could be a *mine* entrance."

"This is a sea cave."

"It's a sea cave that somebody decorated." She swept her arm at the walls. "You don't cut twenty thousand tally strokes into rock for fun. Somebody lived down here, or worked down here, or —"

She stopped. Her light had caught something at the base of the far wall, half sunk in the hardened silt, and whatever it was, it wasn't stone.

It was a box. Iron, maybe two feet square, its lid rounded like a strongbox from a photograph of another century, and the rock had grown over it — grown *through* it — mineral flow sealing its edges to the cave floor as firmly as a tree roots around a fence post. Rust had bloomed across it in orange continents. A keyhole stared out from the front plate, small and round and blind.

Neither of them spoke for a moment. Above them, faintly, the gulls had begun to make noise again — a shifting, restless sound, like a crowd before bad news.

"That's not shipwreck debris," Ines said finally, very calmly, in the voice people use when calm is costing them something. "Shipwreck gets scattered. That was placed. That was placed and then the cave did the rest." She crouched, her nose inches from the rust, reading it like text. "Wrought iron. Riveted seams. Pre-1920, easy. Felix, this has been down here since before anyone living was born, and someone put it here on purpose, in a cave full of tally marks, behind a door the ocean kept locked."

"Or the ocean put it here," Felix said. "Boxes fall off ships."

"With the rock grown through the handles? Over a *keyhole*?" She looked up at him, and the wonder in her face had shifted, curdled at the edges into something else — the same shift he could feel happening in his own chest, delight souring slowly into dread, the way milk goes. "Someone hid this. Someone counted something terrible enough to need hiding, and then they hid the proof of it, and the sea kept their secret for a hundred years, and today — today of all mornings — it didn't."

Felix knelt beside the box. Up close he could see that the rust had patterns in it, streaks where hands had touched it, maybe, long ago, or maybe just water finding its way. He set his palm flat on the cold iron lid, and the cave breathed its slow breath around them, and he thought: *leave it.* He thought it clearly, almost peacefully. Leave it for the tide to take back. Some things are buried at the correct depth. He had spent eleven years learning to fly over the buried parts of the world, his own included, and every instinct he owned said stand up, walk out, let the ocean have it back tonight and forever.

Instead he heard himself say: "We're not getting this out. Not without tools. It's part of the cave now."

"I know." Ines was writing in her notebook, fast, the pencil scratching. "I know. We document it. Position, depth, tide tables, photographs — do you have a camera? We come back. Properly. With people who know what they're looking at." She paused, pencil hovering. "Felix. Whoever cut those marks — that's a lot of marks. That's months of counting. Years, maybe. And then someone sealed a box into the floor and let the sea close the door behind them."

"Yeah," Felix said.

"Nobody does that for fish."

"No," he said. "Nobody does that for fish."

His hand was still on the box. Through the iron, faint but unmistakable, he could feel it: the draft from deeper in the cave, moving against his skin, steady as a pulse. Air meant openings. Openings meant the mountain above them was hollow too, that this black mouth at the tide line was only the lowest room of some larger silence stacked up into the rock all the way toward the glacier.

The gulls screamed all at once, a single ragged chorus, and somewhere far above them — muffled, miles of stone away — came a sound like a door closing.

Ines looked up. "What was that?"

"Mountain settling," Felix said. "Or ice. Or nothing."

He took his hand off the box. On his palm, printed in rust, was the ghost of the maker's stamp he hadn't even noticed seeing: worn letters, half legible, the old serifed confidence of a company that had never doubted it would last forever.

*K. MINING & POWER CO.*

Behind them, far down the emptied seabed, the sun climbed and the tide, patient as everything else down here, began — invisibly, inch by inch — to change its mind.
