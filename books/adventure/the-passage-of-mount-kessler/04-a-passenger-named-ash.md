## 4. A Passenger Named Ash

The call came in on a Tuesday, which was already suspicious. Nothing good ever happened to Felix Joubert on a Tuesday. Tuesdays were for fuel runs and engine oil and the slow administrative rot of keeping a forty-year-old de Havilland Beaver airborne on a coast that wanted it dead.

The radio crackled at seven in the morning, while he was still working a rag around the number three cylinder with the tenderness of a man apologizing to an animal he'd kicked by accident.

"Joubert Air," he said, because the phone in the hangar still rang through the same ancient system as everything else in Kessler Point, and half the time you couldn't tell who was calling or from where.

"Mr. Joubert." A voice like cold water over smooth stones. Educated. Measured. The kind of voice that had never once had to shout across an engine bay. "I understand you do charter work."

"I fly things places. Sometimes people."

"Good. I need to go to Kessler Point. From Meridian. Tomorrow, if possible."

Felix sat back on his heels and looked out through the hangar doors at the water, flat as poured pewter under a sky the color of old zinc. Kessler Point was a ninety-minute hop from Meridian, most of it over open sound. It wasn't a difficult flight. It wasn't even an interesting one. Which meant someone willing to pay charter rates for it either didn't know any better or had a reason of their own.

"Round trip?" he asked.

"One way. I'll make my own arrangements back."

*Everyone says that,* Felix thought. *And then they're standing on my strip in November asking what my rates are like.* But he quoted a figure anyway — high enough to be honest about the hassle, low enough not to scare off whatever this was — and the voice on the other end accepted it without so much as a breath of negotiation. That told Felix more than the voice itself had. People who didn't haggle were either rich or desperate, and usually both.

"Name for the manifest?"

A pause. Short enough to seem casual, long enough to be a decision.

"Ash Corrigan."

"Corrigan. Right. Strip's gravel, Mr. Corrigan. Wear boots you don't love."

He hung up and stood there a moment with the rag in his hand, listening to the tick of cooling metal and the far-off complaint of gulls. Something about the voice itched at him, the way a splinter itches — too deep to grab, too present to ignore. He went back to the cylinder and tried to put it out of his mind, and mostly failed.

* * *

Ash Corrigan arrived the next morning in a floatplane taxi from Meridian wearing clothes that cost more than Felix's annual insurance premium and carrying a leather duffel that had clearly never been anywhere rougher than a hotel lobby. He was tall, mid-thirties maybe, dark-haired, clean-shaven, with the kind of jawline that looked drawn rather than grown — a strong, squared jaw, slightly cleft at the chin, the kind you saw on coins and courthouse friezes and, once, on a faded photograph taped inside the Kessler Point general store, next to a caption reading *E. KESSLER II, 1948.*

Felix saw the jaw and felt the ground tilt a degree, the way it did when a current grabbed the floats before he was ready.

Because Felix knew that jaw. Everyone on the coast knew that jaw. It hung in the store, it hung in the church hall, it hung over the bar at the Anchor like a portrait of a saint nobody could quite bring themselves to pray to. Three generations of Keslers had worn it, each one a little more polished than the last, and the youngest of them — Elias the Third, heir to the timber money, the shipping money, the land that ran from the tideline to the tree line and half the mountain besides — had been photographed in it just last spring accepting some regional business award, smiling with teeth so even they looked rented.

The man standing on Felix's dock called himself Ash Corrigan, and he wore the jaw like a man wearing a borrowed coat, hoping the sleeves wouldn't give him away.

They gave him away immediately. At least to Felix.

"Mr. Joubert." He extended a hand. The handshake was firm, brief, practiced. "Appreciate you making room on short notice."

"Fits your schedule, I fit mine." Felix jerked his chin toward the Beaver, moored at the dock with her paint gone chalky and her struts patched in three places. "She's ugly but she flies straight."

"She's a Beaver, isn't she? DHC-2." Corrigan walked toward her with an appraiser's eye, trailing a hand along the gunwale. "Radial's been rebuilt recently. You can hear it in the idle."

"You know aircraft."

"I grew up around them." Something flickered behind his eyes when he said it — a quick, controlled movement, like a man adjusting a mask that had slipped a quarter inch. "Family had a lodge up north. Floatplanes everywhere."

Felix let that sit. Up north. Sure. A lodge. He loaded the duffel himself, noting its weight — light, mostly clothes, no survey gear, no camping kit, nothing that said *I'm staying* and nothing much that said *I'm leaving either.* Just a man passing through, or pretending to be.

"Where am I dropping you exactly?" he asked, once they were strapped in and the engine was coughing itself awake. "Point's got one strip and it's more gravel than strip."

"The point is fine. I'm meeting someone there."

"Anyone I'd know?"

Another pause. Another decision made in real time. "I doubt it."

Felix taxied out, ran his checks, and took them off the water into a morning that had turned bright and hard-edged overnight, the sea glittering like hammered tin. To the east the mountains stood up white against the blue, Mount Kessler among them, its glacier spilling down the valley in a frozen river that ended, these days, well short of the sea. He climbed to four thousand, leveled off, and settled into the hum.

Corrigan sat in the right seat with his hands folded, watching the coastline scroll beneath them, and after ten minutes of silence he began to talk. Felix had flown enough passengers to know the difference between nervous chatter and prepared questions, and this was prepared questions wearing nervous chatter's coat.

"Long route," Corrigan observed. "You must know every inlet on this coast."

"Some of them."

"That cave everyone's talking about — the one the tide opened up near the point. You seen it?"

There it was. Third question in, no warm-up, straight to it. Felix kept his eyes on the horizon and his voice level. "Seen the mouth of it. Tide dropped lower than anyone's seen in fifty years, left it high and dry for a day. Half the town walked out to look."

"And? What's in it?"

"Mud. Rocks. Smell like a drowned cat." He shrugged, exaggerating the motion so it read through the vibration. "Caves are caves."

"Someone said there were markings on the walls. Carvings."

"Did they." Felix adjusted the mixture, buying himself two seconds. Ines Okafor had talked. Of course she had — she'd spent the whole boat ride back to the strip vibrating with it, and Ines was constitutionally incapable of not telling people things; it was how science worked, she'd said, it was how *everything* worked. But she'd told the town there were carvings. She hadn't told the town what else they'd found, because neither of them had opened the strongbox yet, and Felix had made her promise — *promise me, Ines* — that whatever was in it stayed between them until they understood it.

"Old marks," he said now. "Scratches. Tide does strange things to rock."

"These weren't tide marks. I heard they were deliberate. Rows of them. Like a tally."

Felix looked at him then, briefly, and found Corrigan looking back with an expression of such careful blankness that it was practically a confession. Nobody asked about tallies. Nobody who hadn't already heard a description detailed enough to picture them.

"You're well-informed for a tourist," Felix said.

"I read a lot." Corrigan smiled, and it was a good smile, warm even, and it reached all the way to eyes that didn't participate. "My family's in resource development. We pay attention to anything historical that turns up near our holdings. Liabilities, you understand. If something's found on private land, there are processes."

"There are."

"So if a cave appeared on Kessler Point land — hypothetically — whose land would it be on, exactly? The mean high-water mark shifts with a tide like that. There's case law."

"Hypothetically," Felix said, "you'd want a lawyer, not a bush pilot."

"I have lawyers." He said it without pride, the way you'd say *I have lungs.* "What I don't have is a pilot who knows the ground. Which is why I'm here. I may need more flights. Survey flights, if it comes to that. Regular work."

And there it was, laid out on the seat between them like bait on a hook: regular work. Steady pay. The thing every independent operator on the coast dreamed about and none of them admitted to dreaming about, because wanting it made you ownable. Felix had spent ten years making sure nobody owned him. He owed no one. He stayed nowhere. His hangar lease ran month to month, his bank account held exactly enough, and the only promise he'd kept longer than a season was the one he'd made to the airplane.

But the Beaver needed a top overhaul by fall. He'd known it since June. Fourteen thousand dollars, minimum, and the engine would not be argued with — radial engines were honest that way, the only honest thing in his life.

He flew on in silence for a while, and the silence did its work on him, the way silences always did. Below, the coast unspooled: black rock, green timber, the white stitch of creeks falling into the sound. Somewhere under that water, past the point, a cave sat full of deliberate marks, rows upon rows of them, carved by hands that had wanted someone — anyone — to count. And here in the right seat sat the jawline from the general store wall, asking whose land the counting was on.

*Say something,* Felix thought. *Tell him you know. Watch the mask come all the way off.*

It would be easy. It would even feel good, for about four seconds — the pleasure of catching a liar, of watching a rich man's composure crack over open water with nowhere to hide. And then what? Then Elias Kessler III — because that's who this was, Felix would bet the airplane on it — would simply find another pilot, another boat, another way, and Felix would have gained nothing except the knowledge that he'd flushed the biggest fish of his life over a moment of vanity. Or worse: then Kessler would decide Felix was a problem, and men like that had lawyers and process and, if the stories were true, other tools besides.

Or he could say nothing. Take the money. Fly the man to the point and let whatever came next come, and keep his hands technically clean, which was the dirtiest kind of clean there was.

The glacier slid past below the left wing, blue-white and crevassed like broken porcelain, calving season still weeks away. Felix thought about Ines, who had held the rusted strongbox in her lap on the ride home like it was a sleeping child. He thought about the ledger still sealed in his hangar, wrapped in a tarp, unopened, patient. He thought about fourteen thousand dollars and a rebuilt engine and a winter with work in it.

He thought, finally, about the tally. Hundreds of marks. Somebody had counted those workers one by one, in the dark, with a chisel, and had trusted the rock to remember what nobody else would.

"Regular work," Felix said at last, and his voice sounded, to his own ears, like a door closing somewhere far away. "Depends on the flying."

"Nothing dangerous. Transport, observation. Discretion, obviously."

"Discretion costs extra."

Corrigan laughed — a real laugh this time, startled out of him — and for a moment, just a moment, the mask slipped and something younger and wearier showed underneath, something that looked almost relieved to be caught. Then it was gone, smoothed over, and the jaw was set again, and the plane droned on toward the point.

"Name your price," Corrigan said.

Felix named it. It was higher than fair and they both knew it, and Corrigan paid it without blinking, and the transaction sat in Felix's chest like a swallowed stone for the rest of the flight.

* * *

Kessler Point came up out of the sea the way it always did — a scatter of rooftops and drying nets, the cannery's dead crane leaning over the harbor like a heron that had given up, and behind it all the mountain, huge and indifferent, holding its weather and its secrets in equal measure. Felix set the Beaver down on the gravel strip with his usual economy, rolled her out, and shut down in the lee of the fuel shack.

Corrigan stepped out onto the gravel, looked around at the town with an expression Felix couldn't read — hunger, maybe, or grief, or the particular discomfort of a man returning to a place that didn't know him — and then turned back.

"You've been flying out of here long?" he asked.

"Ten years."

"Ever think about leaving?"

"Every day." Felix wiped his hands on a rag that was more rag than cloth. "Then I refuel and think about it tomorrow instead."

Something moved behind Corrigan's eyes again — recognition, almost, the look of a man hearing his own disease diagnosed in a stranger. "Yeah," he said quietly. "Tomorrow."

He picked up his duffel and walked down the strip toward the town, and Felix watched him go, noting how the townspeople glanced at him and looked away, sensing without knowing why that he was somebody, noting how Corrigan's stride changed the moment he passed the general store — slowed, just slightly, just enough — as though the photograph in the window had reached out and touched him.

Felix stood by the airplane until the stranger was out of sight. Then he walked into the hangar, pulled the tarp off the strongbox, and sat down on an oil drum across from it, the way you'd sit across from a witness you weren't ready to question.

He'd taken the money. He'd said nothing. And now the ledger sat there waiting, and the man with the family jaw was walking into town under a false name, and somewhere between the two facts lay the rest of Felix's life, whichever way he chose to live it.

Outside, far up the valley, the glacier groaned — a long, low sound, like something turning over in its sleep — and Felix realized he was going to have to decide soon what side of the counting he stood on.

Three days later, Ines Okafor burst into the hangar waving a copy of the ledger page and saying she'd found the name, and Felix understood that the deciding, whatever he'd told himself, had already been done.
