## 5. Crew of Four

Ines Okafor had learned to read her brother's silences the way other people read weather, and when she called him from Felix Joubert's hangar — standing in the smell of avgas and cold iron with a 1911 ledger open on a workbench between them like something that might still bite — Tomas went quiet for eleven full seconds before he said anything at all.

"Say it again," he said.

"Workers paid through October. Gone by spring. No deaths recorded. No funerals. No graves." She turned a page with the eraser end of a pencil, careful not to touch the ink with her fingers. "Two hundred and eleven names, if the payroll is complete. And the last entry is initialed by someone whose surname is Kessler."

Another silence. Through the hangar's open door she could see the tide going about its ordinary business, the water climbing back up the rocks it had abandoned two days ago, patient as a debt collector. Somewhere under all that green-gray water was the cave mouth again, sealed as thoroughly as if it had never been found at all. Only the strongbox had come out, and only because Ines had begged and Felix had grumbled and the tide had given them forty minutes of grace.

"You're going back," Tomas said. Not a question. He knew her the way she knew him.

"I'm going back properly. With people. With gear." She looked down at the ledger, at the columns of wages — fourteen dollars a week, thirteen dollars, eleven for the youngest-sounding names — and felt the old vertigo of history, the sense of standing on a floor that was thinner than anyone believed. "The upper entrance has to exist. The airflow says so. That cave breathes, Tomás. Caves that breathe have more than one mouth."

"And you want me to find it."

"I want you to get us there alive finding it."

He laughed then, short and dry, the laugh he'd had since they were children sharing a bunkroom in a station house outside Punta Arenas while their parents argued about grant money in the next room. "You always did ask for impossible things like they were favors."

"Because you always did them."

* * *

Tomas arrived nine days later on the coastal ferry, stepping off into a wind that smelled of kelp and diesel, his duffel over one shoulder and his ice tools wrapped in canvas like a surgeon's kit. He was broader than she remembered, or maybe she'd just gotten used to measuring people against the narrow frame of a bush plane's door. His beard had gone from black to salt-and-pepper at the temples without asking her permission. Forty-one years old and he looked like every glacier guide she'd ever trusted with her life, which was to say: unremarkable until you needed him not to be.

He hugged her hard enough to crack a rib, held her at arm's length, and studied her face with the particular attention of someone who had spent thirty years reading snowpack.

"You haven't slept," he said.

"I've slept."

"You've slept the way a person sleeps in a room where they know something is under the floorboards." He picked up his duffel. "Show me the book."

She showed him the book in Felix's hangar, and she watched her brother — who had crossed crevasse fields in whiteout, who had dug clients out of avalanches with his bare hands, who did not startle — go very still over those pages. He read the way he traveled: slowly, completely, missing nothing.

"This one," he said finally, tapping a name near the bottom of an October page. "Aldous Wren. Eleven dollars a week. That's a boy's wage."

"The records say the mine hired boys from twelve up."

"Eleven dollars." He closed the ledger gently, the way you'd close a coffin lid. "Okay. Who else knows?"

"Felix. And now you."

"That's three people and a dead company, and already I feel like we're conspiring." He looked at her. "Ines. You're going to need more than family for this. If what you're saying is true — if this is what it looks like — you'll need someone who can make it *matter*. Otherwise it's a box of paper in a hangar and the Keslers own everything around it, including the ground it was buried in."

She had known this was coming. She had known it since the night she and Felix sat in the hangar drinking bad coffee and neither of them said the obvious thing aloud: that evidence without witness was just weight, and weight could be dropped into deep water by people with money and patience.

"There's someone," she said.

* * *

Ruthie Calloway lived above the newspaper office in Kessler Point in an apartment that smelled of burnt coffee and old newsprint, surrounded by the sedimentary layers of twenty years of journalism — clippings, proofs, half-written features on things like the new breakwater and the salmon quota dispute. She was sixty-three and built like a sparrow, all sharp bone and quick movement, and when Ines knocked on her door at seven in the morning Ruthie opened it already dressed, already caffeinated, already suspicious.

"Ines Okafor," she said. "You haven't come to see me since your fellowship announcement. Which means either you want something or you feel guilty. Come in. Both, probably."

"Mostly the first."

"Good. Guilt is exhausting." Ruthie poured coffee without asking and gestured at a chair stacked with proofs; Ines moved them to the floor and sat. "So. You've found something."

"How do you—"

"Because you look like my editor looked the day the Watergate story broke, except younger and better rested, and because nobody visits me at seven a.m. to talk about the breakwater." Ruthie settled across from her, hands wrapped around her mug, eyes bright and merciless. "Out with it."

And here was the thing Ines had turned over all night, the thing that had kept her staring at the ceiling of her rented room while the tide came in and went out below: how much to tell. Because Ruthie Calloway was the best journalist on this coast, which meant she was exactly the person they needed, which meant she was also exactly the person who would dig past whatever boundaries Ines drew and keep digging, because that was what she was *for*, and somewhere on the far side of all that digging was a name — Kessler — attached to a family that owned the newspaper's printing contract, the harbor lease, half the town's mortgages, and possibly, Ines suspected, the silence that had kept this story dead for a hundred and fourteen years.

So Ines told her most of it. The cave. The carvings. The strongbox. The payroll. The winter with no graves.

She did not tell her about Ash Corrigan, because there was nothing yet to tell — the stranger hadn't returned, and Ines had half convinced herself he'd been a dream, some rich tourist's idle curiosity. And she did not say the name Kessler, though she watched Ruthie arrive at the edge of it herself, saw the moment the older woman's face changed, the arithmetic happening behind her eyes.

"A payroll," Ruthie said slowly. "For men who died in a winter nobody wrote down. On Kessler land." She set her mug down with a click that sounded final. "Do you understand what you're holding?"

"Yes."

"No, I mean do you *understand*." Ruthie leaned forward. "I've written obituaries for nineteen years. Nineteen years of summing up lives in four hundred words, and do you know what I've learned? That the dead don't need much. A name. A date. Someone to say *they were here*. That's all any of us get, and it's enough, if somebody does it honestly." Her voice had gone quiet and fierce. "Somebody took two hundred and eleven of those from these men. Somebody decided they were cheaper forgotten. And you're sitting on the proof."

"So you'll help."

"I'll help." Ruthie stood, already moving, already pulling a battered leather satchel from a drawer, and Ines understood that the decision had been made before she ever knocked. "But hear me, girl. Whatever you're not telling me — and you're not telling me something, don't bother denying it, I've interviewed politicians for two decades — whatever it is, it comes out before we're on that ice. I don't walk into a story blind. Blind reporters die stupid, and worse, they print stupid."

Ines held her gaze. "Before the ice," she said. "I promise."

It was a promise she already knew she couldn't keep cleanly, and the knowing sat in her chest like swallowed stone.

* * *

They met in Felix's hangar on a Sunday when the light came low and gold through the big doors and turned the dust motes into drifting constellations. Felix had pushed his plane halfway out onto the apron to make room, and someone — Ines suspected Ruthie — had brought a folding table, and on the table were maps, tide tables, the ledger in its acid-free sleeve, and a pot of chili that Tomas had made because Tomas believed no serious decision should be made hungry.

Five chairs. Ines counted them twice.

Felix stood apart from the table, arms folded, wearing the expression of a man attending a meeting about his own funeral. "I want it noted," he said, "that I voted for leaving it in the box."

"Noted," Ines said. "Overruled."

"One vote doesn't override—"

"It does when the vote is yours and the rest of us are right."

Ruthie snorted into her chili. Tomas almost smiled, which for Tomas was effusive. And Ines felt it then, the first fragile stitch of the thing she'd been trying to build alone in her head for two weeks: not a plan exactly, not yet, but a *we*. It frightened her how good it felt. She had spent her whole career in small teams assembled for short missions — dive surveys, core samples, six weeks on a research vessel and then scatter — and she knew precisely how temporary such bonds were, and precisely how much she loved them anyway, and precisely how much it cost when they ended. Here was another one being born, and she could already feel its expiration date somewhere out past the calving season.

"Three weeks," Tomas said, tapping the map. "Maybe less. The melt starts early some years. Once calving opens the lower route, the glacier margin turns to soup and the crevasses stop pretending to be solid. We go up the eastern margin, here—" his finger traced a line through the white — "camp once at the shoulder, push to the rockfall zone on day two. If the upper entrance exists, it'll be within two hundred meters of that fall. Airflow, Ines?"

"Cold air drains downhill. The tide cave exhales steadily even at high tide — I timed it. That volume of exchange means a second opening, higher up. Physics doesn't lie."

"Physics lies constantly," Felix muttered. "It just leaves a paper trail."

"Then we follow the paper trail." Ines spread the supply list Ruthie had typed up — actual typewriter, actual carbons, because Ruthie Calloway trusted nothing digital and nothing newer than 1987. Rope, anchors, tents rated for the shoulder season, ten days of food for five, emergency cache. "We go in light, we document everything, we take nothing out but copies and photographs. The original stays in situ until we decide otherwise."

"Decide otherwise," Felix repeated. "That's a phrase doing a lot of work."

"It's an honest phrase."

"It's a lawyer's phrase."

"It's a scientist's phrase," Ines said, and held his eyes until he looked away first, out the open door toward the water, jaw tight. She knew what he wasn't saying. He'd told her once, late, over that same bad coffee: *Everything I have is this plane and the goodwill of people who let me land on their strips. You're asking me to spend both.* She heard it anyway, underneath his silence, and she hated the fairness of it.

The knock came at four o'clock, while Tomas was arguing about rope diameters and Ruthie was annotating the margin of the supply list with the word *RECEIPTS* in letters visible from orbit.

Ash Corrigan stood in the doorway with a bottle of decent whisky in one hand and a duffel at his feet, and the afternoon light behind him made it hard to read his face, which Ines suspected was a skill rather than an accident.

"Heard there's an expedition forming," he said. "Small town."

Felix's voice came flat off the tin walls. "How'd you hear that?"

"Harbor gossip travels faster than radio." Ash stepped inside, nodded to each of them in turn — polite, unhurried — and set the whisky on the table like a toll. "I'm listening to the shape of this. Glacier approach, cave survey, historical documentation. I've done survey work. Ten years, interior mapping, mostly karst. I'm offering."

"You're offering," Felix said.

"I'm offering."

"Why?" It was Ruthie who asked it, sharp as a gaff hook, and Ines could have kissed her. "People don't volunteer for cold work and strangers' chili out of neighborliness. What do you want out of it, Mr. Corrigan?"

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, quickly mastered. "The same thing you want, I'd guess. To be there when it opens." He looked at the ledger in its sleeve, and Ines watched him look, and thought: *he already knows what's in it. He's confirming.* "I read about the tide. Came to see the cave. Missed it by a day. This is the next best thing, and I'd rather earn it than buy it."

Silence. The chili steamed. Out on the apron, Felix's plane ticked as its metal cooled, a small metallic heartbeat.

Tomas spoke last, which was his habit and his authority. "Can you travel on glacier? Crevasse rescue certified?"

"Self-arrest, haul systems, anchor building. I'm rusty but honest about it."

"Rope's a language," Tomas said. "You forget it fast, and it gets people killed. First day on ice, you're on a short leash. Non-negotiable."

"Fair."

"And you carry your own weight. All of it. We're not a charter service."

"Also fair."

Tomas looked around the table — at Ines, at Ruthie, at Felix, who gave a shrug that managed to communicate *I object, proceed* — and then at Ash, long and level, the assessment of a man who had judged a thousand clients and buried two.

"Welcome aboard," he said.

They shook on it. Ash's grip was cool and brief. When he turned to help Ruthie repack the maps, Ines caught Felix watching the man with an expression she couldn't parse — not suspicion exactly, something older and wearier, the look of a man recognizing a shape he'd hoped never to see again. She filed it away, one more item on the growing inventory of things everyone was not saying.

* * *

By full dark the hangar had emptied — Ruthie home to file nothing and write everything, Tomas asleep in the bunkroom Felix kept for stranded pilots, Ash gone wherever Ash went, walking back toward town through the blue evening with his duffel and his secrets. Ines stayed to fold the maps, and Felix stayed because Felix always stayed, orbiting his hangar like a moon that had forgotten any other course.

"Five of us," she said.

"Mm."

"You didn't want him along."

Felix straightened a wrench that was already straight. "I don't want any of this along. Him especially." He paused. "You ever meet a man who answers questions you haven't asked yet?"

"Every politician Ruthie's ever interviewed."

"Yeah, well." He killed the workbench lamp, and the dark came in off the water, huge and patient. "Politicians lie for votes. This one lies like a man protecting something. There's a difference, and I've flown cargo for both kinds." He looked at her across the dark, and his voice dropped to nearly nothing. "Watch him, Ines. Whatever he told you he wants — it isn't what he wants."

She walked home along the tideline afterward, the ledger's contents turning over in her mind like stones in surf, and above the point the mountain stood black against the stars, breathing its slow cold air down through stone and ice and a hundred years of deliberate silence, waiting to see what they would do with what it had finally shown them.
