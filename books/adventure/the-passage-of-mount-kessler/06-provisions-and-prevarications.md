## 6. Provisions and Prevarications

The hangar smelled of diesel and old canvas and, faintly, of the sea — a smell that had soaked into Felix Joubert's life so thoroughly he no longer noticed it, the way a man stops hearing his own heartbeat. Ines Okafor noticed it. She noticed everything now: the way light came through the corrugated roof in ribbons and lay across the workbench like something spilled; the way the strongbox sat open on the bench, its ledger wrapped now in a flour sack and then a plastic bag and then a dry bag, each layer added without discussion, as though all three of them had agreed by silence that the thing should be hidden even from the room it sat in.

They had three weeks. That was the number everyone kept saying, the way sailors say a tide time or pilots say a ceiling. Three weeks before calving season opened the glacier route and made the overland approach possible — and, not long after, impossible again. Three weeks to provision an expedition, recruit a crew, and get up the ice road to whatever waited behind the rockfall at the cave's upper throat.

Ines had spent the morning making lists on the back of a tide chart, her handwriting getting smaller as the lists got longer. Rope. Stoves. A week's food for five. Radios — two, minimum, because one was a prayer and two were a plan. Tomas would know what else. Tomas always knew.

She looked up when the hangar door rattled back on its track and Ruthie Calloway came in out of the rain, shaking water off a coat that had been good twenty years ago and had decided, sometime since, to stop pretending.

"Your brother says yes," Ruthie announced, dropping onto a crate marked SPARES in Felix's blocky capitals. "Tomas. He's in. He wants to know who's paying for the rope."

"Felix," Ines said.

"Does Felix know that?"

"He will."

Ruthie grinned, then let the grin fade with the deliberation of a woman setting down something heavy. She nodded at the dry bag on the bench. "And that comes too?"

"That's the whole point of going."

"Uh-huh." Ruthie pulled off her boots, tipped them, and poured out a surprising amount of water. "You want to tell me why a marine biologist needs a journalist along on a glaciology survey?"

"Because you asked to come."

"I did. I'm asking why you said yes." Ruthie leaned forward, elbows on knees, and there it was — the look Ines had seen her wear across a hundred harbor-bar tables, the look that had gotten obituaries rewritten and one harbormaster briefly indicted. It was not an unkind look. It was a patient one, which was worse. "Ines. I've known you since we were digging clams for cigarette money. You don't invite me anywhere unless you want something witnessed. So either you tell me what I'm witnessing, or I find out on the ice, and I promise you the second version goes worse for everybody."

The rain ticked against the roof. Somewhere outside, Felix's dog — a retired sled animal named Biscuit who belonged to no one and everyone — shook himself against the hangar wall.

Ines looked at the dry bag. Then at Ruthie. Then at the door, which was closed, and at the window, which was small, and high, and frosted with salt.

"There's a ledger," she said. "From 1911. It records wages paid to men who worked a tunnel under Kessler Point. Cross-reference the names against the town registry and every one of them dies in the same winter — a winter nobody wrote down. No storm record. No burial notices. Nothing. Two hundred and eleven marks carved into the cave wall, one per man, and the last wage entry is signed by a Kessler."

Ruthie had gone very still. Not frozen — Ines knew the difference. This was the stillness of a woman loading a rifle inside herself, chamber by chamber.

"And Felix knows?"

"Felix found it."

"And the tall, well-moneyed stranger who's been asking after seats on this expedition?"

Ines hesitated one beat too long, and Ruthie saw it, and Ines watched her see it, and understood that she had just taught Ruthie Calloway where the soft place in this story was.

"Ash Corrigan," Ines said. "Claims survey experience. Pays his share."

"Sure he does." Ruthie stood, retrieved her ruined boots, and pulled them back on with the grim efficiency of a woman who had already begun writing the story in her head. "Three weeks, you said?"

"Maybe less. Weather's a liar up here."

"Then I'll need film. And a second notebook. And Ines—" she paused at the door, hand on the latch, rain-light behind her, "—when you're ready to tell me the part about why everyone in this hangar keeps looking at that dry bag like it's a bomb, I'll be at the Anchor. I'm easy to find. That's the whole trouble with me."

* * *

Tomas Okafor distrusted Ash Corrigan for reasons he could not have listed if you'd asked him, and so he didn't ask himself, he simply acted on them, the way he acted on the feel of snow underfoot or the color of the sky at four in the afternoon. Trust, in Tomas's trade, was not a feeling. It was a calculation performed continuously, at speed, with incomplete data, and the penalty for error was written into the ice every spring in the form of ropes cut loose and names read into the wind.

He'd met Corrigan exactly once — in the hangar, shaking hands, exchanging the usual nothing — and the calculation had come back wrong in four places.

One: the man's boots were broken in correctly but recently, expensive leather softened fast by someone who wanted to look like a walker without being one. Two: his rope calluses were in the wrong places, palm-heavy rather than finger-heavy, gym calluses, or boat calluses. Three: he watched Felix's hands during conversation, not Felix's eyes, which meant he was thinking about what those hands could do rather than what their owner intended. Four — and this was the one Tomas kept returning to, turning it over like a stone looking for the cool side — the man listened the way guides listen to clients: cataloguing weakness, noting pace, filing away who complained about cold and who lied about it.

None of it proved anything. Men were entitled to be careful strangers. But the expedition would cross crevassed ground roped together, four lives hanging from each other's judgment, and Tomas Okafor did not put unexplained variables on his rope.

So he tested him.

He found Corrigan at the fuel dock on the third day, helping — actually helping, hauling jerry cans with a competence that annoyed Tomas further — and said, casually, "You've done glacier travel."

"Some. Denali, years ago. A season in the Wrangells."

"Crevasse rescue. Show me your prusik hitch."

Corrigan didn't blink, didn't laugh, didn't perform confusion. He took the length of cord Tomas handed him and tied a serviceable — not elegant, serviceable — hitch around the practice sling, then looked up with an expression of mild, reasonable inquiry, as if to say: *satisfied?*

It was exactly the right answer, delivered exactly right, and that was the fifth wrong thing.

"Good enough," Tomas said, and walked away, and stood afterward at the end of the dock watching the tide come in over the flats below Kessler Point — the same flats that had laid the cave bare, now drowned again, keeping its secret the way the sea keeps everything, temporarily and without malice.

His sister thought the ledger was history. The journalist thought it was a story. The pilot thought it was, Tomas suspected, a debt of some kind, though owed to whom he couldn't say. And Corrigan — Corrigan thought it was leverage, or property, or a wound in his family's side that needed packing. Tomas didn't know which. He only knew that when the ice road opened, he would put Corrigan on his rope, and keep him there, in front, where he could see his hands.

* * *

Felix bought the second fuel cache on a Tuesday, in cash, from a man in Port Anselm who asked no questions because Felix paid him forty dollars extra not to.

It wasn't the first cache. Every pilot who flew the coast kept stashes — drums of avgas tucked into sheltered coves, wrapped against bears, logged in a waterproof notebook that lived in Felix's breast pocket like a second heart. But this one was different. This one he bought without logging it, without telling Ines, without telling anyone, and he told himself it was prudence, contingency, the ordinary paranoia of a man whose engine's opinion of him was conditional at best.

He told himself that twice, and then stopped telling himself anything, because he knew what it actually was.

It was the shape of the future he could see if they flew the ledger out. It was the Kessler name on the fuel contracts at three of the six strips he depended on. It was the memory of a phone call he'd overheard the tail end of, years ago, when he still took charter work out of the city — a lawyer's voice, smooth as ball bearings, explaining to someone what happened to businesses that inconvenienced the family. Felix hadn't caught the details. He'd caught the tone. Tone was enough. Tone was how you survived a decade of flying other people's secrets: you learned to hear the weight of a thing before you ever saw it.

So he loaded the drum into the Beaver's float compartment at dawn, ran it up-coast alone, buried it above the tideline in a cove only he knew, and flew home with the smell of turned earth on his gloves and a lie sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Everyone lying a little, he thought, taxiing in through the morning mist. The sister hiding the ledger's meaning from the journalist. The journalist hiding her suspicion from everyone. The brother hiding his distrust behind competence. Himself hiding a fuel drum in the ground like a coward's savings account. Even the mountain was lying, wearing its rockfall like a buttoned coat.

Only the dead, he figured, were telling the truth, and they'd had a hundred years of practice being ignored.

* * *

Elias Kessler III made the call from the payphone outside the Anchor Hotel, at eleven at night, in the rain, feeding quarters into the slot with fingers that did not shake, because he had trained them not to, because shaking was a luxury his name had never permitted.

The line rang four times. Then a click, and breathing, and then the voice he had known since childhood, the voice that had read him bedtime stories and board resolutions in equal measure: his father's attorney, Aldous Wren, who answered private numbers at any hour the way other men answered their own names.

"It's done, then," Wren said. No greeting. There never was.

"The site's real. The ledger exists. I've confirmed it firsthand." Elias watched the rain run down the glass booth, blurring the harbor lights into smears of orange and grey. Across the street, warm in the Anchor's window, he could see the shapes of his expedition — Ruthie laughing at something, Tomas not laughing, Ines gesturing over maps, Felix standing slightly apart the way he always did, a man perpetually halfway out a door. His people. His crew. His witnesses. "There are documents. Originals. Wage records, correspondence, a tally wall. It's everything we were told didn't exist."

A pause on the line, measured to the quarter-second. "And your assessment?"

"My assessment is that two hundred and eleven men died in a tunnel my great-grandfather ordered collapsed to hide a cost overrun, and that this family has spent a century standing on them."

Another pause. Longer. When Wren spoke again, his voice had shifted registers — lower, warmer, the voice reserved for difficult shareholders and dying friends. "Elias. Come home. We can manage this. We've managed worse."

"You mean bury it."

"I mean *manage* it. There are instruments. Land claims, historical societies, preservation easements. The site sits on deeded ground — did you confirm the parcel? The 1908 survey included the point. If the entrance lies within the boundary—"

"It does."

"Then the matter is largely one of access control." A sound like pages turning. "I've retained a survey firm. They'll charter out of the city Thursday. Their report will establish structural instability — public hazard, closure order, sealed pending engineering review. Sealed things stay sealed, Elias. You know how well this family seals things."

Elias closed his eyes. Against the blackness: the tally wall, hundreds of marks, each one a man who had signed a wage book expecting to be paid and gone home instead to nothing, to no marker, no notice, no name spoken aloud in a hundred years. He had put his hand flat against the stone in that side chamber and felt the cold come up through his palm like a voice.

He had also, in the same hour, photographed fourteen pages of the ledger and slipped a fragment of correspondence into his jacket, and he had told himself that was evidence-gathering, preservation, insurance — and had known, even as he told himself, that a man who pockets documents in the dark is not preserving anything except options.

Everyone lying a little. Even him. Especially him.

"Thursday," he said. "How many?"

"Four men and a pilot. Discreet. They'll present as a geological hazard team." Wren's voice smoothed out, professional, almost kind. "Elias — the fragment you took. Return it. Whatever game you're playing, playing it with family papers in your pocket is how boys become defendants."

The line hummed. Rain hammered the booth. Across the street, Ruthie Calloway threw her head back laughing at something Felix had said, and Felix smiled his closed-door smile, and Elias felt the strange vertigo of standing between two worlds that were about to collide over his head.

"They'll be ready," he heard himself say. "Your survey team. For the ice. It's rotten past the second moraine — they'll need a guide who knows the margin."

"We've engaged someone local."

"Who?"

"A guide. Okafor, I'm told. The brother."

The world went very quiet and very large, the way it does in the half-second before a rope comes tight.

"Tomas Okafor is *on my expedition*," Elias said. "He's guiding us up Thursday morning."

Silence. Then, softly, with something that might have been satisfaction: "Well. Then I suggest you decide soon, Elias, which side of the rope you intend to stand on."

The line went dead. Elias fed the last of his quarters into the slot out of pure habit, listening to them fall, and stood a moment longer in the wet glass light of the phone booth — a man with a fragment of paper in his pocket, a lie on his lips, and a choice arriving up the glacier toward him faster than the weather.

Across the street, the Anchor's door swung open and Tomas Okafor stepped out into the rain, collar up, heading for the dock. He paused. Looked across the road at the phone booth. Looked at Elias looking at him.

Neither man moved. The rain fell between them like a curtain half-drawn, and somewhere inland, under a hundred feet of ice, the mountain waited with its mouth full of names.
