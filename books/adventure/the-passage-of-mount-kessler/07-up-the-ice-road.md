## 7. Up the Ice Road

Tomas Okafor had been reading ice since he was nine years old, when his father first put a pole in his hands and told him the glacier would lie to him every day of his life, and that his job was to catch it in the lie. Twenty-two years on the Kessler icefield had not made him arrogant about it. It had made him careful, and careful was the only prayer the ice ever answered.

He stood at the toe of the glacier's eastern margin at first light and read the morning's lie. The surface looked like hammered pewter, clean and confident, running up toward the col in a long white sentence with no commas. But the wind had been out of the southwest for three days, and the wind had drifted snow into the crevasse fields below the shoulder, and drifted snow was the glacier's favorite disguise. Somewhere under that smooth pewter skin were mouths. There were always mouths.

"Rope's the rule," he said, turning to the four of them ranged along the moraine like a row of mismatched chess pieces. "Roped travel, spaced. Fifteen meters between each of you, no less. If I say *down*, you go down and you don't ask why until we're past it. If I say *jump*, you jump where I point, not where looks easy. Nobody walks ahead of me. Nobody. Not even if it looks like a highway."

Ruthie Calloway nodded, already writing something in the waterproof notebook she kept zipped inside her jacket, because of course she was. Ines gave him the small salute she'd been giving him since they were children sharing a bunk on the fishing boat — two fingers off the brow, half mockery, half vow. Felix Joubert stood with his hands in his pockets and the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like, and beside Felix stood the stranger.

Ash Corrigan. Tomas had not liked the name from the first hour. Names that short were usually hiding something longer.

"Questions?" he asked.

"One," Ash said. "When you probe, do you want me on the front or the back of the rope?"

Tomas studied him. It was the right question. It was, in fact, the only question a person with actual glacier time would ask first, and it annoyed Tomas more than any wrong question would have.

"Back," he said. "You'll be my anchor. If I go in a hole, you're the one who keeps the rest of them from coming with me."

Ash nodded once, no bravado in it, and began checking his harness the way Tomas checked his own — buckles doubled back, carabiner gates oriented, chest strap snug. *Competent*, Tomas thought, and disliked him a little more. Competence was a currency, and this man was spending it like he had a bottomless account, and Tomas had grown up in a town where the Kesslers had bottomless accounts and everyone else had arithmetic.

He didn't say any of that. He said, "Rope up," and turned his face into the wind, and led them up onto the ice.

* * *

The first two hours were honest travel — firm nevé, visible crevasses, the kind of ground a guide could almost relax on. Almost. Tomas probed anyway, every three steps, the pole's steel tip ringing against solid ice or sinking with a soft, terrible *whump* into nothing. Behind him the rope came alive and dead, alive and dead, four heartbeats transmitted through braided nylon.

The glacier made its own weather, and this morning it was making beauty, which Tomas distrusted most of all. Light came off the icefield in long amber blades, catching the wind-carved sastrugi and turning them to frozen flame. Far up-valley, the summit of Kessler itself wore a banner of spindrift like a flag of surrender no one had accepted. Ines, breathing hard beside him on a rest stop, said, "It doesn't look like a graveyard."

"It never does," Tomas said. "That's the trick of it. Pretty is how it gets you out past the last safe turn."

Ahead of them, Ash had crouched at the edge of a crevasse the width of a road, looking down into it with the particular stillness of a man reading something. Tomas came up beside him. The crevasse went down forty meters into blue so deep it was nearly black, and the walls of it were ribbed like the inside of a throat.

"Airflow," Ash said quietly. "You feel it? Cold air pouring out of the bottom. The whole icefield breathes through these."

Tomas did feel it — a faint exhale rising from the depths, cold and mineral, smelling of stone. It was a real phenomenon and a useful one, and the fact that Ash knew it, and knew to look for it, sat in Tomas's chest like a swallowed stone of its own.

"Where'd you do your glacier time?" Tomas asked. Casual. Not casual.

"Patagonia. Some in the Alaska Range." Ash didn't look at him when he said it, and didn't look away either, which was its own kind of answer. "You don't trust me."

"No," Tomas agreed. "I don't."

"Good," Ash said. "Trust the rope instead. The rope doesn't have a last name."

Tomas barked a short laugh before he could stop it, and hated that too. He clipped back in and moved on.

* * *

The lie came at midday, as lies do, in the middle of an easy-looking stretch.

They had crossed onto the lower shoulder where the glacier bent around a rib of black rock, and the surface ahead was a wide white apron, unbroken, inviting, the kind of ground where a tired person stops probing. Tomas didn't stop probing. On the eleventh step past the rock rib, the pole sank to its basket and kept going, silent, no resistance at all, and Tomas went down onto his knees and said, "Down. All of you. Down and spread."

They dropped. He crawled forward on his belly, distributed his weight across his forearms, and brushed the surface snow away with the side of his hand like a man uncovering a sleeping face.

Beneath six inches of fresh drift was a bridge of snow over a crevasse four meters wide, and the bridge was thinner than bread. Through a crack in it he could see straight down — thirty meters of blue dark, and at the bottom, the glint of meltwater. In July that water would be a river. In March it was a waiting room.

"Bridge won't take a dog," he said. "We go around. Single file in my steps, and my steps only. The snow on the far side is wind-slab — it'll hold, but it holds like glass. No edging. Flat feet."

He cut the detour with his axe, a long shallow arc out into the apron and back, testing every meter, and the four of them followed in his track like ducklings behind a lead bird. Ruthie's breathing was too fast. He could hear it, the little hitch at the top of each inhale, and he called back without turning, "Talk to me, Calloway. Tell me what you're writing."

"Obituary," she said, and her voice steadied on the joke. "'Ruthie Calloway, journalist, died as she lived — taking notes on someone else's disaster.'"

"Better than most people get," Tomas said. "Most people get 'died as they lived — unaware.'"

Behind Ruthie, Felix said nothing, but Tomas could hear the quality of his silence change. Felix had been quiet all morning in a way that wasn't rest. It was the quiet of a man listening for a second engine.

They came off the apron onto the rock rib and Tomas called a halt, and it was then that Ash did the thing that unsettled him most.

Ines had slipped on the last ten meters of slab — nothing serious, a boot skating, a hand out, a curse — but the rope had come tight for half a second and Felix, anchored above her, had taken her weight without a word, leaning back into his harness with the easy economy of a man who had done it before. And Ash, seeing it, had moved — not to help, but to reposition himself downhill of both of them, re-anchoring the rope's tail against a bollard of ice, so that if Felix went down too, there was a second stop in the system.

Nobody had asked him to. Nobody had even had time. He had simply read the failure chain two links ahead and quietly closed it.

Tomas stood with his axe in his hands and watched this man he did not trust be, in every observable particular, exactly the kind of partner you wanted on a rope. It was like watching a beautifully forged key turn in a lock you knew was a trap. The key still worked. That was what made it frightening.

"Nice catch," Tomas said, when Ash came up beside him.

"Nice detour," Ash said. "You cut that arc wide enough to drive a truck. I'd have cut it tighter."

"I know," Tomas said. "That's why you're on the back of the rope and I'm on the front. Tight gets people killed. Wide gets people late."

Ash smiled — a real one, brief and crooked, the first Tomas had seen — and for one unguarded moment he looked young. Then it was gone, filed away, and the polished surface was back, and Tomas turned uphill and kept moving, because the light was going amber and they still had the col to gain, and because he could not afford to keep looking at Ash Corrigan's face and finding things in it he believed.

* * *

They made the col at four in the afternoon, with the light going long and copper and the temperature falling through the teens like a stone down a well. Below them, the icefield they'd crossed spread out in the evening glow, its crevasses now visible as a lacework of blue shadow — every single one of them exactly where Tomas had expected, and three of them where he hadn't. Behind, the coast was a thin silver line. Ahead, the glacier's upper basin climbed toward the mountain's shoulder, and somewhere on that shoulder, hidden behind a rockfall that had come down nobody-knew-when, Ines believed there was a mouth.

"Airflow," Ines said, crouched at the col with her anemometer and her stubbornness. "The tide cave breathes, Tomás. I measured it — pressure cycling with the diurnal temperature swing. A dead-end cave doesn't breathe like that. There's a chimney somewhere, a second entrance, higher up. Air has to go somewhere."

"Air also goes nowhere," Tomas said. "I've spent my whole life following air that promised me a shortcut."

"This isn't a shortcut. It's a door." She looked up at the shoulder, at the black band of rockfall two hundred meters above them, and her jaw had the set it had had since she was six years old and had decided she was going to touch a seal and did. "It's their door, Tomás. Whoever carved those marks. They didn't come in with the tide. They came down from above."

Camp went in fast, practiced — Tomas and Felix on the tent platform, cutting blocks, Ruthie boiling water with hands that had finally stopped shaking, Ash building the windwall without being shown how, stacking the blocks in a herringbone that would actually stand up to the night's wind instead of the way a novice stacks them, in a pretty wall that lies down by midnight.

Felix worked beside Tomas in silence for a while, sawing blocks, and then said, without looking up: "You've been watching him all day."

"I watch everyone. It's the job."

"You've been watching him *differently*."

Tomas set a block and pressed it home. "He's good," he said finally. "That's the problem. Bad ones I can spot in ten minutes and fire. Good ones I have to let on the rope, and then I'm spending the whole day hoping the rope knows something I don't."

"And if it comes to it?" Felix asked. "If he's what you think he might be?"

Tomas straightened, and looked up the shoulder, at the black rockfall band going purple in the last light, at the door that might or might not be behind it, at the mountain holding whatever it was holding the way it had held it for a hundred years — patiently, and not kindly.

"Then I've spent today teaching him exactly how to kill us," Tomas said. "So tomorrow, I stop teaching, and I start counting."

Felix looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the nod of a man adding a weight to a side of a scale, and went back to his blocks.

They ate in the windshadow of the tent wall, and the cold came down off the summit like a held breath being released, and Tomas took first watch because he always took first watch, because first watch was when you caught the mountain changing its mind.

At nine-forty, with the stars hard and close, he saw it: a flicker of light up on the rockfall band. There and gone. Too steady for a headlamp swing, too brief for a star.

He watched the dark for a long time. Then he woke Felix, and pointed up the shoulder, and said, "Somebody's already looking for that door."
