## 8. The Upper Mouth

Ines felt the cave before she saw it.

It was a breath against her cheek — warm, faintly mineral, wrong in a way her body understood before her mind did. They had been walking the glacier's frozen margin for six hours, Tomas out front reading the ice like a man reading a letter from an old enemy, when the air moved. Not wind. Wind came in gusts, carried grit, smelled of snow. This was steady. This was *exhaled*, as though something beneath the mountain were sleeping and breathing around them.

"Stop," she said, and the word came out louder than she intended, cracking against the ice walls of the valley.

Tomas raised a fist and the line halted — Felix behind him, then Ruthie, then Ash Corrigan at the rear, roped and loaded and silent in the way of people who were not used to silence. Ines stood with her face turned into the moving air like a compass needle finding its north. Warm. Steady. Coming from the talus slope above them, where a spill of grey boulders had poured off the mountainside sometime in the last century and lain there ever since, lichen-crusted and patient.

"There," she said. "Do you feel it?"

Tomas came back down the line, unhurried, and stood beside her. He was not a dramatic man; he had spent too many years guiding rich people up frozen mountains to waste energy on theater. But she saw his nostrils flare, saw the small shift in his weight that meant his whole attention had narrowed to a point.

"Airflow means a second opening," he said. "You called it."

"I hoped I called it." Ines had spent three nights with the tide cave's temperature log scrawled across the back of a supply manifest, working the physics backward. A cave that breathed was a cave with two mouths. The sea had found the lower one. The mountain had kept the upper one, and the mountain was careless with its secrets only when the ice let it be.

"Behind the rockfall," Tomas said. "Look at the sorting. That's not one slide. That's two — the old one's been there long enough to grow that grey lichen, and there's fresher material on top. Something's been moving up there recently."

"Recently meaning?"

"Meaning within my lifetime." He unshouldered his pack. "Meaning possibly within the last ten years. Meaning we go slow."

* * *

The climb up the talus took them an hour that felt like a season. The boulders were the size of cars and houses, tilted at treacherous angles, and every one of them shifted a little under the boot, a little grinding whisper that Ines tried not to hear as a warning. Felix moved like water finding its level, light and certain, and she watched him with the particular attention she had been trying not to pay him since the hangar — the way he touched the rock, the way he never once looked back down at the valley. A man who had spent a decade not looking back. She recognized the posture. She had her own versions of it.

Ruthie climbed badly and cheerfully, narrating her own near-falls in a low deadpan that made Tomas's mouth twitch. Ash climbed well. That was the thing Ines kept circling back to, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: Ash climbed *very* well, with a economy of motion that spoke of real mountaineering, and he said almost nothing, and when he did speak it was to ask questions that were just slightly wrong. Not suspicious-wrong. Curious-wrong. The questions of a man who already knew the answers and wanted to hear how they sounded in other mouths.

*How deep do you think the chambers go? Did the carvings continue below the waterline? Was the strongbox buried, or set?*

*Set,* she had answered, before she understood why the distinction mattered. Buried things were hidden. Set things were *placed*, deliberately, by someone who intended to come back.

Now Ash stood at the base of the rockfall and looked up at the jumbled slope with an expression Ines couldn't read, and she decided — not for the first time, and with less conviction than before — that she was being unfair. Rich men were allowed to be strange. Rich men were allowed to be curious about the history of the coast their families had built.

The gap was behind a slab the size of a barn, wedged against the mountainside at a lean, and if you hadn't been following the warm breath of air you would have walked past it forever. Tomas went first, sideways, pack scraping stone. Then his voice came back down to them, flattened by rock into something hollow and strange:

"Ines. You need to see this."

* * *

The passage beyond the slab was a throat — a crawl of twenty feet through stone polished smooth by water that had not flowed here in a very long time — and then it opened, and Ines stopped breathing.

Not because of the dark. She had expected the dark, had brought three headlamps and two spare batteries and the irrational extra candle she carried on every field expedition for reasons she had never examined. She had expected cold and drip and the close mineral smell of deep rock.

What she had not expected was the walls.

The headlamps found them one at a time as the beam swung, and each time it was like a small act of violence. The carvings from the tide cave — the marks she had touched with numb fingers and told herself were maybe, possibly, some kind of survey notation — those marks were here too. But there they had been a scattered handful, weathered, ambiguous. Here they covered the stone from floor to ceiling, rank upon rank upon rank, cut deep and clean into the grey rock, marching away into the dark on both sides of the chamber as far as her light could reach.

"Oh," Ruthie said, very quietly, in the voice she probably used at bedsides. "Oh, God."

Ines walked to the nearest wall. Her boots sounded enormous. She raised her hand — she didn't remember deciding to — and laid her fingers in one of the grooves.

It was a tally mark. Four vertical strokes and a diagonal slash across them, the oldest counting system human beings had ever invented, older than writing, older than money, older than the idea that a life could be owned. The cuts were deep enough to lose her whole fingertip in. Whoever had made them had used a sharp steel tool and had pressed *hard*, had cut into stone with the kind of force that comes not from the arm but from somewhere behind the sternum.

She stepped back and swept her light along the wall, and the marks went on. Grouped in fives, the fives gathered into blocks, the blocks stacked in columns, column after column, and the columns did not stop. She walked the chamber's curve and they did not stop. She came around a rib of rock and they were on the other side of it, and they did not stop.

"Count the columns," she heard herself say. Her voice came from very far away. "Ruthie. Count the columns for me."

"Why?"

"Because I don't want to do the math alone."

Ruthie counted aloud, her voice getting smaller with each number, and Ines did the multiplication in her head anyway because she was a scientist and her traitor brain did the math whether she wanted it or not. Columns of perhaps thirty marks. Dozens of columns. And this was only the first chamber.

"Thousands," Ruthie said. "Ines, there are thousands of marks. That can't be right. That's not — the ledger only listed—"

"I know what the ledger listed." Forty-one names. Forty-one wages paid, forty-one men who had walked into the Kessler Consolidated Mining Company's employ in the autumn of 1911 and out of every record thereafter. She had lain awake two nights with that number, turning it over, telling herself that the ledger was partial, that the rest would surface, that history was always more ragged than the documents that survived it.

But the walls were not ragged. The walls were *systematic*.

"Two possibilities," she said, and her scientific voice came back to her like a rope thrown down a well, and she grabbed it. "One: this tally counts something other than people. Ore cars. Shifts. Days worked. Tally marks were used for all of it."

"And two?" Tomas asked.

She didn't answer him. She didn't have to. They all stood there in the cone of her lamplight with the columns rising around them like a forest of small grey trees, and everyone did the math, and everyone arrived at the same terrible arithmetic: that if each mark was a person, then the disaster that had fed the Kessler fortune was not a disaster at all. Disasters were singular. Disasters had a day and a date. This — this scale — was a *practice*.

"Keep moving," Felix said. His voice was rough. "Carefully. We document as we go. Ines leads."

* * *

The second chamber was smaller and the tally there was different — not cut but painted, in a pigment that had gone brown-black with age, in a hand that shook. Ines stood before it a long time. Something about the shaking hand undid her more than the thousands of clean-cut marks had. The cut marks were institutional; they had the terrible tidiness of bookkeeping. The painted marks were one person, alone, in the dark, counting something they could not bear to count and could not bear to stop counting.

She photographed everything. The camera's shutter sounded obscene in the silence, a small mechanical insect clicking away in a cathedral.

Beyond the second chamber the passage narrowed, dropped, and opened again — and here the character of the cave changed entirely, and here Ines's awe finally, fully, curdled.

The headlamps found timber.

Collapsed mine timber, shored and splintered, great black beams of it felled across the passage like the ribs of something enormous that had died here. Among the beams: iron. A hand cart with one wheel still upright, its wooden bed rotted to lace. A drill — a long steel star of it, crusted with orange rust. Boots. She saw the boots before she understood she was looking at them, two shapes in the debris that her mind kept trying to file as rocks, and then she saw the laces, and then she made herself look away because there were things she was not ready to know in the first hour.

"Nobody touches anything," she said. Her voice did not shake, and she was proud of that, and ashamed of how much effort the not-shaking cost. "This is a site. From this point on, everything is evidence."

"Evidence of what?" Ash asked.

And there it was again — that question, that slightly wrong question, the one that wanted her to say the thing out loud. She turned and looked at him. His headlamp threw his face into planes and hollows, and behind the light his eyes were unreadable, and he was standing very still, the way people stand when the answer matters to them more than they want it to.

"Evidence of work," she said. "Evidence of a mine that isn't in any record, on land that isn't in any lease, worked by men who aren't in any census. Evidence of a company that wanted this place to stop existing." She let that sit. "That's what evidence of."

Ash nodded slowly. "And if the company that wanted it to stop existing," he said, "was your own family?"

The question dropped into the chamber like a stone into still water, and the silence rippled out from it. Ines heard Ruthie's breath catch. She heard Tomas shift his weight, that small sound of a man recalculating. And Felix — she found Felix in the overlapping lamplight, standing with one hand resting on a fallen beam, looking at Ash with an expression she had never seen him wear: not surprise, but a grim, weary *recognition*, like a man watching a card fall that he had already seen in the deck.

"Ash," Felix said quietly. "Maybe let the scientist do the science."

"I'm asking a hypothetical."

"Sure you are."

The moment stretched. Somewhere deep in the mountain, water moved — a single, resonant plink that traveled down the passage and broke the silence for them, and Ines seized the interruption like a rope.

"Beyond the timber," she said. "That's the gallery. That's what the ledger describes — the east drive, the one that was 'closed for the season' in November 1911 and never reopened. The collapse wasn't the disaster, Ruthie. The collapse was the *solution*." She turned her light toward the black throat of the passage beyond the fallen beams, where the tally marks continued, painted now, shaking-hand marks, marching into the dark. "They sealed them in. They sealed the whole gallery, and they counted what they'd done, and then they walked away and built a city."

Nobody spoke. The warm air breathed past them, out toward the sea, carrying the smell of stone and rust and a hundred and eleven years.

"We can't get through that," Tomas said at last, practical as ever, his voice gentle in the way he was gentle when he was sparing someone. "Not without gear, not without days. The timber's load-bearing. We bring it down further, we bring the ceiling down."

"We're not going through," Ines said. "Not today. Today we document. Today we make it *real*." She turned in a slow circle, her beam sweeping the walls, the columns, the thousands of small deep cuts, and she felt the thing she had come here feeling — the scientist's exaltation, the pure bright joy of *found it* — and she felt it curdle and sink and turn into something with weight, something like responsibility, something like grief for two hundred strangers whose names she did not know and whose deaths had been an arithmetic problem to the people who caused them.

She thought of her brother's face when she'd shown him the ledger. She thought of Felix in the hangar, turning the strongbox's rusted lid in his hands like it might bite him. She thought of the shaking painted hand, alone in the dark, counting.

"Ines." Ruthie's voice, low. "The far wall. Come look at this."

Ines crossed the chamber. Ruthie's light was fixed on a section of wall near the sealed gallery's mouth, where the painted tally gave way to something else — a block of characters, small and dense and deliberate, cut into the stone with the same steel and the same terrible pressure as the tally marks. Not numbers. Letters. A paragraph, maybe two, the lettering archaic and formal, and above it, carved larger, a single word that Ines's light found first and her mind refused for three full seconds to read:

*WE*

She stepped closer. The cold of the stone came through her sleeve. Below the word, the smaller letters waited in their ranks, and Ruthie's hand found hers and gripped it, and somewhere behind them Felix said her name, and she bent to the rock and began, syllable by syllable, in the breath-warm dark of a mountain that had kept its silence for a century, to read what the dead had written.
