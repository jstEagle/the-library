## 9. Tally of the Lost

Ruthie Calloway had spent eleven years writing about the dead, and she had learned the difference between the kinds of silence they left behind.

There was the silence of a hospital room after a monitor went flat — clinical, relieved almost, a silence that meant someone's suffering had ended. There was the silence of an obituary desk at two in the morning, when the fax machine spat out another name and she typed *beloved husband, devoted father* into the same gray column where she'd typed it ten thousand times before. And there was the silence of a closed casket, which was not really silence at all but a kind of held breath, because everyone in the room knew something was being kept from them.

The silence inside the mountain was none of these.

It was older. It had texture. It pressed against her eardrums like deep water, and it had been accumulating for a hundred and fourteen years, and Ruthie understood, standing in the third chamber with her headlamp beam trembling slightly on the wall, that she was the first living person to hear it.

"Ruthie." Ines's voice came from behind her, hushed the way people go hushed in churches, though no one had decided to. "Come look at this section. The marks change."

Ruthie turned. Her boots crunched on gravel that was part stone, part something softer — old timber gone to punk, maybe, or the fibrous rot of rope. The chamber was long and low, its ceiling ribbed with quartz veins that caught their lamplight and threw it back broken, like ice on a windshield. Along both walls, at the height of a standing man's shoulder, ran the tally.

She had seen it yesterday, when they first descended past the rockfall. She had registered it the way you register wallpaper: hundreds of small carved strokes, grouped by fives, four vertical lines crossed by a diagonal. Miner's counting. Prisoner's counting. The oldest arithmetic in the world — one for each, and each for one.

But Ines was right. The marks changed.

For the first thirty feet or so, they were confident cuts, deep and clean, made by a strong hand with a good tool. Then, gradually, they grew shallower. Sloppier. The groups of five became irregular — some fours, some sixes where the carver had miscounted and corrected, gouging out a stroke and starting over. Near the corner where the wall bent inward, the marks were barely scratches, and several were cut sideways, as if the hand holding the tool could no longer control its angle.

"He was getting weaker," Ruthie said. Her voice sounded wrong in the chamber, too loud and too flat, swallowed immediately by all that stone.

"Or colder," Tomas said from the passage mouth. He hadn't come fully inside. He stood with his arms folded, a big man making himself smaller than the room required, and his eyes moved along the walls without stopping anywhere. "Or both."

Felix said nothing at all. He had been quiet since the upper entrance, quieter even than usual, and he stood now near the collapsed timber at the chamber's far end with his lamp aimed down at the floor, at nothing, at whatever a man looks at when he is doing arithmetic of his own.

Elias was gone. Not gone-gone — he had drifted toward the side gallery twenty minutes ago, saying something about airflow, about draft patterns, and Ines had nodded absently, and now his lamp-glow was a faint gold smear around the bend of the passage, moving slowly. Ruthie noted it the way she noted everything now; eleven years of obituaries had taught her that people told you what they were by where they went when they thought no one was watching.

But right now there was only the wall, and the wall was talking.

She pulled off her pack and got out the notebook — an actual paper notebook, because batteries died and ink didn't — and began at the beginning of the tally, walking it foot by foot, counting the groups aloud under her breath. Ines joined her on the opposite wall, calling numbers. They met at the corner.

"One hundred and six," Ines said.

"One hundred and five," said Ruthie.

They looked at each other. Then they looked at the corner itself, at the shallow sideways scratches, and Ruthie crouched and put her bare fingers — she didn't remember deciding to take her glove off — against the stone.

There was one more mark there. Almost invisible. A single stroke, cut by someone whose hand must have been shaking so badly the tool had chattered across the rock, leaving a ragged groove more felt than seen. She found it by touch before she found it by sight.

"One hundred and twelve," she said, and heard her own voice crack on the second syllable, and hated herself for it, and let it happen anyway.

* * *

They worked the rest of the chamber by lamplight while Tomas kept watch at the passage and Felix drifted along the walls like a man reading scripture he already knew by heart.

The ledger pages — the photographs Ines had taken in the hangar, printed out and sealed in plastic — confirmed what the walls said. Payroll, winter 1911. Names in a clerk's careful copperplate: *A. Virtanen, drift runner. J. Okonkwo, timber crew. M. Halvorsen, powder man.* Wages paid weekly through the third week of November. And then nothing. No final disbursement. No severance. No entry at all until spring, when a single line in different ink recorded the tunnel as "closed — structural."

Closed. Structural. Two words doing the work of two hundred and twelve.

Because it wasn't just the tally wall. In the side alcoves, behind the fallen timber, they found the other things. A boot, leather gone stiff as bark, laced still. A tin cup fused to the floor by a century of mineral drip. And in the deepest alcove, half-buried in rubble, a slate — an actual schoolhouse slate, the kind a man might carry to write home to children who couldn't read yet — with letters scratched in chalk that time had eaten down to ghosts:

*TELL ANNA THE PAY IS GOOD*

Ruthie photographed it three times because her hands wouldn't stay steady enough for one.

"They didn't all die at once," Ines said quietly. She was kneeling by the tally wall, mapping the deterioration of the carving onto graph paper. "Look at the spacing. Groups of marks cluster, then gaps. They starved in waves. Or froze in waves. And the last one—" she touched the corner, the ragged final stroke, "—the last one lived longest. Long enough to count everyone. Long enough to know nobody was coming."

"And he counted anyway," Ruthie said.

"He counted anyway," Ines agreed.

"Why?" Tomas asked from the doorway, and it wasn't really a question. It was a demand aimed at the universe. "Why carve it? Who was he counting *for*?"

Ruthie had an answer. She had carried it up from the obituary desk, from eleven years of typing names into gray columns, and it came out of her before she could soften it.

"For us," she said. "He couldn't have known us. But he knew somebody would come. People always think somebody will come. That's the whole reason anyone leaves anything behind — a note, a body, a name scratched in a door. You do it because the alternative is disappearing completely, and something in us refuses. Even freezing. Even starving. Something refuses." She pressed her palm flat against the cold wall, over the clustered marks. "He did the only thing left. He made himself a witness."

The chamber held the words the way it held everything: completely, and without comment.

* * *

It was Felix who noticed the sound.

A scrape, faint and rhythmic, from the side gallery where Elias had gone. Metal on stone. Ruthie heard it too then — a soft regular whisper, like someone filing something, or like a camera shutter working in slow motion.

Tomas heard it last and reacted first. He was moving down the passage before Ruthie could say his name, and she followed, and the three of them rounded the bend into the side gallery together.

Elias — Ash, she reminded herself, *Ash Corrigan*, though the name fit him worse every day — stood with his back to them, angled toward a flat shelf of rock where documents lay spread out. Old documents, water-stained, brittle-looking: correspondence, it seemed, letters with letterheads, a bound sheaf of something. His phone was in his hand, camera active, and the soft scrape-scratch was the sound of him turning pages with a pocketknife blade, photographing each one.

He didn't startle. That was the thing Ruthie noticed most. He simply finished the page he was shooting, lowered the phone, and turned around with the composed, faintly apologetic expression of a man caught taking the last biscuit.

"Airflow samples," he said. "I was—"

"No," Ruthie said.

It came out harder than she intended, and she watched it land — watched something flicker behind his eyes, some calculation completing itself. He glanced at Tomas, filling the passage mouth like a door closing. At Felix, who had appeared silently at Ruthie's shoulder and was looking at Elias with an expression she couldn't read and didn't like.

"The documents," Ruthie said again, steadier now. She stepped forward and shone her lamp on the shelf. Letters. Company letterhead — *Kessler Mining & Timber Co.* — dated January 1912. She read upside down, fast, the way the obituary desk had trained her: phrases leaping out. *"...regrettable loss of life..." "...the matter is considered settled..." "...no widows have presented claims, the workforce having been drawn largely from transient labor..."*

Transient labor. Men with no local families to ask questions. Men whose absence would be noticed by no one with standing to notice.

"You're photographing your family's cover-up," she said. "In secret. On our expedition. With our trust."

"I'm documenting," Elias said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" Felix asked, and his voice was very quiet, which Ruthie had learned was how Felix sounded when he was closest to the edge of something.

Elias held their eyes. And here was the maddening thing, the thing Ruthie would turn over later that night in her sleeping bag unable to resolve it: he didn't look guilty. He looked *sad*. Genuinely, structurally sad, the way a man looks at a house he grew up in while it burns.

"My family did this," he said simply. "Someone in my family has always known, I think, the way you know a smell in a house you stop noticing. I wanted proof. I'm standing in the proof." He gestured at the walls beyond, at the tally neither of them could see from here but all of them could feel, the way you feel weather coming. "Two hundred and twelve men. My great-grandfather signed the order to close that tunnel — I've seen the signature my whole life on portraits in hallways, and I never once asked what the hands in the portrait had done."

"Then why sneak?" Tomas rumbled. "Why lie about your name? Why photograph in the dark like a thief?"

"Because of what happens next," Elias said. "If I walk into Kessler House and announce this, lawyers arrive within seventy-two hours. Land claims. Injunctions. The site gets sealed pending litigation, and litigation takes years, and years is enough time for a rockfall, a flood, a convenient fire. I've watched my family make problems disappear my entire life. I'm trying to get ahead of it." He paused. "That's the truth. You can decide whether it's the whole truth. I'd probably wonder too."

Ruthie looked at the shelf again. And saw it — the gap. A sheaf of papers with a clean-edged absence in the middle, one document missing, its outline pressed into the dust of a century. She looked up at Elias's coat, at the inner pocket that sat slightly heavier than its twin.

Their eyes met. He knew she'd seen. She knew he knew.

Neither of them said it. Not yet. But she filed it away with the precision of a woman who had spent a decade noticing what families chose not to print, and she understood suddenly that this expedition now contained two stories: the one about the dead, and the one about the living man standing among them with a stolen fragment of paper against his ribs.

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, the wind had begun to change. Tomas tilted his head, listening, and Ruthie saw the weather sense move across his face like a shadow.

"That's not glacier wind," he said. "That's a front. We should seal the entrance and get the stove going."

As they gathered to climb back toward the upper mouth, Ruthie took one last photograph of the tally wall — not for evidence, not for the story, though God knew there was a story, the story of her career sitting in this mountain waiting for her.

She took it because the last man who stood here had asked someone, anyone, to remember. And Ruthie Calloway, who had written two thousand obituaries for strangers and never once been asked, finally understood what the job was for.

Behind them, in the dark of the side gallery, the missing page lay in its dust outline like a negative space shaped exactly like a betrayal.
