## 11. Stormbound

The storm came in the way storms do on the Kessler coast — not as weather but as a decision the sky had already made without consulting anyone.

Ruthie Calloway felt it first in her teeth, an old barometric ache she'd carried since childhood on her grandmother's porch in Halloway, watching the barometer needle slide like a slow confession. By the time Tomas pointed at the western horizon and said only, "There it is," the cloud wall had already eaten the sun, a bruised and boiling gray with a greenish underside that meant business.

"We should have moved yesterday," Tomas said.

"We couldn't," Ines said. "We weren't finished."

"We are finished now," Tomas said, "whether we like it or not."

They broke camp in forty minutes, which Ruthie thought was a kind of miracle — five people, three weeks of expedition gear, and the unspoken weight of what they'd found below, all of it folded and cinched and hauled up the glacier's frozen margin while the wind arrived in advance of itself, gusting snow that wasn't snow yet, just the storm's scouts, testing their resolve. The cave's upper mouth — the rockfall-choked entrance they'd widened with two days of patient work — swallowed them one at a time, Tomas last, hauling the rope in behind him like a man pulling the door shut on the world.

Then the world did shut.

Ruthie had been a journalist for nineteen years. She had covered floods in the valley towns and a ferry disaster that killed eleven and a mine fire where she'd stood at the perimeter for three days watching families wait for names. She knew what it was to be near catastrophe, to be the witness standing just outside the event. But she had never heard a storm like this one. The wind came down the glacier like something herding it, and when it hit the rockfall at the cave mouth it screamed — there was no other word — a sustained, throttled scream that rose and fell and rose again, and beneath it came the deeper percussion of stone and ice being moved, rearranged, the mountain doing its housekeeping.

"Nobody goes outside," Tomas said. "Nobody. Not for anything."

"Even if—"

"Especially if," he said, and looked at each of them in turn, and Ruthie saw that under the glacier guide's flat competence there was a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "Two days, maybe three. We have the fuel, we have the stove, we have food for six days if we're careful. We stay alive, we wait, we walk out when the ice tells us we can."

They made camp in the first chamber, the one with the ceiling high enough to stand in and the floor swept clean of the old timber by some earlier collapse. Tomas rigged the stove with the methodical care of a man who had done it in worse places, and the blue flame hissed, and the small heat it made felt less like comfort than like a truce.

Ruthie sat with her back against the wall and opened her notebook, because that was what she did, that was the only prayer she knew — write it down, write it down, write it down — and outside the storm said its long scream, and inside the five of them arranged themselves into a circle of sleeping bags and silence.

* * *

The first night was fine, actually. Ruthie would remember that later, the strange sweetness of it. They ate rehydrated stew and Tomas told a story about a client in 2016 who had insisted on wearing dress shoes onto the ice, and Ines laughed until she had to put her cup down, and even Felix smiled — a real one, unguarded, the first Ruthie had seen from him since the ledger came out of the strongbox. Ash — Elias, though he didn't know they knew, or pretended not to know they knew, or perhaps had talked himself into believing his own alias — Ash told a story too, about a survey job in the north country, and it was a good story, well-shaped, and Ruthie found herself thinking, not for the first time: *he's had practice being charming in rooms where charm was currency.*

She wrote that down too.

The trouble came on the second day, when the storm, having established its authority, settled in for the duration, and there was nothing left to do but be five people in a hole in a mountain with a truth none of them had finished digesting.

It started, the way these things do, over something small. Ines had spread the ledger pages out again — she couldn't stop, Ruthie understood that, Ines had the scholar's compulsion, the pages were a text and texts demanded reading — and she was cross-referencing the wage entries against her notes from the town archive, murmuring names to herself. *Aldous Merrick. Peter Voss. The Ansel brothers, Henrik and Joon.* Names that had appeared in the 1911 payroll and then appeared nowhere, ever again, in any record.

"You should put those away," Felix said.

Ines looked up. "Why?"

"Because we've been down there for two days reading the names of dead men and we haven't eaten a meal without them sitting on the tarp between us. Because it's bad for us."

"Bad for us," Ines repeated, and there it was — the edge Ruthie had been waiting three days for someone to show. "Felix, there are two hundred and eleven marks carved into that wall down there. Two hundred and eleven men who went into a tunnel in the winter of 1911 and did not come out, and the company that sent them in wrote them off as a bad season and used their wages as a rounding error, and you're worried it's *bad for us* to look at the names over breakfast?"

"That's not what I said."

"It's what you meant."

Felix set his cup down with exaggerated care, the way men do when what they want to do is throw it. "What I meant," he said, "is that we are pinned in a hole in the ice by a storm we didn't predict, on a glacier that is going to start calving the moment this weather breaks, with a survey plane that may or may not be coming to look for us, and every hour we spend rehearsing our indignation is an hour we're not spending on how we get out of here alive. The names will keep. They've kept a hundred and fourteen years."

"They kept because nobody was looking," Ines said. "That's the whole point, Felix. They kept because the people who could have looked chose not to."

"And what happens when we look?" Felix said. "Have you actually thought that through? Not the museum version. The real version. Ruthie publishes, and then what? The town that lives off Kessler timber money and Kessler dock money and the Kessler foundation grant that keeps the clinic's lights on — what happens to them?"

"The truth doesn't owe the town its silence."

"No," Felix said. "But the truth doesn't pay for the clinic either. Somebody will. Or somebody won't. That's the part nobody wants to do the math on."

Ruthie wrote: *Joubert, F.: "the part nobody wants to do the math on."* She had learned long ago that the most honest sentences people said were the ones they said while looking at the middle distance, defending a position they hadn't fully chosen.

Ash had been quiet through all of it, sitting slightly apart, and now he spoke, and his voice had that measured quality Ruthie had clocked on the first day — the voice of a man who had learned to be heard in boardrooms, who calibrated before he spoke. "Felix has a point, though. About the math."

"Oh, wonderful," Ines said. "The surveyor weighs in."

"I've watched this happen," Ash said. "Communities, I mean. Where something like this comes out. The reckoning doesn't land on the people who did it — they're dead, they've been dead for a century. It lands on the living, and the living didn't do anything except be born in the wrong town. Restitution sounds clean until you ask who writes the check. It's always the same people. The ones who can least afford it."

"And the alternative?" Ruthie said.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't meant to enter the fight, but here she was, and she found she didn't mind. Nineteen years of obituaries — that was what she'd told Ines, that was why she'd come, because she was fifty-three years old and her byline had become a synonym for endings, and this was the first thing in a decade that felt like a beginning.

"The alternative," Ruthie said, "is what they did in 1911. Which is silence. Which is a ledger in a box in a cave, and two hundred and eleven families who got a wage stub and a closed door, and a town that built itself on top of the hole and called the hole a foundation. I've spent my whole career writing about what happens to the truth when nobody carries it out. It doesn't stay neutral, is what happens. It doesn't wait politely. It rots. It turns into something worse than itself. And the people who kept it — they tell themselves they were being kind, or careful, or protecting somebody, and maybe they even believe it, but the dead don't get a vote, do they? The dead just get deeper."

The stove hissed. The storm screamed its long note against the rockfall.

"I'm not saying bury it," Felix said finally. "I'm saying we get out first. Alive. All of us. Then we decide."

"We've already decided," Ines said. "That's the thing, Felix. The decision was made the moment we came up here instead of flying home with it. Everything since has just been us pretending we still have a choice."

Ruthie watched Felix take that. He was a hard man to read — a decade of leaving places will do that, she thought, a decade of not staying long enough to owe anyone anything — but she saw his jaw work, and she saw him look at the ledger pages on the tarp, at the column of wages, at the last entry with the name Kessler written in a hand that had been steady right up until it wasn't.

"Restitution, then," Ash said quietly. "If it comes out. What does restitution even look like, a hundred years on? A foundation? A plaque? An apology from a man who never met the men his grandfather buried?"

"Names," Ines said.

Everyone turned to her.

"Names," she said again. "You want to know what restitution looks like. It looks like the church at Kessler Point putting two hundred and eleven names on the wall where the founding families' portraits hang. It looks like the Ansel brothers having graves, or at least stones, or at least *words*. You can't pay back a century. But you can stop the lie. That's the restitution. The lie stops."

Ruthie wrote it down. *The lie stops.* She had been doing this job long enough to know when she was hearing the lede.

* * *

The argument burned down to embers as the light failed — not resolved, none of it resolved, just banked, the way family arguments bank, to be raked open again tomorrow. Tomas cooked. Felix checked the rope at the entrance twice, then a third time, and Ruthie watched him do it and understood that the rope was not really what he was checking. He was checking the world. Making sure it was still there on the other side of the rockfall.

Ash went into the side chamber after supper. Ruthie noticed it the way she noticed everything now — that was the other thing nineteen years gave you, a peripheral vision for behavior, for the small departures from pattern. Ash had gone into the side chamber every evening since they'd found the gallery. He always said he was checking the airflow, or the stability of the ceiling, or he said nothing at all, and he always stayed about twenty minutes, and he always came back with the particular settled calm of a man who has completed a task.

Tonight, Ruthie followed him.

Not far. Not obviously. She waited until he'd passed the bend in the passage, then moved to the edge of the main chamber where the lamplight gave out, and she stood in the dark with her notebook against her chest and watched the faint glow of his headlamp play on the far wall of the side chamber.

He was photographing documents.

She'd seen him do it before, or half-seen, or told herself she'd half-seen — a flash of white pages in the lamplight, the careful positioning, the long stillness. But tonight the angle was different, and the storm had driven everyone's nerves to the surface, and Ruthie Calloway had spent two decades noticing what people did when they thought no one was watching, and she watched.

He was working through a stack of papers she didn't recognize. Not the ledger — she knew the ledger, had photographed every page of it herself on Ines's instruction, insurance against exactly this kind of thing. These were loose documents, older, brittle-looking, and he handled them with a gentleness that didn't fit with anything else about him, turning each one, photographing it, setting it aside.

And then he did the thing.

He took one of the documents — a fragment, a torn corner of something larger, she couldn't see what — and he looked at it for a long moment under his lamp, and then he folded it, small, precise, and put it inside his jacket. Not in a pocket of his pack, where gear went. Inside his jacket, against his chest, where a man keeps the things he cannot afford to lose or cannot afford to show.

Ruthie stood in the dark and felt the expedition shift under her, the way the ice shifts, silently, structurally, before anything visible happens at all.

She thought about what she knew. She thought about the jawline she'd clocked on the first morning, the family jaw she'd have sworn on a stack of Bibles belonged to the young Kessler in the portrait that hung in the Kessler Point church hall — the third one, Elias, the heir, photographed at charity regattas, written up in the society pages Ruthie used to edit before she'd fled to obituaries. She thought about the payphone, if there'd been a payphone — she didn't know about that, that was Felix's and Tomas's knowledge, or would be — but she thought about the alias, the money, the oddly specific questions, the survey experience that was a little too polished, the charm that was a little too practiced.

She thought about the fragment against his chest.

And here was the thing, the thing she turned over as she backed away down the passage, quiet as she'd come: she didn't know what it meant. That was the honest truth of it. A Kessler photographing his own family's crimes could be a man building a case against them. Could be a man destroying evidence one page at a time. Could be a man keeping a souvenir, or building a defense, or hedging, the way powerful families hedge, holding both the truth and its denial in two hands and waiting to see which one paid better. She had watched enough powerful people operate to know that they rarely did anything for a single reason, and that the most dangerous ones were the ones who had talked themselves fully into their own story.

*Ash* — she wrote it that night in the notebook, by lamplight, while the others slept and the storm kept its vigil outside. *Gone to the side chamber. Photographing. Took a fragment — folded it into his jacket, left side, against the ribs. Watched him do it. Don't know what it means yet. Find out what's missing.*

Because that was the other thing nineteen years had taught her, the thing she'd have put on her headstone if headstones allowed: when a man takes something from a room, the room is the witness. Count what's gone.

In the morning, when the storm still held them and there was nothing to do but be five people with one truth, Ruthie made a show of organizing the document folders — Ines's system, meticulous, every page photographed and logged — and she counted, casually, the way you'd count anything, and she found the gap. One fragment, torn from the lower corner of what looked like a correspondence page, 1911, unsigned.

She looked up across the chamber. Ash was watching her over the top of his cup, his face pleasant and open and completely unreadable, and outside the storm screamed on, and somewhere beneath them, down in the sealed gallery where the timber had come down a century ago, two hundred and eleven names waited in the dark for someone to decide what they were worth.

Ruthie closed the folder and smiled at him, and began, very carefully, to plan.
