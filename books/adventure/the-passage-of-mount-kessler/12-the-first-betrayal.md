## 12. The First Betrayal

Tomas Okafor had spent eleven years learning to sleep on ice, and the first thing it taught him was that a glacier never truly goes quiet. It breathed. It groaned in its sleep like an old man turning over. It ticked and settled and sometimes, deep in the night, it sang — a high, thin note that came up through the ground and into the bones of anyone lying against it, a sound the old guides called *the mountain counting*. His grandfather had said you could hear your own name in it if you listened long enough, but only if the mountain wanted you to know something.

So when Tomas woke at 2:40 in the morning with his name still ringing faintly in his molars, he did not question it. He lay in his bag at the cave mouth's antechamber, listening past the wind, past Ines's even breathing three meters to his left, past Ruthie's occasional sleep-murmur, past Felix's silence — Felix slept like a man keeping watch even unconscious, curled toward the exit — and he counted.

Four breathers. He counted again.

Five.

The fifth set of lungs was moving away from camp, up-slope, careful, each footfall placed with the deliberate softness of someone who had thought about how to walk quietly and practiced it. Not an animal. Animals didn't hesitate between steps.

Tomas opened his eyes to darkness and let them adjust. The storm had broken that afternoon into a hard, rinsing cold, and now the sky above the cave mouth was stitched with stars, sharp as needles. Moonlight fell down the shaft behind them in a pale column, catching the frost flowers that had bloomed overnight across the inner walls — whole gardens of white ferns growing out of bare stone, delicate as lace, doomed by any warmth. Elias had called them beautiful yesterday. Ruthie had said they were the mountain exhaling.

Tomas slid out of his bag without sound. Eleven years of this. He dressed in layers by feel, boots unlaced and relaced loose so the leather wouldn't crack loud, and he followed the breathing up-slope.

* * *

The upper route climbed through a chimney of rock and packed snow that Tomas himself had rigged with two fixed lines on their way in. Whoever was ahead of him knew the lines were there — used them, he could tell by the rhythm, hand over hand, pausing at the anchor points where a stranger would have flailed — but moved slowly enough to suggest they hadn't scouted anything beyond what the group had already shown them.

That narrowed things. There were only five of them, and one of them was asleep in his own bag back at the wall.

The climber crested the shoulder below the upper entrance and stopped. Tomas flattened himself against a boulder the size of a truck cab, letting the cold stone drink the heat from his chest, and watched.

In the moonlight, Ash Corrigan crouched at the base of a granite fin some forty meters from the entrance — a spot Tomas recognized immediately, because it was the one place on the entire approach where a person standing at the cave mouth would be visible against the sky. A landmark. A reference point. Ash worked quickly, gloved hands moving with purpose, and when he stepped back Tomas saw what he'd done: a small cairn, three stones stacked, topped with a fourth painted — no, not painted. Marked with a strip of reflective tape, the kind surveyors used, the kind that lit up like a struck match under a headlamp or a helicopter searchlight.

A marker. On Kessler land, if the lawyers had their way. A flag planted.

Tomas felt something move through him that wasn't quite anger yet — anger needed confirmation, and he was a man who did not spend fury cheaply. What moved through him first was colder and older: the particular disappointment of a guide watching a client do the one thing that got people killed. Because that was how he framed it, in those first seconds. Not treachery. Not yet. Just a client breaking protocol, wandering off rope, and every guide alive knew that the ones who smiled the widest were the ones who wandered.

He waited until Ash turned back toward camp. Then he stood up out of the boulder's shadow and said, quietly, into the enormous silence:

"Leave the stones."

* * *

Ash froze mid-step. For a long moment neither of them moved, and the glacier sang its thin note beneath them, and somewhere far below, sea ice shifted and boomed like a door closing in an empty house.

"Tomas." Ash's voice was steady. That was the first thing Tomas noticed, and it made everything worse. A guilty man caught at 3 a.m. should have startled, stammered, reached for excuses. Ash just turned around slowly, moonlight silvering half his face, and looked at Tomas the way a man looks at weather he has already checked the forecast for. "You're quiet on your feet."

"Eleven years of clients trying to sneak extra whiskey past me." Tomas came down the slope at an angle, never closing the distance fully, keeping a boulder between them out of habit he didn't examine too closely. "Take the tape off the stone."

"No."

The word landed flat and clean, and it did something strange to the air between them — it ended the pretense all at once, the way snapping a rope ends a conversation about whether the anchor will hold. Ash straightened. And then, with a small, terrible calm, he reached inside his parka and drew out something black and rectangular, holding it up so the moon caught its edge.

"I think we're past the point where I can be useful by pretending," he said. "And I'm tired, honestly. I've been tired since the hangar."

Tomas stared at the object. He knew what it was before his mind finished naming it — he'd seen one once, years ago, in the kit of a mining company scout who'd gotten lost on the Vantage Plateau and walked into a rescue station asking for a charge. A satellite phone. The kind that didn't care about storms or distance or radio dead zones. The kind that cost more per minute than most people earned per hour.

"You've been calling out," Tomas said. "From the payphone. Before we left. And since."

"Yes."

"How many times?"

Ash — Elias, Tomas corrected himself, because Felix had told him on the third day, quietly, without drama, *that jawline is on the Kessler Point banknotes, Tomás, I'd bet the plane on it* — Elias turned the phone over in his gloved hands like a man deciding whether to show a wound.

"Twice," he said. "Once before we departed. Once after the storm pinned us. And once tonight, from the chamber with the timber collapse, while you were all sleeping." He paused. "There won't need to be a fourth."

"There won't," Tomas agreed, and held out his hand. "Give it here."

"No." Elias said it without heat, and that was the frightening part — there was no bluster in him at all, no cornered-animal energy. He sounded like a man reading terms aloud. "You should understand what you'd be taking. That phone is the only line to a crew of four who are currently staged at Kessler Point with a chartered Twin Otter, a land surveyor, and a legal team. They arrive within seventy-two hours of my signal. If I don't signal, they come anyway — my father doesn't wait well, and he's had my itinerary since the payphone call. Either way, they're coming, Tomás. The only variable is whether they come to a marked site or have to find it themselves."

The words went into Tomas like cold water into a boot seam — not painful at first, just present, seeping, and then suddenly everywhere. He made himself stand still. Made himself breathe the way he taught clients to breathe when the ground started moving: slow, low, counting.

"The site is on public land," he said. "The tideline is public. The cave—"

"Is under a claim my family filed nine days ago," Elias said. "Contested mineral rights, historical easement, private access covenant. My father's lawyers are very good at making public things private. They've been doing it for a hundred and fourteen years." He looked down at the cairn, at the little strip of tape winking faintly in the dark. "I placed that marker because if they're going to come regardless, I wanted them coming to a point I chose — fifty meters off the true entrance, on a slope that avalanches in spring thaw. It buys time. Maybe weeks. Maybe nothing."

Tomas heard himself laugh — a short, ugly sound he didn't recognize as his own. "You're telling me the betrayal is a favor."

"I'm telling you what happened." Elias's voice finally cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and Tomas hated that he heard it, hated that some part of him catalogued it as *genuine*, because genuine was exactly the weapon Felix had warned them this man carried. "I arranged the follow-up crew six weeks ago. Before I ever met any of you. Before I knew Ines existed, before I knew what she'd found. I hired myself onto this expedition to control it. That was the plan. Control the site, control the narrative, keep my family's name off the front page of whatever Ruthie Calloway writes." He pulled his glove off with his teeth, and in the moonlight Tomas saw his bare hand trembling — not much, just the fingers, the fine tremor of a man running on adrenaline and self-disgust. "And then I stood in that gallery and read the tally. Two hundred and eleven marks, Tomás. I put my palm flat against the wall and I could feel the chisel strokes. Someone cut each one by hand, in the dark, counting their friends."

The wind moved through the pass above them, carrying a sift of spindrift that hissed across the stone like sand through an hourglass.

"So change sides," Tomas said. "For real. Give me the phone. We wake the others, we decide together—"

"And they fly out the original ledger tomorrow and my father's lawyers meet it at the airstrip with an injunction and a claim of stolen property from a private site," Elias said. "You don't win that. You don't even survive it. Your sister loses her research permits. Felix loses his charter license inside a year — my family has buried smaller pilots than him. Ruthie gets sued into oblivion. And the two hundred and eleven stay names on a wall nobody's allowed to visit." He shook his head slowly. "I've watched my father do this my entire life. I know exactly how it looks from the other side of the table. That's why I'm standing here at three in the morning instead of sleeping."

"Then why the marker?" Tomas's voice came out low and rough. "Why the calls? If you've changed sides, why are you still doing his work?"

Elias was quiet for a moment. When he answered, he didn't dress it up.

"Because I don't trust myself to have changed," he said. "Because six weeks ago I promised four professionals a payday and a clean extraction, and every plan I make turns into my father's plan eventually — it's genetic, maybe. So I built the betrayal anyway. I built it so that if I failed, if I broke, if I turned out to be exactly what I was raised to be — the machinery would still run, and at least it would run badly." He held the satellite phone out then, arm extended, offering it into the space between them. "But I'm asking you not to take it. Because right now it's also the only thing keeping a survey crew off this mountain for another day, and every day is meltwater on the ice road."

Tomas did not take the phone.

He stood in the moonlight with his heart slamming and made himself do the arithmetic, because arithmetic was what he was for. Ines would want to believe him. Ruthie would want to string him up. Felix — Felix would go very quiet, the way he did, and look at Elias the way a man looks at a bridge he has to cross anyway. And underneath all of it, ticking like the glacier itself: calving season, the ice road, the window measured now in days.

"You woke us into this," Tomas said finally. "You lied your way onto a rope team. Every knot we tied, you tied with us, knowing this." He stepped closer, close enough to see the frost on Elias's eyelashes, and let eleven years of carrying other people's lives sit in his voice. "So here's what happens now. You walk back down with me. You wake up with the rest of us, and you say all of that again — the phone, the crew, the claim, the marker, all of it — in front of my sister, in front of Ruthie, in front of Felix. You don't get to ration the truth anymore. You spend it all at once or you spend the rest of this expedition on a short rope at the back of the line."

"And if they vote to tie me to a rock and take the phone?" Elias asked. There was no sarcasm in it. He genuinely wanted to know.

"Then that's the vote," Tomas said. "That's what a crew is. That's the thing your family never learned." He turned downslope. "Come on. Dawn's in three hours, and the ice won't wait for our feelings."

Behind him, after a moment, boots crunched into the snow, following.

And far below them both, down at the coast where the tide cave breathed its slow salt air, the glacier gave a long, settling groan — the sound it made, Tomas's grandfather had always said, when it was shifting its weight. Getting ready to let something go.
