## 13. Whose Truth Is It

The storm had spent itself against the cliff in the night, and what remained was a silence so complete that Ines could hear the glacier breathing — a slow, tectonic sigh from the valley above, the sound of a mountain shifting its weight. She lay in her bag inside the cave mouth and listened to it the way she had once listened to whale song through a hydrophone: something enormous, trying to say something, in a language she almost understood.

The others were still sleeping. Ruthie had curled toward the fire's ashes with her notebook open on her chest, pages fluttering in the faint draft that moved through the cave — the same draft that had led them here from the tide line, the mountain's slow exhalation, the reason Ines had known the upper entrance existed before they ever found it. Air had to go somewhere. Truth, too, she was beginning to think. It moved through whatever channel it could find, and if you sealed every one, it found another.

She crawled out into the morning.

The world had been scoured. Snow lay in wind-carved ridges down the whole length of the glacier's margin, blue-shadowed and glittering, and the peaks above the icefall wore fresh white like men dressed for a funeral. Far below, the sea had gone the flat grey-green of bottle glass, and the tide was out so far she could see the dark seam of the lower cave's mouth, a comma of shadow in the rock, holding its breath.

Two hundred and eleven marks on a wall. She had counted them twice herself, and a third time with Ruthie, their fingers hovering an inch from the stone, not touching. One mark per man. Cut with something sharp and patient by someone who knew that no one was coming to name them, that no ledger would be allowed to remember, that the company's winter of 1911 would close over them like water.

Ines crouched at the melt pool they'd hacked in the stream ice and drank. The water tasted of stone and centuries. Above her, the sky was doing something she didn't like — a high, hard clarity, the kind that came before wind, before warmth, before the mountain decided it was done holding still.

Tomas came out of the tent rubbing his eyes, and she watched him do the same thing she had done: look up, read the sky, and frown.

"Calving's early," he said.

"I know."

"The radio said four days. That was yesterday's sky." He crouched beside her, took the cup she handed him. "Today's sky says less."

They sat together the way they had sat together their whole lives, saying nothing, which between them had always been a complete sentence. Then the tent flap moved and Elias came out — Ash, she still had to remind herself, though the name had worn through days ago like cheap plating over brass — and the fragile quiet of the morning changed texture. Tomas's shoulders set. Elias looked at the sky too, and Ines watched his face do the arithmetic hers had done, and something in his expression told her he had already made his phone call, already set something in motion that none of them could unring.

* * *

They gathered at midday, because Ruthie called them to it, spreading her notebook on the flat rock by the fire like a dealer laying out cards.

"We vote," she said. "Or we argue until we vote. Either way, we decide before we can walk out, because once we're moving we'll be too scared to think."

The evidence lay between them: the ledger itself, its boards swollen with a century of damp, wrapped now in Ines's spare shirt; the photographs Ruthie had taken of every page; the charcoal rubbings of the tally wall, rolled like maps of a country that existed only in grief. Two hundred and eleven men. One winter. A tunnel collapsed on purpose, by order, and the order signed by a man whose great-grandson was sitting at this fire wearing a false name and an expression like a man carrying something breakable.

"Destroy it," Tomas said. He said it flat, the way he said everything, but Ines heard the anger underneath, banked and hot as coals. "The originals. All of it. We put the cave back the way we found it and we say nothing. His family's lawyers are already circling — Elias told us that much. The moment that ledger exists in public, it becomes a thing they can fight. They'll own it in court the way they own everything else. They'll own the *story*."

"They can't own the story if there's no evidence," Ruthie said. "That's exactly backwards, Tom. Silence is what they've had for a hundred and twelve years. Silence is their product."

"Silence is what keeps us alive." He turned his cup in his hands. "You weren't on the radio last night. I was. I listened to that survey plane's chatter for an hour. They've got a crew coming in from the coast, and they've filed a land claim, and the claim is *real*, Ruthie. Paper beats memory. It always has. We walk out of here with nothing and we live. We walk out with that book and we're witnesses, and witnesses have accidents on glaciers."

Ines looked at the ledger. The leather had gone the color of old kelp. She thought about the hands that had held it — a paymaster's hands, probably, ink-stained, counting out wages to men who would die before the wages were spent. She thought about the last entry. *E. Kessler, approved.* A signature like a door closing.

"No," she said.

They all looked at her. She hadn't meant to say it aloud yet; she had meant to build the argument in her head first, the way she built dive plans, methodically, checking her margins. But it was out now, and the fire crackled, and the glacier sighed above them, and she found she didn't want to take it back.

"No," she said again. "We don't destroy it. We don't bury them twice."

"Ines." Tomas's voice gentled, which was worse. "I know what you're — "

"Do you?" She stood, because sitting felt like agreeing. "Two hundred and eleven men went into that tunnel and the company pulled the rock down on top of them and then paid their families off or didn't pay them at all, and then everyone agreed, all the way up to a Kessler, that it hadn't happened. That's not just a crime, Tom. That's a *second* crime, committed every year since, every time someone in Kessler Point says the founding family built this coast with hard work and good luck. Every school kid who learns the name and not the winter. Every plaque. Every — " she gestured at the ledger, her hand not quite steady — "every year those men stay nameless, the second crime happens again. And you want to commit it once more, ourselves, with our own hands, because we're scared."

"Being scared is how we stay — "

"Being scared is *survival math*," she said, "and I'm good at math. I do it for a living. You calculate your margins, you calculate your risk, and you don't dive past what your air allows. I understand. But some things aren't a question of margins." She crouched and touched the wrapped ledger, just her fingertips, the way she'd touched the tally wall. "These men were leverage the day they died. Their deaths were a business decision. If we carry this out and use it — against the family, for money, for safety, for anything — we're doing the same thing the company did. Turning the dead into an asset. The only thing they have left, the only thing anyone can still give them, is their *names*. Names aren't leverage. You can't trade a name. You can only say it."

The wind came up the glacier then, a long cold breath off the icefall, and the fire leaned and recovered.

Ruthie was watching her with the look she got when a source said something true — hungry and sad at once. "Say it," Ruthie repeated softly. "That's the whole case, isn't it. That's the story. Not the family. Not the fortune. The names."

"Two hundred and eleven names," Ines said. "In the church at Kessler Point. On a wall somewhere. Read out loud, once, all of them, by someone who didn't know them. That's all. That's the whole restitution I'm asking for. The lawsuits can go to hell. The money can go wherever money goes. But the names come out."

"And the cost?" Tomas asked. He wasn't fighting anymore; he was asking, which from Tomas was a surrender of a kind. "You've met the cost, right? Because I've been doing that math too, and it's ugly. The survey plane. The lawyers. A family that's owned this coast for four generations and has never once lost an argument it decided to have. They'll come after the book, and then they'll come after us. Felix flies out of a strip they could buy with pocket change. Ruthie writes for a paper that runs their ads. I guide clients on a glacier their company holds the permit for." He shook his head slowly. "There's no version of this where we all walk away clean. Someone pays. Maybe all of us."

Ines had known this. She had known it since the strongbox came open in Felix's hangar and the dread arrived riding in on the thrill's shoulders. But hearing Tomas say it out loud, plainly, without anger — hearing it as arithmetic rather than fear — made it real in a new way. She looked around the circle. Felix, who hadn't spoken yet, sitting slightly apart with his forearms on his knees, staring into the fire with the expression of a man reading a fuel gauge he didn't like. Ruthie, jaw set, pen already moving. Elias — Elias, who had not defended his family and had not condemned them either, who had sat through all of it with his hands wrapped around a tin cup like a man holding onto a rail in heavy weather.

"Someone pays," Ines said quietly. "Yes. I know. I've known since the hangar. But here's what I've decided about that." She made herself look at each of them. "If we bury it, we pay too. It's just a different currency. We pay in the only coin those men never got — we pay in silence, forever, and we carry it the way the paymaster carried that book out of the mountain, knowing what was in it, saying nothing. I've watched what that does to people. My father carried a thing like that for forty years. It didn't keep him safe. It just kept him *quiet*, and there's a difference, and the difference eats you."

Felix looked up at that. Just for a second. Something moved behind his eyes — she thought of what little he'd ever said about his past, a decade of one-way tickets, a man who had made not-staying into a discipline — and then he looked back at the fire.

"Say your piece, Felix," Ruthie said. "You've been quiet all day."

Felix was a long time answering. When he did, his voice was the flat, unhurried voice he used on the radio, the one that carried no weather.

"I've been thinking about what I'd be flying out," he said. "Not the book. The weight. Because everything I've ever carried out of a bad place, I carried for money, and money's a clean reason — you land, you're paid, you leave, nobody owes anybody a soul. This is different. This is the first thing I'd ever fly that someone might shoot at." He turned the cup in his hands. "Tom's right about the math. There's no clean exit. If we fly it out, the Keslers come after us, and they'll start with the easiest targets, and the easiest target is a bush pilot with a ten-year-old airplane and no fixed address. If we bury it, we're safe, and two hundred and eleven men stay a rounding error in somebody's family history." He looked up, and his eyes found Elias first, then Ines. "So I'll tell you what my vote's worth, and it isn't much. I've spent ten years making sure nothing I did mattered enough for anyone to come looking. I'm tired of it. If someone's going to pay for this, I'd rather it cost something I chose than something I avoided." He shrugged, a small, tired motion. "Fly it out. Say the names. Let them come."

The silence after that was the kind Ines had only heard underwater — total, pressurized, full of things moving.

Elias set his cup down. "I'll take the cost," he said. "Whatever it is. It's mine. It's been mine since before any of you were born; you're just the first people to hand it to me where I could actually hold it." His voice caught and steadied. "Ines is right. My family turned those men into a foundation. The only thing I can do that they never did is give them back."

"You could start," Ruthie said, "by telling us who you called from the payphone in Kessler Point."

Elias went very still.

"I'm not accusing," Ruthie said, and her voice was gentle and her eyes were not. "I'm asking. Because whatever's coming for us is coming because of that call, and we vote better when we know what we're voting against."

The wind gusted. Somewhere up-valley, deep in the ice, came a sound like a giant door being drawn shut — the glacier settling, or preparing.

Elias looked at the fire for a long moment. "My sister," he said finally. "I called my sister. And she told our father." He lifted his head, and Ines saw that whatever he was about to say had been sitting in him for days, souring. "And my father didn't send lawyers. Lawyers are what he sends when he wants something *argued*. The survey plane isn't a survey plane. It's an assessment crew. He doesn't intend to claim the cave, Ines. He intends to close it. Permanently. The way it was closed the first time."

The fire cracked. Above them the sky had gone that hard, high, windless clarity, and far up the valley, with a sound like a rifle shot, the first serac of the season let go.

Tomas was on his feet before the echo faded, staring up the ice road they would need in — he checked his watch, and Ines watched him do the math she was doing — perhaps three days now, maybe less.

"Then we decide tonight," Tomas said. "Because the mountain just voted too."
