## 14. Calving Season Opens

The storm broke the way a fever breaks — all at once, and leaving everything changed.

Felix woke to silence so complete it felt like a sound in itself. For two days the wind had screamed through the cave mouth, a white roar that made speech useless and thought difficult, and now it was gone, and in its absence he could hear the glacier.

That was the wrong thing to be hearing.

He'd spent ten years in the north, long enough to know that a glacier at rest made no sound at all, or nearly none — the odd settling groan, the tick of ice contracting in the cold. What he heard now, lying in his bag with his eyes still shut, was a slow percussion. A crack, a rumble, a long descending hiss like a kettle taken off heat. Then quiet. Then again, from a different quarter. Crack. Rumble. Hiss.

The mountain was shedding its skin.

He was out of his bag and at the cave mouth before his boots were fully laced, and the sight that met him stopped his breath the way cold water does. The storm had passed east in the night, dragging the cloud deck with it, and the morning was savage and clear — a hard blue sky, a sun already burning white off the ice, and the whole Kessler icefield laid bare from the cave's high vantage like a map of a country that was being erased while you watched. The glacier's surface, which had been a smooth white plain when they'd come up it nine days ago, was now a chaos of lean-to ridges and open fissures, and along its seaward face, three miles off and impossibly loud, the calving front was letting go.

He watched a serac the size of a church shear off the face and fall. It didn't so much crash as detonate — a flat white concussion, a column of spray, and then the sound arriving across the ice a full ten seconds later, rolling through the rock under his feet like a train passing through the mountain's bones.

"Felix." Ines, behind him, hair still sleep-flattened, binoculars already up. She didn't say anything else for a long moment. She didn't have to.

"Four days," Felix said. "Maybe five if we're lucky. The margin route along the east flank will hold that long. After that the meltwater opens the crevasses and the whole road goes soft."

"Four days." She lowered the binoculars. In the clear light her face looked older than it had nine days ago, or maybe just more honest. "And we're what — eleven miles from the coast strip?"

"Twelve, the way Tomas will want to route it. Around the icefall rather than through it."

"Twelve miles in four days with everything we're carrying." She said it flatly, doing the arithmetic, and he watched her arrive at the same place he had: it was enough, but only just, and only if nothing else went wrong.

Behind them, in the cave's first chamber, the camp was already stirring. He heard Tomas's voice, low and even, giving the morning its shape — *melt snow, pack the ropes, check the sled runners* — and Ruthie answering, and then a silence where Ash's voice should have been, and Felix felt the familiar knot tighten in his chest. Nine days of that silence now, or something like it. Nine days since the confrontation in the side chamber, since Elias had sat on a supply crate with his hands hanging between his knees and told Felix who he was, and Felix had carried the knowledge like a stone in his boot ever since — not sharp enough to stop him walking, but always there, shifting with every step.

They'd made their decision two nights ago, in the storm, with the wind trying to take the roof off the world: the evidence went out. The dead got their names. And now the mountain, with a timing that felt less like coincidence than like testimony, had opened the door and started counting down.

* * *

By nine they had the camp struck and the loads distributed, and Felix stood at the cave mouth with the radio — the old VHF set they'd hauled up the ice road in a padded case, because Ines had insisted that a party of five going inland in calving season would be idiots without one, and for once in his life Felix had agreed with someone about safety without arguing.

He ran the morning check on the marine band, mostly out of habit. Static, the long empty hiss of the off-season coast. Then, threading through it, the Kessler Point harbor frequency, and a voice he knew.

*"—repeat, all vessels, survey vessel *Meridian* inbound to Kessler Point, ETA tomorrow oh-eight-hundred, charter party requesting pilotage and shore access at the point. Be advised, charter party has priority berthing—"*

Felix stood very still.

The voice was Denny Marsh, the harbormaster, a man who had never once in his life used the phrase *charter party has priority berthing*. That was lawyer language. That was language read off a card.

"Felix." Ines had come up beside him without him hearing, which frightened him a little; the ice was so loud now that a person could walk right up on you. She'd heard it too. He could see it in her face, the way the color had gone out of her cheeks. "Survey vessel."

"Chartered," Felix said. He turned the set's volume down as though quieter might make it untrue. "That's a Kessler word. Nobody else on this coast charters a survey vessel with priority berthing."

"Kessler-chartered," Ruthie said from behind them, and Felix turned to find the whole camp had drifted to the cave mouth, drawn by the radio the way animals drift toward water. Ruthie had her notebook out. She always had her notebook out now; the storm days had turned her from a journalist on holiday into a journalist on fire. "Meaning what, exactly? What does a survey vessel *do*?"

"Maps," Felix said. "Surveys. Stakes claims. If a Kessler lawyer wants to establish that a cave is on private land, a survey vessel is how you start. You put a crew ashore, you put instruments on the rock, and you generate a hundred pages of paper that say the site was documented, controlled, and secured before anyone else could get there." He heard his own voice go flat and hard, the voice he used for weather briefings. "They're not coming for the truth. They're coming for the paperwork."

Tomas spat, a habit Felix usually found distasteful and just now found eloquent. "Then we're racing them."

"We're racing them and we don't know the finish line." Felix looked back out at the glacier. Another serac went, off to the north, the concussion rolling through them. "Four days to get down the ice road. The *Meridian* makes port tomorrow morning, and a shore crew could be at the tide cave by tomorrow afternoon — the coast route's fast, no glacier in the way. Which means our real window isn't four days." He did the arithmetic out loud because he wanted them all to feel it land. "It's tomorrow. Maybe the day after, if the weather down-coast slows them. We need to be off this ice and at that strip before their plane or their boat gets eyes on us."

"Their plane," Ash said.

Everyone looked at him. Elias — Ash, Felix corrected himself, *Ash* until the moment it stopped being a lie worth maintaining — stood a little apart from the group, as he had for two days, a man at the edge of his own campfire. He looked, Felix thought, like someone watching a fire he'd set as a boy finally reach the house.

"The family keeps a survey aircraft," Elias said quietly. "A Twin Otter, based out of the city. If the *Meridian* is coming, the plane comes first. It always does — my father likes his eyes in the sky before his boots on the ground." He paused, and Felix watched him choose to say the next part. "And the plane will fly the coast route. It will pass directly over the strip where your aircraft is parked."

*Your aircraft.* Felix felt the words go in like a splinter. His de Havilland, his battered, beloved, ten-year companion, sitting alone on the coast strip at Kessler Point, visible from a thousand feet, identifiable by anyone who'd ever filed a flight plan on this coast — which was to say, by anyone the Keslers cared to ask.

"They'll know whose it is," Ines said. It wasn't a question.

"They'll know whose it is," Felix said. "And then they'll know who's up here. And then—" He stopped. He'd spent a decade making sure no one knew enough about him to finish that sentence, and now the sentence was finishing itself.

Ruthie was writing. He could hear the scratch of her pencil over the ice's rumble. "So we have a window measured in hours," she said, "held open by a glacier that's actively trying to close it, and a family with a plane and a boat and a hundred years of practice at making things disappear." She looked up. "I want to be very clear that I'm saying this out loud so it's on the record: this is the part where a reasonable person quits."

Nobody quit. Felix watched them not quit — Tomas checking the sled runners with the deliberate calm of a man converting anger into torque; Ines wrapping the ledger's dry bag in a second layer of fleece as though it were a patient; Ash standing at the edge with his jaw set in that family line Felix had recognized the first day and hated himself for not naming. Even now, knowing what he knew, Felix couldn't make himself hate the man. That was the worst part. Elias had lied to them for nine days and betrayed them two nights ago, and he was still the person Felix least wanted to see destroyed by all this, and Felix suspected that was not an accident. That was how the Keslers worked. That was how they'd worked for a hundred years.

* * *

They were on the ice by ten, roped in pairs, Tomas on point reading the glacier like a man reading a sentence that kept revising itself. The storm had rearranged everything. Snow bridges that had held on the way up had sagged or gone; new crevasses had opened along the east flank, blue-lipped and hungry, and twice in the first hour Tomas called a halt and probed a route through with the patience of a surgeon and the vocabulary of a longshoreman.

Felix carried the radio. It rode against his spine in its padded case, and every hour, on the hour, he called the coast.

The coast answered with weather, mostly. Wind at the point, backing northwest. Seas moderate. And then, a little after noon, with the sun high and the calving front booming behind them like distant artillery, Denny Marsh's voice again, and this time Felix had the set up to his ear before the man finished his first sentence.

*"—all stations, all stations, aircraft advisory. Twin Otter, white over blue, no visible registration, departed the city this morning, last reported heading north along the coast at low altitude. Charter flight, filed private. Anyone with visual is asked to report to Kessler Point harbor on channel one-six. Repeat—"*

Felix lowered the set. The others had stopped when he had; they'd learned, in nine days, to read his body the way sailors read sky.

"The plane's already up," he said.

"How far?" Ines asked.

"Departed the city this morning. The city's two hundred and eighty miles south of the point." He looked at the sky, at the hard clean blue of it, at the sun that was eating the ice road with every hour it shone. "In this weather, with that load, call it two and a half hours' flying, maybe three with a fuel stop. It's past noon now." He did the arithmetic and hated every number. "It could be over the point by mid-afternoon. It could be looking at our airplane while we're still six miles from the strip, out here where a radio call is the only thing we can throw at it."

"Can it see us?" Ruthie asked. "Out here? Five dots on the ice?"

"Five dots on white ice, at low altitude, in clear air?" Felix shook his head. "It can see a cigarette butt on a runway. But we're small and we're moving and it'll be looking at the coast first — at the cave, at the strip, at the plane. That buys us time. Not much."

Tomas had turned to face back down the glacier, shading his eyes, reading the route ahead. When he spoke, his voice had the flatness Felix had learned to trust more than any amount of optimism. "The margin route holds for four days in normal melt. This isn't normal melt — three days of storm snow on top of spring ice, and now full sun. By tomorrow afternoon the bridges start failing. We need to be across the icefall by tomorrow morning, on the coast by tomorrow night."

"Which puts us at the strip," Ines said slowly, "roughly when their shore crew does."

"Or their plane," Felix said. "Or both."

They stood there a moment, five people roped together on a river of ice that was audibly, continuously, letting itself go, and Felix thought: *this is what a clock sounds like.* Not ticking. Cracking. The mountain itself counting down, each serac a second falling off the face of the world, and somewhere to the south a white-and-blue Twin Otter eating the miles with its family's money in its tanks.

"Then we don't walk," Elias said.

They all turned. He was looking down the glacier with an expression Felix hadn't seen on him before — not the pleasant competence of the first days, not the cornered guilt of the storm, but something harder and more awake. The jawline was the same. The man behind it had changed.

"The ice road takes a day and a half and we don't have a day and a half," Elias said. "But the plane's at the strip, and the strip is twelve miles from here, and I know — I *know* — how my father's people work. They'll overfly the strip first, confirm the target, and then they'll land a crew to secure it. That's a sequence. It takes hours. And a Twin Otter on floats or wheels can't loiter over the coast all night — they'll stage out of the point, come back at first light." He was talking faster now, the words coming up from somewhere below thought. "Which means tonight is the hole in their net. Tonight, the strip is empty, the plane is alone, and if Felix can get that engine running, we can be off this coast before their first-light pass — with everything."

"And if the engine doesn't run?" Ruthie asked, and her voice was gentle in a way that made it worse.

Elias looked at her. Then at Felix. And Felix watched him arrive, visibly, at the thing Felix had already been carrying since the radio call: the possibility that the plane at the strip was not sitting there untouched. That a family which chartered survey vessels and filed private flights and had a hundred years of practice at making things disappear might have made one phone call — the call from the payphone, the call someone answered — and arranged for a bush pilot's beloved airplane to have a problem.

"Then I'll fix it," Felix said. "Or I'll die fixing it. But we're not walking into that net on their schedule. We go tonight."

Above them, with a sound like the world clearing its throat, another piece of the mountain let go and fell into the sea, and the ice under their boots shivered, and the countdown went on.

* * *

They made six miles by dusk, Tomas threading them through the icefall's edge with a skill that bordered on the devotional, and camped in the lee of a moraine ridge with the calving front still booming to the north, closer now, the concussions arriving faster, the glacier shedding whole acres into the dark water. Felix took the first watch because he didn't trust himself to sleep, and sat with his back against the rock and the radio in his lap and the sky going the deep bruised violet of a northern spring evening, and he listened to the coast.

At 2100, channel one-six, Denny Marsh again, tired now, reading from his card: the *Meridian* had made port at dusk. Shore party disembarking at first light. And — Felix's hand tightened on the set — the aircraft advisory, updated. The Twin Otter had not returned south. It was on the ground at Kessler Point. Parked. Waiting.

Waiting for morning, Felix thought. Waiting for the net to close in daylight, the way his father's son had said it would.

He looked down the darkening glacier toward the coast, toward the strip he couldn't see but could map in his head to the foot — the wind sock, the fuel drums, the little corrugated hangar he'd rented for eleven years and never once thought of as a place he'd fight for. Somewhere in the dark down there, his airplane sat under the stars, and he would not know until he turned the propeller whether it was still his.

Behind him, in the camp, Ines slept with the ledger's dry bag zipped inside her jacket. Ruthie slept with her notebook. Tomas slept like a man storing up violence. And a little apart, as always, Ash — Elias — lay on his pad with his eyes open, looking at the sky, and when Felix caught his eye across the dying fire, the man didn't look away.

"First light," Elias said quietly. "They'll move at first light."

"Then we move at dark," Felix said, "and we're gone before their light finds anything but an empty strip and a story they'll spend a hundred years trying to bury twice."

The mountain cracked. Somewhere below, in the black, the sea took another piece. The clock ran on.
