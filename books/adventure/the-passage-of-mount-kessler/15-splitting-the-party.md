## 15. Splitting the Party

Tomas Okafor had spent eleven years learning to read ice the way other men read faces, and in all that time he had never once trusted a person as far as he could throw a rope anchor. Trust, in his experience, was a thing you extended like a bridge across a crevasse — carefully, with anchors on both sides, and never without knowing exactly where you'd fall if it gave way.

So when Felix laid out the plan on the floor of the cave mouth, drawing lines in the frost-scummed gravel with a tent stake, Tomas listened the way he listened to weather reports: not for what was promised, but for what was left out.

"Two ways off this mountain," Felix said. He was crouched, shoulders hunched against the draft that came down from the upper gallery, and the storm-light through the entrance made him look older than his forty-odd years — gray at the temples, creases deep as survey lines around his eyes. "The ice road's open now that the front's moved through. Four days, maybe less if the melt keeps ahead of forecast. And the strip below the point is clear enough to fly from, if the engine cooperates."

"And if it doesn't?" Ruthie asked.

Felix didn't answer right away, and Tomas watched him not answer. The pilot had been doing a lot of that since the storm broke — looking at Elias Kessler, who sat apart from the group with his back to the rock, turning his satellite phone over and over in his hands like a man deciding whether to keep a snake or a stone.

"Then we walk," Felix said finally.

"No." Ines spoke up from where she knelt over the ledger, which lay open inside its dry bag, wrapped in three layers of fleece. Her voice had the flat calm she got when she was frightened and refusing to show it — Tomas knew that voice from childhood, from the winter their father's boat went down, from every bad phone call since. "Walking means the originals stay on the mountain. Walking means whoever comes looking finds everything in one place. We split it."

Tomas felt the word *split* go through the group like a cold current through still water. Ruthie stopped writing. Even Elias looked up.

"Split how?" Ruthie asked.

Ines laid her hand flat on the dry bag. "The ledger stays together — the original doesn't get divided, it's too fragile, the pages would separate along the old folds. But I photographed every page before the storm. All of them. High resolution, two copies of each file." She pulled the camera from her jacket and held it up. "One set goes out by air. One goes out by land."

* * *

They argued about it for most of an hour, there in the cave mouth with the glacier ticking and groaning below them like something enormous turning over in its sleep. The melt had begun in earnest; water ran in bright threads down the face of the ice field, and every so often a chunk the size of a truck calved off the lower seracs and boomed across the valley, and everyone flinched, and no one said anything about flinching.

Ruthie wanted the copies. She argued for it with her jaw set, the way she'd argued about everything since the storm pinned them — as if the argument itself were a form of witness, and silence a kind of complicity. "I'm the one who can write it," she said. "If the plane goes down, or the Keslers ground it, or whatever they're planning happens, the story walks out on my own two feet. That's redundancy you can't buy."

"You're also the slowest of us on ice," Tomas said, and immediately regretted the shape of it, because Ruthie turned and looked at him with an expression that could have stripped paint.

"I'm slow because I broke my ankle in March and it's still full of hardware," she said. "Say what you mean."

"What I mean," Tomas said, "is that the overland route needs someone who knows it. That's me. That's not negotiable — none of you cross the ice road without me, and I'm not leaving my sister on a mountain with a man who marked our camp for a salvage crew."

Every head turned toward Elias.

He took it well, Tomas had to give him that. He didn't flinch, didn't perform wounded innocence. He set the satellite phone down on the gravel between them, deliberately, the way a man lays a card face-up.

"I'll go wherever you put me," Elias said. "But you should think about what putting me next to the original tells your family's lawyers. If I'm standing beside Felix when that plane lifts off, they know exactly where the real ledger is. They'll follow the plane. They won't waste time on foot."

"He's right," Ines said quietly.

"He's strategic," Tomas corrected. "There's a difference."

"There always is," Ruthie muttered. "That's what makes liars useful."

Tomas watched his sister's face while they talked. She had gone still in the way she went still before committing to something — the same stillness she'd had at nineteen, telling him she was taking the research post on the coast, telling their mother she wasn't coming home. It was decision settling into bone. When she spoke again, her voice had that settled quality too.

"The original flies. I fly with it. Felix pilots, Elias navigates — because he knows the patrol patterns, and because if he's in that plane, he's not free to make another phone call." She looked at Elias without softening. "You wanted to help expose your family. This is what helping looks like. You don't get to choose the shape of it anymore."

Elias held her gaze for a long moment. Something moved behind his eyes — relief, maybe, or the particular exhaustion of a man who has been carrying a secret so long that being caught feels almost like rest. "Understood," he said.

"And the copies?" Felix asked.

"Overland." Ines turned to Ruthie. "With Tomas. You carry them in the dry bag, inside your pack, against your back. If you go into a crevasse, the bag floats long enough for us to reach it. If you're caught—" she paused, and Tomas heard her choose the next words with care— "you're a journalist hiking out early because the melt scared you. You've got no documents on you. Nothing written. The files are encrypted and the passphrase dies with the conversation we're about to have."

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing, and Tomas found that preparation steadied him the way it always did — the ritual of knots, of weight distribution, of checking and rechecking. He split the rope team's gear with a miser's care: stove fuel divided so neither party carried more than three days' worth, medical kit duplicated, the good radio with Felix and the backup with him. He re-belayed Ruthie's harness himself, shortening the leg loops until she swore at him, and checked the stitching on her pack straps twice.

"You're fussing," she said.

"I'm anchoring," he said. "It's different."

"Is it?"

He didn't answer. Outside the cave mouth the light was going long and gold, and the glacier ran with meltwater, hundreds of small voices braided into one continuous rush. Somewhere down-valley, a serac let go with a sound like artillery, and the echo rolled back up to them a full five seconds later. Four days. Maybe three. The ice road was already starting to rot at the margins — he'd seen it on the morning scout, dark wet blooms spreading across what had been solid white a week ago, meltwater finding the old crevasse lines and widening them from the inside.

He'd crossed that road eleven times in eleven years, guiding researchers and survey crews and once, memorably, a film crew that had paid him triple to get their equipment out before the season closed. He knew every bridge and every bivouac ledge between here and the coast. He also knew that knowledge had a shelf life measured in hours now, not weeks. A route that was safe at dawn could be suicide by noon. That was the arithmetic of the melt, and no amount of planning changed it. You could only move fast, read the ice honestly, and accept that some fraction of every crossing belonged to luck.

"Tomas." Ines appeared beside him at the entrance, and for a moment they stood shoulder to shoulder looking out at the ice field, siblings shaped by the same hard winters, saying nothing because nothing needed saying yet.

"You're sure about flying," he said. It wasn't a question, but he made it one anyway.

"Felix's engine is fixed. He says it'll hold."

"Felix says a lot of things."

She smiled faintly. "I know. I've met him." Then the smile faded, and she turned to face him fully, and Tomas saw that she'd been carrying something all day that she hadn't shown anyone. "If they catch the plane — if the Keslers force it down, or block the strip, or do any of the hundred things rich people do instead of breaking laws — the copies are the only thing left. You understand that. Whatever happens to us, you and Ruthie keep walking. You don't turn around. You don't come back for us."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly what you'd tell me if I were walking out and you were flying." Her voice gentled. "We made each other promise worse things than this, Tomás. Remember the ferry? Remember what you made me swear?"

He remembered. He'd been twenty-two and she'd been nineteen and the crossing had been in open water in November, and he'd made her swear that if the boat went down she wouldn't wait for him — that she'd swim for the lights on shore and not look back. She'd kept the promise. He'd never decided whether he was proud of that or not.

"I remember," he said.

"So."

"So." He exhaled, long and slow, watching his breath drift out into the gold light and vanish. "You keep walking too. Both of you. No heroics at the strip. If the net closes, you cut loose and run for the water and let Ruthie's boat pick you up. Swear it."

"I swear."

He believed her, mostly. It was the best the ice ever offered anyone: mostly.

* * *

Dusk came down blue and cold, and they ate in shifts, and then Felix called them together one last time at the fire ring, where the flame burned low and steady in the shelter of the rock. The pilot had a map spread on his knees, weighted at the corners with stones, and he went over the timing twice: Felix and Ines and Elias would descend to the coastal strip at first light, a four-hour scramble down the lateral moraine, and fly as soon as the wind allowed. Tomas and Ruthie would take the high route onto the ice road an hour later, moving light and fast, bivouacking at the Saddle Rocks tomorrow night and the moraine camp the night after, reaching the tide cave on the third day — if the third day still existed.

"Radio check at noon and midnight," Felix said. "If either party misses two checks in a row, the other assumes the worst and acts accordingly."

"Acts accordingly," Ruthie repeated. "Meaning what, exactly?"

"Meaning nobody does anything stupid and noble," Felix said. "Meaning the evidence matters more than any of us, and we all agreed to that when we signed on, whether we said it out loud or not."

Silence followed that. Not comfortable silence — the other kind, the kind that has weight and temperature. Tomas looked around the fire at the four faces lit orange against the blue dark: Felix with his map and his ten years of running; Ines with her chin up and her hands quiet in her lap; Ruthie already half-turned toward the entrance, toward the east, as if she could see the story waiting for her on the far side of the ice; and Elias Kessler, sitting slightly apart as he always did, firelight catching the famous jawline, the inheritance he was trying to refuse written into the very bone of his face.

Trust made structural, Tomas thought. Not sentimental. Sentiment was a warm feeling you had about people when nothing was at stake. What they were building tonight was colder and more durable: a load-bearing arrangement, each pair anchored to the other, designed so that if one side failed, the other might still hold. Nobody had to believe anybody. Everybody just had to do their part, and the truth would have two chances to survive instead of one.

It was, he reflected, the closest thing to faith he'd practiced in eleven years.

They shook hands all around, which felt formal and strange and exactly right. When Tomas reached Elias last, the man gripped harder than expected and held on a beat longer.

"For what it's worth," Elias said quietly, under the noise of the meltwater, "I hope you make it. Both of you. And I'm sorry about the marker. About all of it."

Tomas studied him for a moment — the tired eyes, the hands that had dialed a payphone and lived to regret it. "Sorry is cheap where you come from," he said. "Prove it costs you something."

Elias nodded slowly, as if accepting a sentence. "I intend to."

* * *

First light came gray and windless, the best conditions the melt would grant them, and Tomas stood at the cave mouth watching the other three descend the moraine — three small figures picking their way down through the scree, Felix in front reading the route, Ines behind him, Elias last, roped loosely to the others in a way that could be read as safety or custody depending on your mood. Tomas read it as both.

Beside him, Ruthie adjusted her pack straps and rolled her shoulders. The dry bag rode flat against her spine, and she'd sewn the zipper pulls to the fabric with dental floss so they couldn't work loose. She'd insisted on doing it herself. He was beginning to understand that Ruthie Calloway did everything herself, and that this was both her armor and her wound.

"Last chance to trade assignments," she said.

"No trades."

"Didn't think so." She squinted down the valley, where the morning haze was burning off the ice. "How bad is it really? The road."

Tomas considered lying to her, then rejected it — lies added weight, and weight was the enemy. "Bad, and getting worse by the hour. We move fast, we don't stop on anything that sounds hollow, and we sleep in shifts. If I say run, you run."

"If you say run, I run," she agreed. Then, quieter, without looking at him: "My editor thinks I'm covering a fishing quota dispute. When this lands, it's going to be the biggest story of my career, and I got here by lying to everyone who trusts me. Does that bother you?"

"It bothers me that it doesn't bother you more," Tomas said, and was rewarded with a startled laugh.

Below them, the three figures reached the bottom of the moraine and became dots, then suggestions, then memory. Somewhere beyond the haze, at the coastal strip, a battered de Havilland waited with a repaired engine and a cargo that could unmake a hundred-year dynasty. Somewhere beyond that, a chartered survey plane was surely already in the air, and lawyers were sharpening paper into knives.

Tomas clipped into the rope, checked Ruthie's tie-in one final time, and stepped onto the ice road with the morning at their backs and the melt singing underfoot.

They had made it perhaps forty minutes, the glacier groaning beneath them like a ship in ballast, when the radio at his hip crackled to life. Felix's voice, thin with distance and static, tight in a way Tomas had never heard it:

"Tomas. Change of plan. There's a vehicle at the strip — two vehicles. They were here before dawn." A pause, filled with rotor-noise or wind or fear. "And someone's cut my fuel line again. We're coming back up. Get to cover. *Get to cover.*"

Above them, far up the valley, the first serac of the day let go, and the sound came rolling down the ice like a door slamming.
