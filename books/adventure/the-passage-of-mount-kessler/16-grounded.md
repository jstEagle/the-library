## 16. Grounded

The Otter would not start.

Felix knew it before he turned the key — knew it the way a man knows a room has been searched, by the small wrongnesses that accumulate into certainty. The preflight had felt off. His tie-downs had been retied, the knots competent but not his. And now the starter turned with a sound like a spoon stirring gravel, and the radial engine caught once, coughed a fistful of blue smoke from the exhaust stack, and died.

He tried again. The prop swung through its arc with a grinding reluctance, caught, shuddered, and quit.

"No," Felix said, to the airplane, to the morning, to the whole gray sweep of Kessler Point. "No, no, no."

He climbed out onto the wing strut and stood there looking down the length of the coast strip. Gravel and salt grass, the tide flats beyond, the water sitting low and pewter-colored under a sky the color of unwashed wool. Somewhere out over the glacier, thunder rolled — not thunder. Calving. The mountain shedding its skin, opening the ice road home and closing the clock with it.

Ines came around the tail, the ledger's dry bag slung across her chest like a bandolier. "That didn't sound good."

"That didn't sound like an engine at all. That sounded like a funeral." He dropped to the gravel and pulled himself under the fuselage, and it took him less than a minute to find it. The oil line to the number-four cylinder had been backed off its fitting — not removed, just loosened a quarter turn, the kind of thing that would hold through a preflight and a start and then weep the engine dry somewhere over open water. And stuffed into the air intake, wedged deep where no casual inspection would find it, a rag. Grease-blackened, deliberate.

Felix lay on his back in the gravel with his hands shaking and thought: *the payphone.* Elias at the payphone in Kessler Point, three weeks ago, and someone on the other end of the line who had listened, and waited, and driven out here in the night with a wrench and a rag and all the patience in the world.

"Ines," he said, and his voice came out flat and strange. "Get Elias. Tell him to bring himself and nothing else. Tell him if he runs, I'll put what's left of this airplane on the radio to every strip on the coast with his real name attached."

* * *

Elias came. That was something. He came at a walk, not a run, hands visible, and when he crouched beside the intake and saw the rag his face did something complicated — not surprise. Confirmation.

"Marcus," he said quietly. "My father's head of security. He used to detail cars when I was a boy. He ties knots like that." He looked at the retied tie-down. "I taught him that knot."

"So you called him," Felix said, "and now he's unmade my airplane."

"I called him to buy time. I told him the expedition was a survey for a mining client. I told him there was nothing at the point worth anyone's attention." Elias sat back on his heels. The wind off the flats pushed his hair across his forehead and he didn't bother with it. He looked, for the first time since Felix had known him, like a man with no performance left in him. "I was lying to both sides. I've been lying to both sides since I stepped off the ferry. It turns out that's a skill, and it turns out it doesn't keep anyone safe, it just means everyone has a reason to hate you."

"Save the eulogy." Felix was already pulling tools from the belly locker. "Is there a second crew? A follow-up? Tell me now, because I'm not finding out from the sky."

"There's a survey plane chartered out of Cordillera. My father's company name on the paperwork. They were scheduled for Thursday." Elias checked his watch, an old habit, an expensive watch. "That's tomorrow."

* * *

They worked.

Felix had rebuilt engines in worse places — a sandbar on the Yalak, a mining road above the tree line with frost heaving the ground under his knees — but never with a survey plane expected inside twenty-four hours and a two-hundred-and-eleven-name indictment in a dry bag against his partner's hip. He flushed the oil line, torqued the fitting, and went after the rag with a bent screwdriver and a length of safety wire, lying in the intake throat with his arm in to the shoulder, swearing in two languages.

Ines proved to be the kind of woman who, handed a problem, simply became part of the solution. She stripped the cowl panels without being asked, labeled every bolt in grease pencil on the aluminum itself, and held the work light steady for two hours without complaint while the wind tried to take it. When Felix needed a mirror to inspect the back side of the baffling, she sacrificed her compact without a flicker.

"There," she said at one point, pointing with her chin. "Left side. What's that?"

Felix looked. A second rag, wadded into the carb heat box.

"He's thorough," Felix said. "I'll give him that."

"He was thorough with two hundred and eleven people too," Ines said, and handed him the screwdriver.

Elias, to his credit — or to whatever was happening inside him, that slow collapse and rebuilding Felix had watched since the stormbound argument — did not stand around. He went through his own pockets and produced things Felix would not have guessed a Kessler carried: a multitool, a roll of electrical tape, a laminated card with the family's aircraft registrations and tail numbers. He sat on an upturned fuel drum and, in a careful, level voice, recited the patrol patterns his father's pilots flew. Which corridors they favored. What altitude they held coming around the headland. How long the survey plane would circle a strip before landing — twelve minutes, maybe fifteen, long enough to photograph everything.

"They'll have a camera man," Elias said. "And a lawyer's letter in a flight case. If they land, the strip becomes a disputed site by nightfall. Injunctions. My family has buried bigger things than a cave with less effort than it takes to bury a fence post."

"Then they don't land," Felix said, arm deep in the engine, knuckles bleeding, "and neither does anyone, until I'm airborne with that ledger."

* * *

By late afternoon the engine was whole. Felix buttoned the cowl, wiped his hands on a rag that was clean, and stood back, and for a moment the three of them just looked at the airplane — dented, sun-bleached, ten years of coast weather in its skin — the way you look at a friend who has come back from somewhere dangerous.

"Try it," Ines said.

Felix hit the starter. The radial coughed, caught, and settled into its familiar ragged idle, and the sound went through Felix like a hand unclenching around his heart. He let it run, watched the oil pressure climb and hold, watched the temps come up green, and killed it before he burned fuel he might need.

"She'll fly," he said. "She'll fly tonight if she has to."

"She'll have to," Elias said, and pointed.

Out over the water, low and coming on, a white twin-engine aircraft with a company livery Felix knew from a hundred fuel docks — Kessler Resource Group, the stylized mountain on the tail. It was an hour out, maybe less. It was not supposed to be here until tomorrow.

"They moved it up," Elias said. "Marcus. He doesn't wait for permission. He never has." He was quiet a moment, watching the plane grow. "He's the one who taught me to drive, you know. Before my father decided I should learn on things with chauffeurs."

"Is that supposed to make me feel something?"

"No. It's supposed to explain why I know he'll have put a man at the far end of the strip, in the grass, with a radio. He always covers the far end." Elias turned to Felix, and in the flat gray light his face had gone hard and clear, the family jawline finally meaning something other than inheritance. "You can't outfly them from this strip. They'll call ahead, they'll have the Cordillera strip watched, the fuel docks. But there's another way."

"Say it."

"Fly the corridor low. Under their patrol altitude, over the calving field while it's open, and come out at the headland where their radar has a dead spot — I know it's a dead spot because my father complained about it for a decade and spent a fortune trying to fix it and never could." Elias's voice was steady. "I can navigate it. I've flown it since I was sixteen, in my father's plane, learning the routes they used to move things they didn't want on the roads."

Felix looked at him. Ten years of reading passengers in the right seat had taught him the difference between a man selling a story and a man telling one, and the difference lived in the eyes, and Elias Kessler's eyes had nothing left to sell.

"If you're wrong," Felix said, "we die in the ice."

"If I'm wrong, we die in the ice," Elias agreed. "If I'm right, we're at the estate strip by dusk, and I walk this ledger up the drive myself, and my father watches his son do it." He looked down the strip at the white plane, now close enough to show its landing lights winking on against the gray. "I spent thirty years being his. I'd like to spend one afternoon being theirs instead." He nodded at the dry bag on Ines's chest. "At the two hundred and eleven."

The survey plane's engines carried across the water, a thin insect drone growing louder.

Ines had the dry bag off and was holding it out to Felix. Her hands were steady. Her voice was not quite. "The overland team is a day behind us. Ruthie and Tomas. Whatever happens here—" she swallowed, "—the copies make it even if we don't. But I'd rather we did."

Felix took the bag. Slung it. Felt its weight settle against his spine like a hand.

"Get in," he said to both of them. "Both of you. Elias — right seat. You navigate, you don't touch anything, and if this is a trap, understand that I've spent ten years learning how to crash interestingly and I will take your family's plane down with us as a footnote."

Elias almost smiled. "That's the first honest threat anyone's made to a Kessler in a hundred years."

Felix swung into the left seat, hit the starter, and the old radial roared to life on the first blade, as if it too understood there was no time left for hesitation. Down the strip, at the far end, just as Elias had promised, a figure rose out of the salt grass with a radio to its mouth.

"Hold on," Felix said, and pushed the throttle to the stop, and the gravel began to sing under the wheels.
