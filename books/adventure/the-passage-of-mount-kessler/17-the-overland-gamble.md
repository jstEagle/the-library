## 17. The Overland Gamble

The ice road began to sing on the second morning.

Ruthie had heard about singing ice her whole life — the way a frozen lake moans and whistles when the temperature swings, the sound of a body of water talking in its sleep. But she'd never heard it like this, never heard it with this particular urgency, this rising note that Tomas stopped for every hundred yards, head cocked, one hand raised for silence like a conductor demanding quiet from his orchestra.

"Water's moving underneath," he said. "That's the sound of it letting go."

They were eleven miles out from the cave mouth, and the glacier's frozen margin — the road Tomas had promised would hold until calving season opened it properly — was failing ahead of schedule. The storm had broken two days ago and taken winter's authority with it. The sun came up now with actual heat behind it, and the meltwater that had been locked in the ice all season was finding its voice: trickling in the crevasses, pooling in the low places, running silver down the face of the glacier in threads that thickened even as Ruthie watched.

She adjusted the strap of the dry bag across her chest. It rode against her sternum like a second heartbeat, heavy with photocopied ledger pages, Ruthie's own field notes, Ines's sketches of the tally chamber, and the photographs they'd taken of everything before splitting the evidence. Two hundred and eleven marks. Two hundred and eleven names waiting to be matched to town records, to church registries, to the graves nobody had ever explained. The original ledger was somewhere over her shoulder in history — strapped into the right seat of Felix Joubert's de Havilland, if the engine had started, if Elias Kessler was telling the truth about anything at all.

Ruthie Calloway had spent nine years writing obituaries for a paper that paid by the inch, and she had learned in those nine years that the dead don't need much. A name, a date, a sentence or two about who loved them. What the dead needed, what they had always needed, was someone willing to write it down while it still mattered.

"You're doing the thing," Tomas said.

"What thing?"

"The journalist thing. Where you go somewhere inside your head and stop watching your feet."

"I'm watching my feet."

"You're watching your feet the way a man watches his feet when he's thinking about something else." He pointed with his chin at a patch of ice ahead, discolored, faintly blue. "There. Go wide left, then come back to my line."

She went wide left. The blue patch turned out to be thin as window glass; when Tomas probed it with his pole, the tip went through without resistance, into blackness that swallowed the sound of its own depth. Ruthie looked at the hole and felt her stomach perform a slow, deliberate revolution.

"How deep?"

"Doesn't matter."

"It matters to me."

"Fifty feet, maybe sixty. Water at the bottom now, probably. Meltwater comes up through the shafts first." He moved on. "Don't think about it. Think about the bag."

The bag. She thought about the bag. She thought about Felix's face when they'd divided the evidence on the glacier — how he hadn't argued, hadn't hesitated, just nodded once when Ines laid out the plan, as though redundancy were a language he'd been fluent in for years. *Each pair knows the other might be caught.* That was the whole architecture of it. If the plane went down, the copies survived. If the overland party fell through the ice, the original flew on. Nobody carried everything. Nobody was indispensable, which meant nobody could be leveraged, which meant — Ruthie understood this was the real design, Ines's design — nobody could be bargained with.

The Keslers' survey plane had been circling the coast since dawn yesterday. They'd heard it as a drone on the wind, seen it once as a glint above the ridge line, patient as a gull over a fish school. Whatever the family intended, it started with knowing where they were. And the ice road home ran straight and exposed for twenty-two miles.

* * *

By noon the road had stopped being a road.

Tomas called a halt at the edge of a melt channel that hadn't existed when they'd come out — a river running on top of the glacier, braided and quick, maybe eight feet across, cutting a canyon into the ice as they watched. The water was the color of skim milk, glacier-flour suspended in it, and where it poured off the channel's lip into a crevasse it made a sound like static.

"We crossed here yesterday," Ruthie said. "This wasn't here."

"This wasn't here an hour ago." Tomas walked the bank, testing, his jaw working. Ruthie had known him for six years — he'd guided her on two assignments back when she still wrote features instead of obituaries — and she knew the set of that jaw. It was the jaw of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Options," she said.

"One: we wait for it to freeze tonight. Except it won't freeze tonight, not anymore. Two: we go around, add six hours, cross the bad crevasse field below the seracs, which is exactly where I don't want to be when the sun's on it. Three—" He stopped walking.

"Three?"

He was looking downstream, toward the coast, toward the point where the glacier's margin dropped toward the shore and the tide caves opened in the sea cliffs. Toward the cave. Their cave.

"No," Ruthie said.

"The tide cave runs under the point. Ines mapped the airflow — it connects to the upper system, we know that, we came down it. The lower gallery exits at the beach level. At low tide there's a gravel floor the whole way." He was already unshouldering his pack, pulling out the tide table Ines had copied for him, laminated against the wet. "Low tide is at 4:40. We make the lower entrance by three, we're through before the water turns."

"And if we're not through before the water turns?"

Tomas didn't answer, which was an answer.

Ruthie sat down on her pack and looked at the milky river eating its canyon and did the math herself, because she was good at math, always had been, it was why the editors gave her the complicated obituaries, the ones with estates and estrangements and dates that didn't reconcile. The overland route with the detour: six extra hours across ground that was deteriorating by the hour, beneath seracs that shed in the afternoon heat, arriving at the coast strip after dark with no guarantee the route would exist tomorrow at all. The tide cave: two hours underground, racing a tide that had already revealed itself to be capable of things the charts said were impossible.

The chart said 4:40. The chart had also said the low tides this spring would run ordinary, and instead the sea had pulled back farther than anyone alive had seen and handed them a cave full of dead men's arithmetic.

"Tomas," she said. "The freak tides. The ones that exposed the cave in the first place. When's the next one?"

He looked at the tide table for a long moment. "Tomorrow morning. Bigger than the last one, if the pattern holds."

"Then the water's going to be stranger than any chart says today, too. Low might be lower. High might be higher." She stood up. "So we go now, and we treat 4:40 as a rumor."

Something in her chest was hammering, and she recognized it, distantly, professionally, the way she recognized everything now — it was fear wearing purpose like a coat. Terror transmuted. She'd written four hundred obituaries and she had learned that grief and terror were the same substance at different temperatures, and that the only thing to do with either was to move it forward, get it onto the page, get it out of your body and into the world where it could do some work.

Two hundred and eleven people had gone into a tunnel in the winter of 1911 because men with ledgers decided their lives were cheaper than the delay of shoring it. The tunnel had been collapsed on purpose. The wages had been entered neatly, in a fair hand, and then the book itself had been sealed in iron and sunk into stone, and a family had spent a century standing on top of it.

Ruthie Calloway was not going to drown in a hole in the ground holding the only copy of their names. But she was going to try, if it came to that, and the trying was the whole point.

"Okay," Tomas said quietly. "Okay. Tide cave."

* * *

The lower entrance breathed cold air at them like something sleeping.

They reached it at ten past three, having descended the last mile along the glacier's snout on ground that groaned and settled underfoot, past melt streams running so fast they'd cut channels since morning. The cave mouth was a black arch in the sea cliff, half its floor already glazed with a skin of new seawater, and the air coming out of it smelled of salt and stone and something older — the mineral breath of a place that had been closed for a century and had only recently remembered how to exhale.

"Ines said the airflow reverses with the tide," Ruthie said. Her voice echoed strangely, doubled, the cave repeating her in a lower register. "Which way is it going now?"

Tomas held up a strip of cloth he'd tied to his pole. The streamer angled inward, into the dark. "In. Sea's still dropping. Air's being pulled in behind the falling water." He frowned. "When it turns, it'll blow out. Hard. That's our clock too — when you feel the wind change direction, we're past low tide and the floor's coming back."

They roped up — twelve feet apart, Tomas leading, Ruthie's headlamp making a small hot circle on walls that glistened black — and went in.

The tide cave was nothing like the upper chambers. The upper galleries had been human places: timber shoring, tool marks, the tally wall with its terrible patient rows. This was the sea's own architecture, vaulted and dripping, the ceiling lost in darkness above the reach of their lamps. Water ran along channels in the floor, chuckling in the dark. Somewhere ahead, deeper, something dripped in a rhythm so regular it sounded intentional, like a metronome keeping time for them.

Ruthie kept one hand flat on the dry bag at her chest. The plastic was slick with condensation. Inside it, under the copies, was the letter she'd written on the third night of the storm, by headlamp, on the backs of her field notes — the letter to her editor, the one that began *If you're reading this, publish everything attached and don't let them slow-walk it.* She'd shown it to no one. Some insurance you didn't discuss.

The floor narrowed. The water deepened to their ankles, then mid-shin, shockingly cold, the kind of cold that made the bones of her feet feel glassy. The walls pressed closer, slick rock furred with pale growth, and the passage bent twice, sharply, so that each turn erased the entrance light entirely and they moved by lamp alone through a throat of stone.

"Tomas." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "How far to the gallery junction?"

"Eight hundred meters, give or take. Then the collapsed timber section, then the beach mouth." He paused. "You okay?"

"I'm fine."

"You're lying."

"I'm a journalist. Lying is a transferable skill." She splashed forward. "Ask me again when the water's at my knees."

It got to her knees forty minutes later, at the base of a rise where the floor stepped up and the water pooled behind it like a held breath. And there, wading through the pool, her lamp caught something that stopped her mid-stride.

Marks on the wall. Not the sea's work. Chisel marks, small and regular, climbing the rock beside the passage in a column — dozens of them, hundreds, the same tally language as the upper chambers, but different. These weren't the count of the lost. She saw that immediately, the way you see the shape of a sentence before you read it. These marks were grouped in fives and tens, orderly, cumulative, and beside several groups someone had cut short vertical strokes, additions, updates.

"They kept counting down here too," she said. "Or—no." She leaned close, water pushing at her thighs, and traced the air above a cluster without touching it. "These are different hands. Look — the chisel angles change. Someone added marks after the collapse. Over years, maybe. This isn't the company counting workers." Her throat tightened. "This is someone counting *days*. Or counting the dead, again, privately. Keeping their own tally because the official one was a strongbox at the bottom of the sea."

Tomas's lamp swung to her. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the drip-echo filled the silence, patient as ever.

"Somebody knew," Ruthie said. "Somebody who was down here knew, and came back, and marked it, year after year, where no one would ever see it. Until the sea took the floor away and showed us."

"Ruthie." Gently. "The water."

She looked down. It had come up past her knees while she knelt there. The metronome drip had changed its rhythm — faster now, doubled, and beneath it, far down the passage, she could hear a new sound: a long, low exhale, like the cave clearing its throat.

The wind on her wet face had reversed.

"It's turning," she said. "Tomas, the tide's turning."

* * *

They ran, as much as anyone can run through knee-deep water in a flooded cave roped twelve feet apart.

The passage widened as they went — mercy, at least, the galleries opening toward the sea mouth — but the water rose faster than the floor did. Shin-deep became thigh-deep became a wade that dragged at every step, and the exhale from the depths grew to a steady wind that pushed against their backs and carried the smell of open salt water ahead of them. Ruthie's world contracted to the circle of her lamp, the bag against her chest, Tomas's silhouette lunging through the murk ahead, and the arithmetic she couldn't stop performing: low tide 4:40, plus however wrong the chart was, minus whatever distance remained, divided by the speed of two people fighting current in the dark.

Her boot caught on submerged rubble and she went down to her waist before the rope snapped taut and Tomas hauled, swearing, hauling, until she found her feet. The dry bag had gone under completely. She clutched it with both arms and stood gasping, and in the lamplight she saw the water around them — milky, swirling, carrying foam now. Real foam. Sea foam.

"The mouth," Tomas said. "I can see the mouth."

Ahead, a gray rectangle hung in the blackness like a lit window: the beach-level exit, perhaps sixty meters on, the afternoon sky visible through it, impossibly bright. Between them and it, the floor dipped into a final basin where the incoming tide was pouring through some submerged channel, and the water there was moving — visibly, strongly, a current running *into* the cave, feeding the fill.

They crossed it linked together, Tomas upstream, both of them leaning into the push, Ruthie's boots losing the floor twice and finding it again, the bag wedged between her body and the current like a shield. Halfway across the basin the water hit her chest. She remembered, absurdly, the exact phrasing of an obituary she'd written in March for a retired ferry captain — *he died as he lived, on the water, in weather he respected* — and thought, with a clarity that felt like laughter: *I am not dying in a cave with a photocopy. I refuse the irony.*

Then Tomas's hand closed on her pack strap and the floor rose under them, and they were scrambling up a gravel ramp on hands and knees, out of the current, out of the black, into wind and gray-gold light and the enormous wet roar of the open coast.

* * *

They lay on the shingle above the tideline and let the wind press them flat.

The sea was coming back in earnest — the freak-tide flats that had stretched bare for half a mile a week ago were filling fast, channels braiding across them, the water advancing in fingers. Above the cliffs, the glacier caught the late sun and burned white. Somewhere inland, Felix was either airborne or not. Somewhere south, Ruthie's editor was either going to answer his phone or not.

Tomas rolled onto his back, chest heaving, and laughed — a single bark of it, startled out of him. "Four-forty," he said. "Chart said four-forty."

"Chart was a rumor." Ruthie pushed herself up. Her legs shook. Her hands shook. Everything shook except, she noticed, the place behind her sternum where the bag rode — that was perfectly, unnaturally steady, as though the papers inside had weight beyond their ounces, ballast of a kind.

She knelt, unzipped the dry bag, and checked it with the methodical care of a woman defusing something. Dry. Every page dry. The copies of the ledger, her notes, Ines's drawings, the photographs of the tally wall — two hundred and eleven marks, reproduced in gray toner on white paper, each one a person who had gone into the ground with a shovel in his hand and a wage entry to his name and nothing else, no marker, no mass said, no line in any book that survived.

Until now.

Ruthie repacked the bag and stood, facing the water, and let the shaking move through her and out. This was the part she'd never learned in nine years of obituaries — the part where the story stops being words and becomes a thing you carry through rising water. She understood suddenly why Felix flew the way he flew, why Ines drew maps of airflow, why Tomas tested every step twice. Purpose wasn't the absence of terror. It was terror, given somewhere to go.

"Tomas," she said. "How far to the coast strip?"

He squinted along the shoreline, doing his arithmetic again, and this time his jaw eased. "Six miles. We can make it by dark if the beach holds."

"Then we walk." She settled the bag against her chest, and the two of them started south along the tideline, small against the cliffs, while behind them the sea climbed steadily into the cave mouth, filling the passages, covering the tally marks on the wall, closing over the place where somebody had once counted the days in secret — the water rising and rising, patient as ink, ready to keep whatever it was given.

And far down the beach, where the shore bent toward the strip, Ruthie saw something that stopped her mid-stride: tire tracks in the wet sand, fresh, cutting north. Heading toward the cave.

Someone else had found the lower mouth.
