## 18. Flight Through Falling Ice

The engine turned over on the fourth try.

Not the clean catch Felix had been praying for through two days of improvised repairs — nothing so merciful — but a ragged, coughing stumble that caught, died, caught again, and then held with a sound like a man breathing through broken ribs. He sat in the pilot's seat with his hand still on the starter, afraid to let go of it, as if the engine were a wild thing that would bolt the moment he stopped paying attention.

"Is that good?" Ines asked from the wing, where she'd been holding the cowling panel they'd wired back in place with safety wire and hope.

"That's running," Felix said. "Whether it's *good* is a different question."

He let it idle at low RPM and listened the way he hadn't listened to anything in ten years. The old six-cylinder had swallowed salt spray during the storm, and whoever had sabotaged the mags had done it neat — a loosened lead here, grit in a fuel strainer there, nothing dramatic, just enough to make a plane into furniture. It had taken him and Ines most of yesterday to find all of it, working by headlamp in the hangar's leaking dark while the survey plane drew its patient circles offshore like a gull that had decided something below was worth watching.

Now the engine ran rough but true, all cylinders pulling, oil pressure steady, temps climbing toward green. Behind him the cargo bay was empty except for the dry bag lashed to the floor ring, and inside the dry bag, wrapped in three layers of plastic and one layer of Ines's spare shirt, the ledger. One hundred and fourteen years old. Two hundred and eleven names' worth of arithmetic. The whole reason any of them were standing on this strip with ice coming down around their ears.

He looked out the windscreen at what was left of the route home.

The glacier had opened overnight the way glaciers do — not gradually, not politely, but all at once, as if it had been holding its breath for a century and had finally exhaled. The calving field below the strip was a chaos of new blue faces where seracs had sheared off, and even now, in the flat grey light, he could see movement: a shelf at the icefall's edge leaning further than it had been leaning an hour ago, meltwater pouring off it in ropes, the whole mass groaning in frequencies he felt in his back teeth more than heard. Tomas and Ruthie were somewhere out there on the overland route with their copies, racing water that was opening crevasses faster than Tomas could read them. That thought lived in Felix's chest like a stone. There was nothing he could do about it except the thing he could do about it — get this plane up, get the original out, draw the survey plane away from the coast so nobody looked too hard at a boat putting in at Kessler Point.

Ines climbed aboard and pulled the door shut. In the right seat, Elias Kessler III — Ash Corrigan, alias retired sometime during the night without ceremony — was already buckled in, a paper chart spread across his knees despite the GPS unit bolted to the panel, marking it up with a grease pencil in quick, certain strokes.

"Patrol corridor runs here," he said, tapping a line parallel to the coast. "My father's charter flies out of Harrow Bay, comes up the shoreline at fifteen hundred feet, cuts inland at the point, does a loop over the glacier mouth, goes home. They've flown it every day this week. If we're airborne before they launch, we're ahead of them. If we're airborne after—" He shrugged. "Then we want to be inside their corridor when they see us, because a plane flying the corridor is a plane that belongs."

"And if they call it in anyway?"

"They'll call it in," Elias said simply. "But my father's people are careful people. Careful people verify before they act. Verification takes forty minutes. We need eleven."

Felix studied him. Two weeks ago he'd have said the man couldn't be trusted to hold a map right-side up. But Elias had spent yesterday elbow-deep in the engine compartment beside them, sleeves rolled, hands black to the wrist, handing tools before being asked, and he'd done it without once making it about himself — which for a Kessler, Felix suspected, was the hardest work he'd ever done.

"You know the patrol patterns," Felix said. "Because you grew up watching your family watch everyone else."

"Yes."

"And you know how they think."

"I'm afraid I know exactly how they think." Elias's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "It turns out that's the only inheritance I ever wanted."

Felix keyed the radio out of habit, got the weather broadcast — winds shifting onshore, gusting thirty in the passes, visibility dropping after noon — and then did the thing he always did and never thought about: checked the escape routes. Water on the left, glacier on the right, the strip behind them already softening at both ends where the permafrost was giving up its grip. Ahead, the corridor between the calving field and the ridge line, maybe half a mile wide, narrowing where the icefall leaned.

Ten years ago he'd flown out of a place like this with a different kind of weight in the back — money that wasn't his, silence that wasn't his to sell — and he'd told himself then that speed was freedom, that the whole sky was an exit. He'd spent a decade learning otherwise, one departure at a time, until every takeoff felt less like leaving and more like failing to arrive anywhere at all.

This takeoff would be different. This one had a direction.

"Strap everything down," he said. "Once we roll, I'm not stopping for anything short of a mountain."

* * *

They lifted off at 0640, into air that tasted like iron and snowmelt.

The strip was shorter than Felix liked — the melt had claimed the last four hundred feet, turning gravel to porridge — so he held the brakes until the engine was screaming against them, then released, and the de Havilland lurched forward like a dog off a chain. Gravel hammered the underside. The tail came light. The strip's end rushed up wet and brown and he hauled her off the ground with maybe fifty feet to spare, gear kissing the tops of the puddles, and then they were up, climbing hard into the grey, and the mountain was suddenly very close and very awake.

The first serac went while they were still in ground effect.

It didn't fall so much as *let go* — a house-sized blade of ice at the top of the calving face folding inward with a crack that reached them through the airframe itself, a shudder in the metal like the plane flinching. Then the sound arrived properly, a rolling boom that echoed off the ridge and came back doubled, and the dust cloud boiled up white and enormous, swallowing the lower glacier whole.

"Left side," Ines said, calm as a woman reading a grocery list, though her knuckles were white on the armrest. "That was close."

"That was a warning," Felix said. "The mountain telling us the price of admission went up."

He banked toward the corridor. Below them the calving field was a battlefield in slow motion — ice towers the size of church steeples shedding their shoulders, meltwater braiding across the surface in silver veins, whole sections of the face slumping and rising again in the sea like breathing things. The tide that had exposed the cave three weeks ago had been the first word of a sentence the ocean was only now finishing. Everything the cold had held shut was opening at once.

Elias leaned toward the windscreen, chart forgotten. For a moment he looked younger, and sadder, and Felix understood that the man was watching his name die. Every ton of ice that came down was burying the site a little deeper, erasing the approach, closing the upper mouth behind rockfall and snow. By summer there might be nothing to find without dynamite and lawyers. The Keslers had lost the race not because anyone beat them but because the earth had simply changed its mind.

"Corridor's clear," Elias said finally, voice steady. "Stay centerline. Ridge turbulence will be bad on the north wall — the sun hits it first and the thermals come off it like chimneys."

"You've flown this?"

"I've been flown this. Since I was six." A pause. "My father liked to show me the property from above. All of it. He used to say you can't manage what you can't see." Another pause, quieter. "I don't think he ever once looked at it from the ground."

Felix flew. The corridor narrowed as they climbed, the calving face on their right throwing up spray where new ice hit the water, the ridge on their left shrugging off plumes of spindrift. The engine ran rough at full power, a hesitation on the number-four cylinder that Felix tracked like a heartbeat he could lose. Eleven minutes to the coast. He'd promised himself eleven minutes, and now he wanted them the way a drowning man wants air — not as time but as distance, as proof, as the space between the man he'd been on the ground and whatever he'd be when the wheels touched down somewhere that mattered.

Halfway through, the mountain tried to close the door.

It started as a sound behind them — not a crack this time but a roar, sustained, the sound of a thousand tons moving at once — and Felix didn't look back because looking back was for men with time. He shoved the nose down instead, trading altitude for speed, and the de Havilland dove into the corridor's throat just as the shockwave of displaced air rolled up the valley and swatted them sideways. Thirty degrees of bank, gone in a blink; he caught it with both hands and his stomach, wings level, altimeter unwinding, the ridge on the left suddenly much taller than it had been.

"Turbulence!" Ines shouted, which was unnecessary, because his entire body was being informed of it continuously.

"Bag okay?" he shouted back.

"Lashed! Ledger okay!"

In the right seat Elias had gone silent, hands braced on the panel, eyes forward. When the worst of it passed he exhaled slowly and said, almost conversationally, "That was the icefall letting go. The whole east face, I'd guess. My father's surveyors won't fly over that for a week."

"Then we've bought more than eleven minutes."

"We've bought the only thing that matters." Elias turned and looked at him directly, and in the grey light his eyes were the pale glacial blue that Felix supposed had launched a hundred years of Kessler portraits. "I owe you an apology, Mr. Joubert. Not for the lie — you'd earned worse than that. For the plan. I came up here intending to steal that ledger and decide alone what the world deserved to know. My family's whole method, in miniature: one man, one room, one decision, everyone else lives with it." He shook his head. "You put me in a room with four other people and made me live with *you* instead. It's the first thing anyone's ever done to me that worked."

Felix kept his eyes on the corridor. "Don't thank me yet. Your father's waiting at the end of this flight, and I intend to land at his front door."

"I know." And Elias smiled then, really smiled, and it transformed his face into something that belonged to no portrait. "I packed a clean shirt."

* * *

The last three miles were the worst, because they were the easiest.

The corridor opened onto the coastal plain and the plain was calm — flat grey water, flat grey sky, the survey plane nowhere in sight yet, the world suddenly gentle, as if the mountain's violence had been some private argument they'd interrupted. Felix leveled off at eight hundred feet, trimmed the plane, and let his hands stop shaking. Beside him Elias folded his chart along its worn creases. Behind him Ines unclipped her harness for the first time since takeoff and he heard her laugh once, short and disbelieving, the laugh of someone who has just set down something very heavy and discovered her arms still work.

"Felix." Her voice came forward between the seats. "Tomas. Try the radio."

He'd been avoiding it. Avoiding it the way he avoided everything, old habit, because the radio could carry news and news could be grief. But she asked, so he keyed the frequency Ruthie carried — the little handheld, line-of-sight, good for ten miles on a good day — and called, and static answered, and he called again.

On the third try, a voice came through thin and crackling and alive: *"—copy, Otter, we read you. We're at the tide cave. Both of us. Dry bag's dry. Tell the pilot he owes me a drink and tell Ines her brother is insufferable and correct about everything."*

Felix closed his eyes for one second — one second, hands level, plane steady — and let the relief move through him like weather.

*"Tell him the drink's on the ledger,"* he transmitted. *"We're clear of the corridor. Heading south. Boat's yours. Get off the ice."*

*"Getting off the ice,"* Ruthie said, and then, softer, through the static: *"Fly it like it matters, Joubert."*

"It matters," he said, to her, to the dead in the bag behind his seat, to the mountain receding behind them with its mouth already closing. "For the first time in ten years, it matters."

He turned the plane south along the coast, toward Harrow Bay, toward the estate strip carved into the headland like a signature, toward the house that had been built on two hundred and eleven graves and the silence bought to cover them. The engine droned. The sea ran silver beneath the wheels. In the right seat, Elias Kessler III watched his inheritance come closer with every mile and did not look away.

Behind them, far up the coast, the icefield boomed once more — long, rolling, final — and Felix did not look back. Whatever the mountain was saying, it was saying it to itself now. Its silence-keeping was over. So was his.

Ahead, small and white against the headland, the Kessler estate strip came into view.

And parked beside it, unmistakable even from two miles out, stood the survey plane — arrived early, engines cooling, and a tall figure in a dark coat standing on the apron, looking up at the sky, waiting for them.
