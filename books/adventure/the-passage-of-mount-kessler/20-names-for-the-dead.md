## 20. Names for the Dead

The story broke on a Tuesday, which Ines would remember later as the most ordinary possible day for the world to change.

She was in the Kessler Point library — the one room in town with a functioning internet connection and chairs that didn't wobble — when Ruthie's byline appeared on the national wire. *THE TWO HUNDRED AND ELEVEN: HOW A FOUNDING FAMILY BURIED ITS DEAD.* Ines read it twice, her tea going cold beside the keyboard, and the strange thing was that the words felt both enormous and small: enormous because they were everywhere now, on screens in Anchorage and Seattle and a newsroom in Toronto where some editor she'd never meet was deciding how long to hold the story above the fold; small because she had already lived inside them. She had held the ledger. She had counted the marks on the wall with her gloved fingertip while her breath fogged in the dark. The article could only ever be a map of a country she had actually walked.

Her phone began buzzing before she finished the second read. It did not stop for eleven days.

* * *

The researchers came first.

They arrived the way weather arrived — forecast for a week, then suddenly present, filling the harbor with a chartered vessel that was far too clean for these waters, its paint still bright, its crew in matching jackets. There were archaeologists from the university, a forensic anthropologist with silver hair and forearms like a dockworker, two conservators who spoke about corrosion rates the way other people spoke about poetry. They carried permits. Real ones, stamped and laminated, issued by a state that had spent a century not looking and now could not look away.

Ines met them at the dock because no one else would. The harbormaster had developed a sudden limp. The chamber of commerce had dissolved into a series of phone calls made behind closed doors. So Ines stood on the fuel dock in the wind with her hands in her coat pockets and watched the too-clean vessel ease in, and felt the particular loneliness of being the person who had opened a door everyone now had to walk through.

"You're Dr. Okafor." The anthropologist — her name was Halloran — came down the gangway with her hand already extended. "Your airflow hypothesis. The upper entrance. That was elegant work."

"It was my brother's boots and a smoke pencil," Ines said. "The glacier did the rest."

Halloran smiled, but her eyes went past Ines, up the shore, to where the tide line ran gray and flat under an overcast sky. "How is it?" she asked. "The cave. After the storm season."

Ines had gone out to look, three days earlier, in Tomas's skiff. She had stood at the mouth at low tide and understood, with a sensation like a hand closing gently around her heart, that the sea was already taking it back. A shelf of the entrance had calved off — rock, not ice, but the same patient verb applied. The floor was scoured. The lower gallery, the one with the collapsed timber, had taken on gravel from a winter of storm surge. The tally wall still stood. She had counted the marks again, alone, and they were all still there, two hundred and eleven small deliberate cuts in stone, patient as they had been for a hundred and thirteen years.

"It's still there," she said. "But it's leaving."

Halloran nodded slowly. "Then we'd best be quick."

* * *

The reopening took nine days, and Ines was there for all of them, because Tomas was guiding and someone had to be the person who knew which of the carved marks were original and which were the damage from their own desperate passage in winter.

Tomas was different now. She watched him work and saw it. Before the expedition he had guided tourists across the glacier with a kind of cheerful fatalism — *the ice does what it wants, we just negotiate* — and he had come back from the overland run with Ruthie quieter, more deliberate, as though he had negotiated with something larger than ice and won a draw instead of a victory. He read the crevasse fields now like a man reading old letters. He never rushed. When the conservators wanted to move fast, he simply stood in the tunnel and let them feel the silence until they slowed down on their own.

The first official party went in on the fourth day: Halloran, the two conservators, a state archivist named Pemberton who kept saying *extraordinary* in a voice that suggested he had never once used the word at work, and — at Tomas's insistence — old Marta Ellingbye, who was eighty-one and had taught half the town's children to read and who had, in 1962, as a girl, seen her grandfather burn a box of papers in a woodstove and never forgotten the smell.

Ines watched Marta stand before the tally wall.

The old woman had refused a headlamp's full beam; she'd asked for it dimmed, and in the half-light the carved marks rose out of the stone like something surfacing through dark water. She stood there for a long time. Her breath made small clouds. When she finally spoke, her voice was the one she'd used on generations of first-graders sounding out their first words.

"My grandfather used to say the mountain ate them," she said. "I thought it was a story. Like the one about the bear that paid for bread." She reached out and did not quite touch the stone. "It wasn't a story."

"No," Ines said softly. "It wasn't."

Marta turned, and her eyes were dry and furious and ancient. "Then somebody is going to write down every name."

* * *

The names were the hard part, and the names were the whole point.

The ledger gave wages, not identities — *J. Merck, 14 days; A. Lindqvist, 21 days; the Ferro brothers, paid to account* — but wages left a trail, and the trail led through church registries and shipping manifests and a defunct labor agent's records that Halloran's team found in a Seattle archive, misfiled for eighty years. The workers had come from everywhere: Norwegian fishermen gone broke, Finnish loggers, a dozen men from a Cantonese labor contractor's roster, three brothers from a farm outside Dawson who had gone into the tunnel together and, according to a single line in a sister's letter home, *were expected back for spring planting.*

The sister's letter was read aloud on the radio one evening by a researcher with a careful voice, and Ines, listening in the galley of the research vessel, had to go out on deck and stand in the cold until she could breathe again.

*Expected back for spring planting.* One hundred and thirteen years late.

By the end of the second week they had confirmed one hundred and sixty-three names. The conservators' work on the strongbox fragments and the timber stamps filled in another thirty-one with reasonable confidence. Seventeen remained unknown — the tally knew them, but no paper did, and Halloran said, in the flat honest way Ines was coming to trust, that some might stay unknown forever. *The mountain kept them,* she said. *Paper is not obliged to.*

Two hundred and eleven marks. One hundred and ninety-four names. Ines did the arithmetic every night and every night it hurt in the same fresh place.

* * *

The church was Marta's doing, and the town's, in a way no one had predicted.

The Kessler Point church was a squat cedar building with a bell that had not been rung for anything but funerals in living memory. Marta convened a meeting — not of the town council, which was still circling the question like gulls over fish guts, but of anyone who came, and nearly everyone came, because the story was on every screen and in every kitchen and the town could no longer pretend it belonged to anyone else.

The meeting went the way Ines had feared and hoped: badly, then honestly.

The resentment came first, and it came from places she understood. The cannery crew, whose jobs hung from Kessler capital like fruit from a branch. The harbormaster with his convenient limp. An old man named Dree who stood up and said, "My father worked for them his whole life and they treated him fair, and now you want to dig up the past and cost this town everything," and his voice cracked on *everything* and Ines saw that his anger was grief wearing anger's coat — grief for a version of the place he loved that could no longer exist.

But then Ruthie stood up. Ruthie, who had come overland through collapsing ice with a dry bag of copied pages strapped to her chest, who had frost-nipped fingertips that still hadn't fully recovered. She didn't argue. She read names. She stood at the front of the church hall and read them slowly — *Johan Merck. Antti Lindqvist. Wen Ferro. Lu Ferro. Samuel, Elias, and Peter Voss* — and let each one sit in the air like a struck bell, and when she had read forty of them she stopped and said, "They were here. They built the dock you fish from and the road you drive and the tunnel that made all of it possible, and they were buried on purpose, and the only thing anyone ever gave them was a mark on a wall. I'm asking what we're going to give them now."

Nobody answered. But nobody left, either. And in the silence, Marta Ellingbye rose from the front pew and said, "We'll put them in the church," in the voice of a woman who had taught children for forty years and was not asking.

* * *

They went up on a Saturday in late spring, when the light stayed past ten and the air off the water smelled of kelp and thaw.

Ines had imagined a somber affair, a handful of the committed. Instead the church filled and then overflowed, people standing along the walls and out the doors, and the strangeness of it was the mixture — the cannery crew beside the archivists, old Dree in the back row with his cap in his hands, a camera crew held firmly outside by Tomas, who had appointed himself to the door and permitted exactly one photographer, Ruthie's choice, a quiet woman who shot film and did not use a flash.

The names were painted on cedar panels — one hundred and ninety-four of them, in a careful hand, with seventeen blank spaces at the end that Ines had insisted on. *For the ones the paper lost,* she told Marta. *They get their place too.* The panels hung along the north wall, below the small windows, where the afternoon light would find them.

There was no sermon. Marta read a passage from the sister's letter — *expected back for spring planting* — and then the room did something Ines had not anticipated: people simply began to speak the names. Not in unison, not in order. One voice, then another, from different corners of the church, the way a congregation takes up a hymn it half remembers. *Merck. Lindqvist. Ferro. Voss.* The names moved through the room like weather, like tide, and Ines stood at the back with her brother's shoulder against hers and felt the truth of it land in her chest: uneven, incomplete, contested — and real. Truth didn't arrive clean anywhere, she thought. It arrived the way the tide cave had arrived, exposed all at once by forces no one controlled, and then it was everyone's to decide what to do in the light.

Afterward, half the town walked down to the shore. Ines went with them, not leading, not following, just walking. Someone had brought flowers — cut tulips from a greenhouse in Kessler City, absurd and bright against the gray shingle — and people knelt and set them at the tide line, at the place where the cave had shown its mouth to Felix and Ines on a morning of impossible low water. The sea took the first ones within the hour. That seemed right, too. The flowers were not for keeping. They were for saying.

Dree came down last. He stood apart from the others for a long time, cap still in his hands, and then he walked to the tide line and put down not flowers but a tin cup — an old one, dented, the kind that had hung on every work site in the region for a century — and filled it with seawater, and set it upright among the tulips.

"For the crew," he said, to no one, to everyone. "Whoever they were."

* * *

The lawsuits began on Monday, because of course they did.

The Kessler estate's lawyers filed to challenge the site's status, to contest the archive access, to dispute the forensic chain of custody on the ledger — eleven filings in the first week, and Ruthie tracked them all with the grim relish of a woman who had finally found a story worth the frostbite. The foundation in the workers' names was established the same week, funded by a settlement Elias Kessler had forced through by threatening to testify against his own family's interests, and the two legal efforts ground against each other like ice against ice, and Ines understood that this, too, was the truth landing unevenly. The dead did not get a clean victory. Nobody did.

But the researchers kept coming. Halloran's team secured a full season. The university announced a memorial research program. And on a bright morning in June, Tomas stood at the head of the first officially sanctioned expedition party — twelve people, permits and radios and a satellite check-in schedule — and gave the safety briefing Ines had heard him give a hundred times, except this time he ended it differently.

"The cave is closing," he said. "The sea's taking it back a little more every month. We don't know how long it'll be there. So we work carefully, we work honestly, and we don't take anything that isn't ours to take. The people in that wall waited a hundred and thirteen years for someone to come look at them properly." He looked down the line of faces, and Ines, standing at the edge of it, saw something in her brother's expression she had never seen in all his years of guiding: not the negotiator's fatalism, but purpose. "We're not going to rush them now."

They went in at low tide, twelve headlamps bobbing down into the dark, and Ines stayed at the mouth to work the airflow instruments, and as the party's lights receded into the chambers she thought she heard — faint, carried on the draft that had started everything — the sound of Marta Ellingbye's voice, reading names to the wall.

That evening, walking back along the shore, Ines found Felix waiting by the skiff. He looked thinner than he had in winter, and grounded in a way that had nothing to do with engines — he'd told her the night before that his license was under review, that it might be a year, that for the first time in ten years he wasn't going anywhere, and that the strangest part was how little it frightened him. He was watching the tide line, where the last of the tulips still held, ragged and bright.

"They're saying on the radio," he said, without turning, "that there's a plane coming in tomorrow. Charter, out of the south." He paused, and Ines saw his jaw tighten in the fading light. "The tail number's one I know. It belongs to a man I've been running from for ten years."

The tide came in around the flowers, patient as everything else, and did not stop.
