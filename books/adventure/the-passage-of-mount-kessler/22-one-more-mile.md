## 22. One More Mile

The morning of the last flight came in silver and salt.

Felix stood on the apron at dawn with a thermos of coffee going cold in his hand and watched the light come up over the water the way it always did here — slow, unhurried, as if the day had nowhere else to be and neither did he. That was the strange part. That was the part he still hadn't gotten used to, three weeks after everything: mornings without a departure time. A hangar he wasn't secretly planning to leave. His name, for the first time in ten years, attached to something that hadn't flown away from him.

The *Kestrel* sat where he'd left her, tied down against a wind that never quite arrived, her paint faded to the color of driftwood, her fabric patched in six places he could point out like scars on his own body. Ten years of medicine runs and mail sacks and gossip ferried between settlements too small for roads. Ten years of one-way thinking — every destination a waypoint, every town a place to refuel and not be known. He had told himself it was freedom. Most nights he'd almost believed it.

He set down the thermos and ran his palm along her cowling. Cold metal, then warming under his hand, the way living things warm.

"One more," he said to her. "Then we'll see."

In the cargo bay behind the second seat, wrapped in canvas and lashed down with more care than he'd ever given a crate of vaccines, lay the wreath. Ines had made it. Of course Ines had made it — she'd spent two evenings at Ruthie's kitchen table twisting kelp rope and copper wire and sprigs of beach sage into a circle heavy enough to sink, dense enough to hold its shape in cold water, beautiful in the way that true things are beautiful even when they're grim. Two hundred and eleven names were written on strips of oiled linen wound through the green. She'd worked by lamplight, tongue between her teeth, and when Felix had offered to help she'd handed him the pen instead.

"You write some of them," she'd said. "It should be more than one hand."

So he had. Forty-three names in his own careful print, learned from the ledger pages now sealed in the state archive, each stroke an apology a century late. *Aldous Merrick. The Vane brothers. A man recorded only as Sunday, because that was the only day anyone knew him.* He had written them all out and felt something shift in his chest that he didn't have a word for yet — something between grief and relief, the feeling of setting down a weight you'd carried so long you'd mistaken it for your spine.

He loaded the wreath, checked the tie-downs twice, and walked the preflight in no hurry at all. That was new too. For ten years his inspections had been quick and hungry, a pilot's impatience dressed up as thoroughness. Today he let the ritual take its full length. Fuel sample, clear and bright. Control surfaces free. Tires, struts, the little dent in the port-side flap he'd gotten flying out through the calving field with Elias shouting headings beside him and the mountain coming apart behind them like a held breath finally released. He touched the dent. He'd decided not to fix it. Some dents you keep.

The engine caught on the third blade, coughed, settled into its old ragged hum. Felix taxied out past the fuel drums, past the hangar where he'd pried open a strongbox while a marine biologist held a lantern and the past reached up out of the rock to look at him — and lifted off into air so clean it seemed rude to disturb it.

* * *

The flight south along the coast took forty minutes if he flew it straight, and he did not fly it straight.

He climbed instead, higher than the run needed, until Kessler Point was a gray smudge on the edge of the world and the whole coastline unrolled beneath him like a map someone had loved into being — fjords braided with morning fog, rivers the color of pewter, the glacier inland holding its white bulk against the sky with the patience of something that had been there before any of them and would be there long after. From this height he could see where the ice road had been, the frozen margin Tomas had led them across, already broken now, already becoming open water and scattered brash. The season turning over. The mountain forgetting them, or pretending to.

He thought about what the mountain actually kept. He'd been thinking about it for weeks, ever since the researchers' first report came back: the sea was reclaiming the cave. The freak tide that had exposed it had been a once-in-a-lifetime arrangement of pressure and moon and weather, and the ocean was folding the entrance back into itself a few inches a week, patient as interest accruing. By midsummer it would be gone. By next year, a rumor. The tally walls — two hundred and eleven marks cut into stone by hands that had nothing left to count but their dead — would go back under the waterline, back into the dark, back into the keeping of the place that had held them for a hundred years while everyone above forgot.

Some people had been angry about that. Letters to the paper, voices at the church meeting: *the site should be preserved, concrete it open, make it permanent, don't let them bury it again.* Felix understood the anger. He'd felt it too, standing in that chamber with the lantern light jumping across all those small carved strokes, wanting to shout at the rock, at the century, at the sheer effrontery of forgetting.

But he'd come around to Ines's view, sitting in Ruthie's kitchen one night while rain worked the windows. "The cave was never meant to be a monument," she'd said, peeling an orange in one long unbroken spiral, a trick she performed without looking. "It was meant to be a grave. We disturbed it because we had to — because the truth was in there and the truth doesn't keep. But now the truth is out. It's in the archive. It's on the church wall. It's in two hundred and eleven families' mailboxes." She'd laid the orange peel down in a perfect circle, and Felix had stared at it and understood. "Let the mountain have the bones back. We got what we came for."

What they'd come for. He turned the plane gently over the point now, banking wide, and looked down at the town that had become, without his permission, the closest thing to home he'd had in a decade. The church with its new memorial board inside, planed cedar and brass pins, one card per name, the cards lettered by volunteers over six weeks of evenings. The harbor where Ruthie's boat had come in with the copies, her face windburned and triumphant, Tomas gripping the rail like a man who'd argued with the sea and won on points. The estate on the hill, smaller-looking than it used to be, its grounds half-shuttered since the foundation took the endowment and the lawsuits took the rest. And the shore below the bluff, where the tide was doing its slow work, where the water lay flat and gray-green over a cave mouth that was closing like an eye.

Felix throttled down. He brought the *Kestrel* low along the tide line, lower than regulations allowed and lower than he cared to explain, and when he judged the drop right he slid the window open and let the cold slap his face and pulled the wreath up from behind the seat.

It was heavier than it looked. He'd known it would be. Names weigh something, even a hundred years later, even written on linen. He held it out into the slipstream for one breath — long enough to feel it pull at his arms like something alive, long enough to say, silently, in the cockpit roar where no one could hear him and no one needed to: *You're counted now. You're named. Somebody knows.*

Then he let go.

The wreath tumbled, caught the wind wrong, righted itself, and dropped the last thirty feet into the shallows with a sound he felt more than heard. It landed upright. Of course it did — Ines had weighted it perfectly, because Ines weighted everything perfectly. It rode the first wave out, turned slowly, began its drift along the shore where the flowers had been appearing for weeks now, anonymous offerings left at the tide line by townspeople who hadn't asked for any of this but couldn't seem to stop coming down to look at the water.

Felix climbed away from it and did not look back. You only get one look back per ceremony; he'd learned that much from funerals.

* * *

He should have turned north then. North was the strip, the hangar, the routine, the next contract, the next settlement waiting on diesel parts and insulin and the latest rumor about who was leaving whom. North was the life he'd built out of departures, the life that fit him like an old glove fits a hand that's stopped noticing it's there.

Instead he sat level at four thousand feet with his thumb hovering over the radio and had the conversation with himself he'd been postponing for three weeks.

Here was the thing nobody warned you about, after you did the brave thing. After the story broke, after the names went up, after Elias stood on his father's gravel drive and handed the original ledger to a journalist on camera and said, in a voice that carried, *"I resign my name"* — after all of it, the cameras left, the lawyers thinned out, the foundation opened its first accounts, and the world moved on at its usual pace. And what remained was just: a man, a plane, and the discovery that he had no idea how to stay anywhere. Ten years of leaving had worn a groove in him deeper than any glacier. He knew how to be wanted briefly. He knew how to be useful in transit. What he did not know — what made his palms sweat worse than the calving field ever had — was how to be somewhere on purpose. How to file a return trip. How to let someone expect him and then actually arrive.

Because there was someone. There had been for longer than he'd admitted, probably since the hangar, probably since the strongbox, maybe since the moment on the bluff when Ines had grabbed his sleeve and said *land, land, land* and he'd looked down at that impossible black mouth in the tideline and felt, for the first time in ten years, curiosity stronger than the itch to be elsewhere. Since then she'd been everywhere in his days — arguing strategy over charts, writing names beside him at Ruthie's table, standing hip-deep in the surf the day the researchers went in, guiding the first official party while Tomas spotted her from the ridge. And every time Felix had felt the old restlessness rise, the whisper that said *time to move along before anyone gets a claim on you*, she'd been there, steady as a heading, not asking him to stay, just making staying look possible.

Last week she'd said it plainly, the way she said everything. They'd been on the bluff at dusk, watching the water take another inch of the cave mouth, and she'd said, "I'm not going anywhere, Felix. I want you to know that so you can stop calculating your exits." Then she'd kissed him, brief and certain, and walked back toward town without checking whether he followed.

He had followed. But he hadn't answered. Not really. Not yet.

Now, alone at altitude with the whole coast spread out beneath him like a question, he realized the answer had been forming the entire flight — in the wreath, in the names in his handwriting, in the fact that he'd taken off this morning with no destination filed at all, which for Felix Joubert was less a habit than a diagnosis.

He keyed the radio. "Kessler Point traffic, de Havilland Fox-Bravo-Kilo, eight miles north, inbound for landing." A pause. Then, because the tower frequency doubled as the harbor office line and because Ruthie answered it most mornings and because he wanted witnesses, god help him, he added the words he hadn't said aloud in ten years:

"Requesting to file a return trip. Overnight."

Static. Long enough that his heart did something embarrassing. Then Ruthie's voice, dry as beach gravel and twice as pleased: "*Overnight?* Copy that, Fox-Bravo-Kilo. I'll alert the media." A beat. "Also, Ines is standing right here and she says — hold on, she's taking the handset—"

"Don't you dare crash," Ines said, close and clear and laughing, and the sound of it went through him like sunlight through fog. "Land soft. I'll wait."

*I'll wait.* Two words. He'd spent a decade arranging his whole life so no one would ever have occasion to say them, and here they were, and the sky didn't fall, and the plane flew on, and nothing ended.

"Copy," Felix managed. "I'll be the one landing."

* * *

He came in over the harbor at half past ten, the light gone gold and long, gulls scattering off the breakwater in outrage. The strip was exactly as he'd left it and entirely different — because now it was a place he was returning to, which changed its physics completely. He flared, touched down gentle as an apology, and rolled out past the windsock with the engine ticking as it cooled, and sat for a moment in the sudden quiet with his hands still on the yoke, listening to the metal settle, letting it be done.

She was waiting at the fence line. Not rushing. Just there, hands in her coat pockets, hair full of wind, watching him the way she watched the tide — with total attention and zero demand. Felix climbed down onto the gravel and stood on the ground of a place he'd landed a hundred times, and for the first time it held.

"You're grounded," she called.

"I'm grounded," he agreed, walking toward her. "Indefinitely. I hear the pay is terrible."

"The pay is terrible. The benefits are—" she considered, "—me, mostly. And Ruthie's chowder on Thursdays."

"Sold."

They walked in from the strip together as the sun went down behind the ridge and the first lamp came on in the harbor office and somewhere inland the mountain stood in the last light, white and silent and keeping — but keeping differently now, he thought. Keeping its dead, yes. Keeping its secret, no. The silence it held these days was the ordinary kind, the kind a landscape earns, and it belonged to no one.

That night, in a room above the harbor office with a quilt that smelled of cedar and a window cracked to the sound of water working the shore, Felix slept without dreaming of runways. And in the morning, when he woke to gulls and the smell of coffee and found a note propped against the thermos on the sill — *Gone down to check the tide line. Come find me. — I.* — he dressed quickly, and buttoned his coat against the bright cold, and stepped out into a town that knew his name, and went, without hurrying and without hesitating, to be found.
