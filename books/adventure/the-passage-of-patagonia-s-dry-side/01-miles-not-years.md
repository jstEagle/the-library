## 1. Miles, Not Years

The Vencedora sat in forty-one meters of cold green water, and I knew her better than I'd ever known a lover.

That's not a confession I make lightly. Ten years of salvage diving will teach you intimacy with steel the way other people learn it with skin. You learn where a hull is ticklish, where it's scarred, where it flinches under your touch. The Vencedora had been down eleven months — long enough for the Patagonian current to drape her in kelp like a widow's veil, not so long that her bones had forgotten they were once a navy freighter, two hundred feet of gray arrogance that had run aground on an uncharted shoal in a fog she had no business being out in.

Uncharted. That word again. It kept surfacing in my life like something drowned that refused to stay down.

I hung at fifteen meters on the descent line, equalizing, watching the wreck resolve out of the murk below me — first the mast, then the bridge windows staring up with that blind, patient look wrecks have, then the whole long body of her lying on her port side in the sand. Above me, through forty feet of water gone silver with morning light, the surface was a trembling ceiling. Somewhere up there was the *Cormorán*, my boat, my livelihood, my entire address for the past decade. Somewhere beyond that was a coast so dry the mapmakers had given up shading it green.

I checked my gauges. Checked them again, because checking twice is what keeps salvage divers alive and checking once is what gets their names read into the wind at memorial services. Then I dropped toward the cargo hold, and the water closed over me like a hand.

* * *

You measure a life however you can. Some people use years, anniversaries, the birthdays of children they see twice annually. I use nautical miles. It started as a joke in my first season — a skipper in Ushuaia asked how old I was and I told him I didn't know, but I'd logged four thousand miles since I left home. He laughed. I didn't. Because it was true: somewhere around the time I walked off the ferry at Punta Arenas with one duffel bag and a grudge against everything behind me, I stopped counting calendar days and started counting distance. Distance was honest. Distance never lied to you about how far away you actually were.

Ten years. Roughly sixty thousand nautical miles, if you count the crossings and the coastal work and the year I wasted in the South Atlantic chasing a salvage contract that evaporated like everything else does. Sixty thousand miles from a house in Puerto Madryn where a woman named Elena Amberdale used to live, before she decided the sea was easier than staying.

I don't dive thinking about her. I want to be clear about that. I dive thinking about bottom time, decompression tables, the tensile memory of rusted steel. But there are moments — hanging in blue nothing, breathing that mechanical rhythm, weightless as a held breath — when the mind wanders where it shouldn't, and I think: *she would have hated this* or *she would have loved this,* and either way the thought lands in my chest like a hook set too deep.

My crew called me Odile. My clients called me Ms. Amberdale. The navy liaison, a man named Ferrer with a jaw like a filing cabinet, called me "the contractor," which told me everything I needed to know about how he saw me: equipment. A tool with a pulse.

Tools don't ask questions. That was the arrangement, and for ten years I'd honored it beautifully.

* * *

The hold of the Vencedora was a cathedral of collapsed shelving and silt. My torch cut a cone through the gloom, and the particulate hung in it like snow that had lost its way. Mateo was already inside, working the aft section, his bubbles rising in silver strings. Good man, Mateo. Twenty-six, hands like a mechanic and a face that hadn't yet learned to lie convincingly, which in our trade was either a liability or a virtue depending on the day.

"Amberdale." His voice came through the comm unit, tinny and close. "You want to see this."

"Unless it's the manifest crates, no. We're on the manifest crates."

"It's not the manifest crates."

I swam aft. He'd cleared a fall of deck plating and exposed a recess built into the bulkhead — not cargo stowage, but something purpose-built, welded in after construction, which already made it interesting. Set into the recess was a strongbox. Steel, sealed, roughly the size of a sea chest, with no markings except a stenciled number half-eaten by corrosion and a lock mechanism that looked military rather than commercial.

Salvage law is simple and universally ignored: naval vessels are sovereign property, and anything we raised belonged to the navy, full stop. Our contract covered the manifest cargo — copper ingots, machine parts, the boring freight of bureaucracy. It did not cover mystery boxes welded into bulkheads by someone who wanted them found only by people who already knew where to look.

"Ferrer's going to want this destroyed," Mateo said.

"Ferrer's going to want this *documented,*" I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is paperwork."

But even as I said it, I was running my glove along the seal and feeling the wrongness of it. Fresh silicone. Someone had sealed this box recently — within the last year, maybe less. You don't reseal a box on a ship that sank eleven months ago unless you did it before she sailed, expecting her never to arrive wherever she was going.

Or unless you expected her to sink.

I looked at Mateo through his mask. He looked back. Underwater, nobody can hear you decide things, which is convenient, because I decided right there.

"We log it," I said. "Position, dimensions, condition. Standard recovery. Nothing unusual."

"Odile—"

"Nothing. Unusual." I said it the way you say a thing when you mean the opposite, and he heard it, and he nodded, and we both pretended that was normal.

* * *

The winch brought it up at 1400 hours, dripping, barnacle-crusted at the corners but fundamentally sound. The deck of the Cormorán smelled of diesel and salt and the particular staleness of wet rope, and the coast behind us was a brown rampart with no green on it anywhere, just rock and thorn scrub running down to a sea the color of hammered pewter.

Ferrer arrived by inflatable at 1600, precisely, because men like Ferrer are precise about everything except morality. He stood on my deck in his civilian clothes that fit him like a uniform anyway, and he looked at the strongbox, and I watched his face do something very small and very controlled.

"That wasn't on the manifest," he said.

"No," I agreed.

"What is it?"

"You tell me. It's your ship."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise; calculation. "It's ballast," he said. "Counterweight. It has no value. Navy regulations require that unlisted materials recovered from wreck sites be destroyed on-site to prevent contamination."

"Contamination," I repeated.

"Of the site. Of the record." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who has practiced smiling at people he considers furniture. "You'll open it, confirm it's ballast, and dispose of it. Cut it up if you have to. I'll witness."

Now here is a thing about me that ten years of miles have carved deep: I do not like being handled. There's a version of this story where I'm a simpler woman, where I take the contract money, cut the box apart, watch whatever's inside scatter into the sea, and sleep fine. I've met that version of myself at various points in my life. She's efficient. She's survivable. She's also the reason I haven't been home in ten years, because the last time I chose efficiency over curiosity, the cost was a person, and I've been paying it off in distance ever since.

"Regulations," I said slowly, "require destruction. My insurance requires documentation. Let me photograph it first. For the file."

"For the—"

"Insurance," I said. "You know how insurers are. They want everything photographed, catalogued, cross-referenced. Bureaucrats of loss." I shrugged. "An hour. Then we destroy it, you witness, everyone goes home happy."

He considered. I watched him consider — weighing whether I was worth the friction, deciding I probably wasn't, filing me under *compliant tool.* "One hour," he said. "Then it's destroyed. And Amberdale—" he paused at the rail, inflatable idling below, "—whatever you think you found down there, you didn't. Ships carry ballast. Wrecks carry secrets. The smart ones know the difference."

He dropped down to the inflatable and buzzed away toward the navy tender anchored beyond the shoal, and Mateo appeared beside me holding a wrench he had no use for, the way men hold objects when their hands need employment.

"He knows we opened the recess," Mateo said quietly.

"He knows we *found* it. Different thing." I ran my palm over the box's lid. Cold steel, warming in the afternoon sun. "Get the cutting gear ready. Loudly. Where the tender can see."

"And?"

"And then we wait for dark."

* * *

Here's what I did instead of destroying it, and I'll tell it plainly because plainness is all I have left of honesty some days:

At 2300, with the navy tender's lights a distant smudge and Ferrer presumably asleep in whatever cabin he'd claimed, Mateo and I lowered the strongbox into the fish hold beneath the ice plates, under three hundred kilos of crushed ice and a false floor I'd had built years ago for exactly the kind of cargo that doesn't appear on manifests. I welded the floor shut myself. Sparks in the dark, the smell of burnt flux, Mateo standing watch at the rail with a shotgun across his arms that he'd never fired at anything but gulls.

"Why?" he asked finally. Not accusatory. Just asking, the way you ask a question whose answer might change your life.

I sat back on my heels and looked at my hands — scarred knuckles, a missing fingernail on the left ring finger, the hands of a woman who had spent a decade touching other people's disasters for money. Sixty thousand miles. And every one of them bought with the same currency: don't ask, don't keep, don't go back.

"Because a man who seals a box and sinks a ship wants it lost," I said. "And I've spent ten years being paid to find lost things. It would be unprofessional to start leaving them lost now."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I'm giving tonight."

He held my gaze a moment longer, and I saw the war in him — loyalty pulling one way, fear the other, and fear with better funding. Then he nodded once and went below, and I stayed on deck with the welding torch cooling in my hand and the dry coast looming black against the stars.

Somewhere inland, past that brown rampart, there were rivers I'd never followed and canyons I'd never seen and a country that had erased things from its own maps. I didn't know yet that the box under my feet was a door into all of it. I only knew that for the first time in months, the restlessness that lived in my chest like a second heartbeat had gone quiet — replaced by something sharper.

Anticipation. Or dread. At this range, they feel identical.

The radio crackled at dawn with a message from the navy tender: Mr. Ferrer requested the destruction certificate by 0900, and further, a prospector named Tomás Quillén had been asking after me in port — an old man, the message said, sick, insistent, claiming the box was his.

I read it twice, and the second heartbeat started up again, faster.

Sixty thousand miles from home, and it seemed the past had finally decided to swim out and meet me.
