## 2. The Prospector's Bargain

The cantina at Puerto Cabo Raso had a name once, painted on a board above the door in letters that had surrendered to the sun one at a time. Now it was just the place with the ceiling fan that turned with the conviction of a man who no longer believed in what he was doing. I liked it immediately. It was honest about its decline.

I found Tomás Quillén in the back corner, where the light came through a window the color of old beer and fell across a table scarred by decades of knives and elbows and arguments. He was smaller than I'd expected. Salvage work teaches you to estimate mass through water, through silt, through steel plating — you learn to read bulk the way priests read scripture. Quillén had maybe forty kilos left of whatever he'd been, and most of it was arranged around his lungs like a fortress around a dying king.

He was drawing when I sat down across from him. Not writing. Drawing. A line of river on a napkin, careful as a surveyor, with little marks along it that meant nothing to me yet.

"You're the diver," he said without looking up. His voice sounded like gravel being poured slowly from one bucket into another.

"I'm the diver."

"The one who stole from the navy." He looked up then, and his eyes were the pale gray-green of shallow water over sand, the eyes of a man who had spent his life looking into distances. "They're saying you took something off the Vencedora. Something sealed."

"Rumors travel fast in a port this small."

"Ports are all small," he said. "The sea is big. Everything else is small." He folded the napkin with exaggerated care, as if it were a document of state, and slid it into the pocket of a canvas jacket that had been mended more times than I'd been paid that season. "Sit properly. You lean like someone who's about to leave. I need you to stay."

I stayed. Partly because my legs agreed to it, partly because there was something in the way he said *need* — not pleading, not demanding. Inventorying. The way I'd inventory a wreck before committing to the dive: what's here, what's worth the risk, what will kill me.

The fan creaked overhead. Somewhere behind the bar a radio played a cumbia so old the singer was probably dead.

"Tomás Quillén," he said, extending a hand that trembled like a compass needle near iron. "You have me at a disadvantage only in name. I know everything else about you. Odile Amberdale. Ten years on the water. No boat of your own until last year, and now the navy wants your license for lifting their box."

"They haven't proven anything."

"They don't need to prove things. They never have." He coughed then, a long, structural event, the kind of cough that seemed to involve reorganizing his entire torso, and when it passed he sat looking older and somehow calmer, like a storm that has finally made landfall. "That's why you hid it instead of handing it over. You've seen what they do with things they don't want found."

I didn't answer. In ten years of salvage I'd learned that silence is a tool, same as a crowbar or a cutting torch. You use it to see what other people fill the space with.

Quillén filled it with truth, apparently. Or something shaped enough like truth to pass in bad light.

"In 1974 I was twenty-two years old," he said. "I worked the upper river. Prospecting, guiding, a little smuggling when the seasons allowed. And I saw a convoy go up the gorge that spring — six trucks, navy plates, tarps over everything, and soldiers who wouldn't meet your eye. Three weeks later the river below the gorge ran strange. Warm. Oily. And then the maps changed."

"Maps change all the time."

"Not like this." He pulled the napkin out again, unfolded it, smoothed it flat between us. The river line ran up through a canyon drawn in confident strokes, and at the top, where every official chart I'd ever seen showed blank plateau, he'd inked a small square and a word: *Represa.* Dam. "They built a dam that isn't on any list. They flooded a valley that isn't on any map. There was a town up there, Amberdale. Two hundred people, a church, a school, orchards. My mother's cousin lived there. One morning in '74 the trucks came and told everyone the water was coming in nine days. Some believed them. Some packed. Some didn't believe anything the navy said, because the navy had already taken three men from that valley that winter and returned none of them."

His hands were flat on the napkin now, pinning the river down like a specimen.

"And the ones who didn't pack?"

He looked at me for a long moment. The fan creaked. The dead singer crooned.

"The church bell still hangs in the tower," he said quietly. "Underwater. I dove it myself, thirty years ago, before my lungs turned against me. I know what's down there. I put certain documents down there myself, in a waterproof case, because I was young and frightened and I thought someday someone would come asking. Nobody asked for fifty years. Then you lifted a strongbox off a freighter the navy sank itself, and suddenly men with contractor credentials are burning through this coast asking questions, and I think — finally — somebody is asking."

Outside, a truck went past on the gravel road, slow, the way trucks move when the men inside them are looking at buildings rather than the road. I watched it through the beer-colored window without turning my head. Old habit. You learn to watch reflections; mirrors don't lie to the people watching them.

"That truck's been past twice since I sat down," I said.

"It's been past four times since noon. They know I'm here. They don't know what I have, or they'd have taken it already instead of letting an old man cough himself to death in public." He leaned forward, and the smell of him was diesel and eucalyptus oil and something underneath that was simply the smell of ending. "Here is my bargain, diver. I have the full map. Not a napkin sketch — the real thing. Survey notes, depths, the route through the gorge, where the dam stands, how to get under it. Fifty years of a dead man's obsession, drawn by hand because I never trusted paper anyone else controlled."

"And in exchange?"

"A promise." He said the word the way other men say *contract*, or *God*. "Not to sell it. Not to give it to journalists who'll sit on it for a decade. Not to trade it to some ministry for your license back. You carry it upstream. You go to the source. You find what I hid in the bell tower. And then you tell the truth about what happened there in 1974 — out loud, publicly, wherever the telling costs you most. That's the price. That's the whole price."

I laughed. I couldn't help it; it came out short and hard, like breath knocked loose. "You want me to promise to ruin my own life."

"I want you to promise to spend it on something." His eyes held mine, and there was no fever in them, no desperation. Just that shallow-water clarity. "You've measured ten years in nautical miles, girl. I can see it. You count them the way misers count coins, thinking the pile will eventually mean something. It won't. Miles don't mean anything. Direction means everything."

The fan creaked. The radio died mid-song, and nobody moved to fix it.

I should tell you what I thought about, in that long silence, because it wasn't the navy or the contractors or even the drowned town. It was my father's boathouse, the one I left at nineteen with a duffel bag and a grudge, and the way he'd stood on the dock not waving, and how I'd told myself for a decade that distance was freedom when any honest person would call it what it was: a wound I kept salting so it would stay interesting. Tomás Quillén looked at me across a scarred table and saw it in about four seconds, the way good divers read a hull.

"Why me?" I asked. "You could give this to Mateo. To anyone."

"Because you already stole the box." He almost smiled; it cost him visibly. "A person who steals the truth is halfway to carrying it. A person who's given it will set it down the first time it gets heavy."

* * *

We shook on it at sundown, which in that country happens fast, the light going from gold to rust to gone while you're still deciding whether to say goodbye. His hand felt like a bundle of dry sticks wrapped in skin. He pressed the map into my hands rolled in oilcloth, heavy as a promise, light as a sin, and he made me say the words out loud on the cantina steps while the first stars came up over a sea I loved more than I have ever loved anything that couldn't drown me.

"I swear to follow the river to its source," I said. "And to speak what happened there."

"Straight," he said. "No lawyer's corners."

"Straight. No corners."

He nodded once, satisfied, and went back inside to die among his napkins, and I walked down to the dock where the *Marta Elena* sat low and patient on her mooring lines, my boat, my livelihood, my ten years made of fiberglass and diesel and stubbornness. Mateo was aboard, coiling lines in the dark the way he did when he was nervous — tighter than necessary, each loop a small argument with the world.

"Well?" he called softly.

"We have a heading," I said, patting the oilcloth roll under my jacket. "Inland."

He stopped coiling. Even in the dark I could see him doing arithmetic — fuel, checkpoints, contractors, the widening gap between a paycheck and a promise. "Odile—"

"Sleep on it. We leave at dawn either way."

I slept in the cabin, the map under the mattress like a child hiding coins, and I woke at 2:14 in the morning to a light that shouldn't have existed — orange, flickering, alive — playing across the cabin ceiling through the porthole.

By the time I got topside, the *Marta Elena* was fully involved. She burned the way ships do, fast and total, her paint going first, then her deck, then everything I owned stacked inside her, and the heat pushed me back against the dock rail while Mateo shouted uselessly for water that would have been an insult anyway. Nobody came to fight the fire. In Puerto Cabo Raso, everybody understood exactly which fires you fought and which ones you watched.

There was no one on the dock. No boat leaving the harbor. But moored two hundred meters out, dark and riding easy, was a gray-hulled launch with no lights and no registration, and as I stood there feeling the fire's heat on my face and the map's weight under my arm, the launch's engine started — unhurried, almost polite — and it turned toward open water.

Mateo came and stood beside me. The flames lit his face from below, making him look like a stranger deciding something.

"They didn't kill us," he said quietly.

"No," I said. "They gave us a deadline."

Behind us, up the hill, a light came on in the cantina, and I thought of an old man alone with his napkins, and I understood that dawn was no longer soon enough.
