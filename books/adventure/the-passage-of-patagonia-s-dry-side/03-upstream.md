## 3. Upstream

I have piloted boats through ice, through fuel spills, through the kind of fog that makes you pray to saints you stopped believing in at fifteen. But I had never piloted anything up a river that was actively trying to become a road.

The skiff was called *La Paciencia* — Patience — which Odile said was a joke and I said was an instruction. She'd bought it off a sheep farmer two days after her dive boat burned, paying in cash with the flat expression of a woman who had already done her grieving in private and didn't intend to share it. Forty years old, aluminum, shallow as a dinner plate. The farmer threw in a tarp and a warning: "The Río Chico drops a hand's width every day this time of year. By the feast of Santa Rosa you'll be carrying it."

"Then we'll be quick," Odile said.

I said nothing. I am the one who does the arithmetic on this boat, or I was, back when we had a boat worth doing arithmetic for. A hand's width a day. I turned that over in my head like a coin I didn't want to look at directly.

We left the coast before dawn, running dark down the estuary with no lights, because Odile had explained to me — patiently, the way you explain things to a child or a lawyer — that the men who burned the *Mariposa* would be watching the water. She was right, of course. She is usually right, which is one of the more exhausting things about her.

The river took us inland through country that looked like God had started making it and then lost interest. Scrub and gravel bars, wind-carved cliffs the color of old bone, guanacos watching us pass with the mild contempt of animals who have never once been impressed by human machinery. The wind came off the steppe in long shoves that made the skiff wander, and I fought it hour after hour, hands on the tiller, eyes reading the water the way I used to read weather off the Atlantic.

Odile sat amidships with the map open across her knees.

I want to say something about that map. It wasn't a chart. Charts are honest documents; they admit what they don't know. This thing was drawn in pencil and ink on paper gone soft as cloth, and it was less a map than a confession — Tomás Quillén's whole life folded into lines and annotations in a hand that shook toward the end. There were depths marked along the river in fathoms, because the man thought like a diver even on dry land. And there were notes in the margins, small ones, that Odile read aloud sometimes when she forgot I was there:

*"Here the water went brown for three days, August '74."*

*"They told us the dam was for electricity. There are no wires."*

*"Forgive me. I was young and they paid in meat."*

That last one she read twice, and then she folded the map away and didn't speak for an hour, and I pretended to be very busy with the tiller.

* * *

We found the fork on the second afternoon.

The river split around a gravel island maybe four hundred meters long, braided channels on either side, both of them shallow enough that I could see the bottom clearly, which meant I could also see how little time we had before there was no channel at all. The map showed the left branch continuing upstream. The right branch, according to Quillén's marginalia, "went nowhere, but nobody has ever checked."

Odile stood up in the boat to look, shading her eyes against the low sun.

"Left," she said.

"Left," I agreed, and then, because I am a coward in small ways if not large ones: "You're sure? The right one's wider."

"The right one's wider because it's dying slower," she said. "Look at the color. That's stillwater feeding it from somewhere downstream. The left one's moving." She sat back down. "Quillén marked a campsite eight kilometers up the left branch. He wouldn't have marked it if it weren't real."

So I took us left, and the channel narrowed until the willows on both banks leaned over us like gossips, and the water got so shallow the propeller began its complaint — that hollow sucking note every shallow-draft man knows, the sound of a boat asking permission.

And that's when I saw the dust.

It came up behind us on the south bank, where a dirt track followed the river through the scrub. A vehicle. White, high-bodied, the kind of thing contractors drive — the same make, if I'm honest, as the trucks that had been crawling the port since the *Vencedora* salvage started, the ones whose drivers never bought anything and never spoke to anyone. It stopped on the track. Someone got out. Even at that distance I could see him raise binoculars.

"Odile."

"I see it."

"They're not following the river. They're following *us*. How?"

She was quiet for a moment. I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd been doing, only faster, only colder.

"The strongbox," she said finally. "The navy liaison logged everything we raised. Everything except what he ordered destroyed. If someone pulled those logs, they'd know what was missing. And Quillén—" She stopped. "Tomás sold maps before. Small ones. Fishing holes. Someone talked to him about the big one, and he must have told them enough."

"He was dying. They could have gotten anything out of a dying man."

"Yes," she said, and something in her voice made me glad I was not the one she was angry at. "But they don't have the map. We do. So they follow the only boat going upstream."

The vehicle didn't move. It just sat there on the track, keeping pace with us at walking speed, patient as a debt. I put the throttle down as far as the shallow water allowed and we ran the left channel until dusk, until the dust was gone and the willows closed in and my shoulders ached from holding the tiller against the current.

We camped on a gravel bar with the skiff hauled well out of the water and the tarp strung between two coirón bushes. Odile took first watch without being asked. I lay on my bedroll listening to the river get quieter — actually quieter, measurably, night by night, like a man losing his voice — and I did not sleep, and I did not tell her why.

Because here is the thing I have not told her yet, the thing sitting in my chest like a swallowed hook: three weeks ago, before any of this, a man bought me a beer in the port cantina. Friendly. Generous, even. He asked questions about Odile's dives — what she raised, what she photographed, who she reported to. And I answered some of them, because he was friendly, because he was generous, because I am a fool who mistakes attention for friendship. Not everything. But some.

And I have not told her. And every kilometer upstream, the telling gets heavier.

* * *

The checkpoint appeared on the third morning where the river passed under a road bridge — new concrete, government-built, the only structure we'd seen in two days that hadn't been built by sheep or wind.

Two men under it. One in a navy surplus jacket, one in civilian clothes with the particular cleanliness of a man who doesn't work outdoors. A pickup parked on the bank. A folding table, because bureaucracy travels with furniture.

Odile looked at me across the skiff, and I knew what she was thinking, because I was thinking it too: we could run it. The channel was fast under the bridge, and the table was set up on the north bank, and if I hugged the south side—

"No," she said, answering my face instead of my words. "If we run, we confirm everything. We're two salvagers going upriver on contract. That's all we've ever been." She dug into her bag and came out with a folder of papers — genuine ones, salvage permits, the harbor master's authorization for a survey job. Half-true documents. In my experience half-true documents are more dangerous than forgeries, because they invite questions. But her face said otherwise, and her face has been winning arguments since before I met her.

I brought us in slow, killed the engine, and let the current carry us the last few meters sideways to the bank, casual as Sunday.

"Afternoon," said the man in the clean clothes. He had a clipboard. Of course he had a clipboard. "Surveying?"

"Fishing," I said.

The lie came out of me smooth and easy, and I hated myself for how smooth and easy it was. Because that's the other thing I haven't told her: lying is a skill I learned young, in a house where the truth got people hit, and I can do it better than she can, and some rotten part of me was almost relieved to be useful in this way.

"Fishing," the man repeated. He looked at the skiff. At the gear — the dive bags, the tanks, the waterproof cases. At Odile, who had arranged herself in the bow looking bored, sun-hat down, a woman with nowhere to be. "Lot of equipment for fishing."

"We're diving for trout," I said, and shrugged, the universal gesture of men explaining their hobbies to strangers. "Deep pools upriver hold the big ones this time of year. My partner photographs them. She sells the pictures." I tipped my head at Odile. "She's the artist. I'm just the driver."

The man wrote something on his clipboard. The one in the navy jacket walked slowly along the bank, looking into our boat without touching anything, which was its own kind of message. His eyes stopped on the waterproof case where the map lived, wedged under the thwart, and stayed there a beat too long.

"Names?" the clean one said.

"Mateo Ferrán. This is—"

"Dolores," Odile said. "Dolores Arce."

I did not flinch. I want credit for that. Somewhere in the port there was a Dolores Arce — a fish buyer, dead these five years, unloved and unmourned — and Odile had chosen the name the way she chose everything, deliberately, with research behind it. I handed over my actual papers because lying about your name while showing false papers is amateur work, and the man compared my face to my photograph with the thoroughness of a man who enjoyed having power more than using it.

"Upriver's closed past the gorge," he said. "Military reserve. Old business."

"Old business," I repeated pleasantly. "How far's the gorge?"

"Farther than you'll get." He smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Water's dropping. You'll ground out long before it matters." Then he tore a carbon copy off his pad — a transit slip, fishing, two persons, valid seven days — and held it out. "Enjoy the trout."

I took it. Our fingers didn't touch. I put the engine down and took us out into the current, and I kept my face pleasant until the bridge was a gray line behind us, and then I let go of the tiller with one hand because it had gone numb from gripping.

"You lied well," Odile said quietly.

"It's the one thing I'm good at that I'm not proud of."

She looked at me for a long moment. The wind pushed a strand of hair across her face and she didn't brush it away. "Mateo. When this is over—"

"When this is over," I said, "I'm going to sleep for a week, and then I'm going to find whoever burned the *Mariposa*, and then I'm going to sleep for another week."

It wasn't the conversation she'd been starting. We both knew it. We let it go, the way you let go of a rope you're both holding when neither of you wants to be the one who pulls.

* * *

That evening we passed the place where the map said the water had gone brown for three days in August 1974, and I swear to you the water was brown there now — not silt-brown, but a flat, mineral brown, like tea left standing, coming out of a side arroyo that wasn't on any chart I've ever seen. Odile made me stop. She filled a bottle of it and held it up to the last light and said nothing at all, and somehow that was worse than anything she could have said.

And it was there, poling us up the shallows in the blue dusk, that I saw the dust again. South bank. Two vehicles this time, running without headlights, keeping pace.

Seven days, the transit slip said. Eleven days, my own arithmetic said, before the gorge closes. And somewhere behind us, men who burn boats were spending diesel to follow two fishermen who were very bad at fishing.

I didn't tell her about the beer. Not that night. I told myself I'd tell her tomorrow, when the light was better, when the river was wider, when there was more water under the hull between us and the men with the clipboard.

The river, meanwhile, kept dropping. A hand's width a day. And I could feel it dropping inside me too — the level of what I could carry, what I could hide, what I could hold above the surface without drowning us both.
