## 4. The Town That Isn't on Charts

The river told us where to turn before any map could have, which was fortunate, because we didn't have one that admitted the place existed. Mateo spotted it first — a smudge of green against the brown hills, willows and poplars standing in a land that had otherwise given up on trees — and he cut the engine and let us drift, the way you do when you're not sure whether what you're seeing is a town or a mirage.

"Río Seco," I said. "Or nothing."

"There's smoke," Mateo said. "Mirages don't cook lunch."

We came in slow, past a rusted pump barge half-sunk at the bank, past a stone jetty that had been built for boats twice the size of ours by people who'd expected the river to stay generous. The water had other plans. It had shrunk back from the jetty like a dog that had been kicked once too often, leaving a beach of cracked gray mud studded with old mooring rings, each one stranded farther from the current than the last, a timeline of retreat measured in iron.

I've worked wrecks my whole adult life. I know what abandonment looks like underwater — the way silt settles in layers, the way things rot in order of their importance to somebody. This bank was the same thing, just done in air. A rowboat upturned and bleached white as bone. A child's bicycle fused to the mud by rust. A signpost with the paint gone but the holes still legible where letters had been screwed in and later unscrewed, which is its own kind of message: *something used to be written here, and someone decided you shouldn't read it.*

"You're doing the thing," Mateo said.

"What thing?"

"The reading-things thing. You look at junk like it's going to testify."

"Junk always testifies," I said. "It's the living who lie."

He didn't laugh. He'd barely laughed since the checkpoint two days upstream, where I'd smiled at an officer and told him we were sport fishermen running downriver ahead of the season's end, and Mateo had stood beside me with his heart hammering so loud I could hear it over the outboard. He'd lied too — added details I hadn't given, embellished the fiction like a good crewman — and then hadn't spoken for six hours, as if the lie had cost him something he needed to count.

We tied off under the willows, well away from the jetty, and walked up into the town.

* * *

Río Seco had maybe forty buildings and perhaps sixty people, though it took me a while to be sure of either number, because the town had the peculiar quality of seeming to hold its breath when we passed. Not hostile. Worse than hostile — attentive. Curtains shifted. A man repairing a net looked at our boots, our packs, our faces, then went back to his net with the exaggerated concentration of someone pretending very hard not to have seen anything. Two boys on a wall watched us with the frank interest of children everywhere, until a woman called them inside in a voice that wasn't angry so much as urgent.

"It's us," Mateo said quietly. "We're the wrong kind of interesting."

"We're the only kind of interesting they get," I said. "That's not the same thing."

There was no hotel. There was, according to a hand-painted board outside a low building near the plaza, a *comedor*, which is a generous word for a room with four tables and a stove. The woman who ran it — broad, gray-haired, forearms like dock rope — took our order without writing it down and without asking our names, and I understood immediately that this was courtesy of a specific local variety: names were currency here, and she wasn't going to spend hers or accept ours where anyone might overhear.

We ate goat stew and bread that tasted of woodsmoke. Halfway through, she refilled our water jug and said, without looking at me, "You came upriver."

"Yes."

"In a skiff."

"Yes."

Her eyes flicked to mine, one quick assessment, the way I size up a hull before diving it. "Then you'll want to tie her high. River's dropping faster than it should this year. By Friday there won't be water at the jetty at all."

"Thank you," I said.

"And you'll want to eat light tomorrow." She gathered our plates. "Roads are bad for digestion this week."

It took me a moment to translate. Roads are bad for digestion. Soldiers on the roads, she meant. Patrols. And she was telling us because telling us cost her nothing visible and might save us everything, and because in towns like this, kindness is conducted entirely through indirection, like smuggling.

* * *

It was the name that broke the silence open. Not mine, not Mateo's.

An old man sat outside the church — not in it, never in it, I'd learn — on a bench worn smooth as driftwood, watching the plaza with eyes gone pale as shallow water. As we passed, Mateo asked him, politely, whether there was fuel to be had anywhere in town, and the old man shook his head, and then I made my mistake, or my opening; with Tomás Quillén's map folded against my ribs, the distinction has never been clear.

"Do you know the Quillén family?" I asked. "Tomás Quillén. Prospector. He'd be seventy-odd now."

The change was instant and total. The old man's face didn't harden — it *emptied*, the way a house empties when the family leaves and takes even the curtains. He looked at me the way you look at a diver who's come up too fast and doesn't yet know she's bleeding inside.

"No Quilléns here," he said.

"The map says—"

"There is no map," he said, gently, terribly, "and there is no town. Ask me about fuel. Ask me about the road south. Ask me anything except that."

I stood there with the sun on my neck and felt the whole plaza listening. Not watching — *listening*. Doors ajar. A radio turned down to a murmur. Sixty people coordinating without a word, the way a school of fish turns.

Mateo touched my elbow. We walked.

"They erased it," he said. "The town. From the charts, from the signs, from—"

"I heard."

"He's frightened."

"Of course he's frightened." I stopped in the shade of the church wall. "Frightened men don't sit on benches for fifty years guarding a name they claim not to know. Frightened men move away. That man is *stationed* there, Mateo. He's a garrison of one."

* * *

She found me at dusk, or I found her — with Doña Amparo, as I'd come to know her, the line between those things was thin.

I'd gone back down to the skiff to check the lines before dark, because the river was indeed falling — you could see it in the wet band on the stones, a hand's width wider each hour, the water giving ground like a losing army. When I climbed back up the bank, an old woman was waiting by the willows, small and straight-backed, wearing black from head to foot in the manner of women who decided decades ago what the rest of their lives would be about.

"You asked after Quillén," she said. No preamble. Her Spanish had the rounded vowels of the interior, of somewhere greener than this.

"I did."

"My husband dove with him." She said it plainly, the way you'd say *my husband drove a bus*. "In '73. Before."

"Before what?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Whatever she saw in my face must have satisfied her, or maybe she'd already decided before she came down to the river and the looking was just habit, the last ritual of a careful life.

"Before the water," she said.

She reached into her coat and brought out an envelope, soft with age, and from the envelope a photograph, and she held it so that the last light caught it but no one else could see it — an old smuggler's trick, keeping the goods angled toward yourself.

It was a photograph of Río Seco.

Not this Río Seco. A Río Seco with a painted church and a schoolhouse flying a flag, with orchards running down to a wide green river, with a jetty crowded with boats, with — I counted twice — three times the houses the town could currently boast. People filled the foreground, dozens of them, posed and laughing in the stiff formal way of people who don't know they're about to become evidence. On the back, in faded pencil: *Río Seco, fiesta de la Virgen, enero 1974.*

"This isn't this town," I said, stupidly.

"No," she agreed. "This town is what's left."

"I don't understand."

"No," she said again, and there was no cruelty in it, only exhaustion. "You dive. My husband dove. Tomás dove. You people think everything can be brought up and looked at in the light." She pressed the photograph into my hands and closed my fingers over it with both of hers, and her grip was stronger than her size explained. "Some things were put under the water so they would stop being looked at. Understand? Not lost. *Put.* There is a difference, and it is the whole difference, and everyone in this town knows it, and no one in this town will say it while the soldiers drive the road every Thursday."

"When do they come?"

"Thursdays. Mornings." She released my hands. "You should not be here Thursday morning."

"Where's your husband now?"

For the first time her face moved — a flinch, quickly mastered, like a boat righting itself. "Under the water," she said. "Where they put him."

She walked away up the bank, straight-backed, black coat drinking the last of the light, and I stood there holding a photograph of a town that officially did not exist, in a country that had drowned it on purpose, with a dead man's map against my ribs getting heavier by the minute.

* * *

Mateo was waiting at the comedor, and I showed him the photograph by lamplight, and he held it for a long time without speaking.

"Three times the houses," he said finally. "Where did they all go?"

"That's the question the map answers."

"Does it?" He set it down carefully, face up, like something that might bruise. "Odile. Does it actually answer it, or does it just point at more water? Because I've been thinking. Everything we've found so far — the box, the map, this photo — none of it is proof of anything. It's directions to proof. And the men burning your boat knew about the box. Which means they know where the directions lead. Which means whatever's at the end of this river, they've had fifty years to make sure it stays down there."

"So we go anyway."

"So we go anyway," he agreed, miserably, "because I apparently have no survival instincts where you're concerned. But Odile — listen to me. If we find what she's describing, if there really is a village under a reservoir behind a dam that isn't on any chart — that's not salvage anymore. That's not a contract, not a job, not miles. That's the kind of thing that gets people put under the water too."

I looked at the photograph. The laughing people. The flag on the schoolhouse. Somewhere in that crowd, possibly, a younger version of the woman in black, and a diver named for a husband whose grave was a lake bed, and a prospector named Quillén who had spent fifty years carrying the coordinates of a crime in his chest along with the disease that was killing him.

"Then we'd better be worth putting under," I said.

Mateo laughed — a short, unhappy bark, but a laugh — and it was the first one since the checkpoint, and I counted it.

Outside, far off down the valley, an engine droned on the southern road. One vehicle. Moving slowly. The kind of patience that doesn't need to hurry because it already knows where everything is.

Thursday was tomorrow.
