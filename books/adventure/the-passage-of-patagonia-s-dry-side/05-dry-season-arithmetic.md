## 5. Dry Season Arithmetic

The river told us the truth every morning, and every morning Odile made me look at it.

"Mark it," she'd say, and I'd wade in to my shins with the surveyor's stake she'd salvaged from the Vencedora's chart table, drive it into the gravel bar, and tie a red rag at the waterline. By evening the rag hung dry, fluttering like a small surrender. By morning there was a new beach where the old one had been, and the rag looked like it had been planted on someone else's land.

Day one of the count: two fingers' drop. Day three: a handspan. Day five: I stopped measuring in hands and started measuring in what the river was giving up — a dead carapace of some armored catfish baking white on the mud, a fence post that must have stood on this bank when the water was young, a bicycle, half-buried, its chrome gone to lace.

"You're doing arithmetic," Odile said, watching me stare at the bike.

"I'm doing grief," I said. "It looks similar from the outside."

She almost smiled. That was day five, and we were speaking again by then — carefully, the way you walk on river ice, testing each word before you put your weight on it. The radio confession was four days behind us and would not be behind us for years; I understood that. She'd forgiven me the way a captain forgives a crewman who's grounded the boat: technically, provisionally, with the damage report still open. I took it. It was more than I deserved and less than I needed, which is the exact recipe for most things worth having.

Here is what the arithmetic said, and she did the numbers out loud so I couldn't pretend I hadn't heard them. The upper gorge narrowed to a slot canyon maybe forty kilometers upstream — Quillén's map showed it as a black thread with a notation in his cramped hand: *paso seco en verano, no hay vuelta* — dry passage in summer, no way back. Below the gorge the river could float our skiff until roughly the end of the month. After that, the water would drop below the gravel shelves and we'd be pushing a thousand kilos of aluminum over rocks until the engine ate itself or we did.

"Eleven days," she said. "Twelve if we're lucky, and luck is not a currency I've ever held an account in."

"So we run the gorge in ten."

"So we run the gorge in nine, and spend the tenth being wrong about something." She folded the map along its worn creases, and I noticed her thumb went to the same spot every time — the little inked cross where Quillén had marked the source, drawn over so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth. "We travel at first light and last light. Midday we hide. And Mateo—" she looked at me then, and it wasn't the cold look from the confession, it was something worse, something that expected things of me, "—if anyone offers you a radio again, you bring it to me before you bring it to your brother."

"Yes," I said. "Yes to both halves of that."

* * *

They found us on day seven, or rather their dust found us, rising off the eastern mesa in a long yellow plume that meant vehicles, plural, moving fast on the old ranch track that paralleled the north bank. I was at the tiller. Odile was asleep under the fuel drums, finally sleeping instead of pretending to, and I had perhaps ninety seconds to decide how much of her rest was worth spending.

I spent all of it. I let her sleep until the plume crested the ridge and resolved into two pickups with canvas-covered beds, and then I put my hand over her mouth and said her name, and she came up out of sleep the way divers come up — controlled, furious, already working.

"Bank," I said. "Two trucks. Coming downriver fast."

She didn't waste breath asking how they'd found us. We both knew the answer lived somewhere in the frequency bands between here and the port, in whatever other ears were listening besides mine and my brother's. She killed the engine and we drifted into a stand of tamarisk that grew right down into the water, branches dragging, and I shipped the tiller and we lay flat among the drums and watched through the green lattice.

The trucks stopped on the track maybe two hundred meters ahead. Men got out. Not soldiers — soldiers wear their boredom on the outside. These men moved like they were being paid by the result, fanning out along the bank with binoculars, one of them walking down to the waterline and standing there looking at the mud the way Odile looked at mud, reading it.

"He's reading our wake," she breathed. "Drift marks. They'll know a skiff came through inside the hour."

"Can we outrun them?"

"In the boat? Maybe. In the narrows ahead, no — the channel bends east and they'll just drive to the bend and wait. This isn't a chase, Mateo. It's a closing calculation. Same as ours, only theirs has more zeros in it."

One of the men raised a hand and pointed — not at us, at the far bank, and the group began walking back to the trucks, and I felt the beautiful stupid hope rise in my chest before Odile crushed it with four words:

"They're going upstream. To cut us off above the bend." She was already moving, sliding the fuel drums forward, pulling the tarp. "We go ashore here. Now. We take what we can carry and we portage."

"Portage? Through that?" I nodded at the country south of the river, which at that moment consisted entirely of thorn — jarilla and molle and the low, vicious algarrobo scrub that grabs cloth and skin with equal indifference. Beyond it, twenty kilometers of broken ground rising toward the gorge.

"Through that," she said. "Or we stay in the skiff and let them do our arithmetic for us. Their answer ends in a hole in the ground and no report filed. Mine ends in blisters." She looked at me, and for once there was no command in it, only the question underneath every command she'd ever given me. "Your legs. Your choice."

That was the thing about Odile I was only beginning to understand, ten days into being a fugitive: she gave you the choice precisely when there wasn't one, so that afterward you couldn't say she'd taken anything from you. You'd have to admit you'd walked into it yourself.

I chose the thorns.

* * *

We stripped the skiff to what mattered and buried the rest — the spare fuel, the dive gear, the tools, wrapped in the tarp under a cairn of stones that looked like every other cairn in that country, because that country builds cairns the way other places build fences. Odile kept the map against her body, in the oilskin pouch, and made me carry the evidence canister from the strongbox on the principle that a man carrying his own redemption walks faster. She was right. I hated her a little for it. I walked fast.

The first kilometer taught us the country's grammar. Thorn here doesn't mean brambles; it means architecture — plants engineered over millennia to say *no* in every direction. We moved in the washes where the sand was soft, and the sand recorded us like a ledger, and there was nothing to be done about it except keep to rock when rock offered itself and accept that any tracker with eyes would find our line by morning.

"We're leaving a signature," I said.

"We're leaving a story," Odile corrected. "A skiff abandoned mid-river, two sets of tracks going south. Let them read that we panicked. Panicked people run for roads. We'll give them a false heading tonight, then bend west after dark."

"And if they don't buy it?"

"Then tomorrow we learn something new about ourselves." She said it lightly, but I saw her check the sun's angle, doing the arithmetic again — light remaining, distance to the gorge rim, water in the bottles versus kilometers in the legs. Eleven days had become nine. Nine days had become a number with teeth.

We walked through the heat of the day anyway, hiding rules broken, because the trucks on the track were worse than the sun. At midafternoon we climbed a granite spine and looked back, and there they were: three vehicles now, strung along the riverbank below, and men around the abandoned skiff, and one man standing very still at the edge of our tracks, head down, reading.

"He's good," Odile said quietly. There was something in her voice I hadn't heard before — professional respect, the kind you extend to an opponent across a card table. "He'll have our heading inside the hour. He'll also have our weight class, our stride length, and whether we're injured." She paused. "He'll know we're not panicking."

"Is that bad?"

"It's worse. Panicked people make mistakes. Disciplined people make plans." She turned away from the view and started down the spine. "So we stop being disciplined. Tonight we split the trail — you walk a line toward the road, obvious, ugly, boot-prints deep enough to read from a truck. Then you circle back on rock and find me at the dry arroyo, the fork with the dead algarrobo. I'll mark it."

"That's the plan? One of us plays decoy in thorn country at night, alone, near the men with guns?"

"That's the plan." She looked back at me, and the last light caught her face, and I saw she was afraid — really afraid, first time since the burning boat — and offering herself for the decoy run without saying so, because that was the arithmetic she trusted herself with and not me.

"No," I said.

"Mateo—"

"My brother sold me a frequency and I nearly sold you. I'm done paying interest on that. I walk the decoy line. You take the map and the canister and you get to the gorge." I shouldered the pack before she could argue with my hands too. "You always say the river tells the truth every morning. Fine. Tomorrow morning, one of us is going to be standing at that dead tree, and the truth is going to be that I kept my word. Let me have that. I've been short on it."

For a long moment she said nothing, and the wind moved the thorn scrub below us with a sound like slow applause, like the country itself taking sides.

Then she reached into the oilskin pouch, tore a strip from the map's blank margin, wrote something on it in pencil, and pressed it into my palm.

"If you're not at the tree by first light," she said, "I go on without you, and you spend the rest of your life knowing I did exactly what you asked."

"What is it?" I asked, but she was already descending toward the shadowed arroyo, and the paper in my hand was warm from hers, and I didn't open it until full dark, alone at the edge of the thorn field with the truck lights crawling along the river like patient beetles.

It was a single line, in her small diver's hand, the handwriting of a woman used to writing on slate underwater:

*Come back. The arithmetic is better with two.*

I folded it away next to my heart, where I keep everything I can't afford to lose, and I turned south toward the road, and began to lie with my feet.
