## 6. What Mateo Knew

I have lied to my mother, to two girlfriends, to a customs officer in Bahía Azul who asked if I was carrying anything that needed declaring, and once — memorably, at my cousin's wedding — to an entire table of relatives about why I'd stopped playing football. None of those lies cost me anything. They were small coins, spent without thought.

The lie I told Odile Amberdale was not small. It had been compounding interest for weeks.

She caught me on the third morning after Río Seco, just past dawn, while the river ran gray and slow beside our camp and the thorn scrub steamed off the night's cold. I'd waited until she went down to the water with her hair loose and her face still soft with sleep, and I'd taken the little handheld from the false bottom of the tool locker where I kept it wrapped in an oil rag like something shameful, which by then it was. My brother's frequency. The old handshake code we'd used since we were boys signaling each other across our grandmother's patio.

*Estoy bien. Sigo con ella. No sé cuántos días más.*

*I'm fine. Still with her. I don't know how many more days.*

I'd gotten as far as *días* when her shadow fell across me.

There is a particular quality to Odile's silence when she is angry, and I want to be honest here, because honesty is apparently the theme of this chapter of my life: it is worse than shouting. Shouting gives you somewhere to stand. Her silence is deep water — you can't see the bottom, and you know things live down there.

"Who are you talking to, Mateo?"

"My brother."

"I know it's your brother." She held out her hand, palm up, and I gave her the radio because what else was there? "What I'm asking is what you're telling him. And who else is listening."

I looked at the river. The river offered no assistance whatsoever.

* * *

Here is what happened, and I will tell it the way I finally told it, sitting on a cold rock with my hands hanging between my knees like a man at his own sentencing:

Six weeks ago, before the Vencedora job even started, a man came into the cantina in Puerto Hambre where I drink when Odile's paying and sometimes when she isn't. He was navy-adjacent — you learn the type, the haircut that's too short for civilian life and too clean for actual sailors, the boots that have never seen mud. He bought me a beer. Then another. He knew my name, which should have been the first alarm, and he knew Odile's salvage record, which was the second, and he asked easy questions in an easy voice. What kind of sonar did she run. How long did she stay down. Did she ever bring up anything she didn't log.

And I said nothing. Not because I was loyal — God, I wish I could claim that — but because I was confused, and confusion kept my mouth shut.

He left me a card with a phone number and no name, and he said, "You seem like a man who looks out for his sister," and drove away in a truck with government plates.

For three weeks I did nothing with that card. I want that on the record. Three weeks it sat in my wallet behind a photo of my mother, and every time I paid for fuel or food I felt it there like a splinter. I told myself I was protecting Odile by not reporting. That was true. It was also true that I liked being the man someone in a clean uniform wanted information from. I am not a proud man but I am an honest one, eventually, and eventually means now: it felt good to be asked.

Then the contractors burned our boat, and Tomás Quillén died coughing his lungs into a handkerchief in a room that smelled of kerosene, and we were running upstream with a map that men with guns wanted destroyed, and fear stopped being an abstraction. Fear became a passenger in the skiff. It sat between us. It ate our food.

And one night at anchor, with Odile asleep and the stars doing their indifferent wheeling overhead, I took out the radio and called my brother, and my brother — who is not a bad man either, only a frightened one with a family — put me on with the man from the cantina, and I answered questions. Two times. Maybe three. Small things, I told myself. Where we'd anchored. Whether Odile seemed to know where she was going. Whether she'd opened the strongbox.

Whether she'd opened the strongbox.

That last one I answered the night before Quillén died, and forty-eight hours later the contractors found us at the checkpoint below Río Seco, and I have done the arithmetic of that a hundred times since, lying awake in whatever ditch or ruin we slept in, and the arithmetic always comes out the same. They knew where we were because I told them. Whatever else happens on this river, that is mine. I built that.

* * *

"So you're the reason they were waiting at the ford." Odile's voice was very quiet. She hadn't sat down. She stood over me with the radio in her fist and the dawn coming up behind her, turning her into something backlit and unreadable.

"The first time," I said. "Not the gorge. Not the ambush. I stopped calling after the checkpoint, I swear it—"

"You swear it." She turned the radio over in her hands, looking at it as if it might confess too. "Mateo, the guide from Río Seco is dead. Anselmo. He had grandchildren. He died covering our escape because men with rifles knew exactly where we'd be, and you're standing here telling me about the times you *didn't* betray us."

"I know."

"Do you?" For the first time her voice cracked, and somehow that was worse than the silence. "Because I've been out here doing arithmetic of my own, Mateo. Every close call. Every patrol that found us too fast. I keep finding your fingerprints on all of them, and I keep explaining them away, because you're the one person on this earth I trusted without testing first."

That landed where she meant it to land. I am a large man; I have taken punches from dockworkers and once from a bull seal, and none of it compared to a tired woman in a salt-stiffened shirt telling me she had trusted me without testing.

"Why?" she asked. "Money?"

"No." And this, at least, I could say cleanly. "They never offered money. Just — safety. My brother's family. A way for everyone I love to come out of this whole. They didn't buy me, Odile. They scared me. There's a difference, and the difference doesn't matter, but it exists."

She was quiet a long moment. The river made its patient sound against the stones.

"What did you tell them about the strongbox?"

"That you had it. That you hadn't opened it yet."

"And they burned my boat anyway."

"They burned your boat before that," I said, and then hated myself, because it sounded like a defense, and it wasn't, and we both knew it wasn't.

She crouched down then, level with me, and looked at me the way she looks at a wreck from the surface — assessing, deciding whether to dive. "Here's what happens now," she said. "You keep the radio. You'll call your brother tonight, on schedule, so nobody gets suspicious. You'll tell him we're heading for the upper gorge, which is true, and that we're low on food, which is also true. Everything you say from now on, I'll already know you're saying. Do you understand? You're not my betrayer anymore, Mateo. You're my instrument. If they move on what you report, I'll know it came through you, and I'll know how much they know, and I'll use that. That's the only version of you I can travel with."

It was a good plan. It was a cruel plan. It was exactly the kind of thing Odile Amberdale would think of, because she has spent ten years salvaging wrecks and she knows that the most valuable thing on any sunken ship is the thing that's been turned inside out.

"Yes," I said.

"One more question." She stood, slinging the radio's lanyard over her shoulder before remembering it was mine and dropping it back into my lap like something dead. "Anselmo's death. The ambush at the gorge. If I find out you had any hand in that — any at all — I won't argue with you. I won't even raise my voice. I'll walk into the desert and leave you standing there, and I'll finish this alone, and you can explain to my ghost why you did it."

"I didn't," I said. "Odile. On my mother. I didn't."

She nodded slowly, and I couldn't read her face at all, and then she picked up her pack and walked down to the water, and we broke camp and traveled the rest of that day without speaking.

* * *

A day of silence on a river is a long distance. We portaged around a shallow braided section, hauling the skiff over gravel bars hot enough to burn through boot leather, and never once did she ask me to take the other end of a rope, and never once did I fail to take it anyway. We ate lunch twenty meters apart, facing opposite directions, like a couple in the last week of a marriage. Once she laughed at something — a heron, absurdly, stabbing at a fish and missing three times — and caught herself, and the laugh died in her throat, and I pretended not to have heard it, and that pretense hurt worse than the silence.

I thought about my brother. I thought about how he'd sounded on the radio, that first time — relieved, greedy for news, hungry for reassurance that I was safe — and how I'd mistaken his relief for permission. Nobody had forced me. That was the sentence I kept arriving at, over and over, like a boat circling its mooring. Nobody had forced me. A man in clean boots had asked, and I had been flattered, and then I had been afraid, and flattery plus fear turns out to be the entire recipe. No money required. Just a man willing to be both.

Toward evening the canyon walls closed in and the light went copper, and Odile finally spoke, without turning around, her hands on the tiller.

"There's a bend ahead where the map shows the river doubling back on itself. Quillén marked it with a skull."

"A warning?"

"Or a joke. With Tomás it was usually both." She was quiet a moment. "We camp above it tonight. Tomorrow we run it early, before the wind comes up."

"Odile." My voice came out rougher than I intended. "I would die before I let them touch you. I need you to know that, even if you can't believe it yet."

She was silent long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Then, still facing forward:

"I don't need you to die for me, Mateo. I need you to be worth the truth I'm carrying. Those aren't the same thing, and I've buried enough men who confused them."

We rounded the bend at dusk, and she was right about the map and wrong about the joke. The river doubled back through a slot so narrow the walls nearly touched, and on the bluff above it, visible for miles in the last red light, someone had built a cairn of stones and bleached skulls — goat, probably, maybe guanaco — and wired to the topmost stone, fluttering like a flag, was a strip of canvas torn from a navy tarp.

Someone had marked this place for their own people. Someone was ahead of us, and moving slower than fear, and leaving signs.

Odile cut the engine and let us drift, and in the sudden quiet I heard it — faint, downstream, around the next bend: the tick of metal on stone. Someone digging.
