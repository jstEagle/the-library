## 8. The Drowned Village

**Odile**

The river ended the way rivers do in canyon country — not with a fanfare but with a shrug. One moment we were wading through water to our knees, the next the current went slack and wide and glassy, spreading into a basin that shouldn't have existed on any chart I'd ever memorized. Which was the point. Nothing here existed on any chart.

Mateo stopped so abruptly I nearly walked into his back.

"Odile," he said, which was all he said, because there wasn't anything else to say.

The reservoir lay cupped between two walls of red rock like water held in a scarred palm. It was still — genuinely still, the kind of stillness you only get when a body of water has nowhere it's allowed to go. And it was clear. Not coastal clear, not the green murk I'd spent ten years working in, but a high-altitude clarity that went down and down and kept going, until my eyes gave out before the light did.

And at the bottom of it, visible as a photograph under glass, was a village.

I'd seen drowned towns before. Salvage work takes you over them now and then — valleys flooded for hydro projects, churches turned into navigation hazards, farmers' fences standing on the lakebed like the ribs of something swallowed whole. You get used to it the way you get used to anything: by not looking too long.

This one I looked at. I couldn't help it. The water was so transparent that I could see the grid of streets, the orchard trees still standing in rows, their branches white and skeletal. A plaza. And rising out of the plaza, its tower breaking the surface like a periscope, a church.

The bell tower stood above the waterline by perhaps four meters, stone gone the color of old bone, streaked with forty years of mineral stain. A bell hung in the arched opening at the top, tilted, silent. Below the waterline the tower continued, straight down, into that impossible blue.

"Quillén's map ends here," Mateo said quietly. He had it out of the oilcloth, though he didn't need to consult it. The X wasn't even an X anymore; the old man had drawn the tower itself, and beneath it, in a hand gone shaky near the end: *Lo que hundieron sigue sonando.* What they sank is still ringing.

I'm not a sentimental diver. The sea taught me early that sentiment gets people killed — you grieve after the dive, never during. But standing on that shore, looking down at streets where children had walked to school and a bell that had called people to mass on Sunday mornings, I felt something shift in my chest that I hadn't felt since I was nineteen years old and watching my father's boat go down with everything we owned aboard.

Awe, yes. But underneath the awe, horror, cold and patient as the water itself.

Because this wasn't a valley flooded for electricity. This was a village drowned on purpose, behind a dam that appeared on no government list, fed by a river that had been erased from every chart, holding under its surface the proof of whatever Tomás Quillén had sworn me to carry upstream and speak aloud.

"They flooded it with people in it," Mateo said. He wasn't asking. He'd grown up on stories like this; he knew the shape of them.

"Some got out," I said. "Not all."

We made camp in the lee of a rock shelf with sightlines up and down the basin, and I spent the afternoon doing what I do best, which is arithmetic. The tower's submerged section ran maybe eighteen meters from surface to plaza level. At this altitude, in water this cold, free-diving eighteen meters and working at depth — recovering whatever Quillén had hidden — was possible. Possible the way defusing a bomb is possible. Doable, if your hands stay calm and your lungs forgive you.

I've been holding my breath professionally since I was twelve. My father called it my one useful talent. He was mostly joking. Mostly.

* * *

**Mateo**

She told me to sleep before the dive, and I told her I would, and we both knew I was lying. I sat against the rock with the rifle across my knees — the one we'd taken off the contractor patrol back in the thorn country, the one Odile handled like it embarrassed her — and watched the moon come up over the dam wall downstream.

You could see the dam from camp if you climbed twenty feet up the talus slope. A long concrete lip, unmarked, unlit, holding back a lake that held back a town. Somewhere below it, past the gorge where Ríos Secos' guide had died covering our escape, the contractors were regrouping. We'd bought ourselves a day, maybe two. Odile said three. Odile always says three.

I kept thinking about my brother. About the radio calls I'd made and the one call I'd refused to make, and how the difference between those two things had cost us a day of silence on the trail and might yet cost us more. Odile had forgiven me — or said she had, which in her language might be the same thing or might be a debt filed for later collection. Either way I owed her. I'd been owing her since the day she pulled me off a dock in Puerto Deseado and put a wage in my pocket and never once asked about the gaps in my story.

So I watched the water instead of sleeping, and I thought about Tomás Quillén coughing blood into a handkerchief in a cantina that smelled of diesel and fried onions, trading a dying man's last possession for a promise from a stranger. *She dives,* he'd said of Odile, according to the cantina owner. *A woman who goes down. Good. What I hid is down.*

What I hid is down. Forty years he'd carried that sentence. Now it was ours.

Near dawn I heard her moving. She dressed in silence — wetsuit, weight belt, fins, the small mesh bag she'd carried since the Vencedora. No tank. There had never been a tank; tanks were for divers who planned to come up slowly, and the plan today didn't allow slow.

"You don't have to do this alone," I said.

"I know." She checked the strap on her mask. "That's why you're coming to the tower base. Shallow part. You can hold the line."

It was a rope, actually — fifty meters of it, anchored to a boulder on shore. Her lifeline, her measuring tape, and if it came to it, the thing I'd haul on while screaming her name. I knew my job. I hated it. Holding a rope while someone you love walks into the dark is the worst job in the world, and I've done a few.

The water took her without a sound. That's the thing nobody tells you about a good diver: they enter like they were never there at all.

* * *

**Odile**

Cold. That's the first truth of high-country water: it doesn't ease you in, it takes you. The chill closed around my face like a hand and I let it, breathing slow, letting the shock burn off, swimming easy across the hundred meters of open basin toward the tower. Below me the village rose to meet the light — rooftops first, then walls, then the plaza opening up like a hand offering whatever it had left.

At the tower I ducked under and found Mateo already there, treading water beside the masonry, the rope coiled over his shoulder. He'd tied it off to an iron ring some mason had set into the stone a century before anyone dreamed of drowning this place. Good mason. His ring just saved a life.

I gripped the rope, looked down the shaft of the tower, and saw it: the belfry's lower chamber, maybe fifteen meters down, lit in shafts of silver from the arches above. And on the floor of it, exactly where a young Tomás Quillén would have wanted it — deep enough to be safe, shallow enough to be findable by someone who could dive — a shape wrapped in oilcloth and tarred canvas, lashed to the bell's support beam.

He'd weighted it with a paving stone. Practical man. Sentimental placement — directly beneath the bell, so that what they sank would sit under what still rang. I'd laughed at that, if laughing underwater were a thing, which it isn't, though I came close.

Fifteen meters. Cold water, no tank, work at depth. I'd done worse in warmer seas with better equipment and less reason. I spat into my mask, rinsed it, settled it, and pulled the rope twice — our signal for *going down*.

Then I breathed until my lungs felt full past comfort, tipped headfirst, and kicked.

The descent was clean. Equalize, equalize, equalize — the pressure squeezing at my ears like a debt collector. At ten meters the color drained out of the world and the tower became a cathedral of gray-blue stone, the arches overhead burning white. At fourteen my feet touched the belfry floor and I knelt in the silt of a dead town and got to work.

My hands were steady. Ten years of salvage will teach your hands to be steady even when the rest of you is screaming. I cut the lashing — Quillén had used wire, twisted tight, and my knife sang through it — and worked the bundle free of the beam. Heavy. Oilcloth over canvas over what felt like metal boxes, sealed with tar. I stuffed the whole thing into my mesh bag, clipped it shut, and looked up.

That's when I saw the rest.

The bell hung above me, yes. But the belfry floor was scattered with things that weren't ballast: a child's shoe. A rosary fused into a single black knot. A tin cup, upright, as if set down by someone meaning to come back. And wedged in the corner, half-buried in silt, a second bundle — smaller, wrapped the same way, with the same wire.

Quillén had hidden more than one thing. Or someone else had hidden something after him. My chest was starting to burn, my vision narrowing to a bright tunnel, and I made the calculation every freediver makes: the bundle, the ascent, the air I had left. The math said no. The math said leave it.

But I thought of the shoe. Of the foot that had worn it. Of a government erasing a town so thoroughly that even its river vanished from the charts, and of how many bundles like this might be lying in the dark under all the quiet places of this country.

I grabbed the second bundle. Clipped it outside the bag. Kicked for the light.

The ascent was the longest minute of my diving life. Fifteen meters on empty lungs with double cargo, the tunnel of my vision going soft at the edges, my hands remembering for me what to do — kick, streamline, kick — until the surface broke over my face and the sky slammed in, huge and blue and impossibly generous, and I heard myself gasping like a landed fish while Mateo hauled me against the tower and held on.

"Two," I choked out, when I could talk. "There were two."

He looked at the bags. Then at me. Then downstream, toward the dam, where the morning sun had just picked out something new on the concrete lip: the glint of windshields. Three vehicles, parked in a row, patient as vultures.

"Odile," he said. "They're here."
