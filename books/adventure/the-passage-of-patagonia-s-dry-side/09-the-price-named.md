## 9. The Price Named

The dam looked, from a distance, like something the government had tried to unmake and failed. A gray spine of concrete laid across the gorge, weeping rust down its face in long orange tears, and behind it the reservoir — that impossible blue in a country the color of old rope. We came down off the ridge at first light, Mateo and I and the evidence sealed in oilcloth against my back, and I remember thinking: *this is where the river begins, and this is where my luck ends.* Both statements turned out to be true, though not in the order I'd have guessed.

There was a helicopter on the spillway apron.

Not running. Just sitting there with its blades tied down, patient as a spider, and a man standing beside it with his hands in his pockets, watching us come down the scree slope like he'd ordered us off a catalog.

"That's him," Mateo said. "That's the one from the port. Commander Ferrer."

"I know."

I'd seen him twice through the porthole of the *Vencedora*'s liaison office, once across a cantina, and once — the detail that had kept me awake — standing at the rail of the boat that burned. He had the kind of face you forget on purpose. Middle-aged, sun-faded, ordinary. The face of a man who does terrible arithmetic for a living and has never once lost sleep over the sum.

We could have run. The ridge went on. The canyon went on. But the dry season had closed the gorge behind us two days ago, and the evidence against my back weighed what truth always weighs — more than you can carry uphill — and I was tired in a way that had nothing to do with miles.

So we walked down to meet him.

* * *

He let us get within ten meters before he spoke.

"Señora Amberdale." He pronounced it correctly, which told me he'd done his homework, which told me how long they'd been watching. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guys," I said. "Oh, wait. There weren't any."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. The place where a smile would go in a man who'd had the equipment removed. "You're a difficult woman to buy a drink for."

"I'm a difficult woman to buy, full stop. Ask anyone. Ask Tomás Quillén — oh, you can't. Your people saw to that."

"Quillén was a sick old man full of stories," Ferrer said. "The stories were worth money once. They aren't anymore."

"Everything's worth money to somebody. That's practically the national motto."

Behind him, two contractors had drifted out from behind the helicopter's tail boom, rifles slung with the studied casualness of men who wanted you to look at the slings and not the muzzles. Mateo had gone very still beside me, the stillness of an animal doing the math on the fence line. I knew that math. I'd done it my whole life, in one form or another: *how much is the exit going to cost, and can I pay it.*

Ferrer took his hands out of his pockets.

"Let me tell you what you have," he said. "You have an unlicensed dive on a restricted site. You have stolen documents of no legal standing. You have a deckhand with a suspended sentence in Bahía Azul and a brother who's already told us everything. And you have walked eleven days into a canyon that is about to become a furnace, with no radio, no vehicle, and no way down except the one I'm standing on." He spread his hands. "I'm not your enemy, señora. I'm the arithmetic."

"And what does the arithmetic say?"

"It says you can be a wealthy woman by Thursday." He said it mildly, the way you'd discuss freight rates. "The documents. All of them. In exchange: two hundred thousand dollars, deposited anywhere on earth. A signed statement that you found nothing at the wreck but rust and rats. And a flight out — my helicopter, my pilot, to the coast, where your salvage license will be quietly restored and the file on the *Vencedora* will close itself. You go back to the sea. You go back to wrecks. You go back to being exactly who you were before an old man coughed a map into your hands."

* * *

Here is the thing I have never told anyone, not even Mateo, not even the sea.

For about four seconds, I wanted it.

Not the money — money I've had and lost and never mourned. But the *sea*. The word did something to me that no threat ever could. I stood on that concrete in the wind off the reservoir and I saw it entire: the *Vencedora*'s hull in the green morning light, the sound of the hook line paying out, the particular loneliness of a dive at forty meters when the only voice in the world is your own breathing. Ten years of miles. Ten years of measuring my life in nautical miles because it was easier than measuring it in years, in losses, in the name of a town I'd left and never once gone back to.

Two hundred thousand dollars and a ride home.

Four seconds. I'm not proud of them. I'm not ashamed of them either. A lie of omission, and I've made my living off the truth.

"Say the number again," I said, and Mateo's head turned toward me, and I felt it turn like a knife going in, and I hated myself with a thoroughness that was almost refreshing.

Ferrer heard the waver. Of course he did. It's what he was built for. "Two hundred thousand," he repeated, gentler now, a man reeling in something small and tired. "And no more dead guides. No more burned boats. No more of this—" he gestured at the canyon, the reservoir, the whole drowned country behind me, "—carrying other people's ghosts uphill. Quillén's dead, señora. The town's dead. The past is dead. All you'd be doing is agreeing to let it stay buried, which is what everyone else agreed to in 1974, and they all slept fine."

"Did they?" I said. "Because I've been to Río Seco. I've seen how fine they sleep."

"Río Seco is four hundred people and a chapel," Ferrer said. "You are one diver with a bad knee. The arithmetic—"

"Is mine," said Mateo.

* * *

I want to get this right, because I got everything else that morning wrong, and a person should be accurate about the moments that save her.

Mateo stepped forward. Not dramatically — Mateo doesn't do dramatically, he does everything like he's handling cargo, deliberate and a little apologetic. He stepped forward and put himself between Ferrer and me, between me and the rifles, a skinny deckhand with salt-cracked hands and a brother who'd sold us out, and he said, in the flat voice he uses for weather reports:

"The arithmetic is hers. But you should know what it says. You offered her money to bury the truth. She's the only person I've ever worked for who didn't take the money. I radioed you from the river. I lied to a checkpoint. I carried her pack through thorn country. And I watched a man from Río Seco die in the gorge so she could get here — and he got here *first*, señor, because he believed it was worth it. So." He shrugged, that shrug of his that means *the load is what it is.* "You can shoot us. But you can't buy us, because we already priced it, and the price was everything, and we paid."

The wind came down the reservoir and pushed at us, smelling of deep water and drowned stone.

Ferrer looked at Mateo for a long moment. Then at me. And I saw the exact instant the arithmetic failed him — not because we were brave, but because we were *unpriceable*, and a man who runs on numbers has no procedure for that.

"You understand," he said quietly, "what happens now."

"I understand you're out of offers," I said. "That's the first honest thing you've said all morning."

He turned to his men and said something I didn't catch, and the rifles came off their slings, and that's when the sky over the dam did the thing the dry season had been promising us for eleven days.

It went black.

* * *

The first rain in the high country doesn't fall so much as arrive — a gray wall coming up the gorge like a tide, and the wind ahead of it hit the spillway apron hard enough to stagger the nearest contractor. Ferrer shouted an order. The helicopter crew scrambled for the tie-downs. And in that half-minute of chaos — men lunging, blades rattling in their lashings, the world suddenly roaring — Mateo's hand closed on my arm and hauled me toward the spillway stairs, the concrete channel that ran down the dam's dry face like a throat.

"The documents—" he yelled.

"Are going with me!"

We ran. Behind us I heard Ferrer's voice, furious, cutting through the rain: not an order to shoot — the wind and the water had taken even that from him — just my name, *Amberdale*, spat like a debt he intended to collect.

We went down the spillway stairs into the dark, the first rain of the season hammering the concrete, the reservoir behind us rising against its unlisted dam, and somewhere below, the river that wasn't on any chart waiting to carry us — and the truth — down to the coast, and to whatever came after.

The price, I understood, was only beginning to come due.

But for the first time in ten years and ten thousand miles, I wasn't running from it.

I was running *with* something.
