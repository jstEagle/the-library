## 10. Where the River Begins

The spillway was a concrete throat, dry for forty years and slick with the first spit of the season's rain, and we went down it the way I imagine a coin goes down a well — fast, clanging, committed.

Mateo went first. He'd earned that, or maybe he'd simply stopped asking permission somewhere between the dam and the commander's outstretched hand. I followed with the evidence bag strapped to my chest like a second heart, which is what it was by then: Quillén's photographs, the navy's own manifests, the survey that proved the village of Río Seco had not drowned by act of God but by act of signature. Forty-one families. A church bell still hanging in its tower under eleven meters of cold water, visible from the surface on clear days like a thing waiting to be rung.

The rain found us before the bottom did. Fat, warm, improbable drops after months of dust, hitting the concrete around us in dark constellations. Mateo whooped once — an actual whoop, this man who hadn't laughed since the gorge — and I nearly shot him on reflex. Nearly. The rifle was gone anyway; I'd left it wedged in the dam's control room, because you can't carry everything down a spillway and some things you have to put down.

We came off the end of the chute into a boulder field and rolled, and got up, and kept moving, because behind us the contractors were regrouping and ahead of us was only weather and distance and the long gray light of a storm coming over the plateau.

"Odile." Mateo caught my arm. "The relay station. It's — how far?"

"Two days' walk if the river stays low. Half a day if it doesn't."

"And if it doesn't?"

I looked upstream, where the water was already beginning to talk to itself in a new voice, deeper, plural. "Then we swim."

He didn't say what we were both thinking, which was that swimming was my language and not his, and that I'd spent ten years measuring my life in nautical miles precisely so I'd never again be the one standing on a bank watching someone else go into the water without me.

* * *

The relay station sat on a shoulder of the mountain like a rusted crown — a navy installation decommissioned in 1981 and never quite told so, all guy-wires and corrugated steel and one dish aimed at nothing in particular. Inside, someone had left a chair, a desk bolted to the floor, and a transmitter that smelled of ozone and patience.

I'd learned radios the way salvage divers learn everything: badly, urgently, and just enough. But Quillén had made me rehearse this. In his last weeks, propped up in the cantina's back room with his lungs rattling like a bilge pump, he'd made me recite frequencies until I hated him. *You will have one chance, niña,* he'd said. *Men like me get one chance and men like them get many. Spend yours loudly.*

So while Mateo watched the road below through a crack in the shutter, I keyed the transmitter and sent it all — the scans, the coordinates, the names. All forty-one family names, read aloud into the static, because documents can be denied but a woman's voice saying *Familia Cárdenas, Familia Quillén, Familia Antilef* is harder to unhear. Three transmissions, then a fourth, then a fifth, until my voice went raw and the rain hammered the roof like applause or like fists.

Mateo appeared at my shoulder. "There's a truck on the road."

"How far?"

"Close. Coming slow."

I sent the sixth transmission anyway. Then the seventh. When the headlights swept the window I pulled the plug myself, which felt like surfacing too fast — that bright, sickening emptiness where the pressure used to be.

* * *

They caught us at dawn on the high pasture below the station, two contractors and their commander, who had driven all night and looked it. He didn't point a weapon at us. He didn't need to; there were four more men fanning out through the grass, and we were two people standing in the open with nowhere left above us to run.

"You've made yourself expensive," he said. Not angry. Almost admiring, the way you admire weather. "Do you know what happens now? Your license is revoked this morning. Every port registry on the coast. No tender will insure you, no captain will sign you, no dive shop will fill your tanks. You're finished on the water, Amberdale. Permanently."

I waited for the grief to arrive. I'd been braced for it since the gorge, since Río Seco, since the strongbox came up dripping out of the Vencedora's hold with the navy's seal unbroken. Ten years of miles, all of them bought with a license and a pair of hands good underwater, and here was the bill.

But what arrived instead was quieter than grief. It was arithmetic. I thought of the church bell under the reservoir, and of Tomás Quillén dying in a room that smelled of kerosene, trading the last thing he owned for a promise from a stranger. I thought about how every chart I'd ever trusted had been drawn by someone who decided what to leave off.

"I know," I said.

He blinked. Whatever script he'd prepared had a different line there — bargaining, fear, something he could work with.

"The sea was never mine," I said. "It was rented. This—" I touched the bag against my chest. "This is owned. Outright. Paid for by forty-one families and one old man's lungs."

He studied me for a long moment, rain starting again on his shoulders, and then he did the most dangerous thing a man like him can do. He believed me. He turned and walked back toward the truck, and the men in the grass folded away after him like a tide going out, and Mateo exhaled beside me in a long shudder.

"They'll come back," he said.

"Of course they'll come back. But they're late now. Everyone with a radio heard it first."

* * *

We walked down out of the mountains over three days, following the river as it fattened with rain, and the country changed around us the way it does after the dry season breaks — green climbing the canyon walls in days, water running loud in channels that had been silent since we arrived. Twice we saw helicopters. Neither came close. The story was loose in the world by then; we heard it in a roadside hamlet on a battery radio, a newsreader's voice careful and astonished, and an old man by the stove said *Río Seco* out loud like a word in a language he'd been punished for speaking.

Río Seco itself — the living one, the rebuilt one downriver, the town that wasn't on charts because its people had learned the price of being written down — met us at the edge of the fields. Not with soldiers. With Señora Antilef, the woman who had slipped me the photograph in chapter four of this disaster, standing in the mud in her good coat as though she'd known the day we'd come home.

Behind her, half the town. And behind them, the bell.

They'd brought it up themselves, the week the story broke — divers from the coast, volunteers, sons and grandsons of the drowned families, hauling it out of the tower with block and tackle while the whole valley watched. It stood on a timber cradle at the head of the square, green with fifty years of lake-bottom, and when Señora Antilef took my hand and pressed it flat against the bronze she said only:

"It has been quiet long enough."

I am not a sentimental woman. I measure things in meters and minutes and air remaining. But I rang that bell, or helped to — Mateo and I and three teenagers who thought the whole affair was the finest entertainment the town had produced since the dam — and the sound went up the valley and came back off the walls of the gorge, and I understood finally what Quillén had meant by *tell the truth*. Not publish it. Ring it. Make it echo off stone until the stone remembers.

* * *

My license revocation notice reached me six weeks later, forwarded through three hands to the cantina, printed on paper so official it seemed embarrassed to exist. Salvage rights suspended. Diving certification withdrawn. Barred from all registered maritime operations within national waters. Effectively: barred from the sea.

Mateo read it twice and handed it back. "What will you do?"

I looked out the cantina door at the river, high and brown and going somewhere, and at the skiff we'd abandoned in the thorn country — recovered, patched, tied up at the new jetty the town had built with money raised from people who had nothing and gave it anyway.

"There's a wreck upriver," I said. "A convoy truck, 1974, in the old channel above the gorge. And a reservoir full of a town nobody has properly documented. Somebody should map it. Somebody should bring up what's left, name by name, and give it back."

"That's not salvage," Mateo said. "That's archaeology. That's worse than archaeology. That's *work*."

"It pays in something other than miles."

He grinned, and it was the grin from our first season together, before the freighter, before the fire, before any of it. "Then I'm in. Someone has to keep you from drowning, and apparently I'm the only fool left who knows how."

I tore the revocation notice in half, then in quarters, and fed it to the cantina stove, and watched the sea's claim on me curl and blacken and go up the chimney. Exile, they'd call it. Loss. The end of a career measured in nautical miles.

But I had learned to measure differently now. In bells rung. In names spoken aloud into static until the static gave up. In the distance between a lie and its correction — which, in this valley, turned out to be exactly fifty years, and exactly walkable.

That evening the rains came again, steady and certain, and the river rose past the jetty's second plank, and I stood on it with wet hair and empty hands and felt, for the first time in ten years, that I was not leaving anywhere.

I had arrived. The river began here. So, it turned out, did I.
