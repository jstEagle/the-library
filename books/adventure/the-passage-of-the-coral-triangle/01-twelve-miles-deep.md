## 1. Twelve Miles Deep

The sea off Sulawesi doesn't care what you were. That's the whole reason I love it.

Twelve miles out from the harbor at Bitung, the water goes from tea-brown to a blue so deep it looks like it's been drinking for a century, and somewhere under that blue sits the *Meridian Grace*, a coastal freighter that went down in '09 with a hold full of machinery nobody has ever bothered to salvage because the insurance was settled and the paperwork was clean and the world moved on. Paperwork is always clean when you look at it from above. I've built a career on looking at things from below.

I was on the mooring line at forty feet, doing my checks, when Devi's voice crackled through the comm in my ear.

"Bottom time's yours whenever you want it, boss. Current's lazy today. Almost like it's hungover."

"Almost like somebody kept it up too late," I said.

"That was Ruiz. I was asleep by nine like a professional."

"Devi, you were rewiring the compressor at midnight. I heard you swearing at it in two languages."

"Three," she said. "You don't speak enough of them to count."

Devi Nair is my engineer, my dive tender, my conscience when she feels like volunteering for the job, which is rarely. She weighs maybe a hundred and ten pounds and can rebuild a diesel engine with the same hands she uses to eat noodles, and she has never once in four years asked me where I came from. This is why we get along. On my boat — well, technically Ruiz's boat, but boats belong to whoever keeps them floating — the past is a country none of us holds citizenship in. We measure our lives in nautical miles, not years. Miles don't ask questions.

I dumped air from my wing and dropped into the blue.

* * *

The *Meridian Grace* lies on her port side in about thirty meters of water, keel toward the surface like a whale that decided mid-swim to give up on the whole enterprise. She's been down long enough that the coral has claimed her honestly — soft corals furring the railings, a forest of black coral growing off the bridge wing, clouds of anthias turning the superstructure into an aquarium. A wreck this age stops being a grave and becomes a neighborhood. I like that about the sea. It repossesses everything eventually, and it doesn't gloat about it.

My job today wasn't poetry, though. My job was a generator.

A resort developer up in Manado had bought the wreck sight unseen — or rather bought the rights to strip it — on the theory that there was salvageable metal and one working-ish genset worth the charter fee. The fee was good. The fee was three months of fuel, food, and Devi's increasingly expensive relationship with spare parts suppliers. So I swam down the hull toward the engine room hatch with a cutting torch strapped to my side and no thoughts in my head beyond bolt patterns and bottom time.

That's the trick to this life, if anyone asks. You keep your head as empty as the water around you. No ledger of debts, no list of names, no map of where you've been. Just the next fin kick, the next gauge reading, the next job. I'd gotten very good at it. Twelve years good.

The engine room was silted to the knees and dark as a pocket. I clipped my light inside the hatch and worked by its cone, loosening mounting bolts on the genset while a lionfish watched me from the top of the cylinder head with the serene contempt of something that has never once filed paperwork. Forty minutes in, I'd freed three of four mounts and needed to reach behind the unit to cut a corroded bracket. I wedged myself into the gap between the generator and the bulkhead, shoulder against cold steel, and swept my light back to find the bracket.

Instead I found a box.

It was a document case, aluminum, the kind shipping companies use for manifests and bills of lading — the kind that's supposed to be in the wheelhouse or the purser's office, not wedged behind a generator in the engine room where nobody would ever find it unless they were lying flat on their stomach in the silt reaching for a bracket they couldn't see. It had been stowed there deliberately. Hidden by someone who knew the ship, who knew that even if the vessel were salvaged, divers go for the easy metal and skip the crawl spaces.

I should have left it. Let me be clear about that, since everything afterward follows from the fact that I didn't. My contract covered a generator. Contracts are how I stay alive; they draw a bright line around what's mine to touch. A rusted document case behind a genset is not mine to touch.

But here's the other thing about me: I'm a diver, and divers are professionally curious, and twelve years of empty-headed miles have to go somewhere. I unclipped the case, worked the corroded latches until they gave with a crunch that I felt more than heard, and shone my light inside.

Waterproof sleeves. Plastic sleeves, the archival kind, each holding paper that after fifteen years underwater should have been soup and wasn't — someone had sealed them properly, vacuum-packed, the way you preserve things you expect to come back for.

Cargo manifests. I know manifests the way priests know scripture. I read the top sheet by feel and flashlight: container numbers, weights, ports of origin, consignee codes. Ordinary. Boring, even. Then I turned to the second sheet and stopped breathing for a second, which is a bad habit for a diver.

The date stamp read 14 March 2013.

Now, dates on old manifests aren't strange. The ship went down in '09, so a manifest dated four years later should have been impossible — except salvors reuse forms, clerks make errors, stamps wear out and lie. I'd seen older. But this wasn't just a date. It was a date and a routing code, and the routing code was one I recognized, and recognizing it felt like stepping on something soft in the dark.

Because in another life — a life measured in fluorescent lighting and security badges instead of bottom time — I had spent eight months of my existence staring at routing codes just like that one. They belonged to a class of shipment that officially did not exist, moving between places that officially had no connection, in the spring of 2013, which was the season of the incident. The incident had a number instead of a name. The navy scrubbed it so thoroughly that when I tried, years later, to search for it from an internet café in Surabaya, the search results came back cleaner than the water I was sitting beside. Twelve years of scrubbing leaves a shine. You can still see the shape of the thing underneath, the way you can see a wreck's outline from the shadow it casts on sand.

There were eleven sheets in the case. I checked every one. Dates ran from March to June 2013. Consignees were coded, but two of the codes matched ones I knew — knew personally, knew from the inside, knew from a desk in a building where I had once written a report that other people used as a mop.

My depth gauge said I had nine minutes of no-deco time left. My heart rate said I should have surfaced five minutes ago. I did the thing I tell new divers never to do: I made a decision on the bottom, alone, with nitrogen whispering at the edges of my judgment. I zipped the sleeves back into the case, clipped the case to my harness, and started up.

"Boss?" Devi's voice, sharp. "Your ascent rate just went to hell. What's wrong?"

"Found some extra cargo," I said. "Paperwork."

"Paperwork." A pause, the sound of her deciding whether to laugh. "You're bringing me paperwork from forty meters? I married you to a boat, not to a filing cabinet."

"Devi."

"Yeah?"

"Run the deco timer. I'm coming up slow."

* * *

I did my stops hanging on the mooring line like laundry, the document case knocking gently against my hip with the swell, and I used every minute of them thinking about nothing, which is to say thinking about everything. This is the diver's paradox: the decompression stop is the most boring place on earth and the only place where the mind, denied anything else to do, finally opens its mail.

Here is what arrived in mine.

In March 2013, something happened in these waters. I won't describe it yet; describing it means admitting how I know, and I'm not ready to admit that even to myself, alone, sixty feet under the sea with only fish for witnesses. What matters is that afterwards, the record was cleaned. Ships rerouted, logs amended, a survey vessel's itinerary rewritten, personnel reassigned with commendations. I know because I saw the amendments. Some of them passed through my hands. I was twenty-six and I told myself the version I was given, and then, when the version curdled, I told myself I hadn't understood what I was looking at, and then, when that curdled too, I left. New coast, new name, new trade. Lena Lockridge, salvage diver. The woman I'd been stayed behind like a set of winter clothes in a rented locker.

And now, twelve years later, at the bottom of a routine job, the sea — which doesn't care what I was — had handed me a box of dates.

Coincidence is the religion of people who haven't looked closely at anything. I looked closely. Eleven manifests, hidden behind a generator in a ship that sank six years before those documents were stamped. Which meant the papers weren't from the *Meridian Grace*'s last voyage at all. They'd been placed here afterward. Someone had used this wreck as a dead-drop, a locker at the bottom of the sea, and either they were coming back for it or they believed no one would ever find it.

Either way, I was now holding something that someone, somewhere, considered buried.

I broke the surface into heat and glare and the smell of diesel and salt, spat my regulator, and held up the case before Devi could start her lecture.

"What is that?" she said, hauling me onto the swim step with more force than affection.

"Manifests."

"Dated when?"

I hesitated. Lying to Devi is possible the way swimming through a reef is possible — technically, briefly, at cost. "2013."

She went still, a wet rag in one hand. Devi is Indonesian by half and grew up on these coasts; she remembers 2013 the way Americans remember certain mornings. "Lena. Those ships' records were all—"

"I know what they were."

"—sealed. The whole corridor. My uncle fished out of Ambon then, and one day the charts just changed, and nobody would say why, and—" She stopped herself, looked at the case, looked at me. "Why do you have this?"

"Because it was behind the generator and I have the impulse control of a cat."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got right now." I stripped my mask and squinted at the wheelhouse, where Ruiz stood at the rail pretending to check the weather, which meant he'd been listening to every word on the open channel, which meant I had about ninety seconds before his curiosity outweighed his discretion. "Dry it, image it, seal it back up. Don't show Ruiz yet."

"You're going to tell him eventually."

"I'm going to tell him eventually," I agreed. "After I decide what this is."

"And what is it?"

I looked south, toward the deep water, toward the corridor where the charts had been quietly rewritten while I was still the person who helped rewrite them. The sea lay flat and innocent as a fresh ledger page.

"A mistake," I said. "Somebody made a mistake, and hid it twelve miles deep, and I went and found it."

Devi wrapped the case in a towel and carried it below like it was ticking. And I stood on the stern of my own boat, in the country where I had no history, and felt the first hairline crack run through the careful life I'd built out of nautical miles — because the thing about measuring your life in distance traveled is that eventually, if you hold still long enough, the distance comes looking for you.

Ruiz called down from the wheelhouse, casual as a man asking about lunch: "So. What'd you find down there?"
