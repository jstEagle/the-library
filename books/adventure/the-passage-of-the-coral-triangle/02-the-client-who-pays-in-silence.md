## 2. The Client Who Pays in Silence

The man from the government arrived the way trouble always does in the islands: politely, and by boat.

He came out to *Corvus* on a water taxi that cost more than my monthly fuel budget, wearing a linen shirt that had never seen a wrinkle it didn't pay for, and he stepped aboard like he'd been invited — which he hadn't. Ruiz met him at the rail with the expression of a man greeting a customs officer who has already decided to find something.

"Mr. Okafor," Ruiz said.

"Just Okafor is fine." His handshake was dry and brief, the handshake of a man who touches things only long enough to own them. "And you're Captain Ruiz. The famous one."

"I'm not famous."

"You are in certain rooms." He smiled like those rooms were fond of him. "May I?" He gestured at the cockpit without waiting for an answer, which told me everything about how his invitations worked.

I stayed in the shadow of the wheelhouse with a mug of coffee I had no intention of drinking, doing what I do best, which is being furniture. Twelve years of practice. Furniture doesn't get remembered, doesn't get named in reports, doesn't get asked for papers. Furniture watches.

Devi did not watch. Devi stood at the engine hatch with a rag in her hand, ostensibly degreasing something that did not need degreasing, actually listening with the total, predatory attention of a woman who could hear a lie through two feet of steel plating. She caught my eye once and gave me a look I'd learned to translate over three years of working together. It said: *this one.*

I know. I'd known since the water taxi.

* * *

Okafor laid it out simply, the way men lay out simple things when they've rehearsed them.

"A government hydrographic survey team, six personnel, deployed to a reef complex south of here. They completed their work. They were extracted early due to a scheduling conflict." He said *scheduling conflict* the way other people say *act of God.* "In the extraction, some equipment was left behind. Sensitive equipment. We'd like it recovered before the monsoon makes the site inaccessible."

"How much equipment?" Ruiz asked.

"Roughly four hundred kilos. Laptops, survey instruments, hard drives, personal effects. Nothing dangerous, nothing classified in the paranoid sense. Just government property that needs to come home."

"And the team?"

Okafor looked at him with mild, unbothered eyes. "Reassigned."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the answer I have." He spread his hands, an apology made of air. "Captain, I'll be direct, because I'm told you appreciate directness. There are no bodies. There are no questions. There is gear on an island, there is a window of perhaps three weeks before the weather closes, and there is a payment schedule that should make this the easiest money your operation has ever earned. You go, you load, you bring it to a location I'll provide, you get paid. You don't investigate anything. You don't photograph anything. You don't discuss anything. In exchange—" he named a number.

Ruiz kept his face still, but I watched his thumb move against his forefinger, once, the tell he thinks nobody knows. The number was enough to finish the refit we'd been limping toward for eight months. Enough to replace the compressor that sounded like a dying walrus. Enough to stop doing the arithmetic every night where the fuel bill ate the dive fees and the dive fees ate the future.

Enough, in short, to be dangerous.

"We'd need coordinates," Ruiz said. "Site survey. Dive conditions."

"All provided upon acceptance." Okafor produced a folded paper from inside his jacket and set it on the chart table. A contract. Real paper, real letterhead — a ministry I'd never heard of, which in this country meant either very new or very deniable. "Half up front, half on delivery. Standard salvage terms, plus a confidentiality clause that is, I assure you, entirely routine."

Nothing is routine about a confidentiality clause attached to missing people. But Ruiz picked up the paper anyway, and I understood why, and I hated that I understood why, because I'd done the same arithmetic in the dark of the wheelhouse at two in the morning more times than I wanted to admit. The sea doesn't care about your principles. The sea sends invoices.

"We'll review it," Ruiz said.

"Of course." Okafor rose, smoothed his shirt, and paused at the rail as if remembering something charming. "One more thing. My understanding is your diver is quite good. Ms.—" he glanced at the contract, though we hadn't given him names beyond Ruiz's, which meant someone else had — "Lockridge. I'm told she works deep and quiet. That's exactly the temperament this job requires."

He said my name like he was tasting it. Then he stepped down into the water taxi and was gone, leaving behind a contract, a number, and the particular silence of a man who knows precisely how much he's told you and not one word more.

* * *

Devi waited until the taxi was a speck, then until it wasn't even that, then she dropped the rag into the bilge and said, "No."

"Noted," Ruiz said.

"No, I mean it. Did you hear him? 'Extracted early.' Six people leave an island in a hurry and take nothing? Not the laptops? Divers leave fins behind when they're dead, Captain. Survey teams leave laptops behind when they're—"

"When they're what?" Ruiz asked quietly.

Devi's mouth opened and closed. She didn't have a word for it yet. None of us did. That was the thing about the whole conversation — it had been built out of gaps. No bodies, no questions. Extracted early. Reassigned. Every sentence had a hole in the middle of it shaped like six human beings, and Okafor had walked across those holes like they were deck plates he'd installed himself.

"He knew your name," Devi said to me. "Your name. We don't advertise. Our charter listings say 'crew of three, references available.' Nobody says Lockridge."

"Fishing harbor talk," I said. "I dive the wrecks everyone watches us dive. People talk."

"People talk about the boat. Nobody talks about you, because you've built yourself out of fog, Lena, and we both know it, because I helped you pour the concrete." She crossed her arms. "That man knew your name before he ever saw your face. Think about what that costs."

I thought about it. I thought about it harder than I let show, because showing it would have confirmed the thing she already knew, which is that under the name Lockridge there is a locked room, and I am the only one who holds the key, and I have spent twelve years pretending the room isn't there.

"Money's real, though," Ruiz said. He was looking at the contract, not at either of us, which was his way of looking at both of us. "Half up front. That's the refit. That's the compressor. That's six months of not counting cans of diesel."

"The money's real," Devi agreed. "So's the trap. Those aren't mutually exclusive. The best traps are funded generously."

"Nobody's saying yes tonight." Ruiz rolled the contract and tapped it against his palm, a man weighing paper against instinct and hating the scale. "We sleep on it. Lena?"

They both looked at me, and I resented them for it, because I'm the diver, not the decider, and I have arranged my entire life so that the decisions belong to other people. That's the luxury of being crew. Somebody else chooses the wreck; you just go down and see what's true.

But they were waiting, and the truth was I'd already gone down.

Because here's what none of them knew, what I couldn't tell them without opening the locked room: when Okafor said *reef complex south of here,* something in my chest went cold and specific. South of here. The same general water I'd been diving two weeks ago, where I'd pulled a corroded cargo manifest off a wreck that shouldn't exist, stamped with dates from an incident the navy scrubbed so thoroughly that even the incident's name had been sanded away. I'd told myself it was coincidence. Salvage divers find strange paper; that's the job; you log it or you don't.

But governments don't send linen shirts to recover laptops. Governments send linen shirts to make sure certain paper never surfaces twice.

And underneath the fear — I'll be honest, because the sea has taught me honesty the way a mason teaches brick, one hard piece at a time — underneath the fear was curiosity, and underneath the curiosity was greed, and underneath all of it was the oldest pull I know: the pull toward a sunken thing. Twelve years of diving and I still can't pass a dark shape on the bottom without wanting to know what it is. It's the flaw in my design. It's probably the reason I'm alive, and it will probably be the reason I'm not.

"There'll be conditions," I said.

Ruiz raised an eyebrow. Devi looked briefly triumphant, then suspicious, because she knows me well enough to know that when I agree to something it's usually for reasons I haven't finished confessing.

"We take the job," I said, "but we document everything for ourselves. Dates, positions, condition of the site. Quietly. If the gear's just gear, we deliver it and forget it. If it isn't—" I shrugged, and the shrug was a lie, because I already knew it wouldn't be just gear. "Then we'll want to know what we carried."

"That violates the clause," Ruiz said.

"The clause assumes we're employees. We're contractors. Contractors keep records. Ask any salvage lawyer." There are no salvage lawyers within a thousand miles, and Ruiz knew it, and I knew he knew it, and he let it go anyway, because the number was sitting in his chest where the arithmetic used to be.

Devi looked at me for a long moment. "You're not doing this for the money," she said. Not a question.

"Nobody does anything for just one reason," I said, which was also a lie, but a smaller one.

* * *

We signed the next morning. Okafor's man brought the advance in cash — actual banded cash, delivered in a waterproof case, which is not how ministries move money and is exactly how other kinds of organizations do — and coordinates, and a tide table annotated in a neat bureaucratic hand, and a satellite phone in a padded pouch.

"Check in daily at 1800," the man said. "One ring means all's well. Two rings means delay. If you miss a check-in, we assume equipment failure and send assistance."

"What kind of assistance?" Devi asked sweetly.

"The helpful kind," he said, and left.

We spent that day provisioning like people who intended to do exactly what we'd been hired to do and nothing else. Fuel, water, food for ten days, spare filters. Ruiz topped off every tank. Devi stripped and rebuilt the port engine's fuel system because she'd been meaning to and because her hands think better when they're busy.

I checked the dive gear twice, then a third time, and on the third time I found myself adding things that weren't on any list. A backup cutting tool. A spare mask. The little waterproof notebook I use for site surveys, the one with no name in it.

At dusk I climbed to the flybridge, where Ruiz was studying the coordinates like they might confess under sufficient staring.

"South-east of the survey grid," he said, tapping the chart. "Which puts the island..." his finger traced a line and stopped. "Huh."

"What?"

"Twelve miles from where you dove last month. The manifest wreck." He looked up at me. "Small ocean."

"Enormous ocean," I said. "Tiny map."

"Lena." He said it carefully, the way you step onto ice. "If you know something about why a man in a clean shirt is paying us this much—"

"I know what you know. Gear on an island, no bodies, no questions." I held his gaze and gave him the flat calm surface I've practiced in mirrors until it stopped being practice. "Everything else is weather."

He studied me a moment longer, and I could see him deciding — consciously, deliberately — to accept the surface. Captains do that. They hire the version of you that showed up, and they ride with it, and they hope the deeper versions stay below the waterline where they belong.

"First light," he said. "Get some sleep."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat in my bunk with the hatch open, listening to the harbor breathe, and I did the thing I never do, which was open the locked room a crack.

Not all the way. Just enough to let one fact slide out and stand in the dark where I could look at it: whoever Okafor worked for had scrubbed an incident twelve years ago, and I knew that because I had read the scrubbed version. Had held it. Had been, in a life measured in a different name, adjacent to it — close enough to smell the smoke, far enough to survive it, complicit enough that the miles I'd put between me and that coast were never going to be enough if anyone ever started pulling thread.

Six people extracted early. Equipment left behind. And now a fixer hiring strangers to fetch the paper before the monsoon seals the site — or before anyone else finds what the survey team found.

*Found,* my mind supplied, unhelpfully. Not *left.* Found, and then left, along with their shoes.

I closed the room. I lay back and listened to the water slap the hull and told myself the story I'm best at: that I was a salvage diver, that this was a job, that the money was good and the questions were somebody else's department. Greed and curiosity, the twin engines of every bad decision I've ever made, humming nicely in tune.

Somewhere south of us, on an island with no tracks leading anywhere, four hundred kilos of government property sat in the sun, waiting to be carried by people who had promised not to look at it.

By morning we'd be underway. By evening, I suspected, we'd understand why the client pays in silence.

Some silences, after all, are purchased. Others are just the sound of a held breath — six people's worth, waiting underwater for someone brave enough, or greedy enough, or curious enough, to come and let it out.
