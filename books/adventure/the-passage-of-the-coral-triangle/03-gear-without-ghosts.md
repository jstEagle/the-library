## 3. Gear Without Ghosts

I have a theory about horror movies, which is that they're all wrong. The monster always comes crashing through the door, dripping and roaring, and everyone screams. But that's not how the world actually ends. In my experience — and I've had enough of it to hold opinions — the world ends quietly, in an empty room, with everything still in its place.

The island proved me right before we even got the dinghy beached.

It was called Pulau Serat on Ruiz's charts, though I doubted anyone had used that name aloud in years. A green thumbprint of land off the survey grids, maybe four hundred meters across, ringed by reef flat that turned the water pale as milk glass at low tide. From the boat it looked like nothing. That was the point, presumably. If you were going to put six government scientists somewhere and never speak of them again, you'd want somewhere that looked like nothing.

"Beach looks clean," Ruiz said, easing back on the throttle. "No debris line."

"No debris line," Lena repeated, like she was checking his math.

I didn't say anything because I was busy having the thought that would ruin my morning: a camp occupied for weeks leaves a debris line. Plastic, rope, ration wrappers, the shed skin of human habitation. It accumulates whether you're tidy or not. Tidiness itself leaves traces — swept sand holds its broom marks for days in still air.

There were no broom marks either.

We came ashore just after eight, three of us — me, Lena, and Tomas from deck crew, who'd volunteered with the particular enthusiasm of a man who has never once considered what volunteering might cost him. Ruiz held the boat off the reef in case. In case of what, nobody specified. That was the mood of the whole operation: everyone performing competence while privately agreeing not to name the thing we could all feel under the waterline.

The camp sat on a shelf of coral terrace above the beach, half-shaded by casuarinas. Six dome tents in two rows, guy-lines taut, flysheets buttoned down against rain that hadn't come. A folding table under a tarp awning. Solar panels angled at the morning sun on their mounts, cables dressed neatly into conduit. A generator shed with the door latched.

Everything maintained. Everything waiting.

"Okay," Tomas said brightly, "this is fine. They got evacuated fast, right? Quick extraction, no time to pack."

"Tomas," I said, "look at the solar panels."

He looked. He was a good kid; he just needed things pointed at.

"They're aimed at this morning's sun," I said. "Not last week's. Not some average. This morning." I checked my watch against the panel azimuth, did the ugly trigonometry in my head, hated the answer, did it again. "Somebody adjusted those within the last day or two. Maybe less."

Lena crouched by the nearest tent. She didn't touch anything at first — she read it, the way she reads water. Then she lifted the flap.

Inside: sleeping bags rolled. Boots paired at the door, laces tucked in. A paperback facedown on someone's cot, spine cracked to hold the page. A headlamp hung on a loop, exactly where a hand would find it in the dark.

"People don't evacuate without shoes," she said quietly. "Not anywhere. Not for any reason. You can make a person leave a laptop. Nobody leaves barefoot."

Tomas had stopped being bright. Good.

* * *

We worked the camp in a grid, because that's what you do when you don't know what you're looking for and you very much don't want to miss it. I took the technical shelter — a larger tent at the camp's center, aluminum frame, the kind of thing that costs more than my boat did before Ruiz sank his savings into her.

The laptops were there. Three of them, closed, on the work table, power cables coiled beside them like sleeping snakes. One was still warm.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only weapon I had out there. Not warm like sun-heated plastic. Warm like a machine that had been running and had shut down recently enough that the heat hadn't bled out through the canvas. I pressed my palm flat against the chassis and held it there and felt my own heartbeat speed up to meet whatever was in that metal.

"Battery or mains?" Lena asked from the doorway. She'd come over without me calling her. She did that. It was going to get on my nerves eventually, if we lived long enough.

"Mains." I traced the cable to a power strip, to a heavy-gauge feed running out of the tent, underground in conduit, toward the generator shed. "And the generator's off now, but look at the fuel cap."

She looked. The cap was finger-tight. Not sealed, not stowed. Finger-tight, the way you leave it when you expect to open it again tomorrow.

"So somebody turned it off," she said. "Deliberately. Recently."

"Somebody turned *everything* off. Deliberately. Recently. And then walked away from their boots." I opened the nearest laptop. It woke instantly, no password, desktop pristine, browser history a flat zero. Wiped, or never used, or — worse — a display model. A set dressing. "These are props."

"You think the real machines went with them?"

"I think the real machines went *somewhere*, and these went nowhere, and both facts are wrong." I pulled a flashlight and started checking under the table, along the frame lines, the places where field techs hide things from themselves. Tape a drive under the desk, forget it, remember it too late — every expedition has its archaeology of panic.

Under the third laptop's foam insert, wedged into a cutout that existed on no manufacturer's diagram, I found a drive. Ruggedized case, scuffed, one corner cracked. A hand-written label, mostly worn away: something-something-ARCHIVE, and beneath it, in different ink, pressed hard enough to emboss: **DO NOT SYNC**.

"Found something," I said, and my voice came out steadier than my hands.

* * *

Here is what I love about hardware, and why I trust it more than people: hardware cannot lie well. People lie beautifully, fluently, with whole symphonies of plausible detail. Hardware lies badly. It leaves checksums. It leaves wear patterns. It leaves the thermal residue of what it was doing right up until the moment it stopped.

The drive was corrupted — not subtly, either. Someone had gone after it with a magnet or a voltage spike or plain spite, and whole sectors came back as static, gray noise where data should sing. But corruption is rarely total. Data has mass, and mass leaves shadows. I sat in the shade of the awning with my field kit cracked open, a salvaged USB interface, a bench supply I'd built from a drone charger and stubbornness, and I coaxed the dying platters one sector at a time while Tomas stood watch on the beach and Lena pretended to inventory rations and actually watched the tree line.

Most of what survived was boring, and I've never been so grateful for boredom in my life. Calibration logs. Water sample tables. A shipping manifest for freeze-dried rations, forty-two cases, signed for by someone named Adeyemi whose signature trailed off like he'd been interrupted. Weather records, dutiful and dull, six weeks of wind and humidity from a station that had kept perfect notes about everything except its own disappearance.

Then, in a recovered fragment of an email draft — unsent, saved at 03:14 local time, which told its own story about what kind of nights they'd been having — thirty-one words:

*"If anything happens to us, the full record is not here. We moved it. Ask about the Ledger. Whatever they tell you, the Ledger is real and it is not on any island."*

I read it four times. Then I copied it to two separate cards, photographed the screen, wrote the text longhand in my logbook because paper doesn't crash, and then sat there feeling the dread arrive properly, the way deep water arrives — not all at once but by pressure, by degrees, until your ears pop and you realize how far down you already are.

Because here's the thing about that message. It wasn't a cry for help. Cries for help ask for rescue, give coordinates, name names. This was a dead-drop note. A message written by people who had already concluded that help would not arrive, addressed to whoever came looking later, planted where only a certain kind of person would find it — someone who checked under foam inserts, who understood why you'd write DO NOT SYNC, who would recognize 03:14 as the hour when you stop believing anyone is coming.

They'd written it for us. Or for someone exactly like us. Weeks ago, maybe. And we were reading it now, in their chairs, wearing the silence they'd left behind.

"Devi." Lena, low, from the edge of the awning. "Come look at this. Bring nothing. Leave the drive where it is."

I brought the drive. She gave me a look that forgave me and condemned me in the same second, which is a skill she has.

* * *

What she'd found was on the far side of the camp, past the galley tent, where the coral terrace stepped down toward a narrow beach on the island's lee side. Footprints — real ones this time, the first actual tracks we'd seen — pressed into a patch of soft ground between the rocks. Boot prints. At least five sets, maybe six, heading down to the water.

And stopping.

That was the part I'll be seeing when I close my eyes for a while. The prints went down to the tide line, crisp, unhurried — no stride-lengthening, no scuffing, none of the chaos people leave when they're taken somewhere against their will. These were people walking to a boat they expected to board. And then the prints simply ended at the wet sand, because the tide had come in behind them, and beyond that line there was nothing. No drag marks. No blood. No dropped gear, no churned coral, no hull impressions on the flat where a boat would have sat while six scientists climbed aboard.

The sand above the tide line held their last steps like a photograph. The sea below it held nothing at all.

"A boat came in," Lena said. "Shallow draft, probably anchored off the flat — no hull scars means they didn't beach it. They waded out." She crouched, measuring stride with her hand spread wide. "Calm. Organized. They knew the people walking out, or the people walking out weren't afraid yet."

"Or they were afraid and knew better than to show it."

"That too."

Tomas had drifted down behind us, and for once he said nothing at all, which from Tomas was its own kind of alarm.

I stood there with the tide licking at my boots and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the solar panels. Somebody had serviced this camp within the last day or two. Adjusted the panels, run the generator, left it fueled and capped and ready. And the people who lived here had walked calmly into the water and not come back, and their replacements-in-absence had tidied the camp behind them — not erased it, mind, *maintained* it, kept it looking lived-in and merely paused, the way you stage a house for a viewing.

You don't do that when you're evacuating survivors. You do that when you're expecting visitors, and you'd prefer the visitors relaxed.

"They're coming back," I said. "Or they never stopped watching. Either way, this camp isn't abandoned. It's bait."

Lena didn't argue. That frightened me more than disagreement would have. She just looked south along the water, where the light was starting to go bronze and long, and said, "We take what we came for and we're off this rock inside the hour. Devi — whatever else that drive has in it, I want it extracted on the boat, moving, with Ruiz awake."

"And if Okafor asks what we found?"

She was quiet for a moment. The tide filled the quiet.

"We'll decide what we found," she said, "after we know what it is."

* * *

Back at the awning, with the light failing and Tomas rigging a watch rotation that mostly consisted of him refusing to sit down, I made my last pass through the drive. Call it professional thoroughness. Call it the inability of engineers to leave a machine unfinished. Both are true; only one is safe.

In the final recoverable block — timestamped eleven days before the email draft, buried in a directory structure that had been renamed twice to look like sensor calibration data — I found a file index. Just the index; the contents had been shredded past recovery. Forty-one entries. Most were labeled with dates and grid references, dry as ballast. But entry nine had a title that survived intact, and I sat very still under the awning while the casuarinas hissed overhead and read it three times to make sure the corruption hadn't gotten into my eyes.

Entry nine: **LEDGER_v3_FINAL — chain of custody: A. — distribution: NONE — verification pending.**

Chain of custody. Distribution: none. Verification pending. Whoever wrote that wasn't a scientist cataloging samples. That was the language of evidence — of someone building a case, carefully, secretly, in a place they believed no one would ever look, and getting it ready to move.

Forty-one documents. One final version. No copies distributed. Which meant the Ledger — the real one, the whole weight of it — existed in exactly one place in the world, and six people had walked calmly into the sea rather than say where.

I zipped the fragments, split them across three cards, taped one card under the insole of my boot where it rode against my arch like a small cold heartbeat, and went to tell Lena that we hadn't found missing persons.

We'd found a message in a bottle, and the bottle was still warm, and somewhere out past the reef, in the dark coming down over the water, I could not shake the certainty that someone had adjusted the solar panels this morning because they expected us to need the light.

The dinghy's engine caught on the first pull. Small mercies. I kept my hand pressed against my boot the whole way out, and behind us the camp sat neat and patient in the dusk, lamps-off, tents-buttoned, holding its breath — waiting, exactly as staged, for whoever came next.
