## 4. Wake in the Dark

There are two kinds of fear in this business. The first is the honest kind — the reef that appears where your chart says open water, the engine that coughs at the wrong moment, the storm front with your name on it. That kind of fear you can work with. It has a shape. You can put your hands on it.

The second kind is the fear that arrives without a sound, and I want to be clear: I did not hear the black-hulled boat before I saw it. That's what bothered me. In thirty years of running boats through water other people consider impassable, I have learned to trust my ears almost as much as my eyes, and my ears told me we were alone on the Molucca Sea.

They were wrong.

* * *

We'd cleared the last of the survey island's lee by 1900 hours, Devi still quiet in the way she gets when her brain is chewing something it can't swallow. She'd spent the whole afternoon hunched over that corrupted drive fragment like a doctor reading an X-ray she wished belonged to someone else. "The Ledger," she kept saying, not to anyone, just to the screen. I'd asked her twice what it meant and gotten two answers that weren't answers.

Lena was up top with me when I first saw it. We were running the night passage toward Ambon — twelve hours of open water, moonless because the moon had the good sense to stay behind cloud cover, which I was grateful for right up until I wasn't.

"Ruiz." Lena's voice, low. She didn't point. Good instinct. Pointing tells whoever's watching you exactly how much you've noticed.

"I see it." And I did — or rather, I saw the absence of it. A patch of sea off our port quarter, maybe three miles back, where the stars reflected wrong. Every boat makes a wake, and every wake flattens starlight differently than flat water does. Most people never learn to read it. My father taught me off Mindanao when I was nine years old, fishing waters we weren't supposed to be fishing, and he said: *Miguel, the sea always tells on itself. Your job is to listen.*

The sea was telling on somebody.

"Running lights?" Lena asked.

"None."

"Could be a fisherman running dark. Happens all the time out here."

"It could," I agreed. "Fishermen also don't hold position for twenty minutes while another boat passes them at eight knots." A fisherman works his water. This one sat in ours like a spider that had decided the web would come to it.

Lena was quiet for a moment. Then: "How long has it been there?"

"That's the question, isn't it." I checked our wake again. "Could've picked us up at the island. Could've been waiting offshore since before we anchored." Neither possibility made me feel warm inside. One meant they'd followed us from the survey camp, which meant they knew what we'd found. The other meant they'd known where we were going before we did, which was worse in a way I didn't have vocabulary for yet.

"Devi's drive," Lena said.

"Now you're thinking like me, and I hate it for both of us."

* * *

I'll give you the layout of my ship, because it matters. The *Perle Hitam* — yes, I named my boat Black Pearl, no, I will not be taking criticism, I was thirty-one and sentimental about a woman who left me — is forty-two feet of converted patrol hull, Indonesian-built, ugly as sin and twice as stubborn. Twin diesels, shallow draft, a fuel range that lets me run from trouble longer than most trouble cares to chase. Down below: Devi's electronics bench, which looks like a bomb technician's garage sale; Lena's dive gear, racked and rinsed; and a small fortune in salvage equipment that pays for all of it.

Up top: me, a wheel, radar, AIS, and a lifetime of habits I was about to need every single one of.

"AIS off?" Lena asked.

"Not yet." I watched the dark patch astern. "Kill the transponder and any boat with half-decent gear knows exactly what we just did. Right now we're a legal vessel doing a legal transit, and that thing back there is a shadow with no paperwork. Let it stay the one breaking rules."

"And if it closes?"

"Then I get creative."

Devi came up the companionway with two mugs of coffee and the expression of a woman who'd been eavesdropping through the hatch, which she had, because Devi eavesdrops the way other people breathe — automatically, and without apology. She handed us the coffee. "So we're being followed."

"We're being *watched*," I corrected. "Following implies intent."

"The drive fragment mentioned a Ledger," Devi said. "I got maybe four percent of the file restored before it corrupted past recovery. Four percent. And in that four percent there were references to a government survey vessel, a wreck designation, and a phrase I'm going to say out loud because keeping it in my mouth was starting to burn: *witness disposition schedule.*"

I felt that land in my chest like ballast. "Disposition."

"That's the word I flagged too," Devi said. "It's bureaucratic language, Ruiz. Nobody writes *disposition schedule* about cargo. You write it about people."

The sea said nothing. The black patch on our quarter held its distance, patient as debt.

* * *

By 2200 the thing had closed to two miles, and by 2300 it had done something that removed the last benefit of the doubt from the table: it matched our turns. I'd been varying course — ten degrees north, fifteen south, nothing dramatic, just enough to test. Each time, after a lag of four or five minutes, the shadow adjusted. Not perfectly. Not mechanically. But consistently, the way a predator adjusts to prey that keeps changing direction without quite knowing why.

"They're using thermal," Devi said. She'd set up at the radar repeater with a pair of binoculars she'd modified herself, because of course she had. "No active radar paint on us, no lights. They're reading our engine heat against the water temperature. Which means they're not fishermen, because fishermen can't afford thermal imaging, and they're not navy, because navy doesn't hide."

"Navy hides plenty," I said.

"Navy hides *badly* and calls it classified. This is different. This is someone who doesn't want to exist."

Lena stood at the rail, arms folded, watching our wake roll away into nothing. She'd been quiet for an hour, and I know enough about Lena Lockridge to know that her silence isn't emptiness. It's compression. Somewhere under all that calm, calculations were running.

"There's a fishing fleet ahead," she said finally. "Off the Talaud approaches. We crossed their grounds two days ago coming north — dozens of boats, lamp rafts, the whole circus."

"You want to hide in traffic."

"I want to *drown* in traffic. There's a difference. Hiding means staying still. Drowning means becoming one boat among two hundred, all of them hot, all of them moving, none of them interesting." She looked at me. "Can you run dark through that?"

I took my time answering, because the answer mattered. Running dark means killing every light, every emission, navigating by eye, memory, and radar passive returns only — and doing it inside a fleet of small craft who themselves run erratically, change course without warning, and string anchor lines across the water like tripwires. It's the nautical equivalent of driving blindfolded through a market on the assumption that everyone else's panic will keep you safe.

"I can," I said. "But understand what you're asking. If I hit a lamp raft in the dark, we don't get a mayday. We get sunk, and whatever's in that drive goes to the bottom with us, and Okafor's employers send flowers."

"Okafor said no questions," Lena said. "Somebody's asking them anyway."

That was the moment, if I'm honest, when the fear turned over in my chest and became something else. I've been afraid plenty of times in this life — properly afraid, the kind that lives in your hands and makes them steady instead of shaking, because that's what fear does to a man who's decided to use it. Standing there with a ghost on my quarter and a dead man's vocabulary in my engine room, I understood something simple: whatever this was, it had started before we took the job, and it would not end when the job ended. Okafor hadn't hired us despite the danger. He'd hired us *into* it, and the money was just the leash.

"We're already targets," I said aloud, mostly to myself. "Whatever we do tonight, that doesn't change. So the only question is whether we're targets with options or targets without them."

Devi snorted. "Motivational poster material, Captain."

"Get below. Kill the cabin lights, kill the AIS, and pull the breaker on anything that glows. Lena — you're my eyes portside. Call everything, even if you think I've seen it. Especially if you think I've seen it."

* * *

We went dark at 2340.

There's a specific feeling to killing your own lights in open water at night. The world doesn't get darker — the world was already dark — but *you* get smaller. The boat shrinks around you. Your own wake becomes the loudest thing in existence, a white whisper of betrayal trailing behind you visible, I swear, from orbit. Every fisherman within ten miles suddenly seems armed. Every floating log becomes a reef.

I throttled down to six knots to cut our heat signature and our wake together, and I steered for the fishing fleet the way you steer for a storm you've decided is safer than what's chasing you.

The Talaud grounds announced themselves before we saw them: a glow on the northern horizon, green-white, the combined light of a hundred lamp boats drawing plankton and fish and the entire food chain upward into their nets. Then the individual lights resolved — lamp rafts strung in loose lines, motherships anchored among them, pump-boats chugging between clusters with the cheerful lawlessness of men who answer to no flag but hunger.

And threaded through all of it, our shadow.

I picked it up twice more before we reached the fleet's edge. Both times it hung back, maybe two miles, holding its perfect coward's distance. Thermal imaging is wonderful technology until your target parks itself next to forty other heat sources in ninety-degree water over volcanic shelf ground. Then it's a man trying to pick one voice out of a stadium crowd by lip-reading from the parking lot.

"Coming up on the eastern line," Lena called softly. "Three lamp rafts, maybe four hundred meters apart. Anchor lights only — they're working the outer edge."

"Copy. Watch for net floats."

"Always watching for net floats. I've seen what they do to props."

We slid into the traffic.

I won't pretend it was clean. Running dark through a working fleet is a negotiation with chaos, and chaos drives a hard bargain. A pump-boat cut across our bow at the worst possible angle and I had to shove the throttle to idle so fast the whole boat groaned. A string of net floats — unlit, because of course they were unlit, because the men who lay them figure God will look out for them — drifted across our track like a minefield, and Lena spotted them at eighty meters, calling them in a voice so calm you'd have thought she was reading a grocery list. We threaded them. We threaded a mothership dragging its anchor in the current. We passed close enough under one lamp raft to smell the diesel and the drying fish and hear a radio playing some love song with more reverb than melody.

Somewhere in that crowd, our shadow lost us.

I know because I watched for it. Every gap in the lamplight, every stretch of black water between raft clusters, I swept with the night glasses, looking for that wrongness in the starlight, that held breath on the water. Nothing. By 0200 we were through the thick of the fleet and into the scattered western edge, and by 0300 we were alone again — genuinely alone, I believed, for the first time in five hours.

Devi came up with fresh coffee and no jokes, which from Devi is a distress signal all its own.

"Gone?" she asked.

"For now." I took the mug. "But I want to know who they were. Ghosts bother me less when they have names."

"Then you'll like this," Devi said, and produced her camera — actually, my camera, the one I keep in the wheelhouse drawer for documenting salvage sites, which she had borrowed without asking, which is the only way Devi borrows things. On the screen: a long-lens shot taken during one of our shadow's brief crossings through a gap in the lamp light. Grainy. Slightly blurred. But legible.

A black hull, low-slung, maybe sixty feet, twin diesels, a superstructure designed by someone who hated windows. And on the bow, catching the spill of a distant lamp raft, a registration number.

"PD-1187," Devi read. "I ran it the moment I had signal on the satphone. It's a real registration. Formerly assigned to a customs interdiction vessel based out of Bitung." She paused, letting me enjoy the setup, because Devi believes suspense is a form of respect. "Decommissioned two years ago. Scrapped, officially. The hull number was retired."

"So that boat doesn't exist."

"That boat *especially* doesn't exist. Somebody took a dead government hull, refit it with military-grade engines and thermal imaging, stripped its transponder, and pointed it at us. That's not pirates, Ruiz. Pirates buy old ferries and bolt guns on them. This is institutional. This is budgeted."

I looked at the photograph for a long time. There's a particular nausea that comes from seeing proof that the thing hunting you has an accounting department. Fear of a shark is animal and clean. Fear of an organization is worse, because organizations don't get hungry and stop. They get instructions, and the instructions don't expire.

"Lena," I called.

She came up from the rail, hair damp with spray, and I showed her the photo. She studied it the way she studies a wreck on sonar — patiently, completely, like she's memorizing the shape of something she'll have to navigate blind later.

"Customs interdiction," she said quietly. "Bitung station." Something moved behind her eyes, some recognition she didn't share, and filed away. I've learned not to push. Lena Lockridge gives you exactly as much of herself as the situation requires, and I'd begun to suspect the situation required more than she was admitting.

"Whoever they are," I said, "they know we were at the survey camp. They know what we took. And now they know we can run." I handed the camera back to Devi. "Make copies of that photo. Three of them, three places, and email one somewhere that can't be deleted. If we disappear, that hull number needs to outlive us."

Devi raised an eyebrow. "You're planning for us to disappear?"

"I'm planning for the *possibility*, which is different, and which is why I'm still alive at fifty-eight." I turned us back onto our heading for Ambon and let the engines come up to cruising speed, the vibration rising through the deck like a heartbeat steadying itself. "Here's what I know. Two weeks ago my biggest problem was a client who paid late. Now I've got a decommissioned ghost ship on my tail, a dead survey team with no bodies, and a hard drive full of words like *disposition.*"

"And?" Lena asked.

"And I used to think the smart play was to give the drive back, take the money, and forget the island." I watched the eastern horizon, where the first gray suggestion of dawn was gathering like a rumor. "But you don't forget an island like that. And they don't let you. We're already in the story, Lena. The only choice left is whether we're in it as witnesses or as footnotes."

Devi, behind me, said softly: "Footnotes get disposed."

Nobody laughed. The *Perle Hitam* ran east into the growing light, and somewhere behind us, in all that empty water, I had no doubt at all that something was already asking where we'd gone — and that the answer, when it came, would arrive at a fuel stop, wearing a suit, with an offer we'd have to be fools to take and bigger fools to refuse.

I'd met Okafor once. I remembered his handshake. Warm, dry, unhurried — the handshake of a man who has never once needed to grab.

I started planning how to avoid shaking it again.
