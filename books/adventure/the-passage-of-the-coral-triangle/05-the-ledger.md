## 5. The Ledger

There are two kinds of silence in an engine room. The good kind, when everything is running so smoothly the machinery hums below hearing, and the bad kind, when something has stopped that should be going. I spent the morning chasing the bad kind in our port generator — a fuel line with opinions of its own — which is why I was flat on my back under the workbench with a wrench in my teeth when Lena came down the ladder and said my name in a voice I'd never heard her use before.

Not *Devi, the watermaker's acting up.* Not *Devi, we need to talk about the fuel budget.* Just: "Devi."

I slid out far enough to see her upside down. She was holding the drive fragment in a ziplock bag like it was a dead bird she'd found on deck.

"You got it to mount?" I asked.

"No. But it's clicking."

"Clicking is bad. Clicking means the heads are kissing the platters, which means every second it spins, we lose data we'll never get back."

"So don't spin it."

"I can't read a hard drive without spinning it, Lena. This isn't a book." I sat up, cracked my head on the workbench shelf — a routine part of any repair — and took the bag from her. Through the plastic, the little 2.5-inch drive looked exactly as innocent as every other piece of silicon that had ever ruined someone's life.

The survey team had left it behind on the island, wedged under the floor panel of their comms tent, wrapped in a dry bag along with a handwritten note that said only *IF WE DON'T COME BACK* and then nothing, because whoever wrote it had run out of time or nerve or both. Corrupted sectors across a third of the surface. Salt creep on the connector. A drive that had been through whatever those people went through, which apparently included being buried in sand by someone in a hurry.

"I want you to try," Lena said. "And I want you to be careful."

"Careful is my middle name. Careful and Underpaid." I turned the bag over. "Why now? You've been carrying this thing around for four days like it was a grenade with the pin half out."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I want to be honest about what I saw there, because I've thought about it since. She wasn't deciding whether to tell me. She was deciding how much of herself it would cost.

"The manifest," she said. "From the wreck I dove last month. The dates on it matched an incident report I've seen before. Twelve years ago. And this drive belonged to people who were looking into the same thing."

"You've seen the incident report where?"

"That's not the part that matters yet."

"Lena—"

"Fix the drive, Devi. Then we'll both know what matters."

She climbed back up into the light, and I sat there on the deck plates holding a dead woman's — no. Not dead. Missing. Holding a missing person's hard drive, and understanding that my captain had just handed me a live wire and walked away before it could spark.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about data recovery: it's mostly waiting. Hollywood thinks you type furiously for forty seconds and shout "I'm in!" In reality you clamp the drive into a donor enclosure, you image it sector by sector at a crawl, skipping the dead zones, logging every read error like a nurse charting a failing pulse, and then you wait while software does forensic surgery on a file system that's been beaten half to death. It took eleven hours. Eleven hours during which I drank too much coffee, rebuilt the fuel line properly, ate dinner without tasting it, and caught Ruiz watching me twice from the helm with the particular patience of a man who has decided not to ask questions until the answers arrive on their own.

At 0230, with the boat rocking at anchor off a nameless islet and everyone else asleep, the imaging finished. Ninety-one terabytes of raw sectors, sixty percent recoverable. I started carving files out of the wreckage the way you pick shellfish out of a reef — carefully, and with growing unease about what else might be living in there.

The survey team had been thorough. That was the first thing that struck me. Their folder structure was immaculate: dive logs, sonar mosaics, photogrammetry renders, sample registers. They'd been mapping a deep site forty nautical miles east of where we'd found their camp, in water too deep for recreational anything. Container ship, sunk intact, sitting upright on the seabed at ninety meters. Hull number still legible on the stern in their video: a vessel that official registries listed as scrapped in a Bangladeshi breaking yard nine years ago.

It had not been scrapped. It had been loaded, sealed, and sunk.

The second thing that struck me was the cargo manifests they'd recovered from the wreck itself — scanned, indexed, cross-referenced. Shipping containers, forty-footers, dozens of them, all sealed with customs locks from a government agency whose acronym I recognized because it appears, with suspicious frequency, in the procurement documents of every project I've ever been paid to keep quiet about. Inside the containers, according to the surveyors' own inventory notes: paper. Physical paper. Files, ledgers, signed authorizations, financial records. Someone had gone to enormous expense and risk to put a mountain of incriminating paperwork beyond the reach of subpoenas, journalists, and history.

And then a survey team with side-scan sonar and too much competence had gone and found it anyway.

I opened their summary document at 0340. It was written in the dry, numbered prose of people trained to make findings defensible. Finding one: the vessel's sinking coincided within seventy-two hours of the incident twelve years ago — the ferry, the village coastline, the event the navy scrubbed from its own records, the one whose official death toll was revised downward three times until it became a footnote. Finding two: the manifests showed the same officials who signed the cover-up authorizations also signed the cargo sealing orders, using discretionary emergency powers, six days *before* the incident occurred.

Six days before. I read that line four times. You don't prepare evidence containers for a disaster that hasn't happened yet unless you're planning the disaster.

Finding three was worse. The surveyors had pulled personnel files from the containers — witness statements, depositions, names of people who had tried to testify after the incident and been discredited, relocated, or disappeared. There was a subfolder titled WITNESS INTIMIDATION LOG, and inside it, entries spanning years. Payments. Threats documented in the threateners' own handwriting. One entry referenced a junior analyst whose report had been rewritten before publication, whose signature appeared on a version of events she had flagged internally as false, and who had subsequently resigned and vanished from all records.

Her old name was right there in the metadata of a scanned memo.

I knew that name.

I have known Lena Lockridge for six years. Six years of shared watches and engine grease and the specific intimacy of people who live in a steel box on the ocean together. And in six years she has told me precisely nothing about her past, which I always took for the ordinary privacy of a private person. Now I sat in the engine room at four in the morning looking at a scanned internal memo bearing the name of a junior analyst who had existed twelve years ago and did not exist now, and I understood, with the slow cold certainty of a hull taking water, that I was reading the ghost of the woman who slept ten feet above my head.

I didn't open the rest of the witness files. That felt like a line, and I was already standing close enough to it to feel the heat.

What I did instead was verify. Because if I was going to walk up on deck and tell my captain that the missing survey team had found proof that a dozen senior officials had orchestrated an atrocity, sunk the evidence, and hunted down everyone who ever tried to speak — I needed to be sure it wasn't a fabrication planted for us to find. I checked hashes against the raw image. I checked the scan artifacts for signs of digital manipulation; there were none, because these were photographs of physical documents taken underwater, and forgery at that scale would require resources that make our entire operation look like a child's lemonade stand. I checked the surveyors' own working notes, their doubts and corrections, the marginal comment where one of them wrote *verify seal dates independently — if true this changes everything.*

They had verified them. Independently. Twice.

Then I opened the last file in the root directory, dated the day before the camp was abandoned. It was a draft transmission, unsent:

*We have confirmation. The Ledger is real and complete. Requesting immediate extraction — we believe our position may have been compromised. If extraction is delayed, we will move the copies separately. Do not trust—*

The message ended there. Whoever wrote it had deleted the rest, or never finished it, or been interrupted by whatever came for them. No tracks, no bodies, gear left behind, shoes by the tents. People who leave their shoes do not leave voluntarily. People who are taken leave their shoes because shoes take time.

I sat very still for a while, listening to the anchor chain murmur against the hull, and I thought about the shape of what I now knew. Knowledge is weight. Everyone says information wants to be free, but information doesn't want anything; it just sits there being heavy while the people who carry it decide what it costs. And this — this was the heaviest thing I had ever held. Heavier than the drive. Heavier than the boat.

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape: Okafor hadn't hired us to find the survey team. He'd hired us to find out what they'd found, and to bury it deeper than ninety meters. We were not the search party. We were the dredge. Every hour I'd spent restoring this drive, I'd been doing free work for the people who made these people disappear.

* * *

Lena was already awake when I came up. Of course she was. She stood at the rail in the gray pre-dawn with her hands around a mug she wasn't drinking, and when she heard me on the companionway steps she turned, and she already knew. You can't spend six years with someone and miss the way they hold their shoulders when the world has shifted under them.

"It mounted," I said. Unnecessary. She knew. "Ninety-one terabytes. Survey records, sonar surveys of a container ship that officially doesn't exist, manifests, financial authorizations, and a witness intimidation log going back twelve years." I paused. "Including a memo signed by a junior analyst whose name isn't hers anymore."

The mug trembled, very slightly, the way a compass needle trembles near iron. She didn't ask which memo. She didn't perform surprise. That was its own confession, and we both let it stand.

"They planned it before it happened," I said. "The containers were sealed six days early. Lena, that wasn't a cover-up. The cover-up was the plan. The incident was the disposal method."

"I know." Her voice was flat and steady, the voice she uses in bad weather. "I've known since the manifest. What I didn't know was whether anyone else had proof."

"Now there's proof. On this boat. In my head. And in yours." I leaned on the rail next to her, because my legs had developed opinions about standing. "Do you understand what we are now? We're not salvage crew anymore. We're the only copy of the thing they sank a ship to hide. Okafor didn't send us out here to find survivors. There are no survivors in his plan. There's a recovery operation, and we're the recovery mechanism, and when we hand over whatever we find, we become loose ends with sea time."

Ruiz appeared at the cockpit door. I genuinely don't know how long he'd been there; the man moves like ballast, silently and always at the bottom of things. He had his coffee, and his face had the set it gets before he says something nobody wants to hear.

"I heard most of that," he said. "The drive. The ship. The dates." He looked at Lena, and something passed between them that had clearly passed between them days ago, some earlier conversation I hadn't been invited to. Fine. I was used to being the last to know things; it's the engineer's tax. "So we agree. We don't call Okafor. We don't report in. We sit on this until we decide what it is."

"What it is," Lena said, "is a list of names. Including mine. Including—" she stopped.

"Including people from before," I finished for her, gently, because somebody had to say it out loud. "People you knew. People who might still be alive, or might be entries in that log."

She closed her eyes. For a moment, just a moment, she looked like what she probably was at twenty-four: a junior analyst in a fluorescent office, holding a report she knew was a lie, learning what institutions do to truth. Then she opened them and she was Lena Lockridge again, salvage diver, woman with no past, and the transformation frightened me more than the documents had.

"There's something else," I said. "The surveyors' last message. It cuts off mid-sentence. 'Do not trust' — and then nothing. They knew they were compromised, and they knew it was someone close enough to warn about. Which means whoever took them might already know about us. Might already be watching to see what we do with the drive."

Ruiz set his coffee down on the chart table with great care, the way he does when he's angry enough to want to throw it.

"Then we have a decision to make," he said, "and not much time to make it. Because there are only three kinds of people in this story now, Devi. The ones who buried the truth, the ones who dug it up, and the ones who are about to find out which they are."

Lena picked up the ziplock bag from the chart table and held the drive in her palm, this small dead-weight brick of aluminum and glass that weighed maybe a hundred grams and roughly nine hundred tons.

"We keep diving," she said finally. "But not for him. Not for Okafor. From here on, we dive for them." She meant the survey team. Or the witnesses. Or maybe the woman she used to be. With Lena it's often impossible to tell which ghost she's addressing, and I suspect she prefers it that way.

"And when Okafor calls?" Ruiz asked. "He will call. Tonight, probably. He'll want a status report."

Lena looked out at the eastern horizon, where the sky was going the color of old tin, and I watched her choose her answer the way you choose a course through a reef field — slowly, knowing that every option has teeth.

"We tell him the drive was corrupted," she said. "Unrecoverable. Salt damage, total loss. We tell him we found nothing but mud and dead laptops." She turned to me. "Can you make that true? Can you make it look destroyed?"

I took the drive back from her and turned it over in my hands. "I can make it look drowned," I said. "That's easy. Drowned is honest work."

Below us, the anchor chain swung quiet against the hull, and somewhere out past the reef, in water too deep to see, a ship full of secrets sat upright on the seabed, patient as only the drowned are patient. We had brought a piece of it aboard. Now the question — the one that would follow us across every mile of ocean between here and wherever we were going — was whether we'd brought a treasure or a curse.

By noon we had our answer. The radio crackled, and Okafor's voice came through, smooth as bilge oil, asking for our status.

And asking, casually, terribly, why our transponder had shown us drifting forty miles east of the survey site.

Toward the deep water.

Toward the ship.
