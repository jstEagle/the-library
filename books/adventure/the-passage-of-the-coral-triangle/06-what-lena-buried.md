## 6. What Lena Buried

The drive sat in Devi's workshop like a loaded thing, and I couldn't stop looking at it.

I told myself I was checking on her progress. That was a lie with good posture — I stood in the doorway of the engine room for the better part of an hour, watching her solder and swear at a logic board under a magnifying lamp, and she knew exactly why I was there because she'd stopped asking me to hand her things about twenty minutes in.

"You're hovering," she said, not looking up.

"I'm supervising."

"You're hovering *supervisingly*." She set down the iron and finally turned. "Lena. If you've got something to say about what's on this drive, say it. Otherwise go count the spare fuses or whatever it is you do when you're pretending to be useful."

I didn't have anything to say. What I had was a name I hadn't spoken aloud in twelve years, sitting behind my teeth like a fishhook, and every hour that drive spent healing itself was an hour closer to the moment it would tear its way out.

So instead of saying any of it, I went topside, took the first watch even though it wasn't mine, and let the sea do what it had always done for me, which was hold still long enough for me to pretend I was someone else.

It didn't work this time.

---

Her name was Elena Vasquez-Reyes.

Not Lockridge. Not yet. In the before-times I was Elena, junior analyst, Directorate of Maritime Intelligence, third desk from the window in a building in Manila that smelled permanently of toner and rain. Twenty-six years old, top of my intake class, convinced that the work mattered because the work was classified, and everyone knows classified things matter — that's practically the definition.

My job was shipping manifests. I read them the way other people read novels: fast, hungrily, looking for the lie underneath. Cargo that doesn't match the hull's draft. Tonnage that vanishes between ports. Fuel purchases that don't square with declared routes. The sea keeps books, and most people assume nobody audits them. Somebody has to.

Twelve years ago, somebody audited a stretch of water off the southern coast, and the books came back wrong in a way that made my hands go cold.

You have to understand what the incident was called before you understand anything else. Officially it was the *Sibuyan Interdiction*, which is the kind of name governments give to things they want filed and forgotten — clinical, geographic, bloodless. Nine fishing boats. A village that processed their catch. Four hundred and eleven people, give or take, though the give-or-take was itself part of the crime, because the dead were counted by satellite estimate rather than by name. The official finding was a navigational error during a live-fire exercise. Wrong coordinates, wrong time, tragic, regrettable, settled out of sight.

I wrote the report that made that finding stick.

Not the finding itself — I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last honest thing I own. My report said the exercise logs were internally consistent. My report said the fuel consumption data supported the official timeline. My report said, in the careful passive voice they teach you before they teach you anything else, that *no anomalies were identified warranting further inquiry.*

Every word of it was true. That's the part I need you to sit with, because I've had twelve years to sit with it myself. Every word was true, and the report was a lie, and the difference between those two facts is the whole shape of my life since.

Because here's what I actually found, three days before I filed: a requisition order for munitions dated nine days *before* the interdiction, signed through a procurement channel that didn't exist on any org chart I could find. Here's a logistics convoy rerouted away from the exercise area forty-eight hours ahead of schedule, on orders from an office whose name appeared nowhere else in the file. Here's a communications blackout window requested — requested, in writing, in advance — for the exact six hours the shells fell.

You don't request a blackout in advance for an accident. Accidents don't send invitations.

I sat with those documents for one night. One night, in my apartment, with the printouts spread across the floor like evidence in a movie, and I did the arithmetic that young idealists do: if I flag this, someone senior will investigate; the system works; the truth is a machine that runs if you feed it.

Then I went to my section chief, a soft-spoken man named Alcantara who kept bonsai trees on his windowsill and had once, I believed, been a good person, and I showed him the requisition order, and I watched his face do something terrible. Not shock. Recognition. He looked at the document the way you look at weather you've been expecting.

"Elena," he said, and he used my first name, which he never did, "what does your report say?"

"My report says—"

"What. Does your report. Say."

And I understood, in the slow cold way understanding arrives when it's too late to be useful, that the question wasn't about the report. It was about me. It was a door being held open, and the only alternative to walking through it was a kind of falling that I didn't yet have the imagination to picture.

"No anomalies were identified," I heard myself say, "warranting further inquiry."

He nodded, once, and picked up his watering can, and that was that. Three weeks later the report was cited in the closing memorandum. Six months later I was promoted. A year later I was transferred to a quiet annex where the work was real but harmless, where I audited fisheries treaties and port fees, where nothing I found could ever again matter enough to be dangerous.

They didn't punish me. They *promoted* me into irrelevance, which is worse, because punishment at least admits you saw something.

---

I lasted four more years in the annex.

People ask — well, no one asks, because no one knows, which is rather the point — but if anyone asked why I stayed four years after understanding what I was part of, the honest answer is shame. Shame is adhesive. It doesn't push you out the door; it glues you to the floor of the life you've compromised, because leaving means admitting what staying meant. Every morning I rode the ferry to work and looked at the water and thought: *they died in water like this.* Every evening I rode back and thought: *and you filed it as weather.*

The break, when it came, was almost absurdly small. A memo. Internal, routine, cc'd to me by accident — a distribution list error, the kind of thing that happens ten thousand times a day in bureaucracies everywhere. It referenced the Sibuyan file. It referenced *residual documentation concerns*. It recommended, in language so bland it was nearly poetry, that remaining records be consolidated and reviewed for retention.

Retention. As in: whether to keep them. As in: whether to burn them.

Four hundred and eleven people, and the last argument left was over a shredder schedule.

I printed the memo. I took it home. And then I did the only brave thing I did in eight years inside that building, which was also, in its way, the cowardliest: I resigned. Effective immediately, citing family reasons, no forwarding address. I didn't leak the memo. I didn't copy the files. I didn't call a journalist, a lawyer, a priest, anybody. I walked out with my severance and my silence intact, and I told myself that surviving was the priority, that a dead whistleblower helps no one, that I'd find another way.

I never found another way. I found diving instead.

---

Here is what the sea offers that institutions don't: it cannot be lied to, and it doesn't care who you were.

The first certification course cost most of my savings. The instructor was a leathery Australian named Col who asked no questions beyond what the liability waiver required, and when I descended for the first time in open water — really descended, past the light going green and then blue and then the color of held breath — something in my chest unclenched for the first time in eight years. Down there, nobody checks your credentials. Nobody asks what desk you sat at. The reef doesn't audit you. The wreck doesn't care. You are a mammal with a tank and a task, and the task is everything: breathe, equalize, look, return.

I worked dive boats up the Philippine coast, then Indonesia, then wherever the money moved. I learned engines from a Fijian mechanic who communicated mostly in profanity and torque specifications. I learned navigation from a woman named Ruiz — no relation to our Ruiz, or maybe distant relation, the sea is small that way — who taught me that charts are arguments with reality and reality usually wins, so bring a pencil.

And somewhere in those years, Elena Vasquez-Reyes thinned out and Lena Lockridge thickened up. It wasn't a single decision. It was accretion, the way coral builds: a lie here, a deflection there, a new surname bought from a fixer in Dili along with a passport whose photo was mine and whose history belonged to a girl from Portsmouth who'd supposedly gone to sea at seventeen. I practiced the accent until it stopped being practice. I gave myself parents who were comfortably dead. I built a woman whose worst secret was a bar tab.

The miles helped. That was the theory, anyway. Keep moving and the past can't catch up; keep the horizon full and there's no room in it for memory. I measured my life in nautical miles because miles don't ask questions. A mile is just a mile. It doesn't want to know what you did with your twenties.

Devi thinks I'm private. Ruiz thinks I'm disciplined. Both of them are describing the same wall from different sides.

---

The trouble with burying something at sea is that the sea gives things back.

Divers know this. We work wrecks, and wrecks are graves that erode. Hulls collapse, silt shifts, storms rearrange the furniture of the deep, and one season you're swimming a corridor and the next season the corridor is open and there's a skeleton in the galley wearing boots you recognize. The ocean is not a vault. It's a slow witness. Everything goes down eventually, and everything comes up eventually, and the only variable is who's holding the light when it does.

I should have known. God help me, I *did* know — it's the first lesson of the trade and I'd taught it to green deckhands a hundred times, smiling, easy, nothing personal in it: *the sea returns what it's given.* I just never applied it to myself. I thought I'd buried my old name deeper than salvage depth. I thought the sediment had closed over it.

Then Devi's screen flickered in the engine room and threw a fragment of restored text against the bulkhead, and I read two lines over her shoulder — *container manifest, sealed hold, Sibuyan-adjacent grid reference* — and the floor of my chest dropped out the way it does on a bad descent, that lurch where the pressure finds a space you didn't seal.

Because it wasn't just the officials. Of course it wasn't just the officials. The Ledger — whatever it fully contained, whatever Devi was coaxing out of corrupted sectors byte by patient byte — wouldn't stop at the men who ordered the shells. Files like that are sediment too. They settle in layers. At the bottom of every cover-up, under the admirals and the ministers and the fixers, there's a stratum of small hands. Junior analysts. Section chiefs with bonsai trees. People who saw the requisition order and said *no anomalies were identified* because the door was open and the fall was unimaginable and the promotion was right there.

Alcantara is dead now — heart attack, seven years ago, retired and comfortable, and I drank a toast to him alone in a bar in Bitung and hated myself for the toast. But I'm alive. I'm alive and I'm on this boat and I'm the last living hand that touched the original file, and if that drive holds what Devi thinks it holds, then somewhere in its layers is a report with my signature on it. *No anomalies were identified.* The sentence I built my escape on, coming up from the deep on somebody else's line.

Ruiz appeared beside me at the rail. I don't know how long he'd been standing there; he has that talent, arriving without displacement, like a large calm animal.

"Devi says another six hours," he said. "Maybe eight."

"Okay."

"Lena." He watched the water instead of me, which I've always appreciated about him. "Whatever's on that drive — whatever it's going to say about the survey team, about Okafor, about all of it — you're allowed to have a history. Everybody on this boat has one. Mine's got a harbor in it I'll never sail into again. That's not the problem."

"What's the problem, then?"

"The problem is if your history and that drive are the same thing, and you decide to handle that alone." He shrugged, vast and unhurried. "You've been handling things alone since I met you. It works fine until it doesn't, and then it works catastrophically."

I wanted to tell him. The words were right there, twelve years downstream, bumping against the back of my teeth where the fishhook sits. *My name isn't Lockridge. I wrote the report. I'm in the file.*

What I said was: "Six hours. I'll make coffee."

He looked at me for a moment — one of his long looks, the kind that reads drafts you didn't submit — and then nodded and went below, and I stood my watch alone with the sea doing its patient witness act all around us, giving nothing back yet, holding everything.

Eight hours. Maybe less.

Somewhere under the keel, in corrupted sectors and sealed holds and drowned villages, everything I'd buried was already rising, and I stood at the rail of my own carefully constructed life and felt it lift beneath me like a hull coming off the bottom — slowly, then all at once, and no chart in the world showing me the channel out.
