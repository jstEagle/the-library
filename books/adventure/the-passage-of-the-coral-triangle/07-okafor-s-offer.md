## 7. Okafor's Offer

The fuel dock at Pulau Tiga looked like every fuel dock in the Coral Triangle: rusted drums, a shack with a hand-painted price list that changed with the tide and the owner's mood, a dog asleep in a wheelbarrow. What it didn't look like was a place where a man in a pressed linen shirt would be waiting for us.

But there he was. Sitting on an upturned crate beside our berth like he'd been invited to lunch, ankles crossed, reading something on a tablet as if the heat were a rumor he'd declined to believe. Two men stood behind him with the studied boredom of professionals who wanted you to know they were bored on purpose.

"Captain Ruiz," Okafor said, standing, extending a hand Ruiz didn't take. "And this must be Ms. Lockridge. The diver."

"The client," I said. "Come to inspect your investment?"

"Come to make you a better one." He smiled the way accountants smile — everything balanced, nothing felt. "May I come aboard? Your deck looks sturdier than this dock."

Ruiz glanced at me. Devi, who'd been coiling hose on the foredeck, had gone very still in the way she did when she was listening to machinery or to people, and both were about to fail. I thought about saying no. I thought about what saying no would cost, and what saying yes might, and decided — the way I'd been deciding for twelve years — to buy time first and pay later.

"Mind the rail," I said. "It bites."

* * *

Okafor settled into the cockpit cushion like he owned the boat's mortgage. He accepted coffee he didn't drink. He complimented the vessel — "a fine old girl, well kept" — in the tone of a man appraising collateral. Up close he smelled of nothing at all, which struck me as its own kind of expensive. Most men who work the islands smell of diesel or sweat or sunscreen. Okafor smelled of climate control.

"You've been to the camp," he said.

"We recovered what we could find," Ruiz said carefully.

"And what did you find?"

"Gear," Ruiz said. "Tents. Food stores. No people."

"No bodies," Okafor agreed pleasantly. "That was in the contract, I believe. No bodies, no questions." He turned the tablet face-up on his knee but didn't look at it. "The government is prepared to close out the contract early. Full payment, plus a completion bonus. And a further offer."

Devi came down from the bow and leaned against the mast, arms folded, radiating the specific hostility of an engineer whose equipment has been insulted. Nobody had insulted her equipment yet, but she liked to get ahead of things.

"What kind of further offer?" I asked.

"Triple," Okafor said. "The original fee, tripled, paid today, wired before we finish this conversation if you like. In exchange, you hand over any data storage devices recovered from the site — drives, cards, anything electronic — and you forget the survey team existed. You never saw the island. You never took the job. As far as anyone will ever know, you're three salvagers who found a wreck off Sulawesi and went home."

The dog on the dock barked once at nothing, and the sound seemed very loud.

"And the survey team?" I asked. "The six people whose shoes are still lined up outside their tents?"

"There is no survey team," Okafor said, with the mild patience of a man correcting a child's arithmetic. "There was a hydrographic exercise. It concluded. Personnel have been reassigned." He let that sit. "You're not being asked to lie, Ms. Lockridge. You're being paid to agree with the official record. There's a difference, and the difference is worth a great deal of money."

I looked at Ruiz. Ruiz looked at the horizon, which was doing its usual excellent impression of indifference. Devi was looking at me, and her look said: *don't*. Or possibly *do*, depending on which version of Devi was driving. With her it was always hard to tell until the invoice arrived.

"Triple," I said. "That's generous for gear and no questions."

"I'm authorized to be generous."

"Who authorizes you?"

He smiled again, warmer this time, and that was worse. "Let's talk about you instead."

* * *

Here's the thing about building a person out of nautical miles: it works beautifully right up until someone produces a blueprint.

Lena Lockridge had eleven years of paper behind her — certifications earned under a name that matched a birth certificate that matched a passport that matched a woman who had, as far as any database could prove, always existed. I'd built her slowly, the way you build a reef: grain by grain, in water nobody watches. I knew her history better than my own, because I'd written hers and only lived mine.

So when Okafor said, lightly, "You dive very well for someone who didn't exist before 2013," my hands stayed flat on the table and my pulse did not, and I hoped the two facts cancelled each other out.

"That's a strange thing to say," I said.

"It's a strange thing to know," he agreed. "And yet here we are." He tapped the tablet once, not turning it around. "I'm not threatening you, Ms. Lockridge. Truly. If I wanted to use what I know, I wouldn't be sitting on your boat drinking bad coffee. I'd simply mention your former employer's former file number to three people, and you'd be dealing with consequences far less pleasant than me." He spread his hands. "I'm offering you the opposite. A clean slate, maintained. Triple pay. And my discretion, permanently, as part of the package."

"And all I have to do," I said, "is give you a drive I may or may not have, and forget six human beings."

"Forget a clerical error," he corrected gently. "Ms. Lockridge — may I call you Lena? — I've read your file. Both of them." He paused to let that land, and it landed like a hook through the palm. "The second one says you're a competent professional with an unremarkable career. The first one says—" he stopped himself, almost delicately, "—well. The first one explains why you live on a boat and measure your life in fuel stops. I understand escape. I respect it. I'm offering to protect yours. That's more than most people in my position would do."

The wind shifted. Somewhere down the dock the dog had lost interest in existence again. I became aware that my thumb was pressing a crescent into the tabletop and made it stop.

"What was in the drive?" I asked. "If there were a drive."

"Lena." He said my assumed name like a man handling borrowed property. "Don't do this. Don't make me insult us both. You're a smart woman who got out of something bad by becoming someone new. I am offering to keep that transaction intact. All I want is a corrupted fragment of a hard drive that even your engineer can't fully recover — and she can't, by the way; we know her capabilities too — and your signature on a completion form. Then you sail away rich, anonymous, and safe. Everyone gets to remain exactly who they've decided to be." He leaned back. "Isn't that what you want? To remain?"

And God help me, some animal part of me — the part that had spent twelve years swimming away from a single bad year — said *yes*. Said it loudly. Said: take the money, take the silence, take the guarantee that the walls you built stay up. Six strangers' shoes by six tents versus the whole architecture of your life. It wasn't even a hard calculation. That was the shameful part. It wasn't hard at all.

But another part — smaller, newer, grown sometime between the manifest stamped with impossible dates and the tents with no tracks leading out of them — said: *you already opened the door. You read the dates. You know what 'reassigned' means when there are no boats leaving the island.*

I didn't answer him. I stalled, because stalling was the only move that belonged to both women at once.

"You'll have my answer tomorrow," I said. "We fuel at dawn. I don't sign anything with salt on my hands."

Okafor studied me for a long moment, and whatever he saw, he apparently decided was negotiable, because he nodded and stood. "Tomorrow, then. Dawn." At the rail he paused, one hand on the biting metal, and looked back with something that was almost kindness, which from him was the most frightening thing yet. "For what it's worth — the woman in the first file? She made the right call back then, given what she knew. This is the same choice, Lena. Just with better numbers." He stepped down onto the dock. "Sleep on it. But sleep on the right side of it."

The two bored men fell in behind him. The dog watched them go without comment.

* * *

Nobody spoke until the launch carrying them cleared the point. Then Devi said, "He knows your name."

"He knows a name," I said.

"He knows *the* name. The real one." She dropped into the cockpit bench across from me, elbows on knees. "Which means somebody gave it to him, which means somebody's been looking, which means this stopped being a salvage job about four days ago and started being something else, and we're just now getting the memo."

Ruiz came down from the helm where he'd been pretending to check the barometer. "How much do we actually have?"

I looked at him. "What?"

"The drive. The fragment. Whatever Devi pulled off the survey camp's equipment." His voice was level, captain-level, the voice he used for weather. "Because if we're going to sell it, we should know what we're selling. And if we're going to refuse a man who knows who you used to be, we should know what we're refusing him *for*."

The question hung there, and I realized with a small cold shock that I hadn't actually answered it for myself. I'd been carrying the fragment the way you carry a splinter — aware of it, working around it, not digging. Because digging meant knowing. Knowing meant the choice stopped being abstract.

"Restore it," I said to Devi. "All of it. Tonight."

Devi's eyebrows went up. "You sure? Once I crack it, we can't un-know it. And un-knowing is literally what that man is selling."

"I know what he's selling." I looked out at the channel, where the light was going bronze and the fishing boats were coming home in their loose scattered constellations, ordinary lives heading toward ordinary dinners. "I want to know what it costs."

Ruiz exhaled through his nose. "And the money? Triple, Lena. That's a new engine, new compressor, two years of not taking jobs from men like him."

"Two years," I said, "until a man like him finds something else of ours to hold." I stood, needing to move, needing rope in my hands. "Get the generator running. I'll spell you at midnight."

Devi was already halfway to the engine room, laptop bag over her shoulder, muttering the way she did when she was happy and terrified in equal measure — which, for Devi, was her natural resting state. Ruiz lingered.

"You could still take it," he said quietly. "The money. I'd understand. I need you to know I'd understand."

"I know you would." I coiled the hose line, watching my own hands do it, hands that had signed reports once under a different name. "That's what scares me. How easy it would be."

He nodded slowly and went below. The generator coughed, caught, settled into its familiar heartbeat. Down in the engine room, Devi began the quiet surgery of pulling truth out of a dead machine, and I sat in the cockpit keeping watch on a darkening harbor, listening to the dog bark at the moon, waiting to find out which woman I'd been protecting all these years — and which one was about to walk out of the water.

At 0300, Devi's voice came up the companionway, stripped of all its usual irony.

"Lena. You need to see this. And then you need to tell me why your old name is in this file."
