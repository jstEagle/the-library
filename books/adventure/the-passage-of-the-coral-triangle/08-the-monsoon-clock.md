## 8. The Monsoon Clock

I've been running boats for thirty-one years, and in that time I've learned that the sea doesn't kill people. People kill people. The sea just holds the door open and watches.

The morning after the black hull vanished into the fishing traffic off Kabaena, I spread my charts on the mess table of the *Mariana's Wake* and did what captains do when the math turns ugly: I made coffee strong enough to strip paint, and I waited for my crew to stop pretending they were sleeping.

Devi came up first. She always does. The woman treats sleep like a rumor she's heard but never confirmed. She had the drive fragment in a waterproof case slung across her chest like a bandolier, which was theatrical, but I let it go. People need their rituals when they're scared. Mine is coffee. Hers is carrying evidence against her sternum like it might get cold.

Lena came up last, which was also predictable. Lena moves through a boat the way a cat moves through a house — silently, along the edges, appearing only when she's decided to be seen. Twelve years I've worked with her, give or take, and I still couldn't tell you her mother's name or where she learned to hold her breath for four minutes. I stopped asking around year three. Some people are lighthouses. Lena is a submarine.

"Show me," she said.

So I showed her.

* * *

Here's the brutal math, laid out on a table scarred by twenty years of chart weights and spilled rum, for three people who had exactly one thing in common: none of us could go back to port and pretend this was a normal job anymore.

The dry season was dying. You could feel it in the water — warmer, greener, lazier — and you could see it in the sky over Ambon way, where the horizon had gone the color of an old bruise. In six weeks, maybe five, the monsoon trough would slide down from the north and slam the archipelago shut like a book. After that, the straits between the islands turn into washing machines with teeth. No charter runs them. No navy patrol runs them. Nobody runs them except the desperate and the dead, and after a while even the dead stop trying.

Six weeks. That was the clock.

"Option one," I said, tapping the eastern route with a pencil stub. "We go back to Bitung, hand the drive to whatever government office will take it, collect our pay, and pretend we never saw anything."

"Absolutely not," Devi said.

"I'm listing options, not taking votes."

"The government office would be the same government that scrubbed the incident," Devi said. "The same government whose decommissioned hull tried to run us down at two in the morning. We'd be handing them the confession and the confessor in one envelope."

"She's right," Lena said quietly. "It goes in the shredder before we clear the harbor."

There it was. Option one, dead on arrival, killed by its own logic. I crossed it out because crossing things out makes you feel like a man who controls his circumstances.

"Option two." I moved the pencil west. "We keep the drive, we sit tight somewhere neutral — Halmahera, maybe — and we wait out the season. Storms pass. Waters reopen. We deliver in spring."

"And in the meantime?" Devi said.

"In the meantime, the people who own a decommissioned black hull know we have it. They don't have a deadline. We'd be sitting in a harbor for five months waiting for them to get organized, and they are *already* organized. That's the whole problem with them."

Lena turned her coffee mug slowly on the table, not looking at either of us. "They're not waiting now," she said. "Why would they wait longer?"

Exactly. Option two wasn't an option; it was a slow-motion surrender with extra steps. I crossed it out too.

"That leaves option three."

I put the pencil on the western route, the long one, the one that looked on paper like a drunkard's signature — down through the Buton straits, across the Flores Sea, threading the Sape Strait, then the final open-water push to the island where a man named Arif Santoso ran a small independent newsroom out of a building that doubled as a dive shop, because in this part of the world the truth needs a cover business the way a reef shark needs remoras.

Arif was neutral. Not brave-neutral, not heroic-neutral — *commercially* neutral, which is stronger. He took documents from anyone, verified everything twice, and published what survived verification. Governments hated him and needed him in equal measure, which meant he was still breathing. If the Ledger reached Arif with enough time for his people to mirror it outside the country, it stopped being something we carried and became something that existed everywhere at once. You can sink a boat. You can't sink a hundred thousand downloads.

"How far?" Devi asked.

"Eleven hundred nautical miles, call it twelve with the doglegs. At our honest cruising speed, nine days of steaming if nothing breaks. Something always breaks, so say twelve."

"And fuel?"

"We can make it on two stops. One of them is friendly." I tapped a dot south of Buru. "The other one is the problem."

The problem had a name, though nobody used it in print. The fleet. Maybe fourteen boats, maybe twenty, ranging from refitted trawlers to fast gunboats that had been somebody's navy surplus before being somebody else's nightmare. They ran the waters between Buru and Seram like a private tax authority, and their tolls weren't paid in currency. They took cargo, engines, sometimes crew. Every skipper in the archipelago gave those waters a berth wide enough to sail a cathedral through.

"So we don't go through," Devi said. "We go around. South of Timor—"

"Adds eight days and puts us in open Indian Ocean swell during a transitional monsoon. And the southern approaches are where the navy actually patrols properly, because that's where they expect smugglers. We'd trade pirates for the people who already tried to kill us once." I shrugged. "Pick your poison. One bottle's labeled, the other isn't."

Silence. The kind of silence a boat makes when everyone aboard is doing arithmetic they hate.

"There's a fourth option," Lena said.

I knew what was coming. I'd known since the night before, when I saw her standing at the rail at 0300 watching the dark water where the black hull had been, and she wasn't afraid. I've stood next to scared people at rails. Fear has a smell, a posture, a vibration. What Lena had was something else. Something colder and more dangerous.

"You want to go through the fleet," I said.

"I want to *consider* going through the fleet."

"Considered. Answer's no."

"It's not your answer to give alone, Ruiz."

"No," I agreed. "But it's my boat, and my name on the insurance, and my fifty-one years of knees, and I'm telling you the fleet doesn't negotiate. They're not smugglers with a code. They're scavengers with guns."

"They're people," Lena said. "People can be read. Boats with registrations that don't exist can't."

And there it was — the difference between us, laid out neat as a chart correction. I'd spent my career avoiding trouble by knowing which waters held it. Lena had spent hers assuming trouble was already aboard, wearing her face, and the only question was whether you met it running or standing.

* * *

We didn't decide anything that morning. Good captains know that decisions made before breakfast are just fears wearing uniforms. So I sent Devi below to keep torturing the corrupted drive — she'd gotten fragments working, enough to know there was something called the Ledger and that it mattered, not enough to know why — and I went topside to do the thing I actually trust myself to do: look at weather.

The sky over the Banda Sea told me what it always tells me in late October, if you speak its dialect. The trades were getting confused, arriving from directions they had no business arriving from. The barometer on the bulkhead had started doing a slow stutter-step downward, half a millibar a day, nothing dramatic, just the atmosphere clearing its throat. Somewhere north of us, over the Philippines probably, the first real depression of the season was spinning itself together out of warm water and bad luck. It wouldn't come here directly. It didn't need to. It would drag the whole intertropical front down behind it like a bride dragging a dress, and every strait in the archipelago would fill with wind-driven chaos, and the safe windows would close one by one, starting with the northern ones.

Weather doesn't chase you. It just closes doors at a steady, indifferent pace until you're standing in the last one.

I did the numbers again, because numbers are honest even when plans aren't. Nine days' steaming minimum. Two fuel stops, each costing half a day if nothing went wrong. A strait crossing through pirate water that could cost anywhere from an hour to everything. And behind us, unknown but certainly nonzero, whatever assets the people with the black hull could bring to bear once they stopped underestimating a salvage crew in a forty-year-old survey vessel.

Twelve days of margin against a six-week window. It fit. Barely. The way a coffin fits.

That's when I understood the real shape of it, the thing I'd been circling all morning without landing on. This wasn't a delivery job anymore. It was a race with two clocks — the monsoon closing from ahead, the hunters closing from behind — and both clocks were faster than they looked, and we were the only thing on the board moving in the right direction.

Grim resolve, my old instructor at the maritime academy used to call it, back when he was teaching us navigation and I was learning that I liked being wet. Grim resolve is what you feel when every option is bad but one is merely survivable, and you choose it, and you keep choosing it, hour after hour, because stopping means letting someone else pick for you.

I went below and told them we were going through.

* * *

Devi took it like an engineer takes a design flaw: immediately looking for workarounds. "If we transit the fleet's core area at night, dark ship, no lights, no radar reflector—"

"They've got thermal," I said. "Half those boats were coast-guard cutters before they were stolen. They'll see our engine heat from six miles."

"Then we run cold. Drift through on the tide with engines off, inside a fishing fleet's clutter. There's always fishing traffic on the Buru side — dozens of bancas, all of them warm, all of them moving slow. We become one more smear on their screen."

I looked at her for a long moment. "You've been thinking about this."

"Since you said 'the fleet' out loud, yes. Thinking is my function on this boat. Yours is steering and pessimism."

"My function is keeping everyone alive, and pessimism is the tool."

"Then use it on the plan instead of against it," she said, and I had to admit the girl had a point, which annoyed me, which is usually how I know.

Lena said almost nothing through all of it. She sat at the end of the table with the chart light making shadows of her face, and when we finally looked at her — because you always end up looking at Lena, the way you end up looking at deep water — she said only: "Whoever leads that fleet, find out who they are before we're in their guns' arc. People join fleets like that for reasons. Reasons can be used."

"You sound like you've negotiated with pirates before," I said.

She gave me the half-smile that answers nothing. "I've negotiated with worse, Ruiz. Pirates at least tell you what they want."

* * *

We cleared the anchorage at dusk, running east-southeast toward the Buton straits with the last of the daylight gilding the water, and I stood my watch alone because I'd ordered everyone else below. Not sentiment. Discipline. A captain's face at the start of a passage sets the crew's weather for a thousand miles, and mine wasn't ready for viewing yet.

Because here's the truth I hadn't said at the table, the truth underneath the routes and the fuel math and the tactical nonsense about drifting cold through hostile water:

I was afraid. Properly afraid, for the first time in a decade. Not of the pirates — pirates are a business risk, and I've priced business risks my whole life. I was afraid of what we were carrying, and what it meant, and the fact that somewhere out in that darkening water were men paid to make sure it never reached a printing press, and that they were better funded, better armed, and better informed than three people on an aging survey boat with a corrupted hard drive and a conscience.

And underneath the fear, quieter and worse, was this: I wasn't turning back. I'd done the math, and the math said the only survivable route ran straight through the worst water in the archipelago, and some stubborn old engine room in my chest had accepted it without a vote. Grim resolve. My instructor would have been proud. Or horrified. With him it was hard to tell the difference.

At 2100, Devi came up uninvited, carrying two cups of coffee and the waterproof case.

"You should see this," she said. "Before the straits. Before any of it. I got another fragment restored tonight, and Ruiz—" She paused, and Devi never pauses. "Ruiz, there are names in it. Names of witnesses. And dates. And one of the dates matches something Lena mentioned once, years ago, drunk, in Sorong. Something about a report she wrote. Or didn't write."

The coffee smelled burnt. Everything smells burnt when the world tilts.

"Get her," I said.

Devi shook her head slowly. "I think you should both hear it together. And Ruiz — watch her face when we play it. Because I've been watching her face for two days now, and I don't think our diver is who she says she is. I think whoever she really is, the Ledger knows her name."

Out past the headland, the first gust of the changing season came down off the hills and slapped the water flat, and somewhere far to the north, invisible and patient, the monsoon began packing its bags.
