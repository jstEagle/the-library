## 9. Smoke Over Ambon

I have a theory about ports, which is that every one of them is the same port wearing a different shirt. There's the fuel dock, the chandlery that smells of diesel and despair, the bar where expatriates go to be sad in public, and the customs officer whose stamp costs extra if you're in a hurry. Ambon had all of it, plus a volcano looming over the bay like a chaperone who'd seen everything and disapproved of most of it.

We came in on the morning tide with the *Nusantara Star* running clean for the first time in four days, because I had finally convinced Ruiz that the port-side injector was not "character" but a mechanical component with opinions. Lena stood at the bow with her arms folded, doing the thing she does where she looks at a harbor and reads it like a page. She'd been reading pages differently since the Ledger. Quieter. The way you get quiet when you've found out the floor of your house is thinner than you thought.

"We need fuel, water, and a rebuild kit for the starboard raw-water pump," Ruiz said, ticking items on his fingers. "And someone should check on the injector I definitely did not call character."

"And we need to be invisible," Lena said.

"In Ambon," Ruiz said, "that's a relative term. Everyone here is hiding from something. Usually an ex-wife or the tax office. We'll fit right in."

I said nothing, because I was busy having the feeling. You have to understand: I am the engineer, and engineers do not traffic in feelings, we traffic in torque values and failure modes. But I had a failure mode in my chest that morning, a low-grade vibration that said *someone is looking for you and they are not looking to shake your hand.* It started the night of the black-hulled boat and it hadn't stopped. It was like a bearing about to go — you can't point to the exact moment it'll seize, but you know it will, and you know it'll be at the worst possible time.

So when we tied up at the fuel dock and a man in a clean white polo shirt — clean being the operative word, because nobody in a working harbor wears white — asked Ruiz if we needed "assistance with provisions," I made a note of his face. Not handsome, not ugly. The kind of face that's been designed to be forgotten. He had boat shoes that had never seen a boat.

"Just fuel," Ruiz said cheerfully, in the tone of a man who has never once been interesting.

"Long passage?" the man asked.

"Island hopping. My wife gets seasick, so we stop a lot." Ruiz jerked a thumb at Lena, who gave the man a smile that could have curdled milk at forty paces. It was, for her, a masterpiece of wifely warmth.

The man smiled back, said something pleasant about the weather, and walked off toward the chandlery at the pace of a man with nowhere to be, which is the fastest pace there is.

"Boat shoes," I said.

"I saw."

"Nobody owns boat shoes in Ambon."

"I saw, Devi."

* * *

Our harbor contact was a woman named Ibu Rahayu, who ran a parts business out of a corrugated shed near the fish market and who had once, in a previous life I never asked about, moved things across the Ceram Sea that did not appear on manifests. Ruiz had known her for fifteen years, which in their economy meant he owed her a favor and she owed him a favor, and the two debts had achieved a kind of nuclear balance that neither dared disturb.

She looked at the list Ruiz handed her — pump kit, injectors, filters, and, because Ruiz was Ruiz, a case of the good instant coffee — and then looked at us over her reading glasses.

"You've brought weather with you," she said.

"Only the usual," Ruiz said.

"The man in the white shirt was at my shed yesterday." She said it the way you'd report the tide. "Asking if I serviced foreign dive boats. I told him I service fishing boats and fools, and he was welcome to be either."

Lena went very still. It's a particular stillness, hers. Most people freeze like prey. Lena freezes like a scope zeroing.

"Did he say who he was with?" she asked.

"He said he was with a marine survey company." Ibu Rahayu took off her glasses and folded them with great care. "He had a government lanyard in his bag. He thought I didn't see it. Ibu Rahayu sees everything that comes into this harbor, including the things that think they're invisible." Her eyes moved to me. "You. The tall one with the hands. You look like a woman who has seen a ghost and is trying to pretend it was a draft."

"Drafts," I said, "can kill you if they're coming off the right glacier."

That got the closest thing to a smile she permitted herself. Then she took the list, said the parts would take until tomorrow afternoon, and told us — in the tone of a woman discussing the price of mackerel — that if anyone came looking, we were a charter out of Kupang doing coral surveys for a German university, and the German was a Mr. Brandt who was currently ashore being drunk, and would we please act like it.

"I can't act drunk," Lena said. "It's undignified."

"You're a marine biologist," I said. "Dignity left the building when you chose the wetsuit."

* * *

We spent the day doing the things boats do in port, which is to say everything broke at once. The galley pump seized. The anchor windlass developed a stutter I diagnosed as electrical and Ruiz diagnosed as "the boat being dramatic," and we compromised by me fixing the electrical fault while he agreed the boat was also dramatic. I found myself working with one ear cocked to the dock, cataloguing footsteps the way I catalogue engine sounds. Diesel generator: fine. Bare feet on planks: fisherman. Leather soles, unhurried, pausing twice: not a fisherman.

By late afternoon there were three of them working the harbor, and I'd have bet the boat they were working a grid. One at the fuel dock, one at the chandlery, one drifting along the quay with the aimless gait of a man who had been told to look at boats and was looking at boats with tremendous professional commitment. None of them came near us. That was the part I didn't like. If they'd wanted us found, we'd have been found — we were sitting in plain sight with a hull you could pick out of a lineup. They were confirming. Building a picture. Deciding whether to do it loud or quiet.

"They know we're here," I told Lena and Ruiz that evening in the cockpit, under the awning, in the low voices people use when the walls have ears and the walls are water. "They've known since we cleared the outer marker. They're just not sure which boat, or they're waiting for something."

"Waiting for orders," Lena said. "Okafor doesn't improvise."

"You say that like it's a compliment."

"It's an observation. Improvisers make mistakes. Okafor makes appointments." She turned her beer bottle slowly on the chart table, not drinking. "Which means whatever happens next, it'll happen on a schedule. We should be somewhere else when it does."

"We can't be somewhere else until tomorrow afternoon," Ruiz said. "The pump kit. The injectors. We run on what we have, and what we have is a boat that dies at the first sign of sustained pursuit, which — call it a hunch — may be relevant."

"So we spend one more night," I said, "and we leave the moment Rahayu hands over the parts. Dawn plus ten hours."

Nobody said what we were all thinking, which was that one more night was a long time, and that the sea around a harbor at night is full of black-hulled boats that are officially decommissioned.

* * *

The fire started at eleven forty.

I know because I was in the engine room, which is where I go when I can't sleep, because engine rooms are honest. Everything in there is exactly what it is. A bearing is a bearing. A leak is a leak. Nothing in an engine room has ever pretended to be a marine survey company.

The first sign was the light — a dirty orange flicker through the engine room vents, the wrong color for sodium lamps. Then the smell, and I was up the ladder and out on deck before my brain had finished the sentence, because a fire in a harbor is a fire in a wooden throat, and every boat in that bay was a bead on the same string.

The fish market annex was burning. Not the market itself — the long storage shed behind it, the one full of ice, Styrofoam boxes, and diesel for the fishing fleet's gensets. Flames stood up out of its roof like something being born. And the harbor had come alive the way harbors do, which is badly: horns, running feet, someone's dog losing its mind, a fishing boat backing out of its berth with no lights and no plan, and everywhere men shouting in Ambonese Malay, which has a rhythm that makes even rescue sound like an argument.

And here is the thing I will remember until I die, or until something replaces the memory with a worse one: in the middle of all that light and noise, three men in dark clothing came down the quay toward our boat. Unhurried. The fire behind them turned their faces orange and their silhouettes black, and they moved through the crowd like the crowd was water and they were oil. One of them was boat-shoes man. I'd know that walk anywhere — the walk of a man whose shoes have never been wet.

"They're coming," I said. "Starboard side. Three."

Ruiz was already moving, killing the deck lights, and Lena was at the rail with a boat hook held low and casual, the way you hold a thing when you want it to be the last thing anyone notices. I dropped into the engine room and did the only useful thing I know how to do, which is make the boat ready to be a boat: batteries on, fuel valves open, the starboard injector — the dramatic one — coaxed into a state of readiness it had no right to achieve.

The three men stopped ten meters off our bow. Just stopped. One raised a phone, and I realized with a lurch that we were being photographed, documented, confirmed — the appointment being made. And then the fire reached the fuel drums.

I want to be precise about this, because later everyone had a version and none of them matched. The shed was full of genset diesel in drums, and drums fail the way drums fail — not with an explosion, exactly, but with a *whump* and a rain of burning rain, a fat orange blossom of flame that stood up over the fish market and lit the whole eastern half of the bay like a stage. The concussion rattled our windows. Somewhere a man was screaming that his boat was tied on the wrong side. And in the ten seconds when every eye in Ambon was on that blossom of fire — every eye including, crucially, the three at our bow — Ruiz slipped us off the dock.

No lights. No engine noise above idle. He let the current take us off the berth stern-first, quiet as a thought, and the three men turned back from the fire to find our berth empty and our wake already dissolving into the general chaos of a harbor full of boats doing stupid things in emergencies. We were one more silhouette. One more boat fleeing the fire, like everyone else. The best camouflage in the world is a crowd, and Ambon had generously provided one.

We were two hundred meters out, threading between anchored praus, when the second drum went, and the sound of it followed us out into the dark like a hand on the back.

* * *

We anchored at three in the morning off a nameless beach on the south side of the bay, close enough to see the fire still smoldering on the horizon, a red line under the black like a wound that wouldn't close. Nobody slept. We sat in the cockpit drinking Ruiz's good instant coffee, which Rahayu had thrown aboard with the parts — she'd delivered early, an hour before the fire, which I did not think about at the time because I was not yet thinking clearly, and which I would think about for a long time afterward.

"Who set it?" Ruiz asked, finally.

Nobody answered, because nobody knew. That was the thing that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. It could have been the hunters, torching the harbor to flush us out — but that made no sense, because the fire had *covered* our escape, not forced it. It could have been Rahayu, burning her own annex to give us our window, which made a horrible kind of sense, because she'd known they were asking about us, and a woman like Ibu Rahayu solves problems with the tools she has. Or it could have been an accident, a bad wire, a careless cigarette, the universe flipping a coin — and if it was an accident, then the timing was coincidence, and I don't believe in coincidences that convenient, and neither did anyone sitting in that cockpit.

"The parts," I said, because someone had to say something practical. "She got us the pump kit and the injectors before the shed burned. Everything else — filters, the spare impellers, the genset spares, the watermaker membrane I was going to beg off her — that was all in the annex."

"So we have a boat that runs," Ruiz said slowly, "and no spare parts. One failure and we're done."

"One failure and we're done," I confirmed. "I can keep her alive with improvisation for a while. But improvisation has a half-life."

Lena was looking at the fire line. "And Rahayu," she said. "If she set it — or even if she didn't. They'll know she helped us. The man in the white shirt was in her shed yesterday. They'll come back with questions, and Rahayu doesn't answer questions, but she's seventy years old and her business is ash."

"She's not seventy," Ruiz said. "She's sixty-eight and she'd lie about it."

"Ruiz."

"I know." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I know. First the survey team, then the harbor master's cousin who looked the other way for us at the last fuel stop, now Rahayu. Everyone who touches this starts paying. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked — that's the whole point of the Ledger, isn't it? It's not just that they killed people twelve years ago. It's that they've been paying people to forget ever since, and the people who won't forget get billed."

The fire flickered on the horizon. I thought about the annex — about the smell of it, diesel and fish ice and old wood, about the shelf where she kept the parts for Ruiz's boats going back fifteen years, each one labeled in her handwriting, because she was that kind of woman, a woman who labeled things, who kept order in a place that had none. And I thought about the phone raised at our bow, and the appointment being made.

"We should go back," I said. It came out before I'd decided to say it, the way the true things do. "At first light. Check on her. If she needs to move, we move her. If her shed burned, we — I don't know. We owe her more than a wake."

"We owe her our lives and we can't pay it by sailing back into the net," Ruiz said. "They'll have eyes on that harbor until the fire's cold."

"They had eyes on us all day and we still slipped out," Lena said. She was quiet for a moment, and then she said the thing that changed the shape of the night. "Devi's right. Not because it's smart. Because if we don't, we're the people in the Ledger. The ones who take and run and let someone else pay the bill. I spent twelve years being that person without knowing it. I'm not doing it on purpose."

Ruiz looked at her for a long time. Then at me. Then at the fire.

"First light," he said. "In and out in an hour. Devi, you're with me — you can tell if her shed's been searched by what's missing. Lena, you're on the boat. If we're not back in ninety minutes, you run, and you don't argue, because I know you and you'll want to argue."

"I wasn't going to argue," Lena said, which was a lie so transparent that even the fire didn't believe it.

* * *

We went in at gray dawn, when the harbor was all fishing boats and smoke haze and men shoveling burned Styrofoam into piles and cursing the universe with real poetry. The annex was gone — a black rectangle of ash and twisted roofing, still breathing thin smoke. But Rahayu's main shed stood untouched, and Rahayu stood in front of it, arms folded, watching the water, and when she saw our dinghy she did not look surprised, because Ibu Rahayu did not look surprised at things she had already decided would happen.

"You came back," she said. "Stupid."

"Yes," I said.

"Good." She jerked her chin at the annex. "I burned it."

The stone in my chest dropped through the bottom of me. "The drums — the timing —"

"Two drums, wired to a cheap timer I've had since '98, set for eleven forty." She said it the way she said everything, like the tide report. "The men in the dark clothes were going to board you at midnight. I've seen their pattern. They wait for the harbor to sleep. So I made sure the harbor didn't sleep, and I made sure there was a crowd, and I made sure you had a door out. The parts were already on your boat by six. The rest was inventory I can replace. A friendship, I can't." She looked at me, and her eyes behind the glasses were dry and very old. "They will come back for me now, girl. Not tonight. Maybe not this week. But they will come, and they will ask what I know, and I will tell them I know nothing, and they will decide whether to believe me. That is the bill. I knew it when I struck the timer."

"We can take you with us," I said, and my voice did something embarrassing on the second word.

"No." Flat. Final. "I have a daughter in Surabaya and a sister in Masohi and a business to rebuild and a harbor to run. Running is for people with something to hide. I have something to *stand in front of.*" She almost smiled. "But you can do something for me. You can make it worth it. Whatever's in that drive of yours, whatever has men in white shirts walking my quay — you make sure it lands somewhere it can't be buried. Because if it gets buried, I burned my annex for nothing, and I do not do things for nothing. Ask anyone."

Ruiz, who had been silent through all of it, said quietly, "It'll land, Ibu. I'd stake the boat on it."

"You have staked the boat on it," she said. "That's the problem."

She loaded us with what remained — two cases of the good coffee, a coil of spare hose, a bag of rice, and a hand-cranked radio "in case your electronics die, which, knowing Ruiz, they will" — and pushed the dinghy off with her own hands, and the last I saw of her she was standing in front of her shed with the ash of her annex drifting past her like gray snow, not waving, just watching, the way lighthouses watch.

We were clear of the outer marker by eight, running south with the fire haze still hanging over Ambon like a question nobody had answered. I was in the engine room, because that's where I go, torquing down the new injectors and trying not to do the arithmetic I couldn't stop doing: one woman's annex, one harbor master's favor, three days of head start, and no spare parts, and somewhere behind us a schedule with our names on it.

Ruiz put his head into the engine room around noon. He had the hand-cranked radio in one hand and a look on his face I'd only seen twice in six years, both times in weather.

"Devi. Come listen to this."

I climbed out, wiped my hands, and took the crank. The radio was a cheap thing, all static and Indonesian pop stations, but between the stations, faint, on a frequency Rahayu had scratched onto a strip of tape stuck to its side — a frequency I recognized, because it was the one Sari's people had given us back in the old days, the pirate fleet's listening watch — a voice was repeating, in careful English, over and over:

"…vessel *Nusantara Star*, this is a general call on the southern lanes. We have your friends. We know where you are going. We would like to talk about the toll."

Ruiz and I looked at each other across the cockpit, and the monsoon clouds were building on the southern horizon like a wall, and the voice on the radio said, patiently, one more time:

"We would like to talk about the toll."
