## 10. The Pirate Toll

The first sign of trouble was a fishing boat that wasn't fishing.

Ruiz spotted it from the wheelhouse an hour after dawn — a thirty-foot outrigger sitting fat and idle in the middle of the strait, nets stacked dry on its deck, three men visible doing absolutely nothing with great commitment. In these waters, that was either incompetence or reconnaissance, and nobody survived long enough to be incompetent.

"Contact," Ruiz said, without raising his voice. He never raised his voice. It was one of the things I liked about him, back when I could still afford to like things. "Two more off the port bow, behind that islet. They've been holding station for twenty minutes."

Devi came up from the engine room wiping her hands on a rag that had once been white, in some previous geological era. "I heard 'contact.' Please tell me it's dolphins."

"It's boats."

"I hate boats."

"You work on one."

"That's why I hate them."

I stood at the rail and did the math I'd been dreading since Ambon. The strait narrowed ahead of us into a channel Sari's people — because by now we'd stopped pretending we didn't know whose water this was — had been squeezing for tolls since before the monsoon was a rumor. Ruiz's route ran straight through the middle of it, because every other route ran through waters where the hunters could find us, and being found by the hunters was no longer a metaphorical problem. We'd seen what they did to harbors.

"They'll want to board," Ruiz said.

"They always want to board."

"We can run for the reef line. Devi says the engines will hold at full for maybe—"

"The engines will hold at full until exactly the moment I need them not to explode," Devi said. "After that, all bets are off."

"So we don't run."

"So we don't run," I agreed, and hated how calm I sounded, because inside I was doing the other math, the one where I counted the people on this boat who trusted me and asked myself how many of them I was willing to spend. The answer was none. The problem was that the sea doesn't take payment in intentions.

* * *

They came out of the sun, which was old-fashioned of them and effective anyway. Six boats in a loose crescent, closing at a speed that made their outriggers groan, each one carrying more armed men than any fishing crew in history. On the lead boat's bow stood a woman in a salt-stiffened shirt with the sleeves cut off, a rifle slung across her back like she'd forgotten it was there, which somehow made it worse.

She was maybe forty-five. Hard to tell. The sea ages everyone twice, and grief runs a separate tab.

Ruiz killed our way and let them come. That was the whole strategy: don't run, don't fight, be boring. We were a salvage charter with nothing worth stealing, and if you repeated that lie often enough with the right face on it, sometimes the sea believed you.

The woman's boat came alongside with a bump that Devi would later describe as "criminal negligence against my hull." Lines went over. Men boarded — four of them, rifles slung loosely, eyes moving over our deck with the professional boredom of men who do this weekly. And then the woman stepped aboard, and the boredom ended, because her eyes weren't bored at all.

They were looking at me.

"Lena Lockridge," she said. My name in her mouth sounded like something being weighed.

"That's me. Captain. You're standing on my deck."

"Am I." She walked the length of the boat slowly, touching nothing, looking at everything. She looked at the dive gear racked aft longer than anything else. She looked at Devi's open laptop bag. She looked at Ruiz's chart table, where the route to the press contact's island was drawn in pencil under a plastic sheet, because Ruiz was careful about everything except the one thing that mattered most.

"My name is Sari," she said finally. "This is my water. You crossed my line at dawn and you did not stop, and you did not signal, and you did not pay. So now we have a conversation instead of a transaction, and conversations are more expensive."

"What's the rate?" Ruiz asked.

"For a salvage boat? Fuel. Water. Maybe your spare parts, if they're good." Her gaze came back to me. "But you're not a salvage boat."

"We're very much a salvage boat. Ask anyone we've robbed."

Someone laughed — one of hers, quickly smothered. Sari didn't smile. She stepped closer, close enough that I could see a scar running from her left ear down into her collar, old and badly closed, the kind of scar you get when the person holding the knife isn't in a hurry.

"Twelve years ago," she said, "there was a village on the coast north of here. You may have read about it. There was nothing to read, actually. That was the point."

My mouth went dry. Behind me, I heard Devi go very still.

"There were four hundred and eleven people in that village," Sari continued, in the flat tone of someone reciting numbers they'd carved into themselves. "There are sixty-three of us now. We live on the water because the land was taken from us twice — once by fire, once by paperwork. The men who did the burning were soldiers. The men who did the paperwork wore suits, and they are ministers now. Some of them have medals."

"Sari—" I started.

"You know the name of the village." It wasn't a question. "You flinched. People who don't know it look confused. People who know it flinch."

The deck seemed to tilt, though the sea was glass-flat. Twelve years of carefully constructed distance — nautical miles instead of years, wrecks instead of memories, a name bought in a port town and paid for in cash — and this woman had dismantled it in ninety seconds with a scar and a flinch.

"Devi," I said. My voice came out wrong, scraped. "Get the drive."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, because there was no version of slow that worked.

Devi brought up the restored fragment on her laptop, hands steady in the way they only got when things were worst — I'd seen it in the engine room during the storm off Ambon, competence arriving precisely when panic would have been fatal. Sari watched the screen over her shoulder, and I watched Sari watch, and what moved across her face wasn't surprise. It was recognition. The terrible, hungry recognition of a woman seeing her own dead named in a government file.

The survey team had found the container wreck. Inside it: manifests, orders, intercepts — the whole machinery of the atrocity laid out in bureaucratic prose. Who gave the order. Who signed the cover. Which officials had the witnesses silenced, one by one, across a decade, with the patient thoroughness of people filing taxes. And there, in an annex Devi had flagged and I hadn't yet been able to make myself open, a list of analysts whose reports had been rewritten, redacted, or disappeared.

Sari read for a long time. Nobody breathed. One of her men shifted his rifle and she raised one hand without looking, and he stilled.

"This is real," she said at last.

"It's real," Devi said. "We verified what we could. The rest matches known facts too well to be forged."

"And you were taking it where?"

Ruiz opened his mouth and I talked over him, because lying to this woman had stopped being possible somewhere around the word *flinch*. "To people who'll print it. Journalists. Neutral ones, past the reach of the men in the file."

"To courts?"

"No court will touch it. Not here. Not anywhere those names have friends."

Sari nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something she'd known since she was nineteen years old kneeling in ash. Then she turned to me fully, and the weight of her attention settled on me like ballast.

"Alliance, then. My boats take you through the patrol lines. My channels, my reefs, the routes your charts call shoals and my people call home. No toll in fuel, no toll in parts." She paused. "In exchange, I want a truth."

"We just gave you—"

"Hers." Sari pointed at me. "Not the documents. Documents are paper. Paper burns — believe me, I know. I want to know why a woman who reads government files like a native speaker is captaining a salvage boat under a name that isn't hers."

The strait was very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"How do you know it isn't mine?"

"Because I've spent twelve years learning to read people the way you read water, Captain Lockridge. And because last night, in the fishing traffic, your engineer called you something else. Quietly. Half asleep. But I have good ears." She folded her arms. "So. Your truth, or we part ways here, and you take your chances with the black-hulled boats behind you. They're two hours back. I checked."

* * *

I looked at my crew. Ruiz gave me nothing — his face was a wall he'd built over forty years of sailing through other people's emergencies. Devi looked scared, but not for herself; she looked scared the way you look at someone walking toward an edge.

And I thought about the annex. I hadn't opened it, but I knew what was in it, because I'd spent twelve years knowing. My report had gone in honest and come out a weapon. My name had been on the cover page of the rewrite. Somewhere in that file was the paper trail of a junior analyst named — and here my throat closed around it, the way it always did, the name sitting behind a door I'd welded shut —

"Lena," Devi said softly. "You don't have to."

"Yes, I do." I turned back to Sari. "You want to know who I am. Fine. But understand what you're asking. This isn't a confession. It's an amputation."

"Then bleed," Sari said, without cruelty. "Everyone on my boats has bled. It's the only currency here that doesn't devalue."

I told them.

I told them my name — my real one, the one I hadn't said aloud in twelve years, and saying it felt like pulling a splinter that had grown into the bone. I told them about the desk job, the intercepts, the report I wrote that said plainly what was going to happen to villages like hers, and the version that came back from above with the warnings surgically removed and my signature still attached, so that when the fire came, the record showed the analysts had seen nothing. I told them about the inquiry that followed, the one that consumed juniors and protected principals, and how I understood, at twenty-six, that I could stay and be buried alongside the truth, or leave and carry it. I chose to carry it. Then I spent twelve years not carrying it — diving wrecks, moving miles, telling myself that distance was survival when it was really just surrender with better scenery.

"I didn't light the match," I finished. "But I signed the paper that said the room was empty. And then I ran, and I let a dead woman's silence keep me safe. That's who you're allied with."

The silence afterward lasted long enough for me to count my own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

Then Sari did something I will remember for the rest of my life, however long the world allows it to be. She reached out and took my hand — my diver's hand, rope-scarred and salt-cracked — and held it the way you hold something you're returning to its owner.

"In the village," she said, "there was a schoolteacher who used to say that shame is proof the conscience is alive. The men in your file have no shame. That's how we know they'll never confess." She released my hand. "You confessed. To strangers. With hunters two hours behind you. That's worth more than your signature ever was, on any document."

"So we have an alliance?"

"We have an alliance." She turned and shouted orders in the island dialect, and her fleet began to reorganize around us like a immune system accepting a cell it had decided not to kill. "Your boat sails in the center. My fastest pair on point. We move now — the tide turns in an hour, and the reef passage east only works on the ebb."

As her men dropped back to their boats, Sari paused at the rail beside me. Close up, the scar caught the light, and I understood suddenly that she wore it uncovered on purpose. A flag. A receipt.

"One more thing, Captain," she said quietly. "The man who gave the order for my village — the one whose name is third in your file — he has a nephew in the coastal patrols. He'll know we're moving within a day. Whatever you're planning at the end of this route, plan it faster."

She dropped to her deck, and her fleet turned east, and we followed.

Behind us, two hours back, the black hulls were coming. Ahead of us lay reefs no chart admitted to, a patrol line we'd have to thread like a needle, and — Devi said, pale, staring at the recovered fragment she'd finally worked open while the pirates' wake still foamed alongside — a second list she needed to show me immediately.

"Lena," she said. "The annex. There's a name on the witness-silencing order I think you need to see. It's not yours."

She turned the screen toward me.

"But it's someone you know."
