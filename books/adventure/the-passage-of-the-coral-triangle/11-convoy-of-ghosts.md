## 11. Convoy of Ghosts

I've been running boats for thirty-one years, and in that time I've learned exactly three things you can count on: the sea doesn't care, the weather lies, and nobody volunteers to die for a stranger's hard drive.

Which is why what happened on the third night out of Sari's anchorage broke all my rules at once, and I still don't have a clean way to talk about it.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Ruiz tells stories the long way or not at all — Lena says it's because I'm old, Devi says it's because I'm dramatic, and both of them are right, which is the kind of thing a captain learns to live with.

* * *

Sari came aboard at dusk with six of her people and a rolled chart that had never seen the inside of a hydrographic office. She spread it across my table in the wheelhouse, weighted the corners with coffee mugs, and looked at me like a woman about to discuss the price of something neither of us wanted to sell.

"Your route," she said, tapping the official channel we'd planned, "is a shooting gallery. Two patrol boats, maybe three by tomorrow night. They rotate every forty hours. You would be seen."

"And your route?"

She unrolled her chart over mine. It wasn't a chart so much as an argument — pencil lines, depth soundings in a hand that hadn't been taught penmanship by anyone official, little crosses where reefs lived and little circles where they didn't, despite what the government said. Smuggler's cartography. The kind of map that gets updated by running aground.

"Through the Banda back doors," she said. "Reef channels my grandfather used when he was running diesel and cigarettes. Narrow. Ugly. The patrol boats can't follow without tearing their bottoms out, and they know it."

"And the catch," I said, because there is always a catch, and a captain who pretends otherwise ends up feeding fish.

"The catch is that we travel as a convoy. My boats ahead and behind. We run dark except for decoys." She glanced up at me. "You have flares?"

"I have flares, strobes, a fishing-light array I stole from a man who deserved it, and Devi, who can make any of them lie convincingly."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. Sari rationed smiles the way other people ration insulin.

"Good. Because tonight, we are ghosts. And ghosts need light tricks more than guns."

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the theme of this voyage whether I subscribed to it or not: part of me hated the plan. Not because it was bad — it was good, better than anything I could have built — but because accepting it meant accepting that we were no longer captains of our own fate. I'd spent three decades being the man who decided which water my boat touched. Now I was handing the tiller of destiny to a pirate queen with a pencil chart, and every scar on my hands itched.

But then I thought about the black-hulled boat from the night passage, and the decommissioned registration number, and the fact that someone had already decided we were worth killing. Pride is a luxury for people who aren't being hunted.

"Show me the first channel," I said.

* * *

We weighed anchor at 0200, which sounds romantic if you've never done it. It is not romantic. It is cold coffee, wet rope, and the particular misery of watching a radar screen while pretending you're not afraid of it.

Sari's fleet was four boats — fast, low, patched together with the marine equivalent of spite. She put two ahead of us, one astern, and kept her flagship alongside our port beam close enough that I could smell their galley cooking. Fish and chili and diesel. It should not have been comforting. It was.

Lena stood beside me at the helm, quiet in the way she gets when her brain is doing heavy lifting. Devi was below with the drive wrapped in waterproof bags and taped inside a spare impeller housing, because my engineer has the soul of a paranoid squirrel and I have never once regretted it.

"Talk to me about these decoy lights," I said to Devi over the intercom.

"Already rigged," she said. "Two strobes on auto-buoys we drop when Sari says. They'll drift on the current looking exactly like a boat running dark and careless. Meanwhile we're twelve miles east doing fifteen knots through a channel that officially does not exist."

"You stole that trick from—"

"From you. Ambon, '09. You told me never to repeat it."

"I also told you never to date the harbormaster's daughter."

"Some lessons take longer than others."

That's Devi. Give her a war zone and she'll find the joke in it, mostly because the joke is load-bearing. Take it away and the whole structure comes down.

The first hours were clean. No traffic, no radar contacts, just the black water and the running lights of Sari's boats glowing soft green off our port side like fireflies that had made some very bad life choices. Lena took the mid-watch. I pretended to sleep in the chair, which every captain knows is the real job description.

At 0340 Sari's voice came over the handheld, low and flat.

"Contact. Northeast. Two engines, coming down the shipping lane. That's them."

I was on my feet before the sentence ended. On the radar, a blip crawled along the deep-water corridor we'd abandoned six hours ago — except it wasn't following the corridor anymore. It was turning. Toward us.

"They can't see us," Lena said. "Not this far out."

"No," Sari said. "They see my lead boat. He cut his transponder too late leaving the anchorage. They have a heading history."

There was a silence on the net that lasted about a year. In it, I did the math everyone else was doing. Lead boat was ten miles ahead, alone, in open water, with one engine less than whatever was hunting him.

"Sari," I said carefully. "What's the play?"

"The play," she said, "is that Yusuf knows these waters better than they do, and he has done this before."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer there is. Hold your course. Run dark. Do not be a hero, Captain. Heroes are expensive."

I held my course. I want that on the record, because later, when I tell this story — and I will tell it, in bars, badly, for years — I need someone to know that I did what she asked, and that it cost me something I haven't finished paying for.

* * *

Yusuf was twenty-six years old. I learned that afterward, from the woman who loved him, which is how you learn everything that matters — too late and secondhand.

He ran northeast for forty minutes, dragging the hunter after him like a lure on a line, and then he turned into the reef field south of the channel — the one marked on Sari's chart with three crosses and a note in her grandfather's handwriting that Devi later translated for me: *"fast at half tide, death at low."*

It was half tide.

Yusuf went through at twenty knots in the dark, threading coral teeth with nothing but memory and moonlight, and the hunter followed because hunters always do, because pride is a luxury for people who think they're the ones doing the hunting.

We heard it happen. That's the part nobody warns you about. Radar showed his blip slide through the gap, clean, beautiful, and the hunter's blip come on fast behind — and then the hunter's blip stuttered, split, and became a slow scatter of debris echo, and the radio carried the sound of men screaming in a language I don't speak, and Sari's voice saying, once, very quietly:

"He's through. Everyone hold position."

And then, softer, almost to herself: "Hold position."

We held position. Four boats sat dead in the water in the middle of the night while somewhere behind us a ship settled onto a reef and its crew discovered what the sea thinks of men who chase ghosts. Some of them got off. Boats came out of the darkness to collect them — Sari's people, because that's the code even among pirates, especially among pirates: you pull drowning men out of the water, even yours, even theirs. It's the one law the sea enforces personally.

By dawn we had eleven survivors lashed to Sari's decks, shivering and disarmed and looking at their rescuers with the specific confusion of men whose worldview had been capsized along with their vessel.

Yusuf's boat came back an hour later, running slow, riding high. He stood at the bow. He was smiling, and it was the worst thing I saw that week, because he was twenty-six years old and he knew exactly what he'd bought with those forty minutes, and he smiled anyway, and then he stopped smiling when he saw Sari's face, and he crossed the water between the boats and hugged her, and she held on longer than a commander holds on.

I stayed on my own deck. Some grief isn't yours to stand inside.

* * *

The rest of the crossing was the strangest two days of my career, and I once spent a week anchored off a island whose entire economy was cockfighting and counterfeit batteries.

We ran the reef channels by day — narrow green water over white sand, coral walls rising on either side close enough to read the graffiti of past smugglers, painted arrows and dates and names going back decades. Devi stood at the bow calling depths like a human fathometer, delighted beyond reason. "Ruiz! There's a wreck down there! Old one! Can we stop? Can we please—" "No." "You're no fun." "I'm alive. It's underrated."

The freed survivors sat under guard amidships and watched us with eyes that recalculated hourly. One of them, a young officer type with a jaw like a filing cabinet, asked Lena — in decent English — why we were doing this. Running ghosts through smuggler channels for a folder full of dead men's paperwork.

Lena looked at him for a long moment. I was close enough to hear her answer.

"Because somebody buried people I knew," she said. "And I helped bury them without knowing it. This is the shovel going the other direction."

The officer stared at her. Whatever he'd expected, it wasn't that. He didn't ask anything else for the rest of the crossing.

On the second night, Sari came to my wheelhouse with two cups of coffee that tasted like burnt tires and gratitude, and we talked the way people talk at three in the morning, which is to say honestly, against our wills.

"My grandfather ran this channel in '61," she said. "Running rice during the blockade. He said the reef doesn't care who you are. It only cares whether you respect it. Everything else — governments, navies, men like the ones chasing you — they all pretend the rules are theirs. The reef knows better."

"Your grandfather sounds like he'd have liked my mother."

"Everyone's mother is the same person, Captain. That's the secret."

I laughed, and it surprised me, laughing out here in the haunted water with a dead boy's boat trailing us like a flag. But that's the thing about grief braided with gratitude — it leaves room for laughter, and the laughter makes the grief worse, and the grief makes the laughter necessary, and you go on like that until you reach shore or don't.

Before she left, she paused at the door. "When this is done," she said, "and if you're still alive, and if I'm still alive — I'll buy you a drink that doesn't taste like tires."

"Make it two."

"One each. I'm not rich."

* * *

We cleared the last of the patrol line at 0512 on the third day, sliding out of reef country into open blue with the sun coming up behind us like a witness. Sari's boats peeled away one by one — the two leads first, then the flankers, each breaking off with a short blast of the horn, formal as a military funeral, which I suppose it partly was.

Her flagship stayed with us another hour. Then Sari keyed the radio.

"Captain Ruiz. From here, you're on your own. The hunters will regroup, and they'll be angry, and angry men make mistakes — theirs and yours. Keep your speed up. Trust your engineer. And when you deliver that drive—" a pause, long enough that I checked the set was still live, "—say Yusuf's name. Out loud. Somewhere it counts. That's all he wanted. To matter somewhere it counts."

"He does," I said. "Starting now."

Another pause. Then, quietly: "Thank you for holding position."

And she was gone, her wake closing behind her like a book.

Devi appeared at my shoulder with the chart and a face she was trying to keep professional and failing. "Course to the press contact's island?"

"Lay it in."

"Ruiz." She hesitated. "The kid. Yusuf. He—"

"I know."

"We wouldn't have—"

"I know that too." I looked at the horizon, which offered no comment. "So we carry it. That's the fare. Everybody on this boat pays it now."

Lena came up from below, salt-damp, eyes red-rimmed — she'd heard the radio, same as all of us. She stood at the rail a while, then said, without turning around:

"Ruiz. When we get there. When this is done. I need to tell you my real name."

I kept my hands on the wheel and my voice level, which took more seamanship than anything in the last three days.

"I figured you had one," I said.

Ahead, the water lay flat and shining all the way to the edge of the world, and somewhere past that edge, a storm was building with our name in its mouth, and a fuel stop called Ambon waited with smoke already rising over it — though we didn't know that yet, God help us, we didn't know.
