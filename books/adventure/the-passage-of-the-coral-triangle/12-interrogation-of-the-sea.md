## 12. Interrogation of the Sea

The storm found us the way debt collectors find people who've moved twice — patiently, and from an angle we hadn't planned for.

Ruiz had threaded us into a cove on the back side of a nameless island that didn't appear on any chart worth trusting, a slot of green water between two limestone walls so sheer they looked like the island had been split open by someone with a grudge. The *Mariana's Fault* sat at anchor in water the color of bottle glass, and above us the sky went the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom.

We'd been there six hours when the rain arrived. Not rain, really — the monsoon's opening argument, delivered horizontally, hammering the canopy of the jungle behind us into something that sounded like applause for a disaster.

Devi secured the last of the deck gear at 1400. Ruiz double-checked both anchors at 1500. And by 1600, with nothing left to fix and nowhere left to go, the four of us were sealed inside the main cabin while the world outside tried to tear itself apart — and the drive was sitting in its waterproof case on the chart table between us like a loaded thing.

It had been sitting there all day. Everyone had been pretending not to look at it.

I lasted until the second pot of coffee.

"Okay," I said. "Somebody say it."

"Say what?" Devi asked, which was her way of saying she'd been waiting hours for permission.

"The thing we've been not saying since Ambon." I turned the case toward the middle of the table. "What do we do with it?"

* * *

Devi went first, because Devi always goes first when there's a right answer available and she's holding it.

"We leak it," she said. "That's not even a debate. That's arithmetic."

"Nothing is ever just arithmetic," Ruiz said.

"This is." She pulled the restored drive fragment from her pocket — she carried it now, I'd noticed, the way some people carry a worry stone or a rosary — and set it beside the case. "There are names in there. Ministers, officers, a shipping consortium that moved weapons through waters where twelve years ago four hundred people died and got written up as a maritime accident. The survey team found the proof. The survey team disappeared. We have the proof. There is exactly one moral action available and it involves a satellite uplink and a journalist named Wicaksono who Sari says will actually answer his phone."

"And then what?" Ruiz leaned back against the bulkhead, arms crossed, the way he did when he was about to be the adult in the room and hated it. "Then every person whose name is in those files knows exactly one boat took their careers out of a wreck in the Coral Triangle. You think Okafor's the only one they can send? Okafor's a middle manager. He's the guy they send when they want paperwork done politely. The people behind him send different guys."

"So we hide?"

"So we live," Ruiz said. "Which has historically been my priority."

"You said you were already a target," Devi shot back. "In the fishing traffic, night three. You photographed that hull yourself. You said — your words — 'we're dead men who haven't gotten the memo.' So don't stand there telling me caution is a strategy. Caution is just dying slower."

"That was before I understood how many directions the dying could come from."

"There's a third option," I said quietly.

They both looked at me. Outside, thunder rolled down the strait like furniture being dragged across heaven's floor.

"Bury it," I said. "Deep water. Somewhere only I could find again. We walk away, we take the money Okafor already paid, we lose ourselves the way we've been losing ourselves for years, and nobody dies."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

Devi stared at me like I'd started speaking in tongues. "You're joking."

"I'm offering it for completeness."

"You're offering cowardice for completeness."

"I'm offering survival," I said. "For completeness."

"No." She shook her head slowly, and there was something underneath her anger that took me a second to identify, because I'd never seen it on her face before. It was disappointment. Real disappointment, the personal kind. "No, see, I've been watching you since Sulawesi, Lena. Since the manifests. You haven't slept properly in two weeks. You dive alone. You read those documents at two in the morning with a headlamp like you're doing penance. You don't get to sit here and propose burying it, because you know — you *know* — that if we bury it, it never stays buried in you. It'll be the wreck you keep diving. It'll be the thing you measure every mile against for the rest of your life."

"That's melodramatic."

"That's observation," she said. "I'm the engineer. Observation is my whole job."

* * *

Ruiz made dinner, because Ruiz makes dinner when conversations go bad — it's his version of calling a time-out, except the food keeps coming and the conversation doesn't actually stop, it just gets interrupted by rice.

He set the pot down in the middle of us and served nobody, which was itself a statement.

"Let me tell you a story," he said. "Since we're being honest tonight. Apparently that's happening."

"Ruiz—"

"No, no. Fair's fair. Devi called you out. I'm next." He sat down heavily, the cabin rocking, rain drumming the overhead like fingers impatient for an answer. "Before this boat, I ran cargo for a man in Zamboanga. Nothing terrible. Fuel, mostly. Some things that weren't fuel. And one run, I'm told to carry eleven passengers, no manifest, no questions. And I look at these eleven people and I understand they're running from something, and I take them anyway, because the money was good and because I told myself it wasn't my business what they were running from."

"What happened?" Devi asked.

"Nothing happened. That's the story. They got where they were going. Probably fine. Probably alive to this day." He shrugged. "But I never knew. Twelve years — funny number, huh? — twelve years I've told myself it wasn't my business. And every time I hear about a boat full of people going missing in those waters, some small ugly part of me does the math. Was it mine? Could I have asked? Would asking have changed anything?" He tapped the table once, hard. "Not knowing isn't neutral. I learned that late. Not knowing is a debt too. It accrues."

Nobody spoke. The storm filled the silence for us.

"So when I say I want everyone alive," Ruiz went on, "understand that's not cowardice talking. I've done the coward's math already. I know exactly what it costs. It costs you every night for the rest of your life, at compound interest." He looked at me. "So no, captain's orders aren't 'bury it.' Captain's orders are: somebody tell me why leaking it is survivable, because I can't find that answer myself, and I've been looking since Ambon."

* * *

It was my turn, and I'd known it was coming since the coffee.

I put my hands around my mug because they needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

"When I was twenty-four," I said, "my name was Elena Vasquez-Reyes."

Devi went very still. Ruiz closed his eyes briefly, like a man hearing a diagnosis he'd suspected for a long time.

"I was a junior analyst. Maritime intelligence, joint task force, the boring kind — cargo patterns, hull registrations, insurance fraud. My job was spreadsheets. And one quarter I noticed a pattern. Vessels moving through the eastern approaches with cargo declarations that didn't match their drafts. Tonnage appearing and vanishing. I flagged it. I wrote it up. Forty pages, clean methodology, my first real report." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "They loved it. Commendation. My supervisor said I had a future."

"Lena—" Devi started.

"Elena," I said. "Let me finish, because I've never finished this sentence in my life. Six months later, the incident happened. Four hundred people, coastal villages, the official finding was a munitions accident aboard a passing vessel. And I was given a new assignment: reconcile my tonnage analysis with the official finding. Make the numbers agree. And I looked at the numbers, and the numbers agreed *with each other* and disagreed with the finding, and I wrote a one-page addendum saying so, and my supervisor thanked me, and the addendum was never filed, and I told myself — this is the important part, this is the load-bearing lie — I told myself that filing it would change nothing except who got hurt next. Me. My career. Maybe worse. I was twenty-four and I believed institutions absorbed truth the way hulls absorb water, slowly, without anyone drowning."

"And then?" Ruiz asked softly.

"Then I read the Ledger," I said. "And I found my own report in it. Cited. Annotated. 'Handled — analyst cooperative.' My name, in a file kept by the people who did it, filed under assets." I looked at the drive. "Those documents don't just indict them. They indict me. My silence is in there. It's a line item. I am *evidence*, and I've been running from the subpoena for twelve years, measuring my life in nautical miles because miles don't ask questions."

The rain hammered. Devi reached across the table and put her hand over mine, and I let it stay there.

"So when I said bury it," I said, "that wasn't strategy. That was the same lie with better weather. And I'm done telling it." I straightened up. "We leak it. But we do it smart, because Ruiz is right that smart is the only version where anyone's alive to read the headline. One copy to Wicaksono. One copy somewhere Okafor's people can't reach, held as insurance. And we don't transmit anything until we're clear of the patrol lines and the monsoon, because a story that dies with its couriers isn't a story, it's a eulogy."

"Second copy where?" Ruiz asked. Practical to the end. I could have kissed him.

"Sari," Devi and I said at the same time.

Ruiz grimaced. "The pirate."

"The ally," Devi said. "Who lost family in those villages, and who asked for one payment we haven't fully made yet." She glanced at me. "She asked for your truth. You just gave it to us. Give it to her too, and she holds the second copy like it's scripture."

"It's a lot of trust to put in someone with guns," Ruiz said.

"It's more trust than we've ever put in anyone," I said. "That's sort of the point. That's the whole lesson of the last twelve years. Trust nobody, need nobody, be nobody — and look where the boat's anchored."

* * *

We voted, formally, because Ruiz is sentimental about procedure the way some men are sentimental about flags. Three hands for leak-and-insure. Nobody voted for burying, including me, and Devi raised her hand so fast she nearly left her chair.

We sealed the insurance copy in a second case, wrapped it, and stowed it in the bilge locker under the engine — the one place on any boat where nobody voluntarily goes. Devi labeled it *SPARE IMPELLER* in grease pencil, which is either the best or worst security system ever devised.

And then, because storms don't care about resolutions, we did the dishes. Ruiz washed, Devi dried, I put things away, and the ordinary domesticity of it felt almost obscene after what we'd just dug up — ours, each other's, the sea's.

It was near midnight when the knock came.

Three knocks on the hull, spaced slow, from the waterline side. Ruiz froze with a plate in his hand. Devi killed the lamp. We all knew the code, because Sari had taught it to us two nights ago at the toll anchorage: three knocks meant her scouts, coming in low through the rain in a canoe that no radar would bother naming.

Ruiz cracked the hatch. Rain and a young woman in soaked canvas poured in together, and she was smiling, which after everything was somehow the most frightening thing yet.

"They found them," she said, wringing out her sleeve. "Sari's scouts. The missing surveyors. Three of them alive — held at a compound on the coast, two days north. Men guarding them. Boats coming and going." She looked around at our faces. "Sari says you'll want to know one more thing. The boats flying the flag at the compound —"

She paused, savoring it or dreading it, impossible to tell.

"— they're Okafor's."
