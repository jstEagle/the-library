## 13. The Survey Team Found

I was elbow-deep in the generator when the scout boat came back, which is how I prefer to receive world-altering news: with diesel on my hands and a wrench in my teeth. It gives me something to put down slowly, dramatically, when the news turns out to be world-altering.

The scout was a kid called Bima who couldn't have been more than nineteen and piloted an outrigger like it owed him money. He came up the beach at the hidden anchorage fast, talking fast in the local dialect to Sari, and I caught maybe one word in ten but I caught the tone, and the tone was wrong. Not frightened, exactly. Sari's people didn't seem to do frightened. It was the tone of someone delivering a bill.

Sari listened, asked two questions, and then walked over to where Lena was coiling line with the particular concentration of a woman pretending the world was just rope.

"They found them," Sari said, in English, for our benefit. "Your missing surveyors."

Lena's hands stopped moving. Ruiz, who had been dozing in the shade of the wheelhouse with a hat over his face, materialized the way he did — one second horizontal, the next standing, no transition a camera could have caught.

"Alive?" Ruiz asked.

"Three of them. Alive."

The word *alive* did a strange thing to the air. For eleven days we had been carrying the dead with us — the vanished team, the empty camp, the shoes by the tents — and I'd privately done the arithmetic that divers do, the cold arithmetic of water and time and heat, and the answer had always come back *no one survives eleven days*. I had made peace with it the way you make peace with a bad dive: acknowledge it, log it, move on.

"Where?" Lena said.

Sari told her, and the place meant nothing to me, but it meant something to her and to Ruiz, because they exchanged the look — the one where two people who trust each other completely communicate an entire argument in half a second, and I, who trust them both, am left standing there like a man reading a letter held up to a mirror.

"A compound," Sari added. "Coastal. Old plantation house, new fence, generators, a dock. Four guards we counted from the water. Probably more inside." She paused, and I watched her decide how much to say, which is a thing she did often and well. "There is a boat coming for them. Two days. Maybe three."

"Extraction," Ruiz said flatly.

"Extraction," Sari agreed, and the way she said it made the word sound like a euphemism for something with a shovel in it.

* * *

Here is what I knew by then, because I was the one who'd pulled it out of the corrupted drive with a soldering iron, three all-nighters, and a level of profanity that I'm told was audible from the water: the survey team hadn't vanished. They'd been taken. They had found the container wreck, they had found the Ledger inside it, and they had done what any competent team would do — they'd started documenting, verifying, cross-referencing. And the people who buried the atrocity twelve years ago had done what competent criminals do: they'd collected the witnesses before the witnesses could become a story.

Three of them were still breathing, which meant they were still useful, which meant — and this is the part that sat in my stomach like swallowed anchor chain — they were being kept alive as inventory. Leverage. Proof-of-concept for whoever came next. And when the extraction boat arrived, their usefulness would end somewhere between the dock and the deep water, because dead men tell no tales but living ones need feeding, and these people were clearly not in the hospitality business.

We held a meeting that evening on the beach, which is where we held everything now, because Sari's people ran the anchorage and Sari's people believed that walls were for people with something to hide, and also because the mosquitoes were marginally less committed out in the breeze.

The cast: Lena, sitting on an upturned fuel drum with her forearms on her knees. Ruiz, standing, because Ruiz never sat during arguments, as if sitting might be construed as agreement. Me, with a notebook I wasn't writing in. Sari, with two of her people at a respectful distance, close enough to listen, far enough to pretend they weren't. And the Ledger itself, or rather a waterproof case containing the drive and the two copies Devi — that is to say, I — had made, sitting in the sand between us like a landmine everyone was being very polite about.

"Two days," Ruiz said. "Maybe three. And you're proposing we sail toward it."

"I'm proposing we discuss sailing toward it," Lena said.

"Ah, discussion. My favorite nautical activity."

"Ruiz." Just his name, in the tone she used when she wanted him to hear the sentence underneath the sentence.

He heard it. He paced three steps and came back. "Say it, then."

"They're alive because nobody's found them yet. The moment that boat arrives—"

"The moment that boat arrives, it's not our problem. That's the point I keep failing to make elegantly." Ruiz crouched, drew a line in the sand with one finger. "Here's us. Here's the compound. Here's the route we have to run to reach the press contact before the monsoon seals the straits — and notice, if you will, that the route does not pass through the compound. Notice further that every hour we spend not running that route is an hour the hunters use to close the net. We have a drive. We have copies. We have a job to finish. What we do not have is a marine rescue capability, an assault team, or—"

"We have Sari," I said.

Everyone looked at me. Sari's people looked at Sari. Sari looked at me with an expression I couldn't parse, which from her was practically a compliment.

"I have not offered," Sari said.

"No," I agreed. "But you found them. Your scouts found them, which means you were looking, which means you already had an opinion before you walked over here tonight."

Sari was quiet for a moment. The surf did its thing. Somewhere in the dark, one of her boats coughed and caught.

"I was looking," she said finally, "because I know that compound. Not that one — that kind. There was a place like it, twelve years ago, near my village. People went in. People did not come out. The men who ran it wore the same uniforms as the men who burned what was left." She looked at Lena. "You asked me once what I wanted out of this. I told you the truth was the toll. I did not tell you that some debts collect interest." She stood. "I will tell you what I know of the compound. I will not tell you what to do with it. That is your captain's problem."

She walked back to her fire, and Ruiz watched her go with the face of a man who has just lost an argument he hasn't had yet.

* * *

I want to be honest about my own position in that meeting, because honesty was becoming the theme of the voyage whether we liked it or not, and I had spent most of my adult life being honest about everything except the things that mattered.

I wanted to go. That's the ugly truth, or the beautiful one, depending on your tax bracket of cynicism. I wanted to go not because I'd run some moral calculus and arrived at *witnesses must be saved* — though I'd have said that, and mostly meant it — but because for eleven days I had been the keeper of the dead. I'd read every name in the Ledger. I'd built the timeline of the atrocity the way you'd build a hull, plank by plank, until it could hold the weight of what happened, and every name in it had become a small weight I carried below decks. The survey team were the first names on that manifest who might still be crossed off the *lost* column and into the *found* one. I wanted to go because I could not stand to add three more names to the column of things I knew about and did nothing with.

Knowledge is a burden. Everyone says that like it's a metaphor. It is not a metaphor. It weighs approximately three hard drives and it sits on your chest at two in the morning.

"Devi," Ruiz said, when Sari was out of earshot. "Talk to me. You're the one who reads the thing. What's the play?"

"The play," I said, "is that we're already dead if we're caught with it. You've said so yourself. So the marginal risk of a detour is smaller than it looks, and the marginal cost of leaving three witnesses in that compound is—" I stopped, recalibrated, decided to say the true thing. "Ruiz, if we deliver the Ledger without them, it's a document. Documents get disputed, buried, litigated into paste. If we deliver the Ledger *and* three living witnesses who can testify to how they were taken, what was done to their camp, who held them — it's a story. Stories don't get buried. Stories get read."

"Stories also get their tellers killed."

"Everything gets someone killed. Doing nothing just spreads the bill across more people and calls it weather."

He looked at me for a long moment. Ruiz has this ability to make silence feel like an invoice. Then he turned to Lena, who hadn't said a word since the compound came up, and I realized she'd been doing that thing she does — the diver's thing — where she goes very still and very deep and lets the decision come up from below instead of down from above.

"Lena," Ruiz said. "It's your call. You know it's your call."

She looked up. And here's the thing I noticed, the thing I've thought about since: she looked *relieved*. Not eager, not grim — relieved. Like a woman who'd been holding her breath for eleven days and had finally been given permission to exhale.

"Then we're going," she said.

* * *

The problem — and there is always a problem, and on this voyage the problem was usually me — was that going meant telling Sari's people the route.

Not the whole route. But the compound sat on it, more or less; the coastal road of our escape ran past the one place on this coast where the hunters had infrastructure, a dock, and a reason to be watching the water. To rescue the surveyors we had to approach a location the enemy had chosen, on a timeline the enemy had set, and then leave with three civilians who couldn dive, couldn't fight, and would absolutely panic at the wrong moment. And to do any of that, we needed Sari's boats, Sari's scouts, Sari's knowledge of the reefs — which meant Sari's people would know where we were going, when, and why.

"Chain of custody," I said, in the planning session, drawing on the deck with a grease pencil because the whiteboard had gone down with our spare parts in the Ambon fire and I was still bitter about it. "The drive stays aboard this boat. Always. Sari's people get told the destination, not the cargo. The copies—" I tapped the case. "One stays with me. One goes in the bilge of Sari's fastest boat, sealed, no label, and she picks a crew of one to carry it who doesn't know what it is."

"You want to lie to our allies," Ruiz said.

"I want to *underinform* our allies. It's different. It's what governments do, and look how well it works for them."

Lena, to my mild surprise, backed me. "Devi's right. Not because we distrust Sari. Because trust is a route, and routes get captured. We've seen it. Okafor knew where we'd fuel. Someone talked, or someone listened, and either way the lesson is the same: assume everything you tell anyone arrives in the wrong inbox eventually."

Sari, when we laid it out for her, took the underinforming with a shrug that I found either magnanimous or terrifying. "You protect your secret. I protect mine. This is how the sea stays interesting." Then she unfolded her chart — an actual paper chart, annotated in three colors of pencil, the kind of document that in a museum would be behind glass and in her hands was a working tool — and showed us the compound.

It was worse than I'd hoped, which is my standard experience with plans. The house sat on a headland with water on three sides. The dock faced a channel that was deep enough for an extraction boat and shallow enough, on the flanks, to tear the bottom out of anything that got greedy. One road in, from the plantation side. Generator noise would cover a quiet approach until you were close, and then the dogs—

"Dogs," I said.

"Two," said Bima, the scout, with the tone of a man describing a minor weather system. "Old ones. Lazy."

"Old, lazy dogs have woken me up before," Ruiz muttered.

"So we don't go by land," Lena said. She was looking at the chart the way she looked at a wreck site, and I felt the shift in the room, the moment the plan stopped being a discussion and started being a dive. "We come up the channel at slack water, quiet, from the water side. The dock is their front door. Nobody watches their front door at three in the morning when the front door is the sea."

"And if they do?" I asked.

"Then we're swimming," she said, "and I'm better at that than they are."

* * *

We sailed at dusk the next day, which left one full day of preparation that I spent doing the thing I do best, which is turning anxiety into equipment. Spare fuel bladders checked and triple-rigged. The engines — our engines, the temperamental, fire-adjacent, Ambon-scarred engines — stripped, cleaned, and blessed in the manner of my people, which is to say I threatened them personally. Devi's rule of the sea: machines can smell fear, so give them something else to smell. In my case, usually, more grease.

Sari sent three boats. Not her flagship — she held that back, and I understood why without being told; you don't spend your queen to take a pawn — but three fast outriggers with crews who moved like they'd been born on deck and had simply come ashore occasionally to resupply. Bima piloted the lead. The one carrying the sealed bilge copy was crewed by a silent, broad-shouldered woman named Wulan who accepted the case from me with a nod and no questions, and I watched her stow it and thought: *this is either the wisest thing I've done or the most insulting, and possibly both.*

Lena and Ruiz and I stood in the wheelhouse as the anchorage fell behind us, and the coast came up dark ahead, and the channel lights — the few that existed — burned with the patient indifference of a world that did not know or care what we intended to do tonight.

"Devi," Lena said, quietly, when Ruiz went forward to the bow.

"Mm."

"When we get them aboard. They're going to ask what happened. To their camp. To their colleagues." She was looking at the water. "There were seven in the team. We're finding three."

I had done that arithmetic days ago and had, in the manner of a professional, filed it. She was un-filing it now, out loud, and making me hold it with her.

"I know," I said.

"They're going to ask why we came. And we're going to have to tell them the truth — that we came for a drive, and stayed for them. And they're going to look at us the way people look at a rescue that arrived too late to be clean."

"Then we'll be the ones who arrived," I said. "Late, dirty, and arrived. The sea doesn't grade on style."

She almost smiled. "When did you get to be the philosopher?"

"Somewhere around the third all-nighter with the drive. Sleep deprivation is a powerful teacher."

* * *

The compound showed itself an hour past midnight, and it was exactly as advertised: a dark headland, a working generator's hum carried flat across the water, one light burning in what I took to be a guard post, and the dock reaching out into the channel like a finger pointing at us, accusingly, though it had no way of knowing we were there.

We killed engines a kilometer out and drifted in on the tide, which is a sentence I want you to sit with, because drifting silently toward a place where people are held against their will, relying on water you cannot see to be as deep as a chart says it is, requires a specific kind of faith that I do not naturally possess. I possess it in machines. In tides, I possess hope.

Bima's boat came alongside, and Sari's people went in first — four of them, over the sides and into the black water with knives in their teeth like something out of a film, except slower and quieter and infinitely more serious. Lena went in with them. Of course she did. Ruiz and I held station at the edge of the channel light's reach, engines idling at a whisper, and for eleven minutes I experienced time the way it's experienced in a dentist's waiting room: as a substance that has thickened.

Then the generator note changed. Died. And in the sudden silence, three shapes came down the dock — two being half-carried, one walking bent — and Lena's hand rose out of the dark and made the signal, and we moved.

Getting them aboard was ugly and quiet and I will spare you the details except to say that one of the surveyors, a geologist named Osei, gripped my arm with a strength that hunger shouldn't allow and said, "You're real. You're actually real. We thought—" and didn't finish, and didn't need to.

We were turning for open water, engines still at a whisper, the headland sliding astern, when Bima's boat, running our wake, put up a single red flare — the signal for *seen* — and from behind the headland, where the channel opened toward the plantation road, a boat's engine started. Then a second.

"They had a boat hidden," Ruiz said, already pushing our throttle up. "They were waiting for the extraction. They just got it early."

"Can we outrun them?" I asked, already knowing the answer, already reaching for the engine diagnostics I'd need when the answer was no.

"Not both of them," Ruiz said. "Not in the open." He looked at the chart, then at the dark water ahead, then at me with the expression of a man about to do something that would give an insurance adjuster a heart attack. "But we're not in the open. Devi — how do you feel about a reef channel at night, at speed, with company?"

I felt, honestly, terrible about it. I said: "Give me the heading."

And behind us, the first pursuit boat's running lights came on — and ahead of us, out of the dark, Sari's remaining boats slid across our bow to form up, and the night stopped being a rescue and became a race.
