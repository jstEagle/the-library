## 14. Compound Night

The compound sat on a shelf of coastal land like something that had been dropped there by accident and never picked up again — three concrete buildings, a generator hut, a wire fence that had been electrified once upon a time and now mostly hummed to itself out of habit. Floodlights on two poles. A dock below with one boat tied to it, black-hulled, the kind of boat that didn't need a name because everyone who mattered already knew what it was.

Sari's scouts had counted nine men. Devi's drone — a battered quadcopter she'd rebuilt from fishing-shop parts and pure spite — counted eleven. The truth was probably somewhere in between, which in my experience is where the truth usually lives.

We lay in the treeline above it, in the dark before the dark gets thin, and I watched Lena Lockridge — the name I'd worn for twelve years like a coat that finally fit — try to figure out what the woman underneath her was going to do.

"You're quiet," Ruiz said beside me. He said it the way he says everything, low and even, as if the whole world were a chart he was reading at arm's length.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're *quiet* quiet." He nodded toward the compound. "That's the kind of quiet that comes right before someone does something stupid."

"Then we're all in trouble," Devi whispered from my other side, "because stupid is the plan."

She wasn't wrong. The plan was stupid. That's not me being modest; Sari herself had called it stupid, with a certain professional admiration, the way you'd compliment another driver's reckless overtaking maneuver. Three of her people would cut the generator line while two more put a fire on the far side of the fence — smoke and noise, the oldest trick in the book, older than books. Ruiz and I would go through the wire on the seaward side where the floodlights didn't reach, find the surveyors, and walk them out through the mangroves to where Sari's boats waited in the channel.

Eleven armed men against five of us and a fire. The math was bad. But the alternative math — leaving three witnesses locked in a building until the people who'd vanished their colleagues decided they'd stopped being useful — was worse, and worse in a way that would follow us the rest of our lives, however long the rest turned out to be.

"One thing," Sari had said before we left, gripping my forearm hard enough to leave marks. Her face was still raw from the storm, from losing half her fleet, from burying two of hers at sea because there was no ground to bury them in. "If you see the man who runs this place — the tall one, the foreigner — you do not fight him. You run. You hear me? He is not a guard. Guards are hired. He *believes*."

I'd asked what she meant. She'd looked at me with an expression I couldn't read in the dark and said, "You'll know."

* * *

The generator died at 0300 exactly, because Devi had timed the patrol rotations down to the minute and insisted we honor her schedule like a train timetable. The lights went out. Four seconds later, on the far side of the compound, a fuel drum went up in a bloom of orange that lit the whole coastline like a second, sloppier sunrise, and shouting started in three languages at once.

Ruiz and I went over the wire.

The seaward side of the compound was storage — stacked crates under tarps, a forklift with no forks, the general archaeology of places where men are stationed against their will. We moved between the shadows of it, Ruiz ahead, me counting his steps so I could follow blind if the smoke thickened. It thickened. The wind off the water pushed the burning drum's smoke across the compound in a slow gray tide, and within a minute I was navigating by touch and memory and the sound of Ruiz's breathing.

The surveyors were in the eastern building. Of course they were. The eastern building was the one with the windows barred, and barred windows are the world's most honest architecture.

Ruiz tried the door. Locked, obviously. He put his mouth near my ear. "Devi?"

"On it." I keyed the radio twice — our signal — and thirty seconds later, from somewhere behind us, came the small precise sound of an electronic lock giving up its argument with a signal repeater Devi had built out of a marine radio and what I sincerely hoped was legal knowledge. The light on the keypad went green.

Inside: three men on cot frames, blinking at us like creatures dragged up from a depth they'd adapted to. One was crying without any noise at all, tears just running down his face while he stared at us as though we might be a hallucination he'd have to apologize for. The second was on his feet immediately, moving with the wired efficiency of someone who'd spent weeks doing push-ups to stay sane. The third — the oldest, gray-bearded, glasses mended with tape — grabbed my wrist.

"Are you real," he said. Not a question. A verification protocol.

"As real as anything else tonight," I said. "Can you walk?"

"We've been walking the walls for six weeks." His voice cracked on *six*. "Yes. We can walk."

Six weeks. They'd been held since before we ever found their camp, since before the Ledger, since before any of this had a shape. Six weeks of a room with barred windows, waiting to find out whether silence or a shallow grave was their retirement package. I felt something in my chest turn over — not pity exactly. Pity is what you feel from a distance. This was closer than that. This was recognition. I knew what it was to erase yourself to survive. They'd just had it done to them by professionals.

We got them out the back, into the smoke, Ruiz carrying the crier when his legs quit at the fence. The mangroves took us in like they take in everything — mud to the knees, roots grabbing at our arms, the smell of sulfur and salt and rot that I have loved longer than I've loved most people. Behind us the fire roared and men shouted at each other in the smoke, hunting a raid that had already dissolved into the swamp.

And then, at the edge of the mangroves where the boardwalk started, a flashlight clicked on.

Not a searchlight. A flashlight. Held low, pointed at the ground, the way you hold a light when you're not looking for someone — you're *waiting* for them.

The man holding it was tall. Gray at the temples, dressed in civilian clothes that cost more than my boat's engine, standing alone on the boardwalk with his hands visible and empty, as though the entire compound burning behind him were a formality he'd already signed off on.

Sari's voice came through the radio, tight: *"Lena. That is him. Go. GO—"*

But he said my name first. Not Lena.

"Elena Vasquez-Reyes."

My old name. My real name, spoken aloud on a boardwalk in the middle of nowhere by a stranger, and the sound of it went through me like cold water through a wetsuit — total, shocking, intimate. Twelve years since anyone had said it. I'd said it myself only in the privacy of my own skull, the way you keep a wound bandaged even from yourself.

"That's not my name," I said.

"It's the name on your graduate thesis," he said pleasantly. "It's the name on the internal report you filed in March, twelve years ago, the one that was rewritten by your directorate and used to close the investigation into the Palawan transit incident. It's the name of the junior analyst who saw everything and signed the summary anyway." He tilted his head. "I've read your file. All of it. You were very good. You could have been very good again."

Ruiz shifted beside me, and I put a hand on his arm without looking. The surveyors had frozen among the roots behind us. Somewhere out in the channel, engines idled. Everything hung.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"Director Aldous Wren." He said it the way other men say their names into microphones — with the calm of someone who has never once been asked to repeat himself. "Okafor works for me. Worked," he amended, with a flicker of distaste, as if Okafor had recently become an embarrassment, a relative who drank too much at weddings. "He's been improvising. Improvisation is how operations die. I'm here to end this one cleanly."

"Cleanly," I repeated. The smoke drifted past him. He didn't cough. I honestly believe he was incapable of it.

"There are three outcomes available to you, Elena. May I call you Elena?" He didn't wait for permission; men like him never do, permission is a courtesy they extend downward and he clearly didn't think I was below him, which was somehow worse. "One: you give me the drive, and the four of you — five, I count your engineer in the trees, tell her to stop breathing so loudly — sail away rich and anonymous. Two: you refuse, and my remaining personnel recover the drive from whatever's left of your hull, which will be found burned at anchor, a tragic accident, monsoon season, these things happen. Or three—"

He raised the flashlight slightly, and in its spill his face was almost kind. That was the worst part. Kindness, in that face, was a tool kept oiled.

"—or three, you come back. Your old credentials, restored. Your record expunged — the report, the signature, all of it reassigned to your late director, who conveniently cannot contest it. Your name cleared. Your career resumed at the grade you'd have reached if you'd stayed. Analyst, section chief inside five years. You'd have authority, Elena. Real authority. The kind that can make sure nothing like Palawan happens again — from *inside*, where it can actually be prevented." He smiled faintly. "You've spent twelve years diving in the wreckage of that decision. I'm offering you the chance to stand on the deck instead."

And here is the thing I am not proud of: for about four seconds, I wanted it.

Not the career. Not the office. I wanted the *name*. I wanted to be Elena Vasquez-Reyes in daylight, in rooms with windows, in front of people who knew what I'd done and called it service instead of cowardice. I wanted the version of the last twelve years where I hadn't run — where every nautical mile I'd measured my life in had been a choice instead of a retreat. For four seconds I stood on that boardwalk and let him hand me my own history back, laundered and pressed, and I understood exactly why Sari had said *he believes*. He believed it. That offer wasn't a lie to him. In his mind he was being generous. He was rescuing me from myself.

Four seconds. Then I thought about the man on the cot, crying without noise. About Sari's people under the water off Ambon with no graves. About a container ship full of paper that proved powerful men had decided some deaths were administrative. And about the fact that every one of those things had continued existing, unbothered, for twelve years — precisely because people like me had taken offers like this one, or been too afraid to be offered them.

"No," I said.

Wren's smile didn't move, but something behind it did — a recalculation, quick and cold.

"The drive doesn't exist as a thing you can trade for me," I went on, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Copies exist. You know that, or you suspect it, and either way sinking my boat buys you nothing but a louder story. And I don't want my old name back. I don't want my record cleaned. I want the record *uncleaned*. I want the original report published with my signature on it and my name attached, and I want the world to know that a twenty-six-year-old analyst saw what happened and signed the cover page — because that's part of the truth too, and I'm done renting my silence out to people like you at any price. Triple pay, restored careers, clean slates." I shook my head. "You're offering me the life I ran from. I'm telling you I built a better one. It has saltwater in it and terrible coffee and the best people I've ever known, and none of it is for sale, and neither am I."

The mangroves were silent except for the fire behind us and the water ticking against the pilings.

"You understand," Wren said quietly, "that refusing me doesn't make you free. It makes you a problem. Problems get solved."

"So do tyrants," said Ruiz, mild as morning. "Eventually. Ask around."

Something moved in Wren's jaw — the first crack in the laminate. And then, from the tree line, a flare went up. Not ours. Sari's. It burst red over the compound, and out in the channel her remaining boats came alive with running lights, all of them, converging, and over a loudspeaker in three languages came a woman's voice telling the men in the compound, with terrifying cheerfulness, exactly how many guns were currently pointed at their gate and how few reasons she needed to use them.

Wren looked at the sky. At the lights. At me.

"This isn't over," he said.

"No," I agreed. "But it's over *here*." I stepped past him on the boardwalk, close enough to smell his cologne — sandalwood, of course, power always smells like something expensive pretending to be natural — and said the rest of it quietly, for him alone:

"My name is Elena Vasquez-Reyes. I filed the report. I signed the lie. And I'm the one taking it apart. Write that in your file."

I walked. He didn't shoot me. I think — and this thought kept me company for many nights after — I think some part of him had come hoping I'd refuse. Hoping, maybe, to meet one person in his long career who wouldn't take the deal, so he'd know what one looked like. If so, I hope I was instructive.

* * *

We were aboard and moving within ten minutes, the freed surveyors wrapped in blankets in the cabin, drinking tea that Devi made with the reverence of a priest administering last rites. The crier had stopped crying and started laughing instead, a little uncontrollably, which Devi assured him was normal and also that she had biscuits.

Ruiz took us out through the reef channels on instruments and instinct, running dark, and I stood in the cockpit with the wind coming up out of the southeast — warmer than it should have been, heavier, the air pressure dropping in that way the sea announces weather the way a witness announces testimony: reluctantly, and then all at once.

Devi came up beside me and handed me a mug. "So," she said. "Elena."

"Don't."

"Elena Vasquez-"

"I will throw you overboard."

"You won't. You need me to keep the engine alive." She sipped her tea, insufferably pleased. "For what it's worth — I liked the speech. Very dramatic. The bit about the terrible coffee was a nice touch. Very humanizing."

"It wasn't a speech. It was a refusal."

"Same thing, if you mean it." She looked out at the black water, and her voice lost its tease. "Barometer's falling fast. Ruiz thinks we've got maybe eighteen hours before this front closes the straits. And Wren's boats will be behind us before dawn — he didn't strike me as a man who accepts a no gracefully."

"He struck me as a man who's never heard one."

"Well." Devi drained her tea and set the mug down with a click. "He's heard one now. Get some sleep if you can. Something tells me the next twenty-four hours are going to require all of us, fully awake."

Behind us, far back along the coast, the compound's fires had shrunk to a smudge of orange on the horizon — the last light of the life I'd been hiding in. Ahead, the straits waited, black and narrowing, with a storm gathering at the far end of them like a debt coming due.

I stood my watch, under a name that was finally mine because I'd chosen it out loud, and watched the lightning begin to count itself along the southern horizon.
