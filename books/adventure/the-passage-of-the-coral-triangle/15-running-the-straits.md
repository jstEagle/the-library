## 15. Running the Straits

The engines started dying at dawn, which felt about right. Everything in my life that mattered had a habit of announcing its failure at the worst possible hour, like it wanted an audience.

I was in the engine room when the first one went — the port main, coughing twice and then dropping to a sullen idle that no amount of throttle could argue with. The whole deck above me shuddered, and I heard Ruiz's boots cross the overhead, fast, and then Lena's voice through the hull, muffled but urgent: *Devi. Port engine.*

"I know," I said, to nobody, because I always know before anyone tells me. That's the curse of being the one who keeps things alive — you feel them dying in your hands before they do.

The strait was behind us by then, or rather ahead of us, a forty-kilometer throat of water between two islands whose names I'd learned only as coordinates, because Ruiz refused to say them out loud. He said names invited attention. We'd been running dark since the compound, since the night raid that got three half-starved surveyors off that coast and put every gun in Okafor's network on our tail. Twelve hours of head start, he'd estimated. Maybe ten now. The typhoon fringing the eastern approaches had been our ally for exactly as long as it took the hunters to realize we'd use its edges instead of avoiding them.

I cracked the port main's housing and found the problem in under a minute: fuel starvation, the primary filter clogged with something that wasn't diesel. Grit. Fine, gray, deliberate-looking grit, like someone had introduced it to our tanks on purpose back at the last fuel stop — Ambon, maybe, before the fire, before everything. Or maybe just bad fuel from a dockside drum sold to us by a man who smiled too much. In this business you never got to know which betrayal was personal and which was just commerce.

"Port's down to maybe sixty percent," I called up the ladder. "I can get her back if I swap filters, but I need twenty minutes and I need calm water."

"We don't have either," Ruiz said from the helm, not unkindly. Just stating facts, the way he did, like a man reading a weather report for his own funeral.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being an engineer on a boat full of fugitives: your work stops being maintenance and becomes triage. Every system on the *Sampaguita* — Ruiz's boat, though none of us called it that anymore; she was just *the boat*, the way family members stop using names — was old enough to remember better decades, and I had spent two years keeping her alive with improvisation and prayer, mostly the former. Now the surveyors were aboard, three people who hadn't eaten properly in weeks, who flinched at engine noise, who kept asking questions we didn't have time to answer. And somewhere astern, closing, were boats whose crews had been paid to make sure we never reached the press contact's island.

Lena came down while I had my arms inside the filter housing. She didn't ask how long. She'd learned that much about me. Instead she handed me a rag and held the flashlight, steadying it against the roll.

"How bad?"

"Grit in the fuel. Both mains will go eventually unless I bleed the whole system." I pulled the clogged element free and held it up. It looked like a cigarette pressed flat. "This is sabotage-grade. Somebody planned for us to limp."

"Okafor doesn't plan for us to limp," Lena said quietly. "He plans for us to sink."

"Then let's disappoint him."

She almost smiled. It was a thin thing, stretched over fear, but it counted. She went back up, and I worked, and the boat rolled, and I thought about how strange it was that I'd come to love these people in the least romantic way possible — through shared dehydration, shared danger, shared knowledge that none of us would see home ports again if this went wrong.

* * *

The starboard main started missing at oh-nine-hundred, right as the first shots came.

They came from astern, where the horizon had grown two shapes that Ruiz identified through binoculars with the flat tone of a man confirming a diagnosis: fast patrol boats, black hulls, running white water. Not pirates. Pirates we could talk to. These were the professionals, the ones Sari had warned us about — the hunters' line, spread across the strait's mouth like fingers waiting to close.

"Range?" Lena asked.

"Eleven kilometers. They'll have us inside effective range in fifteen minutes if we hold this speed." Ruiz spun the wheel. "Which we can't. Devi! I need everything you've got and then some!"

What I had was a starboard engine misfiring on two cylinders and a fuel system full of grit, and what I needed was a miracle with a torque spec. So I did what engineers do. I made choices.

Bleeding the whole system meant shutting down both engines, and shutting down both engines meant drifting, and drifting meant dying. So instead I rigged a bypass — a secondary filtration loop using a spare Racor housing and a length of hydraulic hose I'd been hoarding against exactly this kind of day, except in my hoarding fantasies the day involved bad weather, not gunfire. I teed it into the starboard supply line, hand-tightened fittings I'd normally torque, and said a small irreverent prayer to whatever saint looks after improvised plumbing.

"Starboard's going to run rough," I shouted up. "Rough means slow. Slow means—"

"I know what rough means!" Ruiz yelled back, and then the first round went over the wheelhouse with a sound like a whip cracking the sky open.

* * *

There's a specific quality to being shot at while lying on your back in an engine room. You can't see anything. You hear the impacts — some near, some far, and one, later, that hits the hull itself with a deep metallic *whang* that travels through the deck plates and into your spine like a struck bell. You learn very quickly which sounds are dangerous and which are merely terrifying. The terrifying ones are constant. The dangerous ones are rare, and each one rewrites your understanding of the situation.

I was under the starboard engine when the bypass started doing its job badly. Pressure gauge fluttering. The engine hunting — surging, dropping, surging — as grit worked its way past my improvised defenses. Above me, Ruiz was steering by dead reckoning, which is a polite term for navigating by memory, math, and faith. No radar lock — the hunters were jamming, or the storm's fringe was eating the returns, or both. No chart plotter he trusted, because the charts of this strait were drawn by people who'd never taken a loaded boat through it at speed. He had a compass, a stopwatch, a log of turns he'd memorized, and a lifetime of smuggler's instinct.

"Devi," his voice came down the ladder, tight. "I need you to understand something. In four minutes I'm taking us through a channel that has maybe nine meters under the keel at high tide. If the engines quit in there, we're aground, and aground means they board us."

"Then the engines won't quit."

"That's not how engines work."

"That's how *my* engines work today."

I got the pressure stabilized by pinching a valve with a pair of locking pliers and lashing the pliers to the rail with zip ties, which is the kind of solution that makes trained marine engineers wake up screaming, and which I intend to defend until my dying day. The starboard main smoothed out from a death rattle to a heavy, laboring roar. Sixty percent power, maybe seventy. Enough.

Up top, the world was noise. Rounds walking across the water astern, closer with each salvo. The surveyors were flat on the deck below the gunwales, and one of them — the older one, Marisol, the geologist — was holding the youngest one's head down and talking to him steadily, the way you'd talk to a spooked animal, and I loved her for it without knowing her name yet.

Then the channel swallowed us.

* * *

Ruiz took us into the reef passage at full throttle, which is the nautical equivalent of threading a needle during an earthquake, and for eleven minutes I experienced what I can only describe as controlled apocalypse. Rocks rose on either side close enough to read the coral. The water color changed from deep blue to a green so bright it looked radioactive, then to brown-green, then to a pale turquoise that meant *you are now in the margin between survival and salvage*. Ruiz called depths from memory — "nine meters... seven... seven and a half..." — in a voice so calm it was clearly costing him something physical.

And the engines held.

Not because they were good. Because I was good, or lucky, or both, and because I'd spent two years learning this boat's moods the way you learn a difficult person's. When the port main stumbled coming out of the narrows — one last insult from the grit in the tanks — I was already there, already bleeding the line at the second filter, already swapping elements with hands that shook only after the work was done, never during. There's a rule about shaking. Before is useless. After is honest.

We cleared the reef at noon, and the hunters fell back.

Not gone. Falling back to the strait's far side, regrouping, or — worse — radioing ahead. Ruiz watched them go with binoculars and said nothing for a long time, which from Ruiz was a paragraph.

"They'll be waiting at the next choke point," Lena said finally.

"Or the one after." He lowered the glasses. "But we're through the line. First time in two days I can say that."

Marisol crept up onto the deck, blinking, and looked at the three of us — Ruiz at the helm, Lena at the rail, me coming up the ladder with filter debris on my shirt — and said something in careful English that I'll keep longer than most things I own:

"You people move like you've done this before."

"We haven't," Lena said. "That's the trick."

* * *

We ran the rest of the afternoon on the bypass loop, and I checked it every forty minutes like a man checking a pulse, because that's what it was. The boat had become, in the truest sense, a single organism — Ruiz the eyes, Lena the will, me the circulation, the surveyors the reason any of it beat at all. Nobody said this out loud. Organisms don't narrate themselves.

At dusk the barometer started falling faster than it should have, and Ruiz stood at the helm watching the western sky go the color of a bruise, and I saw him do the math he'd been refusing to do all day.

"Devi," he said quietly. "How honest are those engines with me right now?"

"Honest enough to run. Not honest enough to outrun anything."

He nodded slowly. "Because that front—" he tipped his chin at the west, where the clouds had organized themselves into something with intent, something with a name the regional center would give it by morning, "—is going to catch us tonight. And behind us, those boats are going to be running ahead of it too. Everybody runs from weather. Weather doesn't care who it catches."

Lena came to stand beside him. For a moment none of us spoke, and the only sound was the laboring heartbeat of two wounded engines and the wind beginning to find its voice in the rigging.

Then Ruiz pointed east, toward a notch in the coastline that wasn't on any chart worth trusting — a hidden anchorage, a hole in the world where Sari's people sometimes sheltered, if Sari's people still existed, if Sari herself—

The radio crackled. Static, then a voice, thin and broken by distance and interference, but unmistakable:

*"Sampaguita, Sampaguita, this is Sari. Do you copy? Storm is turning. It is not going around you. It is going through you. Find shelter or find God. I repeat—"*

The signal dissolved into static. Ruiz and Lena exchanged a look over the helm, and I stood there with grease to my elbows and a zip-tied valve holding my world together, and understood that the race was no longer against men with guns.

The sea had decided to enter the competition.
