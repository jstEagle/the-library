## 16. What the Storm Takes

The sea stopped being a surface somewhere around four in the morning and became a thing with intentions.

I'd been in weather before. Every working diver has — the squalls that come off the equator like a slammed door, the two-day blows that turn a charter into a shared religious experience of vomiting. This was not that. This was the monsoon's opening argument, and it had done its homework.

Ruiz had us running the strait with the freed surveyors lashed into the cabin like cargo that complained. Three of them, hollow-eyed, salt-cracked, still half-convinced we were another flavor of captor. Devi was in the engine room keeping our one functioning heart beating with prayer and baling wire. Sari's boats fanned out behind us in the gray murk, six hulls riding the swells the way only people who grew up on water can — not fighting it, reading it.

And behind *them*, on the radar and then, briefly, horribly, with the naked eye between lightning stutters, the hunter vessel. Black hull, no name, the same silhouette that had shadowed us on the night passage a lifetime ago. It had found us anyway. It had found us through the leading edge of a storm that should have made the whole strait a no-go for anything without a death wish, which told me everything about what Okafor's people thought we were carrying.

They were right, for the record. We were carrying it.

"Range?" Ruiz shouted over the engine noise. He had one hand on the wheel and the other on the chart table, holding it flat while the boat tried to become airborne.

"Two miles and closing," I called back. "They're faster in this. Their hull's heavier."

"Everything's faster in this. That's the problem with weather. It's an equal-opportunity bastard."

Lightning walked down the strait ahead of us, a white fence with no gate. The rain came sideways, hard enough to sting, and the wind made a sound I'd only heard once before, off Sumatra, a sound like the ocean clearing its throat before it says something final.

Devi's head appeared in the companionway, streaked with grease, one lens of her glasses fogged. "Port engine's down to seventy percent. If I push it, we lose it. If I don't push it, they catch us. Pick your apocalypse."

"Keep it at seventy," Ruiz said. "We're not racing them. We're outlasting them."

"Outlast them into what?"

Ruiz didn't answer, because there wasn't a good answer. The strait narrowed ahead — the reef channels Sari's people knew, water so shallow and broken that a heavy black hull would think twice. That had been the plan. Plans, I've learned, are just the story you tell yourself before the sea starts telling its own.

* * *

The hunter vessel made its move at the mouth of the channel, and it was a mistake, and everyone saw it at the same time.

It came in too fast for the water, riding a following sea that was no longer following but attacking, and its bow dug into a swell that had no business being that tall that steep that close to shore, and the sea — which had been waiting, I'm convinced, the way it waits for everyone eventually — took the invitation.

I watched it happen from the stern rail, one hand locked on the lifeline, and even now I can't describe it cleanly. The bow went in. The bow did not come out. The stern rose, and rose, and hung there for a heartbeat like a thing deciding, and then the whole black length of it slid sideways down the face of a wave and the wave closed over it the way a mouth closes, and the lightning flashed, and where a hundred-foot vessel had been there was only white water and debris and, faintly, carried on the wind, the sound of men.

The sound of men is the same in every language.

"Turn around," I said.

Ruiz was already looking at me. "Lena—"

"Turn the boat around."

"They were trying to kill us. They had orders to kill us. I watched their gun mounts track us for the last hour."

"And now they're drowning." My voice came out flatter than I felt. Inside, I felt nothing like flat. I felt like the water looked. "Turn the boat around, Ruiz. Or give me the wheel and go below, but one of us is turning this boat around."

He held my eyes for three seconds, which in Ruiz-time is a senate debate. Then he swore in two languages and brought the helm over, and the *Mariana's Wake* — our tired, patched, over-loved boat — turned her stern to safety and her bow into the worst water I have ever asked a vessel to face.

Behind me I heard Devi say, quietly, to no one, "Of course we are. Of course this is who we are."

* * *

Sari's boats reached the debris field first. That's the thing about her people — they didn't debate. Half her fleet was already running rescue patterns before we'd completed our turn, hauling men out of the water with the practiced brutality of people who'd done this for refugees, for fishermen, for enemies, for anyone the sea spat back at them.

Then the sea took its payment.

I heard it before I understood it — a different sound under the wind, a sound like a building falling over. Sari's flagship, the old converted trawler she loved like a limb, the one with the repaired gunwale and the children's names carved into the wheel, took a breaking sea broadside. I saw her heel. I saw her keep heeling, past the point where anything comes back, her rail going under, her deck lights — still lit, still lit, someone down there hadn't killed the power — glowing up through green water like a drowned constellation.

Half her fleet had been in her lee. The wave that took her went through them like a hand through a card house.

I stopped counting what I saw after that. The sea was stripping the strait down to its essentials: water, people, and the distance between them.

We got our first survivor ten minutes later. A young man, maybe twenty, blue-lipped, tangled in a line, and Devi went over the side on a tether to cut him loose because I was already busy. That's the arithmetic of rescue in collapsing seas. You don't get to be careful. You get to be fast.

I went in four times.

I want to be honest about what it's like, because people imagine diving in a storm as dramatic, and it is, but drama is the wrong word. It's *administrative*. You go in on a tether, and the water is a solid thing moving in six directions, and you find a human being by touch because you can't see past your own mask, and you get a line on him, and the boat hauls, and the sea tries to keep what it caught. Every time. It tries to keep every single one. It's not malicious about it. It's just the sea. It caught them fair and square and it doesn't appreciate theft.

The third one I pulled out was a boy who couldn't have been eighteen, and when I got him to the rail he grabbed my wrist with a grip that had nothing left in it and said something in a language I didn't know, and Sari — soaked, bleeding from the forehead, standing at our rail because her own deck was gone — answered him softly in the same tongue, and the boy let go and wept.

Sari had lost eleven people in under an hour. I watched her not fall apart the way you watch a load-bearing wall not fall apart, and I understood that it was the same thing I'd been doing for twelve years, and that neither of us was going to talk about it.

The fourth time I went in, I found the man sent to kill me.

* * *

I knew him before I knew him. That's the strange part. The water was black and the lightning made everything a negative of itself, and I got a grip on a jacket collar and hauled a body up out of the churn, and even before the lightning confirmed it I *knew*. Some part of me had catalogued him on the night passage — the silhouette at the hunter vessel's rail, the way he stood, the way he'd watched us with the patience of a professional. Vasquez. Okafor's quiet man. The one who'd have put a line in the water for me eventually, or a bullet, whichever was tidier.

He was unconscious. He was tangled in his own vessel's wreckage, a hawser wrapped twice around one leg, and the hawser was still attached to a section of hull the size of a truck, and the hull was going down, and the tether was paying out, and I had maybe a minute of air and a knife.

Here is what went through my mind, and I report it without pride: *let go.*

Not even as a decision. As a reflex. Twelve years of survival, twelve years of measuring my life in nautical miles precisely so that no one could ever have this kind of claim on me, and my body's first honest vote was to open my hand and swim for the surface and let the sea finish the job it had started. It would have been clean. No body, no blame, no witness. The storm takes what the storm takes. Even Sari's people, pulling their own dead out of the water, would not have asked questions.

The lightning flashed again and I saw his face — slack, young-looking without the patience in it, a thin scar through one eyebrow. A man. Just a man, the way the sea had made all of us just men and women in the last hour, stripped of hulls and flags and orders and names. Down here there was no Okafor, no Ledger, no hunter and hunted. There was a drowning man and a diver with a knife and a minute of air, and the sea didn't care which of us had done what. It would drown us both with the same indifference. It had drowned better people than both of us that morning.

Mercy, I understood then, isn't a feeling. Feelings would have had me let go. Mercy is a choice you make with your hands while your feelings are voting the other way.

I cut the hawser. It took three tries because his leg was wedged and the hull was dragging and my air was going, and on the third try the line parted and the hull dropped away into the dark like a door closing, and I got my arm under his chest and pulled the tether signal twice, hard.

The *Wake* hauled us in. It took four of them to get him over the rail, and when they laid him on the deck he coughed out half the strait and opened his eyes, and the first thing those eyes found was my face.

He knew me. I watched him know me — watched the recognition come up through the drowning like a diver's bubbles, and behind it something else, something that might have been the beginning of a question: *why.*

"Welcome aboard," I said, because wryness is also a load-bearing wall. "Try not to make it weird."

He stared at me. Then he laughed — a broken, salt-racked sound, half cough — and passed out, which I chose to take as a compliment.

* * *

Dawn, when it finally came, was the color of dishwater, and the strait looked like a battlefield that had been mopped. Debris everywhere. Fuel sheen. Sari's remaining boats moving slowly among the wreckage, recovering their dead, and the silence over the water was worse than the storm had been.

We counted. The hunter vessel: eleven survivors out of a crew of perhaps twenty. Sari's fleet: she'd lost her flagship and two smaller boats, eleven dead, four wounded, one of them bad. The sea had taken from both sides with a bookkeeper's indifference, and standing on my deck looking at the survivors of both wrecks — my crew, Sari's people, Okafor's hunters, all of them wrapped in the same blankets, drinking from the same thermoses because there was nothing else — I understood what the storm had actually taken.

It hadn't taken the drive. It hadn't taken the Ledger, or the truth, or the survey team's testimony sleeping below decks. It had taken the story we'd all been telling ourselves. The one where there were sides. The water didn't recognize sides. It never had. It had just been waiting for all of us to figure that out, and when we didn't do it voluntarily, it arranged a lesson.

Devi sat down next to me at the rail, handed me coffee that was mostly sugar and said nothing for a while, which is her highest form of communication.

"You went back in four times," she said finally.

"Three. The fourth was a navigational error."

"You went back in for *him.*"

"He was there," I said. "He was drowning. It was a short list of options and most of them made me someone I'd spent twelve years not being."

Devi looked out at the water, at Sari's boats, at the man lying unconscious under a tarp in the lee of our wheelhouse. "He's going to wake up," she said, "and he's going to have a lot to answer for."

"Or," I said, watching the dishwater light come up on the wreckage-strewn strait, "he's going to wake up and find out the people who saved him are the people he was sent to sink, and he's going to decide what kind of man that makes him. People keep telling me that's a choice. I'm starting to believe them."

As if the universe had been waiting for the cue — it usually is — the man under the tarp stirred, coughed, and said, in accented but precise English, "Lockridge. We need to talk. Before they send the next boat."

I looked at Devi. Devi looked at me.

"Well," she said. "That was fast."
