## 17. The Prisoner

I have been interrogated twice in my life. The first time was by a man in a government building who offered me coffee I didn't drink and a career I did. The second time was by the sea, and it was still ongoing.

They pulled me out of the water an hour after my ship went down — after *she* went down into that collapsing gray chaos to get me, which is the part I still can't make sit straight in my head. Lena Lockridge. The woman I'd been sent to find, sent to stop, and if it came to it, sent to end. She swam into a storm for me because I was drowning. Not because I was useful. Not yet, anyway. Because I was drowning.

That distinction matters more than I can explain, so I'll try anyway, since apparently I'm the one telling this part of the story now. Fate has a sense of humor like a wrench has a sense of humor.

* * *

They kept me lashed to the mast step for the first day, which was fair. I would have done the same, only better — I'd have used cable. Ruiz ran the boat the way I imagine he runs everything: quietly, with a shotgun within reach and a cup of coffee going cold beside it. Devi checked my restraints twice and my pupils four times, muttering about hypothermia and secondary drowning like a woman diagnosing a car she also intended to shoot.

Lena sat across from me through most of it. Didn't ask questions. Just watched, the way you watch weather.

It was Sari who broke first. She came aboard at the anchorage on the second night, wet to the waist, having lost half her fleet and her flagship to the same storm that took my vessel, and she looked at me the way you look at a fire that burned your house down but is currently keeping you warm.

"You're the one they sent," she said.

"I'm one of them," I said. "There were three boats."

"Were." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. Then she crouched, forearms on her knees, close enough that I could see the scar along her jaw that I already knew the history of, because I had read her file. All of their files. That's what I did, back when I was a person with a desk: I read people until they became problems or didn't.

"Your name is Vasquez," she said. "First name Rafael. You were Coast Guard before this. You keep a photograph of your sister in your kit, laminated, because you know what salt water does to paper."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say that wouldn't confirm it.

"I know these things," Sari said, "because men like you read files about me. So I learned to read them back." She stood. "You'll talk. Not because of rope. Because you're tired, and because you've been carrying something heavy for a long time, and because tonight the sea took everyone who could punish you for putting it down."

She was right, which annoyed me then and annoys me still.

* * *

Here's what I told them, starting near the beginning, because the beginning is where the rot always shows.

The operation had a budget line buried inside a maritime security initiative. On paper it was counter-piracy coordination — liaison officers, hull registrations, port intelligence. In practice it was a broom. Twelve years ago, certain officials made certain decisions during a counterinsurgency action in the eastern archipelago, decisions that killed a village's worth of civilians and were written up as a tragic navigational error involving mislabeled ordnance. My agency's job, ever since, had been making sure nobody found the paperwork that proved otherwise. The survey team hadn't been lost. They'd been collected. The wreck they stumbled onto — the container ship with the sealed holds — wasn't an accident of geography. It had been sunk there deliberately, with its cargo intact, because destroying documents is evidence and sinking them is just weather.

"And Okafor?" Lena asked. Her voice was level. Too level. I'd heard that tone before, in debriefs, from officers holding themselves together with procedure.

"Okafor built the broom," I said. "He recruited me eight years ago. He's good. He's genuinely good — he believes in the work, or he did once, the way you believe in a religion you joined for the health insurance." I shifted against the rope; my ribs had opinions. "But something changed in the last few weeks. The orders stopped coming through channels. He started running comms off his own relay. When your boat slipped us at Ambon, we got no tasking afterward. No vector updates. Nothing. For a man like Okafor, silence isn't caution. Silence means he's stopped reporting upward."

Devi frowned. "He's gone dark on his own operation?"

"He's gone rogue on his own operation." I let that land. "Think about what he knows. He knows where the Ledger is. He knows who has it. And he knows that if it surfaces through proper hands — journalists, a tribunal, any institution with a spine — the first question anyone asks is *who managed the cover-up*, and the answer is him. His superiors will burn him to save themselves. He's watched them do it to lesser men for a decade. So he's not trying to contain the story anymore. He's trying to own it or kill it entirely, and either way, everyone carrying it dies. Including, eventually, the survey team. Including his own crews. A man who stops reporting upward has already decided his superiors are dead men walking. He just hasn't told them yet."

Ruiz spoke for the first time in an hour. "And you? Where do you fit in his new math?"

"I was his containment," I said. "The last clean tool. And then my boat sank, and tools that sink get written off." I almost laughed. It came out as a cough. "You want the ugly truth? When I went into the water, my first thought wasn't survival. It was relief. Do you understand how much that scares me? Eight years of doing this work, and the moment the sea had me, some quiet part of me said *finally, someone else gets to carry it.*"

Nobody said anything. The anchorage ticked against the hull. Somewhere ashore, a night bird made its idiot announcement.

Then Lena leaned forward. "The delivery," she said. "If the Ledger has to be unstoppable — not just published, but undeniable, unkillable — where does it go?"

And that, right there, is why I ended up telling them everything. Not the ropes. Not Sari's file-reading. That question. She asked it like a professional asking a professional, and underneath it, something in me that had been waiting eight years for permission stood up.

* * *

"The mistake everyone makes," I said, "is thinking evidence needs to go to authorities. Authorities are the problem. Half the institutions that should receive the Ledger are named in it, and the other half owe their budgets to the ones who are. You hand it to a ministry, it disappears into a review process that concludes in fourteen years. You hand it to local police, the police chief gets a phone call. You hand it to foreign embassies, it becomes a diplomatic note, and diplomatic notes are how governments apologize to each other for things they intend to keep doing."

"So where?" Devi again. Sharp, impatient, brilliant.

"The Hague doesn't take walk-ins, but it takes dossiers — if the dossier arrives complete, corroborated, and simultaneously everywhere. That's the key word. *Simultaneously.* Okafor can kill one copy. He can kill two. What he cannot survive is a document that lands in six newsrooms, two legal clinics, and the ICC's evidentiary unit on the same morning, each holder aware the others have it. After that, suppressing it costs more than absorbing it. That's the whole game. Make the truth too distributed to drown."

"There's a contact," Lena said slowly. "An island. Press. Before the rains—"

"Whoever your press contact is, they're a node, not the network. One node is a target. But there's a man in Jayapura — call him Arif, because that's the only name I ever got — who runs exactly the kind of relay I'm describing. Ex-journalist, half-disgraced, which makes him perfect, because disgraced men have nothing left to lose and excellent grudges. He maintains the distribution list. If the Ledger reaches him intact, with the surveyors alive to authenticate it, he fires it everywhere at once. Okafor's entire leverage evaporates by lunchtime." I looked at each of them in turn. "That's your route. That's why the straits matter. It was never about escaping us. It was about reaching Arif before the monsoon closes the corridor and before Okafor decides that killing you is cheaper than chasing you."

"And he's decided that already," Ruiz said. Not a question.

"He decided it the night his superiors went quiet on *him*. He just hasn't told his crews. Most of them still think they're doing patriotic cleanup." I paused. "Some of them would stand down if they knew. Not all. But some."

Sari, from the shadows near the rail: "And you? Would you have stood down?"

I thought about it honestly, because lying to her seemed both pointless and dangerous. "A year ago, no. Six months ago, maybe. Last week, I don't know." I exhaled. "Then your diver jumped into a storm to pull me out of the water, and now I know. That's the answer. That's the whole answer."

* * *

They cut me loose that night. Not all the way — Ruiz kept my boots, which struck me as deeply practical and slightly insulting — but enough that I could sleep lying down instead of sitting up like a museum exhibit. I lay on deck under a tarp and listened to the boat breathe and thought about mirrors.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about being the hunter: you spend years studying your quarry, and somewhere in there, without noticing, you start studying yourself through their eyes. I knew Lena Lockridge's file cold. I also knew, in the way you know a song you hate, that her file was a fiction — that beneath the salvage diver with the scrubbed history was a junior analyst named something else, someone whose report got bent into a lid for the same atrocity I'd spent eight years keeping covered. We were the same age. We'd sat in the same kinds of rooms, reading the same kinds of documents, and we'd made opposite choices. She ran and hid. I stayed and obeyed. And the sea, with its terrible even-handedness, had drowned my ship and spared hers, and now here I was on her deck, being fed rice by her engineer, while the man who signed my tasking orders planned to sink us all.

Enemy turned mirror. I don't know who said that first — probably someone about to be stabbed.

Near midnight, footsteps. Lena. She sat down across from me without asking, handed me a mug of coffee — real coffee, hoarded, the good stuff — and said nothing for a long time.

"You knew my file," she said finally.

"I knew your file."

"Did you know the name under it?"

"Yes."

She nodded slowly, looking out at the black water. "Does it change anything?"

"It changes everything," I said. "It's the reason I'm talking instead of stalling. You want to hear something pathetic? For eight years I told myself the work was necessary. Containment. Stability. And every time I read your file — and I read it more than I should have, it's a good file, whoever built it understood tradecraft — I felt this small, specific shame, like a splinter I couldn't reach. Because you chose the hard thing and I chose the comfortable one, and I dressed the comfortable one up in duty until I couldn't tell the difference." I turned the mug in my hands. "So no, your name doesn't change anything. It just confirms what I already suspected, which is that I've been on the wrong side of my own conscience since before I met you."

"That's a lot of confession for a prisoner."

"I figure the prison's closing either way. Either you believe me, or Okafor finds us, and honestly the second option has worse food."

Almost — *almost* — she smiled. Then it faded into something more careful. "Vasquez. The distribution list. Arif. How do I know this isn't the long play? You give us a destination, we sail straight into a net."

"You don't know," I said. "That's the honest answer, and you deserve it. Everything I've told you is true — I'd stake what's left of my soul on it, which is more than I'd stake my life, because my life stopped meaning much around the time my ship went down. But trust isn't something I can manufacture for you. It's something you extend and then find out about." I met her eyes. "All I can offer is this: I know Okafor's tactics, his frequencies, his fallback plans, and the location of his fuel cache at Ternate. As a prisoner, I'm ballast. As an ally, I'm the map. You don't have to trust me. You just have to do the arithmetic."

She was quiet a long moment. Then: "Ruiz does the arithmetic."

"Ruiz does the arithmetic," I agreed. "Smart man."

She stood to leave, then paused. "Why did you really tell us? The whole thing. The scope, the names. You could have given us the minimum and kept your options open."

I looked past her at the water, where the stars were doing their indifferent accounting, and told the truth because it seemed rude not to, at that point.

"Because tomorrow, or the day after, Okafor finds us. He's methodical; it's the only thing he loves. And when he comes over the horizon, you're going to need to know exactly what kind of man you're dealing with — not the legend, not the file. The actual man, and what he'll do, and what he won't." I set the empty mug down between us. "And because a decade ago, people like me helped bury a village, and the only currency I have left is information, and I'd rather spend it on the side of the ledger where it might buy something besides silence."

She picked up the mug. At the companionway she stopped, half-turned.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the sea doesn't care whose side you were on. It just takes everyone equally. Maybe that's the only fair thing left out here."

Then she was gone below, and I lay back under the tarp and listened to the boat push north through the dark, toward the straits, toward Arif, toward whatever Okafor was building for us over the horizon.

I slept badly, which was appropriate. Around dawn I heard Ruiz wake Devi, heard the low urgent murmur of voices, heard the engine note change — deeper, harder, a boat deciding to run.

I sat up. Ahead, faint against the graying east, smoke stood up off the water in a thin black column. And below it, just visible, a black hull was crossing our bow line — moving fast, moving toward the island, moving toward the press contact's anchorage with the confidence of a man who no longer needed to hide.

Okafor had found the map before we could use it. And he was already halfway there.
