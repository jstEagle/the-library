## 18. Last Port Before the Rains

I have been a captain for twenty-six years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things worth teaching. One: the sea doesn't hate you, it just doesn't know you're there. Two: every engine failure is a negotiation, and you always lose. Three: when a man anchors offshore instead of coming to the dock, he's not being polite. He's being patient.

The island was called Pulau Terang on the charts and nothing at all to the people who lived on it — a green thumbprint of volcanic rock with a fishing village on the leeward side, a diesel generator that coughed like a two-pack-a-day man, and, if Devi's restored drive fragment was honest with us, a man named Arif Santoso who had spent fifteen years as the region's most stubborn independent journalist. Stubborn in the way that gets you exiled, sued, and occasionally shot at, but never silenced. He was the last address in our book. The last dry, storm-free, reachable address.

The sky behind us had gone the color of a bruise going green. The monsoon's first real front was a wall of cloud stacked to the stratosphere, maybe six hours out, maybe four, and the barometer on my bulkhead had been falling all morning like it had somewhere better to be.

"We make the anchorage, offload to Santoso, and we're done," Devi said. She was at the chart table with a mug of coffee she'd been reheating for two hours because she kept forgetting to drink it. "Done done. Testimony, transmission, and then we point this boat somewhere boring and hide until the world either changes or doesn't."

"That's the plan," I said.

"You said it like a man who doesn't believe the plan."

"I said it like a captain. Belief is for passengers."

Lena was up on the bow with the binoculars, and she'd been up there for twenty minutes without moving, which is how I knew before she turned around and said it.

"Ruiz. We have company."

* * *

The boat sat a mile off the island's northern point, black hull, low profile, riding easy at anchor like it had all the time in the world. Same silhouette as the one that had shadowed us through the night passage a lifetime ago — before Ambon, before the pirates, before the compound, before the storm that took Sari's flagship and gave us back a prisoner named Vasquez who talked like a man settling accounts.

I put the glasses on her. No flag. No name. A wake of heat shimmer off her generator exhaust and a RIB tied astern, fueled and ready, the way a man keeps a horse saddled when he's expecting to ride at a moment's notice.

"Okafor," Lena said. Not a question. She'd known him longer than the rest of us, and she said the name the way you'd say the name of a disease you'd already survived once.

"He got here first," Devi said. "Of course he got here first. He's had better engines and worse morals the whole way."

Vasquez came up from the aft cabin, wrists still in the plastic ties we'd swapped for proper cuffs two days ago when he'd stopped being cargo and started being, God help me, an ally. He looked at the black hull and his face did something complicated — recognition, then disgust, then a kind of tired professional appraisal, like a chef seeing someone else's kitchen.

"That's the *Meridian*," he said. "Okafor's own. He doesn't send it. He rides it."

"Then he's serious," I said.

"He was always serious. That's the problem with Okafor. He's not a thug, he's an administrator. He'll have a proposal. It'll be reasonable. It'll be monstrous. And he'll present both halves in the same calm voice, and the monstrous half will sound like the price of the reasonable half."

I looked at the island, green and ordinary, smoke rising from cooking fires. Somewhere on it, a journalist who didn't know he was about to become the most important man in the Coral Triangle. Behind us, a storm front with our names on it. Ahead, a mile of flat water and one anchored boat full of patience.

"Everybody below," I said. "We're going to talk about a bluff. And I need everyone to understand the rules of bluffing before we do it, because the first rule is that the bluffer has to believe it harder than the man he's lying to."

* * *

Here's the situation as I laid it out on the chart table, with the coffee finally drunk and the barometer still falling and the four of us — me, Lena, Devi, Vasquez — crowded around a chart that had more pencil on it than ink.

The drive was real. One copy, on Devi's person at all times, in a waterproof case taped inside her jacket like a second heartbeat. The survey team — three of them, alive, rescued from the compound four days ago — were in the aft cabin under the care of Sari's surviving lieutenant, a quiet woman named Intan who had volunteered to see this through because, as she put it, the sea owed her family a debt and she intended to collect. The journalists' contact was ashore. The storm was coming.

And Okafor was sitting between us and all of it, anchored, patient, with a RIB and, presumably, a proposal.

"He can't just take the drive," Devi said. "If he boards us by force, there's a fight, and even if he wins, the surveyors testify to the boarding. He's an administrator. He wants clean."

"He wants deniable," Vasquez corrected. "There's a difference. Clean is when nothing happened. Deniable is when something happened and nobody can prove who did it. He'll trade, not take. He'll offer something we want for something he wants, and he'll make sure the trade looks voluntary."

"What does he want besides the drive?" Lena asked.

Vasquez looked at her for a long moment. He'd been doing that for two days — looking at her like he was still deciding what she was. A target. A witness. A mirror. "You," he said finally. "The drive is the mission. You are the loose end. Twelve years ago there was an analyst who wrote a report, and the report was used to bury the incident, and the analyst disappeared. Okafor's superiors have spent twelve years assuming she was dead or harmless. Now they know she's neither. He wants the drive *and* a signed statement, your silence, your disappearance — your choice of terms — in exchange for the surveyors."

The cabin went quiet. Below us, I could hear the surveyors moving around — a cough, the clink of a cup, the small ordinary sounds of people who had been prisoners a week ago and still flinched at footsteps on deck.

"And if we refuse?" Devi asked.

"Then he waits. He's anchored in the lee of the island and we're not. The front hits in four hours and we either run into open water in a building storm with three fragile witnesses aboard, or we anchor in his shadow and let him pick the moment. He doesn't need to beat us. He needs to outlast us. Administrators are very good at outlasting."

I looked at the chart. I looked at the barometer, which had dropped another two points while Vasquez talked, the atmospheric equivalent of a man clearing his throat.

"Then we don't refuse," I said. "And we don't trade. We bluff."

Lena's eyes came up. "Ruiz."

"Hear me out. The man's weakness isn't greed or cruelty. It's uncertainty. He's an administrator — administrators can't act on incomplete information, they can only wait on it. So we give him information. We tell him the drive is already copied. Three copies, three custodians, three separate boats already moving. Sari's people. Santoso's network. A dead-man's switch with an international lawyer. We tell him that sinking us or taking the drive accomplishes nothing except turning three quiet custodians into three loud ones."

"That's a lie," Devi said. "There's one drive. It's in my jacket. I've been sleeping sitting up."

"I know," I said. "It's a beautiful lie, though. Look at the shape of it. It has the one thing a man like Okafor can't verify and can't ignore: it makes every option worse. If he believes it, taking the drive is pointless, killing us is worse than pointless, and his only move is to cut his losses and disengage. If he doesn't believe it, then he has to decide we're bluffing, and to decide that, he has to bet the entire operation on being right about three strangers in a storm."

"And if he calls it?" Lena said. "If he looks at you and sees the lie?"

I thought about that. I thought about the honest answer, which was that if he called it, we were finished, all of us, and the surveyors with us, and the storm would tidy up whatever the guns left behind.

"Then he calls it," I said. "And we find out what we're made of. But he won't. Not because I'm a good liar. Because he's spent his whole career managing risks, and a risk with three heads is the one risk he was never paid enough to take."

* * *

We spent an hour building it. That's the part nobody tells you about a bluff — it's not nerve, it's carpentry. You build the thing plank by plank until it holds weight.

Devi seeded the fiction with detail, because detail is load-bearing. She'd "sent" fragments to Sari's network two weeks ago — she named the checkpoint, the courier, the dead-drop protocol, all of it invented but all of it built on real procedures Sari's people actually used, so if Okafor's people checked the shape of the story against anything they knew, it would ring true. Vasquez contributed the internal vocabulary — the operation's actual codename for the evidence chain, the actual name of the tribunal lawyer Okafor's superiors feared, the actual number of people who'd have to be silenced to make the problem go away. "Eleven," he said. "It's eleven. They have a list. They've always had a list. Put yourself on a list with ten other names and a dead-man's switch, and suddenly you're not a loose end anymore. You're an organizational liability."

"And me?" Lena asked.

"You," I said, "are the hardest part."

She looked at me across the chart table, and for a second I saw not the diver I'd hired two months ago in a harbor bar but whatever was underneath — the analyst, the fugitive, the woman who had spent twelve years sanding herself down to nobody. The bluff needed her to sit across from Okafor and lie about the one thing she'd built her whole life to stop lying about.

"You don't have to do it," I said. "I can run the meeting."

"No." Quiet, but it landed like an anchor. "He'll want me. And if I hide behind you, he'll smell it. He's spent his whole career smelling fear." She rolled her shoulders like a diver checking her gear. "I can do it. I've been lying about who I am for twelve years. One more afternoon of it is nothing."

It was the saddest brave thing I'd ever heard, and I've heard a lot of brave things in engine rooms.

* * *

The front arrived ahead of schedule, the way weather and bad news always do. Not the storm itself — that was still an hour out, a black rampart on the southern horizon with lightning walking inside it — but the leading edge of wind, flattening the sea to a hard gray shine, bending the palms on the island like a crowd craning to watch.

I took the *Coral Rose* out to meet him. Not all the way — I anchored four hundred meters off the *Meridian*, close enough to talk, far enough that boarding meant a RIB run in building chop, which is a commitment. I put Lena, Devi, and Vasquez in the cockpit behind me and left Intan below with the surveyors and a shotgun she'd asked for politely and I'd handed over without a word, because there are conversations you don't need to have twice.

Okafor came out on his deck as we set the hook. He was smaller than I'd imagined — I'd built him out of other people's fear, and fear builds people large — a neat, silver-templed man in a dry polo shirt, holding a mug of coffee, waiting for us with the unhurried air of a man who had already won and was simply confirming the paperwork.

I took the RIB across with Lena beside me. Devi and Vasquez stayed on the Rose, visible, armed, and very much alive — hostages to fortune, both directions.

He greeted us at his rail. "Captain Ruiz. Ms. — well." A small, courteous pause, a knife wrapped in a napkin. "Ms. Lockridge. You've had a difficult passage."

"Occupational," I said.

"May I offer you coffee? It's decent. I had it flown in."

"You may not. Let's talk, Mr. Okafor. The weather and I have an appointment."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not anger, never anger; recalibration. He'd expected a frightened man. He'd gotten a man with a schedule. I've found in life that the fastest way to negotiate with someone who thinks he's already won is to be mildly annoyed at the inconvenience.

"Of course." He folded his hands. "Then I'll be direct. You are carrying a storage device containing material that has caused a great deal of trouble. You are also carrying three witnesses whose continued health is, I'm told, a matter of some concern to you. I am authorized to offer the following: the witnesses are released — escorted, safely, to any port of your choosing. The device comes to me. And Ms. Lockridge signs a statement of non-disclosure and steps out of this story permanently. In exchange, no one is prosecuted for anything — the harbor fire in Ambon, the compound, the regrettable events of the last several weeks. Everyone walks away. Everyone lives."

"And the eleven names on your list?" Lena said.

I'll give him this: he didn't flinch. He just looked at her with something almost like respect, the way a chess player looks at an unexpected move.

"The list is a contingency," he said. "Contingencies are expensive. I'd rather not spend this one."

"Then I have news for you," Lena said, and her voice was steady as a depth sounding. "The list is already spent. The drive was copied at Sari's anchorage. Three custodians, three routes, one of them a dead-man's arrangement with counsel in Singapore. You have the names — Vasquez will confirm the protocol, since he built half of it. Taking the device from us now buys you a plastic case full of nothing and turns eleven quiet people into eleven witnesses. That's not a contingency anymore, Mr. Okafor. That's a class action."

Okafor looked at her for a long time. Then at me. Then back at the *Coral Rose*, where Devi stood at the rail with her arms folded and the waterproof case's outline just barely visible under her jacket — because we'd wanted it visible, wanted him to see the thing he couldn't have, the way you show a dog the bone and lock the cupboard.

"Captain," he said slowly. "You understand that if I believe you, I have to disengage. And if I disengage and you're lying, my career ends in a tribunal. So I have to ask, and I have to ask you to understand why I'm asking." He leaned on the rail. "Where are the copies *now*?"

And that was the moment. I felt it the way you feel a barometer drop — the whole bluff balanced on the next sentence, the storm front twenty minutes out and the first gusts already worrying the anchor rode, and this neat, patient, dangerous administrator leaning across a rail waiting to hear whether three strangers in a dying light were worth his entire organization.

I opened my mouth to answer.

And that's when the radio on his belt crackled, and a voice said something in fast, clipped Indonesian, and Okafor's face — for the first time since we'd come alongside — stopped being patient.

He looked past my shoulder, toward the southern horizon, where Sari's remaining boats had just come around the island's far point under full throttle, flying no flag at all.
