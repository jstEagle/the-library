## 19. The Exchange That Wasn't

Okafor's boat sat at anchor a half-mile off the island like a statement of intent. Black hull, no name painted on the bow — just a registration number that I'd have bet my remaining savings was as fictional as my own identity. He'd arrived in the night. Of course he had. Men like Okafor didn't travel by day; daylight was for people with nothing to hide.

Ruiz stood at the rail of the *Mariana's Promise* with binoculars and the expression of a man doing arithmetic he hated.

"One skiff coming across," he said. "Four men. Okafor's in the bow seat."

"Of course he is," Devi muttered from the engine hatch, where she'd spent the last six hours coaxing our port engine back to something resembling trustworthiness. Her hands were black to the elbow and her patience had died somewhere around hour four. "Men like him always come personally. It's not about the drive anymore. It's about winning."

"Then we let him think he's winning," Ruiz said.

I looked at him. He looked back at me with the particular calm he'd been wearing since Ambon — the calm of a man who has already priced out his own death and found it acceptable, which is either wisdom or brain damage, and with Ruiz it had always been hard to say which.

"You're sure about this bluff?" I asked quietly.

"No," he said. "But here's what I know. Okafor is a middleman who's stopped being useful to his masters. The intercepted orders Vasquez gave us prove they've cut him loose. A man like that doesn't know yet that he's dead. Our whole job this morning is to keep him from finding out until it doesn't matter."

"And if he finds out anyway?"

"Then Sari's people surface, there's shooting, and we find out how good Devi's engine repair really is."

Devi, without looking up: "It's excellent. Unlike your plan."

The skiff came alongside. Four men climbed aboard, three of them wearing the flat, patient faces of hired muscle, and behind them Okafor in a linen shirt pressed so sharply it could have cut glass. He looked rested. He looked tanned. He looked like a man on holiday, if the holiday were extortion.

"Lena," he said warmly. "You look well. Monsoon agrees with you."

"It hasn't started yet."

"Give it an hour." He smiled and gestured at the deck, the boat, the horizon, as though appraising property. "May we talk somewhere private? Or does your crew insist on witnessing everything now?"

"They witness everything," I said. "That's rather the point of us."

Something flickered behind his eyes — irritation, quickly filed away. He was a man accustomed to rooms where things happened off the record, and we were refusing to be a room.

"Very well." He clasped his hands. "Let me be direct, since subtlety seems wasted on you. You have a drive. I want it. In exchange, I return your surveyors — all three, alive, fed, only mildly traumatized — and you all sail away rich and anonymous. Triple the original contract. Plus a bonus for your trouble. This is, frankly, the best offer anyone will ever make you."

"That's quite a pitch," I said. "Did you practice it in the mirror?"

"Lena—"

"Because here's my problem with it." I kept my voice level, almost bored, which took more effort than anything I'd done in twelve years of diving. "You don't have the surveyors. Your superiors do. And per the orders your own man Vasquez carried before we fished him out of the sea, those superiors have disavowed you. So you're not offering me their hostages. You're offering me hostages you'd have to go beg for. Which means the thing you're actually selling me is time — enough time for your people to decide whether to clean up this mess themselves or let you drown with it."

The silence that followed lasted maybe four seconds. On a boat, four seconds is a geological era.

Ruiz used every one of them. He leaned against the rail, arms crossed, and said mildly, "Also, copies exist. Three sets. One went out with a fishing boat two days ago. One's with a lawyer in Darwin. One's somewhere even I don't know about, because Devi handled that one and she doesn't tell me things."

"I don't," Devi confirmed.

"So even if you take the drive," Ruiz continued, "and even if you sink us — which, note, would require you to explain to your former employers why their problem got bigger instead of smaller — the Ledger still surfaces. The math stopped working for you days ago, Mr. Okafor. We're just the last people willing to tell you so."

I watched Okafor process it. He was good — better than I'd given him credit for — and for a moment his face did almost nothing at all. But almost nothing, under pressure, is its own confession. His jaw tightened a quarter-inch. His thumb moved once along the seam of his trousers. And his eyes went, briefly, involuntarily, to the horizon northwest of us.

Where, right on schedule, the sea began to boil.

They came up out of the water like something from a bad dream about the ocean: five low hulls riding barely above the swell, engines idling, men standing along the gunwales with the unhurried posture of people who had done violence often enough that it no longer required urgency. Sari's fleet. What remained of it — half of what she'd lost in the storm, but half of Sari was still more than most navies wanted to deal with in these waters.

Sari herself stood in the lead boat, arms folded, watching Okafor the way a cat watches something small and stupid near the edge of a table.

"Ah," Okafor said. His voice had gone up half a register. "An escort. How theatrical."

"They insisted," I said. "They lost people getting us here. They consider the Ledger a family matter now."

"Then perhaps we can all be reasonable." He turned to me and lowered his voice, and for the first time the salesman's polish cracked and something older showed through — something tired and genuinely afraid. "Lena. Listen to me. Whatever you think you know about my superiors, you know less than I do. They don't negotiate. They erase. Give me the drive and I can still protect you. I built the machinery that makes people disappear in this country — I can unbuild it for you. Your name, your history, your little life on this boat. I can make it all safe again."

And there it was. Not the threat. The offer. The real one, the one underneath every conversation we'd ever had: *give me back your silence and I'll give you back yourself.*

Twelve years ago I would have taken it. Twelve years ago I had taken it — taken the new name, the new trade, the thousand nautical miles between me and the report I'd signed. I knew exactly how heavy anonymity felt in the hand. I also knew, now, what it cost to carry.

"No," I said.

It came out quieter than I intended. Just one syllable, dropped into the space between us like a coin down a well.

Okafor blinked. "No?"

"No. And not because of the copies, which are real, by the way — Ruiz wasn't bluffing about those, only about how many. And not because of Sari's boats, though I wouldn't test them. I'm saying no because I spent twelve years being safe, and it turns out safe was just another word for complicit, and I'm done with it. You can't give me back the person I was before. She's the one who helped bury this. I'm not interested in her resurrection."

He stared at me. Behind him, his three muscle-men were doing the quiet arithmetic of professionals: counting Sari's guns, counting their own odds, arriving at numbers they didn't like.

That was when the radio on Ruiz's belt crackled.

Vasquez's voice, thin through the static, relayed from the island where she'd been monitoring the band with Devi's salvaged equipment: *"Intercepted transmission, addressed to Okafor's vessel, authentication code verified. Reading it verbatim: 'Operation concluded. All assets withdrawn. Subject personnel are no longer recognized as acting under authority. Further actions are unauthorized and will be treated accordingly.' End message."*

*Treated accordingly.* In the dialect of men like Okafor's masters, that phrase had a body count attached.

I watched him hear it. Watched the last scaffolding of his certainty come down — not all at once, but in stages, like a building demolished from the inside. His shoulders dropped a centimeter. His hands, which had been clasped with such executive confidence, fell apart and hung at his sides. For a moment he looked less like a fixer than like a man standing in the ruins of his own job description.

"They're leaving me," he said. Not to anyone. To the air.

"Yes," I said. "About an hour ago, I'd guess. Maybe less. Which means everyone on your boat is currently deciding whether they're still employed."

His crew decided fast. Professionals always do. The nearest one simply stepped back from the rail and put his hands where everyone could see them, which in the grammar of armed men is a full sentence meaning *not my funeral*. The other two followed within seconds, a chain reaction of self-preservation. Nobody pointed a gun anywhere. The entire confrontation deflated like a punctured dinghy — all that menace, all that money, all those years of Okafor's careful terrible work, undone not by firepower but by a forged-feeling radio call, a bluff about photocopies, and the sight of pirate boats rising out of the sea on cue.

Nerve. That was all it had been. Ruiz's nerve, mostly, spending borrowed confidence like it was cash. I made a mental note never to play cards with him again, and then remembered I'd already lost to him twice in Ambon, and retired the idea permanently.

Okafor straightened. To his credit — and I use the word loosely, the way you'd credit a shark for swimming efficiently — he composed himself with visible effort.

"The drive," he said hoarsely. "Even disavowed, I could still—"

"You could still nothing," Sari said.

She had come aboard without anyone noticing — a woman of her size should not be able to move that quietly, and I suspected she cultivated the impossibility deliberately. She stood an arm's length from Okafor, and everything about her posture said that the arm's length was a courtesy, not a limit.

"You killed my family's village twice," she said. "Once with fire, twelve years ago. Once with paperwork, every year since. The papers on that drive have my mother's name in them. Filed under *assets neutralized*." She let that sit. "You will leave this water. You will tell whoever still takes your calls that the Ledger is beyond recovery. And you will spend the rest of your life knowing that a salvage crew and a pirate fleet beat you with a bluff and a radio."

Okafor looked at her for a long moment. Then, remarkably, he nodded — a small, precise nod, the nod of a bureaucrat acknowledging receipt.

"The surveyors," I said. "Before you go."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, whatever ambition had lived there was gone, replaced by something flatter and more honest: a man calculating his own survival, and finding the hostages suddenly worthless as leverage and expensive as liabilities.

"Coastal compound, north end of the strait," he said. "Gate code is seven-four-one-nine. My senior man there is called Braga. Tell him the account is closed and he'll stand down — he's loyal to payroll, not to me." He paused. "There's a launch fuelled and ready in their boathouse. Use it. Take them and go, and don't look back, because the people who disavowed me today will not be as polite tomorrow."

"Why help us?" Devi asked, suspicious to the marrow.

Okafor almost smiled. "Because you're the last people on earth who might remember I existed, and I'd prefer to be remembered as the man who surrendered gracefully." He turned to his crew. "Back to the boat."

They went over the side in silence. The skiff pulled away, trailing its wake across the bright morning water, and nobody on our deck moved until it reached the black hull and the black hull began, slowly, to raise anchor.

Sari watched it go, then exhaled through her nose — the closest thing to laughter I'd seen her produce since the storm took her flagship.

"A bluff," she said to Ruiz. "Copies in three hands. Was any of it true?"

"The fishing boat was true," Ruiz said. "The lawyer in Darwin was true. The third copy—" he shrugged elegantly, "—is true as of this morning. Devi finished uploading it to a server an hour before Okafor boarded. I merely declined to mention the timing."

Devi made a sound that was halfway between a laugh and a sob. "You gambled our lives on an upload speed."

"I gambled them on your upload speed," Ruiz corrected. "Which is why I checked it twice."

Sari shook her head slowly, and then, unexpectedly, she put her hand on my shoulder — brief, hard, gone again in a second, like everything else she allowed herself.

"The compound," she said. "We sail within the hour. If Braga stands down, good. If he doesn't—" her teeth showed white, "—then today the sea gives back some of what it took."

I looked north, toward the strait and the compound and three strangers who had vanished into a hole in the world and were waiting, probably, to vanish entirely. Somewhere behind us, Okafor's black hull was shrinking toward the horizon, carrying its captain toward a tribunal he'd once run for other people. Ahead lay the last rescue, the handover, the storm front stacking up on the edge of the sky like a debt coming due.

"Seven-four-one-nine," I said aloud, testing the numbers, making them real.

Ruiz was already moving toward the helm. "Devi! Engines!"

"If you say *if she breaks down mid-rescue, blame the plan*, I'm throwing you overboard," Devi shouted back, already sliding down into the hatch.

The *Mariana's Promise* woke beneath my feet, both engines turning over, the whole battered honest hull trembling with purpose. I took the wheel while Ruiz ran the charts, and we turned our bow north into the strait — toward the gate code, the boathouse, the three ghosts of the survey team, and whatever version of myself would be left standing on the far side of the next twenty-four hours.
