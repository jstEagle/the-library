## 20. Handover

The journalists arrived the way weather arrives in the Triangle — first a rumor on the horizon, then all at once, and then you were inside it.

There were four of them. Two from an international wire service, one documentary producer with a camera drone she kept in a padded case like a sleeping infant, and a print reporter named Adeyemi whose byline Lena — I — had read for years without ever imagining I'd be handing her the end of my old life in a plastic dry bag.

They came off the ferry at dawn, blinking, sunburned already, and looked at our boat — *Vela*, salt-scoured, patched, one engine still held together with Devi's stubbornness and marine epoxy — and I watched them do the math. This was not the operation they'd expected. We were not the people they'd expected.

"Twelve years of documents," Adeyemi said, "and it came to this. A dive boat and six exhausted people."

"Seven," Ruiz said, nodding toward the surveyors' tent on the beach. "The surveyors count too."

"Seven," she agreed, and opened her notebook.

* * *

We'd spent two days preparing for this, which felt absurd given that we'd spent three weeks running for our lives to make it possible. But Devi insisted, and Devi was right: evidence delivered sloppily is evidence destroyed. Chain of custody, authentication, redundancy. She'd made three copies of the Ledger's restored drive, hashed them, printed verification codes, and written out a provenance document so thorough it read like a confession of its own.

"It has to survive contact with lawyers," she told us, hunched over the galley table at two in the morning, soldering iron in hand, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of wetsuit. "Because the moment this goes public, every lawyer those officials can buy will descend on it like gulls on a fish head. If there's one loose thread, they'll pull until the whole thing unravels into 'unverified allegations.'"

"And if there are no loose threads?" Ruiz asked.

"Then they'll pull anyway," Devi said. "But they'll come up empty-handed, and empty hands look bad in front of cameras."

Sari's remaining boats had anchored in the cove overnight — three hulls now instead of nine, the arithmetic of the storm still visible in the way her crew moved, quieter than before, closer together. Sari herself came aboard at first light without asking permission, which was her way, and sat on the rail drinking coffee like a woman attending a wedding she'd paid for personally.

"You're sure about this," she said to me. Not a question, really. A door left open.

"I'm sure."

"You could still take the money. Okafor's gone, but his kind grow back. There will be offers."

"There have been offers." I turned my cup in my hands. "That's sort of the whole problem."

She studied me for a long moment, then nodded once, the way you nod at a diver about to go down on a deep profile. Not agreement. Acknowledgment. *You've done your checks. The rest is yours.*

* * *

The surveyors testified from the island because Adeyemi wanted it that way — their faces against the water where their colleagues had vanished, the same beach where we'd found shoes lined up neatly outside empty tents. The satellite link was temperamental; Devi spent forty minutes arguing with a router as if it were a junior crew member who'd disappointed her, and finally got a signal strong enough to hold.

Dr. Imelda Cruz went first. She was the team lead, the one who'd kept the other two alive through whatever those weeks in the compound had been, and when the connection stabilized and a face appeared on the laptop screen in some newsroom twelve time zones away, her voice didn't shake at all.

"We found the wreck on day nineteen of the survey," she said. "A container vessel, sunk intact, cargo sealed. Inside were documents — shipping manifests, internal correspondence, financial records — establishing that the incident twelve years ago was not an accident, was not an engagement gone wrong, but an operation authorized at the ministerial level and subsequently concealed through the destruction of evidence and the elimination of witnesses."

*Elimination of witnesses.* Four words, spoken calmly, that contained entire villages. I stood at the back of the frame, out of shot, and watched her say them, and thought about how long those words had lived sealed in steel under sixty meters of water while the sea kept everyone's secrets for free.

The interviewer asked how she could verify the documents hadn't been fabricated.

"They can't be verified by me," Dr. Cruz said. "They'll be verified by the institutions that created them, who will now have to explain why their own letterhead is on orders they claim never existed. That's rather the point."

I liked her immediately and completely.

Then it was my turn, and I had known it would be, and I had rehearsed nothing, because everything I might have rehearsed was a lie and the entire purpose of the morning was to stop lying.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about giving up a name you've worn for twelve years: it doesn't feel like taking off a coat. It feels like surfacing too fast. Everything expands at once — the light gets brighter, the pressure drops, and somewhere in your chest the air you've been holding comes out whether you're ready or not.

Adeyemi set up the interview on *Vela*'s aft deck, camera framing me against the wheel, Ruiz's domain, the place I'd been most myself for over a decade. She asked the standard questions first. How did you come to be involved. What did you find. Were you afraid.

Yes, yes, and yes, in various arrangements.

Then she closed her notebook, which I'd learned by then was what she did when she was about to stop being polite.

"Our sources indicate the salvage operator who recovered these materials is operating under an assumed identity," she said. "Is that true?"

The cove went very quiet. Devi paused mid-task with a cable in her hand. Ruiz, at the helm, went still in the particular way he went still when he'd decided not to intervene. Even the water seemed to hold its chop.

"Yes," I said.

"Do you want to tell us your name?"

And here's the thing — I'd imagined this moment a hundred times during the crossing, usually at three in the morning on watch, and in every version of it my throat closed, or I deflected, or I gave them something partial, a first name, a fragment, a compromise. In every version I kept a little of the wall up, because the wall was load-bearing. It had been holding up my whole architecture of escape since the day I'd walked out of a government office with a resignation letter and a new surname and gotten on a boat heading anywhere.

But standing there, with the Ledger authenticated and hashed and triple-copied, with Dr. Cruz's voice still hanging in the salt air, with Sari watching from the rail and Vasquez's warning ringing somewhere in the back of my skull — standing there, I found the wall wasn't holding anything up anymore. It was just a wall. And walls, I knew from wrecks, are just things waiting to come down.

"My name is Elena Vasquez-Reyes," I said. "Before that, it was Elena Reyes. I was a junior analyst with the maritime intelligence directorate. Twelve years ago I wrote a report flagging anomalies in the logistics chain around the incident — fuel purchases, hull movements, communications gaps that didn't match any official account. My report was rewritten before it went upstairs. The version that reached decision-makers concluded there were no anomalies worth investigating. My name stayed on it anyway."

Adeyemi didn't move. Good journalists are like good divers: no wasted motion, total attention.

"I signed off on the cover-up without knowing I was signing it," I went on, and my voice did shake now, but I let it. "Not actively. Passively. By not digging. By taking the reassignment they offered and telling myself the questions I had were above my clearance. Then I resigned, changed my name, and spent twelve years underwater, telling myself I'd escaped. I hadn't escaped. I'd been hiding. There's a difference, and the difference is that hiding doesn't require anyone else's permission, and escape does, and I never asked."

"What changed?"

"The survey team found the documents I should have demanded twelve years ago. And then people started dying to keep them buried, and I realized the report I didn't finish wasn't finished with me." I looked directly into the lens, because Adeyemi had angled the camera slightly and I let her. "My report was used to bury the truth. So it seems right that my testimony helps dig it up. Full name, on the record: Elena Reyes. Whatever happens next, attach it to me. Not to the crew who protected me. Not to the surveyors. To me."

Devi made a small sound, quickly suppressed, that I chose to interpret as professional approval of the redundancy strategy.

* * *

Afterward, I sat on the foredeck alone for a while, which lasted about ninety seconds before Ruiz came and sat beside me, which is its own kind of privacy with him.

"So," he said. "Elena."

"Lena's fine. It always was. Just shorter."

"Elena Reyes." He tried it out like a knot he was testing. "Huh. Doesn't fit you."

"It fits fine. It's just heavier."

He nodded at the water. "You know what I keep thinking about? The day you hired on. You gave me references. Paper references. I remember thinking, nobody this young has paper references this clean unless somebody cleaned them."

"You hired me anyway."

"I needed an engineer who could dive and you needed a boat. I figured whatever you were running from would either catch up or it wouldn't, and either way you'd show me who you were long before any document did." He shrugged. "Took twelve years. Slowest background check in maritime history. But you showed me."

"That's either very touching or very damning of your hiring practices."

"Both things can be true. Ask Devi."

From the galley, without looking up: "It's both things."

* * *

The transmission went out at 1600 local time. Devi had rigged the satellite uplink on the highest point of the island, a granite knob above the beach, and we all gathered around a laptop smaller than a chart folio to watch the story publish — the Ledger excerpts, the authenticated scans, Dr. Cruz's testimony, and beneath it all, in the fourth paragraph, my name. My real one. Sitting in a sentence, in public, where anyone in the world could read it, including every person I'd spent twelve years making sure couldn't find me.

The first response came within eleven minutes. Devi was monitoring the wire feeds and read it aloud: a statement from the ministry denying everything, calling the documents "fabrications assembled by criminal elements," and announcing a commission of inquiry.

"A commission," Sari said flatly. "They investigate themselves."

"They investigate slowly," Adeyemi corrected, packing her gear with the brisk efficiency of someone who'd buried governments before. "Commissions are where truths go to age. But aging isn't erasure. Not anymore. The documents are mirrored in nine jurisdictions as of an hour ago. They can deny the story. They can't un-publish it."

"They'll come for the witnesses," Ruiz said.

"Some of them," Adeyemi agreed, without softening it, which I respected. "Which is why the surveyors fly out tomorrow on a press charter, publicly, with names attached. Sunlight as security detail. It's imperfect. It's also the only armor that scales."

I thought about Vasquez, somewhere in Sari's custody below decks, the man sent to kill me whom I'd pulled out of the sea, and wondered if he was listening to any of this, and what it did to a man to hear his employers called *criminal elements* by the machinery he'd served. That was a problem for tomorrow. Tomorrow had a lot of problems. I let it keep them.

At sunset, Sari found me on the beach. The surveyors were packing; the journalists were filing; Devi was, inevitably, still arguing with the router.

"You understand what you bought today," Sari said.

"A tribunal subpoena and a lifetime of looking over my shoulder?"

"No." She looked out at the water, where her three boats rode at anchor, understaffed and scarred and hers. "You bought the right to stop swimming. All these years, you moved because staying still meant being found. Now you're found, and you're still here. Do you know what that is? That's the first free choice you've made since you were a girl in that office."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth to argue. Nothing came out, because she was right, and we both knew the particular silence of a person recognizing a truth they'd been avoiding.

"Your fleet took losses getting us here," I said finally. "Names I don't even know. I want — I need there to be more than a news cycle at the end of it."

"There will be. Or there won't. Both are possible. That's what justice is — a possibility you fight for, not a guarantee you're owed." She clapped me on the shoulder, hard enough to mean it. "But tonight, the possibility is real. Tonight, eat something. Sleep. Tomorrow the lawyers arrive, and you'll need your strength for people far more dangerous than pirates."

* * *

I slept badly, which surprised me. I'd expected — I don't know. Catharsis with a bow on it. Instead I lay in my bunk listening to the anchor chain tick against the hull and ran the name over and over. *Elena Reyes. Elena Reyes.* Like learning a new dive site from a chart: technically accurate, emotionally unmapped.

Near midnight I gave up, climbed into the cockpit, and found Devi already there with two cups of tea, having predicted my insomnia with the same accuracy she predicted engine failures.

"Regrets?" she asked.

"Ask me after the tribunal."

"That's a dodge."

"It's a scheduled dodge. There's a difference." I took the tea. Below us, phosphorescence trailed in the wake of a night-fishing boat, green fire in black water. "Devi. When you restored that drive — before you knew what was on it, before any of this — did you ever think about just wiping it? Selling the corruption? Nobody would have known."

"Every day for about four hours," she said. "Then I read the first file." She sipped her tea. "You don't unread things, Lena. Or Elena. Whichever. You especially don't unread things when you realize the reason nobody stopped it sooner is that everyone who could have chose not to. I decided I'd rather be the person who chose to."

We sat with that. Somewhere across the cove, one of Sari's crew sang quietly to himself in a language I didn't know, a low looping melody like a work chant, like something meant to keep hands moving and hearts steady through a long task.

"Tomorrow," Devi said, "the lawyers get here, and the subpoenas start, and your old life starts sending you mail again. Are you ready for that? Because I've seen you field-strip a regulator under fire, and I've seen you face down a pirate queen, and I have genuinely never seen you open official correspondence without swearing."

I laughed — really laughed, the first time in weeks — and the sound went out over the dark water and didn't echo, just dispersed, like everything else the sea takes and keeps.

"No," I admitted. "But I'm done being ready. Ready was the old life. From here on I'm just going to show up."

Devi raised her cup to that. And that was when Ruiz's head appeared through the companionway, backlit and grim in a way that ended conversations.

"Radio traffic," he said. "Coastal station, encrypted burst, addressed to this anchorage by description. It's not the ministry." He handed me the printout, and I read it twice, and the tea went cold in my hands.

It was from Okafor. Still alive, still disavowed, and now, apparently, operating entirely on his own authority — which made him more dangerous, not less. The message was short. It named the island. It named a rendezvous. And the last line was the one I read aloud, so we'd all carry it equally:

*"You have the story. I have the last witness. Come alone, or the record dies with him."*

Below decks, behind a locked hatch, Vasquez lifted his head at the sound of our voices — and I remembered, with a cold that had nothing to do with the night air, that Okafor's message hadn't said *her*.
