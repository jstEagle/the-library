## 21. After the Snows of Rain

The news reached us the way most important things do in the archipelago: badly, late, and through someone's cousin.

Devi here. I should probably explain that I'm writing this down at all, because for six weeks after we handed over the Ledger, my instinct was to delete everything. Old habit. You spend enough years keeping a boat's systems running on duct tape and denial, you learn that the safest file is the one that doesn't exist. But Ruiz said — and I'm paraphrasing, because his actual phrasing involved more profanity — that if we were going to have our lives turned inside out by history, we might as well keep receipts.

So. Receipts.

* * *

The story broke on a Tuesday, which felt anticlimactic. I'd always assumed revelations of state-sponsored atrocity would arrive with thunder, or at least a decent headline font. Instead it came as a wire service item buried under a football scandal: *Documents Implicate Former Officials in 2013 Maritime Incident; Parliamentary Inquiry Announced.*

"Maritime incident," Lena read aloud, her voice flat as ballast water. "Twelve years of cover-up, two hundred and eleven people dead, and they're calling it an *incident*. Like it was a fender bender."

"It's how these things start," Vasquez said. He was still aboard then — technically our prisoner, practically our extra pair of hands, emotionally somewhere none of us could chart. He'd spent three days in testimony protection interviews via satellite phone from our aft deck, and he'd come back from each one looking like a man who'd been run through a wringer and found slightly cleaner on the other side. "First it's an incident. Then it's an inquiry. Then, if you're lucky, it's a trial. The language shrinks so the guilty can fit through the gaps."

"You would know," Ruiz said. It wasn't cruel, exactly. It was Ruiz being Ruiz — a man who believed that uncomfortable truths, like bilge pumps, work best when run continuously.

"I would know," Vasquez agreed, and didn't flinch, and somehow that made it worse.

I was in the engine room during most of that conversation, because there is only so much geopolitical reckoning a person can absorb before she needs to go torque something. But I heard it all through the deck plates. Sound travels beautifully through a hull. It's why I like boats better than people — a boat never pretends it isn't making noise.

* * *

The investigations, when they actually began, moved with the speed of a glacier wearing formalwear.

We watched from Makassar, where we'd put in for real repairs — the kind that required parts we hadn't burned in a dock fire, thank you very much, Ambon — and where the charter office had mysteriously decided our paperwork was fine after all. Funny how a global news story makes bureaucrats flexible.

The parliamentary inquiry announced hearings. The hearings announced witnesses. The witnesses included three of the four surviving survey team members, testifying remotely from locations the press was politely not asking about. I watched the feed with Sari's youngest scout, a nineteen-year-old named Bayu who had appointed himself our self-declared security detail and mostly appointed himself to our galley.

"That one," Bayu said, pointing at the screen, where a woman named Dr. Imelda Cruz was describing the container wreck in careful, exhausted detail. "She cried during the break. I saw it on the other camera."

"They usually do," I said. "Crying's just data leaving the body."

Bayu looked at me like I was insane, which, fair.

Some guilty fell. That's the honest accounting. A deputy minister resigned within nine days — nine days, which in government time is roughly the speed of light — and was arrested trying to board a flight to Singapore with what the papers called "significant assets." Two naval officers retired abruptly. One shipping magnate's entire corporate structure dissolved overnight like sugar in rain, though the man himself had apparently decamped to a jurisdiction with excellent beaches and no extradition treaty, because of course he had.

Many didn't fall. That's the other half of the honest accounting, and the half nobody puts in the triumphant op-eds. The inquiry's scope kept narrowing like a channel at low tide. Witnesses recanted or vanished into "administrative leave." A key archive suffered an unexplained fire that the fire marshal attributed, with a straight face I could hear through the radio, to "electrical fault." Twelve years is a long time. Long enough for everyone who signed anything to be promoted, transferred, pensioned, or dead. The machine that buried those documents had been built by professionals, and professionals build machines that survive their own removal.

Lena took it hardest, or rather, she took it most quietly, which for Lena is the same thing but with better posture. She'd given her real name on record — Elena Vasquez-Reyes, junior analyst, the report, the whole archaeology of her old life — and I think she'd expected the naming to feel like an ending. Catharsis, closure, all the words the journalists used. Instead it felt like what it actually was: a door opening onto a corridor with more doors.

"They're going to bury it again," she told me one night on the foredeck, both of us pretending to look at stars. "Slower this time. With committees."

"Probably," I said, because lying to Lena had stopped being possible around chapter twelve of our collective disaster. "But here's the thing about slow burials. Somebody has to keep shoveling. And every shovel is now a documented shovel."

She almost smiled. "You've been talking to Vasquez."

"He talks. I fix engines. We all have our burdens."

* * *

Sari rebuilt faster than anyone.

I don't mean her fleet, though that too — within a month she had four boats running again, painted fresh, running fish and cargo and, I suspect, some cargo that would decline to be specified. I mean *her*. When we'd met her, she'd been a toll gate with a grudge, a survivor who'd turned the sea between her and her dead into a business. After the Ledger went public, she became something else, something she clearly hadn't planned on being: a symbol. Journalists wanted her. Advocacy groups wanted her. The inquiry itself, in a move of breathtaking cynicism, sent her a letter inviting her to testify, as if the people whose predecessors had burned her village were owed her grief on schedule.

She sent back a reply that Bayu read aloud to us, cackling. It was one sentence long. I won't repeat it verbatim, but it involved a suggestion about where the committee could store its invitation, delivered with a nautical specificity that suggested she'd thought about it carefully.

But she did rebuild. She came to see us once, in Makassar, arriving unannounced the way she did everything, stepping aboard with two guards and a crate of mangoes as if she owned the deck. Maybe she did. For an afternoon, she acted like it.

"You lost people getting us through," Lena said. "Ruslan. The others."

"Yes." Sari set down the mangoes. She wasn't a woman who softened, exactly, but she had a way of going very still that was its own kind of honesty. "And you gave me the names of the men who made them ghosts. Ruslan's daughter can visit a grave now instead of a rumor. That is not nothing. That is not everything either. But my people have learned to count in fractions."

"We're good at that," Ruiz said from the wheelhouse door. "Fractional survivors, all of us."

Sari looked at him for a long moment, then at me, then at Lena, and did something I'd never seen her do. She laughed — short, rusty, like an engine turning over after a cold season. "Fractional survivors. Yes. I will steal that phrase. Consider it part of your toll."

She stayed for dinner. She ate everything Devi-cooked — sorry, I ate everything she cooked, force of habit referring to myself in third person when I'm narrating, it's a problem — and she left before dawn without goodbye, which was, Bayu assured us, the highest compliment in her vocabulary.

* * *

Vasquez disappeared the way he'd promised he would, which is to say completely and with suspicious competence.

One week he was doing engine watches and teaching Bayu card games he always inexplicably won. The next, a gray-hulled government launch with no name on the stern pulled alongside at 0400, two people in civilian clothes collected him and a single duffel bag, and that was that. No forwarding address. Testimony protection, they called it. Witness relocation, others called it. I called it what it looked like: a man being filed away in a drawer until the trial needed him, at which point he'd be dusted off, produced, and re-shelved.

He left a note wedged in my toolbox, because of course he knew I'd find it there. It read:

*Devi — the portside fuel line was chafing. Fixed it, but check my work, I'm a hunter not a mechanic. Tell Ruiz the coffee was an abomination and I drank it anyway. Tell Lena — actually, tell Lena nothing. She knows. — V*

I checked his work. It was perfect. I hated him a little for that, and missed him more than I expected, and have decided never to tell him either thing if I ever see him again, which I probably won't, because men who vanish into testimony protection tend to stay vanished. Though I'll admit I sometimes scan gray launches in harbors now, out of habit. Just in case the drawer opens.

* * *

Okafor's tribunal was the part I couldn't stop watching, and I want to be precise about why, because it wasn't schadenfreude. Or not only schadenfreude — I'm wry, not a monster.

Here was a man who had managed tribunals. That was his actual function, we learned from the leaked files: Okafor was the mechanism by which inconvenient inquiries were steered, delayed, defanged, and closed. He was the professional closer. And now he sat on the wrong side of the table in a hearing room in Jakarta, in a suit that no longer fit him quite right — stress does that, eats a man from the shoulders in — answering questions from a panel that included at least two people whose careers he had, by the evidence of the Ledger, personally saved or destroyed depending on the day's requirements.

I watched the footage eleven times. Not the testimony — the *face*. There's a moment, about forty minutes into day three, when the chair of the panel asks him a question about a memo, and Okafor begins to answer with the smooth, calibrated fluency of a man who has talked his way out of everything his whole life, and then stops. Mid-sentence. You can see it happen. The machinery jams. Because the question isn't one he prepared for, or maybe because he finally understood that the room wasn't buying, or maybe — and this is the reading I favor — because he looked up and saw, in the public gallery, a face he recognized from twelve years ago. Someone connected to the incident. Someone who'd lost everything while Okafor managed the paperwork of the loss.

Whatever it was, he stopped talking. And for about four seconds of broadcast time, the man who had closed every inquiry he ever touched sat silent in front of one, with nothing left between himself and the record.

Four seconds. I timed it. I'm an engineer; I time things.

It wasn't justice. Justice would have been two hundred and eleven funerals that never needed to happen. But it was the sound of a lock turning, and I'll take it.

* * *

The verdicts, when the first round finally came, were exactly the mixed bag everyone predicted. The deputy minister: guilty, twenty-two years, appealed instantly. The two officers: one convicted on reduced charges, one acquitted entirely on grounds that made the legal analysts use words like "troubling" and "curious" in tones of barely suppressed fury. The shipping magnate, sunning himself in his extradition-proof paradise: charged in absentia, which everyone understood was a document, not a sentence.

Lena read the summaries aloud at dinner, and we each reacted according to type. Ruiz grunted and poured another drink. Bayu swore creatively in three languages. I did the math — convictions divided by indictments, a ratio I wrote on the whiteboard above the nav table where it stayed for a week like a scar.

"Fifty-eight percent," I announced. "If this were an engine, you'd overhaul it."

"If this were an engine," Ruiz said, "you'd have fixed it in a day and complained about the manufacturer the whole time."

"The difference being engines don't lie about their maintenance records."

"Engines absolutely lie about their maintenance records. You just speak their language."

And Lena said nothing at all, just looked at the number, and then at the window, where the harbor lights were coming on, and I understood that she was doing different math. Hers had variables I didn't have — a report she'd written at twenty-six, a name she'd worn like a borrowed coat for twelve years, people from a past she'd amputated who were now, piece by piece, walking back into the light whether she wanted them to or not.

Later, much later, she told me what she'd been thinking. "I kept waiting to feel finished," she said. "Like handing over the drive was supposed to close the account. But it doesn't close. It just converts. Everything I owed in silence, I'm paying out loud now, and the payments are going to take years."

"So don't pay them alone," I said, which is the closest I get to sentiment, and she knew it, and squeezed my shoulder once, hard, engineer-style, and let it go.

* * *

Which brings me to now, and to the hook, because Ruiz insists every log entry ends with a heading toward something.

Six months after the handover. The boat is rebuilt better than she's ever been — new fuel lines (thanks, Vasquez, wherever you are), new electronics, a galley Bayu has annexed as sovereign territory. The inquiries grind on. Sari's boats run the straits openly now, flying a flag nobody challenged anymore. My corrupted-drive recovery tools have been requested, formally, by two separate investigative bodies, which means I accidentally became an expert witness, which means I own a blazer now, which I want noted as a sacrifice.

And yesterday morning, a letter arrived. Actual paper, actual stamp, forwarded through three addresses and the charter office. It was from Dr. Imelda Cruz — survey team, the one Bayu saw crying during the hearing break. She's leading a new expedition, she wrote. Back to the same waters. Officially sanctioned this time, properly funded, with the inquiry's blessing and a mandate to document the full wreck site for the historical record.

They need a salvage consultant. Someone who knows the site, the currents, the equipment. Someone, the letter said, "who has already proven she can find what others buried."

The letter was addressed to Ms. L. Lockridge.

Lena stood on the foredeck holding it for a long time, the trade winds pulling at the page. Lockridge, or Reyes? The name she built, or the name she paid for? I watched her decide, and I'll tell you what I saw: not hesitation. Selection. Like a diver choosing which tank to rig.

Then she turned around, held up the letter, and grinned at us across the deck — the first full grin I'd seen on her since before any of this started.

"Pack the gear," she said. "We're going back to the beginning."

Ruiz was already at the helm. Bayu was already asking about the food stores. And I went below to check the engines, because whatever waits out there — more ghosts, more paperwork, more of history's unfinished business — it'll go easier if the boat's honest, even if nothing else is.

Besides. Somebody has to keep the receipts running.
