## 22. Miles, Then Years

The wreck hadn't moved. That was the first thing I noticed, and the second thing I noticed was that I'd expected it to.

Twelve years of diving had taught me that the sea rearranges everything eventually — hulls settle, sand migrates, cargo spreads itself across a reef like a confession nobody asked for. But the *Meridian Star* sat exactly where I'd left her, four months and one lifetime ago, her stern buried to the rail in coral silt, her holds still holding what they held. The manifests were gone now, of course. Devi had photographed every page before we cut them free, and the originals had gone into evidence bags, then into lawyers' hands, then into whatever purgatory governments keep for documents that embarrass them. But the ship remembered. Ships always do. They're just bad at talking unless someone comes down with a light and a camera and the patience to listen.

"Bottom's at forty-one meters," Ruiz said in my ear, his voice arriving through the comm line with the faint crackle of a system Devi had rebuilt twice since Ambon. "Current's running lazy. You've got all day if you want it."

"I want all day," I said.

"Figured." A pause, and then, because he's Ruiz and silence makes him itch: "Devi says the new camera rig is recording. Says if you flood it again she's docking your pay."

"My pay is a percentage of a charter that barely covers fuel."

"Then she'll dock your percentage of nothing. She's very thorough."

I laughed, and the regulator turned it into a fog of bubbles, and above me the surface broke into coins of light that drifted down like slow money. Below me the *Meridian Star* waited with her secrets already spent, which made her, in my experience, the rarest kind of wreck there is — an honest one.

I'd come back to document her. That was the official language of the charter anyway, the words we'd put on the permit application that Sari's people had helped grease through two harbormasters' offices: *archaeological survey, non-invasive, photographic record only.* What it meant was simpler. It meant I was diving a wreck not to take something out of it or to lose myself inside it, but to write down what happened there. To make a record. For twelve years I had used wrecks as doors out of rooms I couldn't stand to be in. This was the first time I'd ever used one as a page.

The old habit rose anyway, halfway down the descent line. The old arithmetic. *Forty meters, twenty minutes of bottom time, three-minute stops at nine and six.* Numbers like rungs on a ladder leading away from the surface, away from names, away from everything that knew me. I caught myself counting them the way you count stairs in a house you're leaving, and I made myself stop. Not because the numbers weren't true. They were truer than ever; Devi had drilled the tables into the whole crew after the straits crossing, after we'd learned what happens when you improvise with depth. I stopped because I no longer needed them to mean *escape.* They could just mean *depth.* It's amazing how much lighter a number gets when you stop loading it with your whole biography.

The hold came up out of the blue like a cathedral with its roof torn off. Coral had colonized the ribs in the months since — soft corals first, the opportunists, their polyps open and feeding in the current, purple and orange and that particular bruised pink that only exists underwater. A school of fusiliers poured through the frame of the aft hatch, reversed itself, poured through again, a liquid argument about which way was better. On the deck plate where I'd found the manifest case, a lionfish had taken up residence, fins spread like a warning written in a language of elegant threat.

"Say something for the record," Devi said. Her voice came through thinner than Ruiz's, filtered through the camera rig's hydrophone channel. "Date, site, diver. Standard header."

"Lena Lockridge," I said. And then, because the word had been sitting in my chest for four months waiting for a safe place to set itself down, I said the other one too. My real name. Quietly, into the recorder, into the water, where the only witnesses were fish and a dead ship and two people topside who already knew.

It didn't hurt. That surprised me most. Saying it the first time — on the island, on camera, with the journalists' recorder running and the surviving survey team watching from the shade of the comms tent — that had hurt, the way setting a broken bone hurts, one long sickening moment of things moving back where they belong. But this was different. This was just a name doing its job. Identifying the diver on a survey dive off Sulawesi, water temperature twenty-nine degrees, visibility eighteen meters, subject: the *Meridian Star*, cargo vessel, sunk under circumstances documented elsewhere. My name was data now. Useful, attached to a body of work, belonging to a person who chose it rather than fled from it. You can't imagine what that's worth until you've spent twelve years paying rent on a lie.

* * *

We surfaced near noon, and the boat was exactly the kind of mess that means a crew has stopped performing for each other. Devi's workbench looked like a bomb had gone off in an electronics store and the bomb had feelings. Ruiz had a chart spread over the wheel and weighted with a coffee mug, a wrench, and half a mango, which told me he'd been planning routes through lunch again, which told me he was happy. Six months ago he planned routes like a man digging tunnels out of a prison. Now he planned them like a man choosing where to eat dinner.

"Good dive?" he asked, handing me a towel that had seen better decades.

"She's still there."

"They usually are. It's people who move." He tapped the chart. "Insurance assessor flew into Kendari yesterday. Wants us to do a structural survey on a freighter that went down in '09, some dispute about whether the sinking was weather or negligence. Pays well. Boring as sin."

"Boring sounds incredible," I said, and meant it, and he grinned because he knew I meant it, and that was the whole conversation. That's what recovery looks like from the inside, by the way. Nobody tells you. It looks like being excited about a boring job.

Devi emerged from below with a laptop in one hand and a bowl of noodles in the other, eating with the focused disrespect of someone who considers meals an interruption to work but refuses to skip them. She set the laptop on the bench beside the camera housing and turned the screen toward me without ceremony, because Devi has never once in her life used ceremony when a direct approach would do.

"Footage from the hold. I did a quick color pass." She stabbed a noodle, chewed, swallowed. "Also, the tribunal feed updated overnight. Two more arrests. The deputy minister for fisheries — fisheries, can you believe, the man was signing death warrants between press releases about sustainable tuna — and a port authority director from Bitung. Both named in the Ledger. Both singing."

"Singing or screaming?"

"There's a difference?"

"With men like that? Screaming is when they confess to things that are already public. Singing is when they give up names nobody's got yet."

"Then singing." She turned the screen fully toward me. Grainy footage of a man in handcuffs being walked past reporters, his face doing the thing faces do when the floor disappears — not guilt, exactly. Arithmetic. Men like him are always doing arithmetic right up until the cell door closes, and then, briefly, they look almost relieved, like the equation finally balanced and they can rest. I'd seen that face before, in another life, in briefing rooms with bad coffee. I'd helped build the floor that disappeared under this one. Not the arrest — the original cover-up, the report I wrote at twenty-six that took eleven deaths and filed them under *navigational error.* Four months ago I'd have said I helped build it. Now I'd say I was a brick in it. The distinction matters. Bricks don't choose the wall. But bricks can be pulled out, and I'd been pulled, and the wall had a hole in it shaped like me, and other people had climbed through.

"You okay?" Devi asked, watching my face instead of her noodles, which for Devi was practically a medical event.

"Yeah. It's just strange. Watching it happen and knowing how it happens. Knowing the machinery." I handed back the towel, or tried to; it had migrated somewhere. "For years I thought if anyone ever opened that machinery up, I'd be standing next to it with my fingerprints on everything. Turns out fingerprints wash off if you spend twelve years in saltwater."

"That's not how fingerprints work."

"It's how mine worked."

Ruiz grunted from the wheel, which was his way of contributing to emotional conversations without having to have one. He'd gotten no better at feelings in six months. He'd just gotten slightly better at standing near ours.

* * *

That evening we anchored in the lee of a green island whose name I never learned, one of those Sulawesi outliers that appears on charts as a smudge and in reality is a small steep world of coconut palms and one family's fishing stilt-house and dogs that barked at our anchor light with genuine philosophical outrage. Sari's people had a camp two bays over — what remained of her fleet was rebuilding there, three boats where there had been eleven, and she'd sent word asking us to come by tomorrow because one of her young divers had found something on a reef and wouldn't shut up about it. Sari's messages always read like that. *Come by. Kid found something. Bring Devi, the kid likes Devi, God help us all.*

I sat on the foredeck after dinner while the others argued below about whether the generator's rattle was a bearing or a personality trait. The sky over the archipelago was doing its dry-season exit performance — hard stars, no moon yet, the air holding its breath the way it does in the last weeks before the monsoon turns the whole sea into a argument. A year ago, sitting in this exact posture on this exact deck, I would have been counting miles. Distance to the next port, distance to the edge of the next patrol line, distance between me and the version of my life I'd buried. Miles had been my calendar. You can't measure years when you're trying not to have any.

But somewhere in the last few months the units had changed. I'd noticed it first in Ambon, of all places, walking through the rebuilt market with Devi, past the scar on the pier where the fire had been, past the stall of the harbor contact whose friendship we'd burned and whose forgiveness we'd earned back slowly, in installments, the way you repay anything that matters. I'd caught myself thinking *that was two monsoons ago,* except it wasn't, it was five months ago, and the mistake wasn't carelessness. It was recalibration. My internal clock had switched currencies. Seasons instead of nautical miles. Rain instead of distance. Time you stay instead of time you flee.

It's a smaller unit, a season. Less room to hide in. A mile can swallow a whole identity; a wet season just soaks you and makes you sit with yourself until the sun comes back.

Below decks, the argument resolved itself in the traditional manner — Devi declared victory, Ruiz declared he'd never been arguing, and the generator kept rattling, vindicated. Ruiz came up with two beers, handed me one, and settled against the rail with the sigh of a man whose knees have opinions about ladders.

"So," he said. "Where next?"

He asked it casually, but I'd learned to hear the weight under his casual. It was the same question he'd asked me a hundred times over six years of running charters together, and for a hundred times it had been a logistics question. *Where next* meant *which port, which client, how many days' steam.* Tonight it meant the other thing. Tonight it meant: *now that you're not running, where are you going?*

I let the beer sweat in my hand and looked south, where the water went dark and the charts went thin.

"The kid Sari wants us to meet," I said. "She found something on a reef two bays over. Probably nothing. Old anchor, maybe. Could be a modern wreck, could be trash."

"Could be."

"Or it could be a ship nobody's recorded. Something from the spice era, even. There's hundreds of them out here, Ruiz. Whole centuries of trade lying in thirty meters of water and nobody's written down a single plank of it." I turned the bottle in my fingers. "The tribunal's going to grind on for years. Investigators will want the Meridian documentation, the survey records, all of it — fine, it's theirs, I'm done carrying secrets. But there's other wrecks. Ones that never hurt anybody. Ones that are just history, sitting in the dark, waiting for somebody to come down with a light and say *here, this happened, write it in the book.*"

"That's not an answer to where next."

"It's the beginning of one." I took a breath, and named it — the place I'd never been, the bay on Halmahera's north coast that Sari's scouts had mentioned once, offhand, the way smugglers mention things, a deep-water anchorage where an eighteenth-century brig had supposedly gone down with its whole story intact. I'd never dived it. Never charted it. Couldn't tell you what the currents did there or which village sold the best diesel or where the reef teeth hid. All I knew was the name and the rumor and the fact that when I said it out loud, my chest did something it hadn't done in twelve years.

It didn't tighten. It opened.

Ruiz listened, nodded slowly, and reached into his shirt pocket for the stub of pencil he kept there for reasons he'd never explained, and wrote the name on the margin of the chart weighted down with the mango half.

"Halmahera," he said. "Never been either." He looked up, and in the anchor light his face had the expression I'd started thinking of as his heading-confirmed face — the one he wore when the needle settled. "Monsoon'll seal the north passages for four months. We go after. March, maybe April, when the water clears."

"After the rains," I said.

"After the snows of rain," he corrected, because he'd heard Devi use the phrase once and adopted it the way he adopted all of Devi's vocabulary — without permission and permanently. He tapped the pencil twice against the chart, a little drumroll of decision. "I'll plot it tomorrow. Devi'll complain about the fuel range. The kid from Sari's camp can show us the reef if the something she found is worth showing."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then we find something else. That's the job now." He drained his beer and stood, knees cracking their editorial remarks. At the companionway he paused, back to me, and said the thing he'd probably been constructing the whole evening, disguised as route talk. "You know the difference between the woman who hired onto this boat six years ago and the one sitting on my foredeck tonight? The old one asked where next like she was checking exits. You ask it like you're picking a destination."

He went below before I could decide whether to be embarrassed, which was his gift to me, always — he gave you the feeling and then the privacy to survive it.

I stayed up a while longer. The dogs across the bay had concluded their philosophical position on anchor lights and gone quiet. Out past the reef the water breathed against the coral in long slow strokes, and somewhere under all of it the *Meridian Star* lay in her honest dark, holding nothing now but coral and fish and the memory of being useful to a story.

Twelve years I'd measured my life in miles, because miles end. You sail enough of them and you can convince yourself you're going somewhere, when really you're just putting ocean between yourself and the mirror. Seasons are harder to outrun. They arrive whether you've finished becoming whoever you were becoming or not. The monsoon takes the straits, the dry season gives them back, the coral grows a centimeter, the tribunal hands down another verdict, the kid on the reef finds another something, and you're still here, still named, still on the boat, still looking south.

Ruiz's pencil marks sat on the chart margin in the anchor light. Halmahera. A place I'd never been, going to a wreck I'd never seen, on a schedule written in rain instead of distance.

I finished the beer, killed the deck light, and went below to sleep like a woman with somewhere to be.
