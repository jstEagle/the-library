## 1. Salt on the Wind

The wind came off the dunes carrying salt that had no business being there — three hundred miles from any coast, and still the Empty Quarter breathed brine at dawn, as if it remembered being a sea. Odile Amberdale had learned to read that salt the way other rangers read stars. It meant weather coming out of the west. It meant the season was turning. It meant she had maybe two days before the soft sand turned to soup and every track in the Quarter dissolved like sugar in tea.

Two days to catch Sable.

She crouched at the crest of the ridge and let her fingers hover an inch above the sand without touching it. His prints were there, faint as breath on glass: the outside edge of the right boot worn down from years of walking walls, the left stride half a finger longer than the right because of some old injury he'd never told anyone about. She'd been reading this signature for six years. She could have drawn his feet from memory.

"He camped here." Bex came up the slope behind her, breathing hard, goggles pushed up into a mop of oil-black curls. The engineer carried a battered water condenser over one shoulder like a sleeping child. "Fire ring's cold but not *cold* cold. Ash says four hours, maybe five."

"Then we're close."

"We're always close." Bex set the condenser down with a clank that sounded like an accusation. "Boss. Three weeks we've been chasing this man across half the Quarter. We're low on water, Ferro's knee is singing opera, and Lune hasn't slept in two days because she keeps dreaming he's standing behind her. Which, honestly, given what he is, might just be good instincts."

Odile didn't answer. She was looking north, where the dunes ran in long golden waves toward a smudge on the horizon that might have been cloud and might have been the smoke of Qamar-at-Tin, the last border town before the land went forbidden.

"Bex."

"Yeah?"

"Do you see that?" Odile pointed.

Bex squinted. "Smoke?"

"Chimney smoke. Qamar burns dung fires this time of year — they smell like a latrine. That's too clean. That's a town running its foundries." She stood, brushing grit from her knees, and felt the familiar click inside her chest, the sound of purpose engaging like a gear. "He's resupplying. He'll buy boots, water, and a lie about where he's going. And then he'll go exactly where the lie points, because Sable never lies about anything important. He lies about everything *unimportant*. It's how he rests."

"You know him that well after six years of hunting him?"

"I know him that well," Odile said, "because I taught him half of it."

She said it lightly, the way you'd mention an old scar, and started down the leeward side of the dune before Bex could ask the question underneath the question. Behind her, the engineer muttered something to the condenser, and the condenser, being an honest machine, agreed.

* * *

Qamar-at-Tin smelled of tin, mostly, and hot metal, and under that the sweet rot of dates going over in the market crates. The town sat in the elbow of a dry riverbed like a knife in a sheath, all vertical lines and shadowed alleys, built by people who'd expected the river to come back someday and were still waiting. Odile came in through the eastern gate with her hood up and her ranger's badge turned inward on its cord, because in Qamar the badge was worth less than the cord.

The crew fanned out the way they always did, wordless, a choreography they'd never rehearsed and couldn't have explained if asked. Ferro took the market square, buying supplies and listening with those big quiet ears of his. Lune went up — rooftops, minaret stairs, anywhere the town laid itself out like a map. Ash simply vanished, which was Ash's way; he moved through crowds the way water moved through cloth, leaving no mark and taking information with him when he went.

Odile went to the caravanserai.

It was the third building she checked, after the chandler's and the water-office, and she knew before she reached the courtyard that she'd missed him by hours. She knew it from the way the stable boy wouldn't meet her eyes. From the way the innkeeper's ledger showed a room paid for in silver — Sable paid in silver, always, because gold got remembered and silver got forgotten — for one night only. From the fresh bootprints in the stable muck, walking *out*, the right heel digging deeper than the left, a man moving fast and light and pleased with himself.

She stood in the empty room anyway. Sat on the cot. Looked at the window he'd left open a hand's width, letting in the smell of tin and evening.

On the sill, weighted by a pebble, lay a single dried fig.

Six years ago, in another life, in a green valley that no longer existed except behind her eyelids when she let them close too long, someone had left dried figs on her windowsill. Someone who used to say, *you can't outrun grief, Odile, I've tried, it has longer legs.*

She picked up the fig. Turned it over once. Ate it, slowly, standing at the window, watching the last light drain out of the western sky, and hated him with a hatred so old and worn it had gone smooth as a river stone, and something else underneath the hatred that she refused, as always, to name.

Then she went downstairs and asked the innkeeper which caravan master had bought passage north.

* * *

They gathered in the chandler's back room among coils of rope and barrels of pitch, and Odile laid it out on the scarred table with a charcoal stick and the edge of her hand.

"Sable bought passage with the Marid caravan. They leave tomorrow before first light, heading north along the old pilgrim road." She tapped the table. "Except the pilgrim road doesn't go north past the Wadi Sarh. It turns east. So either he's riding with them to the turn and doubling back—"

"—or he's not following the road at all," Lune finished. She sat perched on a barrel, twin to her brother in face and nothing else; where Ferro was all patient mass, Lune was held together entirely by tension, like a bridge cable. Her eyes were red-rimmed from the dreams. "North past the Wadi Sarh is the Glass Reach. Nobody crosses the Glass Reach in spring."

"Nobody crosses it ever," Ferro said mildly, sorting rope by feel. "That's why they call it forbidden instead of difficult."

"The Reach is closed by decree of the Oasis Compact," Bex said. "Sealed routes, sealed wells. Even the rangers don't—" She stopped. Winced slightly, remembering who she was talking to. "Present company."

"I've been de-certified for four years," Odile said. "You can say it."

"It's suicide with paperwork," Bex said.

Ash spoke for the first time all day, from the corner where he'd materialized out of the dusk. His voice was soft and had the quality of something kept underground a long time. "There's water in the Reach. Old water. Sealed wells from before the Compact, from before the wars. If someone had a map of them, they could cross anywhere in the Quarter. They could own the crossing."

Everyone looked at him. Ash looked at the floor, ears going pink, and added nothing.

"That's a strange thing to know," Odile said quietly.

Ash shrugged one shoulder.

She let it go. There would be time — there was always time, with Ash, like drawing water drop by drop from a deep well — and besides, the shape of it was already assembling in her head, ugly and inevitable. Sable hadn't stolen coin or jewels or the governor's famous clockwork falcon. Whatever he'd lifted in the capital three months ago, it had sent Vane's people sniffing after him within the week, and men like Captain Idris Vane did not spend money chasing jewelry.

Sable was going into the forbidden country with a map in his head. And whatever he was carrying toward it was worth more than all of them.

"Here's what happens now," Odile said, and the charcoal stick came down like a gavel. "We rest tonight. Real rest — food, sleep, water everyone to the lips. First light, we're on the Marid caravan's heels. We stay a half-day behind, close enough to strike, far enough that he doesn't see us coming. In and out of Qamar before the border wardens remember we exist."

Bex raised a hand. "Counterproposal. We rest tonight, tomorrow, and the day after. Resupply properly. Ferro's knee—"

"My knee is fine," Ferro said.

"—and *your* hands are shaking, boss, and nobody's said it out loud yet, so I'm saying it." Bex's voice wasn't challenging; it was worse than that. It was kind. Kindness, from Bex, was a weapon she only drew when she meant it. "Three weeks of hard pursuit. You haven't slept a full night since the capital. You eat standing up. Yesterday I watched you pour water into your boot because you forgot which flask was which. We stop. Two days. The trail will keep."

The room went quiet. Four people who had followed her across half a desert looked at her with varying degrees of hope, and Odile felt the silence open beneath her like a door onto a room she had boarded shut years ago. Because here was the truth she kept moving to avoid: two days of rest meant two nights. Two nights meant lying still in the dark with nothing between her and the memory of a green valley, a burning well-house, names she still said aloud sometimes to the wind when she thought no one heard. *Motion is armor,* her old commander used to say. *A moving target grieves on the run.*

"A day and a half," she said. "We leave at midday tomorrow, not first light — the caravan will be slow getting loaded, we'll make up the gap by nightfall. Everyone sleeps, eats, waters up. Ferro wraps that knee whether it needs it or not. Lune—" she softened a fraction, "—drink the chamomile Ash keeps hiding from you and actually sleep."

Lune blinked. "How do you know about the chamomile?"

"I know everything that happens on my crew. It's the only territory I hold." She rolled the charcoal stick across the table, stood, and pulled her hood back up. "One and a half days, Bex. Not because you're wrong. Because you're right, and I can afford a day and a half of being right."

Bex studied her for a long moment, then nodded, unsatisfied but conceding, which was their oldest dance.

Odile was at the door when Ash's voice caught her, soft as sand shifting.

"He left the fig for you."

She stopped. Didn't turn around. "I know."

"Why does he do that? Six years, and he leaves you little — things. Signs. Like he wants you to find him."

Outside, the wind had risen again, and it came through the chandler's doorway tasting of salt and far-off rain, and somewhere in that taste, faint as a rumor, was the smell of wet earth — impossible, this early, this far south. Monsoon weather, gathering beyond the mountains like an army.

"Because he thinks he owes me," Odile said. "And because he knows the only thing that keeps me moving is him."

She stepped out into the blue dusk of Qamar-at-Tin, and behind her, almost too quiet to hear, Ash murmured to no one:

"Then somebody should tell her the storm's already moving faster than he is."
