## 2. The Job That Was Sworn Off

The border town of Qal'at-Rim smelled of wet stone and old lies, which was fitting, because that was where Idris Vane found me.

I'd been in the tavern's back corner for three hours, nursing a cup of tea gone bitter as regret, watching the door the way I always watched doors. Sable had passed through here two days ago. I knew it the way I knew weather coming — a feeling in the teeth, a pattern in the dust. The stable boy remembered a tall man with a limp he didn't have yesterday and did today, paying in coin minted on the far side of the Quarter. The innkeeper remembered him buying water skins, six of them, and asking questions about the old caravan road east.

East. Toward the deep Quarter. Toward territory the maps marked with a single word: *abandoned*, though nothing out there was ever abandoned so much as it gave up on being survived.

So when the door swung open and the lamplight caught a familiar silhouette — broad shoulders, captain's coat too fine for the dust, that walk like a man perpetually boarding a ship that wasn't there — my hand went to my knife before my mind caught up with my body.

"Odile Amberdale." Vane's voice carried across the room the way it always had, warm and certain, a voice built for giving orders people wanted to follow. "Still drinking alone in corners. Some things hold."

"Some things rot," I said, and didn't stand.

He crossed the room anyway, unhurried, and sat across from me uninvited. Up close he'd aged well, which felt like an insult. Silver at his temples now, arranged like it had been placed by a barber rather than earned by years. The scar through his left eyebrow had faded to a pale thread. Mine hadn't faded at all. Scars keep what they're given.

"You look well," he said.

"Liar."

"I do try." He signaled the barkeep with two fingers, ordered whatever passed for decent wine in a place like this, and leaned back with the ease of a man who had never once been thrown out of anywhere. "You've been chasing him for six years now. Sable."

My spine went cold. "You know his name."

"I know a great many things. It's my profession." He turned his cup slowly on the table, aligning the handle with the wood grain, some old naval habit. "I know he was in Marid-al-Sayf last month. I know he bought passage on a grain barge under the name Corvus. And I know that in eleven days, maybe twelve, the monsoon comes early this year, and every road east of here turns to river."

The wine arrived. He drank. I didn't touch mine.

"If you're selling information," I said, "you've got the wrong buyer. I don't take favors from you."

"Not selling. Hiring." He set the cup down and produced a purse from inside his coat — heavy, the kind of heavy that meant real gold, not the shaved alloy that passed for currency in the border towns. He set it on the table between us and left his hand on it. "An expedition crosses the Empty Quarter before the rains seal it. I need a guide who knows the eastern routes. There is exactly one guide alive who knows them."

"No."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"There are no terms." My voice came out flat and hard, and I was grateful for it, because underneath it something was shaking. "You want to talk about the eastern routes? Fine. Let's talk about them. Let's talk about Wadi Serpent, where the flash flood took Mara and Doss and little Tam. Let's talk about how we buried what we could find of them in sand because the ground was too wet for anything deeper. Let's talk about how you stood on that ridge and watched the water come down and did *nothing*—"

"That's not what happened."

"—and then you walked away with your cargo intact and your crew dead, and you've spent seven years being a *captain* while I've spent seven years making sure I never stop moving long enough to hear their names in my own head."

The tavern had gone quiet around us. Vane didn't flinch. That was the thing about Idris Vane — he absorbed anger like sand absorbs rain, all surface, none of it sinking in.

"Mara chose the route," he said quietly. "Doss agreed. You were there for the argument, Odile. You voted with them."

"I voted wrong. That doesn't make you innocent."

"No," he admitted. "It makes us both guilty. Which is why I came to you and not some hired chart-man out of the coast cities." He pushed the purse an inch toward me. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to guide me. One crossing. The northern shelf road, high ground most of the way, timed ahead of the monsoon. And when it's done, you'll have enough gold to stop chasing thieves for coin and start doing whatever it is you'd do if money weren't the reason."

And there it was. The hook beneath the bait. Because he was right about the shape of my life, and he knew it, and knowing it made me hate him more cleanly than any accusation could.

"What's the cargo?" I asked.

"Survey equipment. Instruments. Papers."

"Papers." I almost laughed. "Vane, no one crosses the Quarter in monsoon season for papers."

"People cross for what the papers are worth." His eyes held mine, steady, patient, the eyes of a man used to waiting out storms both literal and otherwise. "You don't need to know more than that. You need to know the pay, the route, and the deadline. Everything else is my affair."

Everything else. Seven years ago everything else had been a sealed strongbox that four of our friends died defending, and I still didn't know what was in it, and some nights — the bad ones, the ones where the wind off the dunes sounded like water — I thought I might burn a city down to find out.

I looked at the purse. Gold enough to fund two years of pursuit. Two years of Sable's trail, of border towns and stable boys and innkeepers with good memories. Two more years of motion, which was the only prayer I had left.

And east. He was going east. Every piece of trail I'd gathered pointed the same direction — the deep Quarter, the forbidden territory, the drowned ruins at the old crossing where maps went blank. If Sable was running toward something out there, then Vane's expedition wasn't just a job. It was the fastest horse available.

The dread sat in my chest like swallowed stone. The route he wanted — even the safe version, the northern shelf — ran within sight of Wadi Serpent. I would feel it before I saw it. I would smell the wet rock in my sleep. I would wake on the third night hearing Mara laugh, the way I always did when I got close to that country, and there would be no one beside me to say her name back.

But staying meant losing him. Losing him meant the last six years became nothing but a very long way of not grieving.

"One condition," I said.

Vane smiled like a man watching a fish take the hook. "Name it."

"My crew. I choose them. Not your men, not your officers. Bex for the engines if she'll come, the Ferro twins for navigation, and deckhands of my picking. You can bring your soldiers or your surveyors or whoever's in those crates you won't describe, but the boat answers to me on the water."

Something flickered behind his eyes — fast, quickly smoothed over, but I caught it. Surprise, maybe. Or calculation revising itself.

"Agreed," he said. "We sail from the river dock at Qal'at-Rim in four days. Provisioned, fueled, crewed." He stood, took back the purse, and pressed a smaller pouch into my hand instead — earnest money, half again what a guide should command. "It's good to see you, Odile. Truly."

"It isn't," I said. "Don't mistake this for anything but a transaction."

"I never do." At the door he paused, silhouetted against the street's white heat, and said, without turning: "For what it's worth — I think about them too. Every day."

Then he was gone, and the tavern noise swelled back up around me like water closing over a dropped stone.

I sat a long while with the pouch in my hand, weighing it, weighing myself. Outside, the wind had shifted. I could taste it now, faint under the dust and camel dung and frying onions — that green metallic edge that meant rain falling somewhere far to the south, rain that would be here soon, turning the lowlands to rivers and the rivers to seas.

Sable was heading east. So was I.

God help us both, so was I.

* * *

I spent the evening recruiting, and it went exactly as badly as I deserved.

Bex threw a wrench at me. Not at my head — Bex never wasted a good wrench on a head — but at the wall beside it, hard enough to leave a dent in the adobe.

"The eastern crossing." She wiped her hands on a rag already black with grease, and her voice had that dangerous calm she got before explosions, mechanical or otherwise. "That crossing. You're asking me to sign onto *that* crossing."

"I'm asking you to keep a boat running through weather that wants to kill it. Same as ever. Just worse."

"Worse." She laughed, short and ugly. "Mara fixed my elbow joint the night before she died. Did you know that? Little Tam was holding the lamp. She said, *Bex, when we get back, teach me the pressure manifolds.*" Her jaw worked. "She was fourteen."

"I know."

"And you said yes to Vane anyway."

"I said yes because Sable's trail goes east, and this is the only way to follow it, and because—" I stopped. Because stopping means facing them. Because motion is the only armor I've got left. Because the alternative is sitting still in some border town until the grief catches up, and it will catch up, it always does, and I don't know who I am when it does.

"Because?" Bex prompted, merciless.

"Because someone has to make sure Vane doesn't come back from this one either," I said, and it was only half a lie, and Bex knew it was only half a lie, and that was somehow enough.

She stared at me for a long ten-count. Then she picked up her tool roll and slung it over her shoulder.

"Four days," she said. "If the hull's a disaster, I'm mutinying at the first sandbar."

* * *

The twins were easier. Ferro and Lune worked the river pilot trade out of a beached barge they called home, and when I laid the map on their table and put my finger on the northern shelf road, they went quiet in that unnerving synchronized way of theirs.

Ferro traced the route with one finger. Lune watched me with the other twin's eyes.

"This is the ghost road," Lune said.

"It's the fast road," I said. "Ahead of the monsoon, it's the only road."

"We heard about Wadi Serpent," Ferro said. "Everyone has. Half the Quarter swears the flood there rose forty feet. The other half says it took the ridge itself."

"The flood rose thirty-one feet. I measured it after." I don't know why I said that. I'd never told anyone I measured it. "I needed to know how much water it takes."

Neither twin asked why. That was the thing about them — they navigated by reading what people didn't say as much as what they did.

Lune rolled up the map and handed it back. "We're in. But we navigate by consensus on the water. No single orders."

"Done."

"And Odile." Ferro's voice, quiet. "If it starts to go like it went before — you tell us. You don't carry it alone. That's the term."

I took the map. My hand wasn't quite steady, and I let them see it, which cost more than the gold had.

"That's the term," I agreed.

* * *

On the third night, provisioned and committed, I walked the dock alone. The expedition boat sat low in the black water — a converted river gunboat, all iron ribs and patched plating, Vane's men loading crates under lantern light. Long crates. Heavy ones. The kind that rattle wrong when shifted, with a sound like metal sliding against metal inside.

I stood in the shadow of a mooring post and watched, and told myself it was survey equipment, and knew I was lying.

Behind me, footsteps on the boards. Light ones. Deliberate ones. I turned with my knife already loose in its sheath, and stopped.

A young deckhand stood at the rail's end — slight, hooded, face half-lost in shadow. One of the hires I'd approved that afternoon from a list Vane provided, a quiet one the harbormaster vouched for. Ash, the manifest said. Just Ash.

"You shouldn't be out here," I said.

"No," Ash agreed softly. "Neither should you." A pause. The lantern light caught the lower half of a face younger than I'd expected, older than the voice. "Captain. The man you're chasing — the one Vane's really after. He boarded a supply barge at the upstream dock this morning. Under a false name. He's still aboard."

The river slapped against the pilings. Somewhere out on the black water, a night bird cried once and went silent.

"How do you know that?"

Ash's hood tilted, and for a moment I saw the eyes clearly — steady, knowing, carrying something heavy and old for such a young face.

"Because I've been waiting seven years for someone to ask the right questions," Ash said. "And Captain — before you board that barge, you should know. Vane's crates aren't full of instruments. They're full of rifles. And I don't think the ledger is the only thing he means to take from the crossing."

The lanterns swung. The crates rattled their metal rattle. And the monsoon wind, faint and green and rising, came up the river toward us like a promise kept.
