## 3. Crew of Strangers

The river dock at Qal'at-Sar smelled of tar, dead fish, and other people's ambitions. Odile stood at the end of it with her arms crossed and her boots planted on planks that groaned under the weight of the monsoon swelling beneath them. The water had come up two feet in a week. It would come up another six before the season broke.

Vane's boat was a flat-bottomed barge called the *Mercy of Kings*, which Odile considered a bad omen dressed up as a joke. She watched the crew assemble the way a gambler watches cards being dealt — knowing most of them were worthless, waiting for the one that wasn't.

* * *

Bex arrived first, because Bex was always first to anything with an engine in it. She came down the dock road pushing a handcart loaded with tools wrapped in oilcloth, a small woman built like a fist, with burn scars laddering both forearms from wrist to elbow. She took one look at the barge's engine housing and made a sound like a cat stepping on a nail.

"Who maintained this?" she demanded of no one, of everyone, of the river itself.

"The captain's man," said the dockhand.

"His man should be shot." Bex hauled herself aboard, pried open the housing, and stared into the guts of the engine for a long moment the way other people stare into church windows. "Cast-iron block, brass fittings, and someone's been sealing leaks with wax. Wax." She spat over the side. "We'll be lucky if this thing doesn't eat us by the second bend."

Odile came aboard quietly enough that Bex didn't hear her. "You're the engineer."

"I'm the person keeping you alive, ranger. The engineer is a formality." Bex wiped her hands on a rag that was filthier than her hands. "You're Amberdale. Vane said you'd be along. He also said you were reasonable, which I already don't believe."

"Smart woman."

"I'm a smart woman stuck on a wax-sealed death trap going upriver into monsoon country." Bex turned back to the engine. "Go bother somebody else."

Odile almost smiled. It was the closest thing to comfort she'd felt in weeks, meeting someone whose anger was honest and aimed at machinery instead of ghosts.

* * *

The twins arrived together, because they always did, arguing about a map.

Ferro was the taller by half a head and carried himself like a man who expected doors to open for him; Lune walked half a step behind and slightly to the left, which was where she'd been since birth, according to her, correcting his mistakes. They were sandy-haired, river-born, and identical except for the way Ferro looked at the world like it owed him money and Lune looked at it like she intended to collect.

"—I'm telling you the channel shifted after the flood year," Ferro was saying, slapping a rolled chart against his palm. "Your memory is four years out of date."

"My memory," Lune said, "is the only reason you're not a coral reef."

They stopped when they saw Odile. Ferro's face did something complicated — recognition, then recalculation, then a grin he thought was charming.

"Odile Amberdale. My sister used to have your wanted poster on our wall."

"Lune." Odile's voice went flat as slate.

"I was twelve," Lune said, unbothered. "It was a very good poster. You looked heroic."

"She tore it down when I pointed out you'd been wanted for three years without getting caught," Ferro said. "She said either the rangers were idiots or you were."

"And?" Odile asked.

Lune met her eyes. There was nothing flippant underneath, Odile noted. The flippancy was paint over something load-bearing. "Both," Lune said. "Obviously."

Ferro laughed and offered his hand. Odile didn't take it. He shrugged and turned it into a gesture toward the chart instead. "Vane says you know the route better than any living soul. We've got the old survey maps, but they end at the crossing."

"They end there," Odile said, "because everyone who mapped past them died."

"Cheerful," said Ferro.

"Honest," said Lune, and rolled up the chart, and Odile decided — provisionally, the way you decide about weather — that she could work with these two.

* * *

Ash was already aboard when Odile finally made a full circuit of the barge. That was the first strange thing: nobody had seen him arrive. The second strange thing was that he'd organized the cargo hold so precisely that the manifest matched reality for the first time in the ship's history, and he'd done it without asking anyone's permission or speaking more than four words.

He was maybe twenty, maybe thirty, hard to say, with a face like a closed door and hands that moved constantly — coiling rope, squaring crates, folding the same tarp into smaller and smaller shapes. When Odile came down the hold ladder he went still, the way small animals go still.

"You're Ash," she said.

He nodded.

"You signed on through Vane?"

Another nod.

"Have you run the Quarter before?"

The pause before he shook his head was a quarter-second too long. Odile had spent eleven years reading liars across open sand, where there was nowhere to hide a tell, and she knew what a quarter-second meant. It meant: *yes, and I can't say so.*

"Where from, then?" she asked, casual, leaning against a crate. "That route. You stood on the deck this morning looking north like you'd left something there."

Ash's hands resumed their work. Folded the tarp. Squared the crate. "Just checking the wind," he said, and his voice was quiet and even and completely useless, and he climbed out of the hold and left her alone with the cargo and her own sharpening suspicion.

* * *

By evening the whole crew had gathered on deck under a sky the color of a bruise, eating rice off tin plates while the river slapped the hull and the first fat drops of the season fell hissing onto the lantern glass. Vane held court at the stern table with his charts and his gold and his voice pitched exactly between command and charm.

Odile sat apart, as she always sat apart, and listened.

"—three days upriver to the narrows," Vane was saying, tapping the map. "Then we cut east across the flooded flats to the old crossing. After that, well." He smiled. "After that we're all rich, and I never have to see any of you again."

"Touching," said Bex.

"He means he'll miss us," Ferro said.

"He means he'll pay us," Lune said. "Which is the only kind of missing I trust."

Odile watched Ash during all of it. He sat at the edge of the lantern light, not quite outside it, not quite in it, and when Vane tapped the map at the narrows — at the specific bend, at the specific stretch of water — Ash's hands stopped moving. Just for a moment. Just long enough.

She'd seen men freeze like that before. At funerals. At tribunals. Once, at the site of a wreck, watching salvage crews pull things out of the water that used to be people.

*Later*, she told herself. *Get upriver first. Learn what he knows and how.*

But the rain was coming harder now, drumming on the deck, and the river was rising, and somewhere south of here Sable was moving toward the same crossing with a stolen ledger full of every well in the Quarter, and Odile found that the familiar itch under her skin — *move, chase, catch* — had gone strangely quiet. In its place was something worse. These strangers with their tin plates and their arguments were going to be her responsibility for three hundred miles of drowned country, and some treacherous part of her had already started counting them as hers.

Bex caught her eye across the deck and raised her tin cup an inch. *To the wax-sealed death trap,* the gesture said.

Ferro was showing Lune a card trick she clearly already knew. Ash refolded his tarp.

And Captain Idris Vane looked up from his charts, straight at Odile, and smiled the smile of a man who believed he was the smartest thing on the water.

"First light," he said. "We sail with the flood tide."

In the dark downstream, half-swallowed by rain, a single lantern bobbed on the current — a supply barge running without lights, moving fast, moving north.

Moving toward the crossing.

Toward Sable.
