## 4. What Sable Stole

The barge smelled of tar, wet rope, and the particular sourness of men who had been paid in advance and were already regretting the terms. Sable crouched in the dark between two crates of salted fish, listening to the rain begin — the first fat, irregular drops of a monsoon that had no business arriving this early — and counted his exits.

There were three. The gangplank, which was watched. The cargo hatch, which was locked. And the gap in the rail where a section of planking had rotted through, which was watched by nobody because nobody else had bothered to look. That was the difference between him and the men Vane had hired, and it was the only difference that mattered.

He had been on the barge for six hours. He would be on it for six more, riding it downriver to the dock at Serpent's Bend where Vane's expedition was assembling, and then he would walk into the lion's den with the ledger sewn into the lining of his coat, and he would decide — again, for the hundredth time since the vault at Qasr al-Ma — whether he was delivering it or running with it.

The rain thickened. Somewhere on deck, a man cursed and moved under the awning. Sable let his eyes close. He was good at stillness. He had learned it in the archive-houses of the river cities, lying flat under floorboards for entire days while clerks above him argued about irrigation taxes. Stillness was just patience with the fear squeezed out of it, and he had squeezed a great deal of fear in his life.

Which was why he heard her before he saw her.

Not footsteps — Odile Amberdale did not make footsteps, he had learned that much in seven years of being hunted by her. What he heard was the absence of them: a stretch of the barge's deck where the rain should have been drumming on wood and wasn't, because someone was crouched under the rail's overhang, and that someone had been there long enough to be soaked through and had not once shifted her weight.

"You're getting slow," Sable said quietly, to the darkness. "Used to take you three days to find me. This time it was two."

The darkness answered by producing a knife at his throat and a voice like a dry riverbed. "Used to take me three days because I was being polite about it. Move and I'll show you the difference."

"Odile." He said her name the way you'd set down something heavy. "You're dripping on the fish."

The knife didn't waver. He felt her adjust — the small redistribution of weight that meant she was settling in for a long conversation, which was either very good for him or very bad. With Odile it had always been impossible to tell until it was over.

"Seven years," she said. "Seven years I've chased you across the Quarter. I've eaten sand in your wake in four territories. I watched you walk out of a walled city through a sewer because you knew I'd never follow. And now you're sitting on a supply barge bound for the one place in the world I'm going, carrying — what? What is it, Sable? What did you take that's worth this?"

He could have lied. Lying to Odile had never worked, but he could have tried, and the trying would have bought him minutes. Instead he found himself saying, "You'll want to sit down."

"I'll want you to answer."

"It's a long answer."

"I've got a knife and a monsoon. Go."

* * *

He told it to her in the dark, under the rail, while the rain came down harder and the river rose against the hull with a sound like something swallowing.

"It starts with Vane," he said. "Eight months ago. He came to me in Marid's Rest — you remember Marid's Rest, you nearly caught me at the well auction there — and he didn't come to arrest me or hire me to steal a jewel. He came to me with a map."

"A map."

"A map of the old crossing. The deep route. The one your people—" He caught himself. "The one that killed the others. The one that's been sworn off since."

The knife at his throat went very still. He could feel her breathing change, the controlled rhythm going shallow. He pressed on before she could decide what to do with that.

"Vane told me there was a vault at the heart of the crossing. Pre-Compact construction, older than any of the oasis settlements, older than the water-rights courts. And inside it, he said, was the original ledger. Not a copy. The book itself — the first registry, written when the wells were dug, in wet ink, with every claim and every covenant signed by the people who made them."

"And you believed a story like that because—"

"Because I checked. I'm a thief, Odile, not a fool. I spent four months verifying it. I found the survey fragments in the archive at Qasr al-Ma. I found the merchant letters from a hundred years ago that mention a 'registry of waters' lost in the deep Quarter. It's real. It's real, and here's what it is."

He turned his head as much as the knife allowed, enough that he could see the pale shape of her face, rain running off the edge of her hood.

"Every well in the Empty Quarter is in that book. Every settlement, every claim, every covenant — and the covenants have renewal clauses. Old ones. Written for a world where a well was a family's inheritance, not a company's asset. If the ledger is recovered and entered into the water courts, the original terms revive. The wells go back to the people who live beside them. Free water, in perpetuity, for every oasis from here to the Glass Coast."

The knife lowered an inch. "And Vane wants that why?"

"Vane wants it," Sable said, "because the same clauses that free the wells can be reversed. The ledger doesn't just record who owns the water. It records who can be *disinherited*. Strike a covenant, void a claim, and a settlement that's drawn from its well for two hundred years becomes, legally, a squatter camp. Vane doesn't want to free the Quarter. He wants to own the only document that says who owns the Quarter. He hired me to steal it because I'm the best there is at getting into places like that vault, and because he needed someone deniable."

"And then?"

"And then, three weeks ago, I came out of the deep Quarter with the ledger sewn into my coat, and Vane's men were waiting at the rendezvous. Not to pay me. To kill me and take it." He laughed, and it came out wrong, too thin. "They were sloppy. I'm not. I've been running since. And I booked passage on this barge because it's the only boat going where I need to go — which is, I now realize, exactly where you're going, which means Vane is going, which means—"

"Which means you're walking into his expedition with the thing he's killed to get," Odile finished. Her voice had gone flat and distant, the way it did when her mind was moving faster than her mouth.

"Yes."

"You could run."

"Where?" He spread his hands. "Vane has men on every river dock from here to the coast. There's one crossing left before the monsoon seals the deep Quarter for a season, and it's his crossing. He built it that way. He built the whole trap so that the ledger would come to him, because he knew I couldn't sell it anywhere else — the water courts all have his people in them. The only place the ledger is worth anything is the place where he can take it from me." He shook his head. "I'm not running, Odile. I'm delivering myself. I just haven't decided to whom."

* * *

The rain became a wall. On deck, the hired men had given up on the awning and gone below, and the barge rode the rising river alone, lamps swinging, the whole world reduced to water falling on water.

They had moved to the lee side of the cargo hatch, out of the worst of it, and Odile had done something he had never seen her do in seven years of pursuit: she had put the knife away and sat down across from him, knees drawn up, hood back, letting the rain hit her face.

"Say it again," she said. "The part about the covenants. The part about what it can do."

"Free them or enslave them. That's the whole of it. It's not a treasure, Odile. It's a *sentence*. Whoever holds the ledger gets to write what happens to every person who drinks from a well in the Quarter, for as long as paper lasts." He looked at her. "I stole it because I thought I'd sell it to the settlements. Pool the money, buy the freedom of a hundred towns. Then Vane showed me what he was, and now I don't know what to do with it, because every option ends with someone owning it, and I've read enough history to know how that ends."

"You could burn it."

He stared at her. "Burn it? Do you know what that book is worth? Do you know what it could *do*?"

"I know what it's doing right now." Her voice was quiet under the rain. "It's on a barge in a storm, and there are armed men waiting at the end of the river, and a captain I used to trust is going to cross a route that killed my friends to get it. Whatever's written in that book, it's already cost more than it's worth."

"You don't mean that. You've spent seven years chasing me over things worth less."

"I spent seven years chasing you," Odile said, "because chasing you meant I didn't have to stop." And then she looked away, out at the black water, and Sable understood that he had just been handed something no thief should be trusted with: the truth, unguarded, from the one person who had never given him anything but pursuit.

He should have used it. Some cold practical part of him noted the leverage, filed it away. But mostly he just sat there in the rain with her, two people who had made a career of never stopping, and felt the strangest thing — the thing he had felt once, briefly, in the vault at Qasr al-Ma with the ledger open in his hands.

The wish to put it down.

"Odile," he said. "The crew Vane's assembling. Do you know who's on it?"

"My people. Bex. The twins. Ash." A pause. "Why?"

"Because when I came off the deep Quarter three weeks ago," Sable said, "I wasn't alone in those ruins. Vane had a second team — men who went in ahead of me, to hold the vault. And one of them, when I doubled back past their camp, was wearing a coat I recognized." He watched her face. "It was the coat of a man who died at the crossing. The first crossing. The one that was sworn off."

The rain filled the silence between them.

"Vane told everyone those men were lost," Sable said. "He told *you* they were lost. But somebody was down there in the dark with me, Odile. Somebody survived, and has been on Vane's payroll ever since, and is going to be standing on that dock when your boat arrives."

Odile stood. In the swinging lamplight her face was unreadable, but her hands were not — they were shaking, very slightly, the way water shakes just before it goes over the edge.

"Which one," she said.

"That's the thing," Sable said, and heard his own voice go quiet. "I never saw his face. But I've been asking around the river towns, and there's a deckhand signed onto Vane's expedition who nobody seems to know. Quiet one. Signed on under a name that doesn't check out." He met her eyes. "They're calling him Ash."
