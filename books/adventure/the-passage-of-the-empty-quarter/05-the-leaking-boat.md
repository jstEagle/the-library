## 5. The Leaking Boat

Bex heard the river change before anyone else did.

She was below decks, elbow-deep in the bilge pump's guts, when the sound of the water against the hull shifted — a low, grinding note, like the boat had begun to argue with something beneath it. She'd spent eleven years listening to engines and rivers, and she knew every voice they had. This one she didn't like.

"Storm," she said to no one, because no one was there, because that was the way she preferred most conversations.

By the time she climbed into the gray light, the sky over the Empty Quarter had gone the color of wet slate. The monsoon wasn't due for another nine days. Ferro had said so at breakfast, tapping the almanac with the smugness of a man who trusted paper. Lune had agreed. Captain Vane had smiled his thin, expensive smile and said, *Then we have time.*

The wind arrived first, flat and hot, pushing ahead of the wall of cloud stacked on the southern horizon like a mountain range that had decided to travel. Then the rain came — not falling so much as arriving sideways, in sheets, hammering the deck with a sound like gravel thrown at glass.

"Get everything under canvas!" Odile's voice cut through the roar. "Ferro, Lune — lash the cargo! Ash, get below and check the seams!"

Bex was already moving. That was the thing about fear, she'd learned: you could let it sit on your chest like a stone, or you could give it hands. She gave hers work.

* * *

The crack announced itself an hour later, and Bex felt it through her boots before she heard it — a shudder running up from the keel, deep and wrong, the boat's whole skeleton flinching.

She went down the ladder three rungs at a time. The bilge was ankle-deep already, black water swirling with sawdust and something that smelled like rot. Her lantern found it near the stern: a seam had opened along a strake, maybe two feet of it, the planks sprung apart just enough to let the river pour through in a steady, muscular rush.

*We're taking water faster than the pump can push it out,* she thought, and her stomach dropped through the deck and kept going.

"Ash!" she bellowed up the hatch. "Ash, get down here!"

He came fast, dropping into the flooded bilge without hesitating, sleeves already rolled. He looked at the seam the way a doctor looks at a wound — long, silent, assessing.

"How bad?" Bex asked.

"It's not new." He ran his thumb along the split wood. "This crack's been patched before. Badly. Tar and prayer." He glanced up at her, and in the lantern light his face was unreadable as ever, but his voice had an edge she hadn't heard from him before. "Vane's surveyor walked this hull two days before we signed on. He would've seen this."

"You're saying he knew."

"I'm saying I don't believe in luck this bad."

Bex looked at the water rising around their shins and made the kind of decision engineers make: quickly, without all the information, and then you build your way toward being right. "Then we patch it better than he hoped we could. Get me the oakum. And the pitch kettle."

They worked through the worst of the storm's opening fury, wedged in the dark, driving oakum into the seam with caulking irons while the boat groaned around them like something being tortured. Water sloshed at their waists, then their chests, then — slowly, grudgingly, as the patch took — began to fall. Bex's arms burned. Her fingers were pruned white and bleeding at the knuckles. Ash worked beside her without a word for four hours, and somehow that silence was easier than any conversation she'd ever had.

When they finally climbed back on deck, gray dawn was leaking through the rain. The storm hadn't broken; it had settled in, the way a siege settles in. But the boat was holding.

Odile met them at the hatch. She looked at their soaked, wrecked bodies, at the water still glistening on the ladder, and said only: "How long will it hold?"

"Ask me after the next squall," Bex said. "And the one after that. It'll hold until it doesn't. That's the honest answer."

Odile nodded once, the way she accepted all honest answers — like she was filing them somewhere behind her eyes. "Rest. Both of you. That's an order."

Bex meant to rest. She got as far as the galley, where the smell hit her.

* * *

The spoilage was total.

Saltwater had gotten into the hold during the worst of the flooding — not through the cracked seam but through the lazarette hatch, which someone had failed to dog down properly, or which had been failed deliberately, depending on how paranoid Bex was feeling, and she was feeling quite paranoid indeed. Either way, half their grain was swollen and sour, the dried meat had taken on water and begun to turn, and the fresh stores were floating.

Lune stood in the middle of it, doing arithmetic in her head, lips moving. "Three weeks of food," she said quietly. "Maybe two if we're disciplined. Less if anyone gets injured."

"Ferro's already injured," Bex said. "He wrenched his shoulder lashing the cargo. He eats double or he heals slow."

"Then we have ten days."

Ten days. The crossing ruins were six days ahead, if the river behaved, and the river had stopped behaving some time ago. Bex leaned against the bulkhead and did her own arithmetic, the kind that didn't involve food. A cracked hull. Spoiled supplies. A storm that came nine days early, as if it had been invited.

Her paranoia finished its sum.

She found the others by instinct — the way crew find each other in weather, drifting toward the driest corner of the deck where Odile stood at the rail watching the brown floodwater race past. Sable was there too, hood up, looking less like a thief than a man trying to make himself small enough to be forgotten. Bex had stopped thinking of him as prey somewhere around day two. Now he was just another pair of hands, and right now they needed every pair.

"We need to talk about the cargo," Bex said.

Odile turned. "What about it?"

"The crates in the aft hold. The ones Vane told us were survey equipment." Bex kept her voice level. "I opened one. For weight distribution. That's a real reason, before anyone asks."

"And?"

"And survey equipment doesn't need gun racks built into the crate lining. Twelve crates. I count room for thirty rifles. Maybe more. There are ammunition tins sealed in wax, and oilcloth-wrapped bundles I didn't open because I wanted plausible deniability." She paused. "There are also pay-chests. Coin. A lot of coin. You don't pay mercenaries out of a survey budget."

The rain drummed on the canvas above them. Nobody spoke for a moment.

"He's not crossing the Quarter," Lune said softly. "He's invading it."

"The wells," Sable said. They all looked at him. He pushed his hood back, and for once he didn't look clever or slippery. He looked tired, and older than his years, and grimly certain. "The ledger names every well in the Empty Quarter. Every settlement that drinks from them. Whoever holds the ledger holds the deeds — and whoever holds the deeds can decide who drinks." His mouth twisted. "I stole it because I thought it should belong to no one. Vane hired me to steal it because he intended it to belong to him. When I wouldn't hand it over, he decided to stop needing me." He shrugged, a small, bitter motion. "You've all been sailing with his backup plan since the dock."

Bex watched Odile's face do something complicated — anger and confirmation and something underneath both, old and personal. She'd heard the stories about the last crossing, the one that killed people these four had loved. She'd signed on anyway, for the gold, and told herself the past was the past.

The past, it appeared, had been waiting in the hold with the rifles.

"So what do we do?" Ash asked. Quietly, as always. But he asked it looking at Odile, and everyone else looked at Odile too, and Bex realized — with a feeling like a keel settling into deep water — that somewhere in the last few days the chain of command had quietly changed. Vane owned the boat. Odile owned the crew.

"We don't do anything yet," Odile said. "Not openly. Thirty armed men on a leaking boat in a monsoon — we don't win a fight, we just die first." Her eyes moved across each of them. "But we watch. We prepare. And when the moment comes, we take this boat, or we take to the water, or we burn it to the waterline — but nobody takes those wells. Not while any of us are breathing."

It wasn't a speech. It was barely a plan. But Ash nodded, and Lune nodded, and Ferro appeared from the rigging dripping wet with his arm strapped tight and nodded too, and Bex felt the strange, stubborn warmth of it settle into her chest alongside the fear, the two of them fitting together like oakum into a seam.

* * *

That evening, Vane came below to inspect the repair.

He crouched at the patched strake, running gloved fingers along Bex's caulking, and made a low sound of appreciation. "Fine work. Better than fine." He looked up at her with that smile that never reached anywhere important. "I chose well, hiring you."

"You chose cheap," Bex said. "I'm the cheapest good engineer on the river."

"Modesty. Charming." He stood, and for a moment — just a moment — the smile thinned into something else, something with a blade's geometry. "The storm will test us all, Miss Bex. Storms always sort people into what they truly are. I do hope you prove durable."

He climbed back up the ladder. Bex listened to his footsteps cross the deck overhead, counted them to the cabin door, waited until they stopped.

Then she went to the crate she'd opened, took out one of the wax-sealed ammunition tins, and quietly dropped it through the closest scupper into the brown, roaring flood.

One tin. Thirty rifles' worth of patience, spent a handful of rounds at a time. It wasn't mutiny. It was engineering — removing load from a structure before it failed.

Above her, thunder rolled across the drowned Quarter, and somewhere ahead, six days through the storm, the crossing ruins waited with their vault and their ledger and whatever Vane truly intended to do when the water set him down among them.

Bex picked up her tools and went back to the bilge. There was a boat to keep alive, and a family — because that's what they were now, whether anyone had said it aloud or not — that needed it kept alive.

The storm could sort them however it liked. She already knew what side of the seam she was caulked into.
