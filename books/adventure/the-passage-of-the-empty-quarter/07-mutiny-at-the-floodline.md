## 7. Mutiny at the Floodline

The sandbar appeared out of the rain like a drowned animal's back.

Odile saw it first from the bow — a pale smear in the brown floodwater where the river had swallowed the old caravan track and was now eating the high ground too. Vane stood at the rail beside her, rain streaming off his jaw, and when he spoke she heard the smile under his voice before she saw it on his face.

"There," he said. "You and your thief can wait out the worst of it there. Dry enough. The water will drop in a day or two."

She turned slowly. "The water is rising."

"The water always rises." He gestured, and two of his armed men came up from below decks with their rifles slung wrong — casual, like men carrying tools rather than weapons, which was its own kind of insult. "It's nothing personal, Amberdale. You've been useful. But you've also been a complication since Qasr Ford, and I don't pay for complications."

"You paid for a guide."

"I paid for obedience. You were supposed to deliver me Sable and the ledger. Instead you've been having long conversations with him below decks, whispering with Bex over the engine, holding little councils with the twins." His eyes went past her to where Sable stood at the mast, wrists still bound from the night before, rain running down his face like he didn't notice it. "I don't need a guide anymore. The ruins are three miles east. My men know the way."

"You'll drown before you find them," Odile said. "The crossing shifts every season. That's why you needed me. That's why you needed Ash."

Something flickered across Vane's face at the name — quick, ugly, gone. "Ash will come around. He always does."

The sandbar was close now, close enough that she could see the current peeling off it in white ropes, could see how low it sat, how the flood had already claimed its southern edge. It would be underwater by dark. Vane knew it. She knew he knew it. And that knowledge settled into her chest not as fear but as something colder and more precise than she had felt in years.

This was not abandonment. This was murder with a polite face.

* * *

They put her ashore first.

She went because fighting on the deck meant rifles and hostages, and the deck was Vane's ground. The sandbar was hers — or it would be for the few hours it remained above the waterline. She waded the last ten yards through thigh-deep current that tried to fold her legs out from under her, climbed the wet sand, and turned to watch them bring Sable.

They brought him rougher than they'd brought her. One of Vane's men shoved him between the shoulder blades and Sable stumbled into the shallows, went down, came up coughing brown water, and laughed about it. Even now. Even bound. Odile had spent seven years hating that laugh, and standing on the sinking sand watching him spit river out of his mouth, she felt the hatred crack down the middle like a dropped plate.

"Comfortable?" Sable called up to the boat.

"Very," Vane said. "There's a canteen in your pack. Ration it."

The *Kestrel's Wake* backed away, engines churning the flood to foam, and swung east toward the drowned ruins. Rain closed behind her like a curtain. Within a minute the boat was a gray shape, then a suggestion, then nothing but sound — and even the sound faded under the monsoon's steady roar.

Sable worked his bound wrists against a piece of driftwood without being told. Odile crouched beside him and held the wood steady so he could saw faster. Neither of them spoke. There wasn't time to speak. She measured the waterline against a root of dead tamarisk and watched it climb a finger's width while she counted to sixty.

"We have until midafternoon," she said.

"I've escaped from worse."

"No, you haven't."

He grinned, breathless, hair plastered to his skull. "No. I haven't. But I say it anyway. It keeps the hands steady." The rope parted. He flexed his fingers, winced, and looked at her — really looked, the way he almost never did, all the deflection stripped off him by the rain. "You could let me drown, you know. Save yourself the chase. Seven years, Odile. You could be free of me in four hours."

She stared at him. The rain hammered the sand flat around them. And she understood, suddenly and completely, what she had been doing all this time — that the pursuit had been a roof over an empty house, that every mile she'd put between herself and Sable had really been a mile between herself and the graves at home, the names she couldn't say, the sister who'd stopped writing because Odile never stayed anywhere long enough to receive a letter. Chasing him had been the only direction she could bear to move.

And Vane had just tried to end it on a sandbar, like closing a ledger.

"I'm not letting you drown," she said. "Not because of the ledger. Not because of the wells. Because Vane wants you dead and I have decided I no longer take his recommendations." She stood, scanning the eastern flood. "Can you swim?"

"In this current? Badly."

"Good. I swim worse. We'll balance each other." She pointed to a shape barely visible through the rain — a line of broken pilings, the ribs of some old ferry landing, marching toward higher ground half a mile off. "We go piling to piling. Rest on each one. If we try to swim it straight we'll be in the delta by nightfall."

Sable got up beside her, dripping, unbound, and for a moment neither of them moved toward the water. "Why help me?" he asked quietly. "Honestly. No jokes."

"Because you stole the one thing in this desert that matters," she said, "and you did it to give it away. I've spent seven years thinking you were the worst man I ever chased. Turns out you might be the most honest one I ever met. I hate it. Let's go."

* * *

They made the third piling before the crew mutinied.

Odile heard it before she saw it — engines changing pitch, shouting torn thin by wind — and then the *Kestrel's Wake* came back out of the rain wall, listing hard to port, moving fast and badly, the way a boat moves when the person at the helm has never helmed anything. Bex's voice carried across the water, raw and furious: *"Hard left! HARD LEFT—"*

A rifle shot cracked from the stern. Then another. Then Ferro's voice, high and fierce, and Lune answering it, and the deep boom of the engine misfiring because someone who loved that engine was making it scream on purpose.

"They're coming back," Sable said.

"They're coming back *armed*," Odile said. "Get behind the piling."

The boat swept past the sandbar two hundred yards out, close enough to see figures grappling on the deck — Vane's men trying to hold the rail while Bex swung a wrench with both hands, while Ash, quiet Ash, who hadn't raised his voice in the entire voyage, stood at the wheel with his sleeves soaked and his face set like carved stone, putting the boat exactly where he wanted it. Toward them. Toward the sandbar. Toward the two people Vane had thrown away.

A line arced out from the bow — a heaving rope, badly thrown, splashing short. Lune threw again. Short again. The current was dragging the boat sideways and everyone aboard knew it; Odile could see the moment they realized the sandbar was unreachable, the moment the rescue became a wreck.

"*Swim to us!*" Lune screamed, cupping her hands. "*Now! Both of you — NOW—*"

The boat struck something submerged. The whole hull shuddered, a groan rolling through it like a dying animal, and it slewed hard, took water over the rail, and grounded with a grinding crunch on a bar of silt a hundred yards downstream. Not sunk. Not floating either. Stuck fast in rising floodwater, three miles from the ruins, with Vane's armed men aboard and the storm thickening overhead like a fist closing.

Odile swam for it anyway. So did Sable. The current fought them the whole way, and it was Ash who leaned off the listing deck and caught her wrist, and Ferro who hauled Sable up by the collar, and for a moment the six of them clung together on a tilting deck in the middle of a drowning world, breathing hard, laughing in the ragged way people laugh when they've decided not to die today.

Vane and four of his men were backed against the stern cabin, rifles up, rain streaming off the barrels. Outnumbered now — the arithmetic had changed — but not beaten, and everyone knew that too.

"Congratulations," Vane said. His voice was still smooth. Only his eyes moved, calculating, recalculating. "You've seized a grounded boat in a flood. Very bold. Now none of us are going anywhere."

"None of us were going anywhere anyway," Bex snarled. "That was your gift to us, Captain."

"The vault is three miles east," Vane said. "On foot, through floodwater, in this. My men and I will reach it. You'll be bailing. Think about who wins that race."

Odile looked at him — really looked, the way she'd looked at Sable on the sandbar — and felt the last knot inside her come loose. All those years of motion, of chasing, of mistaking distance for purpose, and here was the truth of it standing in front of her with a gun: some men run toward things, and some men just run, and Vane had been running his whole life from the day he'd abandoned their friends in the flood and called it command.

"We're not racing you," she said.

His brow furrowed. "Then you're surrendering the ledger?"

"We're going together," she said, "because if we split up, you'll kill whoever stays behind. Again." She watched that word land. *Again.* Watched Ash's knuckles whiten on the wheel spokes, watched Vane's composure finally, finally flicker. "So here's how it goes, Idris. Rifles down. Everyone walks east. And when we reach that vault, whatever happens there happens between all of us — no ambushes, no marooning, no more of your arithmetic."

"And if I refuse?"

Odile glanced at the rising water lapping the deck, then back at him. "Then you can defend this boat alone. From the river."

A long moment. Rain. The groan of the strained hull. Somewhere beneath them, the silt shifted with a sound like slow swallowing.

Then Captain Idris Vane lowered his rifle — an inch, no more, but enough — and Odile turned east, toward the drowned ruins, with Sable falling into step beside her and her crew at her back, and understood that for the first time in seven years she was not chasing anyone at all.

Ahead, through the rain, the ruins of the crossing rose out of the flood like the bones of something enormous — and among them, faint but unmistakable, a lantern was already moving. Someone had reached the vault first.
