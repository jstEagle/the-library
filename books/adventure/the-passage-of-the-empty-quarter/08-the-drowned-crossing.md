## 8. The Drowned Crossing

The ruins announced themselves before we saw them — a smell of wet stone and old salt rising off the floodwater like breath from a sleeping animal.

Lune stood at the bow of the grounded skiff, rain sheeting off her oilskin, and tried to remember what Ferro had said about the crossing the night before, when he'd been whole and joking and certain. *The old river gate is the way in,* he'd said. *Everything else is drowned or buried.* He'd said it the way he said everything, like a man laying cards face-up on a table, daring the world to argue.

The world had argued. The world was winning.

From here the city — if it had ever been a city — looked like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down in the water to die. Broken arches broke the surface in rows. A tower leaned at an angle that made Lune's stomach clench just looking at it, its crown submerged, its base swallowed whole. Between the structures, the flood moved with a slow brown patience, carrying palm fronds and the carcass of a goat and, once, a door — a whole carved door, turning gently, as if someone had opened it onto the water and walked through.

"Gate's that way." Ash pointed left of the leaning tower, toward a gap where two walls met at an angle too clean to be accident. "If it's still standing."

"And if it isn't?" Bex asked.

"Then we swim."

Nobody laughed. The rain filled the silence for them.

Odile came down the skiff's spine, moving light despite the water sloshing over her boots, and stopped beside Lune. Up close she looked carved out of something harder than flesh — but her eyes went to Ferro first, the way they always did now, checking.

Ferro sat propped against the mast with his leg stretched out, the bandage around his thigh already blooming rust-brown at the center. The sandbar fight hadn't been kind to him. Vane's men had been kind to no one. He caught Lune looking and grinned at her, that grin, the twin-grin, the one that said *we're fine, we've always been fine.*

"Stay close to me down there," Lune said.

"You stay close to *me*," he said. "I'm the one who knows which walls fall down."

It wasn't true. They both knew the ruins equally well — they'd memorized the same charts, argued over the same routes for six years, finished each other's sentences about currents and load lines. But he wanted her to say yes, so she said yes, and hated how easily the lie sat between them, warm as a blanket over a wound.

Odile counted heads with a glance. "Vane's men will have launched by now. We've got maybe an hour on them, less if they cut straight through the flooded quarter. We move fast, we move quiet, and nobody plays hero." Her gaze rested on Ferro a half-second longer than on anyone else. "Nobody."

"I never play," Ferro said. "I only win."

Lune looked away so he wouldn't see her face.

* * *

The water inside the gate came up to their waists, then their chests, then — in the long colonnade where the old market had stood — their chins. Lune swam with one hand on the strap of Ferro's pack, feeling him kick beside her, his breathing rough but steady. The twins had grown up on the river; water was the first language they'd ever spoken. But this water spoke a different dialect. It was heavy with silt and secrets, and every stroke stirred up ghosts — a shattered amphora, a child's sandal, a wall carving of two hands cupped around a drop of water, worn smooth by centuries of flood.

*They knew,* Lune thought, treading water beneath the carving. *They built the vault because they knew what water was worth.*

Ahead, Odile's head broke the surface, then Ash's, then Bex's, the four of them strung through the drowned street like beads on a wire. The sound of the storm was muffled down here, turned into a vast low drumming on the surface above, and beneath it Lune could hear the other thing — the groan of stone settling, the tick and shift of a city deciding which parts of itself to keep.

"The vault's below the old cistern," Ash called softly when they reached shallower ground, a terrace of cracked flagstones waist-deep in water. "There's a stair behind the third arch. Vane's maps end at the cistern. Mine don't."

Lune caught Ferro's eye. *His maps don't.* Ash, who had survived the disaster years ago, who had carried the route in his silence like a stone in his shoe, who had finally spoken it all on the deck three nights ago with his voice shaking. He knew things about this place that no chart recorded. He knew them because he'd bled here before.

They reached the third arch. Behind it, exactly as promised: a stair descending into black water, steps slick with silt, the air below exhaling cold.

And across the flooded plaza, faint through the rain — a lantern. Then another. Then four.

"They're early," Bex breathed.

"No," Odile said, and there was something terrible and calm in her voice. "We're late. Move."

* * *

The stair took them down into the cistern, a cathedral of water and echo where their lantern-light bounced off pillars and died. The vault door was exactly where Ash said it would be: a slab of green bronze set into the far wall, half-submerged, worked with the same image of cupped hands. It had no visible lock. It had a mechanism — Ash knelt in the chest-deep water and began feeling along its seams, lips moving, counting under his breath.

Lune posted herself at the stair with her knife out, watching the darkness above. Ferro planted himself beside her, favoring his leg, and she didn't tell him to go back. There was no back to go to anymore. There was only forward, and each other, and the water rising by inches.

"We could hold them here," Ferro murmured. "The stair's narrow. Two of us could hold ten."

"Ferro—"

"Twins hold the line. That's the oldest rule there is." He smiled at her, and in the lantern-light she saw how pale he'd gone, how the joke cost him. "You go first through the door. I'll follow. That's the deal. Say it."

She couldn't say it. Saying it would make it a promise, and promises in this family had a way of being paid in blood.

Above them, gravel shifted.

They came down the stair fast and quiet — six of Vane's men, blades already drawn, moving with the confidence of people who believed the outcome was settled. Lune had time to register the lead man's face, young and scared under the hard set of his jaw, and then Ferro stepped past her into the water at the stair's foot and raised his knife, and there was no more time for anything.

What happened next happened in pieces.

Ferro meeting the first man before the man reached the bottom, using the water, pulling him off balance. Lune driving her shoulder into the second, both of them going under, the world becoming roar and bubbles and the white slash of a blade. Surfacing. Ferro still upright, still fighting, holding the stair exactly the way he'd promised, his bad leg braced against a pillar. The third man swinging something heavy — not a knife, a boat-hook, an iron hook on a pole —

"LUNE!" Ferro's voice, cracking upward into something younger than he'd ever let himself sound. "THE DOOR — GO THROUGH THE DOOR—"

The hook caught him across the temple. She saw it. She saw him fold into the water the way cloth folds, without resistance, without ceremony, and she screamed his name and the cistern ate the scream and gave it back to her doubled, tripled, a chorus of her own voice telling her what she refused to know.

Hands seized her — Bex, Ash, Odile — dragging her backward through the water while she fought them, while the men on the stairs waded toward the bronze door, while somewhere beneath the surface her brother drifted in the dark.

"The door's open!" Ash shouted. "It's open, we have to go NOW—"

"He's in the water—" Lune's voice didn't sound like hers. "He's in the water, he's — FERRO—"

Bex got an arm around her throat, gentle and immovable. "We get the ledger or he bled for nothing," Bex said, and her voice broke on the last word, and Lune understood that Bex loved him too, that they all did, that this was what a family was — everyone bleeding from the same wound.

They went through the door.

* * *

The vault beyond was dry.

That was the horror of it — after all that drowning, all that dark water, the chamber beyond the bronze door stood empty and airless and bone-dry, a small square room lined with niches, and in the central niche, wrapped in waxed leather and sealed with lead, a book.

Lune stood dripping in the doorway while Odile crossed the room and lifted the ledger out with both hands, reverent and revolted at once, the way you'd lift a snake you'd been sent to save. Water ran off Lune's clothes and pooled on the ancient floor and she stared at the puddle and thought: *he's still in the water. My brother is still in the water and I'm standing in a dry room.*

Behind them, boots on the stair. Voices. Vane's men at the threshold, and beyond them, cutting through everything, a new sound — Vane himself, calling from the cistern, his voice fat with triumph:

"Amberdale! Step aside and we all walk out rich!"

Odile turned. In the lantern-light, with the ledger held against her chest, she looked at Lune — really looked, the way she almost never let herself look at anyone — and whatever she saw in Lune's face made her jaw tighten.

"Your brother," Odile said quietly. Not a question. An acknowledgment. A debt being entered into a different kind of ledger, one that didn't burn.

"He held the line," Lune said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "That was the deal. Twins hold the line."

Outside, the water was still rising. Outside, Ferro floated in the dark. And in Odile Amberdale's hands, the fate of every well in the Empty Quarter waited — while Idris Vane's silhouette filled the doorway, and the storm above them all grew loud enough to swallow the world.
