## 1. The Debt of Optimists

The office above the bait shop smelled like fish guts and expensive cologne, which Wren Blackwood decided was as good a summary of the Voss brothers as any.

She sat in a chair that had been chosen specifically to be uncomfortable—low to the ground, so that whoever sat in it had to look up at the desk, and up at the brothers behind the desk. It was a trick she recognized because she'd used it herself. When you wanted someone to feel small, you didn't threaten them. You just arranged the furniture.

Denny Voss sat on the left, big and pink-faced, with the cheerful energy of a man who genuinely loved his work. Ray Voss sat on the right, thin and quiet, doing a crossword puzzle. He hadn't looked up once since she'd come in. Somehow that was worse.

"Wren!" Denny spread his hands like she was an old friend arriving at a barbecue. "You look fantastic. Sunburnt. Healthy. That's what I like to see in a client—somebody who takes care of herself."

"I take care of myself fine," Wren said. "That's why I'm here."

"See, that's optimism." Denny pointed at her, delighted. "Ray, you hear that? Optimism. That's what I love about this business. Everybody who walks through that door is an optimist. Nobody borrows money from us because they're planning to fail."

Ray turned a page of his newspaper without looking at it. "Nobody plans to fail," he said mildly. "They just fail to plan."

"That's very good, Ray. You should write those down." Denny leaned forward on his elbows, and the good humor in his face stayed exactly where it was, which was the frightening part. It never moved. It was like weather painted on a wall. "So. Let's look at where we are, shall we? You borrowed forty thousand. You've paid back eleven. The storm season ate your plane's engine, your charter bookings fell through, and now you owe us—" he consulted a folder he clearly didn't need to consult, "—fifty-two thousand, four hundred and six dollars. Which is more than you started with, which some people would call unfair, but I'd call it arithmetic."

"Compound interest," Wren said.

"Compound *optimism*," Denny corrected warmly. "We believed in you when nobody else would. Banks don't lend to storm chasers, Wren. We did. Because we saw something in you."

"You saw collateral."

"We saw *potential*," Denny said, wounded. "And potential is what we're here to talk about today. Because here's the thing, sweetheart—we've been patient. Genuinely patient. But my brother's got a wedding coming up in the family, and Ray here needs a new boat, and we can't keep being the only optimists in this relationship."

Wren crossed her legs and made herself comfortable in the bad chair, which was its own kind of message. "So give me a number and a date."

"Ninety days." Denny held up three fingers, like a scout leader demonstrating a knot. "Fifty-two four, paid in full, ninety days from today. Or—"

"Or?"

"Or you work it off our way." He said it lightly, the way a man might offer you a second helping of dessert. "We've got interests. Properties need managing. People need collecting from. You're good with weather, good with machines, good with showing up places people don't want you. There's a career in that, if flying charters doesn't work out."

The room went very quiet except for Ray's pen, scratching out a seven-letter word for *deception*.

Wren had seen what "their way" looked like. Everyone in Cairns had, if they'd been paying attention—a fisherman named Ollie Prade who'd owed the Vosses nine grand and worked it off running cargo up the coast until his boat "caught fire" off Fitzroy Island with him aboard. The coroner called it accidental. Ollie's sister called it anything but, quietly, and then not so quietly, and then she moved to Perth.

"I'll get the money," Wren said.

"Course you will." Denny beamed. "You know what I admire about you? Most people in that chair start crying or negotiating. You just sit there like you're already halfway to the bank. That's the spirit that got you into this mess, and it's the spirit that's going to get you out of it."

He slid a single sheet of paper across the desk. Ninety days, dated, signed. She noticed they'd already prepared it. They'd known before she walked in how this would go.

She signed it. Her signature came out steadier than she felt, which she counted as a professional victory.

"Pleasure doing business," Denny said, shaking her hand with both of his, warm and dry. "Ninety days, Wren. And hey—if you find yourself short, don't be a stranger. Our way isn't so bad. Lots of fresh air."

Outside, the Cairns heat hit her like a wet towel. She stood on the Esplanade watching the trawlers and tried to do the math honestly, which was a thing she almost never did, because honest math was where hope went to die.

Fifty-two thousand in ninety days. Her Cessna was worth maybe thirty if she sold it, and then she'd have no income at all, and the Vosses would own her anyway by winter. Charter season was dead. Her savings were a joke she told at bars. Every road forward ended at the same office above the bait shop, sitting in the low chair, hearing about fresh air.

She needed one job. One long-shot, high-payout, stupid-risk job—the kind she'd built her whole life around chasing. Storms paid nothing unless you got the footage nobody else could get. Salvage paid everything if you found what nobody else knew was there.

She bought a beer at a pub with sticky floors and sat near the window, and that was where she found the pearl diver.

* * *

His name was Gus Ferreira, and he was seventy if he was a day, with forearms like mooring rope and a voice sandpapered down by forty years of salt and rum. He held court at the end of the bar most nights, telling stories that got longer and less verifiable with each round. Wren had heard him talk about a Japanese submarine in the Coral Sea, a pearl the size of a fist in Broome, and a crocodile in the Daly River that he claimed had learned to wait for him specifically.

Tonight he was on New Guinea.

"—and the water comes *in*, see, like the whole sea changes its mind," Gus was saying, gesturing with a glass that had stopped being full some time ago. "King tide. Biggest of the season. And the cave opens like a mouth."

"What cave?" somebody asked, humoring him.

"The cave, mate. Sepik mouth. Old man Kerebu told me about it—river people, they've known forever, they don't go in. War time, the Nips ran caches all through that coast. Crates, crates, crates. Gold pay, medical supplies, silk, whatever they couldn't ship home when the war turned. Some of it went into caves along the delta. This one floods twice a year, only at king tide, and when the tide drops—" he smacked his lips, "—the sea sucks back out and there it all sits. Dry as a sermon. Forty years of nobody touching it because the river people say it's cursed, and the whitefellas are too lazy to check."

"Somebody would've checked," the humor-er said.

"Somebody did." Gus tapped his nose. "1971. Two blokes out of Madang. Came back with enough to buy trawlers." He paused for effect. "Neither of 'em ever went back. Ask yourself why."

"Because it's rubbish and they were embarrassed."

"Because," Gus said, leaning in with the gravity of a man about to part with wisdom, "you only get one tide. Miss your window, and the cave shuts its mouth for another season. And the river—" he shook his head slowly, "—the river forgets nothing, but it forgives even less."

The bar laughed. Wren didn't.

She was already doing the math, and for once the math was kind. A salvage run: fuel, a boat passage, gear, a guide. Maybe eight thousand all-in, money she could scrape together by selling the Cessna's spare propeller and lying to everyone she knew. Against fifty-two thousand in war salvage, minus whatever a broker took, minus the Vosses' cut—

Minus the Vosses' cut. That was the trap coiled under every plan she made. Anything she earned, they'd smell within a week. They had noses for other people's money the way sharks had noses for blood. If she surfaced in Cairns with fifty grand, Denny Voss would be waiting at the airstrip with flowers.

Which meant she couldn't earn it here. She had to earn it somewhere the Vosses' arithmetic couldn't follow—and bring it back quietly, or not come back at all.

New Guinea. Eight thousand kilometers of monsoon, crocodiles, malaria, and a river that forgave nothing. Perfect. It was exactly the kind of plan she specialized in: the kind that sounded brilliant at midnight and suicidal at dawn.

She bought Gus another rum and carried it over.

"Gus. Tell me about the tide."

* * *

He talked for two hours, and by the end of it Wren had a map drawn on a beer coaster—Gus drew like a drunk cartographer, all wobble and confidence—and a schedule in her head. Next king tide at the Sepik mouth: roughly seventy days out. Tight against her ninety, but doable, if nothing went wrong, which nothing ever did.

By the third rum she'd stopped noticing that Gus's story changed details between tellings, and by the fourth she'd stopped caring. Belief, she'd learned, wasn't something that happened to you. It was something you decided, the way you decided to fly into weather the instruments said you shouldn't. You committed first and let the facts catch up.

The next morning, hungover and clear-eyed in the way that only hangovers are clear, she made three phone calls.

The first was to a marine broker in Cairns who owed her a favor, asking after boats heading north. He gave her a name: the *Karawa Star*, a salvage vessel out of Thursday Island doing a survey run along the north New Guinea coast, needing a deckhand with a pilot's license and no questions asked. Pay was terrible. Passage was free. Cover was perfect.

The second call was to her bank, to confirm what she already knew: that her balance wouldn't cover the ferry to Thursday Island, let alone a salvage expedition. She sold the Cessna's spare propeller to a crop-duster outfit in Mareeba for eleven hundred dollars and felt each one leave her like a tooth.

The third call she almost didn't make. She dialed the number above the bait shop, let it ring twice, and hung up. Then she sat in her rented room with the ceiling fan clicking overhead and thought about Ollie Prade's sister moving to Perth, and about the low chair, and about Denny Voss's smile that never moved no matter what happened behind it.

Here was the truth she kept underneath the bravado like ballast: she was twenty-nine years old, she owned half an airplane and no home, and the most consistent relationship in her life was with a debt. She had spent a decade being excellent at leaving—leaving towns, leaving men, leaving wreckage—and she was starting to suspect that you could outrun anything except the accumulating interest on your own life.

Ninety days. One tide. One cave.

She packed that night. Two shirts, a rain jacket, a knife, a waterproof bag for documents, and—because she was who she was—a battered notebook of weather charts going back six years, because a person should travel with the record of every storm she'd ever chased, even if the storms had never once paid for themselves.

At the wharf the next morning, the *Karawa Star* sat low and rust-streaked in the grey dawn water, and her captain, a Tongan named Sione with a face like a closed door, looked Wren up and down exactly once.

"You can handle a winch?"

"Yes."

"You get seasick?"

"No."

"You running from something?"

Wren smiled her best smile, the one that had gotten her out of a hundred bars and into ninety-nine problems. "Captain, I'm running toward something. There's a difference."

"There always is," Sione said, and turned away, which she chose to take as a yes.

The *Karawa Star* cleared the harbor at noon. Cairns shrank behind them into a green smear under a sky stacked with thunderheads, and Wren stood at the stern rail watching the weather build over the ranges—reading it the way other people read letters from home, noting the anvil tops, the wind shear, the beautiful and indifferent machinery of a storm deciding where to land.

Somewhere ahead of her was a river that forgot nothing, a cave that opened twice a year, and fifty-two thousand reasons not to fail.

Behind her, in an office above a bait shop, Denny Voss was probably telling someone about the optimistic young woman who'd walked out of his office with ninety days and a plan.

He'd have laughed, she thought, if he knew where she was going. He'd have said the river people were right to stay out of the water.

Wren turned her face north, into the wind, and let the coast fall away.
