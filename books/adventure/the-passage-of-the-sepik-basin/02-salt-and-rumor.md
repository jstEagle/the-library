## 2. Salt and Rumor

The Saltwater Hotel in Cairns had a ceiling fan that turned with the confidence of a thing that had never once worked, and a bartender who polished the same glass all night like it owed him money. Wren Blackwood liked it for both reasons. Nothing in the Saltwater pretended to be better than it was. She'd spent three weeks pretending exactly that, and she was tired of it.

She sat at the end of the bar with a beer she was making last, her notebook open to a page of numbers that read like a eulogy. Ninety days. That was what the Voss brothers had given her, ninety days, delivered with such warmth—Denny Voss had actually patted her hand, and his brother Callum had laughed like she'd told a good joke—that it took a full day for the horror of it to arrive properly. Interest compounding weekly. Collateral: "whatever you've got, love, and we'll find out what that is." They were cheerful men. That was the worst part. Cruelty you could brace against; cheerfulness got under your door.

The notebook said she had four hundred and eleven dollars, a storm-chasing rig worth maybe eight thousand if she could find an idiot buyer, and a talent for reading weather that had made her famous in a community of perhaps two hundred people worldwide, none of them rich.

"Numbers don't lie," said a voice beside her, "but they do gossip."

The speaker was a man built like something the tide had argued with and partially won. He was seventy if he was a day, sunburned to the color of old rope, with white eyebrows that seemed to be attempting escape in two directions. A canvas satchel slumped at his feet, leaking the smell of brine and rum.

"I'm sorry?" Wren said.

"Your book." He tapped the page with a finger like a knuckle of driftwood. "You keep looking at it like it's going to apologize. It won't. Numbers are gossips—they only tell you what everybody already knows."

"That's very philosophical for a Tuesday."

"It's very drunk for a Tuesday," he agreed, and hoisted himself onto the stool beside her with the grace of a man who had done it ten thousand times and failed twice. "Ted Mowbray. Pearl diver. Retired, mostly. The sea retired me; I didn't have a say."

"Wren Blackwood."

He squinted at her. "Blackwood. Blackwood. You're the weather one. The one who stood on the radio and told everyone the cyclone was going to turn north when the bureau said south, and it did."

"Once. Everyone remembers the once."

"Well, I remember it, and I'm nobody's easy memory." He signaled the bartender with two fingers, then added a third finger for Wren before she could refuse. "A woman doing arithmetic in a pub at seven in the evening is either in trouble or planning something. In my experience the second is just the first wearing a nicer dress."

Wren closed the notebook. "I'm fine."

"You're fine," Ted repeated, with the flat reverence of a man handling a known falsehood. "Right. Well. I'm fine too. Cheers to us both." He drank. She, after a moment, drank too, because refusing free beer felt like bad economics, and economics was the whole of her life now.

* * *

By the third drink, Ted Mowbray had established the following: he had dived for pearl shell from Thursday Island to the Aru Islands for forty-one years; he had been married twice, both times to women who correctly identified him as a liability; he had a boat called the *Patient Wife*, which he described as "the only one that never left me"; and his left ear no longer worked since a barotrauma incident in 1979 that he told with such relish you'd think the ear had died a hero.

Wren mostly listened. Listening was cheap, and she'd learned in the debt-collection offices of the Voss brothers that information was the only currency that never devalued. But somewhere around the fourth drink, Ted stopped telling stories about himself and started asking questions, and there was something in the way he asked—not nosy, but careful, like a man testing ice—that made her answer more honestly than she meant to.

"So what's your trade, weather girl?"

"Storm chasing. Tornadoes, mostly, back home. Supercells. I drove into things other people drove out of, and sold the footage."

"And now?"

"Now I'm between storms."

Ted nodded slowly, as though this were a recognized medical condition. "Between storms," he said. "That's where the sea keeps most of its dead, you know. Not in the storms. In the betweens. Calm water, flat light, bloke thinks he'll just nip across the bay—" He waved his glass vaguely, indicating the entire ocean's capacity for ambush.

"Cheerful."

"I'm a diver. We're the cheerfulest people alive, because we know exactly how close we came." He leaned in, and his breath was rum and salt. "So. New Guinea. What's a weather girl doing signed onto a salvage run out of Port Moresby? Because I saw the manifest, love, and there's no meteorological station paying fares these days."

Wren went still. "You saw the manifest?"

"I helped load the deck winch. Old habit. I read cargo manifests the way priests read scripture—it tells you what people worship." He grinned, gap-toothed and unbothered by her stare. "Don't look at me like that. Half this town knows you took berth on the *Kestrel's Fancy*. The other half is guessing why. I'm the only one polite enough to ask."

"It's cover," she said, and then, because the beer had loosened something she couldn't afford to loosen: "It's a way to get out of the country before some people I owe money to decide to collect in person."

"How much?"

"More than I have. Less than they say."

Ted whistled low. "Voss brothers?"

Her silence answered him, and he nodded like a doctor confirming a diagnosis he'd already written down. "Everyone in the Gulf country knows the Voss brothers. Cheerful buggers. My cousin borrowed off them in '88 for a fishing boat. Paid it back double, on time, every week—and they still took the boat, because he'd signed a clause about 'goodwill maintenance.' Cheerful the whole time. Shook his hand at the end. Gave him a carton of beer." Ted shook his head with genuine admiration. "I've met sharks with worse manners and better intentions."

"Thank you. This is very comforting."

"I'm not comforting you. I'm sizing you up." He turned on his stool to face her fully, and behind the drunkenness there was suddenly something else, something steady and appraising, the look of a man who had spent decades deciding underwater whether a shadow was a shell or a stonefish. "Because either you're a fool who'll be fish food inside a month, or you're the kind of desperate that finds doors. And I've got a weakness for the second kind."

"A door," Wren repeated.

"A door." He drained his glass and set it down with a click that sounded, in the quiet of the Saltwater, like a lock turning. "But first you have to tell me true: how much do you actually know about tides?"

* * *

What Wren knew about tides, it emerged, was considerable but theoretical. She understood the mechanics—the moon's pull, the spring-neap cycle, the way a king tide stacked itself out of lunar perigee and equinoctial alignment like a debt compounding. She had chased storm surges across two continents. She had watched the ocean climb over seawalls and swallowed hard and driven closer.

"But you've never worked one," Ted said. "Never read a flood current coming through a gap, never felt a cave breathe."

"No."

"Mm." He ordered another round, which struck Wren as extravagant, and then he hunched over his beer like a man about to hand over state secrets, glancing left and right at an audience consisting of the bartender and a stuffed barramundi. "Then listen. Because I'm going to tell you something I've told no one sober, and I'm only half sober now, so it counts as a secret."

"Go on."

"Forty years back—no, thirty-eight—I was diving the north coast of New Guinea. Japanese war stuff was still turning up everywhere then. Landing barges, fuel drums, crates of rifles, whole airstrips grown over. Every village had a story about a cache in the bush, and half the stories were true, and the true ones were mostly junk—rust and rice gone to mush. But there was one story that wasn't junk, because I touched it."

He held up his right hand, flexing the fingers as if the memory lived in them.

"Sepik mouth. The river comes out of the country there like a brown sea of its own, miles wide, and the coast around it is all mangrove and limestone—karst, the rock that eats water. There's a headland, doesn't matter the name, and under the headland there's a cave. The locals knew it forever, of course—knew it better than anyone, because it's their country—but during the war the Japanese used it. Stashed things in there. And here's the trick of it, weather girl: the cave opens twice a season. King tides only. The rest of the year the entrance is under six fathoms of water and the current through the gap would strip the skin off a crocodile. The tide goes out far enough, long enough, and the cave drains and stands open like a mouth saying *ahh*. For maybe three hours. Then the sea takes it back and keeps it till next time."

Wren's pen was out. She hadn't decided to take it out; her hand had simply done it, the way her hand reached for the wheel when the pressure dropped. "And you touched what?"

"Crate stencils. I was young and stupid and my partner was younger and stupider, and we rode the ebb in with torches and a dinghy. Saw crates stacked to the roof of the first chamber—medical stores, some ordnance, sealed drums. And past that, deeper, where our lights gave out, the local stories say there's older things. Much older. Things the river people put there before any Japanese ever drew a map." His voice had dropped, and the bartender, polishing his eternal glass, had stopped polishing. Even the fan seemed to slow. "Carved stone. Gold, maybe. Who knows. The elders wouldn't say, and when we asked wrong, they stopped asking us anything at all and rowed us out at gunpoint, politely, with food for the trip."

"What happened to your partner?"

"Drowned two years later off Daru. Nothing to do with the cave. Or everything, depending on who you ask after the fifth beer." Ted shrugged. "Me, I went back to pearls and kept my mouth shut for thirty-eight years. So why am I talking now?"

"Why are you?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and the appraisal in his eyes finished whatever calculation it had been running.

"Because I'm dying, love. Doctor says the lungs. Six months, maybe eight, and the sea can have what's left. And because I watched you do arithmetic like a woman planning an escape, and I thought: there's someone who might actually go. Everyone else I've ever told has wanted the treasure without the tide, the gold without the gap. You chase storms. You understand that the dangerous part and the valuable part are the same part." He tapped her notebook. "King tide at the Sepik mouth comes in nineteen days. Next one after that, the season turns and the monsoon shuts the whole coast down for months. So it's now or a year of waiting, and I'd wager you haven't got a year."

Wren stared at him. Her heart was doing something embarrassing and hopeful, hammering like a fist on a locked door. "You're serious."

"I'm drunk and serious. The overlap is smaller than you'd hope but bigger than you'd fear."

"The salvage expedition—"

"—is your ticket across. Fine. Keep it. Salvage is salvage; nobody asks a salvager what she's salvaging." He dug in his canvas satchel and produced, of all things, a nautical chart, soft as cloth from handling, and spread it on the bar over her notebook. His cracked fingernail found a stretch of coastline west of the Sepik delta, a smudge of islands and mangrove. "Here. Headland. Ask at the river villages for the keeper of the reed houses—an old woman, spirit-keeper, they'll know who you mean. If anyone can tell you whether the cave's story is still true, and whether you're allowed, it's her. And hear me, Blackwood—" his finger came down harder, "—*allowed* matters. Take nothing from that water without leave. I've seen what happens to thieves in that country, and it isn't police. It's older than police."

"Older than police," Wren said. "That's a low bar."

Ted laughed, a great rusty bellows of a laugh that turned heads at the other end of the bar. "There's hope for you yet." He folded the chart and pushed it across to her, along with a stub of pencil. "Nineteen days. Tide tables are in the almanac, but you knew that. You probably feel the moon in your teeth already."

She did, which frightened her a little. All evening she had been doing sums in the margins of her grief—four hundred and eleven dollars, ninety days, interest compounding—and now the sums had changed shape entirely. A cave that opened twice a season. War caches. Older things. Enough, possibly, to buy back her name from two cheerful men who collected in blood. It was a mad plan. It was the kind of plan that ended with your partner drowned off Daru and your lungs full of the sea.

It was also, for the first time in three weeks, a plan that ended anywhere other than the Voss brothers' office.

"Ted," she said slowly, "why give this away? Why not sell it? There are salvage brokers in Townsville who'd pay—"

"Because brokers send crews," he said simply. "Crews with cranes and dynamite and no manners, and they'd crack that place open like an egg and leave the country bleeding. And because—" he paused, and for a moment the drunkenness fell away entirely and he looked, briefly, like a much younger man standing on a deck in 1986, "—because I always meant to go back and do it proper. With permission. And I never did, and now I won't. So I'm giving the errand away. Consider yourself entrusted, not informed."

Wren picked up the pencil. Her hand was steady. That surprised her most of all.

"One question," she said. "If the locals stop asking you anything and row you out at gunpoint—how does a stranger get permission from a spirit-keeper?"

Ted smiled, and began, with tremendous ceremony, to finish his beer.

"Ah," he said. "That, weather girl, is the easy part. You tell the truth. Nobody ever tries it; it works like magic."

* * *

She walked back to her room at the Seaview Flats at midnight, the chart rolled inside her jacket, the salt wind off Trinity Bay pushing at her back like a hand between the shoulder blades. The monsoon season was building somewhere over the Coral Sea; she could feel it in the pressure the way other people felt rain in their knees. Nineteen days to king tide. Ninety days to the Voss brothers' patience running out. For once, the arithmetic favored her.

In her room she pinned the chart above the desk and traced the coastline with one finger, west, west, to the brown mouth of the Sepik, to a headland that wasn't named, to a cave that opened like a mouth saying *ahh*.

Below the window, the tide came in over the mudflats, patient and enormous, keeping its own books.

Wren packed her bag.
