## 3. The River That Forgets Nothing

The Sepik did not look like a river. It looked like a brown country that had decided, at some point in its long and patient history, to move.

Wren stood on the mud bank at Angoram and watched it go by — a mile wide, the color of strong tea left too long, carrying whole trees sideways with a calm that struck her as faintly insulting. In Cairns she had chased storms that screamed. This thing didn't scream. It just took what it wanted and kept moving, like a debt collector with better manners.

"You're staring at it like it owes you money," said the man behind her.

She turned. He was sitting on an upturned fuel drum outside the trade store, mending a net with fingers that moved without his attention, the way her own hands checked barometers. Mid-thirties, maybe. A face that had been carved by weather rather than born smooth, and eyes the flat grey-green of water over sandbar — the color of something deciding whether to let you pass.

"I'm looking for a guide," Wren said.

"Everyone is." He didn't look up from the net.

"Someone who knows the middle river. The channels above Timbunke. The side creeks."

That got his eyes up. Not much. An inch. "The side creeks are not for tourists."

"I'm not a tourist."

"No," he agreed, and there was something in the way he said it that made her feel catalogued. "Tourists have clean boots."

Wren looked down at her boots. They were, admittedly, a disgrace — salt-stained from the tramp steamer out of Cairns, cracked at the toe, one lace replaced with fishing line. She'd made choices about how to spend the last of her cash, and waterproof boots had lost to diesel and dried noodles.

"The boots are load-bearing," she said. "They've been through three cyclone seasons and a divorce."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. It might have been amusement. It might have been indigestion. With this man, she suspected, the two were hard to tell apart.

"Tomas Kerebu," he said, standing. He was taller than he'd seemed sitting down, which felt like a trick. "You'll want references. I don't have any. Everyone I ever guided either died of old age or went home and told lies about me, so my reputation is exactly as good as your judgment."

"That's honest."

"Honesty is cheap up here. Nobody can check it." He coiled the net over his shoulder. "What's the job?"

"A survey," Wren said. "Hydrographic. I chart flood behavior. Storm surge, tidal reach, that sort of thing."

It was a good lie. She'd rehearsed it on the deck of the *Merrie Ann* for eleven days, feeding it to the ship's cat, who had believed every word. But Tomas Kerebu looked at her the way the sea looks at a cork — patiently, with total confidence that the truth would bob up eventually.

"Flood behavior," he repeated.

"Where the water goes when it comes up."

"And where does the water come up?"

"Everywhere, eventually."

He almost smiled. Almost. "You want the cave."

The word landed between them like a dropped anchor. Wren kept her face still, which cost her something; she'd once held a poker face through a category four warning while a Voss brother counted her collateral aloud.

"I don't know what you mean."

"There's always someone, every dry season. Pearl divers, ex-soldiers, one German with a metal detector and a hat like a mushroom. Looking for the war caches. The Japanese stores. The gold somebody's cousin saw in 1944." He shrugged. "They hire a boat, they go up a creek, the tide turns, and they come back with nothing but malaria. Or they don't come back. The river forgets nothing, lady. Including them."

"My name's Wren. And I'm not looking for gold."

"Then you're looking for something else, which is worse. Gold people give up when they find none. People looking for something else—" he shook his head, "—they keep going until the river stops them."

*You have no idea,* Wren thought, *how right you are.* Somewhere south of her, across the Torres Strait and half a continent, Marek Voss was presumably doing whatever Marek Voss did between collections: sharpening things, smiling at people in airports. Ninety days. Fifty-eight left. The arithmetic lived in her chest now like a second heartbeat.

"I can pay," she said. "Cash. Half up front."

"How much?"

She named a figure that was forty percent of everything she had. His eyebrows performed a small, skeptical dance.

"For a survey," he said.

"Surveys pay well."

"Surveys pay institutions. Institutions have letterheads." He studied her again, longer this time, and she had the uncomfortable sense of being read like a gauge — needle trembling, numbers uncertain. "Who told you about the cave? Was it old Manu? He sells that story to everyone off the boats. Or the diver? Salt-crusted fellow, drinks at the Angoram hotel, tells it better after the fourth rum?"

Wren said nothing, which was itself an answer.

"The pearl diver," Tomas sighed. "Of course. He's been telling that story since before I had beard hair. Half of it's true, which makes it more dangerous than if it were all lies." He began walking toward the water, and after a moment she realized she was expected to follow. "Come on. If I don't take you, you'll find someone worse. Old Beno will take you up the Keram in a boat with a soft floor, and then I'll have to hear about it when they drag the creek."

"So that's a yes?"

"It's a yes shaped like a complaint. Most things up here are."

* * *

His boat was a long aluminum runabout with a 40-horse Yamaha that had clearly been maintained by someone who loved it, and a canvas shade that had clearly not. There was a rifle wrapped in oilcloth under the bow seat, a jerry can lashed amidships, and a wooden crate of tinned beef that Tomas introduced as "the crew."

"We're the crew," Wren said.

"You're cargo that talks."

They ran upriver through the afternoon, and Wren forgot, for stretches at a time, to be afraid. That was the thing about the Sepik nobody had warned her: it was beautiful in a way that felt less like scenery and more like anesthesia. The banks unspooled — sago swamp and pandanus, tall grass burning gold where villages had cleared gardens, crocodiles log-still on the mud with their mouths open like punctuation. Children paddled dugouts in the middle of that enormous brown traffic, waving with both hands, laughing at the wake. Hornbills crossed overhead in pairs, wingbeats like loose shutters.

Tomas drove with one hand and talked with the other, though "talked" was generous. He issued commentary the way a judge issues sentences.

"Log ahead, right side. Dead ones float high, live ones float low, and the live ones are annoyed."

"Village on the left. We don't stop. The pastor there owes me eleven kina and pretends to be deaf about it."

"Current changes after the bend. Feel it?" He didn't wait for her to answer. "The river speeds up on the outside of a turn and slows on the inside. Same as life. Cheap lesson. Free, actually. You're welcome."

But between the jokes — and there were jokes, dry little fish that surfaced and sank again — she caught him doing something else. Reading. His eyes moved over the water constantly, not scanning but *reading*, the way she read cloud decks: structure, trend, intent. Once he killed the engine entirely, mid-channel, and let them drift, head cocked, for a full minute.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"Grass seed on the surface." He pointed. A scatter of pale flecks moved against the current, very slightly, very steadily. "Water coming in from a side channel underground. Means there's a spring-fed creek behind that bank. Means fresh water. Means sometimes fish hold there." He restarted the engine. "Also means the bank is hollow in places. Don't camp there."

"You can see all that from seeds?"

"I can see all that from seeds," he said, "because I grew up on this river and it has never once lied to me. People lie. Water doesn't. Water tells you exactly what it's going to do. The trouble is most people don't speak it, so they call it unpredictable." He glanced at her sidelong. "You're a storm woman. You know this already. I can tell."

"How can you tell?"

"Because when we came around the bend an hour ago, you looked upstream first. Before the village, before the birds, before anything pretty. You looked where the weather comes from." He said it without admiration, exactly, and without contempt. More like a man confirming a suspicion he'd hoped was wrong. "Storm women are useful and unlucky. Which are you?"

"Both," Wren said. "Mostly the second."

He grunted. It sounded, improbably, approving.

* * *

They camped that night on a high bank above a mission landing, long abandoned, the church roof gone to bats and the bell tower leaning like a drunk telling a secret. Tomas built a small fire and cooked rice with tinned beef and, from somewhere in the boat's anatomy, produced a lime, cutting it into wedges with great ceremony.

"Rationing," he explained. "One wedge each. The lime is the captain's privilege, but I'm sharing because you paid half up front and I respect a fool with cash."

"Gee, thanks."

"You're welcome." He handed her a plate. Then, while she ate, he asked, casually, the way a man asks a question he has already loaded: "Your survey. Who's paying for it?"

"A private concern."

"What concern?"

"The kind that doesn't like its name in reports."

"Mm." He fed the fire a stick. "In my experience, concerns that hide their names also hide their methods. And their methods usually arrive later, wearing better boots than yours, asking where the survey woman went."

Wren set down her plate. The fire popped. Down on the river, something big rolled once and settled, and the sound carried across the water like a door closing in another room.

"If I told you the truth," she said carefully, "you'd put me back in the boat and take me downriver tonight."

"Probably."

"And if I lie, you'll keep guessing, and you guess well, and eventually you'll guess right anyway."

"Almost certainly."

"So what do you want from me, Tomas?"

He considered this with what appeared to be genuine interest, turning his lime wedge in his fingers like a coin.

"I want to know," he said, "if the trouble following you is the kind that stops at you, or the kind that keeps walking. Because I have an aunt upriver. And a sister in Wewak with three kids. And a village full of people who think that because I read water, I can also read the future, and I am tired of disappointing them." He looked at her straight on, and the firelight did something complicated to those grey-green eyes. "So here is my offer. I take you where you're going. All the way. In exchange, when we get there — wherever 'there' is — you tell me the truth. The whole thing. Even the ugly parts. Especially those."

"Why then? Why not now?"

"Because now you'd lie, and we'd both know it, and then we'd have to spend six days on a small boat pretending otherwise, and I hate pretending. It's exhausting work." He lay back against his pack, hands behind his head. "Later, you'll be tired enough to be honest. Everyone is, eventually. The river sees to it."

Wren stared at him. "You're the strangest man I've ever hired."

"I'm the only man you've ever hired. Your standards will improve."

Despite herself — despite fifty-eight days ticking down in her chest and an enforcer with a smile like a wound somewhere behind her — she laughed. It came out rusty, like an engine started after a season in the shed, but it came out. Tomas heard it. She saw him register it, filing it away next to the grass seed and the dead logs, another fact about the water.

"Sleep," he said. "We make the lake country tomorrow. And after that—" he paused, and for the first time all day something in his easy voice went quiet and careful, "—after that, my aunt's place. Reed houses on the oxbow. If anyone on this river knows what your cave opens onto, it's her. She's the oldest keeper of stories in the basin. People say she remembers things that happened before she was born."

"Superstition?"

"Maybe." He closed his eyes. "But fair warning, storm woman. Ila doesn't ask questions. She just looks at you. And whatever she sees, she says. Out loud. To everyone."

Wren lay back on her mat and listened to the river moving in the dark — that vast, patient, brown country going by, taking what it wanted, forgetting nothing.

Fifty-seven days. She fell asleep doing the arithmetic, and dreamed, for the first time since Cairns, not of debt collectors, but of a woman's face she had never seen, framed in reeds, looking at her with terrible recognition.

She woke before dawn with the words still hanging in her ears, though no one had spoken:

*I know those eyes.*
