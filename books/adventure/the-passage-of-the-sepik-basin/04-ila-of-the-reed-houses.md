## 4. Ila of the Reed Houses

My aunt Ila had buried three husbands, outlived two missionaries, and once argued a crocodile into leaving her fishing net alone, so it takes a great deal to make her go quiet.

I had never seen her go quiet. Not in thirty-one years of being her nephew, her errand boy, her occasional disappointment, and — on two memorable occasions — the reason she had to apologize to the whole of Angoram market.

So when we poled up to her stilt house at dusk and Wren Blackwood climbed the ladder, and Ila looked into her face and stopped moving — stopped the way the river stops before a flood, all that power holding its breath — I felt the hair rise along my arms, and not because of the mosquitoes, though there were plenty of those too.

"Careful on the last rung," I said, because someone had to say something normal. "It's been rotten since the flood of '98."

"I heard that," Ila said, without looking at me. "The rung is fine. The nephew is rotten."

Wren laughed — a short, surprised sound, like she'd forgotten how — and stepped onto the veranda, and my aunt took both her hands and looked at her for a long time.

Too long. Long enough that Wren's smile began to fray at the edges.

* * *

Let me explain how we came to be there, because I had promised myself I would not bring this woman anywhere near my family, and here she was, dripping river water on my aunt's floorboards.

She'd arrived at Timbunke three days earlier with a rucksack full of expensive gear and no idea what any of it cost, asking after passage down the Sepik with the particular confidence of a person who has never yet been told no by a river. She wanted to reach the coast. She mentioned the old trade routes. She did not mention why, and when I asked, she gave me a smile I recognized from men who play cards in back rooms — the smile of somebody holding a hand they think you can't see.

I told her no. Politely. In three languages, so there could be no confusion.

Then she offered to pay double, and I have a daughter who needs school fees in Wewak, and an aunt who pretends she doesn't need a new roof, and a boat engine older than both of us combined. So: yes. But I made conditions. One of them was that we would stop at Ila's village, because anyone traveling the middle Sepik who doesn't pay respects to the spirit-keepers is either a fool or asking for trouble, and I had already decided Wren Blackwood was probably both.

"You didn't tell me your aunt was a *chief*," Wren said now, ducking under the low doorframe into the main room. The word came out wrong — she said it like *cheef*, the way the Australians do — but I let it pass.

"She's not a chief. Chiefs are elected and give speeches. My aunt just knows things." I set down our bags. "There's a difference. Chiefs want your vote. Ila wants your respect, and if you don't give it freely she'll take it anyway."

"That sounds like my mother," Wren said, and then went strange around the eyes, as if she'd surprised herself by mentioning a mother at all.

I filed that away. I was becoming good at filing things away about this woman. Her hands were soft except for rope burns that were weeks old. Her boots were broken in but her feet weren't — she walked like someone learning her own weight. And she watched the water constantly, hungrily, the way some people watch doors.

Ila's house smelled the way it always had: smoke, sago, dried fish, the sweet rot of the river underneath everything, the smell I cannot describe to outsiders except to say it is the smell of home, and home does not always smell nice. Oil lamps threw soft light across the carved posts. On the far wall hung the things Ila kept for reasons she explained only when she felt like it: shell valuables, a cassowary-bone dagger, photographs gone amber with age, and one small stone figure wrapped in tapa cloth that I had been told, repeatedly and firmly, never to touch.

"Sit," Ila said. "Both of you. You'll eat first, and then you'll tell me why my nephew has brought me a stranger with storm-eyes."

"Storm-eyes?" Wren glanced at me.

I shrugged. "Grey. Changeable. Destructive when provoked. It's not a compliment."

"It's a little bit of a compliment," Ila said, and pushed a bowl of fish stew into Wren's hands before the woman could argue, which was wise, because Wren struck me as someone who argued as a form of breathing.

* * *

Here is what I knew about my aunt's history, which is to say, here is what everyone in the village knew, which is to say, almost nothing, wrapped in a great deal of certainty.

Ila had left the basin once, as a young woman, in the years just after the war. Nobody knew exactly where she went or why. She came back two seasons later alone, thinner, quieter, and took up the work of spirit-keeper from old Mama Yarun, who died soon after with what witnesses described as a satisfied expression. Ila married, buried husbands, raised other people's children alongside her own, and became the person you went to when a child sickened strangely, or a canoe went missing, or a man dreamed the same dream three nights running. She settled disputes. She remembered genealogies going back past the Germans. She knew which patches of ground were hungry and should not be built on, and which trees held spirits who objected to being cut, and she was right about these things often enough that even the Lutheran pastor, Pastor Kila, who was a good man and a rational one, would quietly consult her before laying a church foundation.

And once — I was maybe ten — I had seen her sitting alone at dusk looking at a photograph she kept behind the tapa cloth, and she was crying, silently, the way you cry about something forty years old, and when she saw me watching she said, "Tomas. Some rivers run underground. That doesn't mean they stop running." Then she fed me sago and sent me home, and neither of us ever spoke of it again.

I tell you this so you understand that when Ila went still over Wren Blackwood's face, I was not merely curious. I was afraid.

* * *

They ate. Wren ate like a woman who'd learned not to trust the next meal — fast, efficient, but she paused at the first spoonful and closed her eyes, and I saw her remember something, though I couldn't guess what. Sago and smoked fish isn't anybody's first taste of home unless home is here.

"Good?" Ila asked.

"It's—" Wren stopped. Started again. "My father used to make something like this. Badly. He called it his expedition stew. It was mostly tinned things and optimism."

"And where is this father now?"

"Gone." Flat. A door closing. "A long time ago."

"Mm," said Ila, who could extract more from a single syllable than most people manage with a whole interrogation. "And your mother?"

"I don't have one."

Everyone in the room went carefully casual. Even the oil lamp seemed to lean in.

"Everyone has a mother," Ila said mildly. "Some of us are simply separated from them. By distance. By death. By decisions." She ladled more stew into Wren's bowl unasked. "Which kind are you?"

"The kind where it's none of my business," Wren said, and there was such rawness under the flippancy that I looked away to give her privacy, out at the dark water sliding past below the floorboards.

Ila let it go. That was her gift, knowing exactly how far to push before pushing became shoving. She turned to me instead and demanded news of the village upstream, of her cousin's grandchildren, of whether the mission still rang its bell off-key, and for half an hour the conversation was ordinary and warm, and I watched Wren slowly unknot, shoulder by shoulder, until she was laughing at my impression of Pastor Kila's sermons ("and *then*, brothers and sisters, he starts the sermon") and arguing cheerfully with Ila about whether crocodiles hold grudges.

"They absolutely do," Ila said.

"That's anthropomorphism," Wren said. "They're instinct-driven. They don't—"

"A crocodile named Two-Fangs held a grudge against your nephew for four years," Ila said. "Ask him."

"There was an incident," I admitted. "Involving a pig. And poor judgment. And a canoe."

"What happened to the pig?"

"We ate the pig. The crocodile disagreed with the arrangement. Four years, Wren. Every time I fished that bend of the river, he'd surface and look at me. Just look. Like a creditor."

Something flickered across her face at the word *creditor* — quick, there and gone, a shadow under water — and I added it to the file.

Later, Ila spread mats for us on the veranda, where the breeze kept the worst of the mosquitoes down, and as she tucked the netting around Wren's sleeping mat she paused, one hand on the girl's shoulder, and said something in our language, low, that I caught only partly. Something about coming back. Something about *long time*. Wren, already half asleep, murmured, "Sorry, I don't speak—" and drifted off mid-apology.

Ila stood over her a moment. In the lamplight her face was unreadable, which from Ila meant it was extremely readable, if you were family.

"Auntie," I whispered, when we were alone by the rail. "What did you see?"

"Her eyes."

"Her eyes are grey. So are mine. Half the basin has grey eyes; the whalers and the traders saw to that."

"Not the color." She turned the lamp down until it was a red coal. "The shape of them. The way she holds her mouth when she thinks no one is watching. Tomas." She said my name the way she did when she was about to assign me a chore I would hate. "Where did you find this woman?"

"Cairns, originally. She flew into Wewak, came upriver on the mail boat, hired me at Timbunke. Says she's a salvage diver. Says she's looking for trade routes to the coast." I hesitated. "Says nothing else, mostly."

"Salvage." Ila tasted the word like suspect fish. "Of what?"

"Whatever's down there, I suppose. She asked me about the old war caves near the delta. About king tides. About places the maps mark wrong on purpose."

For a long moment my aunt said nothing. Below us the river breathed against the pilings, that endless patient conversation the Sepik has with itself, mud and memory going down to the sea.

"There was a woman," Ila said finally, "who also asked about caves and tides. Forty years ago. She drew maps. Beautiful ones. She came up this same river with a survey team, and she stayed longer than the survey, and she learned our language badly and our songs beautifully, and she laughed like—" The old voice caught itself, steadied. "Like that one laughs. When she forgets to guard it."

The lamp-coal pulsed. Somewhere across the black water a night bird called, three notes, question and answer in one.

"Auntie. What are you telling me?"

"I'm telling you nothing," Ila said, "because I am an old woman and I may be wrong, and because if I am right, then saying it aloud will change everything for that girl, and everything deserves to arrive at the right tide, not the first one." She touched my cheek, which she hadn't done since I was a boy in trouble. "Watch her, Tomas. Not like a guide watches a client. Like a man watches weather. Because I think a storm is coming up this river behind her, and I think it has her name on it."

"She's in some kind of trouble," I admitted. "Money, I think. She won't say. There's a letter she keeps re-reading and burning the corners of."

"Mm." Ila gathered her lamp. At the doorway she paused. "And Tomas — whatever she asks you to take her to see, when the tide goes out — you come to me first. Promise me."

"Auntie—"

"*Promise* me."

I promised. Ila of the Reed Houses had buried three husbands and outlived two missionaries, and I had no intention of joining either list through disobedience.

* * *

I slept badly, which was normal on a stranger's first night in my aunt's village, because the sounds are different and the river talks louder here. What was not normal was waking before dawn to voices below — low, urgent — and finding Wren already awake, standing at the rail in the grey light, staring downriver with an expression I had last seen on soldiers' faces in old photographs.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Probably nothing." But her knuckles were white on the rail. "I thought I heard an engine. Way down. A launch, maybe. Big one."

"At this hour?"

"Fishing boats start early."

"Not launches with engines like that." I listened. Faint, far off, threading under the river-sound: a deep mechanical pulse, there and gone, like something big feeling its way through the delta channels. "That's not a fishing boat. Fishing boats sound like sewing machines. That sounds like money."

Wren said nothing. In the growing light I watched her decide — visibly, effortfully — whether to lie to me. I watched her choose the lie, and I watched it cost her something to choose it, and I filed that away too, because it mattered: she wasn't a liar by nature. She was a liar by circumstance. There is a difference, and the difference can save a man's life or end it, depending entirely on which way the current runs.

"Bad dreams," she said. "Forget it."

"Sure," I said. "Dreams."

Behind us, the door curtain moved. Ila stood there in the pre-dawn dimness, dressed, her small stone bundle already wrapped in its cloth and held against her chest — and I realized with a lurch that my aunt, who had not traveled further than Angoram market in nine years, was preparing to come with us.

"The tide turns tomorrow evening," she said, in the tone she used for announcing floods and funerals. "You'll want to be at the delta by then. All three of us will."

Wren blinked. "All three? I only paid for—"

"My roof leaks," Ila said serenely, "and my nephew is rotten, and I wish to inspect what he's doing with his life. Also—" and here her eyes found mine, and held, and what passed between us was older than either of us, "—some things should be present when their own stories open. Pack the boat, Tomas. We're taking the girl to the sea."

Downriver, faint as a heartbeat, the engine pulsed again. Closer.

Wren's face, when she thought none of us could see it, was the face of a woman doing arithmetic with her own life — and getting an answer she didn't like.

I started packing the boat.
