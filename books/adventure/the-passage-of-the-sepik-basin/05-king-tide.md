## 5. King Tide

The tide began to leave at dawn, and it left like a guest who had overstayed—slowly, grudgingly, sucking the water out of the delta in long grey draughts until the mangrove roots stood up on their tiptoes and the mudflats gleamed like hammered pewter.

Wren watched it from the stilt platform of Ila's reed house, a mug of something hot and faintly fishy cupped in both hands, and tried to calculate.

She was good at calculations. It was most of what a storm chaser was: a person who did arithmetic against the sky. Pressure falling so many millibars per hour meant wind rising so many knots. Cloud tops reaching so many kilometres meant hail or no hail, rotation or no rotation, run or stay. She had spent eleven years turning numbers into decisions made fast and loud from the passenger seat of a truck with a cracked windshield, and she had been very good at it right up until the year the numbers stopped being about weather and started being about money, which was a different kind of storm entirely—one that didn't give you barometric warnings, just cheerful phone calls from men named Voss who said *no rush, Wren, we're family* in voices that made her skin crawl.

So she calculated. The tide charts Tomas had drawn for her in charcoal on a strip of bark paper said the king tide would bottom out at 6:40 in the morning, local time, whatever *local time* meant to a river that kept its own hours. The cave mouth, according to the pearl diver's drunken testimony in a Cairns bar three months ago, sat below the low-water line for all but a handful of days each season. Today was one of those days. The moon had been fat and wrong-coloured last night, hanging over the delta like a lamp left burning in an empty house.

"Drink," Ila said behind her, not looking up from the net she was mending. "You make a face like a woman doing sums. Sums are bad before breakfast."

"It's good arithmetic," Wren said. "That's the problem."

"Arithmetic is always the problem." Ila pulled a knot tight with her teeth. "The river does not count. That is why it is wiser than us."

Tomas came up the ladder from the water with his hair still dripping, carrying two paddles and the expression of a man who had lost an argument overnight and knew he would lose it again by lunchtime. He'd been losing arguments to Wren for six days now, ever since she'd told him what she was actually looking for, and he had developed a way of sighing that managed to be both patient and deeply insulting.

"The channel will be passable for maybe two hours," he said. "After that the tide turns and comes back faster than you can say goodbye. If the cave is real."

"If?"

"If," Tomas said, "you are chasing a story told by a drunk man in a bar. In my experience, drunk men in bars tell stories about treasure the way other men tell stories about women they almost loved. Generously, and with nothing behind it."

"And if it's real?"

"Then it is real, and we go in, and then we come out, and then I take you back here and we never speak of it again." He handed her a paddle. "Also, there may be crocodiles."

"There are always crocodiles."

"Yes. This is true. I mention them so you cannot later say nobody warned you." He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth he'd offered since Cairns, and Wren found herself absurdly pleased by it, the way you're pleased when a cat sits near you without being asked.

Ila looked up from her netting then, and her eyes went from Wren to the eastern horizon where the sea showed itself between the mangroves, a hard bright line of blue-grey under a sky scrubbed clean by last night's wind. Something moved across the old woman's face—not fear exactly, and not warning either. More like the look of someone watching a door open that they had hoped would stay shut.

"Take nothing that breathes," Ila said quietly.

"I'm not going to steal a crocodile, Ila."

"Take nothing," Ila repeated, "that breathes. And be back before the water remembers its manners."

* * *

They ran the channel with the engine at half-throttle, Tomas standing at the bow reading the water the way Wren read radar—every ripple a sentence, every slack patch of current a paragraph of warning. The delta narrowed into a drowned gorge where limestone walls rose sheer out of brown-green water, furred with vines, dripping even in dry air, and the sound of the sea came through somewhere ahead of them: a long, rhythmic exhalation, like the coast itself breathing through its teeth.

"There," Tomas said, and cut the engine.

The cave mouth was exactly where the drunk man had said it would be, which unsettled Wren more than anything else that morning. People lied about treasure; they did not usually lie accurately. It was a black arch perhaps four metres high, cut into the base of the cliff, its floor still slicked with retreating seawater that ran out of it in thin silver sheets, draining away like something exhaling after forty years of holding its breath.

Above the arch, carved into the rock by hands that had not cared whether anyone saw, was a spiral. Old. Salt-eaten. Older than the war crates the diver had promised, older probably than any nation Wren could name.

"That's not Japanese," she said.

"No," Tomas agreed. "That is much older than Japanese. My aunt says the spiral people were here before the river had its name." He shipped the paddles and looked at her sideways. "We can still turn around."

"We can't."

"You owe someone money." It wasn't a question. He'd guessed weeks ago; she'd confirmed it with silence, which was as good as a confession.

"I owe several someones money," Wren said. "Brothers, actually. Three of them. They lend money the way other people keep bees—it looks friendly from a distance, but everything they touch has a sting in it somewhere." She heard herself talking too much and stopped. "Ninety days. Fifty-one left. The interest alone eats a hundred dollars a day."

"And this cave pays debts."

"This cave might." She looked at the dark arch and felt the old familiar heat kindle in her chest, the one that used to get her through tornado country with her heart hammering and her hands steady—the feeling that the world was about to hand her something enormous, if only she was quick enough and brave enough and greedy enough to take it. "Or it might not. But I have to look. You know how it is when a storm's building? Everyone tells you to drive the other way, and every part of you that's worth anything wants to drive straight at it?"

"I do not know that," Tomas said. "But I believe you feel it." He turned the boat toward the arch. "So. Straight at it."

* * *

Inside, the cave opened like a cathedral that had been flooded and forgotten.

Their headlamps swept across vaulted ceilings hung with pale roots, across walls ribbed and fluted by ten thousand years of patient water, across a floor of smooth white silt printed everywhere with the tracks of things that had come in and not gone out. The air was cold and mineral and utterly still, and their voices came back to them flattened, as though the cave were listening politely and declining to comment.

And there were the crates.

Dozens of them. Rotted to lace in places, solid in others, stacked along a shelf of rock above the old high-tide line with a soldier's neatness that had outlived the soldiers. Stencilled characters still legible on one: cargo markings, dates from a war that had washed past this coast like weather. Wren pried one open with a length of driftwood and her headlamp filled with the dull amber glow of brass—ammunition casings fused into a single green-crusted mass, worthless now except as history. Another crate held mess tins. Another held boots that dissolved at a touch. A fourth, smaller crate, its lid sprung, spilled out glass bottles of sake, the rice inside having gone to a soft grey cheese that smelled like regret.

"Bones," Tomas said quietly, from further in.

He crouched beside a shape the silt had half-claimed. Two shapes. A man in the rotted rags of a uniform, and beside him, curled small, someone in cloth that had once been dyed and patterned and was now just the idea of cloth. They had lain down together, or been laid down together, and the cave had kept them the way it kept everything: completely, and without judgment.

Wren stood very still. The greed in her chest—that hot, climbing thing—went quiet for a moment, the way wind goes quiet in the eye of a storm, and she understood suddenly why Ila had said *take nothing that breathes*, because the dead breathed here too, somehow. The whole cave breathed. It was the largest lung she'd ever stood inside.

"Tomas," she said. "There's more. Back there. Past the crates."

"How do you know?"

"Airflow." She held up a strand of the root-fibre she'd been chewing absently; it drifted, faint but definite, toward the rear wall. "Moving air means another opening. Means the cave isn't sealed. Means—" She stopped herself from saying *means the treasure isn't just rusted shell casings*. But Tomas heard it anyway. He was getting annoyingly good at hearing the things she didn't say.

They went deeper, single file along the shelf, the silt giving softly underfoot, and the passage opened into a chamber where the ceiling rose and the headlamps couldn't find the top of it, and there—

Wren had chased storms for eleven years. She had stood within two hundred metres of a wedge tornado as it chewed a farm road into confetti. She had seen the sky turn the colour of a bruise and felt the pressure drop until her ears sang and her fillings ached, and she had thought, more than once, that nothing on earth would ever awe her again, because she had already seen the biggest thing there was to see.

She had been wrong.

The stone sat on a natural pedestal of flowstone at the chamber's centre, roughly the size of a human head, and it was carved—all of it, every surface, dense with spirals and interlocking figures that seemed to move when she wasn't looking directly at them. It was not precious stone. It was not gold. It was grey-green river rock, the same rock as the cliffs outside, and yet her headlamp seemed to sink into it rather than bounce off, and the air around it felt charged the way air feels twenty minutes before lightning, that prickling, pre-electric hush.

And it hummed. Not audibly—or maybe audibly, she genuinely couldn't tell—but she felt it in her sternum, in her back teeth, a low sustained note like a struck bell heard through a wall.

"Oh," she said. It was not a word so much as air leaving her body.

"What is it?" Tomas's voice came from behind her, careful.

"I don't know." She circled it slowly. "It's not a war cache. This predates the war by—by everything. This is the spiral. This is the same carving as over the entrance." Her mind was already running, fast and shameless, doing the arithmetic it did best: provenance unknown, age enormous, uniqueness absolute. Museums paid fortunes for fragments. Private collectors paid worse. An object like this, with a story like the one she could build around it—

She caught herself. Actually caught herself, mid-calculation, the way you catch yourself reaching for a wallet that isn't yours. For one clear second she saw what she was doing: standing in a tomb, next to two people who had died within arm's reach of this thing, pricing it.

*Take nothing that breathes.*

It doesn't breathe, she told the voice in her head that sounded like Ila. It's a rock. Rocks don't breathe.

But the hum said otherwise. The hum said the rock had been breathing longer than her whole species had been keeping records, patiently, in the dark, waiting for the tide to open the door twice a season and let the world come in and try to carry it away—and fail, every time, because things like this did not leave. Things like this got carried a little way and then the carrying stopped.

"Wren." Tomas touched her shoulder. "Your hands are shaking."

"They do that around big weather." She laughed, and it came out strange. "This is big weather, Tomas. Just the slow kind."

He looked at the stone, and then at her, and she watched him decide not to say whatever he was thinking, which was becoming a whole language between them.

"We should go," he said. "The tide turns soon."

"Yeah." She didn't move. Her headlamp stayed on the stone, and the stone stayed humming, and somewhere in the back of her skull the Voss brothers' cheerful voices were already counting. Fifty-one days. One hundred dollars a day. A number that grew while she slept.

She photographed it first—of course she did, eleven years of documentation reflex—and then, telling herself it was for leverage, for insurance, for proof of existence and nothing more, she worked her pack off one shoulder and reached for the stone.

It was warm. River rock in a cold cave should have been cold, and it was warm, blood-warm, and the hum climbed a note when her fingers closed on it, and every reasonable cell in her body screamed *put it down*, and she lifted it anyway, because she was Wren Blackwood, and taking the thing she shouldn't take was the only family trait she'd ever been sure of.

It fit in her pack like it had been measured for it.

* * *

They came out of the cave into light so fierce it felt personal. The tide had turned; water was already creeping back across the flats, thin fingers of it reaching for the arch, and Tomas ran the channel hard with his jaw set and his eyes flicking constantly to the gauge, and neither of them spoke until the gorge opened into the delta and the reed houses appeared, small and golden in the late-morning glare.

Ila was waiting on the platform. She was always waiting on the platform; Wren suspected the woman had been installed there at the creation of the world and simply never left.

Her eyes went to Wren's pack. Not to her face—to the pack, immediately, the way a doctor's eyes go to the wound.

"You took it," Ila said.

"I borrowed it," Wren said, and heard, even as the words left her, exactly how they sounded.

Ila set down her netting. She looked at Wren for a long moment, and there was something in her face that Wren had seen once before, years ago, on the face of a retired meteorologist looking at satellite imagery of a storm that was behaving in ways storms should not behave—not confusion, but recognition, terrible and unwelcome.

"That stone," Ila said, "has been in this basin longer than my grandmother's grandmother's name. It does not belong to me, or to you, or to the men whose bones are keeping it company. It belongs to the cave, and the cave belongs to the tide, and the tide answers to no one." She picked her netting back up, and her hands, Wren noticed, were not entirely steady. "Put it back tomorrow, girl, while the water is low. Or keep it, and learn what follows it home."

"Nothing's following anything," Wren said. "Nobody knows it exists."

Ila's needles paused.

"Child," she said gently, "the river forgets nothing. Neither do I. And neither—" she nodded downstream, toward the coast, toward the sea, "—do the men who chase other men's debts."

Wren laughed, because the alternative was to ask how the old woman could possibly know about the debt, and she wasn't ready to have that conversation. "You sound like a fortune cookie."

"I do not know this food," Ila said, "but I am sure it is wiser than you."

Downstream, far off, where the delta braided itself into the sea, a smudge of smoke stood against the horizon. Too far to read. Too steady to be a fishing boat's cookfire.

Tomas saw it too. He said nothing. But his hand found the gunwale of the boat and gripped it, and Wren, adjusting the strap of her pack—with the stone riding warm and humming against the small of her back—told herself the prickle on her neck was just weather coming in.

It usually was. That was the trouble. It usually was.
