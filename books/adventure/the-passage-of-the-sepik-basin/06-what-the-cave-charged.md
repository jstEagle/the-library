## 6. What the Cave Charged

Ila had kept a great many things in her long life: the names of the dead, the debts of the living, and one small carved stone that hummed like a wasp trapped in a gourd when you held it too close to your ear. She had learned, over eighty years of river, that most trouble came to a person wrapped in something attractive. Trouble wore fine feathers. Trouble arrived on good boats with cheerful voices. And sometimes—this was the rarest kind, the kind that cost the most—trouble arrived with your own eyes in its face.

The girl's eyes were her mother's eyes exactly. Grey-green, the color of the river in the hour before rain, when it could not decide whether to be kind.

Ila had known it at once, there in the doorway of her reed house, with the smoke from the cooking fire curling between them like a question nobody wanted to ask. She had gone still, and Tomas had noticed—Ila could tell by the way he'd shifted his weight, the way a man shifts when the water under his boat goes suddenly quiet—and the girl, this Wren, this Blackwood, had noticed nothing at all. That was the mother in her too. Sera had also been gifted at not noticing the things that would break her heart if she looked at them straight.

Now it was evening, two days after the tide had opened the cave and given up its crates and its bones and its humming stone, and the girl sat across the fire from Ila eating fish with the manners of a seagull, and Ila decided it was time.

Not all of it. Never all of it at once. The river did not give a man the whole current in one gulp; it gave him an eddy first, to see what he would do with stillness.

"You hold the stone wrong," Ila said.

Wren looked up, a fish bone caught in the corner of her mouth. "It's in my pack."

"It is in your pack," Ila agreed, "which is the wrongest place of all." She held out her hand, palm up, the way she had held it out to a hundred frightened people over the years, and waited. Waiting was a skill. Tomas had it. This girl did not, but she was young, and the young could learn anything except patience, which they had to be hit with repeatedly, like weather.

Wren fished in her pack—her movements quick, guilty, the movements of a woman who had hidden things from creditors before—and brought out the stone wrapped in a shirt. Even through the cloth, Ila felt it. A warmth. A faint insistence, like a word being repeated by someone who will not be ignored.

She unwrapped it herself. The carving caught the firelight: spirals within spirals, and at the center a face so old the features had nearly worn back into the stone, leaving only the suggestion of eyes and a mouth that might have been laughing or screaming—the ancients had understood those were often the same thing.

"Where did it come from?" Wren asked. "Really. Not the diver's story. Not the rumor. You know. I watched your face when Tomas told you what we found. You knew."

"I knew," Ila said.

"And?"

"And," said Ila, "you eat like your mother, and you interrupt like her, and you are both far too thin for the amount of talking you do." She turned the stone over in her palms. It hummed against her skin, faintly, patiently. It had been patient for forty years. It could manage one more evening, though it complained about it, the way old things complain.

The girl went very still. Stillness did not suit her; it sat on her like a coat borrowed from a bigger woman.

"Your mother," Ila said, "was called Sera."

* * *

The fire cracked. Somewhere out on the black water a night heron gave its one dry note, over and over, like a man knocking on a door he knows is answered but knocks anyway.

"That's not possible," Wren said.

"It is very possible. It happened."

"My mother's name was—" Wren stopped. Her jaw worked. "I don't know my mother's name. That's the joke. That's the whole joke of me. I don't know anything. So don't—" She stood up. Sat down again. Stood up again, as though the chair and she had quarreled. "Don't do this. Don't hand me a name like it's a gift and expect me to fall on my knees weeping. People lie to me for money. It's practically my national sport. Somebody told you I was coming, somebody paid you, somebody wants the stone and this is how they get it—"

"Who would pay an old woman in a reed house?" Ila asked mildly. "With what? Fish? My opinion of the government? I am rich in nothing but years, and no one has ever tried to steal them."

"I don't know! Something. There's always something."

"There is always something," Ila agreed. "That is true. But not always here, and not always this." She patted the ground beside her. "Sit, daughter of Sera. If I am lying, you may call me a liar to my face, which is more than most people get. If I am telling the truth—" she shrugged, an old woman's shrug, which contains multitudes— "then you will want to be sitting down anyway."

Wren sat. Perched, rather, on the very edge of the mat, coiled like something ready to bolt into the sago swamp, and Ila thought: yes, that is exactly how Sera sat, the whole last month, the whole last terrible beautiful month, waiting for the boat.

"Forty-one years ago," Ila began, "there was a woman who came down the river in a canoe with a broken outrigger and a fever and a baby inside her."

"A baby inside her? Pregnant?"

"Inside her arms. Inside her coat, because it was raining, and she had wrapped the child against the wet. She was white—not white like you are pale from ships, but white-white, foreign, sunburnt peeling—and she spoke English and some German and a little trade pidgin she had learned badly from a missionary. And she was running."

"From what?"

Ila looked at her for a long moment. "You are a clever woman. You have crossed an ocean to get away from men who lend money cheerfully. What do you think she was running from?"

Wren's mouth opened. Closed. Ila watched the arithmetic happen behind her eyes—the Voss brothers, their fathers before them, the cheerful voices, the blood-collected debts—and watched the girl arrive at the answer and then slam the door on it, hard, the way you slam a door on wind.

"No," Wren said. "No, that's coincidence. That's—people run from lenders everywhere, it's the most common sport in the world, it doesn't mean—"

"It does not mean," Ila agreed. "But listen, and then decide what it means. She stayed with us one season. She was a mapmaker—a drawer of maps, a measurer of coastlines. She had come up the Sepik with a survey party before the war, and when the war came she hid in the hills, and when the war ended she came back down, and by then she was carrying more than maps." Ila touched the stone lightly. "She carried this."

"The stone was hers?"

"The stone was given to her. By the cave." Ila said it simply, because it was simple, and simple things sound mad only to people who have never been loved by something older than themselves. "You saw the cave. You saw what the tide keeps. The cave gives gifts to those who enter honestly, and it takes payment from those who don't. Your mother entered honestly. She was afraid, and wet, and honest. The cave liked her. It gave her the stone to carry, and it asked one price."

Wren's voice came out flat. "Which was?"

"That she bring it back. One day. Herself, or her blood." Ila smiled without much humor. "Caves are not sentimental. They are patient, which is worse."

"So she took it. And then?"

"Then the men came." Ila fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Two brothers. Cheerful men. Laughing men. They had lent her family money in another country, in another life, and they had followed her across the world the way a debt follows, which is to say slowly, and then all at once. She heard they had landed at the coast. She had maybe three days."

Wren had stopped breathing somewhere in there. Ila let the silence stretch just long enough, then went on, gently, because the next part deserved gentleness even after forty years.

"She could not run fast enough with a child. She knew it. Anyone could see she knew it, the way you can see a storm deciding. So she came to me, in the night, in the rain, and she put the baby in my arms, and she said—" Ila's voice did something old women's voices do, thinned and steadied at once— "she said, 'Her name is Wren. Small bird, big sky. Tell her I didn't leave her. Tell her I left her *somewhere.*'"

The heron knocked on its door out in the dark. The fire settled.

"And then she took the canoe," Ila said, "and went downriver toward the coast, toward the men, drawing them away from the village. Away from you. And the river—" Ila spread her hands, empty, the gesture she had made ten thousand times at funerals and once, only once, at a christening— "the river keeps what it is given. We found the canoe at the delta, empty, upright, floating. Nothing else. Ever."

* * *

For a while Wren said nothing at all, which Ila recognized as the loudest thing a person can do.

Then the girl laughed. It was a horrible laugh, bright and brittle, a laugh with a crack running through it like ice on a puddle in spring.

"That's a lovely story," she said. "Genuinely. You should sell it to tourists. But here's my problem with it." She held up fingers, ticking them off, and her hands were shaking, which undermined the lawyerly effect somewhat. "One: I'm twenty-nine. Maybe thirty. Not forty-one. Two: I grew up in an orphanage outside Brisbane, with paperwork, with dates, with a matron named Mrs. Colquhoun who smelled like eucalyptus and disappointment. Three: my mother, whoever she was, dropped me at a church with a blanket and half a name, which is not the behavior of a woman who leaves babies with river aunties and detailed instructions. And four—" the fourth finger came up and stayed there, trembling, aimed at Ila like a small accusatory spear— "four: everyone in this basin has known since I arrived that I'm looking for treasure worth a fortune, and now suddenly, conveniently, the scary old spirit-keeper has a story that makes the stone sacred and unsellable and *hers*. Forgive me if I don't burst into tears."

Tomas, who had been sitting in the shadows mending a net with the fixed concentration of a man pretending not to listen, set down the net. "Auntie," he said carefully, "maybe—"

"Maybe nothing," said Ila. "Let her be angry. Anger is a good boat. It moves fast." She looked at Wren, unblinking, unhurried. "You want proof. Good. Distrust is healthy; I raised a nephew who distrusts his own reflection. So. Proof."

She reached into the folds of her skirt and drew out a small object she had carried against her skin for forty-one years, through floods and feuds and one regrettable incident involving a crocodile. She held it out on her open palm.

A locket. Tin, cheap, salt-eaten, the hinge long since rusted shut. On its face, worn almost smooth, the ghost of an engraving: a wren on a branch.

"Sera wore it around her neck," Ila said. "When she gave me the baby, she took it off and pressed it into the blanket. She said, 'So she'll know she was chosen, not lost.' I was going to give it to you when you were grown. Then the mission took you away, and I did not know where grown found you." She closed the girl's fingers around it. "Open it or don't. It is yours either way. That is what proof looks like when it has been waiting a long time—it isn't impressive anymore. It's just heavy."

Wren stared at the tin locket in her fist as though it were a live coal. She didn't open it. Ila watched her not open it with tremendous effort, thumb pressing the rusted seam, jaw tight, eyes bright and furious and terrified, and thought: *there she is. There is Sera's stubbornness, come home to roost.*

"I'm not crying," Wren announced, to the fire, to the river, to no one.

"No one said you were."

"Because I'm not. This proves nothing. A locket. Anyone can buy a locket. Mrs. Colquhoun could have—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, the crack in the ice widening. "If any of this were true, then my mother ran *toward* the men who killed her instead of staying with me. Which means she chose a stranger's baby over her own, which means—" and here the armor finally showed its seam, the voice dropping to almost nothing— "which means I wasn't worth staying for. Is that it? Is that the ending of your lovely story?"

Ila reached over and took the girl's chin in her hand, firm as her grandmother had done to her, and turned her face so their eyes met across the firelight—grey-green meeting grey-green, the river before rain looking at itself.

"Foolish child," Ila said, with enormous tenderness. "She stayed exactly as long as staying meant you lived. Then she spent herself buying you a head start. That is not abandonment. That is the oldest arithmetic there is, and women have been doing it since before caves learned to keep time." She released the chin. "Your mother was not running from you. She was running *as* you. Everything she did afterward, she did moving away from you, so the danger would move away too. You have spent your whole life thinking you were left behind." She picked up the stone, warm and humming, and set it in Wren's lap. "You were sent ahead."

Wren looked down at the stone. At the locket. At her own hands, which had stopped shaking and started gripping.

"I don't believe you," she said, but her voice had gone soft and ruined, and they both knew the words were just the last soldier defending a fort already taken. "I need to think. I need—" She stood, clutching stone and locket, and walked out of the house into the dark, barefoot, toward the water, because of course she did. Because that was what Sera's daughter did with unbearable things: she took them to the river.

Tomas moved to follow. Ila caught his sleeve.

"Leave her. The river talks to people better than I do." She sighed and eased her old bones back against the wall. "Besides, nephew, we have a larger problem than her heart."

Tomas paused. He was a good boy; he read water, and he read faces, and he had read his aunt's all evening. "What problem?"

"The stone woke up," Ila said. "Did you feel it? Two days ago it slept. Tonight it hums. Old things hum when their people come near them—or when their enemies do." She looked out past the reed walls, down the long black throat of the river, toward the delta, toward the sea, where the tide was turning. "Something is coming up this river, Tomas. I felt it in my knees this morning, and my knees have never lied to me, though they complain constantly. Whatever is coming—it is coming for her, or for the stone, and I no longer believe those are different things."

Out in the dark, faint under the heron's knocking, they could hear the girl at the waterline, talking to the river in a low urgent voice, telling it she didn't believe a word of it.

The river, being the river, said nothing, and kept everything.
