## 7. A Boat on the Horizon

Tomas Kerebu had a rule about the delta: the water told you everything, if you let it. The tide spoke in the reeds. The fish spoke in their silences. And strangers spoke loudest of all, usually by pretending not to speak at all.

So when he saw the launch come up the main channel that morning—no flag, no name painted on the bow, engine running too quiet for its size—he did what his uncle had taught him before he was tall enough to see over the gunwale. He stopped looking at it.

He looked instead at the water around it.

The launch sat wrong in the current. A boat that heavy, riding that high, meant men and gear and not much cargo. It held station against the ebb without anchoring, which meant someone aboard knew engines better than rivers, or didn't care about fuel. And it had come in on the morning tide but made no move to leave with it.

Boats that visit are fine. Boats that stay are questions.

He counted four figures on deck through the corner of his eye, which is the only way to count figures you don't want counted back. Three standing like men who stand for a living. One sitting, very still, under an awning, doing nothing at all. In Tomas's experience, the man doing nothing was always the one being paid the most.

"Interesting weather," said Wren Blackwood behind him.

She'd come down to the water with her hair still wet from the village pump and a mug of Ila's terrible ginger tea, which she drank anyway because she was either brave or foolish, and Tomas had not yet settled the question. She followed his gaze out to the channel.

"That's a lot of interest for such boring weather," she said.

"You know that boat?"

"Nope." She sipped her tea. Too casually. Tomas had watched crocodiles lie in the shallows with exactly that expression—everything relaxed except the part that mattered. "Should I?"

"It flies no flag."

"Lots of boats don't."

"In this delta," Tomas said, "every boat flies something. Even the mission dinghy flies a little cross of sticks. A boat with nothing on it is a boat that doesn't want to be asked where it's from."

Wren looked at the launch again, longer this time, and Tomas watched her do arithmetic behind her eyes. He was getting good at reading her arithmetic. It always ended in a number she didn't like.

"Well," she said brightly, "not my circus."

"The last time you said 'not my problem,' we spent two hours pulling your salvage gear out of a sago swamp."

"That was a *logistical* problem. This is a *nautical* one. Completely different department."

Tomas decided not to laugh, mostly on principle. She was funny when she was hiding things, which meant she was funny most of the time, which meant his days had gotten dangerously entertaining since she'd arrived, and he resented every minute of it.

"I'm going to paddle out," he said.

"Out there? Why?"

"To ask them their business."

"And if their business is none of yours?"

"Then they will tell me so rudely," Tomas said, "and I will know something useful. That is how information works here. You go and get slapped, and then you know who slaps."

* * *

He took the smallest canoe, the one that looked poorest, because poor boats got waved through and rich boats got boarded. He paddled out on the slack water with his fishing lines trailing, though both hooks were bare, because a man fishing is furniture and a man paddling purposefully is a question.

The launch let him come close. That itself was an answer. Confident boats let canoes approach.

The three standing men were big in the way of men who lift weights rather than loads—clean hands, new boots, sunburned in patches because they hadn't learned yet that the equatorial sun does not negotiate. The sitting man under the awning was different. Lean, gray at the temples, wearing a shirt too clean for the basin and a watch worth more than Tomas's canoe, his engine, and the shed it lived in combined.

"Morning," Tomas called in Tok Pisin, then English. "Good water today."

The sitting man smiled. It was a good smile, professionally maintained, like the watch. "It is good water," he agreed, in English with an accent Tomas couldn't place—somewhere flat and cold, a country of paperwork. "You fish here often?"

"When the fish agree."

"And do they? Agree?"

"They're like people," Tomas said. "They agree when there's something in it for them."

The smile deepened a millimeter. "I'm looking for someone. A woman. Foreigner, dark-haired, arrived recently. Travels with expensive equipment and worse manners." He said it pleasantly, the way you'd describe the weather. "Possibly alone. Possibly not."

Tomas kept his face doing what river faces do, which is nothing. "This delta has many foreigners this season. Scientists. Photographers. One man who claimed to be a count, though he could not count his own fingers after the betel nut."

"This one would be—" the man paused, and produced from his pocket a photograph, holding it up briefly, "—difficult."

The photo was Wren, taken some months ago, standing in front of a building with steel shutters. She was scowling at the camera with real commitment. Even angry, even flattened into paper, she had a way of taking up more space than she occupied, and Tomas felt something in his chest tighten and immediately filed the feeling somewhere he wouldn't have to look at it.

"Hm," he said. "Difficult how?"

"Difficult financially."

"Ah." Tomas nodded sagely. "Then half the world is looking for her. Including, probably, herself."

One of the standing men shifted, and the sitting man lifted two fingers—not quite a threat, just a reminder that threats were available. "You have a name, fisherman?"

"Tomas."

"Just Tomas?"

"Kerebu. But everyone just says Tomas, it saves time."

"Tomas Kerebu." The man said the whole name slowly, tasting it, the way you'd check a coin with your teeth. "My name is Marek. If the difficult woman comes through your water—and water, as you say, remembers everything—I would be grateful to hear of it. Gratitude, in my experience, is best expressed materially."

"Gratitude is best expressed," Tomas said, "by leaving a man's fish alone."

Marek laughed—a short, genuine-sounding bark, which was somehow worse than the smile. "I like you, Tomas Kerebu. That is unfortunate for everyone." He flicked his own fingers, and the launch's engine turned over, smooth and expensive. "We'll be in the delta a while. Enjoy the fishing."

Tomas paddled away at exactly the speed of a man with nothing to hurry about, which cost him more effort than fleeing would have.

* * *

He found Wren mending a net outside Ila's house, badly. She'd been at it twenty minutes and the net looked worse than when she'd started, which was either sabotage or talent of a kind.

"We need to talk," Tomas said.

"Those words have never once preceded anything fun."

"A boat came into the delta. No flag. Four men. Their leader is called Marek, and he carries your photograph, and he says you're difficult financially."

The netting needle stopped moving. Just for a second. Then it started again, faster.

"Financially difficult," Wren repeated. "That's one way to put it."

"What's another way?"

"Creatively indebted?" She looked up, and her grin was the one she used on customs officers and crocodiles. "Look, everybody owes somebody. Back home I owe my dentist, my landlord, and a man named Doug who claims I owe him for a jet ski. Marek sounds like a collections guy. Annoying, but boring. He'll poke around, find nothing, and go home."

Tomas sat down on the log across from her and waited. He'd learned that from the river: when the current lies to you, stop rowing and listen to what the eddies say underneath.

Wren lasted eleven seconds.

"Okay, fine, maybe it's slightly more than a jet ski situation."

"How much more?"

"It's—" She gestured with the needle at the entire concept of money. "It's structured. There are brothers involved. The Voss brothers. They're lenders. Very friendly lenders. They smile a lot, they remember your birthday, and their contracts have, uh, flexible enforcement clauses."

"Flexible meaning what?"

"Meaning their last client who defaulted," Wren said, examining the net with great fascination, "moved to a town where nobody knew him, and then moved again, and then stopped moving entirely, in the permanent sense."

The ginger tea in Tomas's stomach went cold. "And Marek works for these brothers."

"Marek is what happens when the smiling stops." She said it lightly, but her knuckles were white around the needle. "Look—it's fine. Really. As long as he doesn't find me, this whole thing is a boat-shaped nothing. I've dodged worse."

"You have not," Tomas said. "I've seen you lose a fight to a mooring line."

"That mooring line started it."

"Wren." He said her name plainly, without the joke attached, and saw it land. "A man is sitting in our delta with your face in his pocket. He knows you came upriver. He knows you didn't come to photograph birds. Whatever you're looking for in this basin—the cave, the crates, all of it—you're not looking for it for fun. You're looking for it to pay them."

She didn't answer. The silence stretched long enough that the river filled it—the slap of water against hulls, a kingfisher going off somewhere like a small furious alarm clock.

"Yes," she said finally. Quietly. All the bravado drained out of it, and what was left underneath was younger than her face. "Yes, that's the shape of it."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because telling you makes it real for you too." She set down the needle. "Because you've been decent to me, Tomas, genuinely decent, and the Voss brothers have a way of making decency expensive. Everyone who helps me ends up on a ledger I didn't write. I thought—if I kept it small enough, kept it mine, maybe it would only ever be my problem."

"And now?"

"Now there's a boat with no flag in your aunt's delta," she said, "so apparently I'm bad at keeping things small."

Tomas looked out at the channel, where the launch sat fat and patient against the current. He thought about Ila, who had gone strange and silent the day Wren arrived, who watched the girl like a woman watching a fire she remembered starting. He thought about the stone Wren had carried out of the tide-cave wrapped in cloth, humming with a weight that wasn't grams. He thought about his own rule—that the water tells you everything—and how the water had been trying to tell him since the day this woman stepped off the trader with too much gear and not enough truth.

"You lied to me," he said. Not cruelly. Just setting it down between them, where it could be seen.

"I edited," Wren said. "Aggressively."

"It's the same thing on a river. An edit is a lie with better manners."

She flinched like he'd touched a bruise. "Yeah," she said. "I know."

They sat with it. Somewhere behind the house, Ila was singing to the sago pits, an old song with no ending, the kind sung to keep time with work and grief at once.

"If Marek finds you here," Tomas said, "he doesn't just take you. He takes whatever you found. And he asks the village who helped you, and the village answers, because men like him make answering the safest thing to do. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means I should leave tonight."

"It means," Tomas said, and heard himself say it before he'd voted on it, "that you should decide what you actually trust me with. Because I can get you to Madang overland ahead of the monsoon—but not while I'm guessing. A guide who's guessing is just a lost man with confidence."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—calculation, then something past calculation, something that surprised her more than it surprised him.

"You'd take me," she said. "Even knowing the debt comes with me. Even knowing men with clean boots might follow us into the mountains."

"I'd take you," Tomas said, "because you're the worst liar I've ever met and the best company, and I've decided those cancel out."

That startled a laugh out of her—wet at the edges, but real. Then she looked past him, toward the channel, and the laugh died in her throat.

"Tomas."

He turned.

The launch had moved. It was no longer holding station in the main channel. It had come around the point and now sat directly opposite the village, close enough that he could see the awning furled, the four men on deck, and—one of them raising a hand in a slow, friendly wave—Marek himself, letting them all know he'd known exactly which house to sit in front of all along.

"He's not searching anymore," Wren said softly.

"No," Tomas said. The water had gone very still, the way it did before weather. "Now he's introducing himself."

Behind them, Ila's song stopped mid-verse.
