## 8. Blood in the Sago

The morning Marek's men came up the river, the village was arguing about a pig.

It was a good argument, the kind that had texture to it—old grievances, disputed boundaries, one elderly man insisting the animal had eaten his sago and another insisting the sago had been past eating anyway. Tomas stood in the middle of it translating for my benefit, which mostly meant he translated the insults and skipped the theology.

"That one," he said, nodding at a wiry elder with a voice like a outboard motor, "says the pig is possessed. That one says all pigs are possessed and this one is simply honest about it."

"And who's winning?"

"The pig," Tomas said. "She's eating the sago while they argue."

I laughed. I remember that. I remember it because it was the last easy sound I made for a long time, and because guilt has a way of filing things away like evidence against you.

I'd been in Ambunti village nine days. Nine days of learning to sleep under a roof of woven sago palm that breathed with the wind, of eating fish smoked over coconut husk until I dreamed in salt, of watching Ila move through her mornings with the unhurried authority of someone the river answered to. The stone sat wrapped in barkcloth in her spirit house, and I told myself every day that I would ask about it tomorrow. Tomorrow I would negotiate. Tomorrow I'd figure out what it was worth and how to get it downriver without anyone hating me.

Tomorrow was a word I'd been living on since Cairns.

The dogs started first. Not barking—going silent, all at once, the way birds go quiet before a storm. Then the argument about the pig stopped mid-sentence, and the whole village turned its face toward the river like a field of sunflowers, except nobody was looking at the sun.

A launch. Grey hull, no flag, no markings, riding low in the water the way boats do when they're carrying more men than furniture. It came around the bend below the village with the obscene confidence of something that knows exactly where it's going.

Tomas went very still beside me. Stillness on him was worse than shouting.

"Is that—"

"Yes." His jaw worked. "That is the boat from three days ago. It went past toward Pagwi. Now it comes back."

I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do since I'd seen that same unmarked launch slide through the delta mist. Ninety days on my debt. Six weeks gone. A salvage permit filed in Cairns under my name, a charter logged, a pearl diver paid in rum for a story he probably sold twice more before I'd even left the bar. I hadn't run from the Voss brothers. I'd left a trail of breadcrumbs and then complained about being followed.

"Wren." Tomas turned to face me fully, and there was no anger in him yet, only a terrible patience. "How much trouble are you in?"

"Manageable trouble."

"You are lying."

"I'm *optimizing* the truth."

He looked at me the way you look at weather coming over the range—not angry at the storm, just recalculating everything you'd planned around it. "Men who ride in unmarked boats do not come for manageable trouble."

Down at the landing, the launch bumped against the pilings and six men stepped ashore. Seven. Eight. They moved wrong for tourists and right for work—spread out without being told to, two drifting wide along the waterline, the rest walking up the slope toward the village in loose formation. In front walked a man I didn't know, tall, grey-templed, wearing a shirt too clean for the Sepik and an expression of mild professional regret, like a tax auditor arriving at a funeral.

I knew the type. I'd grown up adjacent to the type. The Voss brothers never came themselves; they sent men whose politeness was the most frightening thing about them.

"Tomas," I said quietly, "get Ila off the path. Get everyone—"

But it was already happening. The village had melted into the bush the way villages here could, women pulling children into the cassowary grass, old men vanishing behind houses as if the ground had swallowed them politely. Within a minute the clearing held only the strangers, the pigs—who, possessed or not, had excellent survival instincts—and me, standing in the open like an idiot, because some part of my brain still believed that if I faced things head-on they couldn't sneak up on me.

The grey-templed man saw me and smiled. It was a good smile. It was the smile of a man who'd been told exactly what his quarry looked like and found the world pleasingly orderly.

"Miss Blackwood." He pronounced it carefully, like a word in a language he respected but didn't speak. "Marek Voss sends his regards. And his patience, which is considerable, though not infinite."

"Marek Voss," I repeated. "So the brothers finally hired someone with table manners."

"I am called Durec." He spread his hands, taking in the village, the river, the smoke rising from cooking fires abandoned mid-meal. "You have made our work difficult, Miss Blackwood. Cairns. Port Moresby. Wewak. You travel like a woman with somewhere to be."

"I travel like a woman running from people who send eight men up a river to collect a debt she already offered to restructure."

"Ah." He nodded as if I'd confirmed a reservation. "You have something of ours. Or something that will become ours, once appraised. The old woman's stone. We understand you've seen it."

My stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"Of course." He sighed, genuinely, and gestured, and two of his men began walking the line of houses with a casualness that fooled no one, lifting barkcloth doorways, poking into cookhouses. One of them kicked over a stack of firewood and didn't stoop to fix it, and something in my chest went hot and ugly at the smallness of it. "Then we will look. And when we find it—and we will find it, Miss Blackwood, rivers tell us things, and rivers say it is here—we will discuss terms. Your debt is substantial. The brothers feel the stone covers it generously. Everyone walks away rich. Well." He smiled again. "Everyone walks away."

*Everyone walks away.* I heard what he wasn't saying underneath it, the way you hear the second drumbeat under the first. Tomas. Ila. Anyone who stood between the Voss family and their property. My debt had always been abstract before—a number, a threat, a weight I carried privately like a stone in a pack. Debts were supposed to be between you and your creditor. Nobody had ever told me a debt could grow hands.

One of the men reached the spirit house.

It stood apart from the dwellings, small and dark and hung with dried leaves and shell, and every villager within earshot must have felt the man's hand on it like a slap. I felt it myself, absurdly, a flare of protectiveness for a building I'd only ever looked at.

And then Ila came out of the cassowary grass like the river itself deciding it had opinions.

I don't know how to describe it except that she was seventy-some years old and four feet ten and the eight armed men stopped moving, all of them, even Durec, even the polite ones. She crossed the clearing at her own pace, staff in hand, barefoot, and planted herself in front of the spirit house door with the finality of a mountain range.

Durec recovered first. Of course he did. "Grandmother. We mean no disrespect. We want only what is inside."

"This is not inside anything," Ila said. Her English was slow and precise, each word set down like a stone in a crossing. "This is mine. This is the river's. You are a stranger, and the river does not give its bones to strangers."

"It isn't the river's we're after, grandmother. It's Japanese. War salvage. Abandoned property."

"Abandoned?" For the first time her voice cracked upward, and I heard forty years in it. "Nothing in that cave is abandoned. Everything in that cave was *left*. There is a difference, and men like you have never learned it."

Durec's patience thinned by a degree I could measure. "Move aside, please."

"No."

"Grandmother—"

"*No.*"

What happened next happened fast and stupidly, the way these things always do. One of the younger men—jumpy, sunburned, the kind of hired muscle who'd signed on for treasure and found himself lectured by grandmothers—stepped forward and put a hand on her shoulder to move her.

Ila drove the butt of her staff into the side of his knee.

He went down screaming a word I won't repeat, and the clearing detonated. Another man grabbed for her; she struck his wrist hard enough that I heard something click. But she was seventy-eight years old and there were eight of them, and the third one didn't reach for her—he swung, backhanded, panicked, the blow of a man hitting a person he'd decided in one bad second wasn't quite a person.

She went down against the spirit house post. The staff clattered on the boards. And the sound she made—not a scream, worse, a sharp indrawn breath of pure outrage, as if her body had betrayed her by bleeding—went through the village like a spear through a fish shoal.

The bush erupted. Men came out of the grass with machetes and fishing spears and the flat black fury of people defending their oldest living relative. Dogs materialized from nowhere with their teeth already committed. Even the possessed pig joined in, charging a mercenary who would tell that story badly for the rest of his life.

I didn't think. That's the truth of it, and I hold onto it, because thinking would have meant calculating, and calculating is what I'd done my whole life. I simply moved—across the clearing, into the knot of fighting bodies, and I got my shoulder into the chest of the man who'd hit her and drove him backward off the platform into the mud, and then Tomas was there, and the village was there, and numbers and fury did what numbers and fury do.

The mercenaries broke. They were professionals, and professionals know the difference between resistance and a village. Durec whistled his men back toward the landing with the same mild regret on his face, pausing only to look at me across the chaos.

"Terms remain available, Miss Blackwood," he said. "They will not remain generous."

Then they were in the launch and gone, engine noise flattening out downriver, and the village stood breathing hard among overturned firewood and one unconscious mercenary's dropped cap, which a boy immediately claimed as a war trophy.

* * *

Ila's cheekbone was split and her wrist was broken—one of the mission-trained men splinted it with bamboo while she sat perfectly still and watched him work with the expression of a queen permitting a servant to adjust her crown.

I knelt in front of her. My hands were shaking. I noticed them shaking and hated them for it.

"Ila. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, this is because of me, they came because of—"

"They came because of you." She said it without heat, the way you'd confirm the weather. "Yes."

"I'll fix it. I'll go talk to them, I'll give them whatever they—"

"You will sit down," Ila said, "before you fall down, because you look like a wet bird, and then you will listen."

I sat. Around us the village was repairing itself with the practiced efficiency of people who had survived worse than eight men in an unmarked boat—righting firewood, calming dogs, feeding the pig, who had earned it.

"My mother," I said, and the word came out strange, half question. "In your spirit house. Whatever's in there. Durec said war salvage. Japanese caches. Is that true? Is that all it is?"

Ila looked at me for a long moment. Her eyes were the color of deep water over sand, and I had thought, since the first day, that there was something familiar in them, the way a melody can be familiar from a song you've never heard.

"You asked me once why I looked at you strangely," she said. "On your first morning. You thought I was deciding whether to trust you."

"You weren't?"

"I was remembering." She shifted her splinted arm carefully into her lap. "Forty-two years ago a woman came up this river alone in a leaking canoe, and she had your eyes, and she was afraid of men in clean shirts. She stayed one season. She drew maps—beautiful ones, maps of water nobody had charted—and she slept in that spirit house you are so eager to sell, and when she left, she left something behind that was not a map."

The river made its long indifferent sound against the bank.

"She left a baby," Ila said. "She left *you*, Wren. And she made me promise that what sleeps in the cave would stay sleeping, because the men her family kept company with—the fathers of the men who sent Durec—had already taken everything else she had, and she would not let them take the last thing too."

The world tilted gently, the way a boat tilts when you stand up wrong. Somewhere behind me Tomas had gone very quiet, in the way that meant he'd known, or guessed, or was doing his own arithmetic.

"So the stone," I managed. "The carved stone. It's—"

"It is hers. It is the river's. It is the reason your mother's family hunted her from one end of the map she drew to the other." Ila leaned forward, and despite the blood on her face and the splint on her arm, there was nothing frail in her at all. "You wanted to know what your debt costs. Now you know. It cost Anwei a cut cheek today, and it cost me a wrist, and it will cost more, because men like that do not leave empty-handed twice. So you must choose, daughter-of-my-daughter's-promise. Choose quickly, and choose better than your mother was allowed to."

I sat there with the word *daughter* ringing in my skull like a struck bell, and out on the river, far downstream, I could still hear the faint pulse of an engine, going away, going to report, going to come back with more men and less patience.

Tomas crouched beside me. He didn't touch me. He just said, very quietly:

"The monsoon is eleven days early. If we are going to cross the Finisterre ahead of it, we leave tomorrow."

I looked at the spirit house. At the barkcloth door. At everything I'd come up this river to steal, sitting thirty feet away, humming with a history I'd just learned was mine.

Eleven days. And I still didn't know what I was willing to carry out of this valley—or what it would cost to leave it behind.
