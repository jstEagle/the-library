## 9. The Choice of Rivers

Tomas Kerebu had a rule about arguments: never have one standing up, because standing up people talk with their chests instead of their heads. So when he came looking for Wren Blackwood at first light, he found her already at the riverbank testing the lashings on the boat, and he made himself sit down on the old landing log before he said anything at all.

She glanced at him sideways. "You're up early."

"You're up earlier." He patted the log beside him. "Sit. I want to talk to you like a person, not like a man shouting at a woman who is halfway into a canoe."

"That's very specific."

"I am a specific man. Ask anyone. They will say, Tomas? Very specific. Annoyingly so."

The corner of her mouth moved — not quite a smile, but the river had taught him that Wren's smiles were like king tides: rare, and you had to be watching at exactly the right moment or you missed them entirely. She sat. Not close. But she sat.

Below them the Sepik ran brown and patient, carrying yesterday's rain toward a sea that would only send it back again. Somewhere across the water a hornbill laughed its ridiculous laugh, the sound of a bird that had heard a good joke four million years ago and never gotten over it.

"Marek's launch is still anchored past the bend," Tomas said.

"I know."

"Two days now. He is not fishing."

"He's not built for fishing. He's built like a filing cabinet with opinions."

Tomas let that sit, because it was true and because laughing would have loosened something in his chest he needed to keep tight. "My aunt's shoulder is worse. The old women are packing it with bark and prayer, which works on everything except shoulders. And Marek's men walked through the village again last evening, slow, the way men walk when they want you to count how many of them there are."

"Six," Wren said. "Seven if you count the fat one who pretends to be a cook."

"The fat one *is* a cook. That is what frightens me. A man who can fight and cook is a man who has decided to stay somewhere a long time."

Wren looked out at the water. Her hands were still in her lap, but her thumbs were moving against each other, working at nothing, the way they did when her mind was running numbers she didn't like. He had learned her tells the way his father had learned the river: slowly, by drowning once or twice.

"So," she said. "Talk."

* * *

He had rehearsed this on the walk down from Ila's house, and like all rehearsed speeches it died the moment it touched air. What came out instead was simpler and worse.

"Give her back the stone."

Wren didn't flinch. He'd expected flinching. She just went very still, the way the river goes still before it decides whether to rise.

"No," she said.

"You haven't asked why."

"I don't need to ask why. I know why. It's the same reason everyone keeps telling me to hand over the only thing keeping me alive. Because it's the right thing to do. Because it belongs here. Because your aunt says so, and the village says so, and the river says so, and everybody gets a vote except the person whose neck is actually on the block." She turned to face him fully then, and her eyes had that flat grey light he'd seen the day they met, storm-light, no bottom to it. "That stone is the only currency Marek accepts. I give it up, and what do I pay him with? Gratitude?"

"You could pay him with distance," Tomas said. "There is another way to Madang. Not the river — the river is watched, and after the monsoon turns it will be suicide anyway. Overland. Through the foothills, up and over where the Finisterre starts to climb. My cousin's people trade that track. It takes nine days if the weather holds, twelve if it doesn't, and it drops you on the coast two hundred kilometers from anyone who knows Marek's name."

"And the monsoon?"

"Is late this year. Late, not absent. We have maybe two weeks of dry walking. After that—" he shrugged, palms open, giving the sky its due— "after that the mountains make their own opinion."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "And the condition. The price of your cousin's track and your legs and your weather-sense. Say it plainly, Tomas. You've been circling it since you sat down, and you circle like a hawk, very dignified, but I'm tired."

He made himself say it. "Leave the stone with Ila. Here. Under the ground where it has been since before either of us was born. Then we go. Light, fast, nothing to steal, nothing to kill for."

"There it is." She stood up. Of course she stood up; the argument had gotten vertical without his permission. "You're asking me to walk into those mountains with nothing. No money, no leverage, no proof I ever found anything at all. Just my word and your good intentions."

"Yes," Tomas said. "That is what I am asking."

"Do you know what my word is worth to men like the Voss brothers? Nothing. Less than nothing — it's an insult, offering it. They'll take the gear off my back and the boots off my feet and then they'll take the rest 'their way,' and their way involves hooks, Tomas. I've seen it. In Cairns, a man named Ferro owed them eleven thousand. Eleven thousand! Chicken feed. They strung him off a trawler crane by the ankles until he signed over his boat, his house, and his mother's ring, and then they dropped him in the harbor anyway, as a lesson. Interest, they called it." Her voice hadn't risen. That was the terrible part. It had gone quieter and quieter, until it was almost conversational, almost calm, and every word landed like a stone dropped down a well. "I am not doing that. I am not crossing a mountain range to arrive empty-handed at the exact same ocean they're waiting at."

"They are not waiting at Madang."

"Not yet."

"Not ever, if we move well."

"You don't know that. Nobody knows that. The only thing I *know*—" and here her voice finally cracked, just once, a hairline fracture across all that grey— "is that the stone weighs less than a kilo and it is worth more than my life, and I would be insane to set it down."

Tomas stayed on the log. Rule number one. He looked at his hands, brown and scarred and patient, hands that had pulled three drowned men out of this river and buried two others, and he thought about what his aunt had said to him two nights ago, sitting up despite her shoulder, while the lamp burned low.

*The girl is mine,* Ila had said. *I gave her away once with my whole heart screaming. I will not beg twice. But tell her this, nephew: a thing taken from the cave is a debt too. Everyone in this basin knows it. If she carries it out, she carries the debt, and debts follow women in my family like crocodiles follow blood.*

He hadn't understood it then. Sitting on the log, watching Wren stand above him shaking with fury and fear in equal measure, he began to.

"Wren," he said. "Sit down. Please. One more minute, and then if you still want to shout at me, shout."

"I'm not shouting."

"I know. That's what worries me."

To his surprise, she sat. Not on the log — on the shingle below it, arms around her knees, suddenly looking much younger than thirty-odd and much more tired than any storm chaser should be. He slid down beside her. The stones were cold under them. The river talked its endless brown talk.

"My aunt told me something," he said. "About the stone. About what happens to whoever carries it out of the basin."

"Spiritual consequences. Curses. I've heard the speech."

"No. Not curses. Debts." He watched her profile. "She says the cave does not give. It lends. And whoever walks out holding its property owes it, the way you owe the Voss brothers, the way Ferro owed them. And she says—" he hesitated, because saying it aloud made it heavier— "she says debts run in her family. Like a tide. Like something in the blood."

Wren went still again. This time the stillness lasted long enough that a wagtail landed on a stone three meters away, considered them both, and left, unimpressed.

"She thinks I'm her daughter," Wren said finally. Flatly. "She told me. Four nights ago. I told her she was wrong."

"What did you tell her?"

"That my mother was a secretary from Brisbane who died when I was six. That I have a birth certificate. That I've seen it." A pause. "That I don't look like anyone from here."

"You have her eyes," Tomas said. "Exactly her eyes. The whole village saw it before she said a word. Old Matiu crossed himself and then pretended he hadn't."

"People have similar eyes."

"Yes. And rivers have similar bends, and yet a good pilot never mistakes one for another." He picked up a flat stone and skipped it — badly; it sank on the second touch, and Wren, despite everything, huffed something that was almost a laugh. "I am not asking you to believe her. Believe whatever you like about who bore you. I am asking you to believe something smaller. I am asking you to believe that Ila took a machete blow on her shoulder rather than let Marek's men into that storeroom. Seventy years old. She put her body between them and a rock. Why would she do that for a curse?"

"Because it's hers."

"Because it's *yours*," Tomas said. "Or because you are hers. Either way — she was not protecting a stone, Wren. She was protecting an inheritance. And inheritances are the one kind of wealth that doesn't come with hooks attached."

* * *

They sat there while the sun climbed and the mist burned off the far bank and the village behind them woke up in stages: smoke from the cooking fires, a dog complaining about theology, children already shrieking at the water. Normal sounds. Sounds from a world where nobody owed anybody eleven thousand dollars.

"I keep doing the arithmetic," Wren said at last, quieter now. "It's a sickness. Storm chasers have it. You spend your whole life calculating odds against weather that wants to kill you, and after enough years you can't stop. Everything becomes odds. The stone is my odds. Without it I'm a woman with wet boots and a pretty guide, running from professionals." She rested her chin on her knees. "With it, I'm at least a woman worth negotiating with."

"You are worth negotiating with anyway."

"To Marek?"

Tomas opened his mouth. Closed it. Honesty was a habit he couldn't break even when it hurt, and she saw it land, and something in her face closed like a door.

"Right," she said.

"But," he said, stubbornly, because the sentence deserved finishing, "Marek is not the only person who negotiates. My aunt says the coastal brokers at Madang will not touch that stone once word travels — and word always travels faster than launches. Customary claim. Taboo. You could not sell it there if you wanted to. So what is it worth to you, truly? A kilo of rock that every broker between here and Rabaul will refuse? Leverage against a man who has already decided not to negotiate?" He shook his head. "It stopped being money the moment Marek stepped onto that launch. Now it is only weight. Weight you carry uphill. Into snow."

"It's not snow at the passes this time of year."

"It is this year. The old men read the birds. The birds are early. The birds are never wrong about cold, only about fish."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her. "Your aunt reads spirits, your cousins read tracks, the old men read birds. Does anyone in your family just *not know things*?"

"My uncle Bogo. He knows nothing beautifully. It is a full-time occupation."

This time she did laugh — short, startled out of her, gone immediately, but real. And in the space that laughter opened, Tomas said the thing he had not planned to say, the thing that had been sitting in his chest since the night Marek's men tore through the sago stands and he'd carried Ila bleeding to her house with Wren's hand pressed over the wound, both of them shaking, neither speaking.

"I will take you to Madang," he said. "Over the mountain, ahead of the monsoon, past Marek, past the Voss brothers, past all of it. I will carry your pack and bleed for the track if the track asks. I would do it if you carried ten stones. I would do it if you carried nothing at all." He kept his eyes on the river, because some things are easier said to water. "But I am asking you — not as payment, not as a condition, I am asking you as the man who is going to be walking beside you for twelve days — leave the stone. Not for Ila. Not for the cave. For yourself. Because everything you told me about Ferro, everything about the Vosses, every hook and crane and harbor — that is what debt looks like when it wins. And that stone is a debt wearing a beautiful coat. I have watched you hold it at night. You sleep worse than you did before you found it."

She didn't answer for a long time. The river filled the silence, as rivers do, generously, taking the burden of conversation onto itself.

When she finally spoke, her voice was steady and it was the steadiness that frightened him.

"If I put it down," she said, "and I cross that mountain, and I get to Madang with nothing — and Marek finds me anyway, and the Voss brothers find me after him — then I gave up the only thing that could have bought me back, for a feeling. For trust. And trust, Tomas, is the one currency I have never once seen honored." She stood, brushing shingle dust from her knees, and looked down at him, and her eyes were dry and bright and terribly clear. "Ask me again tomorrow. Maybe tomorrow I'll be brave. Today I'm just broke."

She walked back up the bank toward the village, straight-backed, and did not look around, which was how he knew how much it cost her.

Tomas stayed on the cold stones a while longer. Then he noticed what he should have noticed twenty minutes ago: her pack, still lashed to the landing post where she'd left it at dawn.

And resting on top of the pack, wrapped in oilcloth, unmistakable by its shape alone —

the stone.

She had brought it down to show him. Or to give him. Or to argue with. With Wren Blackwood, he suspected, those were all the same verb.

Downriver, faint on the morning air, came the sound of Marek's launch starting its engine.

Tomas picked up the pack, and followed her.
