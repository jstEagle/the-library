## 10. Leaving the Water

The boat went into the river at dawn, and I watched it go like a woman watching a coffin sink.

Tomas had chosen the spot with the same care he chose everything—a bend where the current ran brown and fast, where the current would carry the long canoe under the hanging roots of a fig tree and hold it there, half-sunk, invisible to anyone passing on the main channel. He'd loaded it with stones to sit it low in the water. He'd even left my spare shirt drying on the thwarts, a little flag of surrender to anyone who looked too hard. *They'll think we went downriver,* he'd said. *Toward the coast. Toward Madang by water, like fools in a hurry.*

"And going up into the hills instead isn't foolish?"

"It is foolish," he said, shouldering his pack. "But it's foolish in the right direction."

I had lived my whole life by the principle that the right direction was whichever one the storm wasn't coming from. Tomas Kerebu operated on the opposite theory: you walked toward the weather, because the weather would be there anyway, and better to meet it on your own feet than in someone else's hands. Marek's hands, specifically. Marek, who had burned a village's sago stores and put a rifle butt across an old woman's back, and who was somewhere behind us on the river even now, moving fast, paying men to be greedy on his behalf.

So we left the water. Ila's country had always been water—the great grey Sepik unspooling through its floodplains, the reed houses lifted on stilts above the mud, the whole world breathing with the tide. But behind Ila's village the land began to gather itself, foothills rising in green shoulders toward the Finisterre Range, and it was into those hills that Tomas led me, up a pig track that was less a path than a rumor of a path, while the river we'd abandoned shone silver and treacherous below.

"You're limping," he said, without turning around.

"I'm adjusting my gait."

"You're limping."

The truth was that my left boot had spent three days in a flooded cave and had opinions about it now, and the blister on my heel had graduated from complaint to ultimatum. I said none of this. I had signed on to this expedition as a salvage diver with a forged résumé and a straight face; I could certainly limp with dignity.

We climbed through gardens first—sweet potato mounds in tidy ridges, banana trees leaning over fences of split bamboo, a scatter of huts with smoke rising straight up in the windless morning. A woman planting taro looked up at us, looked at Tomas, and said something in a language I didn't know, and he answered in the same one, and whatever he said made her laugh. I caught the word *ambit* in it, and later I asked him.

"I told her you were my trouble," he said. "She said all men's troubles look the same from behind."

"Charming woman."

"She's my cousin. She's right, too."

By midday the villages were behind us and the forest closed in, and I learned what rainforest means when it isn't a postcard. It means a green dark that starts at your eyes and works inward. It means air you can chew. It means every sound arriving at once—the whir of cicadas like a power line about to fail, the sudden detonation of a hornbill's wings, water dripping somewhere with the patience of a clock—and no sound at all that tells you which way anything is. It means mud that takes your boot with the affection of a lover and gives it back grudgingly, an inch at a time.

Tomas moved through it like a man walking through his own house at night. He never hesitated, never consulted the sun, never did any of the things I did—stopping, squinting, pretending to check my pack straps while I got my bearings. He read the land the way he read the river: by texture, by slope, by the species of tree and the pitch of birdcall. Twice he stopped, crouched, and touched the ground, and both times he said, "Pig," and moved on.

"Is that good or bad?" I asked the first time.

"It's a pig."

"I mean for us."

"Wren." He stood and looked back at me, and there it was again, that look he had—the one that was half exasperation and half something warmer that he kept putting away like a knife he didn't want to need. "Everything out here is good or bad depending on what you do next. The pig is just a pig."

I thought about that for a long time after. It was, I realized much later, the first genuinely useful thing anyone had said to me in years.

* * *

The leeches found us in the afternoon.

I felt the first one as a faint coolness on my ankle and thought, absurdly, *how nice, a breeze,* and then I looked down and screamed—a short, controlled, professional scream, the scream of a woman who has stared down a category-four cyclone and is not going to be undone by a worm. Tomas was back beside me in four strides.

"Don't pull it," he said.

"It's *in my skin.*"

"If you pull it, the head stays. Then it infects, and in three days you can't walk, and in five days Marek finds us because you're crying by a trail like a wounded animal." He knelt, produced a small flat tin from his pocket, and shook out a pinch of salt. "Hold still. This is the worst part, and it's not even bad."

The leech came off with a pop I felt in my teeth. He pressed a thumb hard on the bite until the bleeding slowed, smeared it with something from a twist of leaves—bitter, green, medicinal—and bound it with a strip of cloth torn, I noticed, from the hem of his own shirt.

"How many times have you done this?" I asked.

"For others, many. For myself—" he shrugged. "You stop counting your own blood. It's like counting rain."

We walked on, and I checked my ankles with the vigilance of a customs officer, and found two more leeches anyway, one behind the knee and one—this I declined to discuss with anyone, ever—somewhere I still can't write down. Tomas salted them without comment, which I decided was the single most respectful thing a man had ever done for me.

By the time the light went bronze and thick under the canopy, my heel had stopped negotiating and started issuing demands, and a headache had moved in behind my eyes like a tenant who intended to renovate. I was sweating in a way that had stopped being about the heat. I knew the signs. I'd had dengue in Darwin; I knew the difference between *tired* and *sick*, the way sick announces itself first as a wrongness in the light, a wrongness in your own hands.

"Tomas."

"I know," he said. "Your walk changed an hour ago. There's a place ahead."

"There's always a place ahead with you."

"Yes," he said, and for the first time all day he smiled. "That is what competence is."

* * *

The place was a hunter's shelter on a ridge spur: a low frame of saplings roofed with sago-leaf thatch, black with age and smoke, set against a fallen trunk so that the wind couldn't get under it. Tomas built a fire with an efficiency that bordered on insult—two minutes, one match, no drama—and put water on to boil, and only then turned and looked at me properly, and I watched him arrive at the diagnosis I'd been hiding.

"You're feverish."

"I'm warm. There's a fire."

"Wren."

"It's nothing. It's the climb. I'm acclimatizing."

"You're shivering next to a fire." He put the back of his hand against my forehead, briefly, the way you'd test a kettle, and whatever he found there made his mouth go flat. "Sit down. Take off the boots."

"I'm fine."

"You are not fine, and lying to me costs you energy you don't have. Sit. Boots. Now." And then, because he was Tomas, he said it more gently: "Please."

I sat. I took off the boots. The heel was worse than I'd let myself believe—angry, weeping, the skin around it hot and tight—and when I saw it I felt the fever and the fear meet somewhere in my chest and shake hands. Because here was the arithmetic, the ugly arithmetic I'd been refusing to do all day: a woman who can't walk in the hills is a woman who waits. And waiting, in my experience, was just another word for being found.

Tomas cleaned the blister without flinching and without letting me flinch either—he held my ankle in one steady hand and talked the whole time, low, unhurried, about nothing: the cousin who'd laughed at us, the taste of the river in dry season, the way his grandfather claimed you could hear the Finisterre snows coming a week off, a sound like faraway cloth tearing. He lanced it, packed it, wrapped it, and set my boots by the fire to dry, stuffing them with crumpled leaves to hold the shape.

"You should sleep," he said.

"You should sleep. I'll take first watch."

"On that ankle and that fever, you'll take first sleep, and I'll take the night. Argue with me and I'll salt you."

Despite everything—the fear, the fever, the leech I still wasn't discussing—I laughed. It came out cracked and surprised, a laugh I didn't recognize as mine, and Tomas looked at me as though he'd heard it too and was filing it away somewhere.

I lay down on the bed of palm fronds he'd cut, and the forest came in around the firelight like a congregation, and I lay there listening to the enormous dark breathing, and I thought about the day. I thought about how I had crossed an ocean and a sea and half a country on the strength of being fast, being sharp, being the woman who could read a storm two days out and get the hell out of its way first. And how in one day of walking uphill, the forest had stripped all of it off me. It had taken my boots' loyalty, my ankles' dignity, my temperature's cooperation, and my entire strategy of never needing anyone. It had left me lying on cut palm fronds with my feet wrapped in another man's shirt, waiting for him to decide when we'd walk tomorrow.

And the terrible thing—the thing I turned over and over like a stone in the dark—was that I didn't feel diminished. I felt *held*. Somewhere between the salt and the fire and the low sound of his voice, something in me that had been clenched since Cairns, since before Cairns, since the Voss brothers' smiling office with its cheerful contract and its cheerful blood-clause, had let go by a single degree. Not trust. Not yet. But the beginning of the shape trust makes.

"You're awake," Tomas said, from the other side of the fire.

"How can you tell?"

"You breathe differently when you're thinking. Faster. Like you're arguing."

"I'm not arguing."

"You're always arguing. You argue with the river, you argue with the trail, you argue with your own feet." He poked the fire, and sparks went up and died among the leaves. "The forest doesn't argue back, Wren. That's its trick. It just waits for you to stop fighting and start listening. Everything you need to know, it tells you. Where the water is. Where the pig went. Where the men behind us went, too, if you listen."

The cold walked up my spine, fever or no fever. "How far behind us?"

"Half a day. Maybe less. They have a man who knows this spur—he's from upriver, and he's not walking for love." He said it plainly, without drama, the way he said everything, and somehow that was worse. "We move at first light. You'll walk slower, so we start earlier. I'll carry more of your weight. And Wren—" the firelight moved on his face, "—when I say run, you run. Even if you don't know why. Especially then."

"Understood," I said, and for once in my life I meant it without adding a single clever thing.

I slept, or something near it, and dreamed in green. And near dawn, in the grey hour when the birds start up like the whole forest clearing its throat, Tomas's hand came down on my shoulder—gentle, but not gentle enough to be anything but urgent.

"Wren. Listen."

I listened. Under the birdsong, far down the slope behind us, thin as a wire: the sound of men moving through wet brush. More than two. Coming on.

"Run," he said.

I ran.
