## 11. Ila's Truth

The night before the ascent, Ila sat by the fire in her reed house and let the smoke do its slow work on her old bones. Outside, the rain had settled into its patient monsoon rhythm—not the violent kind that tore roofs off, but the steady kind that made people honest. Rain like this loosened tongues. It loosened memories too, and hers were heavy things she had carried forty years up and down this river.

Wren Blackwood was asleep in Tomas's boat, curled around the carved stone like a dog around a bone it had stolen from a bigger dog. Ila could see her through the doorway, a pale smudge in the dark, one hand resting on the stone even in sleep. Even unconscious, the girl guarded it. That told Ila more than any conversation could.

She had known since the first moment—the eyes. Green as river glass after a flood, the exact green of a woman who had once stood in this same doorway dripping wet and refusing to apologize for it.

Ila fed the fire a piece of sago bark and began, as her own grandmother had taught her, to tell the story to the fire first. The fire would hold it if no one else did.

* * *

Her name had been Marguerite Voss, though she arrived at the basin under the name Marguerite Hale, and for three days she claimed to be neither—just a mapmaker passing through, which was true in the way that a knife is technically a spoon if you're determined enough.

It was 1938, or close enough; the mission calendars disagreed and Ila had never seen reason to care. The woman came upriver in a government launch with two porters who turned back at Angoram, terrified of something they wouldn't name. She stayed. She drew maps. She drew maps of places the government men said didn't need mapping, and she asked questions about the coast caves with the careful boredom of someone who has practiced looking uninterested.

"I thought at first she was running toward something," Ila told the fire. "A treasure. A survey. Some white man's errand." The fire crackled, noncommittal. "It took me a month to understand she was running away."

From what, the woman would not say at first. But she flinched at certain names. Once, down at the trading post, a letter arrived bearing a crest—a bird of prey, wings spread—and Ila watched Marguerite Hale go the color of the inside of a shell and walk into the river up to her waist, just stand there in the current, letting the water hold her up because nothing else would.

The name on the envelope was Voss.

"They are not new," Ila said to the fire, and to the sleeping girl outside, and to whoever else was listening—perhaps the stone itself, which hummed faintly when it was spoken near, like a cat deciding whether to purr. "People think the Voss brothers are young trouble. Cheerful men with soft voices and hard hands. But their fathers lent money along this coast before the war, and their fathers' fathers before that. They lend to dreamers. Dreamers always sign. And when the debt comes due, they collect whatever bleeds."

Marguerite had borrowed. That was the shape of it, assembled over months of small confessions, each one offered the way you offer food to a stranger—cautiously, watching to see if it gets spat out. She had been a cartographer with more ambition than money, and the elder Voisses had financed an expedition, and the expedition had found something, and the Voisses had decided the something belonged to them, and Marguerite had decided otherwise.

"She stole from them," Ila said, and felt the old anger rise warm in her chest, familiar as arthritis. "One thing only. A stone. This stone." She looked at the bundle in the sleeping girl's arms. "And everyone in her family called it theft. But you cannot steal what was taken from the river. The river does not recognize their papers."

* * *

The stone had come out of the sea cave—the same cave, Ila was sure of it now, the one Wren had swum into at king tide with salt water burning her throat and greed shining out of her face like a lamp.

Long before the Japanese hid their crates of rifles and rice and sake bottles in that dark throat of rock, the basin people had used it. Not often. Only at the great tides, and only for one purpose: to give things back. When someone died far from home, when a canoe was lost, when a child was taken by the sea—something of theirs went into the cave at the next king tide, so the water would know the debt was acknowledged and stop reaching for others.

The stone was older than that custom. No one knew how much older. It had been in the cave when Ila's grandmother's grandmother was a girl, and the story then was already that it had always been there. It was carved all over with spirals, and if you held it against your chest in the dark, it hummed—not sound exactly, but a feeling in the ribs, like standing too close to thunder.

"It is not worth anything," Ila told the fire. "That is the whole secret, and no one believes it. It is worth everything, which is different. You can sell worth. You cannot sell everything. Everything is not for sale. Everything is what holds the floor up."

Marguerite had understood this. That was why she'd taken it—not to sell it, though God knew she needed money, but because the elder Voisses wanted it and would have sold it, shipped it to some collector in Sydney or London who would put it behind glass where it would go silent. A humming stone behind glass. Ila still found the idea obscene, like caging weather.

So Marguerite ran. Down the coast, across the water, hiding the stone and the maps and herself. And the Voisses ran after her, patiently, the way debt runs after everyone. Patient men. Cheerful men.

* * *

Here the story bent, the way stories do, toward the part Ila had never told anyone whole.

Marguerite stayed in the basin nearly two years. She learned the language badly and the river well. She paid for her keep by drawing charts—real ones, good ones, the kind that saved lives—and by arguing with Ila about everything. Where to plant. Whether the new mission teacher was a blessing or a spy. Whether crocodiles held grudges. ("They do," Ila had insisted. "You're thinking of elephants," Marguerite said. They had never resolved it. Ila maintained, to this day, that she had won.)

"And then there was a man," Ila said, softer. The fire leaned in. "There is always a man in these stories, and he is never the point, and he is always the reason the story bends."

He was a surveyor, half-Chinese, half-Tolai, come up from Rabaul with instruments and good manners. His name was Daniel. He laughed easily. He taught Marguerite to fish with a net instead of a line, and she taught him to read clouds, and between lessons they managed to fall in love in the ordinary, catastrophic way.

Daniel died in the second year. Fever—not the dramatic kind, just the ordinary kind that takes the strong ones because they refuse to lie down until they've fallen down. He lasted nine days. Marguerite sat with him every hour and drew his face over and over in the margins of her charts, and after he was gone she drew nothing at all for a month, and Ila had believed the silence was grief.

It was not only grief. She was pregnant.

"She told me in the dry season, standing right where that girl is sleeping now." Ila pointed her chin at the doorway. "She was terrified. Not of the baby. Of the name. She said—" and here Ila's voice did the thing it did, cracked down the middle like a dried gourd, forty years later, every time— "she said, 'If they ever learn she exists, they'll take her. Debt doesn't die. It waits for heirs.'"

So the plan was made, and it was a terrible plan, and it was the only plan. Marguerite would draw the Voisses away—leave openly, visibly, alone, letting them believe the stone was with her. The child would stay. Born here, named here, given to the river people, given no paper, no record, nothing to inherit but water.

"She chose the name herself. She said, 'Call her something that means wind, or rain, or leaving. Something that can't be pinned to a map.'" Ila smiled at the fire, and the smile hurt. "I named her Wren. Small brown bird, loud voice. Marguerite laughed until she cried and said it wasn't what she meant, and then she said it was better than what she meant."

* * *

The birth nearly killed them both. Three days of it, in this house, with the midwives and the storm outside competing to see which could make more noise. And when it was done, and the baby was screaming with a vigor that promised decades of argument, Marguerite held her for one hour.

One hour. Ila had counted. She had counted every time for forty years.

Then Marguerite wrapped the baby, kissed her forehead, handed her back, and walked down to the water where the launch was waiting, and she did not look back, because she had asked Ila beforehand: *Don't let me look back. If I look back I'll stay, and if I stay they'll find us both.*

"She was brave," Ila told the fire. "Everyone says it like a comfort. She was brave, yes. Bravery is just fear that has learned to row."

What happened after, Ila knew in pieces. Marguerite led the Voisses' men on a chase down the coast, through the islands, always visible, always moving. Letters came twice, then once a year, then not. The last letter, years later, was short. *They've stopped following. I'm going to be careful anyway. Tell her nothing until she's strong enough to be angry about it.* No return address. After that, silence, and silence is a country with no maps at all.

Ila had waited for the day Wren would come up the river. Every launch, every stranger with green eyes, every loud small-boned woman at the trading post—she had looked. Forty years. The river gives nothing quickly; that is its whole character. And then one morning a storm-chaser stood in her doorway with her mother's exact eyes and her mother's exact stubbornness, asking about a cave, and Ila's heart had gone off like a gunshot inside her chest.

"Strong enough to be angry about it," Ila murmured. "Well. She is angry. Half of it's even aimed correctly."

* * *

The problem—the fresh, urgent, bleeding problem—was that Wren had the stone and intended to sell it.

Ila understood why. She had spent two evenings listening to the girl talk around her debts without naming them, the way her mother had talked around hers. The Voss name had come up once, dropped casually like a stone in a well, and Ila had heard the splash all the way down. So the granddaughter was in debt to the grandfathers' creditors. The river forgets nothing, and apparently neither do moneylenders.

But the stone could not be sold. Not because of spirits—Ila had lived long enough to know that most spirit-talk was either true or useful, and this was both, but it wasn't the main reason. The main reason was simpler and worse.

"If she sells it," Ila said to the fire, "they will have won. Forty years late, they will have won. Her mother gave up everything—gave up *her*, gave up watching her grow, gave up her own name and safety—to keep that stone out of their hands. And the girl proposes to hand it over for money. To *them*. To pay *them*." She shook her head slowly. "It would be like paying a thief with your mother's grave."

No. There was another way, and it had come to her during the raid, while she lay with blood in her hair and Marek's men tearing through the village, and the girl had thrown herself over Ila's body like a shield. That moment—that stupid, magnificent, suicidal moment—had told Ila everything. The girl was her mother's daughter. Cornered, she protected. Cornered, she got *angry*.

Anger could be steered. Ila had steered angrier things.

The plan was forming the way plans formed in the basin: slowly, in water, taking the shape of whatever contained them. Take the stone overland to Madang. Let the coastal brokers see it. Let the customary claim be spoken aloud in front of witnesses—*this is basin property, taboo for sale, under my name and my clan's*—so that no local man would touch the transaction, and Marek's leverage would rot in his cheerful hands. Then return the stone to the cave at the next king tide, and give the girl the only thing the Voisses had never given anyone in four generations.

A clean ledger.

"She'll fight it," Ila told the fire. "She'll say the stone is her only card. She'll be right, and it won't matter. Some cards you play by losing them properly." She poked the embers. "Her mother was the same. Argued with me for a week about leaving the stone here instead of carrying it away. A week! In the end I told her, 'You cannot outrun them forever, but you can outrun them today, and today is the only place anyone has ever lived.' She hated that. She wrote it in her journal anyway. I saw her do it. People always write down what they hate, if it's true."

* * *

Near midnight, the rain thickened, and Wren stirred in the boat. Ila heard her sit up, heard the stone shift against her ribs with its faint buried hum, heard her whisper something—checking, always checking, counting her assets in the dark like a miser counting coins, except the coin was her mother's whole sacrificed life and she didn't know it yet.

Tomorrow they would leave the water. Tomas had already begun arguing porters and provisions out of reluctant neighbors, and the Finisterre wall stood up in the north like a question nobody wanted to answer. The snows were early this year. The monsoon was early too. Everything early, everything urgent, the whole world hurrying them uphill.

Ila rose, joints cracking their usual commentary, and stood in the doorway. Wren saw her and stiffened, hand going to the stone.

"You should sleep," Wren said. "Big day tomorrow."

"So should you. You cannot climb a mountain on greed. It sits in the stomach like bad pork."

Something flickered across the girl's face—annoyance, and under it, barely, the beginning of a question. Ila watched her almost ask it. *Why do you care? Why does this stone matter to you? Who am I to you?*

Not yet. Tomorrow, on the trail, when the truth was the only ration left. Tonight, Ila only said:

"When we stop tomorrow night, I will tell you a story. About a mapmaker."

Wren frowned. "I don't like stories."

"Nobody likes stories," Ila agreed. "We only need them." She turned back into her house, and behind her she heard the girl settle again, the stone humming faintly against her heart like something that recognized its own.

Outside, far to the north, lightning stitched the black shoulder of the Finisterres, once, twice—like a signal. Like a door opening.

Or a warning.
