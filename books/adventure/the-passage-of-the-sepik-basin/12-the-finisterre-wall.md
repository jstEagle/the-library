## 12. The Finisterre Wall

The mountains announced themselves three days before we saw them.

Tomas felt it first, of course. He always did. We were poling up a brown tributary that had no name on any map I owned—just a wrinkle of blue ink and the word *rapids* in some cartographer's tidy hand—and he stopped mid-stroke, tilted his head like a dog hearing a whistle too high for the rest of us, and said, "The water's gone cold."

I put my hand over the side. He was right. The river that had been bathwater warm since the delta was now carrying a chill from somewhere higher and older, snowmelt from a range we couldn't yet see.

"Finisterre," he said, as if introducing me to a relative he didn't much like. "She's early this year."

"She's a mountain range," I said. "Ranges don't keep calendars."

"This one does." He resumed poling. "And she's angry."

I'd have laughed at him a month ago. I'd spent six years chasing storms across the American plains, and I'd heard every kind of weather superstition from every kind of man—the ones who swore tornadoes followed county lines, the ones who could smell hail coming, the one in Oklahoma who claimed his bad knee predicted supercells better than Doppler radar. I'd catalogued them all under *folklore* and driven straight at the sky anyway with my instruments humming.

But Tomas Kerebu read water the way I read pressure gradients, and he hadn't been wrong yet. So when he said the Finisterres were angry, I shut up and watched the river.

* * *

We made landfall at a place Tomas called Kambaramba's landing, though there was no Kambaramba in evidence, only a shelf of mud, a lean-to roofed with sago palm, and four men waiting for us with woven packs and the patient expressions of people about to be paid to do something difficult.

"These are your porters?" I asked.

"These are the porters who agreed to come," Tomas said carefully. "There is a difference."

There was. There very much turned out to be a difference.

Their leader was a compact, cheerful man named Yawan who shook my hand with genuine delight and immediately tried to sell me a cassowary bone dagger, which I took as a good sign. A man still doing business while standing at the foot of a mountain range has either no imagination or total confidence, and I've learned to bet on confidence.

The other three were quieter: two brothers, Simeon and Pita, who carried between them enough rice for ten days and an argument they'd been having since before I was born; and a lanky teenager called Boas, who was Yawan's nephew and had volunteered, according to Tomas, "because he wants to see snow."

"There's no snow where you're going," Yawan told him cheerfully. "There's cold rain and misery."

"I want to see cold rain and misery."

"He has seen our politics," Yawan explained to me. "He wants variety."

I liked them instantly, all four of them, which should have been my first warning. People you like are people you can lose.

* * *

The first day was jungle, and the jungle was honest about what it wanted. It wanted us wet, bitten, and turned around. Leeches found the gap between my boot and sock within the first hour and drank from me with a persistence I came to respect. The trail—if you could call it that—climbed through stands of bamboo so dense that Simeon walked ahead hacking a tunnel that closed behind us like water behind a boat.

But by the second day the trees began to change, and that was how I knew we were leaving one world for another. The lowland giants gave way to oaks hung with moss, then to beeches, then to stunted things twisted by wind, and the air thinned and cooled until my breath showed faintly in the mornings. Boas kept asking if this counted as almost-snow. Yawan kept saying no.

On the third morning we cleared a ridge and the Finisterre Wall stood up in front of us.

I have stood under supercells the size of small nations. I have watched a wall cloud rotate above a Kansas wheat field and felt the particular silence that comes before the sky decides what it's going to do. But I had never seen anything like this. The range rose out of the cloud forest in gray limestone steps, ridge after ridge, each one taller than the last, until the highest peaks wore white caps that looked painted on, impossible this close to the equator. Cloud poured through the saddles between them like slow smoke.

Nobody said anything for a long time. Even the argument between the brothers paused, which I suspect had not happened since 1974.

"That's the pass," Tomas said finally, pointing at a notch maybe two-thirds of the way up, a thin dark line against the rockface. "Three days' climb if the weather holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

He looked at me with something that wasn't quite pity. "Then it closes, and we wait for dry season, and Marek's men do not have to climb anything."

Right. Him. Our shadow. We'd seen signs of pursuit for two days now—a fire ring, a discarded cigarette packet of a brand sold mostly in Lae—and Tomas had stopped pretending they weren't there. Four men behind us, moving well. Marek traveled with professionals.

"So we beat the weather," I said. "Or we beat them. Preferably both."

"You say that like it's simple."

"I chase storms for a living, Tomas. Nothing about it is simple. That's the entire job description." I slung my pack. "Let's move."

* * *

The porters deserted on the fourth night.

I'll give them this—they did it honestly, or as honestly as desertion allows. Yawan woke me near midnight, crouched beside my shelter with his face lit by the little fire, and told me plainly: the mountain was making noises it shouldn't make. Wind from the wrong quarter. His knee—everyone's knee, apparently, in this country knees were oracles—said the snows were coming weeks early. The pass would close. And there were armed men behind us, which meant the smart money was downhill.

"How much do I owe you?" I asked.

He waved it off. "Tomas paid. And—" he hesitated, and then, because he was fundamentally a cheerful man, he grinned, "—you gave Boas a story. *The white woman who chased storms.* He will tell it wrong for years. That is worth the wages."

"What will he get wrong?"

"Everything. Especially the part where you're brave."

Then they were gone, melting into the dark with their packs and their arguments, and it was just the two of us and the mountain, and the mountain breathing.

I lay awake a long time listening to that breathing. Wind moving through the saddles in long, regular gusts—not random, not chaotic, but *patterned*, the way air moves when a big system is organizing itself somewhere over the Bismarck Sea. I'd fallen asleep to that sound a hundred nights in a hundred motel rooms across Tornado Alley, earplug in one ear, radio crackling in the other, waiting for the hook echo on the radar to tell me where the beast would set its feet.

I knew that sound. I had chased that sound across eleven states.

And lying there in the dark on the shoulder of the Finisterres, I realized something that should have frightened me and instead did something else entirely: I understood this storm. Not the way Tomas understood rivers—with scripture and inheritance—but the way you understand a thing you've studied until it lives in your hands. The wind was backing from southeast to east-northeast. The gusts were lengthening. The cloud deck overhead had lowered by a thousand feet since dusk.

A monsoon surge, arriving early, exactly as the old man's knee had promised. Maybe thirty-six hours out. Perhaps less.

Which meant the pass would close in perhaps forty-eight.

Which meant Marek's men, however professional, were climbing into a trap unless they turned back—and men like Marek don't turn back.

I got up, fed the fire, and started repacking in the dark.

* * *

Tomas found me at first light already breaking camp, and I watched him do the arithmetic on my face before I said a word.

"You know something," he said.

"The winds backed overnight. East-northeast, steady, gusts lengthening. The cloud base dropped about a thousand feet between dusk and midnight, and the pressure—" I tapped the cheap altimeter on my wrist, which also did barometric duty and had been quietly falling for eighteen hours. "It's falling fast. That's not mountain weather. That's a synoptic system, a real one, a monsoon surge with our name on it. It makes landfall tomorrow. Maybe the day after."

"You can tell that from wind and a wristwatch."

"I can tell that from twenty years of doing nothing else." I heard my own voice come out flat and certain, and I realized I wasn't performing. Somewhere between Cairns and here, between signing Voss's paper and bleeding leeches off my ankles, the bravado had burned away and left something underneath that actually worked. "Tomas, I've spent half my life learning to read skies that want to kill me. This is the first time it's ever been useful instead of just expensive."

He studied me for a moment. Then, slowly, he smiled—not the guarded half-smile he rationed out, but the full one, the one that transformed his whole face into someone younger.

"My aunt said you had weather in your blood," he said. "She said it like a warning. I'm beginning to think it might be a gift."

"Your aunt says a lot of things like warnings."

"Yes. She's usually right. It's infuriating." He shouldered his pack. "So. How long?"

"If the surge tracks true? Landfall tomorrow afternoon. The pass gets ugly ahead of it—snow above four thousand meters, rain below, and wind that will take your pack apart. We need to be through the notch by tomorrow midday. Which means today we climb like the devil's collecting."

"And Marek?"

I looked back down the trail, into the green dark we'd climbed out of. Somewhere down there, four men were waking up and starting upward, patient as debt.

"They're a day behind us," I said. "They'll push hard to close the gap, because that's what enforcers do. They'll reach the pass right as the front arrives." I felt something cold and clear settle in my chest—not cruelty, exactly, but the honest arithmetic of storms. "Marek's smart. He'll read the sky late, but he'll read it. Whether he turns around in time..." I shrugged. "That's not ours to carry."

"It might become ours to carry."

"Then we'll carry it. After we're through."

* * *

We climbed.

God, how we climbed. The trail became a staircase cut by hands nobody remembered, then stopped being a staircase and became a scramble, then stopped being anything at all and became a slope of wet limestone where every hold had to be tested and trusted. The cloud forest closed around us, bearded with moss that drank the mist and dripped it back down our collars. Orchids grew out of bare rock, absurdly beautiful, like jewelry left on a battlefield.

Tomas set a pace I wouldn't have believed possible for a man who'd taken a bullet graze twelve days ago, and I matched it, and somewhere around noon I caught myself counting my own breathing—not because I was struggling, but because I was *measuring*, the way I measured everything, and the measurement came back good. My legs were strong. My lungs were strong. Six years of hauling radar units across plowed fields and sleeping in cars had built a body that did what I asked it to.

When had that happened? When had I become someone whose body answered?

I thought about the woman who'd signed the Voss brothers' ledger in Cairns—Wren Blackwood, salvage diver, debtor, expert at being underestimated including by herself. She would not have believed this climb. She'd have booked passage around the coast and hidden in a cabin and called it strategy.

This woman—this one with limestone dust on her palms and moss-water down her spine—was going over the top.

"Stop smiling," Tomas called back. "It wastes oxygen."

"I'm not smiling."

"You're smiling like a person who has just won an argument with herself."

* * *

We hit snow at midafternoon, and Boas would have wept.

It came as fine granular pellets riding a wind out of the south, sticking to the black rock in patches that thickened as we gained. Above five thousand meters the world went white and silent and utterly still except for the wind, which never stopped, which spoke continuously in a language of pressure and intent that I translated as we went.

*"Gust front's still two hours out. We have a window. Move."*

*"Wind's veering—that's the surge wrapping in. An hour less than I thought. Faster."*

*"Shelter in that saddle, not the ridge—ridges funnel wind, saddles spill it. Trust me, I've slept in worse ditches than this."*

Tomas watched me work, reading the sky aloud, choosing lines up the mountain, and I saw him filing it all away the way he filed everything—water knowledge, river knowledge, and now this: her strange inland craft, her gospel of pressure and wind.

At the last bench below the notch, we stopped to rope up for the final pitch, and I looked back the way we'd come.

Far below, tiny with distance, a line of figures was ascending the lower slopes. Four of them. Moving fast. Closing.

"Tomas."

He looked. He was quiet a moment. "They'll make the pass by tonight," he said. "Same as us."

"The front makes it by tomorrow morning. They're cutting it fine."

"Marek cuts everything fine. It's how men like him stay employed." He tied off the rope and handed me my end. "One more hour. Then the notch. Then down the far side before the sky finishes deciding."

I took the rope. My hands were steady. Six days ago they'd been shaking in a village that smelled of blood and sago, and now they were steady, and I understood that the difference wasn't courage. It was purpose. Fear with somewhere to go stops being fear and becomes fuel.

"One hour," I said.

We climbed into the notch as the sun went down behind the wall, and the light came up the far side red as a warning flag, and the peaks to the north were already wearing their new snow like a verdict.

From the saddle, looking down into the cloud-choked valleys of the far slope, I saw it: the leading edge of the surge, a great gray-blue wall of cloud marching in off the Bismarck Sea, organized, enormous, inevitable. I knew its shape the way I knew my own handwriting.

Behind us, torchlight flickered on the lower pitches. Four points of flame, climbing.

"Tomorrow," Tomas said quietly, "the mountain chooses."

"No," I said, and pulled my hood up against the rising wind. "Tomorrow, we're already down the other side. Let the mountain explain itself to Marek."

The first flakes of the real snow began to fall as we descended into the dark, and somewhere below us, faint through the wind, I thought I heard a voice shouting orders—and then the storm arrived, and swallowed everything, and the mountain closed its white door behind us both.
