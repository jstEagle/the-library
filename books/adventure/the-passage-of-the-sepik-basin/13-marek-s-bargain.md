## 13. Marek's Bargain

Marek Voss had a theory about rain. He'd developed it somewhere over the Coral Sea, wedged into a seat built for smaller men, watching water hammer the plane windows like it held a personal grudge. The theory was this: rain was the world's way of telling you that you had made a mistake, and the tropics were simply places where people made mistakes so large and so often that the sky never stopped commenting.

He thought about this theory now, standing under the eaves of the mission station at Yimban, watching his shoes dissolve.

Not literally. Though at the rate things rotted up here—the leather, the canvas, the morale of his hired men—he wouldn't have been surprised. Everything in New Guinea was either growing or decaying, sometimes both at once, and Marek, who liked his world to sit still and behave, found the whole arrangement offensive.

"Boss." Dez, his second, materialized beside him with the particular slouch of a man who had given up on dryness around day four. "The brothers called again."

"And?"

"And they want to know why we haven't got her yet."

Marek turned his collar up against the drip from the eaves. "Tell them the terrain is hostile."

"They know the terrain is hostile, boss. They own half of it."

"Tell them the woman is resourceful."

"They'll say resourceful is what we're paid for."

Marek sighed. This was the trouble with family business: there was no one to complain to who wasn't also the problem. His brothers sat in Cairns behind desks the size of dining tables, signing papers with fountain pens, and they believed—sincerely, cheerfully, the way men believe in Father Christmas—that money moved through the world like water through pipes. You put pressure on one end, it came out the other. They did not believe in mud. They did not believe in rivers that ate boats. They did not believe in Wren Blackwood, apparently, because they kept asking why one small storm-chaser with bad credit was taking two weeks and six men to run down.

Marek believed in her. That was the difference between him and his brothers, and it was why he was here, dissolving, while they were dry.

* * *

The mission station at Yimban was a scatter of buildings on a shelf of land above the river: a church with a corrugated roof that played drums whenever the rain changed its mind, a schoolhouse, a dormitory, a vegetable garden going gloriously to seed, and a bell tower from which the whole valley could be watched, which was why Marek had taken it over three days ago with the gentle firmness of a man renting a hall for a wedding.

Father Anselm, the station's sole remaining priest, a wide Dutchman gone soft and brown as old bread, had not objected. He had objected to nothing, actually, which unsettled Marek more than resistance would have. The priest simply went about his business—feeding porters, bandaging feet, saying Mass to a congregation of eleven—and treated Marek's men like weather. Unpleasant weather, but weather.

"You are still here," Father Anselm observed that evening, setting a plate of rice and tinned fish in front of Marek without being asked.

"We are waiting for someone."

"You have been waiting for someone for three days. In my experience, people who are waited for arrive when they arrive, and God does not require you to eat all my fish in the meantime."

Marek looked at the plate. It was, he had to admit, better than what Dez cooked. "You're not afraid of us," he said. "Six armed men take over your station and you bring me dinner."

The priest lowered himself into a chair with the creak of a man whose knees had opinions. "I was in Ambon in '42," he said. "I have seen what fear looks like when it is wearing uniforms. You are not that. You are accountants with guns."

"That's an unkind description."

"It is the kindest one available." Father Anselm folded his hands over his stomach. "The young woman. The one your men keep asking the porters about. She came through the lower trail yesterday with the Kerebu boy. She is feverish, I think. Or frightened. Both look the same at a distance."

Marek set down his fork. "How far behind them are we?"

"Half a day. Perhaps less, if the river behaves, and it will not." The priest smiled mildly. "I tell you this not because I approve of whatever you intend, but because if you chase them into the high country in this season, you will die, and I would rather you did your dying somewhere with a roof."

"That's almost charitable."

"I am paid to be charitable. By God. He pays badly but the hours are flexible." The priest rose, groaning. "Eat your fish, accountant. Tomorrow you will want the strength, whichever kind of strength it turns out to be."

* * *

They caught up with them at noon the next day, and it was almost embarrassingly easy.

Wren Blackwood and Tomas Kerebu came down the forest track toward the station like two people who had been arguing for a hundred kilometers and had run out of argument and were now simply walking inside their own silence. Wren was ahead. She moved well—Marek noted it professionally, the way she read the ground, testing each foothold, head always moving, cataloguing the sky. A storm chaser. Of course. The brothers had laughed at that on her loan application. *Occupation: meteorological consultant.* Marek had not laughed. People who chased storms for a living were people who had decided, at some point, that standing still was the truly dangerous thing.

Tomas walked half a step behind her, and Marek watched him watch her—not the trail, her—with an expression Marek recognized from long ago, from a different life, before the ledgers. It was the expression of a man who had already done the arithmetic nobody should do: what he would pay, what he would lose, what she was worth against all of it.

Dez stepped out onto the track twenty meters ahead of them, and the other four came out of the treeline on either side, unhurried, rifles slung but visible, the whole choreography designed to say *this is not a robbery, this is a meeting.*

Wren stopped. She didn't reach for anything, didn't bolt. She just stood there in the green light under the trees, soaked to the ribs, mud to the knees, and looked at Marek with an expression he'd seen on debtors a hundred times and never gotten tired of: the quick flicker of calculation, the inventory of exits, and then—the part that always interested him—the settling. The moment they realized the exits were furniture.

"Marek Voss," she said. "You look terrible."

"You look worse," he said, "and I've been in the country twice as long." He spread his hands, friendly. "Miss Blackwood. Mr. Kerebu. What a coincidence."

"Nobody says 'what a coincidence' holding a rifle."

"This isn't a rifle. This is a conversational aid." He gestured up the slope. "There's a station up there with a roof that mostly works and a priest who cooks. I propose lunch. We can talk like civilized people, or we can talk out here where the leeches can join in. Your choice."

Wren glanced at Tomas. Something passed between them—not agreement, exactly. Alignment. Marek filed it away. Aligned people were harder to split than agreeing people, but easier to threaten, because aligned people had something shared to lose.

"Lunch," Wren said. "Then you talk fast, because we're leaving."

"Of course," said Marek. "Everyone is always leaving. It's the national pastime."

* * *

The priest fed five extra mouths without comment and then, with the tact of long practice, found urgent business in the garden. Marek's men took the veranda. Wren and Tomas sat across the table from him in the dim schoolroom, which smelled of chalk and mildew, and for a while nobody spoke, because Dez had brought the food and the food required attention, and Marek had learned long ago that the best negotiations started with everyone chewing.

It was Wren who broke first. She always broke first; it was in her file. Not weakly—she broke forward, like ice under load.

"How much," she said.

Marek smiled and dabbed his mouth with a cloth that had once been white. "Straight to it. Good. I hate the part where we pretend to catch up." He laid it out flat on the table, the way he'd lay out cards. "Principal plus accrued interest, penalty clauses, the cost of this expedition—which, incidentally, Miss Blackwood, is not cheap; do you know what it costs to charter a floatplane in this country? No, of course you don't, because you stole a ride on a barge like a stowaway in a novel. Total figure, as of yesterday: forty-one thousand, six hundred and twelve dollars, Australian."

She didn't flinch at the number, which told him something. She'd known it would be big. Big numbers were just weather to her now.

"And the alternative," she said, "is what you told me in Cairns. Work it off 'your way.'"

"The offer stands. My brothers remain fond of it. There's a boat out of Darwin and a hold that needs a certain kind of management." He let that sit. Tomas had gone very still across the table, and Marek watched him go still, and understood that the guide knew exactly what kind of management, or could guess, and was doing arithmetic of his own now. "But I'm not here for that. I'm here because my employers have become—flexible."

"Voss brothers don't flex."

"They mature." Marek leaned back. "Here is the new shape of it. You took something from that cave. Don't—" he raised a hand as she opened her mouth— "please. I have six men and a hundred kilometers of informants. The stone. Carved, heavy, older than the war crates. My brothers want it. Not for sentiment—for leverage, for sale, for whatever rich men want old stones for. Bring it to me, and the debt dies. All of it. Forty-one thousand dollars, the interest, the penalties, the—arrangements in Cairns. Gone. You walk away clean. Signed release, witnessed, notarized if we can find a notary, which frankly at this point I'd settle for the priest."

Silence. Rain on the tin roof, drumming its opinion.

"And if I don't have it," Wren said carefully, "or if I don't give it."

Marek sighed, genuinely sorry for the next part, because he liked her—there, he admitted it, he liked her, the way you like a good horse or a worthy opponent—and what came next was going to hurt them both.

"Then I take collateral," he said. "Him."

Tomas didn't move. But something in the room changed temperature.

"He owes you nothing," Wren said. Her voice had dropped into a register Marek had only heard once before, in Cairns, the day she signed. "His name isn't on any paper. He has no debt. You can't—"

"No," Marek agreed pleasantly. "He has no debt. That's what makes him excellent collateral. Debts can be disputed. People can't." He turned to Tomas directly, and gave him the respect of looking at him properly. "Mr. Kerebu. Here is what happens if she refuses. My men and I escort you downriver. There is a boat out of Madang. There is work on that boat. It is not good work, and it does not end until my brothers say it ends, and my brothers are cheerful men who live a long time. Meanwhile she goes free—debt intact, hunted, but free. So understand the shape of this: her choice doesn't save you both. It chooses which of you pays."

"That's not a bargain," Wren said quietly. "That's a hostage trade."

"Yes," said Marek. "That's what most bargains are, when you scrape off the paperwork. I'm just honest enough to skip the paperwork." He tapped the table. "You have until dark. The priest has a guest room with a lock on the inside, which I suggest you use, and I'll have bread sent up, because whatever you decide, deciding hungry is stupid."

He rose, gathered his cloth, and paused at the door.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "I hope you give me the stone. Not because my brothers want it—I couldn't care less about rocks. Because I've read your file, Miss Blackwood. All of it. And a woman who crossed an ocean to escape a number shouldn't die for a rock she dug out of a hole in the dark." He glanced back, once. "Rocks don't love anybody. Ask him. He knows."

* * *

Evening came down the valley like slow water. Marek stood in the bell tower, which was the driest place on the station and had the additional advantage of letting him see everything, which was the closest thing to peace he ever got.

Below, lamplight in the schoolhouse window. Two shadows behind it, close together, heads bent—one talking, one listening, or maybe both talking, or maybe neither, maybe just standing in the light because the light was warm and the world wasn't.

Dez climbed up, breathing hard, and handed him tea that tasted faintly of smoke.

"She asked me something today," Dez said. "While you were talking to the priest. Asked if the brothers would really honor a release. If the paper meant anything."

"What did you tell her?"

"Told her the truth. Paper means whatever the brothers feel like it means." Dez scratched his jaw. "She said, 'So why give up the stone?' And I said, because the stone's worth nothing to you and everything to them, and a bad trade you survive beats a good one you don't." He shrugged. "She looked at me like I'd said something profound. I was just repeating what you told me in Cairns."

Marek drank his terrible tea and watched the shadows in the window.

"Boss," Dez said. "What if she doesn't deal? What if she calls it?"

"Then we take the guide at dawn, and she follows us downriver screaming, and my brothers get their stone eventually anyway, because she'll trade it for him inside a week. Everyone in this story trades in the end. It's just a question of price." He said it steadily, because it was true, and because Dez needed it to be true, and because if Marek admitted there were people who didn't trade—if there were people who'd burn the stone rather than hand it over, people who'd walk into the Finisterres in monsoon season rather than come down to the boat—then the whole tidy machine of his life developed a rattle he didn't want to hear.

Below, the schoolhouse door opened. Wren Blackwood stepped out into the rain alone, no lamp, and stood in the yard with her face turned up to the sky, letting it hit her, the way people do when they've stopped trying to stay dry and started trying to decide something.

And Marek, watching, felt the rattle in the machine and ignored it, the way he ignored all weather.

Because that was the thing about Wren Blackwood that his brothers would never understand, and that he understood perfectly, and hated:

She wasn't looking at the sky like a debtor.

She was reading it.
