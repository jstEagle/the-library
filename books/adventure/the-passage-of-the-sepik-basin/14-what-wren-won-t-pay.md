## 14. What Wren Won't Pay

The mission station at Yankopa sat on a shelf of land above the river like something the flood had forgotten to take: a church with a corrugated roof gone orange with rust, two dormitories, a bell tower that leaned at an angle suggesting it had heard better sermons. A German missionary had built it in the twenties. The Germans were long gone, but the bell still hung there, and Father Anselm—a Papuan priest with reading glasses held together with wire and prayer—still rang it twice a day for no congregation in particular.

"We can rest here," Tomas said, and his voice had that flat carefulness I'd learned to hate, because it meant he was doing arithmetic he didn't want me to see.

We'd been three days out from the sinkhole where the monsoon had nearly buried us alive. Three days of descending through cloud forest so wet that my boots never dried, not once, not even by the fire, until I'd taken to sleeping with them off and accepting that putting them back on each morning was its own small act of courage. The stone rode in my pack against my spine, warm as a living thing. Marek's men had been behind us the whole way—I'd seen their cooking smoke twice, heard them once, crashing through the undergrowth like men who believed volume was a tactic.

Tomas counted heads at the station. Four porters left of the nine we'd started with. Two of those four were already drifting toward Father Anselm with the particular body language of men negotiating a release from a contract they no longer found spiritually nourishing.

"Rest," I said. "You mean hide."

"I mean rest." He set his pack down against the church wall and lowered himself beside it with the economy of a man conserving everything—movement, words, hope. "Marek won't come up the valley in this rain. His boats are useless. His men are miserable."

"Marek doesn't need boats. He needs me to stop moving."

"And you will stop moving eventually," Tomas said, "because your legs are shaking."

My legs were shaking. I sat down next to him before they could file a formal complaint.

Father Anselm appeared in the church doorway holding a lantern and the expression of a man who had been expecting trouble since roughly 1974. "Two travelers," he said. "In monsoon season. From the direction of the Sepik." He looked at us over his glasses the way you look at weather coming in. "There is rice. There is tea. There are questions I will ask after you have eaten, because hungry people lie badly and I am too old to enjoy watching it."

I decided immediately that I liked him enormously.

* * *

We ate in the rectory kitchen, which was a room with a wood stove, a table worn smooth by forty years of elbows, and a calendar from a Madang hardware store featuring a large and unsmiling pig. Father Anselm fed us rice and tinned fish and a relish so hot it briefly made me forget every problem I owned, then asked his questions while we ate, which was cheating, but effective.

He got the shape of it out of us without ever asking directly—the stone, the men following, the debt. When Tomas mentioned the Voss name, the old man's ladle stopped moving for exactly one second, then resumed as if nothing had happened. But I saw it. I was getting good at seeing things like that. Forty years ago somebody in my family had taught these highlands to flinch at that name, and the flinch had been passed down like a recipe.

"You should go on to Madang," Father Anselm said. "It is four days down if the rivers behave. They will not behave. But there is a coastal trader that calls at Madang, and money talks to traders in ways it cannot talk to mountains."

"The stone isn't for sale," I said, mostly to hear myself say it.

"No," he agreed mildly. "I imagine it isn't. Things that hum usually aren't."

I stared at him. "You know about the stone?"

"I know about stones," he said, refilling my tea. "I have spent fifty years in this country learning the difference between what people carry and what carries them. You, Miss Blackwood, are being carried. It is not an insult. Most of us are. The question is only whether you notice before you arrive somewhere you did not choose."

Tomas made a sound into his tea that might have been a laugh or might have been a cough. I kicked him under the table. He didn't even flinch, which told me more about how tired he was than any complaint would have.

We slept in the dormitory, on cots with blankets that smelled of mildew and camphor, and for the first time in a week I slept without dreaming of water.

Which is why I woke to the sound of the bell.

Not the lazy two-stroke evening ring. This was fast, urgent, someone hauling on the rope like the rope owed them money. I came off the cot already reaching for my pack, and Tomas was already up, already at the window, and the look on his face closed my throat.

Lanterns in the yard below. Six of them. And in the middle of them, tall and dry despite the rain, stood a man in a canvas coat with his hands folded in front of him like a choirboy waiting to be praised.

Marek.

* * *

They let us come down. That was the first thing I understood—they could have come up, they could have dragged us out of our cots, but Marek wanted an audience, and Father Anselm's station gave him one: the priest, the two remaining porters, all of us arranged in the lamplight of the church porch while the rain came down silver behind him.

"Wren Blackwood." He said my name like he was checking it against a list, and finding it correct, and being mildly disappointed about that. "You've cost me two boats and a lot of shoe leather."

"You should invoice the monsoon," I said.

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not warmth. Recognition, maybe—the look of one professional acknowledging another's form. "Here is the situation," he said. "The brothers want the stone. The stone is worth more than your debt, which is why they sent me instead of a letter. So. You give me the stone, and the ledger closes. Every kina. The interest. The—arrangements. All of it. You walk away clean."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I take something else of comparable value." He turned his head slightly, unhurried, and looked at Tomas. "Him, for instance. The brothers have uses for guides who know the Sepik. Not pleasant uses. But the brothers believe in recycling."

The rain filled the silence. My heart was doing something complicated and arrhythmic, and beneath the fear there was a colder thing, an accountant's thing, running numbers: one stone against one man. One object I couldn't sell anyway, guarded by a taboo I half-believed, against a person whose fever-bright eyes I'd watched across a fire for eleven days. It wasn't even arithmetic. It was the easiest sum I'd ever done.

And that was exactly why I couldn't do it.

Because Marek knew that too. That was the whole design of the offer—that I'd hand it over, that anyone would, that everyone has a price and the price is always someone else. Give him the stone and I'd be free, yes. Free and finished, because I'd have told the world, and myself, that Tomas Kerebu was a line item. That love was a currency I could spend when the exchange rate suited me.

"No," I said.

Marek waited, giving me time to be sensible. Sensible people always took the deal; that was why sensible people worked for people like the Vosses.

"No," I said again, louder, and pulled the pack off my shoulder—not to surrender it, but because I wanted my hands free. "You can't have the stone and you can't have him. Come take both and see what's left of you."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Marek sighed, genuinely regretful, the sigh of a man closing a folder. "I hoped you'd be reasonable," he said, and snapped his fingers.

What happened next happened fast and badly and mostly in the rain. One of his men grabbed for my pack and I went down with it, which was fine, because going down put me at knee height and knee height is where people keep everything important. I hit him with the pack itself, stone and all—it hummed against the man's skull with a note I felt in my teeth—and then Tomas was past me, taking on two of them at once with a boat hook Father Anselm had apparently kept by the door for exactly this sort of ecumenical purpose.

Someone fired a gun. In the rain, at night, at moving targets, which tells you everything about the marksmanship. The bullet meant for me clipped the lantern instead and the yard went dark, and darkness is a country where I have citizenship. I found Tomas by grabbing the nearest arm and pulling, and we ran—up the slope behind the church, into the treeline, porters scattering ahead of us like startled birds, Father Anselm's voice behind us ringing the bell faster than ever, which I suspect was less alarm than cover.

We ran until the station lights were gone and the rain swallowed everything, and then Tomas stumbled, and then he fell.

* * *

I got him upright somehow, got us somehow to an overhang of rock where the rain couldn't reach, and struck a match with hands that would not stop shaking.

The bullet had kissed him. That's the only word for it—a furrow along the outside of his left arm, shallow, ugly, bleeding freely but not fatally. A graze. The word sounds small. The blood was not small. Blood never is.

"It's nothing," he said, which is what everyone says, and which I ignored, because I'd spent years around people who said *it's nothing* right before the sky turned green and ate the town. I cut his sleeve away, washed the groove with rainwater caught off the rock lip, bound it with strips of my shirt and the last of the antiseptic, which had been intended for leech bites and was now serving democracy. He watched me work with an expression I couldn't read, and when I tied off the last knot he said, quietly:

"You could have given him the stone."

"Don't start."

"He offered you everything. Debt gone. Clean name. Free." His voice had a strange looseness to it, fever already climbing, words arriving without their usual gatekeeping. "One rock. Rocks are common. I have seen ten thousand rocks."

"This one hums."

"So do I, apparently, when I'm shot." He laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle, and I realized how hot he was. Not wound-fever only. He'd been fighting the wet for days, and bodies keep accounts too. "Wren. Answer the question. Why? Why does the rock matter less than me?"

And I opened my mouth, and nothing came out.

That's the honest truth of it. I sat there in the dark with a bleeding man leaning against my shoulder and rain hammering the world like applause, and I searched for the answer, and there wasn't one. Not one I could say. Because the true answer was enormous and shapeless and terrifying—it was something about how the stone was leverage and leverage is just a word for the future, and how I'd looked at Marek's face and understood that futures bought with people are counterfeit, and how some part of me that I hadn't met until this country made me had simply refused, the way a storm refuses to explain itself—

But none of that was language yet. It was all still weather.

"I don't know," I said finally. "I don't have the answer yet."

He was quiet a long moment. Then, drowsily, sliding toward sleep and fever both: "Good. Better honest than quick." A pause. "My aunt says the river asks everyone the same question eventually. What are you worth. She says most people answer with money because it's the only answer they've been given." His eyes closed. "Don't answer with money, Wren. You're bad at math anyway."

"That's slander. I'm excellent at math. I'm just selective about which sums count."

"Mm," he said, and was asleep, or near enough, his head heavy on my shoulder, his breath coming fast and shallow, the bandage already showing a bloom of red at its center like a flower deciding whether to open.

I sat very still, holding him up, holding the question up, and outside the rain went on falling on a world that owed me nothing and was paying me nothing and had, for reasons I still couldn't itemize, given me this man anyway.

Somewhere down the valley, faint through the rain, I thought I heard the trader's horn from Madang roadstead—one long note, patient, indifferent. Four days' walk. Maybe five, carrying him. With Marek behind us and the rivers rising ahead.

I shifted his weight onto my back, the way I'd carried survey gear through cyclone country, the way you carry the things you refuse to put down.

"Hold on," I told him, not sure which of us I meant. "I'll find the answer by the coast."

Above us, through a tear in the cloud, the moon showed itself for exactly three seconds—thin, white, and going—and then the monsoon drew its curtain, and we started down toward the water.
