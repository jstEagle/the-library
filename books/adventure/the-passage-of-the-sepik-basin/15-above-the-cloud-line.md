## 15. Above the Cloud Line

The mountain did not begin all at once. That was the first lie the maps told, Tomas thought, watching Wren fold hers for the fourth time that morning and frown at it as though the paper had personally deceived her. On the map, the Finisterres were a neat brown spine with a dotted line crossing it like a stitch. In life, the range arrived in stages—first the foothills soft as a sleeping animal's back, then the ridges that made your calves argue with you, and then, above everything, the wall itself, blue-black and wearing its clouds like a man wears a hat he refuses to take off even indoors.

"You keep looking at it like it's going to apologize," Wren said.

"It won't," Tomas said. "Mountains never do. Rivers sometimes. Mountains just wait to see what you're made of."

"And what are we made of?"

He considered the honest answer—rice, fear, and about six days of bad sleep—and decided against it. "Enough," he said.

They had left the last village two days behind them, where the headman had traded them sweet potatoes for Wren's storm lantern and told them, through Tomas's careful translation, that the snows had come early this year. Early snows on a mountain near the equator sounded like nonsense until you stood at the base of the Finisterre Wall and felt the cold coming down off it like water off a ledge. The porters had turned back there, all four of them, with the particular politeness of men who wished you well and intended to watch your funeral from a safe distance.

So now it was the two of them, and the stone wrapped in oilcloth inside Wren's pack, and whatever was left of Marek's men somewhere below them in the green dark, also climbing, also cursing, also learning what the mountain charged.

Tomas had been guiding this country since he was fourteen years old. He knew his body the way a boatman knows a hull—the sounds it makes when it's sound, and the sounds it makes when it isn't. For three days now, the hull had been making the wrong sounds. The graze on his ribs had closed into an angry red seam, but the fever lived underneath it like a fire banked in wet wood, flaring at night, sulking by day, never quite going out. He had been managing it the way you manage a leak: bailing steadily and saying nothing.

He was good at saying nothing. It was practically a family trait. His aunt Ila could out-silent a stone, and Tomas had inherited enough of it that Wren had taken three full days of travel to stop asking him if he was angry with her every time he went quiet.

But this morning, stepping over a fallen strangler fig whose roots could have swallowed their whole camp, his knee simply declined to participate. Not painfully. Just—a refusal, brief and total, like a crew member setting down his oar mid-stroke and folding his arms.

He caught himself on the fig root. Kept his weight off it. Made it look almost casual.

Wren, ten paces ahead, turned around. She had developed an unnerving habit of turning around at exactly the wrong moments, which he suspected was less instinct than attention—she watched him the way she watched weather, reading small changes in pressure that other people missed.

"Your knee," she said.

"Stepped wrong. It's fine."

"Mm." She looked at him the way she'd looked at the barometer in Cairns before the storm season broke—that flat, unfooled stare. "Say it again but slower, so I can hear the part where it's true."

"Wren."

"Tomas." She came back down the trail to him, took his arm without asking, which was unlike her, and steered him onto a log beside the trail. "Sit. Don't perform sitting. Actually sit."

He sat. The forest went on around them doing its enormous quiet business—birds arguing about territory, some distant tree giving up its grip on the slope with a long groan of roots. He realized, sitting there, how loud his own breathing had become. When had that happened?

"Show me the wound," she said.

"It's healing."

"The fever, then. Show me the fever." Her voice wasn't hard. That was the terrible thing about her; she could make concern sound like an invitation rather than an accusation. "You've been bailing for days, haven't you. I've been watching you ration yourself. Half rations of everything—water, talking, walking. You think I don't notice because I'm busy being dramatic about leeches."

"You are very dramatic about leeches."

"I am *appropriately* dramatic about leeches, they're horrifying, that's not the point." She crouched in front of him, and up close her eyes were the color of the deep channel water at the delta, and just as difficult to see the bottom of. "How long?"

"Since the station," he admitted. "Maybe before. It comes and goes."

"Comes and goes," she repeated, in the voice of a woman filing something away for later prosecution. She pressed the back of her hand to his forehead, then his neck, professional and quick, and frowned. "You're cooking. You've been cooking. And you let me set the pace for four days."

"You were setting a good pace."

"Tomas Kerebu." She said his full name the way his aunt did when he'd done something specifically stupid. "I have spent my entire adult life being lied to by people who thought they were protecting me. Banks. Brothers named Voss. Men with clipboards. Do not join them. You're better company than any of them and worse at it."

Something in his chest loosened and hurt at the same time. "The pass is still two days up," he said. "Three, if the cloud sits on us. After that it's downhill to Madang and rivers again, and rivers I can handle sick or well."

"And between here and there?"

"Between here and there," he said, "you'll have to be the guide."

She blinked. He watched the idea land on her—not gently, the way ideas usually landed on Wren Blackwood, who caught everything like it might be a weapon—and then settle, and change shape, becoming something else entirely.

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon, and the forest began to change around them the way it always did on the big mountains—by subtraction. The great trees thinned. Moss took over everything, thick and green-gold, hanging from branches like the fur of some animal too large to see. The air itself grew damp and cool and close, and then the cloud came down and folded them inside it.

Cloud forest, the old guides called it, and Tomas had always thought the name was exactly right. You walked inside a cloud the way you walked inside a wool blanket. Ten meters of visibility. Sound arriving strangely, muffled and then suddenly intimate. Water dripping from everywhere at once, so that after an hour you couldn't say whether it was raining or whether the sky had simply given up on the distinction.

It was slow going, and slower because of him. He knew it, and she knew it, and neither of them said it, which was its own kind of conversation. He navigated by memory and by the feel of the slope under his boots, calling out the turns—"left at the split trunk, bear right where the moss fails"—and Wren moved ahead of him through the white, breaking trail, testing each foothold with her staff before committing her weight to it.

She was good at it. That surprised him, though he supposed it shouldn't have. A woman who chased storms for money had already learned the two essential lessons of bad country: respect the thing that can kill you, and count everything twice. She counted their water twice a day. She counted the hours of light. She noticed, before he did, that the moss on the north side of the trunks grew thicker, and asked him why, and listened to the answer all the way through instead of nodding along.

At the steepest pitch, where the trail became a suggestion and the mountain became a ladder of roots and wet stone, his knee failed him properly.

Not dramatically. That was the humiliating part. No cry, no fall. His leg simply informed him, midway through a step, that it was no longer a party to this arrangement, and he found himself sliding, slowly, with tremendous dignity, back down a meter of slope into the arms of a tree fern.

"Tomas!" Wren was above him instantly, dropping to her knees in the moss, reaching down. "Are you—"

"I'm fine," he started, and then heard himself, and stopped. Closed his eyes. Started again. "No. I'm not fine. My leg has resigned and my fever is negotiating. I need to sit for a while, and then I need you to go on without me to the saddle, mark the route, and come back with help from—"

"Stop talking," she said. "That plan is garbage and we both know it. Nobody's leaving anybody on a mountain. We're not doing that."

"Wren—"

"We're *not doing that.*" And there it was under the words, plain as a scar: the debt collectors, the launch with no flag, every person in her life who had ever been left behind or left her behind, all of it packed into three words. She hauled on his arm. "Up. Come on. Up."

He came up. And then, because the slope allowed nothing else, she did the thing he would remember for the rest of his life, in whatever life he had left after this mountain.

She put his arm over her shoulders, took his weight against her side, and climbed.

* * *

She was smaller than him. Lighter by a good deal, though she carried it like a woman used to hauling gear. The arithmetic of it was simple and brutal: he outweighed her, the packs outweighed both of them, and the mountain did not care about anyone's feelings. So they invented a system, the way people do when the alternative is stopping forever. On the shallow pitches she half-carried him, his weight distributed across her shoulders, her breath sawing in his ear. On the steep pitches they crawled, actually crawled, hands and boots in the moss, moving like animals, like something older than people.

Somewhere in the middle of the worst hour, delirium got its hooks into him. The cloud came apart and reassembled. He saw his aunt's kitchen, the reed houses at the delta, the river running silver under a moon. He heard his mother's voice, though his mother had been gone twenty years, telling him something urgent about the water level that he couldn't quite hold on to. At one point he was certain they were being followed by a crocodile, uphill, which even his fever recognized as unlikely, and he laughed about it, and Wren said, "What? What's funny?" and he said, "Nothing, keep climbing," and she kept climbing.

What he remembered afterward, clearly, past all the strange bright noise of the fever, was her voice. Not what she said—she talked the whole time, a low steady stream of nonsense and encouragement and swearing, mostly swearing—but the quality of it. Steady. Unafraid of him seeing her like this: sweating, filthy, straining, terrified. There was no performance anywhere in her. He had known her since the delta, weeks now, and he understood that he was watching something happen that had maybe never happened to her before in her whole life.

She was letting herself be seen working.

Later, in the lee of a granite shoulder where the cloud thinned enough to show a strip of violet evening sky, they stopped and ate cold sweet potato and he slept two hours and woke clear-headed for the first time in a day. She was sitting with her back against the rock, pack straps loose, cleaning the mud out of her boot treads with a stick. Methodical. Thorough. A woman doing a job because the job needed doing.

"You know what I figured out," she said, without looking up, "somewhere around the crocodile part?"

"That I'm delirious and charming?"

"That you were right, back at the fig tree. About me being the guide." She flicked a clot of mud into the dark. "I've spent years being nobody on purpose. Invisible. Bad credit, fake name, no fixed address—you can't collect from a ghost, right? That was the whole strategy. Be no one, owe nothing, want nothing." She looked up at him then, and the last light caught her face, and she was smiling, a small crooked thing. "And then today I carried a grown man up a mountain in a cloud, and I knew exactly where the water was, and I counted our food twice, and nobody was chasing me except the weather. And I thought—huh. Turns out I'm someone. She was here the whole time. She was just waiting for something heavy to carry."

Tomas wanted to say something worthy of that. He was, he reflected, a river guide with a cooked brain and one functioning knee; the gods send us our moments and rarely check our qualifications.

"You dropped me twice," he offered.

"*Once,* and the fern broke my fall, and you were delirious and insulting my technique."

"I stand by the insult. Your crawling is undignified."

"Good," she said. "Stay mean. It means the fever's losing." She reached over and put her hand flat on his forehead, and left it there longer than the checking required, and neither of them mentioned it, and neither of them moved away. Above them, through a tear in the cloud, the stars came out over the saddle they still had to cross—cold, sharp, indifferent, beautiful. Below them, far down in the dark green, something that might have been a flashlight beam swept once through the trees and was gone.

Wren saw it too. Her hand didn't move from his forehead. But her eyes changed, the way the sea changes when the wind swings around.

"They're still climbing," she said quietly.

"Yes," Tomas said. "So are we."

She helped him to his feet, and they went up into the last of the light, toward the snow line and the pass, while far below them the small patient lights of Marek's men rose through the cloud forest like a slow constellation, following.
