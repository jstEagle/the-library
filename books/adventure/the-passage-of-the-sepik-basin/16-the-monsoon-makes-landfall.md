## 16. The Monsoon Makes Landfall

I have chased two hundred and eleven storms in my life. I could tell you the barometric pressure at which a supercell drops its first hailstone, the exact shade of green the sky turns over a Kansas wheat field when a tornado is deciding whether to be born. I have stood close enough to feel lightning raise the hair on my arms and called it a good day's work.

So believe me when I say: I knew this one was coming three days before it did. And knowing made no difference whatsoever.

It started as a smell. That's the thing nobody tells you about monsoons—they don't arrive so much as ripen. The air went thick and sweet, like fruit left too long in a bowl, and every insect in the Finisterre foothills began behaving as though it had somewhere urgent to be. The birds stopped singing at midday, which Tomas said was the mountain holding its breath.

"Two days," he said, squinting at the horizon from beneath the tarp we'd rigged over our camp. His arm was still bound against his chest where Marek's bullet had grazed him, and he'd gone thin in the face, but his eyes were as steady as ever. "Maybe less."

"One," I corrected. "Eighteen hours, if the pressure keeps falling like this."

He looked at me sideways. "You can smell that?"

"I can *read* that." I tapped my temple. "Ten years of professional paranoia, Kerebu. It's finally useful."

He almost smiled. He'd been doing that more lately—almost smiling—and each time it landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there, warm and inconvenient, like a stone I'd swallowed. "Then we should be grateful," he said, "that your paranoia has such good timing."

The pass ahead of us—the only route down the wet side toward Madang—was a notch in the ridge called by our last remaining porter, old Simon, *the throat*. Simon had deserted us that morning along with his cousin, which meant our expedition now consisted of me, a half-healed guide, a canvas pack of rice and tinned fish, and a carved stone wrapped in oilcloth that hummed with bad history every time I touched it.

Which was often. Because I couldn't stop touching it.

* * *

Here is what I told myself about the stone: it was leverage. Here is what was actually true: it was the first thing I had ever owned that felt like it owned something back.

Ila's voice still lived in my head—*it is not a treasure, daughter, it is a debt of its own*—but Ila was a week and a hundred kilometers behind us, her ribs strapped where Marek's men had knocked her down, and debts were the one language I spoke fluently. You don't survive the Voss brothers without learning that everything is currency. Your gear. Your name. Your future labor. Your silence. I had signed away most of myself in a Cairns office that smelled of rum and expensive cologne, and the stone was the first asset I'd ever held that they wanted more than I did.

That was the theory, anyway. The theory was getting harder to hold onto. Some nights, when Tomas slept and the rainforest clicked and dripped around us, I unwrapped the thing just to look at it—a fist-sized piece of riverstone carved with spirals so old the grooves had softened, as though the rock itself were trying to forget them. It didn't glow. It didn't whisper. It just sat there being older than any country I had citizenship in, and I sat there being a woman who intended to sell it, and between those two facts there opened a gap I kept falling into.

"You hold it like it might apologize," Tomas said once, catching me at it.

"It's going to make me rich."

"Mm." He rolled toward the fire. "My aunt says the cave charged for what it gave. She says you're carrying the bill."

"My aunt," I said—it still startled me, saying *my*, the word fitting my mouth like a borrowed shoe—"has never met the Voss brothers."

"No," he agreed quietly. "But she met their fathers."

I hadn't asked about that yet. There was a whole story there—Ila's story, the cartographer, the flight across forty years—and I wasn't ready for it. Some doors you leave shut because you can hear breathing on the other side.

* * *

The storm came eighteen hours early, because of course it did. My own forecast, betrayed by its subject. Somewhere out over the Bismarck Sea, a wall of cloud two hundred kilometers wide decided it had waited long enough, and the sky over the Finisterres turned the color of a bruise.

I felt it before I saw it—a pressure drop so sharp my ears popped, a wind shift that came around through the north instead of the southeast, wrong, backing, the signature of a system organizing itself with intent. Ten years of chasing had taught me to read storms like faces, and this face was looking directly at me.

"Tomas." I was already moving, pulling the tarp, lashing the packs. "It's not coming tomorrow. It's here."

He was on his feet without argument, which I'd learned was one of his best qualities. "The throat?"

"We can't be in the throat when it hits. A narrow pass in a cloudburst—" I shook my head. "Flash flood, debris flow. We'd be gravel by noon."

"So we go up?"

"We go *sideways*." I pointed east along the contour, where the ridgeline bulged above a fold of jungle. "There'll be karst up there. Limestone. Sinkholes, caves—somewhere the water goes down instead of across."

He stared at me. "You know the stone country."

"I grew up in Tornado Alley, Tomas. Karst is just tornado country that got rained on for a million years." I hoisted the pack. "Come on. We've got maybe four hours before the leading edge makes landfall, and I intend to be underground when it does."

We climbed. God, how we climbed. The foothills of the Finisterres don't rise so much as *stack*—jungle stacked on jungle, roots stacked on mud, mud stacked on air. Tomas moved through it like a man walking through his own house, calling holds before I needed them, and I moved through it like a woman who had spent a decade running toward weather instead of away from it and had finally found the race she couldn't win.

Halfway up, the first fat drops came down through the canopy, each one hitting the leaves like a thrown stone.

"Faster," I said.

"There is no faster," Tomas said. "There is only *not slower*."

"That is the worst motivational philosophy I've ever heard."

"And yet." He gestured upward with his good arm. "We continue."

* * *

We found the sinkhole an hour later, and I have never been so relieved to see a hole in the ground.

It opened in the flank of the ridge like a mouth—a limestone funnel thirty meters across, ringed with ferns, dropping into shadow. Rainwater had cut channels down its inner walls over centuries, feeding some underground river we could hear but not see, a sound like slow applause. At the bottom, a dry alcove curved back under an overhang, floored with sand and leaf litter, high above the waterline.

Tomas lowered himself down hand over hand, favoring his bad arm, and I passed the packs after him, then the stone, then swung down myself just as the sky opened.

Not rain. *Release.* The monsoon's leading edge hit the ridge like a dropped reservoir, and within minutes the jungle below us was roaring, a white curtain of water hammering the canopy until the whole world was noise. Lightning walked along the valley in long stuttering strides, and thunder came not as sound but as occupation—the air itself taken over, shaken, owned.

I sat with my back against cold limestone and laughed.

Tomas looked at me like I'd gone mad. Maybe I had. But you have to understand—for ten years, storms had been the thing I ran *toward*, the only thing that ever made my blood move right, and every one of them had ended the same way: me standing in a field with instruments and debt collectors' letters in my truck, watching the sky spend all its fury on nothing, on flat land, on emptiness. This storm had *teeth*. It had a target. It was trying to kill me personally, and there was something almost flattering about it.

"What?" I shouted over the din.

"You're smiling!"

"I'm *home*!" I shouted back, and then heard myself, and stopped smiling, because that was either the truest or the saddest thing I'd ever said.

* * *

We were in that hole for two days.

Two days of water falling like the sky had a grudge. Two days of tinned fish eaten cold because smoke would show, of listening to the mountain come apart around us. On the second morning we heard the landslides—a deep tearing sound, geological, patient, followed by avalanches of mud and trees going down the valleys like the world clearing its throat. The throat, I thought. Our pass. Whatever had been a route yesterday was a scar today.

Tomas confirmed it at midday, climbing to the sinkhole's rim during a break in the rain and returning gray-faced. "Gone. All of it. Half the ridge came down where the track was."

"So we find another way down."

"On the wet side? In monsoon?" He shook his head slowly. "Wren, the rivers will be up for weeks. Every ravine is a waterfall now."

"Then we wait for them to drop."

"And Marek?"

I didn't have an answer for that. Marek and his men were somewhere behind us, also fighting the weather, also waiting—but men like Marek treated weather as an inconvenience, not a verdict. They'd come. Weather always ends; men with contracts don't.

So we sat in the dark with the stone between us, literally—I'd set it on a flat rock in the middle of our little camp like a centerpiece neither of us had chosen—and on the second night, with the rain finally softening to a steady drum, Tomas broke the silence.

"Ask me."

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not asking since the mission station." He poked the coals. "Why I came with you. Why a man with a bullet hole walks into the Finisterres for a woman who lies about how much trouble she's in."

"I don't lie about—"

"Wren."

"Okay." I pulled my knees up. "Fine. Why *did* you?"

He was quiet long enough that the rain filled the space. "Because when Marek's man put the gun to my aunt's door, you stepped between. You're half his size and you stepped between. I have watched you do that since the delta—put yourself between trouble and other people, and pretend afterward it was strategy." He glanced at me, firelight moving on his face. "Strategy doesn't bleed like that."

"It's not—" I started, and stopped, because the denial died on its way out. What was it, then? Stupidity? Debt? Some inherited reflex from a mother I'd never met?

"The stone," Tomas said softly. "When you give it back to Ila—and you will, eventually, because you keep looking at it like a letter you're afraid to open—when you give it back, what will be left that you want?"

The question hung there. Below us, the underground river applauded its slow, endless applause, indifferent to debts and dynasties and the two small mammals shivering in its doorway. The storm didn't care about the Voss brothers. The mountain didn't care about the stone. Nature had been making and unmaking this range for millions of years and would go on doing it long after Marek's ledger was dust, and there was a strange comfort in that—a permission, almost. If nothing here was permanent, then nothing here was owed forever.

"I don't know yet," I said honestly. "Ask me again at the coast."

He nodded, and lay back, and within minutes his breathing evened out—which was its own kind of trust, a man sleeping beside a stranger who was starting, horribly, to feel like home.

I stayed up and listened to the storm die by degrees, and near dawn I noticed something that turned my blood colder than the rain ever had.

Downslope, far off through the torn jungle, a light. Not lightning—steady, low, moving. A torch, or a lantern, working its way along the contour below our sinkhole. Then a second light beside it.

Marek's men weren't waiting for the rivers to drop.

They were already climbing.
