## 17. The Descent to Madang

The wet side of the Finisterres did not welcome them so much as swallow them and then complain about the taste.

Tomas felt it in his boots first—the squelch that never dried, the way the ground had begun to think of itself as a river that simply hadn't committed yet. Above them the cloud forest still held its green dark ceiling, but below, through a tear in the mist, he could see what waited: ravines running brown and fast, waterfalls where last month there had been only moss, and far down, a haze of heat and rain that was the lowland making ready for the monsoon.

"We're going down into that," Wren said beside him. Not a question. She'd stopped asking questions two days ago, somewhere above the cloud line, when she'd carried him on her back through a stretch of trail no sensible person would have walked at all.

"Yes," Tomas said.

"And the rivers are rising."

"Yes."

"Because of course they are." She adjusted the strap across her chest—his pack now rode on her shoulders along with her own, because his arm was still bound and his ribs still sang whenever he breathed too deeply—and looked at him with an expression he was learning to read as affection wearing annoyance's clothes. "You know, most guides would have mentioned 'seasonal flooding' in the brochure."

"There is no brochure."

"That's the problem with this country, Kerebu. No brochure." She started walking before he could answer, which was also becoming a habit, and he followed, because following Wren Blackwood downhill through a drowning world had somehow become the shape of his life.

His shoulder throbbed where Marek's bullet had kissed it. A graze, the mission doctor had called it, in the tone of a man who considered a graze a personal favor from God. Fever had come and mostly gone, leaving Tomas weak in the way that infuriated him most: not weak enough to stop, just weak enough to be carried. He kept replaying it—that moment on the mission station steps when Wren had said *no* to Marek Voss, flat and final, over a stone worth more than the whole station, and then had put herself between the gun and him without appearing to make any decision at all.

He had asked her why, feverish, in the night. She had no answer yet. He intended to collect.

* * *

By midmorning they reached the first of the flooded ravines, and Tomas understood that the mountain had decided to test them properly.

It should have been a stream. He knew this country by reputation if not by foot—a porter who'd come up from Madang had described the descent to him at the mission, drawing lines in spilled flour: three ravines, then the ridge road, then the long wet stair of foothills to the coast. The first ravine was supposed to be knee-deep and bad-tempered. It was now chest-deep and furious, a brown torrent hauling branches and whole sections of hillside toward the sea like a man clearing out a house after an argument.

Wren stood at the edge and studied it the way she studied storms. That was the thing about her that had unsettled him at first and steadied him ever since: she didn't look at danger like something to flee. She looked at it like something with habits. Something that could be known.

"It's faster in the middle," she said. "Slower against the outside bends, where the current curls back on itself. See how the foam collects there?" She pointed to a seam of white water hugging the far bank, thirty meters downstream. "That's an eddy line. If we cross there, the river's fighting itself, not us."

"You can read that?"

"I've spent eleven years reading air. Water's just air that got heavy." She glanced at him, and there it was—the crooked almost-smile that arrived whenever she was about to do something reckless and wanted a witness. "Rope?"

"Rope." He got it out one-handed while she anchored it around a tree whose roots gripped the bank like a man holding a door shut in a gale. They tied in together—he insisted on that, and she let him, which told him more about the state of her heart than anything she'd said aloud—and stepped in.

The river took his breath first, cold climbing to his sternum, then his footing, gravel rolling under his boots like ball bearings. But Wren moved the way she'd promised, angling downstream into the eddy, using the river's own confusion, and when they dragged out on the far bank she was laughing—actually laughing, breathless and soaked and alive in a way that made his ribs hurt worse than the bullet had.

"Did you see that? Did you—" She wrung out her hair. "I love this. I hate that I love this. My whole life people told me chasing storms was a death wish, and it turns out it was just practice."

"For what?"

"For whatever this is." She gestured at the drowned world generally. Then her face did the thing it did when humor was a door closing rather than opening. "They're behind us, aren't you thinking. In this weather."

Tomas looked back the way they'd come. The mist had closed over the pass; nothing moved in it that he could see. But he'd grown up on this water, and water taught you that what you couldn't see was usually the biggest thing in the river.

"Marek's men want the stone," he said. "But the weather doesn't care what anyone wants. If they're smart, they're camped until it eases."

"And if they're not smart?"

"Then they're dead, and we'll find out how at the bottom."

She was quiet a moment, hand resting on the pack where the stone rode wrapped in oilcloth, humming its silent hum that only she seemed able to feel. "I keep waiting to feel glad," she said. "About them maybe drowning. I can't find it anywhere."

"That's not a flaw in you, Wren."

"Feels like one. Feels like the last one I've got left." She shouldered the pack. "Come on. Two more ravines."

* * *

The second ravine nearly killed them both, and the third they didn't attempt at all.

The third had become a waterfall—not fed by one, but *been* one, the entire cut transformed into a single pouring chute of white. They stood on the rim watching tons of water fall into itself, and Tomas did the arithmetic he hated: the ridge road east would add two days, two days the monsoon might erase entirely, two days Marek could use.

Or there was the other way. The old way. The way his grandfather had spoken of once, drunk on bush lime beer, pointing at the cloud-wrapped slopes and saying *the karst eats men, boy, but it also hides them.*

"The sinkholes," Wren said, before he could say it. She'd been staring at the same broken country to the south—limestone terrain full of shafts and hollows, drained underground, the rivers vanishing into the rock and reappearing who knew where. "We go through the karst."

"It's dangerous."

"So is standing here discussing it." She turned to face him fully, rain streaming off her chin. "Tomas. I need to say something before we go in there, in case the karst does eat us."

His heart performed a maneuver he chose not to comment on. "Say it."

"I'm sorry." The words came out rough, like they'd been stored a long time in bad conditions. "For lying to you about the launch. About how deep the debt went. About all of it. You were right to distrust me, and you trusted me anyway, and you took a bullet for the privilege, and I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Whatever happens down there or at the coast, I needed you to hear it while you could still walk away from me."

Tomas considered several replies. Most of them were foolish. He settled on the true one.

"Ila named you kin," he said. "Before the whole village. You don't get to apologize your way out of a family, Wren. We argue. That's the membership fee."

Her eyes did something complicated and bright. "That's the worst acceptance speech I've ever heard."

"I'm a guide, not a priest."

"No," she said, and smiled, and it was the real smile, the rare one, the one that made him understand why men wrote songs about women who were terrible for them. Except she wasn't terrible. She was just weather. And he had always loved weather.

They went into the karst.

* * *

The limestone country was a city built by water and abandoned mid-construction. Shafts opened at their feet without warning; sinkholes big enough to hold villages yawned between walls of grey rock hung with vines; and everywhere beneath them, they could *hear* it—the underground rivers going about their business, a sound like the earth murmuring in its sleep.

They moved from hole to hole, Wren navigating by a logic Tomas slowly came to admire: she treated the karst like a storm system, reading the drainage, the flow paths, predicting where water would gather and where ground would hold. Twice she stopped him with a raised hand ten paces from a sinkhole rim that crumbled into the dark behind them within the hour.

At midday they sheltered in a dry cave and shared the last of the mission station's rice, and Wren talked—not about the stone, not about Marek, but about Cairns, about cyclone seasons, about the pearl diver who'd told her the rumor that had started everything.

"He was so drunk he fell off his stool twice telling it," she said. "Cave opens at king tide, full of war crates and older things. I thought, ninety days, one miracle, easy math." She laughed at herself, softly. "Nobody tells you the miracle comes with a mother, and a man, and a village, and a debt of a completely different kind."

"What kind?"

"The kind you can't pay off. Only live inside." She looked at him across the small fire's smoke. "Is that what belonging is? Just... living inside something bigger than you and stopping pretending you're separate?"

"My grandmother used to say the river doesn't carry the canoe," Tomas said. "The river *is* the carrying. The canoe just agrees to be carried."

"That's either very wise or very wet."

"In my family those are the same thing."

She laughed, and the sound went up through the cave mouth into the rain, and for a little while the pursuit and the mountain and the debt all seemed very far away.

Then the light changed.

It came down the shafts first—a bruised green-dark shift, the particular dimming Tomas had known since childhood, the color the sky turned when it stopped threatening and began delivering. He stood. Wren stood a half-second later, head cocked, listening with that instrument she carried instead of ears.

"Pressure's dropping fast," she said. "Wind's backing around. Oh, that's not a squall cell. That's the front wall. Tomas, the monsoon's making landfall *now*, and we're—" She stopped. Looked at the cave around them. Looked at him.

"We're in the best shelter in the range," he finished.

"But the rivers."

"Yes," he said grimly. "The rivers."

* * *

They came out of the karst into a world being unmade.

Rain fell not in drops but in sheets, in ropes, in a manner that suggested the sky had been holding a grudge since creation and had finally been given permission. The third ravine—which they had wisely refused—was now a brown sea moving through the cut with a sound like continuous thunder. Every gully was a torrent. The trail was a rumor.

But the monsoon worked both directions. From a ridge shoulder that evening, soaked to the soul, Tomas looked back the way they'd come and saw what the storm had done to their pursuers: far below, at the mouth of the second ravine, a knot of men on a flooded bank, marooned, their boats gone or swamped, their gear scattered, tiny figures waving arms at each other in fury and fear.

Marek's men. Fighting the same water. Losing to it.

"They can't cross," Wren breathed beside him. Rain ran off her nose; she didn't wipe it away. Her eyes had gone bright with the fierce joy he recognized from her stories of the storm years—the look of a woman watching a forecast come true. "Look at them. They're pinned. That ravine won't drop for days, and when it does, the ridge road will be mud soup. We're—" she counted, lips moving, doing her easy math again, "—we're a full day ahead. Maybe two."

"Two days to Madang," Tomas agreed. "If the coastal track holds."

"If it holds," she echoed. Then, quieter, watching the stranded men shrink into the rain: "I hope they get out. I hate that I hope it. Marek I could watch float out to sea on a door, honestly, but those others are just... men doing a job badly."

"That's called mercy, I think."

"It's called exhaustion." But she leaned into him, briefly, her soaked shoulder against his good one, and let him take some of her weight, and the rain came down on both of them equally, indifferent and enormous, and Tomas thought: *this is what my grandmother meant. This is the carrying.*

They descended through the night's edge, following the coastal track down through foothills gone liquid, and near midnight they saw it—far below, past the last black shoulders of the range, a scatter of lights where the land gave up and became harbor.

Madang. Boats riding at anchor. The coastal trader's mast lights strung like a festival.

And on the quay, unmistakable even at this distance, even through rain—a figure standing motionless under the customs shed lamp, arms folded, watching the inland dark. Waiting. A man who had not been caught by the flood after all, who had come around by sea while his men drowned in the hills, and who was looking up at the mountains as though he could see two small figures descending through the rain straight toward him.

Marek Voss had beaten them to the coast.

Beside Tomas, Wren went very still, the way the basin went still before the tide turned.

"Well," she said at last. "So much for easy math."
