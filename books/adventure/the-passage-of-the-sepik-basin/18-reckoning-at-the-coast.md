## 18. Reckoning at the Coast

Madang smelled of diesel and frangipani, which is a combination I'd never have believed could coexist until I stood on its harbor front at six in the morning, soaked to the ribs, with a stone in my pack that hummed like a struck bell and a man named Marek walking toward me across the wet coral gravel.

Tomas was beside me, his arm still bound where the bullet had kissed him at the mission station, and he was leaning on me more than he'd ever admit. Nine days over the Finisterres had stripped us both down to essentials: one machete between us, half a bag of rice, and the particular silence of two people who have said too much in the dark and are pretending they didn't.

"There," Tomas said quietly. "The tall one. The one who walks like the ground owes him money."

"That's about right," I said. "It's usually the other way around."

Marek came alone, which I hadn't expected. His men were strung along the harbor front like beads on a wire—three by the copra shed, two near the fuel drums, one eating something out of a paper cone with the unhurried air of a man who knew exactly how long this would take. They'd come down the wet side of the range behind us, fighting the same flooded ravines we had, and they looked it: mud to the thighs, faces scoured raw by rain. Even enforcers, it turns out, are subject to weather. It was the first comfort I'd had all week.

"Miss Blackwood." Marek stopped ten feet away, hands loose at his sides, smiling the way the Voss brothers smiled—warmly, professionally, the smile of men who genuinely enjoyed their work. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other guy."

"I did. He's limping." His eyes flicked to Tomas, then back. "But you made good time. I'll give you that. The Voss brothers said you were resourceful. They say it fondly."

"Then you can tell them I'm flattered, and hand them my regards, and we'll call it a day."

"We won't." He said it pleasantly, the way you'd decline a second cup of tea. "You know why I'm here. You've been carrying it across a mountain range for nine days. I watched your porters desert you at Kegsugl. I watched you carry a grown man through the cloud forest on your back, which, frankly, I didn't think you had in you." He tilted his head. "So let's not do the part where you pretend you don't know what I want."

I looked out at the harbor. Fishing boats were coming in with the morning catch, their hulls low and heavy, the fishermen shouting prices up at women on the seawall who shouted back louder. A coastal trader sat at anchor beyond the reef, riding at her moorings with the patient arrogance of a ship that had nowhere better to be. Somewhere behind me a church bell rang, badly, as if the ringer had opinions about tempo.

"The stone," Marek said. "For your debt. Clean slate. Interest waived—the brothers are feeling generous, the season was kind. You walk away owing nothing. You walk away *free*, Miss Blackwood. Isn't that what you crossed a country for?"

And here is the thing I will not pretend about, because Ila taught me better than that: for about four seconds, standing there in the smell of diesel and flowers, I wanted to say yes. I wanted it the way a drowning woman wants air. Ninety days of terror in Cairns, the ledger with my name on it, the cheerful phone calls from men who collect in blood—all of it, gone, for the price of handing over a rock I'd pulled out of a hole in the sea.

Four seconds. Then I heard Ila's voice in my head, dry as river reeds: *You want to sell your grandmother's bones to pay your grandfather's debts. That is not freedom, daughter. That is just a smaller cage.*

"Here's my counteroffer," I said, and unslung the pack.

Marek's smile widened a fraction. Behind him, one of his men straightened up from the fuel drums.

I lifted the stone out into the morning light.

It was smaller than people imagined—a fist of dark green stone, carved all over with spirals so old the grooves had gone soft-edged, and even here, even now, it did that thing it did: the air around it seemed to lean in. The nearest fisherman paused mid-haggle. A dog lying under the copra shed raised its head. I felt it against my palms like holding a bird that hadn't decided whether to be afraid of you.

"One condition," I said. "Not the harbor. Not a boat. We do it properly—public sale, local broker, papers signed in front of witnesses. Your brothers want clean title? Then we get it clean. No midnight deals. I'm done with midnight deals."

Marek studied me. "Why?"

"Because I want witnesses when you cheat me," I said. "At least then someone will know."

He laughed—a real laugh, short and surprised—and I understood suddenly why the Voss brothers kept him. He wasn't a thug playing at civilization. He was a civilized man who happened to be very good at violence, and the difference mattered enormously.

"All right," he said. "Public sale. There's a broker two streets up—Kamane. He handles everything that comes off the traders. We'll go now, before the heat."

We went. Tomas walked on my left side, and I felt him cataloguing every one of Marek's men as we passed, counting exits the way other people count change. His shoulder pressed mine. Neither of us said anything. There wasn't anything left to say that the mountain hadn't already said better.

* * *

Kamane's office was a room above a store, furnished with a desk, two chairs, a ceiling fan turning with the enthusiasm of a tired swimmer, and a wall of ledgers going back, judging by the dust, to the German administration. Kamane himself was a small, precise man in a shirt ironed within an inch of its life, and he received the stone onto a square of felt with the reverence of a priest handling a relic and the eye of a pawnbroker appraising a watch.

"Mm," he said, which after a long moment became, "Mm-hm," which after another moment became, "Where did this surface?"

"A cave," I said. "Sepik coast."

"Mm." He turned it once. The spirals caught the fan-light and seemed to move. "And you wish to sell."

"She wishes to sell," Marek said from the corner, where he'd installed himself with the ease of a man in his own office. "Cash sale. Clean title. The usual commission, Mr. Kamane, plus whatever you consider appropriate for discretion."

Kamane did not look at him. This, I began to realize, was a skill of his.

"Before we proceed," Kamane said, "there is a formality." He produced a ledger—older than the others, its spine held together with tape gone amber—and opened it to a page marked with a strip of tapa cloth. "Every item of customary significance offered for sale in this district is checked against the register of claims. Taboo listings. It takes only a moment."

"Taboo listings," Marek repeated.

"Customary law," Kamane said mildly. "It predates your employers' interest rates by some centuries, but it is still recognized. Still enforced. Every broker on this coast honors it, because every broker who has failed to honor it—" he turned a page delicately, "—has found himself unable to buy copra, hire a crew, or purchase bread. The coast is a small place, gentlemen. It remembers."

He ran his finger down the page. Stopped.

"Ah," he said softly.

My heart did something complicated.

"Item forty-one," Kamane read aloud, in the tone of a man reading scripture. "*Carved stone, green, spiral-work, recovered from the sea-cave at the mouth of the Sepik. Claimed under customary taboo by Ila, spirit-keeper of the reed houses, daughter-line of the basin. Sale prohibited. Handling prohibited. Removal from district prohibited.*" He looked up. "Dated eleven days ago."

Eleven days ago. Eleven days ago Ila had been lying in a reed house with a bruised rib cage and blood in her hair, and she had sent word ahead of us—not after us, *ahead* of us—by channels I would probably never fully understand. Canoe relays. Radio men. Church networks. Whatever the coast used instead of telegraph, she had used it, bleeding, from her bed.

She had known I'd try to sell it. Of course she had. She'd known me for three weeks and read me like weather.

"There must be some mistake," Marek said.

"There is no mistake." Kamane closed the ledger with both hands, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "The claim is registered. The item cannot be sold. Not by me. Not by any broker in Madang, or Lae, or Rabaul, or Port Moresby—I'll save you the telephone calls; the network is faster than you'd credit. Any attempt to move it privately would be..." he considered, "...unwise. The traders talk to each other. The crews talk to each other. My wife talks to everyone." He folded his hands. "Is there anything else?"

Marek was quiet for a moment. I watched him do the arithmetic—watched the whole structure of his leverage come apart in his face like a wall meeting a landslide. He couldn't sell the stone. He couldn't threaten to sell it. He couldn't even take it by force and *try*, because the moment it touched a broker's felt anywhere on this coast, the taboo would land on it like a net, and the Voss brothers would own the most famous unsellable object in the Pacific, and Marek would own the blame.

What he could still do was take it out of the country by force, consequences be damned. But that was no longer business. That was piracy, and piracy required a different set of instructions from Cairns, and I'd have bet everything I had left—which was approximately nothing—that the Voss brothers hadn't given him those instructions.

"You planned this," he said to me. Not angry. Almost admiring. "The old woman. The registration. You crossed the mountains knowing this was waiting."

"No," I said honestly. "I crossed the mountains because staying was worse. But she planned it. And if you'd spent five minutes with her instead of sending men to beat her, you might have guessed she would."

Something moved behind Marek's eyes at that—something that wasn't quite guilt, but was at least aware of its address.

"The debt stands," he said finally. "The taboo protects the stone. It doesn't protect you, Miss Blackwood. You still owe the Voss brothers a very large sum of money, and I am still authorized to collect it."

"I'm aware."

"And your guide here—" his gaze slid to Tomas, pleasant as a knife laid on a table, "—is not party to any taboo either."

Tomas shifted against my shoulder. I felt him gather himself, fever and all, the way water gathers before it goes over a ledge.

"Mr. Kamane," I said quickly, "thank you for your time."

"Gentlemen." Kamane bowed slightly, gathered his stone-less felt square, and showed us out with the impeccable courtesy of a man washing his hands of the entire century.

* * *

Outside, the heat hit like a wet towel. Marek fell into step beside us, his men folding in from the corners of the street without hurrying, and I understood that the negotiation was not over—it had merely changed venues. We walked toward the harbor because walking away wasn't an option and standing still was worse, and the frangipani trees along the seawall dumped their scent on us like some tropical joke at our expense.

"So this is it," I said. "You fly it out yourself. Take the taboo and break it over your knee."

"I could." Marek walked with his hands behind his back, a man strolling. "And then I'd spend the next ten years unable to hire a boat crew east of Lae, and my employers would spend them wondering why their coastal interests kept failing, and neither of those outcomes is in my contract. No, Miss Blackwood. The stone is dead weight now. Which means you're carrying the only asset left in this transaction." He stopped. Turned to face me fully, and dropped the smile for the first time since the harbor. What was underneath was worse. "You."

The fishing boats were coming in. The women on the seawall were shouting prices. The bad bell rang again, still with opinions.

"Come to dinner," Marek said. "Tonight. The Voss brothers' account holds a tab at the hotel here—don't ask me why; they like options. We'll discuss terms like adults. You'll find I'm more flexible than my reputation." His eyes went, briefly, deliberately, to Tomas. "Or we can continue this conversation in whichever way you prefer. I'm told the monsoon season is excellent for hard bargaining. Nobody's going anywhere."

He nodded, courteous as a consul, and walked off down the seawall, and his men peeled away after him like a tide going out.

Tomas exhaled slowly. "Well," he said. "That went well."

"Did it?"

"He didn't shoot anyone. In my experience that constitutes well."

I looked down at my empty hands, then out at the trader still riding at anchor beyond the reef—and my stomach dropped, because she was raising sail. Small figures moved on her deck. And at her rail, wrapped in a shawl despite the heat, small and upright and unmistakable even at that distance, stood a figure watching the harbor with the patience of a woman who had rowed a war canoe at sixty and beaten men half her age at everything since.

"She came," Tomas said, following my gaze. He started to laugh, then winced, then laughed anyway. "Of course she came. She registered the claim from her bed. Did you think she'd stop there?"

The trader's dinghy was already being lowered.

"Dinner with Marek tonight," I said faintly. "Terms like adults."

"Mm." Tomas settled his weight more comfortably on my shoulder, entirely too content for a man with a bullet graze. "Then I suggest we eat first. And that you decide what you're willing to pay him—because he'll ask for something, Wren. Men like that always do. The taboo took his leverage, not his appetite."

Down at the water, the dinghy touched the beach, and Ila stepped out into the shallows, and the entire seawall seemed to turn toward her at once, the way a field turns toward rain.
