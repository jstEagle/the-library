## 19. The Price of Survival

Marek Voss had been called many things in his life—thug, enforcer, the polite end of a very impolite family business—and he had learned, over the years, to wear each name like a coat that almost fit. But the one thing nobody had ever accused him of was being unprepared. He kept lists. He kept ledgers. He kept, in a waterproof pouch inside his jacket, a small notebook in which he recorded every debt owed to the Voss brothers, every payment made, and every interest calculation down to the second decimal place.

The notebook was his bible. And this morning, sitting on an upturned crate at the edge of Madang's harbor while the monsoon wrung itself out over the tin roofs like a giant, exhausted sponge, the notebook told him something he did not want to read.

*Wren Blackwood: principal outstanding, 41,300. Interest accrued through transit and pursuit, 9,880. Total: 51,180.*

And against that figure, what did he actually hold?

A woman who had walked out of the Finisterre Range looking like something the mountain had chewed and politely returned. A carved stone that no broker in Madang would touch because an old riverwoman had stood on the wharf that morning and spoken words in her own language that had emptied the harbor office of buyers faster than a fire alarm. And two of his own men—one with a hand wrapped in bandages from a machete he should never have reached for, the other with a deep spiritual aversion, newly discovered, to leeches.

Marek closed the notebook.

"You look," said a voice beside him, "like a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like."

He didn't have to turn around. He knew the voice. It had spent three weeks being sarcastic at him across rivers, ridgelines, and one memorable standoff at a mission station where she'd thrown a kettle of boiling water at his feet—not at him, at his *feet*, which was somehow worse, because it meant she'd been aiming.

"Miss Blackwood," he said. "You're punctual."

"I'm desperate," Wren Blackwood corrected, dropping onto the crate beside him as if they were old friends sharing a beer. Up close, the storm-chaser bravado was still there—he could see it, the chin up, the eyes level—but it sat differently now. Thinner. Like paint over weathered timber. The mountain had sanded her down to something more honest. "There's a difference. Punctual people choose to show up."

"And you?"

"I chose." She shrugged. "That's the whole point of today, isn't it? Choices."

Marek studied her sideways. In Cairns, ninety days ago, she had signed his ledger with a flourish and a joke, all swagger and borrowed confidence, a woman performing courage she hadn't yet earned. He'd seen a hundred of them. They were his favorite kind of client, honestly—the optimists. His brother Denny used to say the Voss brothers weren't in the lending business, they were in the optimism business, and optimism always came due.

But this woman on the crate was not the same woman. This one had crossed the Finisterres ahead of the snows carrying a fevered man half her size twice her weight. This one had looked at Marek across a mission station courtyard and refused a deal that would have cost her nothing but a rock.

He found, to his considerable irritation, that he respected her. Respect was a professional liability. He made a note to mention it to Denny and then decided against it, because Denny would only laugh.

"Here's where we are," Marek said, and opened the notebook out of habit before catching himself and closing it again. Old habits. "The stone is dead to us. Ila—" he pronounced the name carefully; he'd practiced it in the mirror that morning and still got it slightly wrong, "—has made sure of that. Every broker from here to Lae knows that if they touch it, they're touching her claim, and nobody in this trade touches a spirit-keeper's claim. Not for a stone. Not for a cathedral."

"So your leverage is gone."

"My leverage is *adjusted*," Marek said. "Which brings us to you. Fifty-one thousand, one hundred and eighty dollars, Miss Blackwood. Give or take the cost of the bandages."

"Give or take," she agreed solemnly.

They sat in silence for a moment. The rain thinned to a drizzle. Somewhere behind them, the fish market was waking up, and the smell of mackerel and diesel drifted over the crates like an apology neither of them wanted to accept.

*

Here is a thing about Marek Voss that he had never told anyone, not even Denny, especially not Denny: he had not wanted this job.

Oh, he'd wanted *a* job. Work was work, and the family business paid well and required no aptitude for anything except showing up and being large. But chasing a debtor across an ocean and into a jungle, through floods and fevers and one genuinely traumatic encounter with a cassowary that his men still refused to discuss—that was not the work. The work was standing outside pubs in Cairns and being cheerful at people until they paid. Marek was good at cheerful. Cheerful was his whole method. You smiled, you remembered their children's names, you made it clear that the alternative to paying was not violence but *disappointment*, and disappointment, properly deployed, was far heavier than any fist.

Wren Blackwood had beaten him with a stone he couldn't sell, a riverwoman he couldn't intimidate, and a guide who had once told him, with the serene certainty of a man reading scripture, that the river remembers who poisons it. Marek had written that sentence down in the notebook. He wasn't sure why. It seemed important.

"The way I see it," Wren said now, breaking into his thoughts, "you have three options. One: you take me back to Cairns, where I have nothing, and Denny pays for my passage just to be disappointed. Two: you do something unpleasant to me, which I notice you've had three weeks to do and haven't, so either you won't or you can't, and honestly I've stopped caring which. Or three—"

"Or three," Marek said, "we settle."

"We settle," she agreed.

She reached into the canvas bag at her feet and began laying things out on the crate between them, item by item, like a merchant setting up a stall. A salvager's license, creased soft from handling. A brass compass, dented. A waterproof watch that had stopped at some point during the crossing and now served, she said, as a reminder that time was a scam. A bundle of survey charts of the Sepik delta, annotated in her small fierce handwriting. And finally—a folded document, oil-stained, that made even Marek sit up straighter.

"What is that?"

"Expedition salvage rights," she said. "My name on the charter. The *Kestrel*'s manifest has me listed as primary claimant on anything recovered north of the delta. That's worth money. Real money, if you know who to sell it to, and you know exactly who to sell it to, because you've already tried to buy it off me twice."

"I offered you a fair price."

"You offered me a price designed for someone who doesn't read contracts," she said pleasantly. "I read contracts. It's how I ended up owing you fifty-one thousand dollars."

"That was optimism," Marek said. "The contract was very clear."

"The contract was clear the way quicksand is clear."

He almost laughed. He turned it into a cough, because enforcers laughing at debtors set a bad precedent, but it was a near thing, and he suspected she knew it, because she gave him the smallest, most infuriating smile.

*

They haggled for two hours.

This was, Marek would later reflect, the most enjoyable part of the entire expedition, which said something depressing about the expedition. Wren Blackwood negotiated the way she apparently did everything—with total commitment, zero sentiment, and a running commentary that made it impossible to stay angry at her. She surrendered the salvage rights, then fought him tooth and nail over the compass ("It was my father's." "Then it's sentimental, and sentimental items are worth nothing on a ledger." "Then why do you want it?" "...Because it's a nice compass, Miss Blackwood."). She threw in the charts willingly and then revealed, twenty minutes later, that half the channels marked on them had shifted in the last flood season, making them beautiful and nearly useless, a fact she disclosed only after Marek had agreed to a higher price for them.

"That's fraud," he said.

"That's *the market*," she said. "You of all people."

In the end, the pile on the crate looked like this: the salvage rights, the license, the charts, the compass (he got the compass; he'd earned the compass), the watch, her dive gear, her spot on the *Kestrel*'s return manifest—which meant she'd be working her passage home in the galley, a detail she volunteered with suspicious cheer—and a promissory note for four hundred dollars she swore she could raise from a cousin in Darwin who owed her a favor and possibly a kidney.

Marek added it up in the notebook. Thirty-eight thousand, six hundred. Against fifty-one, one-eighty.

"There's a gap," he said.

"There's always a gap," she said. "That's how you people stay in business."

Denny would have pushed. Denny would have leaned across the crate with that smile of his, the one that made grown men remember childhood debts, and squeezed until the gap closed or something broke. Marek had watched him do it a hundred times. It worked. That was the terrible thing—it worked, and it left Denny rich and everyone else smaller, and Marek had spent forty years telling himself that was just how the world was shaped.

He looked at the gap in the ledger. Twelve and a half thousand dollars. On paper, unforgivable.

On paper, so was everything else.

"Here's my offer," Marek said, and heard his own voice come out quieter than he intended. "I write the balance down as *paid in full*. Interest waived. My brother will scream. Let him scream; he screams beautifully, it's practically a hobby. In exchange, you give me two things."

"Name them."

"One: you never borrow from the Voss brothers again. Not a dollar, not a favor, nothing. If I ever see your name in our book again, the waiver evaporates and we start over from day one with compound interest and my brother's full attention. Are we clear?"

"Crystal."

"Two." He hesitated. This was the part he'd rehearsed on the walk over, and the rehearsal had gone fine, and now that he was saying it aloud it came out wrong anyway, softer than planned. "Two: you tell me why. Not the money—I don't care about the money, the money's just paper with anxiety printed on it. Why the cave. Why the stone. Why cross a mountain range rather than hand over a rock worth less than the boots you ruined getting here. Nobody does that for greed. Greed stays home where it's dry. So what was it?"

Wren was quiet for a long time. Out past the breakwater, the sea had gone the color of hammered pewter, and a coastal trader was coming in low and heavy in the water, riding high now, its cargo gone to whatever harbor had wanted it.

"That's not for sale," she said finally. "Not today. Maybe not ever. Ask me again in a year, if you're ever back this way and feeling talkative."

"That's not a settlement, Miss Blackwood. That's a maybe."

"It's the best offer on the table," she said, "and you know it, because you've met the other party involved, and you know she wouldn't sell it either. Some things aren't debts, Marek. Some things are just *true*, and you can't collect on true."

He looked at her. She looked back, level and unafraid, and there it was again—that respect, arriving uninvited like a leech in a boot.

Marek Voss opened the notebook to the page with her name on it. Fifty-one thousand, one hundred and eighty dollars, tracked across three weeks, two mountain ranges, one cassowary incident, and a war of attrition against an entire river system that had, frankly, sided with the enemy. He uncapped his pen.

Then, because he was who he was, he wrote it out properly, in full, in his careful accountant's hand:

*Received from W. Blackwood: salvage rights, equipment, sundries, per inventory. Balance settled. Interest waived. Account closed.*

He signed it. He tore the page out along the perforation—notebooks mattered, but some pages were meant to leave—and held it out.

She took it, read it twice, and something went through her face that he couldn't name and didn't try to. Relief, maybe, or grief, or the strange flat feeling that arrives when a thing you've been carrying so long you mistook it for a spine is suddenly, gently taken away.

"Account closed," she said.

"Account closed." He stood, joints cracking, and gathered the salvage rights and the compass into his bag. At the last moment he put the compass back on the crate. "For your father," he said gruffly. "Sentimental items are worth nothing on a ledger. Consider it a rounding error."

She stared at it. Then at him. "You're a strange man, Marek Voss."

"I'm a *professional*," he said, with dignity. "There's overlap."

He was three steps away when she spoke again.

"Marek. Your men. The one with the hand, and the one with the leeches—are they going to be all right?"

He stopped. Of all the questions. Fifty-one thousand dollars settled, and she asked after his hired muscle. He thought about the answer honestly, because she'd earned honesty if anyone had.

"Bruno's hand will heal. Petar may never go near a river again, but between us, I think the river's relieved." He paused. "Tell Kerebu the river can stand down."

"I'll pass it along," she said gravely, "in the appropriate scripture."

Marek walked away down the wharf with a bag full of paper and a ledger twelve and a half thousand dollars lighter than it should have been, and found—to his genuine astonishment—that his shoulders felt lighter too, as though the debt had been his to carry as well. Behind him he heard her boots on the boards, heading not toward the town, not toward the ships, but upriver, toward the reed houses and the old woman and the guide who read water like a holy book.

He did not look back. Professionals didn't.

But he did open the notebook one last time, right there on the wharf, and beneath the torn stub of her page he wrote a single line, not a debt, not a figure, just a note to himself in his tidy hand:

*Some accounts close. Some people pay themselves.*

Then he went to find a telephone, and prepare, with the thoroughness that was his one true gift, for the conversation with his brother—the only reckoning left on the coast that Marek Voss feared at all.
