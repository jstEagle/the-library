## 21. Storm Season

The first thing the monsoon taught me was that I had been wrong about rain my entire life.

I'd chased storms across three continents. I'd stood in the backs of trucks with hail hammering the roll bar like an angry god demanding entry, and I'd called myself a student of weather. But Cairns storms were tourists—loud, dramatic, gone by morning. The monsoon in the Sepik Basin was a resident. It moved in, unpacked its things, and settled onto the land with the patience of something that intended to outlive everyone present.

It rained for eleven days straight when it arrived, and then it rained for reasons of its own after that.

"You're counting wrong," Tomas said, on the twelfth day, watching me tally figures in my sodden notebook under the eave of Ila's largest reed house. "You keep writing down how much falls. That's not the question."

"Then what is?"

"Where it goes." He nodded at the river, which had climbed the bank and was now considering the village's lower gardens with the mild interest of a landlord inspecting property. "The sky is only half the story. The other half comes down the mountains behind us, days after the clouds have passed. You're reading today's letter. The river is reading last month's."

I looked at him. He was mending a net, fingers moving without his attention, the way mine used to move over a radar console back when I still believed instruments could save me from myself.

"Say that again," I said.

He said it again, slower, because he thought I hadn't understood his English. But I'd understood it fine. I was just busy feeling the ground shift under a lifetime of assumptions.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about staying: it's harder than leaving. Leaving requires only momentum, and I had always been full of that—a woman made entirely of exits, each one faster than the last. Staying required me to sit in one place while the water rose and the air went green and thick as soup, and to be known. Thoroughly. Daily. By people who had watched me lie, steal, run, and bleed, and who had decided—without consulting me—that I was worth keeping anyway.

Ila decided it first, in her way, which was to hand me work. She put me to sorting sago with the older women, who interrogated me about Australia with cheerful ruthlessness and corrected my technique until my arms ached. She let me sit with her in the evenings while she told stories I was only beginning to understand were not stories at all but maps—of kinship, of obligation, of where the river kept its grudges.

Tomas decided it second, in his way, which was quieter and considerably more inconvenient to my heart.

And I decided it last, on a morning in the third week of the rains, standing hip-deep in floodwater helping push a stranded canoe off a submerged fence line, laughing so hard I swallowed half the Sepik, when I realized I hadn't once thought about the next horizon. Not the coast, not Madang, not some rumor of treasure waiting over the next ridge. Just the canoe. Just the water. Just Tomas swearing beautifully in three languages as the fence gave up its claim.

That was the day I stopped being a fugitive from my own life and started living in it.

* * *

The charts began as a favor and became an obsession.

The river people navigated the flood season by memory and inheritance—grandfather's knowledge, grandmother's warnings, the accumulated wisdom of generations who had read this water since before anyone wrote anything down. It was magnificent knowledge. It was also, I noticed, organized around the villages that had survived to pass it on. Whole channels appeared in no one's memory, because the families who'd used them had moved or died or been absorbed elsewhere. The basin was full of orphaned waterways.

"I want to map them," I told Tomas one evening, and heard myself say it and flinched, because the last time I'd spoken those words aloud they'd belonged to someone else—my mother, the cartographer, the woman whose maps had gotten her killed by men with my debt-collectors' eyes. "I mean—I want to try. If that's—"

"It's what she did," Ila said from the doorway. She said it plainly, the way she said everything now, having spent her truths like a woman spending her last coins on something worth buying. "She drew the water. You draw the sky. The same eye, turned different directions."

So we built the charts together, and I will tell you honestly that it was the best work of my life, and I include in that category every storm I ever chased.

We went out in Tomas's boat when the rain eased to a steady drumming instead of a deluge—which the locals called *good weather*, delivered without irony—and we sounded the flooded channels with weighted lines and marked snags and sandbars and the treacherous new currents where the river had decided to rearrange itself for the season. I brought what I knew of pressure systems and rainfall cycles, sketching the shape of the weather weeks out the way I'd once sketched supercell structures for men who paid in adrenaline and never in enough money. Tomas brought the river's own testimony: the color of the water, the behavior of the birds, the smell of the wind off the ranges.

"You watch the clouds," he said, "and I'll watch the water, and between us we might know one whole thing."

Between us, we knew several whole things. We knew that the channel past Keram Bend would silt shut by month's end and reopen along the far bank. We knew which cutoff lagoons would drain clean and which would rot into stagnant traps for fish and careless boats. We drew them all in pencil on waterproofed paper, and Ila's grandson inked them properly, and by the middle of the season there were four copies circulating among the villages, and old men who had barely spoken to me in my first months were stopping me on the banks to argue about contour lines.

One of them, ancient Onei, who remembered every flood since before the war, studied my chart of the middle basin for a long time and then tapped one channel twice with a knuckle like a judge bringing a gavel down.

"This one," he said. "In the big flood year, my father took the wounded through here at night. Nobody else knew it. Now everybody knows it." He looked at me with eyes gone pale as river-pearl. "That is a good thing you've done. Don't let it be the last."

I promised him nothing, because promises were currency I'd devalued long ago. But I wrote it in the back of my notebook anyway, and I found I wanted to keep that particular promise, and wanting to keep a promise was such a novel sensation that I sat with it a while, like warmth from a fire.

* * *

The Voss brothers' letter arrived in the fourth month, carried upriver by a trader with the apologetic manner of a man delivering bad news he'd been paid to soften. I recognized the envelope before I touched it—heavy cream paper, absurd in the tropics, already wilting in the humidity like everything else that didn't belong here.

I didn't open it. I put it under a stone in Ila's house and went out and worked until dark, and then I sat on the bank with Tomas and told him about it.

"They'll want interest," I said. "Or they'll want me. Or they'll write something courteous and poisonous, the way they do. Marek's report will have reached them by now—the salvage rights, the gear, the ledger. They got their payment. But men like that don't close accounts, they just rotate them."

"And if they come again?"

I turned that over honestly, because lying to Tomas had become a habit I was actively trying to break, like biting my nails or expecting the worst.

"Then they come," I said. "And they find me harder to collect than I used to be. I'm not worth much on paper anymore, Tomas. No gear, no rights, no name on any ledger. What I'm worth now—" I gestured at the charts drying on Ila's line, at the boats pulled up safe above the flood line because of them, at the village where children had started calling out my name when I passed, not *waitress* or *outsider* or *that woman* but *Wren*, my actual name, used the way you use the names of people who belong—"what I'm worth now doesn't fit in an envelope."

Tomas was quiet a moment. Then he said, "Your mother wrote letters too. To the mission, sometimes. Asking about schools for me, when my father died." He smiled at the river. "Heavy paper. Same as yours. I burned mine."

"Why are you telling me that?"

"So you know burning is allowed." He bumped his shoulder against mine. "Whenever you're ready. Not before."

I wasn't ready yet. But knowing the option existed changed the weight of the thing under its stone. A letter you must answer is a chain. A letter you may burn is just paper, waiting to learn what it really is.

* * *

There was a night in the deep middle of the season—it might have been February; the calendar had dissolved somewhere around week six—when the rain stopped entirely, for the first time in weeks, and the clouds tore open, and the whole basin filled with moonlight like a bowl filling with milk.

Everyone came outside. It was that kind of event. The village stood along the banks in the silver hush, and the river ran high and slow and gleaming, and somewhere a night bird said the one thing night birds say.

Tomas found me at the end of the landing stage, where I'd gone to check the gauge out of pure habit and then stayed because the world had turned into something worth not leaving.

"My mother used to bring me out on nights like this," he said. "After the worst of the wet. She said the river forgives the sky eventually. Everything the sky takes, the river gives back to the sea, and the sea gives it back to the sky, and everyone apologizes to everyone."

"That's not how the water cycle works."

"No," he agreed. "But it's how marriage works, my aunt says, so perhaps both are true."

I laughed, and he caught my hand, and we stood there while the moon did its patient work on the water.

"Wren." He said my name carefully, the way he said it when it mattered. "You know you could go. Dry season's coming. Boats south, planes from Wewak. You could be anywhere by April. Nobody here would think less of you. Some of them would throw a feast."

"I know."

"You could be anywhere," he said again, "and you're here. On a landing stage. In the wet season. Checking a stick in the water." His thumb moved over my knuckles. "I need to hear you say why. Once. Plainly. Because I've loved you since the day you lied to me about how much trouble you were in, and I have learned to wait—you come from a long line of women who make people wait—but I am not a patient man by nature, Wren Blackwood, and the monsoon has tested even my considerable virtues."

I looked at the river. I looked at him. Somewhere in between the two, I found the truth, and it was simpler than anything I'd ever said to anyone.

"I spent my whole life running toward weather," I said, "because it was the only thing that ever matched what was happening inside me. And then I came here, and the biggest storm of my life hit, and for the first time I didn't want to chase it or outrun it or sell tickets to it. I wanted to stand in it. With you." I turned my hand over and laced my fingers through his. "I'm not staying because I can't leave, Tomas. I'm staying because I finally can, and I choose this. Every wet, rotten, miraculous day of it."

The night bird said its one thing again, approvingly, I chose to believe.

Tomas kissed me under the torn-open sky, and the river ran below us, carrying everything the sky had taken back toward the sea, everyone apologizing to everyone, nothing left to forgive.

* * *

Dry season came the way it always does—not arriving so much as being revealed, the clouds lifting gradually like a curtain drawn by a hand that wanted you to see the change slowly. The river dropped. The banks emerged, glistening, rearranged. My charts needed revising within a fortnight, which pleased me more than I can reasonably explain.

I was re-sounding the channel at Keram Bend with Onei's grandson when the trader came upriver again, and I saw the cream-colored envelope in his hand from two hundred meters, and my stomach did the old familiar drop—the debt-drop, the Marek-drop, the drop that had governed my life for years.

But then I saw the rest of what the trader was carrying: a parcel, wrapped in oilcloth, addressed in Ila's hand. And Ila herself was waiting at the landing, straight-backed, wearing her formal shell ornaments, the ones she wore for naming days and reckonings.

"A letter," she said, taking the envelope from the trader and weighing it in her palm like a stone she was deciding whether to skip or keep. "From the men who follow money the way sharks follow blood."

"You know about them?"

"I have known about them longer than you have been alive." She handed the envelope to me, and her eyes held mine, dark and level and absolutely certain. "Read it if you need to. Then we burn it on the beach tonight, your mother's daughter, and you walk back up this river with me, and we finish what our family started."

The envelope was heavy in my hand. Cream paper, foreign stamp, the whole soft machinery of a world I'd escaped.

Downstream, the sea waited with all its patience. Upstream, the river ran clear and low through country that knew my name.

I tucked the letter into my shirt, unopened, and picked up my sounding line.

"There's a channel past the bend," I said, "that isn't on anyone's chart yet. Come and show me where your mother used to cross."

And we went upriver together, into the dry season, into whatever came next.
