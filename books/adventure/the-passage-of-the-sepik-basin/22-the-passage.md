## 22. The Passage

The dry season came to the Sepik the way good news comes after a long illness: gradually at first, so you don't trust it, and then all at once.

Wren noticed it in the color of the river first. All through the monsoon months the water had run the brown of strong tea left too long, swollen and muscular, carrying whole trees like matchsticks. Now it had dropped back inside its banks and turned a deep, patient green, and the sandbars it had swallowed in February were surfacing one by one like whales coming up to breathe. The reed houses along the shore stood higher out of the water every week. Children who had spent the wet season paddling between doorways were suddenly walking again, barefoot and indignant about mud.

She noticed it in herself, too. Her hands had stopped shaking. Somewhere in the last three months—the exact day she couldn't name—her body had decided it was allowed to rest.

"You're smiling at the water," Tomas said from the stern of the boat. "That's either love or heatstroke."

"It's November," Wren said. "It's thirty-one degrees and I'm wearing a shirt I've mended four times. If this is heatstroke, it's very committed."

"Good. Stay committed." He poled them around a half-submerged log with the lazy precision of a man who had done it ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more without ever once being bored. "Ila wants us at the village by dark. She says there's a thing arriving on the coastal trader tomorrow, and she wants to be standing next to you when it does."

Wren's stomach did a small, practiced drop. Three months ago that sentence would have sent her reaching for her pack and counting escape routes. Now she just felt the drop, named it, and let it pass through her like weather.

"That's ominous."

"Ila is never ominous. Ila is *specific*. There's a difference." Tomas glanced at her, and his mouth twitched. "Also she's absolutely ominous. But she's your mother, so you have to pretend otherwise."

*Your mother.* The words still landed strangely, like a key that fit a lock she hadn't known was on her. Some mornings she woke in the reed house and heard Ila singing to the river before dawn—an old, low song, half prayer, half complaint—and for a moment she was a child again in a house she'd never lived in, being sung to by a voice she'd been listening for her whole life without knowing it.

Other mornings she was just a woman with bad knees and a debt history, and that was fine too. That was the miracle of it, actually. Both things could be true. She could be no one's daughter and Ila's daughter. She could be a woman who had once signed her name to ruin and a woman who now charted safe passages through flood country for villages that needed them. The wilderness, she had learned, forgave nothing—but it also kept no ledger. It simply asked, every morning, what you would do today.

The trader came into the delta two days later, riding the last of the morning tide, its hull streaked with salt and rust and the accumulated gossip of the coast.

Wren saw the mail sack come ashore and felt nothing in particular, which was itself remarkable. Mail, in her former life, had been a weapon. Bills shaped like knives. Final notices shaped like final notices. She had flinched at envelopes the way other people flinched at gunfire.

This envelope was different only in that it was worse. She knew that before she opened it. She knew it from the paper—heavy, cream-colored, absurdly expensive—and from the handwriting on the front, which was elegant, unhurried, and addressed to *Ms. Wren Blackwood, care of Kerebu, Sepik Basin*, as though the Voss brothers' clerk had personally enjoyed writing each word.

Tomas watched her turn it over twice. "Bad news?"

"Worse. Polite news." She sat down on the beach driftwood, because some things you should receive sitting down. "It's from the Voss brothers."

He went still. Not dramatically—Tomas never did anything dramatically—but the way the river goes still over a deep hole. "You don't owe them anymore. Marek took everything. Your gear, your salvage rights, your name on the ledger. You told me. You made me a list. It was a very thorough list."

"He took what I *had*." Wren worked her thumb under the flap. "What I *owed* was always a separate question. That's how they work. They're not collectors, Tomas. They're accountants with fists."

The letter inside was one page. Of course it was one page. Men like the Voss brothers never needed two pages; two pages implied doubt.

---

*Dear Ms. Blackwood,*

*It is our pleasure to inform you that the outstanding balance on your account has been settled in full, together with all accrued interest, penalties, and administrative costs. We are advised that the recovery achieved through the disposition of your salvage interests exceeded projections, and we are pleased to consider the matter closed.*

*We note with interest reports of your recent activities in the Sepik region and wish you continued success in your charting enterprise. New Guinea is a large place, but not, as you know better than most, an infinite one.*

*Please accept our compliments on your survival. It was, frankly, better than expected.*

*With warm regards and no further claims,*
*V. & A. Voss*

---

She read it twice. Then she read it a third time, hunting for the hook in it, the clause, the trapdoor—and found exactly what she'd known she'd find. Nothing. Nothing except *New Guinea is a large place, but not, as you know better than most, an infinite one,* which was not a threat the way a knife is a threat. It was a threat the way a horizon is a threat. It simply stated a fact and let you do the arithmetic yourself.

"They waived the interest," she said aloud, because saying it made it realer. "They *waived interest*. The Voss brothers have never waived interest in the history of money. They charged their own grandmother interest. Tomas, they charged *me* interest on a loan I took out to pay interest."

"So it's finished."

"It's finished." She laughed, and it came out wrong, half a sob dressed in a party dress. "It's finished, and they *congratulated me on my survival*. Like I'm a horse that didn't break its leg."

Tomas sat down beside her on the driftwood. He didn't take the letter from her or ask to read it. He just looked out at the water, where the tide was turning and the light was doing that late-afternoon thing it did here, gilding everything without promising to keep it.

"What will you do?" he asked.

And Wren looked down at the cream-colored paper, at the elegant handwriting, at the courtesy wrapped around the warning like silk around a brick, and she understood something with total clarity.

The letter wasn't a bill. It was a bookmark. It was the Voss brothers placing a small, polite flag in the map of her life that said *we know where you are.* They would never come. Probably. Almost certainly. But they wanted her to carry them with her—to feel the flag every time she planned something ambitious, every time she thought about leaving, every time she imagined a future with edges beyond this river. Debt closed, they'd say. But people who had survived the Voss brothers carried the Voss brothers. That was the actual product they sold. Not money. Occupancy.

Three months ago she would have burned the letter anyway, but she'd have burned it angry, and anger is just fear wearing boots. She'd have stood there on the sand hating them, and hating them would have been a way of keeping them, and keeping them was the one thing she swore she'd never do again.

"Well," she said. "First, I'm going to need a fire."

* * *

Ila insisted on witnessing it, naturally. Ila insisted on witnessing everything now—it was, Wren suspected, the compensation of a woman who had missed forty years of her daughter's life and intended to miss nothing further, including, apparently, the ceremonial destruction of correspondence.

"A fire needs a reason," Ila said, arranging herself on the sand with the gravity of a supreme court. "Otherwise it's just burning."

"The reason is that these men put a flag in my life and I'm taking it back."

"Mm." Ila considered this. "A good reason. Also, your knots are still terrible. While the fire is going, you should practice."

"You're supposed to be supportive. There's literature about this. Mothers are contractually obligated to be supportive."

"I supported you through a bullet graze, a landslide, and a monsoon. I am permitted one knot critique per season." But her hand found Wren's shoulder and stayed there, warm and heavy, and that was the support, really. It always had been. Ila's love came disguised as logistics.

Tomas built the fire the way he did everything—without fuss, without wasted motion, feeding the driftwood until it caught and stood up bright against the dusk. The village children gathered at a respectful distance, because fires on beaches were interesting and because everyone in the village understood by now that when the foreign woman and the old spirit-keeper did something together, it was worth watching. Word had traveled, over the monsoon months, the way word travels in river country: slowly, inaccurately, and eventually correctly. The woman who had taken the stone from the cave had given it back. The woman who had owed blood money had paid it in rivers instead. The spirit-keeper's lost daughter had come home by the hard road.

None of it was quite true. All of it was true enough.

Wren stood, letter in hand, and found she wanted to say something first. Not for the crowd—for herself. For the girl in Cairns with the shaking hands and the bravado like cheap armor, who had signed a salvage contract as cover for running away, who had walked into a cave full of bones and come out holding a stone she thought was her ticket out.

"This is from two men who measure everything," she said, mostly to the fire. "Money, time, people. Everything's a number to them, and every number has a price. And I spent most of my life being one of their numbers. Being nobody, and charging myself accordingly." She turned the paper over once, feeling its expensive weight. "But it turns out you can pay a debt with a river. Who knew. Nobody in Cairns knew."

"She's stalling," Ila observed to the crowd.

"I'm *savoring*."

"Savor faster. The tide's coming in."

Wren laughed—really laughed, the kind that came up from somewhere below the ribs—and touched the corner of the letter to the flame.

It caught reluctantly, cream paper curling brown at the edges, the elegant handwriting blistering first at the *V* of the signature. She held it until the heat reached her fingers, then dropped it onto the coals, where it flared up, shrank, and became a small black ghost that lifted on the sea breeze and was gone over the water.

The children cheered. One of them, a boy named Simon who had appointed himself Wren's shadow since the landslide, applauded with the sincere enthusiasm of someone who had no idea what had just been destroyed and didn't care. Fires were fires.

"There," Wren said quietly. "Now it's infinite."

Tomas looked at her sideways. "What?"

"Nothing. Inside joke with two men who'll never hear it."

* * *

They stayed on the beach until full dark, because nobody wanted to be the first to leave, and because the dry-season sky here was a spectacle that made even Ila go quiet. No clouds. No haze. Just stars stacked on stars, dense as sago, the Milky Way laid across the dark like a spilled river of its own.

"The wet season comes again," Ila said eventually. It wasn't a warning. It was a calendar. "In five months. The river will rise, and the cave will open, and the traders will ask for charts."

"And I'll make them," Wren said. "Better ones than last year. I got the eastern channels wrong by half a kilometer. Simon nearly drowned proving it to me."

"Simon nearly drowned because Simon is Simon," Ila said. "But yes. Make them better."

Wren leaned back on her hands and let herself feel it—not happiness exactly, nothing so simple, but the thing underneath happiness, the load-bearing wall of it. Safety. Not the temporary kind she'd chased her whole life, the kind you bought with forged signatures and fast boats, but the earned kind. The kind that came from having crossed a mountain range, buried a debt, returned a stone, and been named kin by a woman who sang to the river at dawn.

Somewhere out past the reef, the sea breathed against the sand. Inland, behind the black line of the treeline, the Sepik moved in the dark, doing what it had always done—carrying everything toward the coast and forgetting none of it.

*The wilderness forgives nothing,* she thought. That was the first lesson, and it had cost her blood to learn it. But there was a second lesson underneath it, one she'd only found on the far side of the Finisterres, in a fever-haze above the cloud line with Tomas's weight on her shoulders: the wilderness also *keeps* nothing. Every mistake washed downstream. Every debt dissolved in the wet season and laid down fresh as silt. You didn't get forgiven here. You got something better—you got another morning, and the river asking, the same question it always asked:

*What will you do today?*

She stood up and brushed the sand from her knees.

"You're going somewhere," Tomas said.

"I'm going upriver." She said it easily, testing the weight of it, and found it weighed nothing at all. "The dry season's the only window to survey the middle channels before the water drops too far to run the shallows. Ila's got half the village convinced I can predict storms, and I'd hate to disappoint them by being useless in fair weather."

"At night?"

"At dawn. I want to see the water at first light." She held out a hand and pulled him up off the driftwood, and neither of them let go afterward, because there was no longer any reason to pretend there was. "Come on, guide. Bring the pole. I'll bring the charts."

"And if the river asks what we'll do today?"

Wren looked upriver, where the current ran green and patient under the stars, toward the reed houses and the cave and the mountains she'd crossed and the whole long passage of her life that had somehow, against every odd she'd ever bet on, brought her here.

"Same thing we do every day," she said, and stepped into the boat. "Pay attention."

The current took them gently, the way it takes everything home.
