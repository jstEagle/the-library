## 1. The Debt in the Drawer

The cranes at Port Moresby's main wharf moved like patient herons, dipping and rising, dipping and rising, hauling containers the color of rust and old blood off ships that smelled of diesel and distant places. Ulyana Okafor had spent four years among them, and in that time she had learned that the harbor told you everything you needed to know about a person by how they carried a rope. The tourists gripped it like it might bite. The sailors coiled it without looking. The men who came down from the highlands to find work held it too tight, as if letting go meant admitting they'd already lost something.

She was coiling a hawser thick as her forearm when the letter found her.

It came the way most bad news comes — folded into an envelope that had been handled by too many people, passed from the harbormaster's office to the tally clerk to the boy who ran messages along the quay, until it arrived in her calloused hand smelling of other people's cigarettes. Her name was typed on it. *U. Okafor, Stevedore, Berth 4.* No title. No *Miss*. Just the name, like a weight dropped on a scale.

She finished the coil first. That was a discipline her mother had taught her before everything else: finish the work, then face the ruin. Her mother had died believing that. Her father had never learned it at all.

Under the corrugated shade of the shed, she opened the envelope with a thumbnail. Two sheets. The first was heavy cream paper, lawyer's stock, the kind of paper that cost more per page than she made in an hour.

*Mrs. Ulyana Okafor,* it began, which was wrong on two counts, and she felt the old anger rise in her throat like bile. *We write regarding your father, Dr. Emeka Okafor, presently leading an archaeological expedition on the Fly River system, Western Province.*

*It is our understanding that contact with the expedition has been lost for a period exceeding nineteen days. Our client, whose interests are secured against certain advances made to Dr. Okafor, has requested we inform you that, in the event of Dr. Okafor's incapacity or death, responsibility for the outstanding sum — together with accrued interest — passes to his next of kin.*

*The outstanding sum is currently One Hundred and Twelve Thousand Kina.*

*We would advise you to treat this matter with urgency.*

There was a signature at the bottom, a name she didn't bother to read, and beneath it, stamped in red ink like a wound: *KESSEL HOLDINGS LTD.*

Ulyana read the letter three times. Around her the dock went on breathing — the groan of cargo, the gulls fighting over fish heads, someone laughing near the fuel pier. Nineteen days silent. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Nineteen days up the Fly River was not silence. Nineteen days up the Fly River was a grave with the water still running over it.

The second sheet was thinner, softer, wrapped in tissue. It had come separately, forwarded through the same office, and when she unfolded it something small and heavy slid into her palm.

The compass.

Her father's brass compass, the one he'd carried since before she was born, the one he'd let her hold once, only once, on the porch of their house in Lae when she was nine years old, turning slowly in place while he watched her face instead of the needle. *"It doesn't point north, little one,"* he'd said. *"It points where I need to go."* She had thought that was magic then. Later she'd understood it was just what men like him said instead of *I'm leaving.*

The case was warm from the tissue paper, dented along one edge where it had clearly taken a fall. The hinge worked. Inside, under glass, the needle swung in slow, drunken circles — north, east, south, west, around again — never settling, never resting, like something trapped and panicking.

She held it flat. She turned her body until she knew — the way any dockworker knows, in the bones — exactly where north lay, past the breakwater, out over the Coral Sea. The needle ignored her. It spun its useless carousel and pointed everywhere and nowhere.

"Broken," she said aloud, and a tally clerk nearby glanced over, and she closed her fist around the compass until the brass bit her skin.

* * *

That night she sat in her room above the chandlery with the letter spread on the table beside a bowl of rice she wasn't eating. The room was one of the reasons she stayed at the docks — cheap, loud, and honest. You could hear every ship coming and going, and no one pretended the world was anything but work.

One hundred and twelve thousand kina. She earned eleven kina a day when the ships came, less when they didn't. She did the division twice because the first answer seemed like a joke. Ten years. Ten years of coiling rope and signing manifests, if she paid nothing else — no food, no roof, nothing — to settle a debt contracted by a man who hadn't called her in six years, who had missed her mother's funeral because, according to the university's polite letter, *field commitments prevented Dr. Okafor's return at this time.*

Field commitments. She turned the phrase over. Somewhere up a brown river in the far west of the country, her father had chosen mud and old bones over his daughter's grief, and now the mud and the old bones were sending her bills.

And yet.

She picked up the compass again. Under the bare bulb it looked older than she remembered, the brass worn to the color of dark honey, the engraving on the back nearly rubbed away. She tilted it to catch the light. *E.O. — F.R. 1974.* Her father's initials. The Fly River. The year he first went up it, young and hungry, before the degrees, before the funders, before the leaving became a habit.

The needle still wouldn't settle. She watched it swing and thought: *compasses don't break like this.* A broken needle sticks. A broken needle drags. This one moved like it was alive, like it was searching, like somewhere ahead of it there was a thing it wanted and couldn't reach.

Or like something near it was pulling wrong.

She set it down and pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. There was a version of this night where she walked to the harbormaster tomorrow, handed him the letter, and said: find whoever's next of kin after me, because I'm not it. He stopped being my father the day my mother went into the ground without him. Let Kessel Holdings chase a ghost. Let the river keep him.

That version was clean. That version let her sleep.

But she kept seeing the needle spin. And underneath the anger — beneath the ten years of unanswered letters and the empty chair at the funeral and the birthday calls that stopped when she turned twenty-two — underneath all of it was a memory she hated: her father crouched in the red dirt behind their house in Lae, showing her how to read the ground, how a slight greening of the grass could mean a wall buried beneath, how the earth keeps secrets but leaves receipts. *Everything buried wants to be found, Uli. That's why we dig.* He'd said it like a confession. She'd remembered it like a curse.

Nineteen days of silence. A debt with her name on it. A compass that pointed nowhere, mailed to her by lawyers, as if even her father's abandonment had arrived secondhand.

If he was dead, the debt collectors would come for her, and she would fight them, and she would win or lose, but either way she'd do it knowing. If he was alive —

If he was alive, then he was up that river guarding whatever he'd found, and something had gone wrong enough to cut off nineteen days of transmissions from a man who filed reports the way other men breathed.

She got up, pulled the tin cash box from under her bed, and counted. Four hundred and sixty kina in notes, some coins, a ferry ticket stub she kept for no reason. Not enough for passage up the Fly. Not enough for supplies, for a guide, for anything. Enough, maybe, to get to the delta town where the river boats gathered, where men with leaky vessels took money from fools.

She looked at the compass on the table, spinning its slow circles in the lamplight.

"I'm not coming to save you," she told it, and her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated herself for that too. "I'm coming to settle accounts."

Outside, a ship's horn sounded, long and low, heading out past the breakwater toward open water. Somewhere upcountry, past the mountains, past the savannah, a river the width of a sea was rolling down through the rainforest toward the same ocean, carrying silt and secrets and, if the letter was right, the wreckage of her father's last ambition.

Ulyana put the compass in her jacket pocket, where it sat against her ribs like a second heartbeat, still searching, still refusing to rest. Then she packed a bag: rope knife, rain shell, the photograph of her mother she'd never once shown anyone, and the letter, folded sharp.

In the morning she would go to the boat yards at the edge of the harbor district, where the salvage crews drank and the hulls rotted and every captain had a price. She'd heard the names already, in the way dockworkers hear things — half gossip, half warning. Boats that ran the middle reaches of the Fly. Men who asked few questions for the right money.

At the door she paused, and the needle inside her pocket gave one final lazy sweep, and for half a second — she would swear this later, swear it on her mother's grave — it stopped. Held steady. Pointed northwest, upriver, into the country where the maps went thin.

Then it started spinning again, and she stepped out into the dark, and locked the door behind her.
