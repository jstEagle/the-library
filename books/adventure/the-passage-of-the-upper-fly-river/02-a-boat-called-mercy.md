## 2. A Boat Called Mercy

The boat was called the *Mercy*, and she was dying.

Ulyana saw that before she saw anything else about her — the way she sat low in the brown water at the Kiunga docks, listing to port like a drunk leaning on a friend, her paint gone the grey of old fish scales, her name stenciled on the bow in letters that had faded to a whisper. Water wept from a seam near the waterline in a thin, constant thread, as if the river had already gotten inside her and was letting itself out slowly, patient as a debt collector.

"You're the one who wants upriver."

The voice came from behind her, low and unhurried. Ulyana turned. The man was broad through the shoulders and narrow through the rest, as if the river had carved away everything it didn't need. His skin was the deep brown of wet teak, his hair iron-grey at the temples, and his eyes — she would remember this later, when it mattered — were the flat, unreadable brown of the Fly itself in dry season. Calm on top. Something moving underneath.

"Tevita," he said, and did not offer a hand. He looked at her the way a man looks at weather he doesn't trust.

"Ulyana Okafor." She let the name sit there, watched for a flicker. There was one — a small contraction around his eyes, gone almost before it arrived. So he knew the name. Good. That meant he'd know why she was here, and she wouldn't have to explain her father, and the letter, and the debt, and the compass sitting in her jacket pocket with its needle spinning slow, lazy circles like a thing drunk or dying or lying.

"Kiunga's a long way up," Tevita said. "And past Kiunga it's no way at all, unless you count the way the crocodiles count it."

"I can pay."

"Everyone can pay." He turned and walked up the gangplank, which was less a gangplank than two planks and a prayer, and Ulyana, after a moment's fury at being dismissed, followed.

* * *

The deck of the *Mercy* was a country with three inhabitants.

The first was a mountain. He sat on an ammunition crate near the stern with a rifle across his knees — not cradling it, not showing it off, just letting it exist, the way a scar exists. He was pale in the way of a man who'd been in the tropics long enough to stop burning and start tanning badly, and he had the stillness of someone who had learned that movement attracts attention. He looked at Ulyana once, catalogued her, and looked away. She had the distinct feeling of having been filed.

"Bram," Tevita said, by way of introduction, and Bram gave a nod that might have meant *hello* or might have meant *I know seventeen ways to stop your heart* and had not yet decided which to communicate.

The second inhabitant was underneath the deck. Ulyana found her by following a stream of profanity in three languages down a hatch and into an engine room that smelled of diesel, rust, and hot metal. A woman was buried to the shoulders in the guts of the engine, only her legs visible, one foot tapping a rhythm against the deck plates like a metronome keeping time with the swearing.

"You're leaking," Ulyana said.

The tapping stopped. The legs withdrew, and a woman emerged — broad-faced, sun-dark, with grease to the elbows and a smudge of it across one cheekbone like war paint. She looked Ulyana up and down with the frank appraisal of someone pricing a used part.

"Of course I'm leaking," she said. "She's a boat. Boats leak. The trick is deciding where." She wiped her hands on a rag that was filthier than her hands, which took some doing. "Sefina."

"Ulyana."

"Passenger?"

"Charter."

"Mm." Sefina's eyes flicked toward the hatch, toward the deck, toward Tevita somewhere above, and Ulyana understood that the word *charter* had just been entered into a ledger somewhere, to be weighed later.

The third inhabitant found her rather than the reverse. Ulyana was back on deck, studying the river — wide here, slow, the color of milky tea, carrying whole islands of floating vegetation past the docks like it was in no hurry to get anywhere, which was a lie, because her father's last letter had described the Fly as a snake wearing a river's clothes — when a voice at her elbow said, "You're going where he went."

She turned. A young man, maybe twenty, thin as a whipstock, with headphones around his neck and a portable radio rig cradled under one arm like a baby. His eyes were too big for his face and too quick for his years.

"Junie," he said. "Radio. I heard the traffic this morning. About the expedition." He said the word carefully, the way you'd handle a wet grenade. "The one that went quiet."

"You heard traffic about it?"

"Everyone's heard traffic about it. Port Moresby's been buzzing for a week. Planes that don't file flight plans. A boat with no name running fuel up the Middle Reach." He lowered his voice, though there was no one within thirty feet. "And a man named Kessler asking questions at every fuel stop between here and the gorge. The wrong kind of questions. The kind you ask when you're not looking for someone, you're looking for what someone *found*."

The compass in her pocket seemed to grow heavier. Ulyana kept her face still, an old skill from a childhood spent across dinner tables from a father who watched for reactions the way other men watched the horizon.

"I'm looking for my father," she said. "That's all."

Junie looked at her for a long moment with those too-quick eyes, and she had the uncomfortable sense of being listened to rather than looked at — of being a frequency he was tuning.

"Sure," he said, in a tone that meant *no*, and wandered off with his radio.

* * *

They cast off at first light, and Ulyana learned the *Mercy* the way you learn a person with a limp — by watching where she hurt.

The engine had a stutter in it, a hitch every fourth revolution, and she could hear Sefina talking to it through the deck, a low constant muttering, *come on, girl, come on, not today*. The boat took the current at an angle, crabbing upstream, and Tevita stood at the helm with one hand on the wheel and the other holding a chipped enamel mug of tea, steering mostly with his knees, his weight, his attention. He didn't look like a man operating a vessel. He looked like a man in conversation with one.

Ulyana climbed to the wheelhouse on the second morning, when the banks had narrowed and the jungle had come down to the water's edge like a crowd pressing against a barrier, and said, "You knew my father's name."

Tevita didn't look away from the river. "Everyone knows the name Okafor. He's been running up and down this river for fifteen years with a shovel and a permit."

"That's not what I asked."

The wheel turned a few degrees. A half-submerged log the size of a church pew slid past the bow, close enough to touch, and the *Mercy* shrugged it off with a shudder.

"No," Tevita said. "It isn't." He drank his tea. Ulyana waited. She was good at waiting; she'd had a childhood of it, waiting for a father to come home from the field, waiting for letters, waiting for the phone call that was always about the work and never about her. She could out-wait a statue.

Finally, without turning: "There's a man named Kessler. Works for people who buy things that aren't for sale. He's on the river ahead of us, or behind us — with him it's hard to tell, because he likes it that way. He has a fast boat and slow manners." A pause. "He's asking about your father's camp. And he's asking about a compass."

The river made a long, green hiss against the hull. Ulyana's hand went, without her permission, to her jacket pocket.

"Brass case," Tevita said, still watching the water. "Old. The lid's got a bird engraved on it — some kind of eagle, but wrong. Local work, very old. The needle doesn't point north."

"How do you know that?"

"Because your father showed it to me once. Six years ago." He set down the mug. "He was drunk, which he wasn't often, and scared, which he was even less. He said he'd found a door, and the compass was the key, and he'd made a mistake taking it." Tevita finally looked at her, and his eyes were the river: calm on top, something moving underneath. "I told him to give it back. He said he would. Men say things."

Ulyana's mouth had gone dry. "A door to what?"

"That," said Tevita, "is the question Kessler's boat is going to be asking all the way up the Middle Reach. And when asking stops working, he'll move on to other methods." He turned back to the wheel. "You want passage to your father, I'll take you. But understand what you bought. This isn't a rescue. This is a race. And the *Mercy* —" he patted the wheel housing the way you'd pat an old dog — "she's not fast. But she's stubborn. Stubborn has beaten fast on this river before."

* * *

That evening they tied up against the bank in a backwater where the current went soft and the mosquitoes rose off the water in columns like smoke. Sefina cooked rice and tinned fish on a deck stove, and the four of them — five, counting Ulyana, not quite counting her — ate in a circle of lamplight while the jungle screamed and chattered around them.

It was Sefina who broke the silence first, pointing her spoon at Ulyana. "You patched a seam today. Port side, midships. I saw the caulking." A pause. "It was terrible."

"I've never caulked anything in my life."

"I could tell." But there was something in her voice that wasn't mockery, or wasn't only mockery. "You'll do it again tomorrow, and it'll be less terrible. That's how it works. The boat teaches you or the boat sinks you. No third option."

Bram said nothing at all. He ate the way he did everything — efficiently, without pleasure or complaint — and when he finished he cleaned the rifle's action with a cloth, piece by piece, and Ulyana caught Junie watching Bram watch the river, and caught herself watching Junie, and understood that everyone on this boat was watching everyone else, and that this was not paranoia. It was seamanship.

"Your father," Junie said quietly, across the lamplight. "The expedition. What was he looking for?"

Ulyana turned her spoon in the tin plate. "He never told me. He never told me anything." The resentment came up her throat, familiar as bile, and she let some of it out, because these strangers had fed her and it seemed like the least she owed them. "Fifteen years of digging holes in other people's countries. Fifteen years of letters that read like grant applications. I got a lawyer's letter last week instead of a phone call. He owes money to people who break fingers, and apparently now I owe it too, because I'm what's left." She looked up. "I'm not here to rescue him. I'm here to make him explain himself."

The circle was quiet. Somewhere across the water a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"Good," said Sefina, and went back to her rice.

* * *

Later, when the lamp was out and the others had turned in, Ulyana sat on the foredeck with her back against a crate and took out the compass.

The moon was a thin nail-paring overhead, and in its light the brass case glowed dully, the engraved eagle spreading wings that were not quite an eagle's — too long in the body, too clever in the eye. She opened the lid. The needle swung, drifted, swung again, and settled — pointing not north but upriver, into the dark where the gorge waited and her father waited and something waited that a man had called a door.

She thought about what Tevita had said. *He made a mistake taking it.* Her father, who had lectured her at fourteen about the ethics of excavation, who had written papers on custodianship and consent, had stolen this. Had lied. And the needle lay in its bowl pointing upriver like an accusation.

A shadow moved at the edge of the lamplight from the wheelhouse. Tevita, standing his watch, said nothing. But as she closed the lid, she heard him speak softly, to the boat or the river or himself:

"Fast boat behind us," he said. "Two days, maybe three."

Ulyana put the compass away, and the *Mercy* creaked against her mooring lines, and the river ran on toward the gorge in the dark, patient as a debt collector, carrying them all toward whatever it kept.
