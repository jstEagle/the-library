## 3. Static on the Middle Reach

Junie had a rule about static: it always meant something. Silence on a radio was nothing — a dead set, a dead battery, a dead man. But static was alive, a hiss of electrons arguing with the atmosphere, and somewhere inside that argument, if you were patient enough, voices lived.

She'd been listening to the middle reach for two days now, working the frequencies in slow rotation while the *Mercy* chugged upstream through water the color of steeped tea. The Fly at this stretch ran wide and braided, splitting around sandbars that shifted with every flood, and the banks were a green wall so dense it looked poured rather than grown. Junie sat in her corner of the wheelhouse with the radio rig Sefina had rebuilt twice since Port Moresby, headphones clamped over her ears, logging every blip and carrier wave in a school exercise book.

Mostly she logged nothing. Fishing boats chatter on 16. Mission stations check in at dawn and dusk. The syndicate boat — the one Tevita called *the grey boat* because nobody would say its real name — broadcast on encrypted bursts that came and went like birdsong.

Then, at 4:17 in the afternoon, with the light going amber and the rain holding its breath, the static changed.

Junie sat up straight. It wasn't weather-static, which rolls and breathes. This was mechanical, rhythmic, a stuttering pulse like a heart running too fast. She swept the dial, found the edge of it, tuned into the center, and put on the recorder.

"—say again, say again, this is *Kikori Star*, expedition vessel Okafor—"

Ulyana's head came up from where she'd been mending a torn awning seam in the stern. Junie saw her freeze, needle still in the canvas, and something crossed her face that Junie couldn't name — hope and dread wearing the same expression.

"Okafor, we read you," Junie keyed the mic. "This is salvage vessel *Mercy*. Dr. Okafor, do you copy?"

The voice that came back was young, male, cracked down the middle. "Whoever you are, listen. We're at the site. Coordinates follow. He won't stop transmitting them, he says people have to know, he says—" A burst of pure noise swallowed everything. Then, faintly: "—not alone out here. There's another boat. It's been circling since yesterday. If anyone receives this, don't come up the—"

The scream cut through the transmission like a machete through cane. Short. High. Ending in a wet sound Junie had heard once before, years ago, when a crocodile took a dog off the bank at Wewak, and she had never managed to forget it.

Then coordinates. A clean, fast burst of numbers, spoken by a different voice — older, precise, unhurried, a voice reading from a page. Ulyana's father's voice. Junie wrote them down without thinking, her hand steady even as her stomach turned to river water.

Then static. Then nothing. She swept every band until dark. Nothing.

* * *

They anchored that night in the lee of a cutbank, engines cold, mosquito coils burning. Nobody suggested they turn back. That was the thing about the *Mercy*'s crew, Junie thought — nobody ever suggested anything sensible. They just looked at each other across the wheelhouse lamp and understood.

Tevita spread the chart on the table and marked the coordinates with a grease pencil. His finger rested there a long time.

"That's not on any survey I've seen," he said finally. "Side channel. Limestone country. The kind of place the river eats."

"He's alive," Ulyana said. "That scream — someone screamed, but the transmission kept going. He kept reading the coordinates after." Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a woman holding a door shut against a flood. "He's alive."

"Someone else is alive too," Bram said from the doorway. He'd been quiet all day, cleaning his rifle with a cloth that never seemed to get it cleaner. "Or was. You heard it."

"The other boat," Sefina said. She was sitting on the engine cover, hands black to the wrist, though she hadn't touched the engine in an hour. "Grey hull, twin diesels, satellite dome. They passed us at Kiunga two days back doing twenty knots. We do eight on a good day." She looked at Tevita. "If they heard that transmission, they're already there."

"If they heard it," Tevita said, "they were close enough to make it."

Nobody said what everyone was thinking: that the scream and the second boat might be the same fact.

Junie closed her exercise book. In the margin, next to the coordinates, she'd written the time stamp and one word: *why?* Why transmit coordinates on an open band, begging rescue, unless you believed no rescue was coming otherwise? And why did the first voice sound less like a man calling for help than a man leaving evidence?

* * *

They reached the supply camp at midmorning of the second day, where a tributary mouth widened into a gravel bar backed by pandanus. Tevita brought the *Mercy* in slow, bow-first, and Bram went ashore first with the rifle slung, moving through the grass the way he moved through everything — like a man revisiting an old argument with the world.

The camp had been a proper one. Four tents on platforms, a generator shed, a drying rack, crates stacked under tarps. Now the tarps were shredded, the crates stood open and empty, and one of the tent platforms was burned down to blackened joists. Rain had been at everything, but rain doesn't scatter boxes across fifty meters of gravel. Rain doesn't pry open ammunition cases with a crowbar.

"Looted," Sefina said, crouching over a crate stenciled with a university crest. "But look — they took the fuel drums, the food, the radios. Left the books. Left the samples." She lifted a specimen jar of muddy water, unbroken, and set it down gently. "Thieves take what sells. These men took what matters."

"Or they were looking for something specific," Tevita said, "and didn't find it."

Junie drifted toward the generator shed, because sheds were where operators lived, and operators left traces. Inside, the bench was wrecked, the backup transmitter smashed with a single deliberate blow — not looted, executed. But taped underneath the bench, in the universal hiding spot of every operator who ever feared a visitor, she found a notebook wrapped in plastic.

She crouched there a moment before touching it. The plastic was dry. Taped recently. Whoever hid it had done it knowing someone was coming.

Inside, in a careful engineer's hand: dates, battery logs, transmission times. And on the last written page, three lines:

*K. came again. E.O. refuses to hand over the location. K. says the debt is called in either way. R. thinks we should give it up. E.O. says some things are worth dying in front of.*

*Last night the grey boat's searchlight swept the bar for two hours.*

*I am taping this where only another operator would look. God help us. He isn't here for the artifacts.*

Junie carried the notebook back to the others and read it aloud, because some things shouldn't be read silently. When she finished, the gravel bar was very quiet except for the river.

"E.O.," Ulyana said. Her father's initials. She was standing very straight, holding the mended awning canvas she'd brought ashore without noticing, and Junie watched her knuckles go white around it. "Emeka Okafor. Refuses to hand over the location." She laughed, one short note with no humor in it at all. "I spent six years telling myself he forgot I existed because the work mattered more. And here he is, dying for the work, exactly as advertised."

"You don't know he's dying," Sefina started.

"I know someone screamed on an open channel," Ulyana said, "and I know a man named K. told my father a debt was called in, and I know whose debt that is, because a lawyer sent me the paperwork." She turned to face them all, and Junie saw that the resentment Junie had watched her carry since Port Moresby — the armor she wore like a second skin — had cracked, and behind it was something worse than anger. Fear. "I thought we were coming to rescue him. Rescue implies somebody wants him alive."

Bram said quietly, "Now you understand why I asked about your insurance coverage back at the dock."

"Bram," Sefina warned.

"No. She should hear it plain." He slung the rifle properly, businesslike. "Syndicate crews don't leave witnesses. Not the scientists, not the camp, and not the salvage boat that comes asking questions afterward. We are not a rescue party anymore. We're loose ends floating upstream toward the men who tie them off."

* * *

They were back aboard by early afternoon, and that was when Junie, sweeping the bands out of habit more than hope, picked up the grey boat.

Not a transmission. A radar return — Sefina's cheap unit painting a contact astern, four kilometers back, holding their speed exactly. Holding it for an hour. When the *Mercy* slowed to thread a sandbar channel, the contact slowed. When they sped up, it sped up. Never closing. Never falling away.

"A shadow," Tevita said, studying the screen. His face gave away nothing, but his hands, on the wheel, had gone very still, and Junie had learned that Tevita's stillness was louder than most men's shouting.

"It knows we heard the broadcast," Junie said. "Everyone within three hundred klicks heard that broadcast. But we're the only ones who answered it on an open mic."

"So they know our name," Ulyana said.

"They knew our name at the dock," Tevita said.

The rain came again as the light failed, soft and endless, and the grey boat dissolved into the weather but stayed on the screen, a patient ghost four kilometers back. Junie stayed at the rig through the evening watch, logging, listening, and near midnight she caught one last fragment — a short encrypted burst, strong and close, addressed to no one she could identify. Before the encryption scrambled it, her recorder caught a half-second of carrier tone, and beneath the tone, almost lost, a voice speaking English with an accent like polished steel:

"—the daughter is aboard. That simplifies things."

Junie sat in the dark of the wheelhouse with her headphones on and her heart going like the *Mercy*'s engine at full throttle, and she made the oldest decision in the operator's book: some transmissions you log, and some you sit on until you understand them.

Upstream, past the black wall of the banks, her father's coordinates waited. Behind them, four kilometers of rain away, something patient waited too.

She wrote the time in her exercise book, and beside it, instead of *why?*, she wrote: *they want her, not him.*
