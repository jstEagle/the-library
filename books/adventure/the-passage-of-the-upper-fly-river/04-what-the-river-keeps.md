## 4. What the River Keeps

I have run the Fly for nineteen years, and I have learned that the river keeps two kinds of secrets: the ones it swallows, and the ones it saves for the people who deserve them least.

Ulyana Okafor stood at the bow rail that third morning, her father's brass compass open in her palm, watching the needle swing in slow, drunken circles that had nothing to do with north. She'd been at it for an hour. The rain had thinned to a fine grey mist that beaded on her braids and ran down the collar of the borrowed slicker she hadn't asked for but wore anyway.

"You're going to wear a hole in that deck," I told her.

She didn't look up. "The needle's wrong. It keeps pointing upriver."

"Then it's pointing home."

That earned me a look — sharp, measuring, the look of a woman deciding how much of a stranger she could afford to trust. I'd been on the receiving end of that look for three days now. I turned back to my wheel and let her keep it.

The truth was, I'd known her father's name before she ever set foot on the Mercy. I'd known it for eleven years, since the season the middle reach took a survey boat and gave me back to my family with a cracked rib and a debt I couldn't calculate. Dr. Emeka Okafor had gone into that brown floodwater after me when every other man on that bank had already written my eulogy. He'd held my head above the current for forty minutes against a snag, in water full of crocodiles, and when they pulled us both out he'd laughed — actually laughed — and said, *Now we're even, Tevita.*

We were not even. A man who pulls you from the river owns a piece of you the river never gets back. I'd told myself for eleven years that neutrality was the honest position for a boatman: cargo up, cargo down, no questions. Then his daughter walked up my gangplank with a lawyer's letter and a spinning compass, and neutrality became a thing I had to hold with both hands, like a wheel in a current.

Sefina came up from the engine well wiping her hands on a rag that had once been white. "Bearing's running hot on the port cylinder," she said. "She'll hold till the fuel stop if I baby her."

"Baby her," I said.

Junie leaned out of the radio shack, headphones around her neck. "Middle reach is loud today. Storm cell building west of the gorge. Weather service says it'll hit by late afternoon."

"Then we make the gorge before it does," I said, and pushed the throttle forward, and the Mercy groaned and answered, the way she always did, like an old woman asked to carry one load more.

* * *

We didn't make the gorge.

The squall came down out of the western ridges an hour early, the way monsoon squalls do — not arriving so much as *descending*, a black wall of rain that erased the far bank, the near bank, the sky, everything but the water hammering the deck and the sound of Sefina screaming engine instructions through the deck plate. I drove by memory and the feel of the current through the hull, and when the channel bent where it had no business bending — the river rearranging itself the way it does in high water — I made the only decision left and ran us for the limestone bluffs on the eastern side, where the maps showed overhangs and the old boatmen's talk remembered caves.

The Mercy slid into a mouth of black rock with a meter to spare on either side of the beam, and the storm closed behind us like a door.

We tied off to a stalagmite the size of a church pillar and stood in the sudden enormous quiet, dripping, listening to the rain roar past the cave mouth like a freight train that never finished passing. Bram got the lamp lit. Junie got a fire going from the dry driftwood stacked at the back — other boatmen had sheltered here before us, and left their leavings, and their luck. Sefina went below to check her engine. And Ulyana Okafor walked into the dark of the cave with the lamp, the way people do when a storm gives them nothing else to do with their fear.

I should have gone with her. That's what I tell myself now. Instead I stayed at the fire and let the debt sit in my chest like ballast.

She came back an hour later, and I knew from her face before she said anything that the cave had given something up. She was holding a waterproof pouch, river-stained, its canvas rotted at the seams. Inside was a single page — a field journal page, the handwriting cramped and urgent, the paper warped from years of wet.

She read it by firelight. I watched her read it, and I watched her face change, and I thought: *there it is. The river keeps its secrets, and it gives them to the daughters.*

* * *

"Read it," she said, when she could speak. Her voice came out flat, which is how grief sounds when it's still deciding what shape to take. She held the page out to me, and I took it because refusing would have been its own confession.

The handwriting was Emeka Okafor's. I'd seen it on shipping manifests years ago, on the crates I'd carried for him upriver in another life, before I understood what he was really hunting. The page read:

*Third survey, left bank gorge. The cavern is not a burial site. It is older — a repository, deliberately flooded, sealed. The gold work is beyond anything in the provincial records. If I report this accurately, the permits collapse, the funders descend, and the site will be stripped within the year. So I will report a minor find. I will log coordinates falsely. Let the river keep what it keeps. I did not come all this way to hand it to men like Kessler.*

*The compass was not mine to take. I know this. The custodians trusted me. I told myself I would return it. I am telling myself many things.*

Ulyana took the page back before I'd finished. "He lied," she said. "To his funders. To the permits. To—" She stopped. "To me. Every letter. Every Christmas. 'Nothing much to report, little one. The river is quiet.' He found a city and he buried it in paperwork, and he stole from people who trusted him, and he wrote it down in his own hand like it was a *confession* instead of a plan."

"Maybe it was both," I said.

"Don't defend him." The firelight made her eyes bright and hard. "You didn't know him. You don't get to—"

"I knew him," I said, before I could stop myself.

The cave went very quiet except for the storm. Bram and Junie had the good sense to find reasons to be at the other end of the fire. Ulyana turned to me fully, and I understood that whatever I said next would decide what the Mercy was to her for the rest of this voyage — a hired boat, or something else.

"Eleven years ago," I said, "the middle reach took my boat with me in it. Your father went in after me. Forty minutes against a snag, in floodwater, with crocodiles working the eddies. He held my head up until the bank party got a line to us. I owe him a life debt, Ulyana. In my family's custom, that debt doesn't expire and it doesn't transfer and it doesn't get forgiven. It gets *paid*."

"So that's why you took me on." Her voice was cold now, and I deserved the cold. "Not the money. Not the passage. A debt."

"That's why I took the job," I said. "Why I'm still here, with Kessler's boat behind us and the river rising, is something I haven't finished explaining to myself."

She stared at me a long moment. Then, quieter, in a voice that had cracks running through it like the limestone above us: "He stole the compass. From a family who trusted him. And he knew it was wrong, and he did it anyway, and he wrote it down, and he mailed it to me like — like a *bequest*. Like I was supposed to finish the theft."

"Or like he was trying to give it back," I said, "the only way he could — by putting it in the hands of someone who might be better than him."

She looked down at the compass in her lap. The needle had stopped its drunken spinning. It pointed steady and true upriver, into the gorge, into the dark where the cavern waited and where her father had gone silent three weeks ago.

"The custodians," she said. "The family he stole from. Do you know if they're still—"

"The gorge families keep their lines," I said. "If anyone survived his theft, they'll be upriver. Where everything else is."

She closed the compass. Her thumb moved over the worn brass, once, the way you'd touch a scar.

Outside, the storm was dying. I could hear the river dropping back into its channel, patient, unhurried, a thing that had already decided how this would end. I put another branch on the fire and didn't say what I was thinking, which was that a man who lies to funders to protect a site is not the same as a man who steals from a trusting family to keep it — and that Emeka Okafor had been both men at once, and that his daughter was going to have to decide which one she'd inherited.

Behind us, deeper in the cave, something shifted in the dark. Not stone. Not water.

Junie's head came up off her pack, headphones snapping around her neck. "Tevita," she said, very quietly. "I've got a signal. Weak. Repeating." She looked at the readout, then at me, and her face had gone pale in the firelight.

"It's the expedition's frequency. It's coming from upriver — and it's not a distress call." She swallowed. "It's a coordinates burst. Same one from the last transmission. And someone else is answering it."
