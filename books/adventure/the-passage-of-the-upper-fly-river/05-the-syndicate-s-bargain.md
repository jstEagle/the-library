## 5. The Syndicate's Bargain

The fuel stop at D'Albertis Junction was less a town than an apology — a shelf of corrugated tin and diesel-stained timber wedged between the river and the jungle, where the water ran wide and brown as brewed tea. A single pump on a rickety pontoon, a trade store selling warm SP lager and colder engine parts, and a veranda where three old men watched the Mercy come in with the flat, incurious eyes of people who had seen every kind of boat and every kind of trouble float past.

Ulyana stood at the rail as Tevita brought her alongside, one hand on the wheel, one hand raised to the fuel man in a lazy salute. The monsoon had given them two days of grudging mercy — high grey cloud, steam heat, the river running fast but honest. She'd used those days badly, she thought. She'd slept, eaten Sefina's tinned corned beef with actual appetite, and let herself almost forget that somewhere behind them, Kessler's white-hulled boat ran its engines through the night like a patient predator.

Almost.

"You're doing it again," Junie said beside her, not looking up from the radio log she kept in a school exercise book, her handwriting small and furious.

"Doing what?"

"Watching the river behind us like it owes you money."

"Maybe it does." Ulyana turned her back on the downstream current. "My father's up there. His expedition went silent eleven days ago. And a man who smells like money is following us upriver like we're leading him somewhere."

"Maybe you are," Junie said, and closed the book.

* * *

They took on four hundred litres of diesel and a case of drinking water that Sefina pronounced "technically wet," and while the hose gurgled into the tank Ulyana walked the pontoon to stretch legs that had forgotten land. The trade store's veranda boards were soft with rot. A dog of no fixed opinion lay in the shade of the fuel drum, watching her with one eye.

She heard the other boat before she saw it — twin diesels idling down, the particular expensive purr of machinery maintained by someone else's money. The white hull slid past the junction point and eased toward the far side of the pontoon, and Ulyana felt the temperature of the morning change, the way a room changes when a stranger walks into it.

Kessler came up the ladder alone. That was the first thing she noticed — he'd left his men aboard, four silhouettes arranged along his rail like chess pieces waiting for instruction. He wore a linen shirt that had never known sweat and carried nothing in his hands, which was its own kind of message. Men who carried weapons wanted something they could take. Men who carried nothing wanted something you'd give them.

"Miss Okafor." He stopped a polite three metres away, out of arm's reach, and inclined his head as though they'd been introduced at a function. "You look better than your file photographs."

"You have file photographs."

"I have a great many things." He glanced at the Mercy, taking her in with the frank appraisal of a man pricing a horse — the patched hull, the rust bleeding from the rails, Sefina's engine cover propped open like a mouth mid-argument. "This is what you spent my employer's money on?"

"It's what I spent *my* money on. The debt is mine now, apparently. Your lawyers were very thorough about that."

"They are thorough." He smiled without warmth. "May I buy you a coffee? The store has instant. It's terrible. But the conversation will be worse, so there's a certain symmetry."

"I'll stand."

"As you like." He leaned against the rail, unhurried, letting the silence do some of his work for him. Down on the pontoon, Tevita had appeared from the wheelhouse and stood with his arms folded, saying nothing, radiating the kind of stillness that was louder than shouting. Kessler acknowledged him with a nod, the way one acknowledges weather. "Your father borrowed against a great deal more than money, Miss Okafor. Did you know that?"

"I know he borrowed money. I know the debt landed on me like a dropped piano. I know your syndicate funds expeditions and then owns whatever they find."

"We fund *discovery*," Kessler corrected gently. "Ownership is simply how civilisation says thank you." He reached into his breast pocket and produced, instead of a weapon or a contract, a photograph — creased, printed on glossy stock. He held it out. After a moment's war with herself, she took it.

Her father. Emeka Okafor, standing on a riverbank in field khakis gone grey with mildew, holding up a brass object to the camera. Even in the photograph, even at that distance, she knew it. The compass. The one that had arrived in her mail slot in Port Moresby with a lawyer's letter and a needle that spun wrong, that sat now in the bottom of her duffel bag wrapped in a shirt, heavy as a sin.

"He sent this to you," Kessler said. "Before the silence. Which tells us two things. One: he knew he might not come back. Two: he trusted you with the only piece of this puzzle that matters."

"It's a compass. It doesn't work."

"It works perfectly. Just not north." He took the photograph back, folded it along its existing crease, and returned it to his pocket with the care of a priest handling relics. "There is a site upriver, Miss Okafor. Older than anything the universities have admitted to. My employers have spent years and a regrettable amount of money locating it. Your father found it in six weeks, because he had the key, and then he did what idealists always do — he tried to keep it for himself. For science. For posterity." His mouth thinned. "For vanity."

"And you want the compass."

"I want to make everyone whole." He spread his hands, a gesture of beautiful reasonableness. "Here is my offer, and I will only make it once, so listen with your whole attention. You give me the compass. In exchange, the debt dies. Not deferred — dead. Signed release, today, before you leave this junction. Your father's obligations, your inheritance of them, all of it — gone. Clean slate. You walk away from this river a free woman, and we handle everything upstream ourselves. Rescue, recovery, archaeology, whatever euphemism you prefer. None of it touches you again."

The dog on the veranda sighed and resettled. Somewhere across the river a bird screamed once and thought better of it.

Ulyana became aware, distantly, of how easy it would be. That was the obscenity of it — how *easy*. One envelope in a duffel bag. One signature. The number that had lived in her chest since the lawyer's letter, that had woken her at 3 a.m. in a bunk on a leaking boat, would simply stop existing. She could turn the Mercy around tomorrow. She could go back to the docks, to the cranes and the salt air and the small honest exhaustion of a day's work, and never think about the Fly River or the father inside it again.

And her father would be alone up there. Whatever had cut off his transmission — whatever had made a man who trusted nothing mail away the one thing he trusted most — would have him, and Kessler's version of "rescue," and no one coming.

"That's not an offer," she said. "That's a price tag on my father's life."

"That's arithmetic," Kessler said. "I'm sorry it feels personal. Money always does, to people who don't have it." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Consider it tonight. We'll both be at Kiunga within the week; the river narrows everyone's options eventually. If you change your mind—" He drew a card from his pocket, plain cream stock, a phone number embossed in silver and nothing else, and set it on the rail between them, weighted under a stone from the pontoon's ballast. "—you know how to reach me. No shame in it. Everyone sells eventually. The only variable is the price."

He pushed off the rail, nodded to Tevita — who did not nod back — and descended the ladder to his white boat, which pulled away with that same expensive purr, unhurried, a man who knew the river would deliver what he wanted whether he chased it or not.

Ulyana stood a long moment looking at the card under the stone. Then she picked it up, read the blank face of it as if it might confess something, and put it in her pocket — because throwing it in the river felt too much like a scene in a film, and keeping it felt like evidence.

Tevita was watching her from the wheelhouse door. "Well?"

"He offered to kill the debt for the compass."

"And?"

"And I told him no."

Tevita studied her for a long moment, and something in his face shifted — not approval exactly, but a recalculation, a man revising a chart. "Fuel's topped," he said. "We make the middle reach by dark if we leave now."

* * *

They left the junction at noon, the Mercy riding lower with her full tanks, the jungle closing around them green and total. Ulyana helped Sefina stow the hose, and Bram coiled lines on the foredeck with his usual economy, and for an hour the river was just the river.

It was near dusk, checking the deck cleats before nightfall, that Ulyana passed the forward hold and saw Bram at the rail with his back half-turned, a square of cream-coloured card in his hand, turning it over in the last light. He must have lifted it from the rail after she'd set it down — or palmed it when she'd picked it up; he was quick that way, ex-soldier quick, hands that moved before decisions finished forming.

Bram looked up. Their eyes met. For one suspended second neither of them moved, and Ulyana saw the calculation run through him like a fish under ice — deny, explain, apologize, threaten — and then he did none of those things. He simply held her gaze, folded the card away into his chest pocket, and gave her a small, unreadable nod, the nod of a man acknowledging a debt of his own.

Ulyana said nothing. She didn't know yet why she said nothing — whether it was trust, or fatigue, or the strange new weight she'd felt on the pontoon when Kessler had called her *Miss Okafor* and meant *decision-maker*, the vertigo of realizing that what she chose next would decide whether these people ate or drowned or bled.

Power, she was learning, didn't feel like strength. It felt like holding a live wire and smiling.

She climbed to the wheelhouse, where Tevita stood at the wheel and the compass sat in her duffel below, its broken needle dreaming of a direction that wasn't north. Ahead, the middle reach opened dark under the first fat drops of rain, and somewhere up that darkness, her father waited with his secrets, and Kessler waited with his patience, and Bram waited with a telephone number pressed against his heart like a second bullet.

Junie's radio crackled softly behind them, catching fragments of static and, beneath it, something that might have been a voice.

Nobody mentioned it.
