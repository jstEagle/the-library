## 6. Leaking

The river told you things before it hurt you, if you knew how to listen. Sefina had spent twenty-two years listening to engines, and the Mercy's engine had been talking all morning.

A new note in the harmonic. High, thin, riding just above the thrum of the cylinders — the sound a bearing makes when it's about to give up on you. She lay flat on the deck plates with her ear against the housing, one hand on the throttle linkage, and listened while the boat bucked through the middle reach, and the sound got louder, and she said a word her mother would have washed her mouth out for.

"You hear that?" she called up through the engine hatch.

Tevita's voice came down, calm as still water. "Hear what?"

"Exactly. That's the problem." She wiped her hands on a rag that had been black so long it was more rag than cloth. "Bearing's dry. I greased it at Kiunga and it's already gone. Something's eating the grease. Something's eating *everything* on this boat."

That was the truth of the Mercy, and everyone aboard knew it and no one said it: the boat was dying by inches. She was a forty-year-old river freighter with more patches than hull, held together by Sefina's welding, Tevita's stubbornness, and a superstition among the crew that naming a leaking boat Mercy kept the river from taking her. Sefina didn't believe in superstition. She believed in zinc anodes and sacrificial metal. She believed everything afloat was always in the process of sinking, and the only question was whether you were honest about it.

The woman was honest about it. That was the thing Sefina had decided about Ulyana Okafor on the second day, watching her bail the bilge without being asked, watching her learn the boat the way you learn a difficult person — patiently, with your guard up. The daughter had her father's stubborn jaw and none of his charm, and she'd stood at the rail that morning with the brass compass in her fist, staring upriver like the river owed her something.

Sefina knew that look. It was the look of someone doing arithmetic with a family member's life.

* * *

The rapids came at the bend below the D'Albertis junction, where the Fly narrowed and the water went from brown muscle to white fury in the space of two hundred meters. Tevita brought the boat in low and slow, reading the water the way Sefina read schematics — and for the first kilometer it was beautiful, the old girl threading the channels, spray coming over the bow in silver sheets, Bram at the bow post with the pole looking like he'd been born there.

Then the bearing let go.

Sefina heard it before she felt it: a crack like a rifle shot under the deck, and the harmonic died, and the shaft began to scream against metal that no longer wanted to hold it. The vibration came up through her knees, through her teeth. Above her, Tevita's voice — "Hold on" — and then the world went sideways.

They didn't capsize. The river was kinder than that, or crueler. A submerged shelf caught the hull amidships and the Mercy slewed, shipped a ton of green water over the gunwale, and ground to a stop on the shelf with the current tearing at her like a dog at a bone. The impact opened her up. Sefina knew it before she got the hatch open, knew it by the sound — not the bang of impact but the long, wet, sucking groan of a hull taking water below the waterline.

She came up on deck into chaos. Junie clinging to the radio shack, Bram hauling the bow line, Ulyana on her knees with river water streaming off her, and Tevita at the wheel, holding the boat on the shelf with the throttle like a man holding a door shut against the wind.

"How bad?" he asked Sefina.

She looked over the side. Through the churn she could see it — a seam below the waterline, maybe a meter of plating sprung along a weld her father's generation had laid down before she was born. Water coming through in a gray ribbon.

"Bad," she said. "Not sinking bad. Yet."

Tevita nodded once, and that was the whole meeting. He looked at Ulyana. "Can you swim?"

"I grew up on a dock."

"Good. You're on patch duty."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon and into the dark.

The plan was Sefina's, because the plan was always Sefina's: ground the boat at low water on the gravel tongue downstream of the shelf, get the seam above the waterline if they could, patch it with the rubber sheeting and the epoxy and the canvas from the spare awning, and pour a fiberglass jacket over the whole wound. The problem was the river didn't wait for low water. The monsoon owned the schedule. So they did it in the current, in the dark, in water to their waists, with the flood rising a centimeter every hour and the boat's whole weight resting on a gravel bar that could shift under them like a sleeping animal.

Sefina went in first. She always went in first — it was her boat's wound, her shame, her job. The current hit her like a wall of hands. She got a line around her waist, Bram on the other end, and waded to the seam with the patch kit in a dry bag over her shoulder.

Ulyana came in beside her without being told.

"Stay on my left," Sefina shouted over the water. "The current's a liar on the right. It'll take your legs."

They found the seam by touch, in the black water, feeling along the cold plate until Sefina's fingers found the gap — a lip of torn metal, sharp as a promise. She got the rubber sheet pressed over it and held it there, arms shaking, while Ulyana mixed epoxy in the dark by feel, because the lamp they'd rigged kept drowning in the spray.

"You've done this before," Ulyana shouted.

"I've done everything before. That's the whole problem with being old."

"You're not old."

"I'm old enough to know the boat's going to kill one of us someday, and old enough to have made peace with it not being me." Sefina felt the girl laugh — felt it through the water, through the hull, a small shaking against her shoulder. Something in her chest went loose. "Hold the sheet. Press from the top edge. Water gets under the top edge, the whole thing's done."

They worked like that for hours. The epoxy needed to cure and the river wouldn't let it, so they took turns holding the patch against the hull with their whole bodies, pressing themselves flat against the cold steel like women comforting a wounded animal, while the flood rose around them. Tevita ran the pump in shifts. Junie kept the lamp dry and kept the coffee coming in a thermos passed hand to hand like communion. Bram stood the line, waist-deep at the anchor, saying nothing.

Somewhere past midnight, Ulyana's hands were shaking too badly to mix the last batch. Sefina covered them with her own.

"Hey. Look at me." The lamp caught the girl's face — gray-lipped, exhausted, eyes huge. "You know why I named her Mercy? My father named her. He said a boat is a promise you make to people. You promise to bring them home. Everything else — the cargo, the money, the schedule — that's just what you trade to keep the promise." She pressed the girl's hands flat against the hull, over the patch. "You're holding the promise right now. Feel it?"

Ulyana nodded. Her jaw was set the way her father's jaw was set, but her eyes were wet, and Sefina pretended not to see, because some things you let a person keep.

They poured the fiberglass jacket at three in the morning, by feel, in the rain. By dawn the seam was holding. The pump was winning. The Mercy sat on the gravel tongue with the flood swirling around her, wounded but afloat, and Sefina climbed aboard on arms that didn't want to work and lay down on the deck plates next to her engine and slept like the dead.

* * *

She woke to voices. Low ones.

It was still dark, or dark again — she'd lost track. The lamp in the engine room was down to an ember. Through the open hatch she could see the aft deck, and Bram standing at the rail with the portable radio set, the little handheld they used for shore work, cupped close to his mouth.

Sefina didn't move. Twenty-two years around engines had taught her the value of lying still and listening.

Bram's voice was a murmur, pitched under the sound of the river. She caught pieces. *Still on the bar... hull damage... two days, maybe three...* A pause, long. Then, quieter, something she couldn't make out at all, and then a single word that she did catch, and that went through her like a splinter:

*Kessler.*

Bram lowered the radio. Stood looking at the water for a long moment. Then he turned, and Sefina closed her eyes and breathed slow and even, the way you do when you want to be asleep, and she heard him cross the deck and go below, and she heard nothing else.

She lay in the dark for a long time afterward, her hand resting on the engine housing, on the new bearing she'd have to fit in the morning, on the boat that was a promise.

Bram had pulled her out of the current twice that night. Had stood in the flood for eight hours holding their lives on a line. Had given Junie his dry socks without being asked. That was the thing about a crew, Sefina thought, staring up into the black of the engine room. A crew was a family you didn't get to choose, and a family was a wound you didn't get to see all of. She thought about waking Tevita. She thought about it for a long time.

She didn't. Not yet. Because she wasn't sure, and because a wrong word on this boat, this far upriver, could sink them faster than any hole in the hull.

But when she finally slept again, she slept with one ear open. And in the morning, passing Junie at the radio shack, she saw the girl's face — pale, tight, her eyes flicking once toward Bram at the bow post and away again, her hand flat on the logbook like she was holding something down.

Sefina looked at her, and Junie looked back, and neither of them said a word.

Two of them knew now. The river carried them upriver toward whatever came next, and the patch held, and the boat held, and the promise held — for now.
