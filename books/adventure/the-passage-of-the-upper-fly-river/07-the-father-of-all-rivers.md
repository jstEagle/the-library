## 7. The Father of All Rivers

The river told us before Junie's radio did.

For two days the Fly had run brown and swollen, carrying its cargo of silt and secrets past cutbanks and drowned palms, but that morning the water changed — it went quiet in a way rivers shouldn't, sliding flat and glassy between walls of limestone, as if holding its breath around something sacred or rotten. Tevita cut the engine to a whisper. Sefina stood at the bow with a pair of binoculars she'd repaired with tape and spite.

"Smoke," she said.

Not smoke — haze. A thin gray thread rising from a bend ahead where the cliffs pinched together and the jungle grew right down to the waterline like hair over a skull. Junie appeared at my elbow without her headphones for once, which frightened me more than anything else had so far. When Junie took off her headphones, things were serious.

"There was a burst transmission last night," she said quietly. "Two seconds. Morse. Just the word *Mercy* repeated three times."

Bram laughed from the stern, short and ugly. "Somebody's praying to the boat."

"Or to you," Sefina said, looking at me.

I didn't answer. I had spent eleven days on this river telling myself I was coming for money, for the debt, for the simple transactional decency of settling accounts. I had not let myself say the other word. But the needle of my father's brass compass had steadied two nights ago — stopped spinning wrong for the first time since Port Moresby — and it pointed up this side channel now, steady as a finger, and I understood that whatever waited around that bend had been waiting for both of us.

Tevita brought us in along the eastern bank, engine off, poling through the shallows the way he did when he wanted the river to forget we were there. The *Mercy* slid under hanging vines, and the world went green and dripping and silent except for the drip of water off the canopy and the far-off complaint of birds.

Then the smell reached us. Woodsmoke, yes, but under it something sweeter and worse.

"Camp's just past that shelf of rock," Bram said. He'd been here before, or somewhere like it; his voice had gone flat and professional, the soldier's voice. "One way in, one way out. Good defensive ground. Bad place to be surprised."

"We're not surprising anyone," Tevita said. "They'll have heard us a mile back."

We tied off to a strangler fig and went up the bank single file, Tevita first with a machete, then Sefina, then me, Bram and Junie behind. My father's compass rode in my shirt pocket against my breastbone like a second heart. It had been doing something strange all morning — not spinning, but trembling, ticking against my ribs in little arrhythmic pulses, as if it could feel what was ahead.

The camp sat on a terrace above the channel: two collapsed tents, a tarp strung badly between trees, crates stacked into a rough wall facing the water. A fire pit, cold. And scattered across the mud, the archaeology — screens and shovels, a total station tripod bent double, survey flags fluttering orange in the green like wounds.

And the smell. I found its source behind the crate wall.

He was sitting with his back against a tree, and for one merciful second I did not recognize him. Then I did, and I wished I hadn't. Dr. Emeka Okafor had been a big man, careful and precise, a man who ironed his field shirts in the middle of nowhere because, he said, discipline began with creases. The man under the tree wore those same shirts, or their ghosts — torn, black with mold, buttoned wrong. He had lost maybe thirty pounds. His beard held things in it. His eyes tracked us as we came through the treeline with the slow, deliberate sweep of a man who had been watching the jungle try to kill him for weeks and had stopped being sure which side of the watching he was on.

"Ulyana." He said it like a man testing whether a rope would hold his weight. "You got my letter."

"I got a lawyer's letter," I said. "And a debt collector. And a compass in the mail. That's not the same thing as a letter from you."

"No." He almost smiled. "No, it wouldn't be."

Sefina made a sound behind me — small, choked. She was looking at the other end of the terrace, where two shapes lay under a groundsheet weighted with stones. One sheet was longer than the other. Under the shorter one, near the edge, a woman's boot showed, and beside it, placed neatly as an offering, a broken rock hammer.

"What happened here?" Tevita asked. His voice was gentle in the way I'd learned meant danger.

My father looked at the sheets for a long time. "Nadia fell," he said finally. "At the cavern mouth. The ladder rotted through. I told them the line was good. I checked it twice, I thought. Nadia fell forty meters onto the rocks below the spring." His hands opened and closed in his lap, measuring nothing. "That was nine days ago. Maybe ten. The days don't hold still."

"And the other?" Bram said.

My father's eyes came up, and there was something in them I had never seen in him, not even in the worst years, not even the night he walked out of our house in Port Moresby with a duffel bag and a grant proposal and left me standing on the veranda at fourteen years old. It was fear. Not of the jungle. Of himself.

"Pavel tried to take the compass," he said.

* * *

He talked for a long time after that, and we let him, because the story had to come out of him whole or not at all. We sat in a half circle in the mud while the light went amber through the canopy, and he spoke in the cadence of a lecture he'd given too many times to empty rooms.

"The site is real," he said. "That's the terrible part. If I had done everything correctly — permits, consultation, the custodianship protocols I used to teach — it would be the find of the century. A karst cavern system, sealed since before the river found its present course. Ceramics in styles nobody has catalogued. Gold work. Trade beads from four hundred years before anyone thought this reach was settled. Evidence of a civilization the river swallowed and kept." He laughed, a dry cracked sound. "The father of all rivers keeps his children's toys at the bottom of the house."

"You stole the compass," I said.

His head dropped. "From the Waimiri family. Downstream. They are the custodians — have been for generations, though the government papers call them squatters. The compass isn't a compass, Ulyana. It's a key. A bearing-stone. It doesn't point north; it points home, to the cavern mouth, by ways only they know. Old man Waimiri showed it to me at a conference in '19, let me hold it for ten minutes, and I—" His voice failed and restarted. "I came back in March with a forged research permit and a crew who didn't ask questions, and I took it out of his house while his granddaughter made us tea."

Nobody moved. On the river below, a hornbill called once, absurd and beautiful.

"The debt," I said. "Kessler. The syndicate. Is that how you funded it?"

"He wasn't a syndicate then. He was a financier. A generous one. Too generous — I should have seen it. When the transmission went out about the initial finds, his people started arriving. Faster boats. Better guns. They want the site stripped, the gold lifted, the cavern dynamited if it makes extraction easier. And they want everyone who knows the location dead, because a secret site is worth more than a published one." He looked at me, and his eyes were wet and ancient. "I wrote to you because you were the only person I trusted and the only person I had no right to ask anything of. The debt transferred to your name because I signed it that way. I made you collateral, Ulyana. The way I made old man Waimiri's hospitality into a burglary. It's what I do. I take things and call it scholarship."

"That's not true," Sefina said suddenly, fiercely, and I could have kissed her. "People aren't equations—"

"They're exactly equations," my father said. "That's the whole disease of me. I loved the past more than the living. I loved the *find* more than the people who kept it safe for a thousand years so that men like me could come and be amazed at them." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Pavel found out what I'd done. Read the permit, recognized the forgery — he'd worked permits for twenty years, he knew the seal was wrong. He said we were going downriver, all of us, and returning the compass, and turning ourselves in. And Kessler's men were already in the channel, and Pavel went to meet them alone to buy us time, and they—" He stopped. "There was a knife. There was a great deal of blood. I dragged him up here and I have been sitting with him because I will not leave him for the pigs, and every day I tell myself tomorrow I'll bury him properly, and every day I sit here instead, guarding a hole in the ground full of dead people's gold, waiting for the men with guns to come and make it simple."

Junie had gone white. Bram was staring at the long groundsheet with an expression I couldn't read — not horror exactly. Recognition. Somewhere in another life, Bram had knelt beside a body he hadn't buried either.

Tevita crouched in front of my father. "How many men does Kessler have?"

"Six that I saw. Maybe more upstream. They know I can't move the artifacts alone. They're letting me do the work." He smiled horribly. "I'm excavating for them now. Isn't that the perfect ending? Every grant application I ever wrote, and in the end I work for free for thieves."

"How long," Tevita said, "before they come?"

My father looked at the sky through the canopy, reading weather the way he read strata. "The monsoon breaks tonight. High water by morning. They'll come at dawn — they need the flood to float the crates out through the lower channel." He turned to me then, and held out his hand, palm up, trembling. An old man's hand. A beggar's hand. "Give me the compass, Ulyana. Please. Whatever happens next, it belongs in the ground with them, not in Kessler's vault. Let me put it back where I stole it from. Let me do one thing correctly."

And God help me, my hand went to my pocket. Because he was my father, and he was dying by inches in front of me, and some buried girl in me still wanted to hand him anything he asked for and watch his face light up the way it never had for me, only for pottery shards and drowned cities.

But the compass ticked against my fingers, quick and alive, pointing — I realized with a chill — not at the cavern, not at the river, but downstream. Toward the channel. Toward the dark.

Toward the boats already moving in the night.

"Tevita," I said. "Kill the fire. Wake the camp. They're not coming at dawn."

My father stared at me. "What—"

"They're coming now."
