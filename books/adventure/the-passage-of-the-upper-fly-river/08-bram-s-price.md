## 8. Bram's Price

Bram had kept watch a thousand nights in a dozen bad places, and he knew the arithmetic of dawn better than he knew his own mother's face. First light was when men moved. Not midnight — midnight was for amateurs and films. Dawn was when the sentry's eyes went grainy, when the coffee ran out, when the body decided it had survived the night and let its guard down like a man unbuttoning his collar.

So when Kessler came, he came at first light.

Bram heard them before he saw them: the soft knock of a paddle against a hull, muffled with wrapped cloth, upstream where the mist still held the river in its fist. Three boats. He counted the paddles by sound alone, the way you count heartbeats through a wall. Nine men, maybe ten. Kessler wouldn't bring fewer. Not for a site worth killing for.

Bram sat on the root of a fallen fig tree at the camp's edge, rifle across his knees, and did the other arithmetic — the one he'd been doing since the fuel stop, since the card in his pocket with the satellite number written in fountain-pen ink. *One call buys your debt clean,* Kessler had said. *And puts ten thousand kina in your hand.* Bram had told himself it was insurance. A soldier's hedge. If the Mercy sank, if Tevita's luck finally ran out, Bram would have somewhere to land that wasn't the bottom of the Fly River.

He'd radioed twice from the shallows while Sefina patched the hull. Both times Junie had been asleep. Both times he'd told himself that too.

Now the mist was peeling off the water and the arithmetic was coming due.

* * *

He could have warned them. That was the thing he would carry afterward, the splinter under every later thought: there had been time. Forty seconds, maybe fifty, between the paddle-knock and the first shot. Time to walk back into camp, put his hand on Ulyana's shoulder, say *they're here, get down.* Fifty seconds in which he was still a man who hadn't chosen.

Instead he did what soldiers do. He brought the rifle up and sighted into the mist, because watching is not the same as warning, and watching lets you tell yourself you were only gathering information.

The first shot cracked the morning open.

It came from the water — a single round, high, meant to scatter rather than kill, and it worked exactly as designed. The camp erupted. Dr. Okafor came out of his tent screaming about the cavern, about the artifacts, arms flailing like a man trying to hold up a collapsing sky. Tevita rolled off his sleeping mat already moving, silent as ballast shifting, and had the .303 in his hands before Bram finished his first breath. Sefina grabbed Ulyana by the collar and hauled her behind the fuel drums, and Junie — small, quick Junie, who talked to ghosts in the static all night — snatched up the radio and started shouting coordinates into it, calling for anyone, for the patrol post two days downstream, for God, for anybody.

Then the mist gave up its boats and the morning filled with men.

Kessler's crew came ashore in a disciplined wedge, and they were good. Professional. They laid down suppressing fire in measured bursts and advanced on the camp the way water advances on a matchstick — patient, total, without hurry. Bram watched them from his tree and felt the old cold competence settle over him like a coat, and hated himself for how well it fit.

"Okafor!" Kessler's voice carried across the water, smooth as engine oil. "You have something of mine. And now I have something of yours." A pause, punctuated by a burst of fire walking across the tents. "Everyone walks away today, Doctor. Or no one does."

Bram rose from behind the fig tree.

He didn't decide to. That was the horror of it, looking back — there was no decision, no weighing. His legs simply stood him up, because nine years of soldiering will do one true thing even in a coward, and his voice came out of him flat and carrying:

"Left flank! Two in the reeds, three coming along the bank!"

Ulyana turned and looked at him. Just looked. And Bram understood, in the half-second their eyes met across the burning camp, that she already knew. Had known, maybe, since the fuel stop, or since the rapids, or since some look he'd given that he hadn't known he was giving. She didn't scream. She didn't curse him. She just looked at him the way you look at weather coming, and turned back to the fight.

That was worse than any bullet.

* * *

The fight went wrong for everyone at once.

Tevita fired from the hip, unhurried, each shot placed like a signature, and drove the bank team to ground. Sefina got the Mercy's engine coughing — the woman could coax life out of anything with a spark plug and spite — and the boat swung at its mooring line like a dog straining a leash. Okafor bolted for the cavern mouth with a satchel of gold bars clutched to his chest, and Kessler's men let him run, because he wasn't the prize anymore. The compass was. The compass on Ulyana's neck.

They came for her in a rush of three, and Bram saw it happen the way you see a crash before the sound arrives.

Junie saw it too.

She was small and she was slow compared to bullets, but she was between them and Ulyana before Bram could draw a full breath, radio still in one hand, and she swung it like a club and bought exactly one second. One second was what a knife needed. The blade caught her above the hip, a short ugly stroke, and she folded around it with a sound Bram would hear for the rest of his life — not a scream, just an exhale, like something deflating.

Ulyana killed the first man with the fuel drum's edge and the second with Tevita's machete, and Bram never learned how, because he was moving now, truly moving, the last fifty seconds of his cowardice burning off behind him like fog.

He dropped the third man with two rounds, center mass, and stood over Junie where she lay in the mud with Ulyana's hands pressed hard against her side, blood pushing up black between her fingers in the grey light.

"Bram." Ulyana didn't look up. Her voice was iron and river water. "How many more?"

"Six," he said. "Maybe five. I—" The sentence died in his throat. There was nothing he could say that wasn't a confession, and every confession was a debt Junie was paying interest on.

Kessler's voice again, closer now, from the treeline: "Pull back. Burn the camp. Nobody leaves this bend alive — the syndicate doesn't buy silence twice."

And Bram heard it. Really heard it, stripped of the fuel-stop charm, the fountain pen, the easy arithmetic. *Nobody leaves.* Not the doctor, not the girl, not the boatman who'd fed him, not the engineer who'd taught him the Mercy's engine, not Junie bleeding into the mud at his feet. Witnesses. That was all any of them had ever been, from the moment Kessler first smiled at them across a fuel drum. Product to acquire or product to erase.

Bram had sold men before. He'd told himself that too, over the years — that everyone in his trade sold someone, that the selling was just weather, just climate, just how the world was built. But he had never sold *these* people. And standing over Junie's wound with her blood on his boots, Bram Okonkwo-Vailele discovered the exact weight of what he'd tried to sell, and found he could not lift it.

He raised his rifle toward the treeline, toward Kessler's voice, and fired.

* * *

What followed was not heroism. Bram wanted to be honest about that, even inside his own skull, because honesty was the only currency he had left that wasn't counterfeit. What followed was a man spending everything he had to buy back one hour of being decent, and knowing the exchange rate was theft.

He fought the retreat with them. That was the shape of it: Kessler's men pressing from the treeline, the camp burning behind them, and six people — five and a half, counting Junie's fading pulse — falling back toward the Mercy with the river rising brown and furious at their backs. The monsoon had been building for two days, and the Fly was answering, climbing the banks in long hungry surges, dragging whole trees past the bend like it was clearing its throat.

Sefina held the boat against the mooring line and screamed at them to move. Tevita covered the rear, firing once for every three rounds the syndicate spent, making each one argue. Ulyana carried Junie — carried her, this woman half again her size, stumbling through the mud with the compass swinging against her chest — and Bram walked backward beside them and shot until the barrel stung his palm.

Okafor was already gone into the cavern, into the dark with his gold, and nobody had time to grieve a man who was still technically alive.

They reached the boat. Kessler's men reached the bank. And between those two facts stretched fifteen meters of open water going vertical with rain, and a mooring line, and Sefina's outstretched arm, and the arithmetic of dawn come due one final time.

"We can't cast off!" Sefina screamed over the rain. "Line's fouled on the anchor snag — someone has to free it from the water!"

Bram looked at the brown flood, at the trees running past like freight trains, at Junie grey-faced in the bilge with Ulyana's jacket knotted against her wound. Then he looked at the fouled line, and understood the price on the tag.

"I'll cut us loose," he said, and went over the side.

* * *

The river took him the moment the current found his boots. It hit like a living thing, like a mob, wrenching him downstream toward the snag where the anchor line was wrapped around a drowned trunk. He got a hand on the rope. The water hammered him flat against it, tore at his grip, filled his mouth with the taste of the whole watershed — silt and bark and things rotted and drowned. Somewhere above him the gunfire stuttered, then stopped, then resumed, and he couldn't know whose side was winning and understood suddenly that it no longer mattered which. He had picked. This rope was his signature on the contract.

The knife was still on his belt. Of course it was; Kessler's man had used its twin on Junie. Bram got it out one-handed, sawed at the fouled line while the flood tried to peel him off the snag like wet paper, and thought, with strange clarity, of his father teaching him to tie knots in Apia harbor thirty years ago. *A knot holds or it doesn't, boy. There's no almost.*

The line parted.

The Mercy swung free into the current, and the current took her, and Bram rode the rope hand over hand toward the stern as the boat swept past the snag — close, so close, Sefina's arm stretching down out of the rain —

— and caught his wrist.

For a moment he hung there over the water, one arm, one grip, the whole flooded river pulling at his legs like a debt collector, and he looked up into the boat and saw them all: Sefina hauling, teeth bared; Tevita at the tiller, giving him one slow nod, which from Tevita was a speech; Ulyana kneeling over Junie, looking down at him with that same weather-coming face.

"You don't get to drown," she shouted through the rain. "You get to earn it. Haul."

Bram hauled.

Behind them, on the shrinking bank, Kessler stood in the burning camp with the flood climbing his shins, and Bram watched him turn — not toward the boat, but toward the cavern mouth, where a mad old man was guarding a fortune as the river rose to take it. Kessler raised one hand, and his remaining men began to follow him into the dark.

Into the cavern. Where the water was already waist-deep and climbing, where the ceiling dripped like a countdown, where the whole flooded heart of the mountain waited with its gold and its dead.

"Tevita," Ulyana said, and her voice went quiet in a way that cut through the storm like a blade through silk. "My father's still in there."

Tevita looked upstream. Looked at the sky, which had gone the color of a bruise, and at the river, which had begun to roar.

"Hold on," he said, and turned the bow into the flood.
