## 9. High Water

**Ulyana**

The river spoke first.

She heard it before she saw it — a sound like the whole sky collapsing into the gorge, a low rolling thunder that had nothing to do with lightning. She stood at the mouth of her father's cavern, the brass compass heavy in her jacket pocket, and watched brown water climb the limestone steps in surges, each one higher than the last.

"It's coming up fast," Sefina shouted from the Mercy, tied to the drowned trees at the cavern's lip. The boat sat low, riding gray-green swells that an hour ago had been dry stone. "Ulyana! We have to move her to the deep channel or she'll be on the rocks by dark!"

Her father stood behind her, at the edge of the flooded inner chamber, where the water had already swallowed the lower shelves of artifacts. He was gaunt, wild-eyed, his expedition shirt hanging off him like shed skin. In his hands he cradled a gold funerary mask as if it were an infant.

"The water won't take them," he said. Not to her — to himself, to the river, to whatever god he'd been negotiating with for three months alone in the dark. "It brought me here. It keeps what it loves."

"Father." She hated how the word still cracked something open in her chest. "The cavern is flooding. We have maybe twenty minutes."

"Then we work fast." He set down the mask and waded into the chest-deep water toward a submerged crate, moving with the terrible efficiency of a man who had stopped listening to anything but his own purpose. "Help me. You came all this way — help me."

And God help her, she almost did. Her boots were already wet. Her hands were already reaching. Twenty years of wanting him to ask her for something, anything, and here it was at last: *help me carry my stolen gold out of a drowning cave.*

The compass needle spun against her ribs, wrong, wrong, wrong, and she thought of the custodian family's elder, of the forged permits, of the two men lying dead deeper in the complex — one under a collapsed shelf, one with a wound no accident makes.

"No," she said.

He looked up at her across the brown water, and for one naked moment she saw not the great Dr. Emeka Okafor but an old man drowning slowly in his own lie.

Then the river took the choice away from both of them.

* * *

**Tevita**

Tevita felt the Mercy shudder before he understood why.

He was at the stern, hands on the gunwale, watching the cavern mouth, when the flood surge hit — not a wave but a wall, the river's whole weight swinging into the gorge like a door slamming. The bow line snapped with a crack he felt in his teeth. The stern line held, swung the boat broadside, and the current rolled her.

He went under with her.

Cold closed over him like a fist. The world became brown chaos — hull planks, rope, Sefina's toolbox spinning past like a fleeing bird. He found the surface, gasped, and saw the Mercy come over onto her beam, pinning Bram against the drowned fig tree where he'd been cutting the second line. Bram's arm was trapped between hull and trunk, his face white above the waterline, screaming something the wind shredded.

Tevita swam.

Twenty years on this river had taught him its grammar, and right now it was shouting. He got a shoulder under the hull's edge, braced against the tree, and shoved. The boat didn't move. It weighed nothing and everything; it was wood and air and forty years of his life, and it was crushing a man who had betrayed them all and screamed anyway when the river came for him.

"Bram!" he roared. "On three. Breathe when I say."

Bram's eyes found his. Something passed between them there in the roar — not forgiveness, maybe never that, but recognition. Two men in the same current.

"One—"

* * *

**Ulyana**

Ulyana saw it all from the cavern steps: Tevita diving into the flood, the Mercy heeled over like a wounded animal, Bram pinned and going under in gulps.

"Rope!" Junie was beside her, throwing the coil's end to Sefina on the high rocks. "We can't hold the boat — we can only hold *them*!"

Ulyana tied the line around her waist because there was no time to think about whether she trusted Sefina's knots, and dove.

The water hit her like a train. It tore at the rope, spun her, filled her ears with gravel-roar. She surfaced once — long enough to see her father in the shallows of the cavern mouth, hauling a crate toward higher ground, then stopping, turning back, going in again for another. Gold over survival. Of course. Of course.

Then she saw Tevita go under.

He'd been holding the hull off Bram, and the surge came again — the monsoon's true peak arriving like a debt collector — and the hull dropped, and both men vanished beneath it.

She swam. The rope burned her waist. She dove where they'd gone down, eyes stinging in the silt, hands searching blind through brown murk until they found fabric — Tevita's shirt — and hauled. He was tangled in the stern line, wrapped twice around the leg, dragged along the bottom like an anchor. His face was slack. Too long. Far too long.

She got the knife from her belt — Sefina's knife, pressed into her hand on the rocks, *you'll need it more than me* — and cut. Once, twice. The line parted and the river tried to take him and she held on, both arms around his chest, kicking for the light, the rope at her waist snapping taut as Sefina and Junie hauled from above.

Behind her, through the churning brown, she saw her father.

He had gone back into the deep chamber one last time. He surfaced in the cavern mouth with both arms locked around a crate — she could see the gold bars through the split slats, gleaming even in the murk, beautiful and obscene. The current had him. He looked at her across ten meters of roaring water, and his eyes were clear for the first time since she'd arrived, and he did not let go of the crate.

"Ulyana!" His voice carried, impossibly, over everything. "Take it! Take it and you'll never owe anyone—"

The river pulled him sideways. He fought it, boots finding rock, crate held high like an offering.

"*Let it go!*"

For a heartbeat — one suspended, eternal heartbeat — she believed he would. She saw the choice happen in his face, saw love rise in him like a diver coming up for air, saw it reach the surface —

— and the crate was heavier. It was always going to be heavier. Twenty years of absence, of letters unanswered, of a daughter funding her own life because his money always went somewhere else — it had all been training for this, teaching her exactly what her father held onto and what he released.

The surge took him.

He went under still clutching the gold, swept out of the cavern mouth into the main channel, and the brown water closed over him without ceremony, without mercy, without even a ripple that lasted. The river kept what it loved.

Ulyana screamed — she heard herself do it, a raw animal sound torn out of her — and then the rope hauled her and Tevita both up onto the rocks, and she was coughing water, and Tevita was coughing too, alive, alive, his hand finding hers and gripping hard enough to hurt.

* * *

**Tevita**

Later — minutes later, hours, he couldn't say — Tevita lay on his back on the wet limestone while Sefina pressed on Bram's crushed arm and Junie wrapped it with a shirt, and the rain finally began to slacken, the monsoon spending itself against the gorge walls.

The Mercy floated. Half-swamped, engine drowned, but floating, snagged against the fallen fig, and Sefina would get her running because Sefina could raise the dead if they held a wrench.

Bram stared at the sky, gray-faced, and said, "You came back for me. After everything."

"You were under my boat," Tevita said. "That's all it was."

It wasn't all it was. But some truths keep better below the waterline.

He turned his head and found Ulyana sitting at the water's edge, soaked through, staring at the channel where the river ran fast and brown and empty. No sign of Dr. Emeka Okafor. No sign of the crate. The Fly River does not return its deposits.

"He chose it," Ulyana said, without looking at him. Her voice was flat, scraped clean. "I saw him choose it. There was a moment — I *saw* it — and he held on anyway."

Tevita pushed himself up on his elbows, every muscle shrieking, and moved to sit beside her. Below them the river thundered on, indifferent, ancient, the father of all rivers doing what fathers do: taking everything offered and most things that weren't.

"I knew your father twenty years," he said quietly. "I owed him my life. And I'm telling you what I've learned on this water, girl: nobody chooses the river. The river just shows you what you were carrying all along."

Ulyana was silent a long time. Then her hand went into her jacket and came out with the brass compass, dripping, its needle still spinning its slow wrong circles. She looked at it the way you look at a wound you're deciding whether to cauterize.

"There's an elder downstream," she said. "A family this belonged to."

"Aye," Tevita said. "There is."

She stood, joints stiff, and looked back at the Mercy — at Sefina cursing at the engine, at Junie binding Bram's arm with steady hands, at the battered hull that had carried strangers into a family. Behind her the cavern gaped, half-drowned, full of things worth killing for that she was done killing for.

"Tevita," she said. "Can she make it downstream?"

He followed her gaze to his boat — his leaking, stubborn, impossible boat — and felt something in his chest settle for the first time since the letter had come upriver with her name on it.

"She's the Mercy," he said. "She'll manage."

Ulyana nodded once, pocketed the compass, and turned to help Junie lift Bram aboard — and that was when Junie, sorting through the sodden kit, went very still, and held up a waterproof pouch marked with Kessler's syndicate crest, its contents intact.

"His ledger," Junie said. "It must have been in Bram's pack." She looked up at Ulyana, rain streaming off her chin, eyes bright. "Every payment. Every name. Including the ones he owes *you*."

Ulyana took it. Downstream, somewhere in the narrowing gorge, the river carried her father away into the country of no return — and ahead of them, at the fuel stop where it had all begun, a man waited who thought he owned her family's debts.

"Get us running, Sefina," Ulyana Okafor said, climbing aboard. "We're going downstream."
