## 10. Downstream

The river took three days to forgive us.

I watched it happen from the deck of the *Mercy*, morning after morning, as the brown fury of the flood settled into something older and calmer. The water dropped a hand's width each day, then a forearm's width, peeling back from the trees it had swallowed, leaving mud on the trunks like a tide line on a bath. By the third morning the limestone mouth of the cavern stood above the waterline again, dark and dripping, holding whatever it held.

I did not go back in. Neither did Tevita.

We buried what we could bury. Bram dug the graves himself — he refused help, and no one offered it twice — two mounds above the flood line on the bluff where the expedition camp had been, marked with river stones because there was nothing else to mark them with. My father's grave was the third. Tevita carried him up from the bank on his shoulders, alone, and laid him down with a gentleness I had never seen in the man, and I understood then that whatever debt he had spoken of in the cave, it had never been about money or favors. It had been about a promise made to a drowning man twenty years ago on a river not so different from this one.

I said no words over the grave. I had rehearsed some — anger, mostly, and a few lines of forgiveness that felt like wearing someone else's coat — but when the moment came, all of it drained out of me the way the flood was draining out of the valley, and what was left was only this: a woman standing in the mud, looking down at the man who had taught her to read a river before he taught her to read a book, and who had lied to everyone who ever loved him, and who had drowned clutching a crate of gold because he could not, even at the end, let go.

The river had judged him. I found I had nothing to add.

* * *

On the fourth day, we went to the village.

It sat on the far bank, up a tributary that Junie's charts called nothing and the people who lived there called everything — the whole world, in their language, or close enough. The elder's name was Wariu. He was small and straight-backed and old in the way of river trees, gnarled but standing, and when we came ashore he was waiting for us on the beach as though he had known the hour of our arrival, which knowing him now, I believe he had.

I carried the compass in my shirt pocket, against my skin, the way I had carried it since the flood. The brass was warm from my body. The needle still spun wrong when you held it level — had always spun wrong, I understood now, because it was never meant to find north. It was meant to find the site, the drowned cavern, the thing in the limestone that my father had stolen his way to and died for.

Wariu looked at it for a long time before I took it out. He looked at me longer.

"You are the daughter," he said. Not a question.

"I'm the daughter," I said. "He took this from your family. From your father, I think — from the custodian before you. I came to give it back."

I held it out on my open palm. For a moment he didn't move, and I felt the whole beach holding its breath with me — Sefina behind me, Bram at the boat, Tevita standing apart the way he always did, watching the water instead of the people. Then Wariu reached out with both hands, the way you receive something sacred, and lifted the compass from my palm, and the weight of it leaving me was so sudden and so complete that I nearly staggered.

I had been carrying more than brass. I had been carrying him.

Wariu turned the compass over, thumbed the worn lid, and something moved through his face that I will not try to name — grief for a dead man who had wronged him, maybe, or grief for a father long gone, or simply the old tired recognition of a thing coming home. He closed his hand around it.

"He came to us as a friend," Wariu said. "That was the first lie. There were others. But the river does not keep accounts the way men do." He looked past me, at the water. "It keeps the things themselves. And it gives them back when it is ready."

"Kessler is still out there," I said. "His boat, his men. What's left of them. They'll come for the site."

Wariu smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man who had watched a hundred years of floods and knew exactly how small a syndicate was.

"Let them come," he said. "The river knows its own."

* * *

Kessler came to us instead.

We were at the fuel depot at D'Albertis Junction, taking on diesel and ice and the small ordinary supplies of the living, when his boat slid in against the far side of the dock — scarred, riding low, half his men gone and the ones remaining looking like men who had been through something they did not intend to describe. Kessler himself came down the dock with his shirt pressed and his smile intact, and I will give him this: the smile was a masterpiece. It did not reach his eyes, but it arrived.

"Ms. Okafor." He tipped an invisible hat. "I heard there was a flood. I heard there were casualties. I heard the site is underwater and may stay that way for a decade." He let each item sit in the air between us. "I also heard your father is dead. My condolences. That does, of course, complicate the matter of his outstanding obligations."

"Does it," I said.

"Debts pass to kin. It's an ugly custom but a durable one. However—" and here he produced a leather folder from under his arm, and I saw Sefina's hand drift casually toward the engine room, and Tevita shift his weight off the rail — "I am a reasonable man. The compass, or its equivalent value, and we part as friends. The river's taken enough from both of us."

I had thought about this moment for three days. I had rehearsed it in the dark of the cabin, lying awake while Junie's radio murmured and the river breathed against the hull. And every version of it ended the same way, not with the speech I'd planned but with something simpler.

"Before you go," I said, "you should know what I have."

His smile thinned by a degree. "And what is that?"

"Your own paperwork." I took the folder from my jacket — not his folder; mine, assembled from what Junie had pulled off his radio traffic, what Bram had copied from the card Kessler gave him before Bram turned his gun around, what Sefina had photographed off the man's own chart table the night he'd boarded us at the fuel stop, because Sefina missed nothing and had never once said so. "Permits forged in my father's name with your syndicate's letterhead. A bribe schedule for the provincial lands office. And a transmission ordering your men to, and I'm quoting, *clear witnesses at the site*." I let that last one hang. "The district commissioner at Kiunga has copies. So does a journalist in Port Moresby who has been waiting three years for something exactly like you. If anything happens to me, to my crew, to this boat — it all goes out at once."

Kessler looked at me for a long moment. Around us the depot went on with its ordinary noise — diesel pumps, a dog, someone laughing — and the two of us stood in the middle of it in a silence that had teeth.

"You'd burn your own father's name to save yourself," he said at last. "He was a great man."

"No," I said. "He was a thief with a doctorate. And I'm not doing this to save myself. I'm doing it so that the next person who finds something in that river gets to decide what happens to it — not you, not the funders, not the men with the pressed shirts." I held his eyes. "The debt dies with him, Kessler. Take it up with the river."

He looked at me a moment longer, and then he laughed — one short, genuine, ugly bark of it — and closed his empty folder.

"Your father would be proud," he said. "He was better at this than I gave him credit for."

"He was worse," I said. "That's the whole point."

He went back up the dock. His boat cast off an hour later, running downstream, running away, and no one on the *Mercy* watched it go except me, and I only watched long enough to be sure.

* * *

The map burned on the beach at D'Albertis Junction, on a windless evening with the river running gold in the last light.

It was my father's hand — the master chart, the one he'd kept folded against his chest through the flood, water-stained now, the ink blooming like bruises. Every contour of the site. Every passage of the cavern. The coordinates, the depths, the locations of the artifacts he had catalogued in his beautiful, lying script. Everything anyone would ever need to strip that place down to its limestone bones.

Sefina brought the kerosene. Tevita struck the match. But they gave it to me to hold over the flame, and I understood why, and I did not hesitate.

The paper caught and curled and went up in a column of orange, and I held it until the fire touched my gloves, and then I let the last of it go into the river, where it hissed once and was gone. The needle of a compass I no longer owned would have pointed at the ash, I thought. The river would keep the rest.

I stood there a while after it was done. Bram came and stood beside me without speaking, which was his way of saying the things he couldn't say — about the graves on the bluff, about Junie's arm in its sling, about the card in his pocket he had finally thrown into the water that afternoon, watching it float away like a dead leaf. Junie sat on the rail behind us with her radio in her lap, listening to static that was only static, and for the first time since the Middle Reach, it sounded like peace.

"Fuel's topped," Sefina said, coming up the beach. "Charts are clean. Weather window's tomorrow at dawn, if the river's done with us."

"The river's done with us," Tevita said. He was looking downstream, the way he always did when he was deciding something. "She wants to know what we're going to do with her now."

It was not a question about the boat.

I looked at the four of them — the engineer with grease permanently under her nails, the soldier learning how to dig graves and mean them, the radio operator who had kept every secret until the only secret left was the truth, the boatman who had owed a dead man a life and paid it to the living instead. Three weeks ago I had been a woman on a dock in Port Moresby with a lawyer's letter and a spinning needle and no one in the world. The river had taken my father. It had given me this.

"Downstream," I said. "All the way. And then wherever the next river goes."

Tevita nodded once, and that was the whole ceremony.

* * *

We cast off at dawn.

The *Mercy* turned her bow downstream and the current took her like a hand on the shoulder, and I stood at the rail with the wind coming up off the water and watched the Upper Fly fall away behind us — the bluffs, the cavern mouth, the three mounds on the high ground with their river stones, all of it sliding backward into the country of things that are finished.

My father had spent his life looking for what the river kept. He had died holding it. I had spent three weeks learning that the river keeps nothing for anyone — it only lends, and it remembers, and it carries you home if you let it.

Junie put on the kettle. Sefina began arguing with the engine in a language only they shared. Bram laughed at something — actually laughed, short and rusty, like a door opening after years. Tevita stood at the wheel with his hands light on it, steering by the water itself, the way his people had steered for ten thousand years.

I took out the lawyer's letter, the one that had started all of it, and read it one last time in the morning light. *Your father's expedition has gone silent.* They had it backwards, I thought, and folded it small, and dropped it over the rail, and watched the river take it.

Then I went aft to help with the lines, because there was work to do, and family to do it with, and the whole long bright river ahead.
