## 1. The Weight of Miles

I measure my life in distances. Not years—years are for men who keep ledgers, who count birthdays and anniversaries and the slow accumulation of debts. Distances are cleaner. A life is just a line drawn across a map, and if you draw it carefully enough, it never has to cross itself.

Twenty years, four hundred and eleven expeditions. I know because Teo counts them for me, keeps them in a battered notebook he thinks I don't know about. Four hundred and eleven treks over passes with names like apologies—Paso del Perdón, Col de la Veuve—and not once in all that distance have I walked back down into the valley where I was born.

This is how you stay invisible at altitude: you become useful. A guide who gets clients up and down alive is a guide nobody questions. Questions require looking at a man directly, and there's something about my face that makes people prefer to look at the horizon instead. Maybe it's the scar. Maybe it's the way I answer before they finish asking. Either way, it's served me well.

The morning the courier came, we were three days out from the lodge on a routine crossing—the Salto Traverse, twelve clients, none of them dying yet, which by October standards counted as a triumph.

* * *

Teo found me at first light, crouched over the stove behind the cook tent, performing the small theater of competence that passes for breakfast at four thousand meters. The boy is twenty-three, all elbows and questions, with the particular hunger of someone who grew up flat and fell in love with vertical.

"Isandro." He said it the way he says everything, like the word might get away from him. "There's a man at the trailhead. Says he has something for you."

"Tell him to give it to the mountain. It'll hold it longer than I will."

"He asked for you by name."

Nobody asks for me by name. That's the whole architecture of my life—Isandro Fairweather, guide-for-hire, no surname history required, no references requested. The name is a coat I wear; underneath it I am nobody from nowhere, and nowhere suits me fine.

"What name did he use?"

"Fairweather." Teo hesitated, and I watched him decide whether to say the rest. "He said—" He stopped. Started again. "He said the sender said you'd know what it meant. The seal, I mean. He wouldn't show me. Just said it was from Vega."

I burned my hand on the kettle and didn't notice until the smell reached me.

Vega. Twenty years since that word had been spoken anywhere near me, and here it was at six in the morning, riding up a mountainside on the back of some courier who probably thought he was delivering an invoice.

"Set it down," I said, "and walk away."

"It's already in your tent."

Of course it was. Couriers who climb for a living don't wait around for signatures.

* * *

I didn't open it for two days. This is a discipline I recommend to anyone: when the past arrives, let it sit. Let it freeze overnight. Let it gather the same frost as everything else at altitude, so that when you finally touch it, it feels like part of the landscape instead of what it actually is—which is a hook, baited and patient, waiting for exactly the kind of fish I've spent two decades refusing to be.

But I opened it. Of course I opened it. A man can refuse a letter for only so long before curiosity does what curiosity does, and mine had been sharpening itself against the inside of my skull for forty-eight hours.

The paper was heavy, cream-colored, absurdly formal for a document that would end up in a pack getting rained on. The seal was pressed in dark green wax: a river coiling through a stylized valley, the mark of Vega Expediciones, the company Marisol built out of the wreckage of everything our families once shared. Beneath the seal, in handwriting I recognized the way you recognize a voice in a dream—

*Señor Fairweather. I have a job only you can do. The Río Ceniza, source to mouth, before the dry season ends. Double your rate. Bring your crew. Come alone to the trailhead lodge at Puerto Ceniza within the fortnight, or I will come to you, and we both know how much you'd hate that.*

*M.V.*

And beneath that, one more line, smaller, almost gentle, which is how I knew it was the knife:

*I know about the Cordillera Negra. All of it.*

I read it four times. Then I folded it along its original creases, put it in the inside pocket of my jacket, right over the heart, which felt less like sentiment and more like carrying a stone to throw later, and went out to lie to my crew.

* * *

Lying to a crew is harder than lying to a client. Clients want reassurance; crews want truth, or at least the shape of it. And mine—they're strays, every one of them, collected over the years like stones in a boot. Rafa, who left a wife in Quito rather than explain where he goes each season. Old Berta, who cooks at altitudes where bread behaves like a grudge and talks to her pots like old friends. And Teo, who watches me the way young men watch older ones—not for instruction, but for evidence. Evidence of what a life adds up to.

That evening, around the fire, Teo sat down next to me with the deliberate casualness of a man approaching a wild animal.

"You got a letter," he said.

"I get letters."

"You burn most of them. I've seen the edges in the stove ash. Little corners with writing on them." He poked the fire. "This one you kept."

"The courier charged by weight. Seemed wasteful."

He laughed, then saw my face, then stopped laughing, which is the usual trajectory of conversations with me. "Who's Vega?"

"A company."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

He waited. The boy has learned that from me—waiting—but he hasn't yet learned that waiting works both ways, that silence is a door that swings in every direction, and eventually someone walks through it. In this case, it was me, though not far.

"There's a river," I said, "called the Ceniza. Ash River. It comes down out of the high country through a gorge no map agrees on, and at the top of it there's a place where the water starts, and the government would very much like everyone to pretend that place doesn't exist."

"Why?"

"Because pretending is cheaper than explaining." I fed a stick to the fire. "Somebody wants to go there. Somebody with money and a memory."

"And you said no."

I looked at him. "Did I?"

"You haven't said anything. But you've got the look."

"What look?"

"The one you get when you're measuring something." He shrugged, embarrassed suddenly, young again. "You do it with distances. Rafa noticed. You talk in kilometers when you're deciding things. How far to the pass, how long to the ford. Like if you can put a number on it, it can't surprise you."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the scree with a sound like pages turning.

"Twelve days to Puerto Ceniza," I heard myself say. "Three if the weather holds and we push."

Teo went very still. "We?"

"I said nothing."

"You said twelve days."

"I was thinking out loud."

"You were counting," he said softly, and there it was—that loyalty of his, arriving ahead of any good sense, the way weather arrives ahead of the storm that made it. "Counting how fast we could get there."

* * *

That night I took out the letter again and read it by headlamp, and this time I made myself look at the last line until it stopped being words and became a fact: *I know about the Cordillera Negra. All of it.*

All of it. Nobody knows all of it. I have spent twenty years making sure of that—twenty years of half-answers and changed subjects and a story about equipment failure polished smooth from repetition. The disaster on the Negra took nine men and my father's survey and whatever name I might have made for myself, and it left me holding a blame I never explained because explaining would have required telling the truth, and the truth required names, and the names belonged to powerful people, and I was twenty-four years old and tired and guilty in ways that had nothing to do with what actually happened.

So I ran. Upward, mostly. It turns out you can outrun almost anything at altitude, because altitude thins everything—air, judgment, memory. For twenty years I believed that. I built a whole philosophy on it. Measure your life in miles, never look back down the trail, and the past stays where you left it, buried under snow that never fully melts but never quite lets go either.

Marisol Vega just sent an avalanche warning down that slope.

Here is what I told myself, lying in the dark with the glacier groaning somewhere above camp like an old man in his sleep: I told myself I hadn't decided. I told myself the letter meant nothing, that I'd go to Puerto Ceniza only to refuse her in person, that double pay was an insult and a threat wrapped in cream-colored paper, and that whatever she knew about the Negra, knowing wasn't the same as proving.

I told myself all of it. And then I caught myself doing the thing Teo had named, the thing I apparently cannot stop doing even inside my own head—I measured. Twelve days to Puerto Ceniza. Ninety kilometers as the condor flies, a hundred and forty by trail. The dry season had thirty-one days left, maybe fewer. The Ceniza gorge, if the old survey maps were right—and they were right, because my father drew them—ran sixty-two kilometers from the last checkpoint to the source.

Sixty-two kilometers to the place where my father walked into the mountains and did not come back.

Sixty-two kilometers to the records that would prove who he was, and who I am, and what really happened on the day the world decided I was the one to blame.

I lay there hating myself for counting, and counted anyway, and somewhere past midnight I understood that the decision had already been made—not by me, but by arithmetic. The numbers had voted. All that remained was for the rest of me to catch up.

In the morning I broke camp early, while the others slept, and stood looking east toward the haze where the low country began, where rivers gathered themselves and ran down toward towns and lodges and a woman with a green wax seal and twenty years of patience.

Behind me, boots on gravel. Teo, wrapped in his sleeping bag like a misbehaving burrito, watching me with those eyes that missed nothing and forgave most of it.

"Puerto Ceniza?" he said.

I should have denied it. Instead I heard myself say, "A hundred and forty kilometers."

His grin split his whole face. "Then we'd better start walking."

And that—that easy, terrible joy—is how I knew the invisible life was over. Because you cannot be unfindable once you start walking toward something instead of away from it, and the Ceniza runs uphill, and everything I'd buried at its source was about to come loose with the meltwater.
