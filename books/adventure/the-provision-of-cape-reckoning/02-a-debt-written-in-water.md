## 2. A Debt Written in Water

The courier arrived at the lodge on a mule that hated him, which I respected. Everything else about the man was wet and apologetic—his coat, his papers, his smile—and he held out the oilskin packet like it might bite him.

"It's marked for Fairweather Expeditions," he said.

"Then you've made a mistake. There's no such thing."

He looked at me the way people look at a locked door they've been paid to open. "Sir, I was told to put this in your hand or walk back down the pass without it. The mule prefers the second option."

I took the packet. It was heavier than paper had any right to be, and it smelled of wax and river water, which should have told me everything before I broke the seal. Inside: a contract on stock so fine it felt like skin, a set of permits stamped in three colors of ink I'd only ever seen on documents meant to be destroyed rather than read, and a map.

The map was the problem. Not because maps are rare—I own forty, and half of them lie—but because this one showed the Río Ceniza from its mouth to its headwaters, drawn in a hand I recognized, corrected in margins by another hand I knew better than my own. My father's survey lines ran up the left bank like stitches closing a wound. Someone had traced them recently, in red, all the way to a small inked star at the top of the world where no mapmaker is supposed to go.

At the bottom, in a woman's slanted script: *You know where to find me. Or I'll find you. Either way, we talk.*

Marisol Vega.

I fed the contract to the stove first. Then the permits, which hurt, because even forged things that beautiful deserve a longer life. The map I held over the flames for a long time, watching the red line curl and blacken, and then I didn't burn it. That was my first mistake of the season, though not, as it turned out, my last.

* * *

She came up the trail four days later, which told me she'd known exactly how long I'd take to decide nothing. No mule this time. Marisol Vega climbed the last switchback in town boots, dust to her knees, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip torn from an official banner, and she was smiling the way weather smiles before it turns.

"Isandro." She said my name like she was setting it down on a table between us. "You look terrible."

"You look expensive."

"One of us has been doing well." She stepped past me into the lodge without waiting for an invitation, because Marisol has never once waited for anything, and stopped at the stove, warming her hands over the ashes of her own contract. "You burned it."

"I burned all of it."

"The map?"

"Most of the map."

She turned, and there it was—the look I remembered from twenty years ago, when we were two young fools arguing about rope lengths on the Cordillera Negra. Amusement laid over something harder, the way frost lies over rock. "Most," she repeated. "So you kept the part with the star on it."

I said nothing. She took off her gloves finger by finger, enjoying herself.

"There's a room upstairs," I said finally. "Teo will bring you tea. We can talk in the morning like civilized people."

"We're not civilized people, Isandro. That's the entire point of you." She sat down at the long table anyway, unrolled a fresh copy of the map—of course she had copies; she probably had the printing press—and weighted the corners with her cup, a tin of matches, and a stone she'd picked up off my own trail. "Sit down. I'll say it once, and then I'll buy your crew dinner, and then you can pretend to think about it overnight if it helps your dignity."

Against every instinct I have cultivated over twenty careful years, I sat.

* * *

Here is what she offered: double pay for every member of my crew, hazard bonuses written into the contract instead of promised verbally and denied later, permits that would carry us through three checkpoints that officially do not exist, and a resupply cache already positioned halfway up the gorge. Here is what she wanted: to follow the Ceniza upstream to its source before the dry season ended and the high trails closed. Six weeks. Maybe five, if the weather turned mean early.

Here is what she did not offer: a reason.

"A river's source," I said. "There's ice up there and a view. You could buy the view."

"I could buy the mountain." She tapped the star. "But not this. This stretch belongs to nobody, which means it belongs to whoever gets there first. My company wants the water rights settled before someone else draws the boundary."

"Your company has lawyers for boundaries."

"My company has lawyers for *paper* boundaries." Her finger came down harder. "This one's written in glacier melt, and it's moving."

I pushed back from the table. "No."

"You haven't heard the rest."

"There isn't a rest that changes the answer. Marisol, I lead treks. Flower meadows. Old men who want to see condors. I don't run expeditions into country where the checkpoints take bribes and the last survey party that went up that river never came back down."

Something moved behind her face—not surprise, not quite. Recalculation. "Never came back down," she said softly. "Is that the story you tell? Or the story you were given?"

"Don't."

"Because I've read the other story." She stood too, and now the amusement was gone entirely, and what was underneath it was older and sadder and much more dangerous. "The Cordillera Negra. Twenty years ago. A survey led by Tomás Fairweather, abandoned mid-season, its guide struck off the rolls in two countries, its leader's body never recovered." She let that sit in the cold air of the lodge. "I know what happened up there, Isandro. All of it. And I know what you traded to keep the rest of it quiet."

The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, Teo was chopping wood with the steady rhythm of a young man who has never once considered running anywhere.

"If you know," I said, and my voice came out lower than I intended, "then you know why the answer is no."

"I know why the answer *was* no. For twenty years, very impressively." She began rolling up the map, unhurried, a woman packing away a weapon she could always unpack again. "But answers get old, Isandro. Debts don't. And there's a debt written into that river with both our names on it, and the dry season ends in six weeks, and after that the water goes and takes the evidence with it." She tucked the roll under her arm and smiled at me, and it was almost the old smile, almost kind. "Dinner for your crew tonight. Think about it. Or don't think about it—it doesn't matter. You were always going to come. You just hadn't admitted it yet."

She went upstairs. I stood alone in the lodge with the ashes of her contract and the corner of a map I hadn't burned, and I realized my hands were shaking, which they had not done since the last time I stood above moving water and made a choice.

* * *

She bought the dinner, as promised. Roast goat, flatbread, a crate of beer hauled up the pass by a mule that presumably hated everyone equally. My crew ate like the grateful starving men they were and pretended not to notice that their employer sat apart from the table, drinking nothing, watching the door as if the mountains themselves might walk in and object.

They noticed anyway. Of course they did. I trained them to notice.

It was Teo who came over afterward, while the others washed pans and Marisol's voice drifted down from upstairs, talking permits with Rosa in the low fluent tones of a woman who has never been refused anything except, apparently, by me. Teo sat across from me and put his elbows on the table, which he knows I hate, which is why he does it.

"So," he said. "Double pay."

"It's not happening."

"Hazard bonuses. In writing."

"Not happening."

"Permits through the closed checkpoints." He leaned back. "Jefe, I've been reading the regulations since I was fourteen. Those permits aren't just good, they're *impossible*. Nobody stamps those colors anymore. Where did she even—" He caught my expression and changed course, the way young men do when they realize the current is stronger than they thought. "The crew talked. While you were out checking the lines. We voted."

"You voted."

"Quietly. Unanimously." He had the decency to look slightly sorry about it, which is more decency than the rest of them managed. "Rosa's mother needs the operation. Dov owes money to people who don't write contracts. Pilar hasn't been home in three years because she can't afford the crossing. And me—" He shrugged. "I'd follow you up that river for free and you know it. But I wouldn't mind eating meat for a year afterward."

"And if I say no?"

He met my eyes. He's twenty-three and still growing into his own shoulders, but he held the look, and I felt something in my chest shift like scree underfoot. "Then we say no. You're the anchor, jefe. That's the whole arrangement." He paused. "But you should know we'd all understand if the anchor wanted to move."

He went off to help with the pans. I sat there a long while, listening to the lodge settle, the wind come down off the pass, and upstairs the murmur of a woman buying my crew's loyalty for the price of a meal she could expense.

Cornered. That was the word for it, and I knew it, and knowing it didn't help. A man can refuse a rival. A man can refuse a threat. But a man cannot easily refuse five hungry loyal people who have quietly decided to trust him with their winter, especially when the alternative—the thing Marisol held over him like a blade made of old water—is the one story he has spent twenty years making sure no one would ever need to hear.

I went up the stairs. Her door was open; she was waiting, of course, sitting on the sill with the map across her knees, and she didn't gloat, which was somehow worse.

"Six weeks," she said.

"Five," I said. "If the weather turns mean early, we turn around, and you pay full rate for a partial route, and you never mention the Cordillera Negra to me again."

For a moment she just looked at me, and I saw something flicker there that wasn't triumph at all—something that looked almost like grief, quickly buried. "Deal," she said.

I went down to tell the crew. They received the news the way strays receive food: quietly, watchfully, already planning. And it wasn't until I was back at the stove, feeding it the last unburned corner of the map—the corner with the star, the corner I'd sworn I'd keep only as evidence against her—that I understood what she'd done.

She hadn't hired a guide. She'd hired a man who couldn't afford to ask where she'd learned his father's name.

Above the lodge, the wind shifted, and for the first time that season it carried the smell of the high country: stone, ice, and something faintly mineral, like water remembering where it came from.
