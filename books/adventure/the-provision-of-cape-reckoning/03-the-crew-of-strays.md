## 3. The Crew of Strays

I've been guiding long enough to know that every expedition crew is a family you didn't get to choose, assembled by someone who did. Most crews, though, are made of people with ordinary reasons for being there: rent, adventure, a divorce they're outrunning at walking pace. This one was different. This one was a collection of people who had, each in their own way, gone missing on purpose.

Teo here — that's me, by the way, the youngest by a decade and the only one who still thought this was going to be an adventure.

* * *

The lodge at Puerto Ceniza smelled like woodsmoke and old rope, and it was there, three days after Marisol Vega's contract turned Isandro's face into something I'd never seen before, that the crew began to arrive.

First came Berta Ochoa, our medic, who stepped off the supply truck carrying a canvas bag older than I am and a expression that suggested she'd already catalogued everyone's likely injuries and found us all wanting. She'd been blacklisted from the provincial hospitals, though nobody knew exactly why, and she had the particular silence of a woman who has stopped explaining herself to strangers. She looked at me once, noted my age, and said, "You'll do until someone better comes along," which I later learned was her version of affection.

Then Ruy Salcedo, our rigger, who arrived without luggage beyond a coil of rope he carried like a sleeping child. Ruy hadn't spoken above a whisper in four years — not since whatever happened in the mines up north, which nobody asked about because asking Ruy questions was like asking a locked door why it was locked. He communicated in gestures, in knots tied and untied, in the way he'd tap twice on your shoulder if he trusted you and not at all if he didn't. By the end of the first day, he'd tapped my shoulder twice. I nearly cried.

Last came Odalys Ferrán, our cook and quartermaster, who arrived with more baggage than the rest of us combined and refused to discuss any of it. She'd been the head cook at a hotel in the capital before, according to rumor, she'd set fire to the kitchen — deliberately, some said; accidentally, others insisted — and she cooked now with the intensity of a woman proving something to somebody who wasn't present. Her food was extraordinary. Her silences were longer.

And then there was Isandro Fairweather, who wasn't a stray at all, or so we'd all believed. Isandro was the anchor. Isandro was the man who showed up when you said you would, who remembered your mother's name, who kept the ledgers balanced and the ropes inspected and the young guides from doing anything permanently stupid. For six years I'd worked under him and I had built my whole understanding of what a good man looked like out of the materials he provided.

Which is why watching him that week was like watching a house discover its foundation is sand.

* * *

He moved through the preparations like a man being dragged behind his own expedition. He checked the rigging twice as often as usual. He re-read Marisol's permits until the paper went soft at the folds. When Berta asked him a direct question about altitude protocols, he answered correctly but a half-second late, as if translating from some other language first.

"He's scared," Berta told me quietly, on the second evening, while we repacked the medical stores. "Not of the mountain."

"Of what, then?"

She tied off a bandage roll with unnecessary force. "Of arriving."

I didn't understand her then. I want to note that clearly, for the record: I did not understand, and I want credit for at least admitting it.

Because here's what I knew about Isandro Fairweather: nothing, essentially. Six years, and I knew he took his coffee bitter, slept on his left side, counted steps under his breath when he was thinking, and went quiet in a specific way around rivers. I knew he sent no letters and received almost none. I knew he flinched — actually flinched, a small thing, quickly mastered — whenever anyone mentioned the Cordillera Negra, which most people didn't, because most people had learned not to mention anything that made Isandro go quiet.

We called ourselves his crew. We were, if I'm honest, his evidence that a man could live twenty years without a past and still be worth following.

* * *

The reveal came on the third day, and it came sideways, the way real things usually do.

A woman came to the lodge gate selling fruit — small hard plums and a sack of something like apricots that grew only in the high valley, she said, sweetest ones in the province, and she said it in the valley dialect, that clipped, singing speech the lowlanders imitate badly and outsiders don't bother with at all.

Odalys haggled with her in standard Spanish and got nowhere. The seller named a price; Odalys countered; the seller smiled the patient smile of a woman who has all day and knows you don't.

And Isandro, standing at the edge of the courtyard with a manifest in his hand, said — without looking up, without any apparent decision to do it — "Dile que si baja un tercio el precio, le compro las dos talegas y le devuelvo la canasta mañana."

The valley dialect. Fluent. Unhurried. The accent of someone who'd learned it young, from the inside, the way you learn a mother's voice.

The seller's head came up sharply. She looked at him the way you look at a stranger who turns out to be wearing your brother's coat. Then she laughed, delighted, and answered in kind, fast and teasing, and they went back and forth for two full minutes while the rest of us stood frozen among the crates. I caught maybe one word in five. Berta caught less. Ruy watched with his whole body, the way he watched anything that mattered.

When she left, plums sold and basket promised back, the courtyard was very quiet.

"Valley dialect," Odalys said finally, in the tone of a woman adding a line to a ledger she'd been keeping longer than any of us knew.

"Learned it on treks," Isandro said, not looking at anyone.

"You just conjugated a subjunctive," Berta said. "Nobody learns a subjunctive on a trek."

Isandro put the manifest down very carefully, the way a man puts down something he'd rather throw, and said, "Load the mules at dawn," and walked into the lodge, and that was the end of the conversation, officially.

Unofficially, it had only begun.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, which was normal, and so I sat on the loading dock, which was also normal, and at some point Ruy appeared beside me with two cups of Odalys's terrible chicory, which was not normal at all, because Ruy didn't offer things to people. He handed me a cup and sat and drank and said nothing, and the nothing was comfortable, and after a while he tapped the dock boards twice with one knuckle — his gesture, the trust-tap — and pointed toward the window where Isandro's lamp still burned.

I understood him perfectly. Some people talk. Ruy pointed.

"Why does he hide it?" I asked aloud, mostly to hear how the question sounded.

Ruy shrugged, elaborately, a whole-body performance of *how should I know*, and then held up one finger, waited, and drew in the dust of the dock with a stick: a small square — a house — and beside it a smaller figure, and between them a long line, the kind of line you draw on maps.

Leaving home. That was his theory. That was everyone's theory, probably, spoken or drawn. Isandro Fairweather had left somewhere once, and left it so completely that even his own language had stayed behind, folded up and put away like a suit from a life that ended.

Here's the thing about curiosity, though, at least mine: it doesn't stay soft. It hardens. It goes from *I wonder* to *I will find out* to, eventually, somewhere along the line without my noticing, *and whoever hurt him can expect to hear from me about it*. Somewhere between the plums and the dust-drawing, my wondering became loyalty. I decided, sitting on that dock with a mute rigger and bad chicory, that whatever Isandro was running from, we — this crew of strays, this family nobody chose — would be running alongside him. Not dragging him. Alongside.

I just hadn't yet understood that he wasn't running from something behind us.

He was being pulled toward something ahead.

* * *

We loaded the mules at dawn, as ordered. And it was during the loading, in the gray half-light, that I saw the last piece of the puzzle — or rather, the piece that proved the puzzle was bigger than I'd imagined.

Isandro was checking the harnesses, working his way down the line, murmuring to the animals the way he always did, and I realized he wasn't murmuring Spanish. It was the dialect again, soft, automatic, the private language of a man talking to himself in the only tongue that felt like himself. And one phrase came clear across the yard, repeated twice, gentle as a lullaby:

*"Con calma, viejo. Vamos a casa."*

Easy, old man. We're going home.

He said it to the mule. But he wasn't looking at the mule. He was looking upriver — up the gorge, toward the source of the Río Ceniza, toward whatever Marisol Vega's map marked and his whole carefully constructed invisibility was arranged to avoid — and his face had none of the dragged look from the past week. For one unguarded moment, before he caught me watching and shuttered it away, Isandro Fairweather looked like a man going home against his better judgment, and wanting to.

By noon Marisol's trucks arrived at the trailhead, gleaming and wrong against the dust, and Isandro gave the order to move out in flat, flawless, anonymous Spanish.

But I'd heard it. We all had, in our separate ways. And as we started up the river road, I found myself walking a little closer to him than I needed to — close enough to catch him if the dragging got worse, close enough, maybe, to learn what *home* meant to a man who claimed he didn't have one.

The river, meanwhile, ran beside us, narrow and quick and cold, going the same direction we were.

Toward everything.
