## 4. Downstream of Every Law

Maps, I have always believed, are a form of polite fiction. They are drawn by people who assume the world will hold still long enough to be measured. The Río Ceniza had never once held still. Every map we carried showed the gorge as a tidy blue thread stitched between two ranges, and every one of them was lying by at least a kilometer, because the river moved its bed each flood season the way a restless sleeper moves in the night.

"You knew that," Teo said, watching me fold the survey sheet away without ceremony. "You knew the maps were wrong and you let us plan on them anyway."

"I let us *pack* on them," I said. "Planning is a different animal."

He didn't laugh. None of them did, that first morning in the gorge, when the walls closed in and the light went from gold to a thin greenish gray, like looking up from the bottom of a well. The Ceniza gorge does not welcome you. It tolerates you, briefly, the way a landlord tolerates a tenant he intends to evict.

We entered at dawn through a notch in the limestone that the locals call *la Garganta*—the Throat—and by midmorning I understood why. The canyon swallowed sound. Our boots, our ropes, our voices, all of it dropped into the water noise and came out flattened, so that the crew spoke less and less until we were moving in single file with nothing between us but the river's low constant argument with the stones.

Marisol rode the lead mule for the first hour, which told me everything about how the trip was going to go. She'd brought permits—real ones, stamped and sealed, the kind that open checkpoints for men whose names aren't on any blacklist—and she wore them in a oilcloth wallet against her chest like a second heart.

"First checkpoint is at the Narrows," she said, not looking at me. "The sergeant there takes cash, but he counts it twice. Don't insult him with small bills."

"You've bribed him before."

"I've *paid* him before. Bribery implies illegality. This is a toll." She finally turned, and her smile had twenty years of rust on it. "Though I suppose you wouldn't know the difference, Isandro. You've spent two decades pretending the law simply forgot about you."

"The law has an excellent memory. That's the problem."

* * *

The checkpoint at the Narrows was exactly what she'd promised: a plank hut wedged between rock walls so tight the soldiers had strung their laundry across the gap like festival bunting. A sergeant with a face like a closed ledger looked over our papers, then over our faces, then—longest of all—over mine.

I kept my eyes on the middle distance, the way you do when you want to be furniture. It's a skill. Twenty years of practice. But the sergeant's gaze snagged on me anyway, the way cloth snags on a nail.

"Your guide," he said to Marisol. "He looks familiar."

"He looks like every weathered man in this province," Marisol said smoothly. "It's the sun. Terrible thing, the sun."

The sergeant grunted, counted the bills twice as promised, and waved us through. But as we passed, he said—quietly, just to me, or so he thought—"Fairweather. Like the guide who died on the Negra."

"Not him," I said, and walked on, and felt his eyes on my back the whole length of the gorge beyond.

Teo caught up with me at the first bend. "That man knew you."

"That man knew a name."

"Isandro—"

"Watch the footing," I said. "The gravel here rolls."

It was a coward's answer and we both knew it. He fell back, but I could feel the question riding behind me like a third mule.

* * *

By afternoon the gorge narrowed until the sky was a ribbon overhead and the river ran brown and fast beside us, swollen with snowmelt though the dry season was eating it alive. Marisol dismounted and walked, which surprised me; she moved well for a woman who'd spent her life in offices, picking her line through the talus with more sense than half my crew.

"You learned that somewhere," I said, before I could stop myself.

"My father took me into these hills when I was nine," she said. "Before the family decided mountains were beneath us. He wanted me to love them." A pause. "He stopped wanting things after your father's expedition failed. Did you know that? Men who come back from a disaster are never quite the same shape."

"Your father wasn't on the Negra."

"No. But he signed the report that blamed you." She said it flatly, factually, the way you'd note the weather, and then stepped over a slick of green stone as if she hadn't just handed me a live coal. "I'm telling you because you'll hear it eventually, and I'd rather you hear it walking than bleeding."

"You're all generosity, Marisol."

"I'm honest, which is rarer." She glanced back at me. "You could ask why I'm telling you now. You could even say thank you."

"I could do a lot of things."

"Yes," she said. "That's what I'm counting on."

* * *

The rockslide came an hour later, the way rockslides do—not announced, not gradual, but simply *decided upon* by the mountain.

I heard it before anything else: a sound above the water noise, a dry clicking, like dice shaken in a cup. Twenty years ago I would have known instantly what it meant. Twenty years of avoiding exactly this kind of country had dulled the reflex, but not killed it. My body remembered before my mind did.

"Off the wall!" I shouted. "Everyone off the wall—*now!*"

They scattered. Most of them made the open ground near the riverbank. Teo did not. He'd been traversing a shelf of broken limestone with the rope coiled over his shoulder, and the shelf went with him—a whole slab of it, peeling away from the cliff face like bark off a dead tree, taking him down into the slide path.

I don't remember deciding to move. That's the part I've never been able to explain to anyone who hasn't stood where I stood. There is no decision. There is only the interval between the stone starting and the stone arriving, and what you do inside it.

I crossed maybe thirty meters of scree in a time no stopwatch would believe. The slide was still coming—small stuff first, then the big blocks, the mountain clearing its throat—and Teo was on his back in the fall line, winded, staring up at a boulder the size of a table swinging down toward him with terrible patience.

I hit him at the hips and drove us both sideways, off the shelf's debris fan, into the hollow behind a granite rib that some older, wiser flood had left standing. The boulder passed close enough that I felt its wind. Then the world filled with dust and thunder and the sound of the mountain finishing its sentence.

When it stopped, Teo was coughing under me, and I was holding him down with both hands, and neither of us was dead.

"How," he managed finally. "How did you *move* like that?"

"Adrenaline," I said.

"That wasn't adrenaline. That was—you read the slope, you picked the rib, you knew the hollow was there before the dust cleared. Nobody knows that. That's not trail-guide knowledge, Isandro. That's—" He stopped, because he'd heard it himself, the word he was reaching for. "That's rescue knowledge."

I got off him and stood and brushed the dust from my sleeves with hands that were not entirely steady.

"I told you," I said. "I used to work bigger mountains."

"You told us you quit."

"I did."

"Why?"

Above us, the last stones were still settling. Below us, the river went on arguing with its stones, indifferent as arithmetic. And Marisol stood at the edge of the slide field with her arms folded, watching me with an expression I recognized from twenty years ago—the look of someone who has just watched a man do the thing he swore he couldn't do, and is filing it away.

"Because the last time I worked a big mountain," I said, "people died, and I was the reason they wrote it down that way."

Nobody spoke. The gorge held our silence the way it held everything—briefly, and at interest.

Then Marisol unfolded her arms. "There's a campsite past the next bend," she said, briskly, terribly kindly. "And tomorrow we reach the stretch where the maps give up entirely. Rest while you can, Fairweather. The river gets honest from here."

She turned and walked, and the crew followed, and Teo lingered a moment longer, looking at me with something new in his face—not doubt, exactly. Recalculation.

That night I lay awake listening to the Ceniza run below the tents, cold and quick and going somewhere I'd sworn never to return to. Somewhere upstream, in country no map admitted to, my father's records waited under stone. And somewhere downstream, a sergeant with a ledger face was probably already writing a letter about a familiar-looking guide.

The past, I was beginning to understand, doesn't chase you. It just walks beside you, patient as water, waiting for the gorge to narrow.
