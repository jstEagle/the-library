## 6. The Dry Season's Bargain

There are three ways to measure a day on the Ceniza, and by the ninth day of the climb I had stopped liking all of them. You could measure it in meters gained, which sounded impressive and felt like nothing. You could measure it in blisters, which was honest but demoralizing. Or you could measure it in water—the way Isandro did, silently, every morning, tipping each canteen to the light like a priest checking whether the saints were still home.

By the ninth day the saints were mostly gone.

"Rationing starts now," Isandro announced at dawn, in the tone of a man reading a verdict he'd already written. "Half liters. We refill at the seep above the narrows if it hasn't dried. Nobody drinks from the river."

"Because of the ash," said Ruthie, our cook, who had a voice like gravel learning to be polite.

"Because of the ash," Isandro agreed.

The Ceniza ran gray-green below us, carrying down silt from some collapse far upstream, and even the mules wouldn't drink it. That was the bargain of the dry season, the one nobody had put in the contract: the river we were following was dying as we climbed it, and we were racing its corpse to the source. Every day there was less of it. Every day the air got thinner and the sun got more personal about it.

I did the arithmetic in my head, because that's what I do instead of sleeping. Nine people. Fourteen canteens. A seep that might or might not exist. And somewhere above us, a glacier that the maps swore still fed the river, though the maps also swore the trail was passable, and the trail had tried to eat Ruthie twice since Tuesday.

Hope, it turns out, rations about the same as water. You give everyone a little in the morning and tell them there's more at the next camp, and you try not to think about what happens when the next camp is a lie.

* * *

The rival outfit showed up on the eleventh day, the way trouble always does—first as a rumor, then as a smudge of dust in the wrong canyon, then as a campfire glow on a ridge we'd vacated that same afternoon.

"They're reading our mail," Ruthie muttered. She'd spotted the glow from the latrine trench, which meant she'd earned the right to be bitter about it.

"Someone's feeding them our route," said Chelo, our wrangler, spitting with the precision of a man who considered spitting a form of punctuation.

Marisol stood at the canyon rim with her binoculars for a long time. When she lowered them her face had done something complicated and settled on cold. "Vásquez's people," she said. "I know the orange tarps."

"Your company's tarps," Isandro said. Not a question.

"My company has many departments," Marisol said. "Not all of them love me."

"Must be exhausting," Isandro said, "being surrounded by people who know exactly what you're worth."

I expected her to cut him. She had a whole repertoire—twenty years of unfinished argument, sharpened daily at breakfast. But she just looked at him, and for a second she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with altitude, and she said, quietly, "Yes," and walked away, and Isandro watched her go with an expression I couldn't file anywhere.

That night I took first watch, which mostly meant sitting on a cold rock listening to the river get quieter. Somewhere across the canyon a lantern moved, patient as a tick. I counted our water again. Eleven canteens. I counted their crew. I couldn't tell. I counted the reasons we were still ahead of them, and got to two: Isandro knew this country like a scar, and Vásquez's people didn't.

I was still counting when I smelled smoke that wasn't a campfire.

* * *

It came from the boulder field below camp, thin and chemical—paper smoke, not wood. I picked my way down through the frost-slick rocks, telling myself it was a stray ember, a dropped match, Ruthie burning the rice again.

It was Isandro.

He crouched against a boulder with a letter in his hands, one corner already black, feeding it to the flame a little at a time like a man paying off a debt in installments. The firelight did things to his face I'd never seen on a trail—made him look younger and worse, both at once. There was a whole page still unread, and I could see his eyes on it, and I could see that he was reading it the way you read something you've memorized and are checking for changes.

"Teo." He didn't look up. Of course he didn't. The man could hear a mule misstep at four hundred meters.

"Paper's precious up here," I said. "Ruthie'll be upset. She's been saving the good kind for her will."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then the flame took another corner and he watched it go, and I crouched down across from him because my legs decided before I did.

"Who's it from?" I asked.

"Nobody."

"Nobody writes letters that long to nobody."

The wind came up the canyon and the flame flattened and he shielded it with his palm, an old reflex, and I realized he'd done this before. Burned this exact letter, or its siblings, in exactly this posture, on a dozen cold nights in a dozen places I'd never seen him. This was a habit, and habits that old are just wounds with good posture.

"Isandro." I said his name the way he said *rationing*—flat, final. "I'm the youngest one here, not the dumbest one. Somebody's been writing you for years. Somebody who knows where you are, which is more than most people can claim. And you burn every one before anyone can see the handwriting."

The fire ate the last of the page. He closed his fist around the ash and let the wind take it, and for a long moment there was just the river, faint below us, running out of itself.

"It's my sister," he said at last. "Lucía."

There it was. I'd have bet my whole season's pay on it, and I'd have been broke and right.

"And she says?"

"She says the same thing she's said for twenty years." His voice was level, which is how you know a man is spending everything he has to keep it there. "That the door's open. That the valley remembers me. That there's a room with my name on it if I ever—" He stopped. Started again, lower. "She writes it every year on the same date. Like a report. Like she's filing evidence that I still exist."

"And you burn it."

"I read it first."

"That's supposed to make it better?"

"It's supposed to make it *mine*," he said, and there was an edge on it now, the first sharp thing he'd ever pointed at me. "Teo. You don't know what it is to have a name that costs something. You walk into a valley with my name and doors close. You walk in with my face, worse. She's offering me a room in a house where half the town still crosses itself when my family's mentioned. That's not a door. That's a debt with curtains."

"Then pay it," I said. "You're good at debts. You've been paying this crew's wages out of your own pocket since Chelo's knee went. You paid for Ruthie's mother's funeral. You think nobody notices, but we're guides, we count things." I leaned forward. "You've spent twenty years paying everyone except the one person who left the door open."

The silence after that was the kind that has weight. Above us the stars were brutal and close. Below us the rival outfit's lantern had gone out, which meant they were either sleeping or moving, and I didn't like either option.

"You think belonging is a place," Isandro said finally, and his voice had gone quiet again, the dangerous quiet. "A valley. A house. A room with your name on it. It's not. Belonging is a thing you flee, Teo, because the moment you stand still somewhere, somewhere else starts needing you, and then you have to choose, and choosing is how men like me end up—"

"Blacklisted?" I said. "Or *found*?"

He looked at me then, really looked, and I watched him decide whether I was a crewmate or a boy. It took him a second too long, and that second hurt more than the whole argument.

"Go back to your watch," he said.

"I'm not done."

"You are." He stood, brushing ash from his knees with terrible care. "Because you're right, and I can't afford right, not on this mountain, not with Vásquez behind us and a dead river ahead. Ask me again at the source. Ask me again when there's nothing left to hide behind but sky."

"That's a coward's appointment," I said. "You'll never keep it."

"I know," he said, and climbed back toward camp, and the worst part—the part I'm still carrying—was that he said it like a man keeping a promise.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat on my rock and did the arithmetic again, but this time I counted different things. I counted how Isandro's shoulders had dropped when he said his sister's name, like setting down something he'd been holding so long his arms had forgotten their shape. I counted the way he'd said *ask me again at the source*—not *never*, not *don't*, but *later*, which from Isandro was practically a signed confession.

And I counted the canyon, because around the fourth hour the quiet changed. The river below us had gone almost silent, its voice down to a whisper, and into that whisper came a sound that didn't belong: the click of a boot on stone, upstream of us, where nobody should be.

I put out the watch fire with my boot and woke Isandro with a hand on his shoulder, and he was awake before I finished the sentence, the way he always is, the way a man is when he's spent twenty years expecting the past to come find him.

"Vásquez?" he murmured.

"I don't know." I pointed upstream, to the black notch of the narrows. "They're not behind us anymore."

Isandro was on his feet, and above us, faint and wrong, the wind carried a smell I'd only read about in the old survey manuals he thought none of us had studied: cold stone and old iron, the smell of high ice.

The glacier was close. So was everything else.
