## 7. Blacklist Country

The ford at Quebrada Hondo was the kind of place a mapmaker draws with a shrug—a narrowing where the Ceniza squeezed itself between two walls of slate and came out the other side angrier. I'd crossed it twice in my life, both times alone, both times without anyone shooting at me. I had hoped to keep the streak.

We reached it on the ninth day out from the gorge, with the dry season pressing down like a thumb and the river running thin enough to argue about. Thin was good. Thin meant we could cross without losing the rope line or our tempers. What thin did not mean was safe, because for three days Teo had been reporting dust plumes behind us that Marisol insisted were heat shimmer and I knew better than to believe anything that convenient.

"Shimmer doesn't hold formation," I told her, crouched at the water's edge, testing the current with my staff. "Shimmer doesn't stop when you stop."

Marisol stood with her arms crossed, silhouetted against the far bank's scree, looking—there was no other word for it—regal. Even at altitude, even unwashed, even wearing the same field jacket she'd worn since the trailhead, she managed to look like someone who owned the landscape rather than rented it. It was one of her more infuriating talents.

"It stops," she said, "because it's nothing. Because you want it to be something so you have an excuse to turn around."

"I don't need an excuse. I've never needed an excuse." I straightened up. "I need a reason, and double pay stopped qualifying around day four."

Behind us, the crew waited with the particular patience of people who had learned that watching Isandro and Marisol argue was free entertainment and possibly educational. Teo sat on a boulder with his canteen held in both hands like something holy. Rosa, our cook and unofficial conscience, was already eyeing the far bank for a campsite. Old Bautista—the only man older than me on this expedition, which made him ancient by any honest measure—was cleaning his rifle with the calm of a man who had also noticed the dust.

"The ford first," Marisol said. "Argue after."

It was sound advice, which is why I should have distrusted it.

* * *

A ford is crossed in stages: rope line first, then the heavy packs ferried by the strongest swimmers, then the people, then whatever you can't afford to lose last. We got through the rope line fine. We got the packs across fine. We were halfway through ferrying the people when the first shot cracked off the canyon wall above us and turned the afternoon into arithmetic.

Not warning shots. Whoever they were—and I had my suspicions, suspicions shaped exactly like the rival outfit Marisol kept pretending didn't exist—they'd done their homework. The first round took out our rope anchor on the near side. The second put a splash in the Ceniza six inches from Teo's hip as he waded back for the medicine chest.

"Down!" I shouted, and the word barely left my mouth before the far bank answered.

They weren't on the far bank. That was the ugly genius of it. They'd come down the north fork during the night and set themselves above the narrows, on the high shelf where the slate overhung the water, and now they had what every ambush dreams of: height, cover, and a target that couldn't scatter. Six of us in the open channel, packs piled on the gravel bar in the middle like an offering, and the crossing too narrow to flank.

Rosa went down first—not shot, thank God, but bowled over by the current when she panicked and lost her footing. Bautista hauled her up by the collar without ever putting down his rifle, which told you everything about Bautista. Teo made the gravel bar and flattened himself behind the pack pile. And Marisol—

Marisol was still in the water.

She'd been mid-crossing when the shooting started, and instead of doing the sensible thing and dropping flat in the shallows, she'd done the Marisol thing and kept coming, head down, pushing against the current like it owed her money. A round spanged off the rocks above her and a second found the gravel at her feet, and then her boot caught on the slick slate shelf under the surface and the river took her sideways.

I've written elsewhere—I've said elsewhere, to anyone who'd listen—that I don't do heroics anymore. Heroics are how a man ends up blacklisted in two countries. But there is a difference between heroics and reflex, and reflex is just twenty years of muscle memory refusing to take the hint.

I was in the water before I decided to be. The Ceniza at the ford was maybe chest-deep in the channel, but chest-deep and fast is enough to kill you if it wants to, and it wanted to. I caught Marisol's jacket at the shoulder seam as she cartwheeled past me, and the river hit us both like a debt collector, and for about ten seconds the world was white noise and slate-gray water and the taste of glacial silt.

Then the eddy below the bar caught us—it always catches you, if you know where it lives; I'd mapped this ford in another life—and threw us up onto the gravel like two fish someone had decided not to keep.

Marisol was bleeding from the scalp where the rocks had introduced themselves. Her left arm hung wrong. Not broken-wrong, I judged, getting hands on her while bullets chewed the air overhead—dislocated, maybe, or a bad sprain, the shoulder having been asked to do something shoulders decline. She was cursing in three languages, two of which I hadn't heard since childhood, and the third of which was mine.

"Stay down," I said.

"You think?" she snarled, and then, because she was Marisol, "The packs—"

"Forget the packs."

"Forget the—" She tried to sit up. I put her back down with less gentleness than she deserved. "Isandro. The records case. The green one. If they get the green case—"

I looked at the gravel bar. The green case was sitting in plain view between the food sacks and Teo's elbow, and Teo, bless his reckless heart, had already understood. He was dragging it toward the waterline, low, using the packs for cover, moving with the awkward crab-scuttle of a man who has read about combat but never been introduced.

Bautista solved the rest. From somewhere on the south bank he put three rounds onto the shelf above the narrows—not at anyone in particular, just at the ledge lip, chipping rock into their sightlines—and whoever they were, they'd apparently budgeted for a skirmish, not a siege. The firing slackened, moved, and died away upstream, the way a retreat sounds when it's trying to sound like a repositioning.

By full dark we had counted ourselves—six, all breathing—and inventoried what remained. Which was: most of the food, half the medical kit, neither of the tents, all of the rope, and the green case, silt-stained but intact. Gone were the satellite phone, the spare batteries, and roughly forty years of my remaining patience.

"They'll be back," Bautista said, spitting into the fire. "That was a probe. Somebody paid for a probe."

"Somebody inside Vega Consolidated," Rosa said, not looking at Marisol, which was its own kind of accusation.

Marisol sat propped against a boulder with her arm bound in a sling Rosa had improvised from a shirt, gray-faced and furious about being gray-faced. "If there's a leak in my company, I will find them myself and—"

"And what?" I said. I hadn't meant to say it loudly. It came out loudly anyway. "You'll bury them the way your family buried everything else?"

The fire popped. Nobody moved. Teo looked at me with an expression I couldn't afford to look at for long.

"That's not fair," Marisol said quietly.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Very little out here is." I stood up. "Get some sleep. Watches as planned. We move at first light."

But sleep wasn't going to happen for me, and Marisol knew it, and an hour later she appeared at the edge of the firelight, moving carefully, and sat down beside me without asking. The river talked to itself in the dark. Somewhere upstream, the glacier feed groaned—the sound had been getting worse every night, a deep tectonic complaint, like the mountain clearing its throat.

"You're going to ask," I said.

"I'm going to wait," she said. "There's a difference. Waiting has better manners."

I laughed. It surprised both of us.

* * *

Here is the thing about a confession: you don't decide to make it. You decide not to make it, daily, for twenty years, and then one night the deciding wears out, the way a rope wears out, strand by strand, until the load does the rest.

"Cordillera Negra," I said, to the fire, because I couldn't say it to her face yet. "You've been carrying it since the lodge. Everyone thinks they know the story. Blacklisted guide abandons his survey team, expedition collapses, two men die, Fairweather walks away and won't say why. Men who need a scapegoat found one who wouldn't defend himself. Convenient."

"You wouldn't defend yourself," Marisol said. "For twenty years."

"Because the truth was worse." I fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Or smaller. Depends how you measure. There was a boy, Marisol. A local kid, fourteen, maybe fifteen, who used to hang around the survey camp running errands. Emilio. He followed us up to the high traverse on the day of the storm—the day we shouldn't have been up there at all, the day your father's schedule demanded we be up there, because the funding window was closing and the reports were due." I heard my own voice go flat and distant, the voice I use for altitudes. "The meltwater took out the crossing at the Col de las Uñas. You remember the season. Everything was running early."

"I remember the season," she said, very quietly.

"Emilio went into the flood channel. Nobody else saw him—he'd gone ahead to scout, against my orders, which makes it my orders' fault, if you follow the chain of command all the way down to where it actually rests. The rest of the team was roped up on the wrong side. The safe side. If I'd stayed with the rope, I'd have been safe too, and the boy would have drowned alone, and the expedition would have continued, and the survey would have finished on schedule, and nobody would ever have known there was a choice."

"But you went in."

"I went in." The firelight did something unsteady, or I did. "I cut the rope. My own rope, off my own harness, and went into the channel after him. And I got him. That part's true, and I hold onto it on the bad nights—I got him out, I dragged him onto the bank, he lived, he's a mechanic in Valverde now with two kids, I checked, once, from very far away. But cutting the rope meant the team couldn't reach me, and the storm closed in, and by the time I got back to camp—two days late, half-dead, carrying a boy on my back—the second storm cell had taken out the upper camp. Tomás and Reyes. They'd sheltered where the plan said to shelter, except the plan assumed the rope team would signal the route change, and the rope team was me, and I wasn't there."

The silence afterward had weight, the way deep water has weight.

"Your father signed the schedule," I said. "And when the inquiry came, the men who ran the survey needed the blame to land anywhere but on the funding timeline. So it landed on the guide who abandoned his post. Who cut his rope. Who vanished into a valley where nobody asks questions." I finally looked at her. "I didn't refuse to explain because I was guilty. I refused because explaining meant saying your father's name in the same sentence as those two men, and I was tired, and the boy was alive, and I thought—God help me—that disappearing was the honorable version."

Marisol's face in the firelight was something I'd never seen on it: undone. "Isandro—"

"Don't apologize yet. Not until you hear the rest, the part you came all this way to find and I came all this way to avoid. Your father didn't just sign the schedule. He knew the meltwater was early. There's a letter in the survey records—my father's records, the ones at the source, the ones you're really chasing whether you admit it or not. My father wrote to him. Warned him. Asked him to delay." I watched her understand, watch it arrive in stages, like weather. "He didn't delay. And my father never came home, and I have spent twenty years wondering if the same men who buried my name buried him too."

Above us, the glacier groaned again—longer this time, closer, a sound like the roof of the world shifting its weight. Marisol stared into the fire with her sling-bound arm pressed to her chest, and when she spoke, her voice had none of the boardroom in it.

"Then we'd better get there," she said, "before they do."
