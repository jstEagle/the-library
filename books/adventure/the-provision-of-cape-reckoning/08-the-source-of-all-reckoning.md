## 8. The Source of All Reckoning

The last waterfall did not appear on any map, which was fitting, because neither did I.

It came out of the rock the way grief does—suddenly, and all at once, a white seam splitting the canyon wall two hundred meters above us, feeding the Ceniza its final, furious argument. Below it, the river narrowed to something you could spit across. Above it, nothing. The maps I'd memorized as a boy ended at this latitude with a cartographer's shrug: *terra incognita*, and beneath it, in my father's handwriting on the one survey sheet that survived him, a single word that had kept me awake for twenty years.

*Adelante.*

I stood at the base of the falls with the spray soaking through my shirt and felt twenty years old again, which is to say I felt terrified and certain in equal measure, a combination no one survives twice.

"Isandro." Teo's voice behind me, thin over the roar. "There's no route up that."

"There's always a route."

"That's what worries me about you."

He wasn't wrong. The face was wet limestone seamed with ledges, the kind of climbing they don't certify you for because most people who attempt it become a cautionary statistic. But I'd been reading rock since before I could read words, and this face read like an old letter—every hold where it should be, every ledge worn smooth by water that had been writing here for ten thousand years. My father had come up this way. I knew it the way you know your own name in someone else's mouth.

"We go light," I said. "Rope, records kit, water for six hours. Everyone else holds here."

"The hell we do," said Rosa, our cook, who had once fended off a bandit with a ladle and considered herself qualified for anything since.

"It's not a democracy."

"It never is with you," she said, "and yet here we all are, voting anyway." She crossed her arms. Behind her, the others arranged themselves into the particular stubborn geometry of people who had decided to follow me somewhere stupid. Teo stepped forward last, and he didn't argue at all. He just looked at me with those open, unfindable eyes of his, and I understood that whatever I decided, he would decide harder.

"Fine," I said. "Teo comes. The rest of you anchor the rope and pray to whatever gods handle fools."

* * *

We climbed for three hours.

I won't dress it up. There were moments—I remember one in particular, a traverse above the falls' second tier where the rock went glassy with spray and my left boot found nothing but prayer—that I understood exactly why the mountain had kept my father. Not malice. Mountains don't hate. They just don't care, which is worse, and cleaner, and in its own strange way a comfort. You can't betray a thing that never loved you.

Teo climbed like water finding its level, no wasted motion, no fear he hadn't budgeted for. Twice he called up corrections to my line, and both times he was right, and both times I pretended not to notice, and both times he let me pretend, which is a kindness only the young know how to give.

Near the top, where the falls braided into three white threads, I pulled myself over a lip of stone onto ground that had never known a boot print—or so I thought until I saw the cairn.

Small. Waist-high. Stones fitted with a precision I'd have recognized anywhere, because I'd learned it at a kitchen table, watching broad hands stack river rocks while a voice explained that a cairn is a promise: *someone was here, someone will come back.*

"Dios mío," Teo breathed beside me.

Beyond the cairn, tucked into a fold of the headland where the glacier's meltwater began, stood the camp.

Twenty years had done its work—tents rotted to lace, crates swollen and split—but the bones of it held. A surveyor's tripod, legs sunk half a meter into frozen gravel. An anemometer still turning in the wind with a sound like slow applause. And at the center, a stone shelter built against the cliff, its entrance sealed with fitted slabs, deliberate, patient. A man who expected to return.

Or a man who wanted whoever followed him to find what he left.

My hands shook getting the first slab off. I'll admit that freely. Twenty years of pretending I didn't care whose son I was, and there I was, trembling like the boy from the Cordillera Negra, prying open my father's last word.

Inside: dry as a tomb. He'd built it well. Oilcloth bundles, stacked with a clerk's exactness. Survey plates. Field notebooks, dozens, spines cracked but whole. And on top, wrapped separately, a leather folio with my name on it—not Isandro Fairweather, the alias I'd worn like a borrowed coat, but the name I'd been born with, the one I'd buried along with everything else.

*Isandro Reyes.*

I sat down hard on the cold stone and opened it.

* * *

My father wrote the way he climbed: directly, without ornament, trusting the reader to keep up.

The records told the story the official version had buried. The 1998 survey of the Ceniza headlands hadn't failed because of his judgment or his crew's discipline. It failed because Vega Consolidada—the mining concern Marisol's grandfather built, her mother expanded, her family had fed from for three generations—had filed false mineral claims covering the entire upper watershed, claims based on samples my father proved, plate by plate, notebook by notebook, had been salted. Planted. The company needed the headlands declared barren of anything worth protecting so their claim would stand unchallenged, and my father's honest survey stood in the way like a small man before a landslide.

So they buried him instead. Cut his resupply. Falsified his radio logs to suggest he'd gone rogue, off-permit, into country he'd sworn not to enter. When the expedition collapsed—and it collapsed, men scattered, equipment lost, the whole enterprise dissolving into scandal—the blame needed a place to land, and it landed on the nineteen-year-old son who had abandoned his post on the Cordillera Negra to pull a drowning boy out of a flood-swollen creek.

I had taken the fall for leaving. The Vegas had made sure the fall went all the way down.

And here was the part that stopped my breath: my father had known. His final entries weren't survey notes. They were testimony. Dates, names, sample numbers, chain-of-custody details, a letter addressed to the Ministry of Mines that was never sent because there was no one left to send it. He'd written it knowing what was coming for him. The last entry read:

*If my son reads this, tell him the mountain did not take me. Men did. And men can be answered.*

Above the camp, the glacier groaned. A long, deep sound, tectonic, like the mountain clearing its throat. The dry season's heat had been chewing at the ice for weeks, and now, in the late afternoon light, I could see the meltwater running off the seracs in ropes and sheets, the feed of the very waterfall we'd climbed beside swelling past anything the season should allow.

I was still holding the letter when I heard the scrape of boots on stone.

Marisol came around the cairn looking like a woman who had argued with the mountain and lost on points. Her arm was bound with a strip of shirt, the ambush wound from the ford reopened and angry, her face gray with pain and altitude and something else—something that had been eating her longer than any injury.

"You went ahead," she said. "Of course you did."

"You followed."

"I always follow. That's my tragedy." She lowered herself onto the cairn's edge, breathing carefully, the way you do when breathing is a negotiation. Her eyes went to the open shelter, the oilcloth, the folio in my hands. "So. You found it."

"You knew it was here."

"I knew *something* was here. I've known since I was twelve, when I found my grandfather burning papers in the hacienda courtyard and he told me some rivers are best left unexplored." She laughed, a dry sound with no humor in it. "I've spent thirty years and my family's money getting upstream of that sentence, Isandro. Do you understand? Everything—the permits, the crew, hiring you, humiliating myself in front of a man who hates me—all of it was to find out what my grandfather burned."

"And now you have."

She looked at the notebooks. At the plates. At the unsent letter to the Ministry, and I watched her read enough of it, upside down, across my knee, to understand what it meant. Her family's fortune. Her name. Her inheritance, laid out in her grandfather's own forged hand.

"They salted the claims," she said quietly. "They killed your father."

"They cut his resupply and let the mountain do the rest. Yes."

The wind picked up. Above us the glacier spoke again, longer this time, and a crack ran through the serac field with a report like a rifle. Meltwater surged in the channel below the camp, brown and fast, carrying ice the size of tables.

Marisol sat with it. I'll give her that—she didn't flinch, didn't reach for the lawyer's reflexes she'd been born with. She just sat, a wounded woman at the roof of the world, doing arithmetic with her entire life.

"I can bury these," she said finally. "Right now. One push, into the meltwater, and no one ever knows. My family keeps its name. Your blacklist stands. We go downstream and you go back to being no one, and I go back to being a Vega, and we both die comfortable."

"That's one option."

"Or—" She stopped. Looked at the letter. At me. "Or I let the truth out, and everything burns. The company. The claims. My mother's foundation. Thirty years of my own work, because I built it on top of what he built underneath."

The glacier answered before I could. This time the crack didn't stop—a rolling, grinding roar, and down the headland a section of the ice tongue calved away, sliding toward the melt channel, and the water below us rose visibly, hungrily, the Ceniza beginning to eat its own source.

"We're losing the high route," Teo called from the rocks below, voice tight. "Whatever you're deciding, decide faster!"

Marisol got to her feet, using the cairn, using her bad arm and not flinching. She held out her hand—not for the folio, I realized. For the letter. My father's testimony.

"Help me carry it down," she said. "All of it. Every page. If my family drowned a man to protect a lie, then the lie drowns with me." Her eyes were bright and terrible. "But hear me, Isandro Reyes—you get to be the one who sends it. It's your father's voice. It should be your hand."

I gave her the letter. Then I took it back, because it was mine, and because she was right, and because for the first time in twenty years both of those things could be true at once.

Behind us, the glacier gave way another meter, and the waterfall that had guarded this place all my life began, slowly, to die—taking the route home with it.

We had a long way down, and the river we'd followed up was leaving without us.
