## 9. Upstream of Forgiveness

I have spent my whole life learning to read my family's handwriting, and I can tell you this: the Vega signature is a beautiful lie. My grandfather practiced it the way other men practice prayer—daily, devoutly, with an eye toward what it could get him. When I held Isandro's father's survey ledger in my frostbitten hands at the top of the world, the first thing I noticed was that Tomás Fairweather wrote like a man who expected to be believed. No flourishes. No artistry. Just numbers and elevations and a running commentary in the margins that read less like science and more like a man arguing with a mountain he already loved.

The second thing I noticed was the date on the final page, and the third thing was the name signed beneath it as witness: *E. Vega*. My grandfather. At a camp he had sworn under oath, twice, in two countries, that he had never approached.

Above us, the glacier feed groaned like an old ship. Below us, somewhere down twenty switchbacks of scree and ice, the rival outfit was climbing toward us with the patience of men being paid by the day.

"Marisol." Isandro's voice came from the mouth of the survey camp, low and even, the way he spoke when the ground was unstable. "We need to decide."

*We.* Twenty years ago that word would have been a knife between us. Now it just sounded tired.

* * *

Let me tell you what I had planned to do with those records, because I am not the saint this chapter will otherwise make me out to be.

For eleven years I had built Vega Cartografía into something my grandfather would have recognized—a company that owned its history the way it owned its maps, by redrawing anything inconvenient. The Río Ceniza concession, the water rights, the mineral surveys that had never seen daylight: all of it rested on one legal fiction, that the original 1998 expedition had failed through Fairweather negligence, voiding every claim Tomás Fairweather had staked. My family's fortune sat on that fiction like a house on a fault line. I had come up this river not to find truth but to *manage* it—to get there first, control the narrative, bury the ledger deeper than the glacier itself.

That was the plan. I want it on the record that I had a plan, because what I did instead was pure improvisation, and improvisation is how Vegas die.

"Decide what?" I said. "It's evidence. We take it down and give it to lawyers like civilized people."

Isandro looked at me across the ruined camp—Tomás's canvas still pegged after two decades, his tin cup still on the stove ring, everything waiting for a man who never came home—and he said, "Lawyers take years. The river takes days. And they—" he nodded downhill, toward the sound of boots on stone that Teo had spotted an hour earlier, "—take orders from whoever's still paying your family's invoices."

He was right, which annoyed me, and he was calm, which annoyed me more. This was the man I had spent half my life hating for a cowardice he hadn't committed, and here he was handing me the cleanest exit anyone had ever offered: burn nothing, publish nothing, let the lawyers slow-walk justice until my inheritance outlived the scandal. All I had to do was stay quiet. Staying quiet was the family trade. I'd been apprenticed to it since I was six years old.

The glacier cracked again, longer this time, a sound like the mountain clearing its throat before saying something unforgivable.

"Teo," I called down the slope.

He scrambled up, all elbows and alarm, and I handed him the satellite transmitter we'd been rationing like insulin. "How does this thing work?"

His face did something complicated. "You're going to—"

"I'm going to read aloud," I said, "every page that matters, on an open channel, logged and timestamped, until someone in a valley town with a radio picks it up. Then I'm going to keep reading until my voice gives out or the battery does. Whichever comes first."

Isandro said nothing. That was the worst part. He didn't argue, didn't thank me, didn't give me the absolution I suddenly, shamefully wanted. He just took off his outer glove and set the ledger on a flat rock between us, open to the page with my grandfather's name, and stepped back like a man leaving a verdict to a jury.

* * *

I read for four hours.

I won't reproduce it all here—the full text exists now in three archives and a hundred retransmissions, thanks to a schoolteacher in Puerto Ceniza named Beatriz Ocampo who stayed up all night copying my voice onto paper—but I'll tell you the shape of it. Tomás Fairweather had found what the company sent him to find: not gold, not the source itself, but the water table, the true geometry of the glacier feed, the fact that the entire upper valley sat on reserves worth more than the mines my grandfather had been selling stock against. And then Eusebio Vega had arrived at the camp with twelve armed men and a contract Tomás refused to sign, because signing meant the numbers would disappear into a vault and the valley's towns would keep buying their own water back by the bottle.

The last entries were dated nine days before the disaster. In the margin of the final page, in handwriting gone hurried and small: *E.V. means to take the records whether I sign or not. If anything happens, the boy knows where I kept the copies. He always listens.*

*The boy.* Isandro, at nineteen, sitting outside tents pretending not to hear, the way children of important men learn to do.

When I read that line aloud, my voice broke on *he always listens*, and I heard Isandro behind me make a sound I had never heard him make—not grief exactly, more like a door opening after twenty years of rust. Teo had stopped taking notes. Even the radio static seemed to lean in.

And below us, faint but unmistakable, the rival outfit stopped climbing. Because their employer, whoever in my company had hired them, had just heard their own crime read out over an open channel, timestamped, unkillable. You cannot buy back a broadcast. My grandfather understood leverage; he would have hated this, and I found, to my surprise, that I was smiling.

"There," I said, when the last page was done and my throat was raw. "Now it belongs to everyone. Including the parts that hang me."

Isandro came to stand beside me at the camp's edge. Below, the Ceniza ran silver and thin—the dry season eating it alive, the river we'd followed up now visibly dying behind us.

"You didn't have to," he said.

"No," I agreed. "That was rather the point."

* * *

The descent nearly killed us, and I say that with the flatness of a woman who has recently stopped finding death dramatic.

The glacier feed went at midday on our second day down—a crack like the sky splitting, then a brown wall of meltwater and debris slamming into the gorge below us, erasing the ford we'd used coming up, erasing the trail, erasing any argument about taking the long way. The river we had climbed beside for two weeks turned, in the space of an hour, into something with opinions.

What saved us wasn't skill. It was the crew—all of them, every stray Isandro had collected over the years, moving without orders because he'd spent two decades quietly teaching them to move without orders. Rosa fixed the rope anchors while the water was still roaring past her knees. Damián carried double loads without being asked and complained about it beautifully, which is its own kind of morale. Teo read the terrain the way Isandro read faces, finding the high line down through scree that hadn't yet decided to become a landslide. And Isandro himself worked the dangerous stretches alone, the crossings no one else should attempt, and I watched him do it and finally understood what I'd refused to understand at nineteen: that the man the Cordillera Negra called a coward had simply been the only person on that mountain willing to trade his reputation for a child's life, and had paid the invoice without ever once showing anyone the receipt.

At the second-to-last crossing, with the water at our thighs and rising, he pulled me out of a channel that wanted to keep me, and for a moment we stood chest-deep in the ruin of the river, gripping each other's forearms, laughing—I swear to you, laughing—like two people who had finally finished the longest argument of their lives and discovered neither of them had been right, only late.

"The valley towns," I shouted over the current. "Beatriz Ocampo's broadcast. By now it's everywhere. Your name's clean, Isandro. Do you understand? It's actually clean."

He looked at me with water streaming off his jaw, and what he said was: "Then why does it feel the same?"

"Because names are just paper," I told him. "The feeling comes later. Trust me. I've spent my whole life confusing the two."

* * *

We made the lower gorge at dusk on the fourth day, where the dying river pooled and gasped among boulders it had once moved like furniture, and there we found them waiting: not the rival outfit—they had melted away somewhere above, unpaid and unpunished, which is the most honest fate money offers—but mules, supplies, and a woman in a red coat standing at the waterline with a letter in her hand, scanning the gorge the way you scan a horizon for a ship.

Isandro's sister. I knew her instantly, though I'd never met her—the same stubborn set to the shoulders, the same way of holding her chin like the world owed her an argument.

She saw him and didn't run to him. She just lifted the letter higher, so he could see it across the distance, and stood there, patient as water, letting him close the miles himself.

I hung back with Teo while he walked toward her, and the boy glanced up at me with that look the young reserve for moments they suspect matter. "So what happens to you now?" he asked. "With the company and everything."

I watched Isandro stop an arm's length from his sister. I watched him take the letter.

"Now?" I said. "Now I go home and lose everything gracefully. There's a technique to it. My family invented it." I shouldered my pack, lighter than it had been in eleven years. "But first I'm going to stand here a minute, because I followed a river to its source looking for my family's sins, and it turns out they were upstream of mine the whole time."

Downstream, far below, the real water waited—wide, unhurried, carrying everything at last toward the sea.
