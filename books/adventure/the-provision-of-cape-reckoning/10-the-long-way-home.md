## 10. The Long Way Home

The Río Ceniza ends the way most rivers do—not with a flourish but with a shrug. Twenty days ago it was a glacier's cold argument at the roof of the world; now it spreads itself thin across a delta of grey silt and mangrove stumps, apologizing to the sea for all the noise it made upstream.

I stood where the fresh water gave up and became salt, and I did what I had done at every river mouth of my adult life: I measured it. Distance from the source—two hundred and eleven kilometers by Teo's GPS, less if you trusted the old survey maps in my father's records, which apparently you could. Distance from the trailhead lodge where Marisol had cornered me with her impossible contract—a lifetime, or so it felt. Distance from the man who'd walked out of that lodge refusing the job and then, three days later, signed it anyway because his crew voted yes without asking him first.

I used to think measuring things kept them honest. Miles don't lie. A kilometer is a kilometer whether you're running toward something or away from it, and for twenty years I had been doing both at once, which is a trick only the truly committed can manage.

But standing there in the delta mud, with my name cleared and my father's records sealed in a waterproof case against my back, I found the numbers wouldn't come. The habit was there—I reached for it like a man reaching for a light switch in a house he no longer lives in—but the switch did nothing. The distances were just distances. They didn't mean anything anymore.

That, more than the glacier calving, more than the ambush at the ford, more than reading my father's handwriting after two decades of believing him dead or worse—that was what told me everything had changed.

* * *

We came down off the last ridge on a morning so clear the sea looked painted on. Teo spotted it first—he always spotted things first, being twenty-four and still under the impression that the world rewarded attention—and he let out a whoop that scattered a dozen herons and probably violated some treaty.

"Salt!" he shouted, cupping water from an estuary channel and tasting it, grimacing, tasting it again anyway. "Actual ocean! Do you know how long it's been since any of us saw an ocean?"

"Four days," said Rosa, our medic, without looking up from re-taping Marisol's ribs.

"An *ocean*, Rosa. Not a puddle with ambitions."

Marisol laughed, then winced, then laughed again, which pretty much summarized her condition. The break in the ford hadn't broken her—cracked two ribs and her pride, but she'd climbed the last waterfall on a rope I belayed personally, and she'd stood beside me in the hidden camp while I read what my father had written, and she'd made the choice I still couldn't quite believe: she burned her own family's claim to save mine. Broadcast the survey records from the one satellite phone that survived the rival outfit's ambush, sent every page to every regulator, journalist, and university archive she could reach. By the time we descended below the tree line, the story was already ahead of us, moving faster than the dying river.

*Vega heiress torches family legacy,* the headlines said. *Blacklisted guide Fairweather exonerated after twenty years.*

They got the order wrong. She wasn't a Vega heiress when she did it. She was just Marisol, standing in a melting cirque, holding evidence that her grandfather had falsified the Cordillera Negra survey and let my father take the fall—let *me* take the fall, when I came down the mountain alone carrying a drowned boy's guilt and no explanation anyone would accept.

"You could have kept it," I told her that night, our last night above the treeline, the glacier groaning behind us like an old man turning over in bed. "One signature. One quiet settlement. Nobody would have known."

"Everybody would have known," she said. "I would have known." She looked at me across the fire, and for once there was no argument in her face, just exhaustion and something underneath it that might have been peace. "You spent twenty years paying for a crime you didn't commit, Isandro. I spent twenty years inheriting one. We're even now. Or close enough."

Close enough. From Marisol Vega, that was practically a love letter.

* * *

The delta town was called Puerto Ceniza, though the locals called it El Fin—the End—which struck me as either very honest or very lazy naming. It was a town built on fish and forgetting: tin roofs, drying nets, a cantina whose walls displayed forty years of photographs of boats that no longer existed. The kind of place where a man could be invisible, if a man still wanted that.

I checked into the only hospedaje, took a room with a window facing the water, and sat on the bed with the letter.

I should explain about the letter. In Chapter Six of this disaster—no. Forgive me. Old habit; my father was a surveyor and thought in chapters and coordinates. Let me start again.

There was a letter. My sister Lucía had been writing to me for years, care of post offices and lodges and the general ether, and I had been not-reading them for years, because reading one meant admitting there was a home to be invited back to, and I had organized my entire existence around the principle that homes are things other people have. Three weeks ago, at the high camp, Teo caught me burning one in the cook fire. He didn't yell. That's the thing about Teo—he doesn't yell. He just looks at you with those serious dark eyes and says things like, "Belonging isn't a place you flee from, jefe. It's a place you walk toward slowly enough to notice."

Then he'd handed me another letter—one he'd apparently rescued from an earlier fire, singed at one corner, smelling faintly of smoke and eucalyptus—and I had carried it, unopened, through the gorge, the ford, the waterfall, the reckoning. Carried it like ballast. Like a stone I couldn't put down because putting it down would mean deciding what it weighed.

Now I sat on a creaking bed in El Fin and opened it.

Lucía's handwriting hadn't changed. Still too many loops, still pressing hard enough to emboss the next page. *Isandro.* Just my name first, alone on the line, the way she always began letters, as if to establish that she knew exactly who she was writing to, whatever name the world was using for me these days.

*I'm not going to ask you to come home. You've heard enough demands in your life. I'm going to tell you instead. Mamá's garden is still there. The lemon tree finally died and I haven't had the heart to dig it out, so it stands there like a monument to stubbornness, which feels right. Your tools are in the shed where you left them. The valley remembers you better than you remember it—the old ones still ask after you when traders pass through. They never believed the stories. They said a man who carries a drowning boy up a mountain in a whiteout is not the man who abandons a survey. It took the rest of the world twenty years to figure out what the valley knew all along.*

*Come when the rains end. Or don't come. But answer this letter, Isandro. Even one line. Even your name. I've been talking into the dark for a long time and I'd like to know if anything answers back.*

*Your sister,*
*Lucía*

I read it twice. Then I sat with it on my knees while the light went gold and then grey over the delta, and fishing boats came home trailing their long evening shadows, and somewhere below a woman sang half a song and forgot the rest.

Twenty years of not answering. And the answer, when it finally assembled itself in me, was embarrassingly simple.

*Lucía. I'm coming. Not when the rains end—before. Save me a bed and don't dig out the tree. Some things should stand as monuments.*

*Your brother,*
*Isandro*

I wrote it on the back of Marisol's contract, which seemed fitting. The job was finished; the paper deserved better than to die blank.

* * *

Teo found me at the cantina that evening, where I was buying the crew dinner in flagrant violation of my own budget. Rosa was arguing with Bautista about whether the sea counted as "big water" for rationing purposes—it did not, Rosa insisted, we were done rationing, eat the damn fish—and Marisol sat at the end of the table with her arm in a sling and a beer she wasn't drinking, watching the crew the way she'd watched them the whole expedition: like a woman memorizing something she intended to keep.

I sat down across from Teo and slid the letter across the table.

He read it. His face did the thing it does—nothing dramatic, just a slow rearrangement, like weather clearing. "You answered."

"I answered."

"Took you long enough."

"Twenty years, give or take."

"I was going to say three weeks, but sure, round up." He pushed the letter back and leaned forward on his elbows. "So what happens now? You go back to the valley, hang up your ropes, become a lemon-tree monument yourself?"

"That's the plan for the first week. After that—" I turned my beer glass in a slow circle, buying time I didn't need, because the truth was already out and walking around the table introducing itself. "After that, I'm starting a guiding outfit. Small. Valley-based. High-altitude treks, the routes nobody else runs because nobody else knows them." I looked at him. "My father's routes. I've got his survey books. Every pass, every spring, every camp between here and the ice. Twenty years of knowledge nobody wanted from me, and now it turns out it was worth having all along."

Teo went very still, in the way of young men trying not to look like they've just been handed the thing they want most. "You're offering me a job."

"I'm offering you a partnership. Junior partner. Terrible pay, worse hours, and a boss who argues with the route notes of a dead man."

"A dead man who was right about everything," Teo said quietly. "I read some of those pages, jefe. At the source. Before you sealed them up." He met my eyes. "He mapped that country like he loved it. Like he was writing letters to whoever came after." A pause, and then, with the ghost of a grin: "Junior partner. Fine. But I negotiate the canteen budget."

"Everyone negotiates the canteen budget. It's the first rule of expeditions."

From the end of the table, Marisol raised her untouched beer an inch. "Fairweather Mountain Works," she said. "I'll be your first client. There's a ridge in the Cordillera Negra my grandfather never surveyed honestly, and I want it surveyed honestly. Family tradition, corrected." Her voice was light, but her eyes weren't. "Consider it reparations. Mine, this time."

Rosa demanded hazard pay for listening to us talk business during dinner. Bautista demanded a boat ride before we all went back to mountains, on the grounds that a man should see what rivers are for. The cantina filled up around us with smoke and argument and the smell of fried fish, and I sat in the middle of it—middle, not edge, middle—and understood that this was the thing I'd been fleeing for twenty years without knowing its name.

Not a place. A table. People who argued with you and stayed anyway.

Later, alone on the seawall, I did the thing I'd done at every river mouth of my life. I measured. Two hundred and eleven kilometers upstream, a glacier was feeding a river that ended here, in this grey water, under this indifferent moon. Four hundred kilometers east, a valley held a lemon tree and a sister and a shed full of my father's tools. And directly beneath my feet, holding me up, was ordinary ground—the first ground in twenty years that asked nothing of me except that I stand on it.

I didn't write the numbers down. For the first time, they weren't going anywhere, and neither, I suspected, was I.

Behind me, from the direction of the hospedaje, Teo's voice carried across the dark, calling my name—not *jefe*, not *Fairweather*, just my name, the way family says it—and I turned toward the sound and started walking.
