## 1. The Mood of the Water

The Verrazano in low-water season is a liar with a dry throat. It looks calm from the bank—brown glass, sleepy bends, herons standing around like unpaid sentries—but underneath it's all teeth. Sandbars move overnight the way rumors do, and a channel that held four feet on Tuesday will hold two and a half by Friday, just to see what you're made of.

I'd been reading that river for fifteen years, and I still gave it my full attention every morning, because the day you stop is the day it collects.

I stood at the wheel of the *Kestrel* at first light, coffee going cold in the tin cup wedged against the binnacle, and watched the water tell me its mood. There's a way the current folds over a bar—a smoothness like skin drawn over a knuckle—and if you know to look for it, you live longer. The morning was giving me easy water as far as Miller's Bend, then a wrinkle past the old ferry landing where the channel had wandered east again. Fine. I could be polite about it.

"Morning, Captain." Delia Marsh, on the dock at Corben's Landing, hauling a crate of nails up my gangplank like she was mad at it. "You look terrible."

"I look exactly like a woman who slept well and minds her own business."

"That's what terrible looks like on you."

Delia runs the freight office and half the gossip trade between Corben's and the head of navigation, and she knows better than to ask me questions, which is why we've gotten along for a decade and a half. No debts, no questions, no past—that's the whole of my operating philosophy, stamped somewhere behind my eyes where the important things go. The *Kestrel* takes cargo upriver and brings resin, pelts, and silence back down. I pay cash for fuel and repairs. I tip in exact change. I have never once told anyone where I learned to read water, and the one man who guessed got a look from me that he still describes to strangers as "the weather turning."

She dropped the manifest on my chart table. "Nails for Tarrow, kerosene for the mission, and—" she paused, which Delia never does, "—this came on the morning wire. Addressed to you by name."

Nobody addresses telegrams to me by name. People address them to *the Kestrel's master*, or *captain, freight boat, Corben's Landing*. My name is not a thing I hand out. It's not even the name I was born with, but that's a different ledger, kept in a different ink, in a drawer I don't open.

I looked at the telegram anyway, because refusing to look is its own kind of telling.

**MARCHETTI STOP TRIPLE RATES PLUS BONUS STOP REQUIRE BOAT CAPABLE GORGE RUN STOP SUPPLIES HALVORSEN SURVEY CAMP ABOVE DEVILS THROAT STOP REPLY WESTERN MINERAL CONCESSIONS CARE LAND OFFICE STOP URGENT PERMIT WINDOW**

Triple rates. Plus bonus. Up past Devil's Throat, which isn't a place so much as an argument the river has with itself and always wins—a gorge where the Verrazano drops through limestone in a series of rapids that have eaten better boats than mine and spat out their ribs for the tourists to photograph.

"You've gone quiet," Delia said. "That's quieter than usual, which is saying something."

"It's nothing."

"Triple pay is nothing?"

"Triple pay is a flare," I said. "You don't fire a flare unless something's wrong. And whoever's paying it wants me to run the Throat in low water, which means they either don't know the river or they don't care who comes back down it."

Delia considered this with the expression of a woman deciding how much of it to sell. "Halvorsen," she said. "That's the survey outfit. Been up there since spring, staking claims for the mining people. Big money, they say. Engineers and theodolites and a cook who complains."

"They say a lot of things."

"They say the company men came through last month asking which captains would take the gorge run. Most everyone said no. Old Piet said yes and then remembered his boat had a hole in it." She shrugged. "So now they're asking you."

Now they were asking me. Which meant they'd worked down the list and found it short, or they'd started with me, and both of those possibilities smelled the same: like a deal going bad before it's even closed.

Here's what I know about money offered in a hurry. I grew up inside a family that moved fast on paper and slow in person, and every fortune I ever saw up close had a body under it somewhere—sometimes literal, usually just financial, occasionally both. Money that arrives polite and patient is commerce. Money that arrives desperate is a hook, and hooks are baited with exactly the thing you want most.

What did I want most? That was the uncomfortable question, sitting there in the morning light like a cat that had decided my lap was now its property.

I wanted the *Kestrel* re-plated below the waterline, for one. Her hull was older than my lie about myself, and the yard at Tarrow quoted a figure that made me laugh in a way that worried the yardmaster. Triple rates plus bonus would cover it twice over and leave enough to winter somewhere warm, which at forty-one years old I was beginning to admit sounded less like weakness and more like arithmetic.

And underneath that, in the drawer I don't open, something smaller and meaner wanted to be asked. Fifteen years of being nobody—the reliable captain, the woman who nods at church socials and doesn't stay—fifteen years of that builds up a pressure, and some mornings it whistles.

"Reply costs you nothing," Delia said. "Replies are free. It's the trips that cost."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said on this dock all year."

* * *

I spent the afternoon loading and pretending I hadn't decided anything, which is a skill, and by evening I had to admit the pretense was failing. I sat on the cabin roof with the telegram and a second cup of coffee that had also gone cold, and I made lists, because lists are how I pray.

Reasons to say no: the Throat in low water. A client whose letterhead I didn't know. A permit window, which is corporate for *clock ticking, don't think, hurry*. And the general principle that desperation travels upstream faster than any boat, and if Western Mineral Concessions was desperate, then whatever they were desperate about was already waiting above the gorge, patient as a snag.

Reasons to say yes: the hull. The winter. The whistle.

And one reason I didn't write down, which was that the Halvorsen camp sat above country I'd never had a good excuse to see—high limestone country, blue-green water coming off cold rock, villages up there that bought their medicine and mail from whoever was brave enough to run it. I'd spent fifteen years making myself small on this river, and smallness starts to feel like safety right up until you notice it's also a kind of drowning, just drier.

The mood of the water, I thought. That's what my father used to call it—not the river's mood, though he meant that too, but your own. *Read yourself the way you read the channel, girl, or you'll ground on something you can't see.* He'd been dead eleven years by the official count and considerably longer by the honest one, and I still heard him every time the current folded over a hidden bar.

I went down to the wireless shack Delia let me use and sent three words: **TERMS ACCEPTED DEPART THURSDAY.**

Then I stood there a moment with my hand on the key, feeling like a woman who'd just signed something without reading it, which was ironic, because reading everything was supposed to be the one lesson I'd never forgotten.

* * *

Thursday came up gray and windless, the best kind of loading weather—no chop at the dock, no argument from the river. I took on flour, salt pork, lamp oil, batteries, rope, a crate marked INSTRUMENTS FRAGILE that I decided not to be curious about, and a sealed strongbox the freight clerk handled like it might apologize. Manifest said survey supplies. Manifests say lots of things.

Word travels on a landing faster than fire in a hay barn, so by midmorning I had a small audience of the professionally opinionated.

"Gorge run," said old Piet, spitting with ceremonial precision into the water. "In October. With that hull." He patted the *Kestrel*'s rail almost fondly, the way you'd pat a horse you expected to outlive you. "You'll want to line her down through the Squeeze, not run it."

"I know how to take the Squeeze."

"Everybody knows how until they don't." He squinted at me. "Triple rates, they're saying. What's up there worth triple rates?"

"Supplies," I said. "Scientists eat too."

Piet looked at me the way old rivermen look at young ones heading into water they shouldn't—with a mixture of pity, respect, and the quiet satisfaction of a man who intends to be at the dock when you come back, so he can say he told you so. "Devil's Throat didn't get its name from the scenery," he said. "My grandfather ran timber through there. Lost two men and both rafts. Said the gorge makes its own weather and its own rules, and neither one favors the living."

"Then it should get along fine with mining companies."

He laughed despite himself, which I counted as a small victory, and helped me cast off, which I counted as a larger one, because Piet helping with lines meant he thought I'd come back.

The *Kestrel* took the current and the landing fell away behind us—Delia raising two fingers off her hat brim, the warehouse cats, the smell of creosote and fish. Ahead the valley narrowed by degrees, the way a conversation narrows before someone says the thing they actually called about. The hills went from farmed to forested to sheer, and the water went from brown to a hard green-gray that moved like it had somewhere important to be.

By the second day I was running water I didn't know, and I hated it and loved it in equal measure. Unknown channels are exhausting—you sound every foot, you read every fold, you tie up early and walk the banks with a pole checking depths like a suspicious landlady—but they're also the only water that makes you feel entirely awake. Fifteen years of the familiar lower river had worn grooves in me, comfortable ones. This was outside the grooves.

On the third morning the country changed its mind about being beautiful. The walls closed in, limestone going white and ribbed like bone, and the river dropped into a long series of chutes where the noise came up through the hull and into my boots and stayed there. I lined the *Kestrel* through the worst of it, winching her along the bank by her own anchor, sweating through my shirt in air cold enough to see my breath, and when I finally stood again at the wheel with open water ahead, my hands were shaking in the pleasant way, the way they do after something that could have killed you politely declined to.

Above the last chute, the valley opened into high country—pines, scree slopes, a thread of woodsmoke somewhere upstream. The map said the Halvorsen camp lay another six miles on, at the mouth of a creek, on a bench above flood line. Good siting. Someone competent had chosen it.

I checked my watch. I checked the sky, which was doing nothing, which in October up here could mean anything. I put the wheel over and headed up into the last reach.

It wasn't until I rounded the bend below the camp and cut the engine to drift in quiet that I noticed what was wrong, and what was wrong was the silence. River camps are never silent. There are generators, voices, the ring of hammers on stake pins, a cook banging something out of pure temperament. Even a sleeping camp breathes.

This one didn't breathe.

The landing planks were scorched black at the edges. One of the supply tents had collapsed inward, slashed, not blown down. And on the bank, half in the shallows, something pale was caught against a root wad, moving gently with the current, and it took me a long moment—longer than I'd like—to understand that it was canvas, and that canvas doesn't float alone.

I reached for the throttle, then stopped, because the other thing I understood, all at once and completely, was that whatever had happened here, I was now the only person on this river who knew it had.
