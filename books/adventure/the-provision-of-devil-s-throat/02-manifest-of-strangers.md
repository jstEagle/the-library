## 2. Manifest of Strangers

The manifest said four hundred pounds of flour, two hundred of salt pork, a crate of survey instruments, medical stores, lamp oil, and—this was the line that made me read it twice—one sealed case, contents unspecified, to be handled personally by the client's representative.

I'd run cargo for fifteen years and I had learned that the items with no listed contents were always the heaviest things aboard. Not in pounds. In everything else.

I loaded at the Corbeny docks before dawn, when the light came up gray as dishwater and the only other people moving were gulls and men paid too little to ask questions. The Kestrel sat low on her lines, hungry for water she wouldn't get until the rains. Low-water season on the Verrazano is a season of arithmetic: every pound you take aboard is a foot of draft you'll regret above the shallows. I loaded by feel, by habit, by the old rule my father taught me before I stopped using his name—you load for the river you have, not the river you want.

The flour went amidships, dry and honest. The pork went forward where it could sweat without offending anyone. The instruments I wrapped myself, because a crate marked *Meridian Survey Co.* is a crate some dockhand will drop just to see what rattles, and I didn't want to be the one explaining broken theodolites to a man with a permit.

The sealed case went last.

It wasn't heavy. That was the first wrong thing about it. A case built like that—brass corners, waxed canvas, locks that wanted a key rather than a crowbar—ought to have held something worth its own weight. This one I could lift one-handed. It held paper, or something near it. Documents, maybe. Maps. And yet the representative had paid for "personal handling" like it was a sleeping child.

Which meant whatever was inside mattered more than its weight. Which meant someone downstream was already nervous.

* * *

Odile Vance arrived at seven, exactly when she said she would, which told me she'd never worked a river dock in her life. People who work docks arrive early or late; punctuality is a luxury of solid ground.

She was small and neat and dressed like weather was a rumor she'd declined to believe. Gray traveling suit, good boots ruined deliberately at the heels so they'd look broken in, a hat that shaded her face without hiding it. She carried a leather folio under one arm the way a gambler carries his last stake—close, casual, lying about how much it meant.

"Captain Marchetti." She pronounced it correctly, which surprised me. Most people hear *Marchetti* and reach for something Italian and wrong. "Emrys Marchetti."

"That's me."

"You come recommended."

"By whom?"

"A man at the freight office. He said you don't talk."

"I talk plenty," I said. "Just not about things that aren't mine to talk about."

Something moved behind her eyes—not quite approval, not quite disappointment. Recalculation, maybe. She'd been told I was discreet, and here I was confirming it while also making clear I wasn't stupid, and I think those two facts together hadn't been in her briefing.

We walked the deck together while her hired hands finished lashing the last crates. She inspected my boat the way an auditor inspects a ledger, touching nothing, missing nothing. She looked at the patched planking below the gunwale and didn't ask about it. She looked at the name on the stern—*Kestrel*, repainted so many times the letters stood proud of the wood like scar tissue—and didn't ask about that either.

Everyone asks about the name eventually. It's a small boat, and there's not much else to look at.

"Three days to the Halvorsen camp," she said. "Four if the water's against you. You know the stretch above Devil's Throat?"

"Some."

That was true the way most of my answers are true. I knew it the way you know a dog that's bitten you once—respectfully, from a distance, counting its teeth. Fifteen years on this river and I'd gone above the Throat exactly twice, both times for men who paid extra and both times understanding why the pay was extra. The gorge doesn't forgive draft. It doesn't forgive hesitation. Mostly it doesn't forgive anything.

"The concession expires in eleven days," Vance said. "Halvorsen's crew has been surveying since spring. They need these supplies, they need the instruments, and they need confirmation that the company still stands behind their permits. My job is to deliver all three."

"And your job is to bring back whatever they've found," I said.

Her chin lifted a fraction. "Whatever they've found belongs to the company. Yes."

I nodded and said nothing, because there was a whole sermon in that sentence and none of it was mine to preach.

* * *

Here is what I noticed, and here is why noticing is a skill I practice the way other people practice piano:

Vance gave me a copy of the shipping papers, as law and custom require. The papers named the consignor as the Meridian Consolidated Mining and Survey Company, registered out of Corbeny, and the letterhead was beautiful—engraved, embossed, the kind of stationery that costs more per sheet than my deckhands make per day.

But I've seen Meridian paperwork before. Two seasons back I ran drill steel for one of their subsidiary operations below Harrow Bend, and their manifests came on a different stock entirely—thinner, cheaper, printed with a slightly different eagle over the company seal. A company that big has offices the way a river has tributaries, and each office orders its own stationery, so a mismatch proves nothing by itself.

Except.

The seal on Vance's papers was the new-style Meridian seal, adopted—I happened to know, because a bored clerk once complained about it to me for forty miles—the year after the subsidiary I'd worked for folded. And the registration number beneath it belonged to a Meridian charter that had been dissolved. I knew that too, because dissolutions get posted at the land office, and I read the postings the way some men read scripture: looking for my own name, finding everyone else's.

So the letterhead was real. The company was real. But the authority signing those papers was, at best, borrowed, and at worst, invented—and either way, Odile Vance was carrying permits that would not survive a careful reading by anyone whose job it was to read carefully.

I handed the papers back and said, "Looks fine."

Because that's the trade. That's the whole ancient trade of the river: you don't see what you're paid not to see, and the people who hire you know you haven't seen it, and everybody keeps their hands clean by agreeing the water is opaque. I grew up inside a deal going bad—watched it from the galley of my father's boat, watched grown men smile at each other across manifests that lied, watched how it ended with a wreck and a magistrate and names nobody said anymore. I know the smell. It smells like fresh ink and old money and a hat worn indoors.

Vance took the papers, and for half a second her face did something unguarded—a flicker of relief so brief you'd miss it if you hadn't spent fifteen years learning to miss nothing. She'd expected more resistance. Or she'd expected questions. Either way, my silence had cost her something she'd budgeted for, and getting it cheap made her trust me a little and fear me a little, in roughly equal measure.

Good. Equal measure is where you want them.

* * *

We cast off at eight. The Kestrel took the current like an old woman taking stairs—slowly, complaining in her knees, but going.

Vance stood at the rail for the first hour, watching Corbeny shrink behind us, and then she did the thing every passenger does eventually: she started talking, because silence on a boat feels like drowning to people who aren't riverborn.

"You've worked this water long?"

"Fifteen years."

"Family?"

"No."

That answer was a locked door and she heard it lock. To her credit—or her training—she didn't push. Instead she changed course the way clever people do, coming at the same reef from a different heading.

"The man at the freight office," she said, "said you once turned down double pay rather than carry a shipment on a Sunday. Said you had principles."

"He said that?"

"He found it remarkable."

"It wasn't principles. Sundays the inspectors drink, and drunk inspectors wave boats through with bad cargo. I won't carry bad cargo. It sinks at inconvenient times."

She laughed—a real laugh, short and surprised out of her, and for a moment she looked younger and less armored, and I understood that whatever she was, she wasn't the top of her own chain. Nobody at the top laughs like that. People at the top have forgotten how.

"So the sealed case," she said. "You didn't ask what's in it."

"Would you tell me?"

"No."

"Then asking would just be a way of finding out whether you lie well. I'd rather find that out later, when it matters."

Another flicker in her eyes. We were playing cards neither of us had named, and we both kept drawing the same ones.

At noon I put her below with coffee and hard bread and went forward to check the lashings, and while I checked them I let myself do the arithmetic I'd been refusing all morning.

Triple pay. Eleven days on the concession clock. Paperwork that was almost right, which is worse than paperwork that's obviously wrong, because obviously wrong can be refused at the door, while almost right gets you all the way inside before it comes apart. A sealed case lighter than it looked. A woman sent alone upriver, past the Throat, into country where the company's writ runs thin, carrying authority that might dissolve in her hands like wet paper.

And somewhere ahead, a camp full of surveyors who'd been out of contact long enough that a supply run needed triple pay to tempt anyone.

Money like this means someone is desperate. I'd known that when I took the telegram, and I'd filed it under *acceptable risk*, the way you file things when the alternative is another winter of hauling coal and pretending the river isn't the only thing that ever felt like yours.

But desperation travels upstream. That's the other rule. Goods float down; trouble walks up. And I was now carrying trouble's luggage.

I cinched the last lashing and looked north, where the water ran low and gray toward a bend I couldn't see, and somewhere beyond it the Devil's Throat waited with its limestone teeth and its reputation, and somewhere beyond that, a camp that hadn't answered its radio in nine days.

Nine days. I'd told myself the silence was equipment failure. Equipment fails all the time up there—antennas ice, generators flood, operators quit. There are a hundred innocent reasons a camp goes quiet.

There are perhaps three guilty ones, and all three of them smell like fresh ink and old money.

I went aft, took the wheel, and steered us into the current, and Odile Vance came up beside me with her folio held close, and asked me, pleasantly, as though it were small talk between professionals:

"Captain—if you got up there and the camp was empty, what would you do?"

I kept my eyes on the water, because the water deserved them and because I didn't want her reading my face when I answered.

"Depends," I said, "on what empty looks like."

She nodded slowly, as if I'd passed a test, and maybe I had, and maybe we'd both just agreed, without saying it, that we were sailing toward an answer neither of us wanted to write down.

Ahead, the river narrowed, and the first white water of the low season showed its teeth around the bend.
