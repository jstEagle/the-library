## 3. Dead Air Above the Gorge

The Verrazano above Split Rock is not a river so much as an argument the mountains are winning. By the third day past the last trading post, the water had gone from brown to white to a color I can only describe as *opinionated*, and the Kestrel was answering every one of its points with the marine equivalent of gritted teeth.

She's forty years old, my boat, and she handles rapids the way old women handle stairs—one hand on the rail, no sudden movements, and a low muttering all the while. Her engine is a diesel that predates two wars and outlasted three mechanics who swore it couldn't be fixed. I talk to her constantly. She has never once talked back, which puts her ahead of most of my acquaintances.

The channel narrows as you climb toward Devil's Throat. The banks stand up on either side like witnesses called against you—black limestone streaked with quartz, dripping even in dry season, wearing their waterfalls like jewelry they can't take off. The current through the Narrows runs eight knots when it's feeling generous. I've run it eleven times in fifteen years, which is ten more than any sane pilot would attempt and one more than Odile Vance's paperwork strictly permitted.

I had tried the radio every hour since dawn.

*Kestrel to Halvorsen camp, Kestrel to Halvorsen camp, do you copy?*

Static. Not the clean static of distance but the thick, granular static of a set turned on with nobody home, or somebody home and choosing not to answer. There's a difference. A dead radio sounds empty. A silent radio sounds like someone standing very still behind a door, listening to your footsteps.

By noon I'd stopped expecting an answer and started listening for other things: gunfire, which carries strangely over water; machinery; the particular silence of a camp where everyone is pretending not to exist. What I got instead was the smell. Smoke, faint and wrong—not woodsmoke, which smells like breakfast and company, but the chemical sweetness of canvas burning, of stores going up, of somebody destroying their own supplies rather than abandon them to anyone else.

I have smelled that before. Once. I was nineteen, and I've spent fifteen years making sure I'd never smell it again.

"Damn it," I told the Kestrel, and gave her more throttle than she liked.

* * *

The landing at Devil's Throat sits in a fold of the gorge where the river briefly forgives you—a gravel shelf maybe sixty meters long, backed by a cutbank, sheltered enough that Halvorsen's people had built a proper dock out of green timber. You could see it from half a mile downriver, which meant I saw it while there was still time to turn around.

There wasn't much left of it. The pilings were charred to the waterline and the decking had collapsed into the shallows, black ribs sticking up like something that had died face-down. Behind it, where the tents should have been—Halvorsen ran a big operation, nine men and a cook according to Vance's manifest—there was a field of ash and aluminum. Tent poles bent into shapes geometry doesn't approve of. Crates split open, their contents scattered or taken. A surveyor's tripod stood upright in the middle of it all, untouched, which struck me as either sentiment or mockery.

And the tents themselves—the two that hadn't burned—were slashed. Not from outside. I walked the site for an hour before I understood what my eyes kept trying to tell me, and once I understood it I wished I hadn't. The cuts went outward. Every tear in that canvas opened from the inside, fabric burst along the seams, as if whatever came out of those tents had been in a hurry and hadn't bothered with zippers.

Inside work. Men cutting their way *out* of their own shelters, in the dark, fast.

I stood in the ash with my hand on the Kestrel's winch handle—it's not a weapon, exactly, but it's heavy and it's always nearby—and I made myself look at everything. The cook's cast-iron oven, tipped into the fire pit. A boot, one boot, wedged in the rocks at the high-water line. No bodies. That was the thing my mind kept circling back to like a fly: nine men, no bodies, no blood worth the name. Either everyone had walked away under their own power, or someone had been thorough about removing them, and I knew which possibility I preferred and which one I believed.

The wind came down the gorge and stirred the ash, and for a moment the whole camp lifted around my ankles like it was trying to leave without me.

That's when I heard the water.

Not the river. A canteen, being poured. A thin, desperate trickle from somewhere up in the cutbank, in the shade of an overhang where the limestone folded back on itself. I climbed toward it with the winch handle leading the way, because fifteen years on this river teaches you that anything alive and hiding is either prey or trap, and you don't get to know which until you're close enough to lose.

He was wedged into a crevice like something the river had deposited and forgotten. Younger than I expected—thirty, maybe, though the sun had cooked ten years onto him—and dressed in the remains of a surveyor's khakis gone gray with dust and worse. His lips were cracked into a map of their own. His hands, when he raised them, shook so badly the canteen rang against his teeth.

And clamped between his forearm and his chest, wrapped in both arms the way a drowning man holds a spar, was a brass canister the size of a loaf of bread. Sealed. Stamped. The kind of case survey companies use for core samples and maps they don't trust to paper alone.

"Don't," he said. It came out as mostly air. "Don't touch it."

"I'm not touching it," I said. "I'm looking at you. Can you walk?"

He stared at me the way men stare when they've been deciding for days whether help is real. Then his eyes went past me, down to the river, to the Kestrel sitting at the ruined dock like an answer to a prayer neither of us had filed, and something in his face broke open—not relief, nothing so kind. Recognition of a debt he didn't know how he'd pay.

"You're the supply boat," he whispered.

"I'm the supply boat."

"They said—" He stopped. Swallowed. Tried again. "They said nobody would come. They said the company would let the permits lapse first." A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh if laughter were capable of rust. "Nine days. That's what they gave us. Nine days, and then it wouldn't matter anymore."

"What wouldn't matter?"

His arms tightened around the canister. Whatever answer lived in that brass case, he pulled it closer, and his eyes did something I've seen twice in my life and never wanted to see again: they looked at me and decided I was a danger too.

"Water," I said. "Food. Then we're leaving. You can tell me on the river or you can tell me never, but I'm not camping here tonight."

He drank when I made him drink. He ate half a tin of beans and kept the rest in his lap like a child hoarding against winter. And when I asked him his name, he worked his mouth around it for a long moment before it came.

"Tomas Reyes," he said. "Surveyor. Second class." Then, quieter, staring at the burned dock below us: "There were nine of us, captain. Nine. And I am the only one who—" He stopped himself, jaw working, and pressed the canister against his sternum hard enough to leave a mark. "You don't understand what's in here. Nobody understands. That's why they—"

"Why who?" I said. "Reyes. Why *who*?"

But he had already gone somewhere behind his own eyes, some gallery deeper than the gorge, and no amount of asking brought him back. He sat in the shade of the overhang holding his brass secret and shaking, and I stood over him doing arithmetic I didn't like.

Nine men. One survivor. A canister he guarded like scripture. Tents cut open from within. And somewhere downstream, a woman with mismatched letterhead who paid triple rates and smiled like she was counting.

* * *

We got him aboard by dusk. He moved like a man walking on a frozen lake—every step a negotiation—and when I settled him into the wheelhouse bench he arranged the canister on his knees and put his hands flat on top of it and did not move them again.

I cast off from the ruin of the dock just as the light failed, because I did not intend to spend a night at Devil's Throat, and if you think that's superstition you haven't seen the place after dark. The gorge swallows the stars early. The walls go up and up until the sky is a rumor, and the river runs black and quick beneath you, and every sound comes back doubled off the stone. Somewhere above us, water dripped in the galleries with the patience of a clock.

I took us downriver slow, running the eddy lines, engine at a murmur. Reyes watched the banks slide past with the fixed attention of a man checking for pursuers, and after a while I realized I was doing it too.

"Reyes," I said, keeping my voice low. "Whoever did this. Are they coming back?"

He didn't answer for so long I thought he'd gone mute again. Then: "They didn't leave, captain. That's the thing you don't understand." His voice had found a little weight now, a little bottom. "They were *us*. Some of them. The ones who thought we should report it straight, and the ones who thought—" His hands flexed on the canister. "It stopped being an expedition on the fourth day underground. It became something else. And the ones who are left, the ones who won—" He looked at me, and the last of the daylight caught his eyes, and there was nothing in them I could comfort. "They'll want this. And they know I'm alive, because they let me be."

"They *let* you?"

"A message travels better than a body." He almost smiled. "Carry the canister downriver, they said. Tell them what we found. Let the companies fight over it. Let them burn each other's permits. That's what they wanted. That's what they *made* me for."

The Kestrel slid through the dark water, steady as she ever was, and I gripped the wheel and felt the dread in my chest stop being weather and start being ballast. Cold. Settled. Permanent.

Because I understood it now, or the shape of it anyway. That canister wasn't evidence. It was a match thrown into a room full of kerosene—mining concessions, permit deadlines, corporate fixers, and a river full of villages whose names weren't on any of the paperwork. And Tomas Reyes was the courier, whether he'd volunteered or not, and I had just made myself the second courier by pulling him out of the rocks.

Behind us, far upstream, a light flickered on the gorge wall. Small. Steady. Deliberate—three flashes, a pause, three again.

Reyes saw it too. His hands went flat on the brass.

"They're signaling the narrows," he breathed. "Captain—they're signaling *downstream*."

I put the wheel over and gave the Kestrel everything she had.
