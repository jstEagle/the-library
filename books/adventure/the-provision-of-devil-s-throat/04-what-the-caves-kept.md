## 4. What the Caves Kept

Tomas Reyes didn't speak for two days.

I let him be. I'd learned long ago that a body coming back from something terrible needs to remember it's a body first—water, salt, sleep, the small animal comforts that tell you the world still has edges. So I fed him, kept the Kestrel's stove going against the river damp, and said nothing about the brass canister he kept hooked under one arm even in sleep, like a man holding onto a rail in rough water.

On the third morning he woke before me and was sitting at the little table when I came out of the wheelhouse, and he said, "You burned the landing."

"I did what was left of it," I said. "Mostly it burned itself."

"The tents were slashed from inside." His voice was gravel and rust. "I want you to know I saw you looking at them."

"I look at everything," I said. "It's cheaper than asking questions."

He almost smiled. It didn't take. He turned the canister over in his hands, once, twice, like a man counting coins he wished were someone else's. Then he said, "Do you know what's in here?"

"Ore samples, I'd guess. That's what surveys carry."

"Ore samples." He said it the way a priest might repeat a heresy, testing whether it still sounded true. "Yes. There are ore samples. And there is everything else."

I poured coffee. Two cups. I'd stopped offering him the choice on day two; he drank whatever was put in front of him, which told me more about his condition than his silence had. I sat down across from him and waited, because waiting is the other half of not asking questions, and I've had fifteen years of practice at both.

* * *

He talked in fragments, the way ice talks when it starts to go out—a crack, then a long groan, then nothing, then three cracks at once. I've stitched his pieces together as best I can, though some of the seams are mine, and I won't pretend otherwise.

They'd come upriver in June, eleven of them, Halvorsen's outfit: surveyors, a geologist named Brandt who chewed tobacco like it owed him money, two drill men, a cook, Halvorsen himself with his concession maps and his deadline breathing down all their necks. The permits gave them until the end of the season to prove payable ore above the Throat or the whole claim lapsed back to the land office. Nine days now, by my count. Nine days, and everything they found or failed to find locked in a brass tube under a mute man's arm.

The limestone galleries opened up in the second week. Not caves exactly—the geologist insisted on *karst*, on solution channels, on words that make a hole in the rock sound like a filing system—but caves is what the crew called them, and caves is what they were: dry galleries running back into the bluff above the gorge, cool as a cellar, wide enough in places to drive a wagon through.

The ore was there. That part of the story was simple, and Tomas told it simply, the way men describe weather. Copper carbonates staining the walls green as pond water, running in veins thick as a man's wrist, richer than anything Halvorsen had dared hope. Brandt walked the first gallery with his lamp held high and laughed out loud, one bark of a laugh, and then went quiet, because even he understood what a find like that meant: money, lawyers, men with guns, the whole downstream machinery of a mining town assembling itself in the dark like ants finding sugar.

Then they went deeper. And that was where the story stopped being about ore.

"There were workings," Tomas said. "Timbered adits. Old ones—the wood was black, stone-hard, older than anything the company ever cut. Drainage channels cut into the floor. Spoil piles sorted by size, which means somebody knew what they were doing, and cared about doing it well." He looked at his hands. "And tools. Stone mauls. Bone awls. A hammer of antler set with quartz bits, worn smooth from use. Hundreds of years of use."

"Miners," I said.

"Miners." He nodded slowly. "Before any map says anyone lived here. Before any treaty, any survey, any record this company or the government or the church ever drew. People worked these galleries, Emrys. Worked them for generations, and then sealed them and left no name, no potsherd on the surface, nothing. They hid themselves so completely that the maps say they never existed. And the ore we came to claim—" his voice caught, steadied— "the ore was already claimed. Already taken, gallery by gallery, and what's left is what they chose to leave."

I sat with that. Outside, the river made its low continuous argument with the banks, the sound I've slept to for fifteen years, and for once it didn't steady me.

"What else is in the canister?" I asked, because I already knew there was an *else*. Men don't hold onto brass tubes like drowning men hold onto spars over ore samples.

"Brandt's notes. Photographs. Rubbings off the gallery walls." Tomas's hands had gone still around the canister. "Markings. A script nobody recognized—not decoration, Brandt was sure, not symbols of power or worship. Records. Tallies. Ownership marks, maybe. Names, if names is what they were." He swallowed. "There was a chamber at the deepest point. Sealed. The seal was broken recently—within a year, Brandt thought, by water, not by hands. Inside were the dead. Dozens. Sitting upright, arranged, each one with a small bundle in their lap. Ore, tools, the markings on slate. Everything they were. They didn't hide the mine from us, Captain. They hid *themselves*, down there, at the end, with the proof of everything they'd been. It was a library and a grave both."

The stove ticked. Somewhere under the hull, the current pressed its shoulder against us, patient as always.

"Halvorsen wanted it reported as virgin ground," Tomas said. "That was the argument. Brandt said the workings made the claim void—that you can't file on ground that's already been mined, that the antiquities laws alone would freeze the concession for a decade of litigation, and worse than litigation, that reporting it would bring every archaeologist, every government man, every company fixer in the territory down on those galleries like wolves on a carcass. Halvorsen said burying it was theft. Brandt said reporting it was desecration. And the crew split down the middle, and the split got loud, and then it got dark."

"How dark?"

Tomas looked at me for a long moment. He has eyes the color of weak tea, and in the grey light of the cabin they looked like water over something deep.

"Drill men don't argue like surveyors," he said. "One of them, Okafor—he sided with Halvorsen. Said the find belonged to whoever could hold it, that dead men don't file claims. There was a night when the shouting stopped being words. I went outside. When I came back—" He stopped. Started again. "Halvorsen was dead. Brandt was dead. Two others. Okafor and the rest were gone, took the longboat downriver in the dark, and the camp was burning. I don't know who struck first. I honestly don't. I've gone over it a hundred times and I can't make the memory hold still long enough to testify to it. I grabbed the canister because Brandt shoved it at me and said, *whatever happens, this doesn't belong to any of them*—and then I ran, and I've been running along that bluff for six days, drinking out of seeps, waiting for a boat that wasn't theirs."

"And you think the boat that finds you might be theirs."

"I think," Tomas said, "that knowledge is the heaviest cargo on this river, and everybody wants to load it onto somebody else."

* * *

I didn't sleep much after that. I stood in the wheelhouse with the lamp doused and watched the water slide past black and silver, and I thought about the things people hide, and why.

I grew up inside a secret myself, though mine never killed anybody outright—it just drowned four men and broke my father's name on Split Rock shoal, and taught me early that the truth is a thing you can carry or a thing that carries you, and either way it steers. I'd spent fifteen years being nobody on purpose. No debts, no questions, no past. And here was a man holding fifteen years' worth of somebody else's past in a brass tube, and the past was radioactive, and everyone downstream wanted it, and nobody wanted to *be* it.

Because that's the trick of a discovery like his. It looks like treasure. Richer ore than any company dreamed, a find worth fortunes—and underneath the fortune, a people erased so thoroughly that proving they existed would unmake every permit, every title, every comfortable lie the territory runs on. Report it, and the galleries fill with lawyers and soldiers and the dead get photographed and catalogued and argued over in courtrooms a thousand miles from their river. Bury it, and you become the thief, keeping stolen goods on behalf of men who died for less.

There was no clean hand in it. I understood that much. Whatever Tomas carried, carrying it had already cost eleven men their camp and five of them their lives, and it hadn't finished costing.

I heard him behind me, barefoot on the deck boards. He stood in the wheelhouse doorway a while without speaking, the way river people do, letting the silence do its work.

"You could throw it in the gorge," he said finally. "Some nights I want to. Let the Throat keep their secrets. It's kept worse."

"But?"

"But Brandt gave it to *me*. Not to the river. Not to Halvorsen's ghost." His jaw worked. "He said it belongs to the people the maps forgot. And I don't know who they are anymore, or if there are any left, or how you return something to people who hid themselves so well the world decided they were never here. But I know it isn't mine, and it isn't Halvorsen's, and it sure as hell isn't the land office's."

The current shouldered the hull. Downstream, somewhere below the narrows, nine days' worth of deadline was ticking toward somebody's signature, and I remembered the smell of Odile Vance's paperwork that didn't quite match her letterhead, and I felt the particular cold that comes up your spine when you realize a deal has started going bad and you're standing in the middle of it.

"Tomas," I said, "how many men did Okafor have with him when they took the longboat?"

"Four. Maybe five."

"And how long does a man with four friends and no boat wait downriver before he starts hiring?"

Tomas went very still. "You've seen something."

"Not yet," I said, and picked up the glasses, and swept the southern bank where the side sloughs braid into the main channel like veins off a heart. "But the water's been wrong since noon. Too quiet. Like it's holding its breath."

Through the lenses, at the mouth of the third slough, something moved that wasn't a bird.
