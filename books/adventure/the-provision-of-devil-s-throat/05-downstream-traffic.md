## 5. Downstream Traffic

I'd been running the Verrazano long enough to know that the river tells you things before they happen. Not in words—the river doesn't waste words—but in the way the water moves, the way the birds go quiet, the way a morning smells when something upstream or downstream has gone wrong. That third morning out of Devil's Throat, with Tomas Reyes sleeping off his dehydration under an oilskin in the stern and the brass canister locked in my chart box like it might hatch, the river told me plenty.

It started with the birds.

Gulls work the narrows below the Throat all season, picking at whatever the current discards. But as I came down through the last bend above Split Rock, they were all on one bank, packed shoulder to shoulder on the shingle like commuters waiting for a ferry that never comes. Birds don't do that unless something's disturbed their stretch of water. And nothing disturbs gulls except people who don't care about being quiet.

I killed the engine to half-throttle and listened.

There it was, underneath the river's usual argument with itself: a diesel. Not mine. Running steady, patient, the way a boat runs when it's working a channel slowly, deliberately—sounding it, maybe, or looking for something. Salvage crews run loud and sloppy. Patrol boats announce themselves from a mile out because announcing is half their job. This one was running quiet and methodical, and that combination on this stretch of river meant exactly one thing: somebody was hunting.

I eased the Kestrel toward the north bank where the willows grow right down into the water, thick enough to hide a hull if you know how to slide in among them without cracking a branch. Fifteen years of practice makes that kind of entry look lazy. You drift, you let the current do the work, you never reverse your propeller where the sound carries. I killed the engine entirely fifty yards out and ghosted in on momentum alone, the bow sliding through the willow fringe so gently that a heron standing three feet away didn't even bother to look up.

Then I waited.

Tomas woke sometime past noon. I heard him moving in the stern, careful, still moving like a man whose body had learned not to trust itself. He crawled forward on his elbows and settled beside me in the pilothouse shadow, and we both watched the narrows through the green lattice of leaves.

The other boat came through around two o'clock.

She was a converted patrol hull, forty feet, gunmetal gray, with her name painted over and the patch job done badly—you could see the ghost of the old letters through the new paint if you knew to look, which I did, because I had spent fifteen years learning to see exactly that kind of thing. Painted-over names are a confession. Honest boats keep their names.

There were six men visible on deck. Four carried rifles the way soldiers carry them, slung but ready, hands finding the stocks every few seconds like they were checking a wound. The other two worked a fathometer and a chart table set up on the afterdeck, and every so often one of them would point at the bank—at my bank—and the boat would slow, and a man would go ashore, and the gulls would lift and resettle further down.

They weren't sounding the channel. I'd watched salvage crews work for years; they read the water, not the banks. These men were reading the banks. Looking for landings. Looking for camps. Looking, though they couldn't have known it yet, for a burned landing site and slashed tents and whatever trail a starving surveyor had left crawling down to the water.

Looking for evidence. Or for witnesses.

"They're not Halvorsen's people," Tomas said. His voice was still rough, a voice used once too hard, but it was working. "Halvorsen hired local crew. Those men are company."

"I noticed."

"Vance's letterhead," he said, and left the rest unsaid, because there wasn't any more of it to say. The paperwork hadn't matched because the paperwork wasn't meant to match. It was meant to be deniable. Somewhere downriver, men in offices had written a contract designed to fail, so that when things went wrong above the Throat—and they had planned for things going wrong, or had made sure of it—no signature would connect back to anyone.

Nine days. That's what Tomas had told me, hoarse and shaking by the fire the night before: nine days until the concession permits expired, and everything found—or hidden—in those caves reverted to the provincial trust, open claim, public record. Nine days for whoever held the real paper to secure the site, secure the find, and secure the silence around it.

And here came the securing, riding a painted-over hull through my narrows.

* * *

We lay in the willows through the afternoon while the gray boat worked its way down the narrows and then, to my considerable displeasure, worked its way back up.

That was the part that changed everything. A boat passing through is traffic. A boat searching is a net. They ran the stretch twice more, dropping men at each old landing—there were four between the Throat and Split Rock, all of them known to me, all of them now being visited by men with rifles—and each time the boat came back to the channel, I marked what they'd carried back aboard. The first time, nothing. The second time, a canvas pack that one of the chart-table men opened and went through page by page while another man stood over him holding a lantern, though it was broad daylight. Papers. Somebody's papers, pulled out of somebody's camp.

Tomas watched that with his jaw working. "Those'll be Halvorsen's field logs," he said quietly. "Or what's left of them."

"You said the crew split."

"The crew split." He said it the way a man says a thing he's said to himself ten thousand times without surviving the saying. "Half of them wanted to report everything. The ore, the galleries, the—" He stopped. His hand drifted toward his jacket pocket, toward the shape of nothing, because the canister was in my chart box now and he knew it. "The rest thought some things should stay buried. Both halves were right. That was the problem."

"And the argument ended badly."

"The argument ended with rifles," he said. "Rifles end most arguments up there. The ones they don't end, the river does."

I kept my eyes on the gray boat. Six men, maybe eight—there could be more below decks. Rifles, a fathometer, a chart table, and a mandate with a nine-day fuse. Against that I had a thirty-year-old riverboat, a half-dead surveyor, a sealed canister full of the most dangerous paper on the Verrazano, and a talent for not being noticed that had just stopped working.

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape, sitting in the willow shade with the gulls complaining downstream: they had visited every landing but mine. My mooring above the Throat had been burned, my tracks were in the mud at the waterline, and a boat answering no radio call had come and gone from a dead camp. Sooner or later—one of these sweeps, or the next—they would find the Kestrel's wake, or a fisherman would mention a green-hulled boat heading downriver with a passenger, or Vance herself would put the pieces together, because Vance struck me as a woman who put pieces together for a living.

Invisibility, I had spent fifteen years building it, plank by plank, debt-free year by debt-free year. No debts, no questions, no past. And it had worked, the way a dam works—right up until the water behind it gets deep enough.

* * *

Toward evening the gray boat anchored mid-channel below Split Rock, dead center on the narrows, which was either arrogance or a message. A lamp came up on her masthead, and a launch went down over her transom with two men in it, and they ran the launch along the south shore with a spotlight playing over the rocks and reeds.

I took the Kestrel deeper into the slough.

There was a side channel off the north bank, half a mile back from the main stem, that the charts marked as dead water—marsh and deadfall, good only for frogs and mosquitoes. The charts were wrong, because charts usually are; there was a navigable gut through it, six feet deep at low water, that I had found eleven years ago during a season when I needed very much to be nowhere in particular. I backed the Kestrel into it now, stern-first through a curtain of alders, until the hull kissed soft mud and the alder branches closed over the cabin top like a hand closing over a coin.

From there, through a gap in the brush, I could watch the narrows. Tomas sat across from me in the darkened pilothouse, and neither of us spoke for a long time. The mosquitoes found us anyway. Some things you can't hide from.

"They'll sweep the side channels tomorrow," Tomas said finally. "That's standard. Secure the site, then clear the approaches."

"You know a lot about standard."

"I was army before I was a surveyor." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Emrys. Listen to me. What's in that canister—if it gets delivered to the right office, the companies eat it, bury it, and the villages above the Throat lose their water rights inside five years. If it gets delivered to the wrong office, same thing, faster. If it gets destroyed, the permits lapse, the whole concession dies, and nobody profits and nobody's erased. There's no version of this where handing it over helps anyone but them."

"I know."

"Do you?" He leaned forward. In the last light through the alders his face looked carved out of something patient and tired. "Because I've watched men hold that canister for six days, and every one of them started out saying *I know*, and every one of them ended up either dead or trying to sell it. It weighs on you. It finds the price of you and pays it."

I looked out at the narrows, at the gray boat riding her anchor light in the middle of the channel like a padlock on the river. Below her, somewhere downriver, Odile Vance was coming with her mismatched letterhead and her triple-pay telegram and whatever else she knew about me that I hadn't told her. Above me, the Throat held its burned camp and its dead men and its secret galleries. And here in the dead water, in the mosquito dark, sat the only boat, the only witness, the only copy of the truth that anybody's employers didn't already control.

Fifteen years of being no one, and it turned out no one was exactly who everybody was looking for.

"We move at first light," I said. "Before they sweep. Down through the narrows, past them, in the fog if the fog cooperates."

"And if it doesn't cooperate?"

"Then we go through anyway," I said, "and I finally give this river a reason to remember my name."

Tomas looked at me a moment too long, the way men do when they're deciding whether you're brave or broken, and I confess I wasn't entirely sure myself. Then he nodded, once, and went aft to sleep with his hand resting on the empty pocket where the canister used to live.

I stayed up and watched the anchor light burn, and counted the hours until dawn, and understood—for the first time since I'd taken Vance's money—that the hunted stillness was over. Whatever came next, I would be doing it moving.
