## 6. The Price of Silence

I heard the other boat before I saw it—the particular arrhythmia of an engine that had been pushed too hard for too long, the sound of money being spent like it was water. Which, on the Verrazano in low season, was a joke the river would appreciate.

I'd put the Kestrel into a backwater slough two miles below the Throat, tucked under willows with her engine cold and my coffee going lukewarm in its tin cup. Tomas slept in the wheelhouse, or lay there with his eyes shut, which for him amounted to the same thing. The canister sat between us like a third passenger who didn't pay for his berth.

The boat came around the bend at half past noon. Steel-hulled, twin screws, new paint over old rust—a company boat dressed up as something else, the way a fixer dresses up as a surveyor. It slowed at the burned landing. I watched through the leaves as three men got out and stood among the slashed tents, and one of them crouched where I'd crouched four days ago, reading the ground the way I read water.

They didn't find what they were looking for, because what they were looking for was upstream of them, hiding under willows, drinking bad coffee.

Then the woman came down the bank.

Odile Vance moved differently from her hired muscle—no hurry, no wasted motion, the walk of someone who had never once been surprised by a river. She stood at the edge of the landing for a long moment, looking out at the current, and then she turned her head, slowly, toward the willows where I hid.

She couldn't have seen me. There was no way she could have seen me. But she raised one hand anyway—not waving, exactly. More like acknowledging a debt.

I sat very still and felt fifteen years of careful invisibility peel off me like paint off a hull that's been scraped once too often.

* * *

She came aboard alone. That was either courtesy or confidence, and I'd learned to fear both equally.

"Captain Marchetti." She stepped onto the deck without waiting for permission, which told me everything about how this conversation was going to go. "You're a difficult woman to find."

"I'm not difficult to find. I'm just not standing still."

"Fair." She looked around the wheelhouse with the flat appraisal of someone pricing a used boat. Her eyes touched the canister—I'd covered it with a tarp, but Vance struck me as a woman who could see through tarps the way her employers saw through letterhead—and moved on. "May I sit?"

"You may stand. Boats are for people who ask nicely."

Something flickered across her face that might have been respect, if respect could survive being filed under *assets*. She leaned against the doorframe instead.

"Tomas Reyes is aboard," she said. Not a question.

"He's asleep."

"He's a witness. So are you. And here's the arithmetic, Captain, because I know you've already done it: the Halvorsen concession expires in nine days. Whatever Reyes pulled out of those caves is worth nothing after that unless it reaches the land office with a signature attached. My employers would prefer it reached them quietly. The men on that steel hull—" she tipped her head downstream, "—would prefer it reach them loudly. You're currently the only thing standing between those two preferences, and I promise you only one of them involves you walking away."

"And your preference?"

"My preference," she said, "is that everyone keeps their name."

That landed wrong, and I let it show. Vance noticed. Vance noticed everything; it was presumably why they paid her.

"Here's the offer." She said it briskly, like reading terms aloud. "You deliver the canister to me. Today, tomorrow, I don't care about the drama of it. In exchange: no testimony required from you or Reyes, no magistrate, no record. The expedition's fate gets written down as misadventure—bad water, bad luck, the kind of thing the Verrazano does every spring. Your cargo fee triples. And nobody ever asks you another question again, which I understand is the closest thing to heaven a woman like you believes in."

"A woman like me," I repeated.

"Emrys Marchetti." She said the whole name carefully, tasting each syllable, and then she did the thing I'd been dreading since I first read her permit paperwork upside down in the fuel office at Corben's Landing. She smiled. "Or should I say—Marchetti by choice?"

The coffee in my cup had gone fully cold. I set it down anyway, because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

"There was a boat called the *Aldernay*," Vance went on, conversational now, almost gentle, which was worse. "Ran the narrows above Split Rock fourteen years ago, overloaded, unlicensed, at night. Went down with six men aboard. Four bodies recovered. The owner was never charged, because the owner's name on the registry belonged to a man who'd been dead a year already—a paper captain, a ghost. The real owner was nineteen years old and had bought the registry with her father's insurance money and her mother's silence."

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Tomas hadn't stirred behind his curtain, but I had the sudden, irrational certainty that he was listening, that he'd been listening the whole time, that the dead of the *Aldernay* were crowded into the cabin behind me with their river-soaked patience.

"The family name was Fairweather," Vance said. "Bram Fairweather's daughter, actually—there's a romance in it, if you squint. The daughter took her mother's maiden name and went upriver and became nobody, and it worked beautifully for fifteen years. Do you know why it worked, Captain? Because nobody was looking. But somebody is looking now. My employers employ people whose entire profession is looking. And when they look at you, they won't see a reliable supply runner. They'll see a woman who sank a boat full of men to save her own skin, and whether that's true or not will matter considerably less than how useful the story is."

"It isn't true."

"No?" She tilted her head. "Tell me how it happened, then. Tell me the version you've been rehearsing in the dark for fourteen years. Because here's the beautiful part of my offer, Captain: you don't have to tell anyone. Deliver the canister, keep your mouth shut, and the *Aldernay* stays exactly where it is—fourteen years underwater, six men sleeping, one girl ashore with clean hands. Silence all around. Everyone's name stays clean. Isn't that what you came upriver to buy?"

I looked at her for a long time. She was good. She was better than good—she'd found the exact seam in me, the hairline crack I didn't let surveyors map, and she'd driven a wedge into it with the precision of a woman who'd done this to other people in other rooms.

And the terrible thing, the thing I want to be honest about even now, is that for about ten seconds I considered it.

Ten seconds. Long enough to feel the weight of it: triple pay, no magistrate, no questions, the past staying sunk where I'd moored it. Fifteen years of careful nothing, preserved. I could hand her the canister and go back to being unremarkable, and the river would keep my secret the way it keeps everything—without judgment, without memory, without mercy either.

Then I thought about Tomas's face when he'd finally started talking, three days ago, hoarse as a crow: *they were already there, Captain. Somebody mined that vein before us and hid it, and the maps say nobody ever lived there, and the company wants to write them out twice.* I thought about the villages above the Throat that buy their medicine from boats like mine because the companies can't be bothered. I thought about what it costs to belong somewhere, and how I'd spent fifteen years believing belonging was something you could purchase with silence, the way you purchase a harbor berth—paid up, no questions, nobody asks your business.

It doesn't work that way. I think I always knew. Belonging bought with silence isn't belonging. It's just a longer lease on the same loneliness.

"No," I said.

Vance's expression didn't change, but something behind it recalculated. "Captain—"

"You want the canister, take it." I stepped aside from the tarp, deliberately, letting her see I wasn't guarding it like treasure. "But understand what you're taking. If it goes to your employers, it goes with my affidavit attached—filed with the river magistrate at Corben's Landing, witnessed, dated nine days before expiry. Full account of the camp. Full account of the caves, as Reyes tells it. And full account of a steel-hulled company boat harassing a licensed vessel in restricted water, which I believe carries a fine your employers would find educational."

"That affidavit is a bluff. You'd be naming yourself."

"I named myself the day I picked Reyes up." I shrugged. "Turns out it weighs less than I remembered."

For the first time, Odile Vance looked genuinely uncertain, and I'll confess it was the finest thing I'd seen all season. She studied me the way she'd studied the wheelhouse—pricing, appraising—and whatever number she arrived at, she didn't like it.

"You're making a mistake," she said quietly. "The men on that steel hull don't offer terms. When they catch you—and they will, because you can't outrun twin screws in a loaded riverboat—they won't ask nicely, and they won't care what name you're wearing."

"Then I'd best not get caught."

"They'll board you at Split Rock. It's the only place the narrows slow enough to come alongside, and they know it, and now so do you." She straightened her coat, and just like that she was a businesswoman again, closing a file. "Last chance, Captain. Clean names. Both of ours."

"I've had a dirty name for fourteen years," I said. "I'm rather fond of it. It's mine."

She held my eyes a moment longer—measuring, filing, committing me to whatever ledger she kept behind her face—and then she stepped off the Kestrel onto the mud without a backward glance. I watched her walk up the bank to the landing, watched the steel hull back water and swing downstream toward the narrows, and I understood that I had just traded a comfortable lie for a running fight, and that the running fight would happen at Split Rock, and that she'd told me where because she wanted me to run there. Wanted me predictable. Wanted me scared.

Behind me, the curtain in the aft cabin moved.

"She knows about the *Aldernay*," Tomas said. His voice was stronger now, days of water and rest having given it back to him. He came forward into the wheelhouse, and he didn't look at me with pity, which I decided I'd allow. "How long has she known?"

"Since before she hired me, I'd guess. Why else hire a ghost to do a ghost's errand." I turned back to the wheel, to the channel, to the nine days ticking down like water out of a cracked hull. "Doesn't matter. What matters is she gave us the location and the timeline, which means either she's careless—"

"—or it's a trap," Tomas finished.

"Or it's a trap." I rang the engine bell, soft, twice, the way my father used to when he meant *get ready*. "Tomas, how do you feel about running the narrows at night?"

He looked at the darkening water, then at me, and for the first time since I'd found him clutching that brass canister among the ruined tents, he almost smiled.

"Captain," he said, "I survived Devil's Throat. Show me Split Rock."
