## 7. Nine Days to Expiry

There is a particular kind of dark that comes off the water at night—not the absence of light but the presence of everything the light would have let you avoid. The Verrazano in low-water season after sundown is that kind of dark. It doesn't hide the river from you. It makes you negotiate with it.

I ran the Kestrel anyway.

Not because I wanted to. Nobody wants to read a river by feel, with the engine idled down to a mutter and the bow wave making less noise than your own breathing. You do it because the alternative is arriving second, and second place on this stretch of water has historically meant a shallow grave with good views.

The rival crew had gone to anchor below Split Rock when I slipped past them—two lanterns swinging lazy on their deck, a man smoking at the rail, the whole performance of men who believe they have time. Nine days until the permits expired. They thought they had nine days. What they didn't know was that Odile Vance had already spent most of her leverage on me, and that leverage had come back from the exchange smelling like smoke.

So: night water. The moon was down to a paring and wouldn't rise for hours. I stood at the wheel with the chart rolled shut under my arm because charts lie at this stage of the season—they were drawn in spring water, and spring water is a different river wearing the same name. Every sandbar had moved. Every channel had narrowed. The Kestrel drew four feet loaded, and the bars between here and the land office at Corben's Landing drew anywhere from three feet to a rumor.

You test them by feel. That's not poetry; it's procedure. You bring the bow in slow until the bottom comes up through the hull as a shiver instead of a sound, and then you read that shiver—mud gives soft, like pushing through cold porridge; sand gives hard, a quick grating kiss; rock gives you nothing at all until it's too late, which is why you never trust the places where the current goes quiet. Quiet water over rock is the river holding its breath before it laughs at you.

I did this for six miles. It is not an exaggeration to say I did it with my feet. Through the soles of my boots, up through my knees, into the small of my back where fifteen years of helmsmanship had deposited its archive. The Kestrel and I had an arrangement: she told me what she felt, and I pretended to be calm about it.

Tomas Reyes sat on the cargo hatch behind me, wrapped in a blanket, holding a mug of coffee he hadn't drunk. He'd started talking again around dusk—fragments first, then whole sentences, as if his voice had been a boat aground and he was working it off a bar one shove at a time.

"You could go faster," he said.

"I could also be on the bottom," I said. "One of us gets to choose."

He was quiet a while. The engine muttered. Somewhere off the port side a night bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and I marked it, because birds go quiet over deep channels and get chatty over shallows where the fish are trapped. The bird said shallow. I swung wide.

"The vein," Tomas said. "You've been thinking about it."

"I've been thinking about sandbars."

"You've been thinking about it since you asked me what was in the canister and then didn't open it." He shifted. "That's a particular kind of discipline, Captain Marchetti. Not opening it. I watched eleven grown men fail at exactly that."

"Eleven men and one canister," I said. "And how did that arithmetic end?"

The silence that came back was answer enough, and we both let it sit. The current shouldered the bow and I corrected with two fingers on the wheel, feeling for the channel's spine the way you'd feel for a pulse.

"It's not ore," Tomas said finally. "Or rather—it isn't only ore. The galleries are worked. Somebody mined that copper out generations ago, and they did it with fire-setting and stone hammers and a patience nobody in this century has. And they left marks. Tallies, signs, a script nobody on the crew recognized—and Halvorsen had a linguist who'd worked three continents. The maps say no one lived there. The maps say the gorge was empty until the survey companies put their names on it."

"And your people couldn't agree on what to do about that."

"Halvorsen wanted to report it—all of it. Said the truth would protect the communities upriver, give them standing, make the concession impossible to grant quietly." His laugh came out wrong, like a cough. "Vance's employers wanted the copper and nothing else. And the middle of the crew—the ones with families and debts—they just wanted it all to go away. Reporting it meant litigation for a decade. Hiding it meant complicity forever. Doing nothing meant the deadline decided for them." He looked at his untouched coffee. "Doing nothing is how the fight started. Nobody decides, and then everybody decides at once, badly."

Ahead, the river bent hard to starboard around a bluff the charts called the Deacon, and below the bend the water spread out wide and shallow—the worst kind of crossing, half a mile of braided channels that rearranged themselves with every flood. I brought the Kestrel down to bare steerage way and listened. You can hear a bar at night if the current runs over it—a low, continuous hiss, like the river whispering *not here* over and over. The trick is telling which whisper means *go around* and which means *you're fine, keep coming*. There's a difference in pitch. Sand whispers lower. I don't have a better way to explain it than that, and I've never met anyone who did.

I took the eastern channel because it whispered lowest, and the Kestrel's keel kissed something once, softly, apologetically, and slid free.

"That was close," Tomas said.

"That was fine."

"That sounded close."

"Tomas." I kept my eyes forward. "Everything on this river sounds close. Close is the water's native language. What matters is whether it stays close or becomes *here*."

* * *

We tied up at a driftwood snag above Miller's Bend to wait out the worst of the dark—not from fear, but because there was a stretch ahead I knew I couldn't read blind even with good feet, and pride sinks boats faster than rocks do. I lit the stove low, made real food, and Tomas talked, because talking was clearly doing him more good than eating.

"They'll say I stole it," he said. "The canister. When this is done, whatever this is, Vance's people will build a story where I'm the thief and the murderer and the reason the expedition failed. Stories are cheaper than evidence. That's why they burned the landing—to make sure the only story left standing was theirs."

"Then we make sure there's another story standing next to it."

"With what? A brass tube neither of us will open?"

"With testimony." I handed him bread. "You saw the galleries. You can describe them. Descriptions are evidence too, if enough people swear to them."

"And then what?" He set the bread down without eating it. "You file it with the magistrate at Corben's Landing, and the land office opens an inquiry, and the inquiry takes two years, and during those two years every village above the Throat gets visited by lawyers and then by men who aren't lawyers. You think the companies lose those fights? They don't need to win. They only need the fighting to cost more than anyone upstream can pay. Halvorsen understood that at the end. It's why he stopped arguing. He wasn't convinced. He was tired."

I fed the stove a stick and watched it catch. "And what do you want?"

"I want—" He stopped. Started again, slower, the way a man tests ice. "I grew up in Ferro Bend. My mother sold fish to the steamers. If that copper gets granted quietly, Ferro Bend gets a company town, a company dock, and a company price for everything we sell and everything we buy. If it gets reported loudly, we get ten years of court and men asking questions door to door. Either way the river stops being ours." He looked at me. "Is there a third door, Captain? Because I've been sitting in that camp for nineteen days looking for one, and all I found was a brass canister and a lot of dead friends."

The stove ticked. Out on the water, the current went on doing what current does, which is refuse to consider anyone's position.

"There might be," I said, "but it isn't a door. It's a window, and it's narrow, and it closes in eight days."

"What window?"

"The permits expire. All of them—Vance's employers' and the rival claim both. If the land office at Corben's receives a formal objection before expiry, the whole concession reverts to review. Not granted, not denied. Frozen." I turned the bread loaf in my hands. "Frozen concessions don't make company towns. They don't send question-askers door to door either. They just sit there, unprofitable, while everyone with money loses interest. I've seen it happen on the upper Wold. A frozen claim outlasts its owners."

"And the objection? Who files it?"

"Anyone with standing. Which is the problem, because standing requires a name, and names are what got your camp killed." I looked at him across the stove light. "Unless the name belongs to someone who stopped needing to hide."

He studied me for a long moment. "Whose name?"

"Mine," I said, and heard myself say it, and was surprised at how little it weighed. "It turns out I have one. I've been keeping it in storage."

* * *

I cast off at three in the morning, because the last leg to Corben's Landing was twelve miles of the meanest water on the Verrazano and I wanted daylight's first gray hour for the final approach. Tomas took the bow with a sounding pole and a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, calling depths back to me in a voice that grew steadier with every call—as if the river were giving him his own voice back one fathom at a time.

"Four feet!" he'd call. "Three and a half! Three—back off, back off!"

And I'd back off, and swing, and try the next line, and feel the bottom tell me things no chart ever bothered to record. We threaded the Narrows of the Little Saints with six inches under the keel and Tomas laughing somewhere in the middle of being terrified, and I realized I was smiling too, and that the smile had teeth in it. Speed feels like honesty. That's the trap and the truth of it both. Running fast doesn't mean you're right—but standing still had gotten eleven men killed, and motion, any motion honestly chosen, beat the grave-stillness of a decision deferred.

By the time the sky went the color of dirty pewter, Corben's Landing was five miles downstream, and the land office opened at seven, and I had the Kestrel doing a honest eight knots in water that deserved four.

Which is when Tomas, at the bow, went still in a way that made my stomach drop before he said anything.

"Smoke," he called back. "Aft of us. Coming fast. Two engines."

I looked over my shoulder. Downriver astern, hugging the shadowed bank where the light was weakest, a long black hull was gaining on us with the patience of something that had always known where we'd be—and at our bow, rising out of the mist where the river split around Split Rock, a second boat was already waiting in the channel, broadside, engines idling, leaving water on either side of us exactly one boat wide.

They hadn't been chasing us at all. They'd been herding us.

"Tomas," I said, and reached for the wheel, "hold on to something that believes in you."
