## 8. Ambush at Split Rock

Split Rock announces itself before you see it, the way bad news does. The river narrows to a third of its width there, and the water goes from brown to a kind of bruised green, and the sound changes from the ordinary gossip of a moving river to something lower and more insistent, like a crowd deciding whether to be friendly. I had run the narrows maybe two hundred times in fifteen years, and every one of them I'd said the same thing to myself: *respect the throat of it, Emrys. Everything that goes through gets swallowed a little.*

Tomas was in the wheelhouse with me, sitting on the bench I'd padded with a life vest, holding a mug of coffee he hadn't drunk in an hour. Nine days to expiry had become eight, and eight had become this grey dawn, and somewhere behind us—half a day back, maybe less—Vance was burning fuel to catch us. Ahead, if the shape in the morning mist resolved the way I feared it would, was the other thing.

"There," Tomas said. His voice had come back to him over the last two days in pieces, like cargo floating up out of a wreck, and he still used it sparingly, the way a man rations water.

I saw it. A boat, anchored off the north point above the narrows, riding low and riding wrong—low the way a boat rides when it's carrying more men than cargo. No running lights the night before, no registry pennant, no paint scheme I knew. Not salvage. Salvage men work lazy and drink earlier. Not patrol. Patrol boats fly something.

"That's them," Tomas said. "The ones Halvorsen was afraid of. The ones he wouldn't name on the radio."

"He should have named them anyway," I said. "Names are free. Everything else costs."

I cut the Kestrel toward the south channel, the one the charts call impassable and the charts are half right about. It was a gamble dressed up as a plan, which is the only kind of plan I've ever trusted. The south channel runs close under Split Rock itself, close enough that in high water the spray comes aboard, and it has a heart of gravel right in the middle that has ended better boats than mine. But it was the only water that didn't pass within hailing distance of that anchored shape.

Tomas went below without being told. He'd learned my boat in two days the way a drowning man learns a rope. He took the brass canister from its hiding place under the floor plates—my hiding place, chosen for exactly this kind of morning—and stood in the companionway holding it against his chest with both arms, and I want to say he looked calm, but he didn't. He looked like a man carrying something that had already decided to kill him.

"Whatever happens," he said, "don't let them have it."

"I don't intend anything to happen."

"Captain." He said it flat, no respect in it, just arithmetic. "Whatever happens."

* * *

They let us get into the narrows before they moved. That was the tell of professionals. Amateurs board you at the dock, where you can still run. These waited until the Kestrel was committed, wheel hard over, the south channel's gravel bar a hundred yards ahead and the current doing half my thinking for me, and then the other boat's engine turned over with a sound like a fist unclenching and she came off her anchor fast and ugly, cutting the corner, running the north channel straight at the throat of the narrows where the two channels braid together and no chart on the river will tell you what the water is doing.

"Emrys," Tomas said from the companionway.

"I see them."

They came alongside on the starboard side before I could finish the turn, and I will give them this: they were good. A grapnel on a line, thrown by a man who'd thrown a hundred of them, took my starboard rail amidships, and the two boats slammed together with a sound I felt in my teeth. Then they came over the rail—four of them, five, boots on my deck, men with the flat faces of people being paid to do a thing rather than people who wanted it—and the fight started the way fights on boats start, which is badly, in a space too small for it, with the whole world tilting.

I have been in exactly three fights in my life and won one of them, and the one I won I won because I stopped fighting like a person and started fighting like a boat. You don't trade blows on a pitching deck. You give ground until the deck does the work. I gave ground. The first man who reached the wheelhouse door I gave a full turn of the wheel and a wall of the wheelhouse itself, and the river helped, a swell off the north channel's current rolling us both, and he went down among my cargo crates and did not get up quickly. The second one caught my arm and I caught his wrist and we stood there for one long second, close enough that I could smell the river and the coffee on him and the cheap tobacco, and I understood with a strange clarity that he was not my enemy, that he was a man with a job, that the thing between us was a canister and a deadline and a company letterhead, and then I broke his grip by dropping my whole weight and butting forward with the top of my skull, which is not a thing I recommend, and he sat down hard on my deck plates.

The third man had a knife. Of course he did. There is always a third man with a knife, and he always waits until you're busy.

Tomas saved me, or the canister did. He came up out of the companionway swinging a hand spike from my own tool rack, and he was not a fighter, he was a surveyor, he held it like a pencil, but the third man flinched away from the swing and the flinch put him against the rail and the rail, in the narrows, in a swell, is not a place you flinch to. He went over. I heard the water take him and then I heard nothing, and I have decided—this morning, writing this, I am deciding—that the current carried him to the shallows on the north side, because the current owes me that much and I intend to collect.

"Captain," Tomas gasped, and then the fourth man hit us both.

What happened after that happened in pieces. The wheel. The gravel bar showing its pale teeth off the port bow, thirty yards, twenty. The grapnel line still holding us to the other boat, the other boat's helmsman panicking now, both vessels slewing toward the rock together, two animals tied at the throat. The fourth man wrenching at the companionway hatch where Tomas had stowed the canister, and Tomas on the deck with blood in his mouth, and me with the wheel in my hands and a choice arriving faster than thought.

I could scuttle her. Open the seacocks, sink the Kestrel, the canister, the fight, all of it, forty feet of my life going down into the narrows with the evidence in her hold. It was the clean option. It was the option I would have named, a week ago, as the smart one.

I didn't take it. You don't burn a house to kill a rat. The Kestrel had carried me through fifteen years of being nobody, and I was done being nobody, and a woman with a name does not drown her own boat.

So I did the arithmetic instead. The gravel bar ahead. The braid of the two currents. The place, a quarter mile below the narrows, where the river drops into the Throat itself—the gorge, the real one, the one the maps draw as a jagged line and the rivermen call the Swallower, where the water goes down through limestone into channels no diver has ever come back from, where anything lost is lost the way the dead are lost, permanently and with interest.

I put the wheel hard over to starboard and gave the Kestrel to the current.

The fourth man understood one heartbeat before I did. He came off the hatch, came at me, and I met him with the wheel spoke and my shoulder and the full rolling weight of the turn, and the two of us went down into the cargo together as the gravel bar kissed us—kissed, scraped, a shriek of keel on stone that I will hear in my sleep for the rest of my life—and then the current took the scrape and turned it into a slide, and the Kestrel came off the bar the way she always came off things, complaining but obedient, and we were through the narrows and into the fast water below with the other boat tearing free of the grapnel line behind us and falling away.

The fourth man got up. Tomas got up slower. And the canister—the brass canister, the whole weight of the Halvorsen expedition, the vein, the caves, the people the maps say never lived there, the permits, the concessions, Vance's clean name and mine—was in the fourth man's hands.

He stood at the rail with it, breathing hard, and behind him the Throat's edge came up fast, the water going green and then black, the sound of it rising from a murmur to a roar, and he looked at the canister and he looked at me and I could see the whole transaction in his face: *deliver it, get paid, go home.*

"Put it down," I said. The wheel was under my hands and the river was doing the rest. "You have about ten seconds to decide how you want to spend them."

He decided wrong. He decided the way men decide when the money is real and the water is abstract. He turned for the wheelhouse door, canister in his arms, and I put the Kestrel into the seam where the two currents met, the one I'd found at nineteen and trusted at forty, and the boat rolled.

She rolled hard, harder than I meant, hard enough to put the starboard rail down to within a hand's width of the water, and the fourth man went down with her, and the canister went out of his arms.

I heard it hit the deck. Once. A brass sound, a bell sound, a small clear note under all that roar. Then it rolled—Tomas swears it rolled, and I believe him, because everything else that day rolled—and it went over the low rail, and the Throat took it.

No splash. That's the part I keep returning to. There was no splash, or none we could hear over the roar of the gorge, and no float, no glint, no second chance. The water there doesn't give things back. The limestone below goes down and down into cold black galleries of its own, older than any vein, older than any claim, and the canister is in one of them now, past the reach of divers, dredges, lawyers, and companies. Unreachable. Unrecoverable. *Unreported.* The word landed in me like an anchor finding bottom.

The fourth man clung to the rail, white-faced, and I looked at him and he looked at me, and I said, "Get off my boat."

He got off my boat. He went over the side into the eddy below the Throat's mouth, where the water is survivable if the river is feeling generous, and swam for the north shore, and the other boat, hull scraped and pride worse, turned downstream and did not follow.

* * *

After, there was quiet. The kind of quiet that comes after a thing is done and cannot be undone, which is not peace but is a cousin of it. I ran the Kestrel slow through the recovering water below the gorge with both hands on the wheel because my hands wanted something to hold, and Tomas sat on the deck plates among the scattered cargo with the hand spike across his knees like a fallen standard.

"You destroyed it," he said finally. Not accusing. Testing the shape of the sentence to see if it fit.

"I released it," I said. "There's a difference. Small one. But it's mine."

"The vein. The people who worked it. Nobody will ever know now."

"Somebody knows." I nodded at him. "Somebody knows at me. That's going to have to be enough, because the river keeps what it keeps, and it just kept everything."

He was quiet a while. The Throat fell behind us, and the water widened, and the morning came up properly at last, gold on the water, obscene in its beauty.

"Vance," he said. "She still has the other thing. Your name. Your father's wreck."

"I know what she has."

"She'll use it."

"She'll try." I found I was smiling, and it surprised me, the way a scar surprises you when you find it. "But she was holding a canister, Tomas. You can't squeeze a man with an empty hand. The permits lapse in eight days with nothing to show for them, and every company on this river is about to learn that the great Halvorsen find is at the bottom of the Swallower. Her leverage died the moment that brass hit the water. What's left is spite, and spite doesn't scale."

"And you?" he said. "What's left of you?"

I thought about it honestly, because he'd earned an honest answer and because the wheel didn't need me for a moment. What was left of Emrys Marchetti, invisible woman, careful woman, woman with no debts and no questions and no past? A boat with a scraped keel. A witness statement waiting to be made. A father's wreck and a drowned crew and fifteen years of running, all of it surfacing now like a snagged hull, inevitable, mine.

"A name," I said. "Finally. All of it. I'm going to go give it to the magistrate, every syllable, and let it weigh what it weighs."

"And after?"

"After," I said, and put the wheel over toward the land office landing, toward the town, toward the truth, "after, I'm going to find out who I am when the hiding's done."

The Kestrel ran on toward town, keel singing where the rock had kissed her, and behind us the Throat kept its silence, and ahead of us—though I didn't know it yet—Vance was already waiting at the landing, and she had not come alone.
