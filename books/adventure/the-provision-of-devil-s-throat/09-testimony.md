## 9. Testimony

The canister went over the rail at Split Rock, and the river took it the way the river takes everything—without ceremony, without receipt. I watched the brass flash once in the white water and then it was gone, swallowed into the Throat's deepest channel where no dredge and no diver and no company man with a bonus riding on his back would ever reach it. The current there runs at forty feet a second through a gap you couldn't drive a coffin through sideways. Whatever the Halvorsen expedition found in those limestone galleries was now a rumor with excellent security.

The rival crew's launch hung off our stern for another ten minutes, engines idling, men on the rails looking at me like I'd thrown their pensions overboard instead of a coffee can full of paper. Maybe I had. Their skipper—a wide man with a surveyor's transit scarred across his knuckles—shouted something about obstruction of commerce. I shouted back that he was welcome to dive for it, and that the Throat paid its divers in exactly one currency.

They withdrew at dusk. Not because of me. Because of arithmetic.

* * *

The arithmetic went like this: Odile Vance's employers held permits contingent on a verified mineral survey filed before expiry. The verification lived in the brass canister. The canister lived at the bottom of Devil's Throat. Nine days later, the permits lapsed, worthless as wet matches, and every claim staked above the gorge reverted to open ground—which is to say, to the villages that had worked that country for longer than any map admitted.

Vance came aboard the Kestrel the morning after the scuttling, and I'll grant her this: she didn't waste breath on threats. She stood on my deck in her city boots, which were ruined by then anyway, and looked at the water going by with an expression I recognized from mirrors—the look of a person recalculating.

"You understand what you've done," she said.

"I've done a great many things," I said. "You'll have to be specific."

"Fourteen months of negotiation. Two hundred thousand in option payments. A concession that would have made everyone holding paper rich." She turned to me. "And you sank it for what? For people who will never know your name?"

"They know my name," I said. "That was rather the point."

She studied me for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her stop pressing. Maybe it was the fact that I wasn't afraid anymore. Fear is leverage only against people who have something left to lose, and I had spent the whole run upriver discovering I'd already lost everything worth taking—fifteen years of careful nothing, a name worn smooth as river stone from all the handling I'd given it to keep anyone from reading it.

"There will be an inquiry," she said finally. "The magistrate at Grayling Bend has jurisdiction over the waterway. You'll be summoned."

"I know."

"You could lie. You're good at it. Fifteen years of practice."

"That's the thing, Miss Vance," I said. "I'm tired of being good at it."

She left the way she'd arrived, by launch, and I watched her go downriver toward the world of letterhead and lapsed paper, and I felt lighter than I had any right to feel standing next to a crime scene.

Because it was one. Let's not prettify it. I destroyed property that didn't belong to me. I did it deliberately, in front of witnesses, and I'd do it again before breakfast. But the law doesn't run on breakfast, it runs on testimony, and when the summons came up from Grayling Bend three days later, I read it twice, folded it, and put it in my breast pocket over my heart like it belonged there.

Tomas Reyes watched me do it from the wheelhouse door.

"You're going to tell them everything," he said. It wasn't a question. His voice had come back to him slowly over those nine days, arriving in pieces like cargo off a damaged barge—first single words, then sentences, then, one night at anchor, the whole terrible inventory of what happened in the caves, delivered flat and complete while I sat very still and let him empty himself out. He hadn't spoken of it since. He didn't need to. It lived in both of us now, the way ballast lives in a hull: invisible, permanent, load-bearing.

"Everything," I said.

"They'll ask why you burned the survey records at the Throat's edge too. The copies. The maps."

"Because paper floats," I said. "Paper washes into eddies and gets found by boys with fishing lines and sold to men with briefcases. Brass sinks. That's the whole science of it."

"And they'll ask who you are."

I put my hand flat on the wheel. The Kestrel's deck hummed under us, the old girl talking her usual talk, wood and rivet and current.

"Yes," I said. "They will."

* * *

Grayling Bend smelled of creosote and catfish, the way county seats always smell, as if justice were cured in barrels. The magistrate's chambers occupied the top floor of the land office—a room with a ceiling fan turning slow enough to count the blades, a window overlooking the confluence, and a desk older than the territory itself.

Magistrate Okonkwo was not what the fixers downriver had promised me he'd be. They'd described a man you could buy. What sat behind the desk was a woman in her sixties with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair and the patient, unimpressed eyes of someone who had heard every version of every story the Verrazano could produce and believed perhaps a third of them.

"Emrys Marchetti," she read from the summons. "Master of the vessel Kestrel. Is that your full legal name?"

Here it was, then. The moment I'd been rowing toward for fifteen years without knowing it.

"No, ma'am," I said.

The fan turned. Somewhere below us, a clerk was arguing with a fisherman about mooring fees.

"My name is Emrys Marchetti-Fairweather," I said. "My father was Bram Fairweather, master of the paddle-wheeler Coriander, which went down at Halloway Shoals eleven years ago this spring with a cargo manifest he never should have signed and nine men who never came home. I was his deckhand. I survived. And for eleven years I have run this river under half my name because the other half was attached to an inquiry, a settlement, and a reputation, and I thought if nobody connected the two halves, I could outrun the wreck."

Okonkwo took her glasses down off her head and put them on. That was all. She gave me the courtesy of her actual attention, and I found—to my considerable surprise—that it didn't burn.

"The Fairweather inquiry," she said. "I remember it. I was junior counsel on the review board." She let that sit. "Your father was cleared of negligence, Captain. Did you know that? The finding was pilot error upstream of him—a sandbar charted wrong after the flood year. The settlement went to the families regardless, because the shipping line settled rather than fight. Your name was never legally implicated. Only socially. Which on this river, I grant you, amounts to the same thing."

I had known the finding. I had read it four times the winter it came down, alone in a rented room in Grayling Bend, and then I had gone back to the water and buried it, because a verdict on paper had never once stopped a dockhand from spitting when the name Fairweather came up. Nine drowned men make their own weather, and it follows you.

"So you see," Okonkwo continued, "the question before me today isn't really about your father. It's about the Halvorsen expedition, the destroyed survey materials, and the permit lapse that cost certain parties a great deal of money. Three of those parties have filed complaints. One of them has filed three, which tells you something about how the loss sits with them." She folded her hands. "Tell me what happened above the Throat. All of it. Take whatever time you need."

So I did.

I told her about the telegram with triple pay and the smell of desperation on it. About Odile Vance's letterhead that didn't match her permits. About the burned landing and the tents slashed from the inside and Tomas Reyes dehydrated and mute with a brass canister welded to his hands. About what the caves kept—not just ore, though the ore was real enough to make honest men greedy, but the galleries beneath it: worked stone, timbered shafts, tools of a pattern no museum in the territory could date, evidence of a people the surveys swore had never lived there. A vein already claimed, generations deep, by hands the companies' maps had erased.

I told her how the crew split—Halvorsen wanting it reported because a find that big couldn't be hidden and shouldn't be; the company men wanting it sealed because a prior claim, even a dead one, even a prehistoric one, meant litigation that would outlive everyone in the room. How the argument ended violently on the third night, and how Tomas alone walked out, and how he'd carried the canister not to save the discovery but to keep it from either side.

And I told her, plainly, with my hands flat on the armrests so she could see they weren't shaking, that I had burned the records at the Throat's edge and dropped the canister into the gorge, and that I understood this constituted destruction of property, and that I would answer for it.

The fan turned. The clerk downstairs won his argument or lost it; the shouting stopped either way.

"Do you regret it?" Okonkwo asked.

I thought about giving her the polished answer, the one with legal shape to it. Instead I gave her the true one.

"For eleven years I carried my father's wreck like a debt I was paying interest on," I said. "Every port, every season, I kept the ledger balanced by keeping quiet. And it nearly killed me, ma'am—not the carrying, but the hiding. Then I spent two weeks hauling other people's secrets up that river, and I learned something at the bottom of the Throat: truth hoarded weighs more than truth spoken. Every hour. Compounding. I don't regret sinking the canister. I regret every year I spent trying to sink myself."

Okonkwo removed her glasses and cleaned them on her sleeve, which I came to understand was her version of a long pause.

"The prior-claim evidence is unrecoverable," she said. "Which means the villages above the gorge hold their ground by default, and the companies hold nothing, and everyone with a lawyer is furious. The destruction of the survey materials is, strictly, an offense." She looked at me over the rims. "But the survey materials documented a fraud—the concealment of a prior claim to secure a concession. Destroying evidence of a crime, when the destruction prevents the crime, occupies a gray stretch of water that this office has navigated before." She set the glasses down. "You'll pay a fine for the property. You'll file a full deposition of the cave findings from memory—you and Mr. Reyes both, separately, cross-checked—for the historical record, so that what was found is at least known, even if it can't be owned. And Captain—" here she almost smiled, and it transformed her face entirely, "—you'll sign that deposition with your full name. Both halves. The record in this territory has been missing a Fairweather long enough."

I signed it that afternoon, sitting at the same desk, with the fan turning and the confluence glittering past the window. Emrys Marchetti-Fairweather, daughter of Bram, master of the Kestrel. The pen scratched. The ink dried. And I swear to you the name weighed less on the page than it ever had in my mouth—lighter than a hull after you've pumped her out, riding high, answering her helm.

Walking out, I passed Vance in the corridor. She had a train ticket in her hand and a look on her face like a woman doing final arithmetic.

"They'll be back," she said. "Companies don't grieve. They wait."

"Then the river will be busy," I said, "and I'll be on it."

* * *

Tomas was waiting at the dock with our gear packed and the Kestrel fueled, and when I came down the stairs he didn't ask how it went. He just looked at my face, and whatever he saw there, he nodded once, the way rivermen nod at a gauge reading that says the water's safe.

"Well?" he said.

"I'm a Fairweather," I told him. "Officially. There's paperwork."

He grinned—first time I'd seen the whole of it since the caves. "Does the boat know yet?"

"She'll figure it out," I said. "Boats always do."

We cast off at midday, running easy water toward home, and I stood at the wheel with the wind coming upriver ahead of the season's turn, carrying the smell of rain on far-off hills. Behind us lay the Throat, keeping its secret at the bottom of its deepest channel. Ahead lay the villages—the mail runs, the medicine runs, the school supplies and seed and salt, the small honest traffic of people the maps had tried to erase and failed.

There was paint in the hold. Good white enamel, and a brush I'd been saving for eleven years without knowing what for.

Tomorrow, I'd give the Kestrel her real name too.
