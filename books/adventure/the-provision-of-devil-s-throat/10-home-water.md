## 10. Home Water

The season turned while we were still above Split Rock. You could feel it in the water first—the Verrazano ran a half-foot higher each morning, cold and quick-tempered, the way it gets when the high country starts letting go of its snow. The low-water season was over. The river had remembered its own strength, and so, I suppose, had I.

We came down through the Throat narrows on a gray morning with the current doing half our work and the Kestrel grumbling about the rest. She'd taken a beating—there was a scar along her port side where the rival crew's boat had kissed her hard enough to leave a memory, and my hands still had the ache of rope burn and worse—but she ran true. Boats do that, if you're honest with them. It's people who lie.

Tomas Reyes sat in the pilothouse with a mug of coffee he hadn't touched, watching the water the way a man watches a former friend across a room. He'd been talking more in the three days since the magistrate's office. Not in floods. In trickles, like a spring coming back after a dry summer. That morning he'd managed four full sentences before breakfast, which by my count was a record.

"You could have kept it," he said now. "The canister. You could have sold it to either side and bought a new boat."

"The Kestrel's not for sale," I said.

"That's not what I asked."

I eased her around a snag that hadn't been there in spring—a whole birch, root ball up, riding the current like a drowned ghost. "No," I said. "It isn't."

He nodded and went back to watching the water. That was the thing about Tomas I'd come to respect: he didn't push. He'd spent enough time in the caves to know that some things come out of the dark on their own schedule, and hauling on them only breaks them.

* * *

We stopped at Ferris Landing that afternoon, the first village above the Throat, and I learned what it means to be known for the wrong reason and the right one at the same time.

Word travels on a river faster than any boat. The magistrate's statement had gone into the record at Split Rock, and from Split Rock it had gone out on the wire, and from the wire it had come back to Ferris Landing dressed in rumor's Sunday clothes. By the time I tied off, there were a dozen people on the dock, and not one of them was looking at me the way they used to—like I was a fixture, a piece of the landscape, a woman who moved cargo and asked nothing and was owed nothing.

"Captain Marchetti," said old Widow Ansel, who had sold me eggs for fifteen years and never once asked my first name. "They say you burned the survey records."

"They say wrong. I let the river have them. There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed, "if you're particular." And then she pressed a basket into my hands—eggs, bread, a jar of preserves—and would not take payment, and stood there looking at me until I understood that this was not a transaction. It was a verdict.

Tomas got a quieter reception. The villages had known the Halvorsen crew—had sold them meat and mules and, in two cases, daughters' cooking and sons' labor. When he climbed down the gangplank, a boy of maybe ten stepped forward and asked, "Did you find gold?"

Tomas crouched down to the boy's level, which I hadn't seen him do before. "We found something older," he said. "Something the river kept safe a long time. And now it's going to stay kept."

"Was that the right thing?"

Tomas looked at the boy for a long moment. "Ask me in twenty years," he said. "But yes. I think it was."

* * *

The mail contract found me before I found it. That's the way of these things—you stop running from your name and the world starts sending you letters.

The river post agent was a narrow, ink-stained man named Corben who came down the dock at Ferris Landing waving a paper like it was on fire. "Marchetti. They tell me you're running upriver regular now. They tell me you're the only captain who'll take the narrows above the Throat in turnover season."

"They tell you right."

"Mail run. Fortnightly. Ferris Landing, Coldbrook, the Ansel settlements, all the way up to the mission at High Water. Medicine runs when the doctor sends word. Pay's not triple. Pay's not even double." He looked almost apologetic. "But it's every month, and it's honest, and nobody's ever shot at a mail boat."

"Nobody's ever shot at a mail boat *yet*," I said, and signed.

Tomas watched me sign it from the deck of the Kestrel. When Corben had gone, he said, "You could run cargo. You could make real money now. Your name's worth something on this river."

"My name's worth something *because* I stopped trying to make it worth something," I said. "I'd like to keep it that way for a while. Mail doesn't come with permits attached."

He smiled—actually smiled, the first I'd seen that reached his eyes—and went below to check the bilge, which had developed a small opinion of itself and needed emptying twice a day.

* * *

We wintered at Coldbrook, or near enough to it. The river froze at the edges first, then in the slack water, then everywhere but the main channel, and the Kestrel sat in the ice like a knife in a sheath. I hired a boy from the village—Widow Ansel's grandnephew, all elbows and opinions—to help with the caulking, and Tomas, who turned out to be handy with tools when his hands had something to do besides shake, rebuilt the pilothouse door that had never hung right in fifteen years.

"You never fixed that?" he asked, planing the wood. "Fifteen years?"

"It hung well enough. A door that sticks keeps people out."

"And now?"

"Now I'd rather it opened," I said, and he had the grace not to look at me while he worked.

The villages fed us through the cold months. Not charity—I paid, mostly, though Widow Ansel's preserves kept appearing in my galley regardless of my objections—but something adjacent to charity, something with the smell of bread and the weight of obligation. They knew what I'd done. Or what I hadn't done, which amounted to the same thing. The cave records would have erased them—would have turned their homes into a concession, their river into a company asset, their names into line items in a litigation nobody upriver could afford. I had put that future over the falls, and they knew it, and they fed me like family, which is a debt I'm still working off and expect to be working off for the rest of my life.

Tomas taught letters to the Coldbrook children in the evenings, in the schoolhouse with the stove that smoked. He'd been a surveyor; he knew numbers and maps, and maps, it turns out, are just stories drawn honestly. He never went back to the Throat itself. I asked once, in the spring, if he wanted to see it again, and he said, "I know what's there. It doesn't need me checking on it." Which was as clean an answer as any of us would get.

* * *

Spring came up the river the way spring always does—slowly, then all at once, then with mud. The ice went out with a sound like artillery, and the Kestrel floated light in her new caulking, and I stood on the dock at Coldbrook with a can of paint and a brush and a decision I'd been carrying since Split Rock.

The hull said KESTREL in letters gone soft and gray, the paint faded by fifteen years of sun and ice and my own deliberate neglect. Below it, where a home port should be, there was nothing. Fifteen years of nothing. A boat without a home port is a boat that belongs nowhere, and I had chosen that, the way I'd chosen no debts and no questions and no past.

I opened the can. The paint was a deep blue, almost black in the shadow of the dock, and it smelled like beginnings, which is a thing paint does whether you're ready for it or not.

Tomas came down with the mail sacks and stopped when he saw the brush. "You sure?"

"No," I said. "But I'm doing it anyway. That's most of what being sure is."

I painted the home port first, because if I did the name first I might lose my nerve before I got to the hard part. The letters went on crooked—the dock moved with the current, and so did my hand—and I let them be crooked. Honest letters. Then I stood there with the brush dripping, looking at the space where the name went, and thought about my father, and the wreck, and the drowned men, and the magistrate's clerk reading my full name into the record in a voice that made it sound like just words, which is all a name is, until you decide what it carries.

I painted it small. That was deliberate. Not hidden—small. A name doesn't have to shout to be true.

MARCHETTI. And under it, in letters half the size: *E. Fairweather Marchetti, Coldbrook.*

Tomas read it upside down from the dock. "Fairweather," he said. "That's the wreck name."

"That's the wreck name."

"You're putting it on your boat."

"I'm putting it on *my* boat," I said. "The wreck was my father's. The name was mine first. I'm just taking back what was always mine."

He didn't say anything to that, which was right. Some things you say to a person and some things you let them read on a hull.

* * *

We made the first mail run of the new season on a morning so clear the mountains looked cut from paper. The Kestrel rode the current down from Coldbrook with her crooked letters drying in the sun, mail sacks stacked and lashed, a crate of the doctor's medicine riding soft in straw, and Tomas at the rail watching the water with a face that had, over the winter, remembered how to be a face.

At Ferris Landing, Widow Ansel was on the dock with her basket and her verdict-face. She looked at the hull a long time.

"Fairweather," she said. "There was a boat by that name. Went down above the Throat, oh—twenty years back. Bad business."

"Bad business," I agreed.

"You related?"

"I'm the relation."

She looked at me then, really looked, the way she'd looked when she handed over the preserves, and whatever she saw seemed to settle something. "Well," she said. "River remembers. Good to know somebody on it finally does too." She handed me the basket. Eggs, bread, preserves. "For the run. You'll want the preserves by Coldbrook—the doctor there has no sense of a proper meal."

I took the basket, and the mail, and the wheel, and took the Kestrel out into the current with the sun coming up the valley and the water running high and cold and alive.

Fifteen years I'd made myself unremarkable, and it had kept me alive, and I wouldn't pretend otherwise. But alive and afloat aren't the same thing, and it had taken a burned camp and a mute surveyor and a brass canister and a woman named Vance and nine days and a fight at Split Rock to teach me the difference.

The river took us down toward the Throat, and above it, and past it, and on. The current did half the work. The Kestrel grumbled about the rest. Her name was on her hull, crooked and small and true, and the water read it as we passed, the way water does, and kept it.

The river remembers. Now, so do I.

And that, I'd come to believe, was the whole of the job—not the cargo, not the mail, not even the name. Just this: to be on the water, carrying what needed carrying, to the people who needed it carried, and to be *known* while I did it.

The Throat opened ahead of us, dark water and white, and I turned her nose toward it, and did not look back.
