## 1. Fuel and Rumors

The dawn came up over the Talkeetna flats the color of old tin, and Zephyrine Marchetti was already awake to meet it, because dawn was not a thing you admired from a bed when you had a medicine run scheduled and a window of weather that would hold for perhaps six hours. She stood in the doorway of her cabin above the airstrip with a tin cup of coffee going cold in her hands and watched the frost smoke lift off the grass in long gray ribbons, and she did what she always did at this hour: she counted.

Forty-one miles to Greyling Junction. Ninety-three more to the mission outpost at St. Elias Fork. Fuel enough for both legs and a margin, if the wind out of the northwest held where it was supposed to hold and didn't do anything creative. Two crates of penicillin and insulin under waxed canvas in the back of the Cub, packed by a pharmacist in Coldbank who wrapped each vial like it was an infant. One passenger manifest: none. One name on her paperwork that was not her own, because it never was — *M. Calloway*, licensed out of a territory so far south the clerk who'd issued it had probably never seen snow.

Seven years of names. She was getting good at wearing them the way other people wore coats, shrugging them on and off without thinking, until some mornings she had to stand very still and remember which one she'd put on that day.

She drained the coffee, rinsed the cup with water from the rain barrel, and went down to the hangar.

* * *

The de Havilland Cub sat on its skis in the blue half-light like something patient and half-wild, frost furring the struts, the fabric skin tight as a drum in the cold. Zephyrine walked around her twice before she touched her — nose to tail, checking the airframe the way a rancher checks a horse, palm flat against the cowling until the metal's chill came up through her glove and told her what she needed to know. Oil level good. Control surfaces free. The propeller blades she wiped down with a rag, though there was nothing on them but cold.

"Morning, girl," she said, because talking to the airplane was the only conversation she'd had in three weeks that hadn't been about fuel prices or freight weights, and she found she preferred even this thin kind of company to none. "Big day. Nothing dramatic."

Nothing dramatic. That was the whole architecture of her life now, load-bearing and invisible: nothing dramatic, nothing memorable, nothing that would make a man in a bar squint at her and say, *Marchetti? Now where have I heard that name.* She flew medicine and mail into strips that didn't appear on charts, took payment in cash and smoked fish and occasionally nothing at all, kept her logs in two sets of books — one honest, one for anyone who might ever ask — and moved on every eighteen months or so, before faces got familiar, before anyone's curiosity could put down roots.

It was a small life. She knew that. It was also, measured precisely, exactly large enough to keep her free, and she had done the arithmetic on that trade often enough that she no longer flinched at the sum.

She hand-propped the engine because the battery was temperamental in cold like this, braced her boots against the strut, and pulled. The prop caught on the third swing with a cough and then a roar that scattered a pair of ravens off the spruce tops, and the sound filled her chest the way it always did — that great mechanical heartbeat, the closest thing to a promise the world had ever made her and kept.

Greyling Junction first. Then north.

* * *

Greyling Junction was not a town so much as an argument between a river and a railroad, and the river was winning. A fuel cache, a trading post whose porch listed starboard, eleven houses, and a gravel strip that flooded every spring and was rebuilt every summer by men who complained about it in the same words every year, as liturgy. Zephyrine set the Cub down light as a moth landing, taxied up to the drums, and killed the engine to let the silence come flooding back in — that enormous interior silence, bigger than any silence in a city could dream of being, broken only by the tick of cooling metal and somewhere far off the complaint of a sandhill crane.

Hollis Vann came out of the trading post wiping his hands on a rag, which was how Hollis did everything: wiping his hands on a rag, whether they were dirty or not, as if perpetually preparing to refuse a handshake.

"Calloway," he said, using her current name with the flat courtesy he extended to all pilots, weather, and acts of God. "You're early."

"Wind's cooperating." She climbed down and began uncoiling the fuel hose. "Got a run up to St. Elias Fork. Insulin. Can't sit on it."

"Insulin." He said it the way he said everything, as though filing it away in a drawer he'd burn later. "Everybody's sick this year. Or everybody's scared of being sick. Same business, near enough."

He dragged a drum over and they worked the pump together, the gasoline gurgling up through the clear hose in amber pulses while the morning warmed by degrees too small to measure but real nonetheless. Zephyrine watched the fuel gauge on the drum and let her mind go into the low idle it went into during refueling — the only truly empty minutes she allowed herself, when there was nothing to fly and nothing to fix and nothing to be vigilant about except static electricity and spilled gas.

That was why she heard it. That was the trouble. If she'd been busy she'd have missed it entirely, and then the day would have stayed the shape it was supposed to be.

Two men had come around the side of the trading post, and they were not from anywhere along the river. She registered them the way she registered everything now, automatically, the way a deer registers movement — a fraction of attention, no more. City boots, wrong weight of coat, one of them carrying himself like a foreman and the other like a clerk pretending to be outdoors. They stopped at the corner of the building, just out of easy earshot, and lit cigarettes against the wind, cupping their hands, and their voices carried anyway across the cold air in fragments, the way voices do over water.

"—third strip this week," the foreman-voiced one was saying. "Nobody's heard of him. Old-timers either clam up or lie."

"He'll surface," the other said. "They always surface. Man can't eat scenery forever."

"Survey team's paying good money for a sighting. Five hundred, Vann said. For a *name*."

Zephyrine's hands did not stop moving. This was the discipline, the whole seven years of it compressed into a single practiced motion: keep pumping, keep your eyes on the gauge, let your face go to the same mild blankness it wore for weather talk. But inside her chest something had gone very quiet and very fast, the way the world goes quiet right before a stall warning.

A survey team. Asking after a pilot. Paying five hundred dollars for a name.

There were only so many names worth five hundred dollars to a survey team, and she had spent seven years making sure hers wasn't one of them. *Marchetti* was a dead word, buried twice over — once by the conviction, once by the fire that had supposedly taken the pilot Marchetti's records in the Coldbank courthouse annex, a fire she had never confirmed starting and never asked about. Nobody looked for a dead woman. That had been the entire design.

Unless somebody had decided to dig.

"You hear that?" Hollis said, low, not looking up from the drum. His rag had gone still in his hands, which from Hollis was the equivalent of shouting.

"Hear what?" she said.

"Mm." He worked the pump handle twice more, watching the gauge, and when he spoke again his voice was pitched for her alone under the gurgle of the gasoline. "They were here yesterday too. Came in on the afternoon freight, asked me did I know any bush pilots working this stretch. I told them pilots are like mosquitoes. Too many to count and all alike." He spat, expertly, into the gravel. "Then this morning they're asking different. Not *any* pilot. A specific one. Name of Marchetti."

The pump handle was smooth and cold under her glove. She made herself finish the drum, made herself close the cap, made herself coil the hose in its proper figure-eight, because the moment she let her hands shake was the moment she'd start believing this was real.

"Marchetti," she repeated, and gave a small, bored shrug that cost her more effort than any aerobatic maneuver she'd ever flown. "Sounds foreign."

"Sounds like trouble is what it sounds like." Hollis straightened, finally looking at her, and his eyes — pale, watery, older than the rest of him — held something she hadn't expected. Not suspicion. Something almost like regret. "I didn't tell them nothing. I'm telling you that plain, so you know. Whatever a man did or didn't do, I don't sell names. Names are the last thing a person owns out here."

"Appreciate it," she said, and meant it, and hated that her voice came out steady, because steadiness felt like lying to a man who'd just told her the truth.

The two men by the corner of the trading post flicked their cigarette butts into the mud and started walking toward the strip, unhurried, and Zephyrine understood with a clarity that arrived like ice water that they were going to walk past her plane, look at her, look at her tail number, and file it. There was nothing dramatic she could do about it. Drama was the one currency she couldn't spend.

So she finished her preflight walk-around at the tail, unhurried, and when the foreman-voiced man drifted close enough and offered a nod that was trying hard to be casual, she nodded back with the exact degree of friendliness a stranger named Calloway owed another stranger, and climbed into the cockpit, and checked her magneto switches, and hollered *"Clear!"* out of pure habit though there was nobody within fifty feet of the prop, and swung the engine to life.

* * *

The Cub lifted off the gravel at Greyling Junction and the town fell away beneath her — the listing porch, the fuel drums, the two city coats standing small and still on the bank, watching her climb. Watching. She felt their attention on her tail the way she'd feel a hand between her shoulder blades.

Northwest then, up the river valley, and she put the plane into the rhythm that was the only prayer she had left: scan, trim, breathe. Instruments. Horizon. Fuel pressure steady, oil temperature climbing sweetly toward green. Below her the Kessler country unrolled in its autumn livery — rust and gold muskeg, black spruce standing in dark congregations, the river braiding itself silver through the flats — and for a few minutes the sight did what it always did, which was to remind her that the world was vast and mostly indifferent and therefore survivable.

But the thought kept circling back and landing, like a bird she couldn't shoo off the strut.

*A specific one. Name of Marchetti.*

Who? A survey team. Survey teams meant mineral claims, meant syndicates, meant money moving through the interior with its lawyers and its enforcers behind it like a wake behind a boat. And there was exactly one piece of ground in all this country where her name and a survey team and a syndicate belonged in the same sentence, and she had spent seven years not thinking about it with the focused, athletic discipline of a woman not thinking about a wound.

Mount Kessler. The sealed camp. The assay office, the ledger, the night everything she was got taken apart and reassembled into a fugitive.

*They always surface,* the clerk had said. *Man can't eat scenery forever.*

She was not a man, and she ate scenery fine, thank you, and she had surfaced exactly nowhere in seven years. But the crack was there now, hairline, running through the practiced ease of her morning like a flaw through windscreen glass. You could fly with a cracked windscreen for years. You could also wake up one morning in rough air and find the whole pane coming apart at thirty-five below.

Over St. Elias Fork she found the mission's little meadow strip, dropped low, waggled her wings twice — the signal the sisters knew — and set down in the grass with the insulin cold and safe in its waxed canvas. Sister Agnes came out across the tussocks in her habit and rubber boots, waving, and Zephyrine handed down the crates and refused the coffee she was offered and the lunch she was pressed to take, and lied cheerfully about having eaten, and was back in the air within twenty minutes.

It was only on the climb-out, banking south over the river with the sun finally clearing the ridgeline and laying gold across the water, that she let herself say it, aloud, to the empty cockpit, to the engine's roar that swallowed everything:

"Somebody's asking."

And the strange thing — the thing she turned over and over on the long leg home, the thing that kept her from feeling afraid exactly, though she knew fear would come — was that underneath the cold prickle of huntedness there was something else, small and unwelcome as a splinter. Because for seven years the name *Marchetti* had existed nowhere but in her own mouth, and she had gotten used to that, the way you get used to a missing finger. And now the name was out in the world again, moving through trading posts and freight manifests, being spoken by strangers for money — and some traitorous part of her, some part she despised on principle, had heard *Marchetti* in a stranger's mouth and felt, for one shameful half-second, less alone.

She throttled back over the divide and let the Cub settle into cruise, and she did the thing she always did when the past got too close: she made a list. Fuel remaining. Wind. Next fuel stop, next name change, next territory line. Concrete items, checkable items, a life measured in miles because miles could be trusted and people and the past could not.

The list was short today. Shorter than usual.

Because at the bottom of it, in handwriting she didn't remember deciding to write, was an item she'd been avoiding for seven years, and the morning had just inked it in: *Tobias Wrenn's cabin, upper Kessler. Overdue for a visit. He owes you a chart.*

She'd been meaning to check on the old prospector for a month. His supply order had come down the river with the last mail boat, scrawled in a shaking hand that hadn't shaken last spring, and she'd told herself the strip was soft this time of year, the loads too heavy, the weather marginal. All true. None of it the reason.

Now, with strangers buying her name for five hundred dollars a mention, the reason had changed shape entirely. Wrenn's cabin sat at the mouth of the Kessler headwaters, closer to the sealed camp than any other roof in the interior, and if anyone in this country remembered the old survey — remembered *her* — it would be a man who'd staked claims out there before the syndicate closed the valley like a fist.

She banked south toward home, toward the hangar and the second set of books and the careful, load-bearing nothing-dramatic of her life, and told herself she was just flying.

Below her, far up the valley, half-hidden in haze, Mount Kessler stood with its glacier shouldering the sky, white and patient, waiting the way mountains wait — as if it had all the time in the world, and knew better than she did that her time was running out.
