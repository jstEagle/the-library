## 2. The Dying Prospector

The smoke told her something was wrong before she saw the cabin.

Zephyrine had been flying the upper Kesselline drainage for seven years, and she knew every plume that belonged there. Woodsmoke rose thin and gray from a dozen winter cabins scattered through the valley like dropped stitches — the hermit at Kessler Bend, the Lindqvist brothers at their trapline, the widow Aune who still cut spruce gum and sold it to tourists in Coldbank for a price that would have embarrassed them both. Those fires breathed. They swayed with the wind and thickened toward evening when someone fed a stove.

This smoke did none of those things. It stood up out of a hollow below the glacier's moraine in a single unbroken column, straight as a plumb line, the color of wet slate, and it did not sway even though the wind at two thousand feet was pushing her tail a steady fifteen knots. Green wood, she thought. Someone burning green wood means they can't go out for dry. Which means either they're new here or they can't walk.

She banked the Cub over the ridge to look, because looking cost her nothing but a minute of fuel, and because seven years of flying alone had taught her that the difference between curiosity and survival was often just timing. The cabin sat in its clearing with its roof half collapsed under last winter's snowload, sagging like a tired shoulder, and beside it — this was what made her thumb move to the transmit switch and then stop — a cache on stilts, a woodshed, a drying rack hung with fish that had gone white and stiff weeks ago. Somebody had lived here a long time. Somebody had stopped living here recently.

There was a strip of gravel bar along the creek wide enough to land if the wind cooperated, and the wind did, and so she landed, which was the kind of decision Zephyrine Marchetti had stopped agonizing over years ago. You saw a thing, you went to it, you dealt with what you found. Hesitation was a luxury for people whose names weren't on a warrant.

* * *

The man on the bunk inside the cabin was mostly beard and bones, and he was awake, and he had a rifle across his chest with his finger nowhere near the trigger, which told her he'd been a serious man once and was now simply too weak to be dangerous.

"Don't come closer," he said. His voice came out of him like air out of a slow leak. "I've got nothing worth taking."

"Everybody says that," Zephyrine said. She stayed by the door, letting her eyes adjust, letting him see her hands. "Everybody's wrong about it too."

He studied her the way old prospectors studied ground — patiently, thoroughly, with an eye trained to notice what didn't belong. Her flight jacket. Her boots. The way she stood balanced, weight even, ready to leave.

"You're the pilot," he said. "Marchetti."

The name went through her like a splinter finding its depth. She kept her face still. Up here, pilots were known by their airplanes and their reputations, not their surnames; nobody in the interior used surnames unless they wanted something. "People call me Zeph," she said. "Who told you mine?"

"A man in Coldbank. Bought me whiskey I didn't want and asked questions I didn't answer." He coughed then, a long tearing sound, and when it passed he lay with his eyes closed and his chest working like a bellows with a cracked seam. "You fly medicine runs up the Kesselline. Tuesdays and Fridays. You don't ask questions at the stops. That's why I waited for you instead of one of the others."

"Waited for me how long?"

"Four days." He opened his eyes. They were pale gray, almost colorless, set deep under brows like snow fences. "Since the rock came down on my leg."

She looked where he nodded. His left leg was pinned beneath a folded blanket, and the blanket was stiff in places it shouldn't have been, and the smell underneath the woodsmoke and the sour fish-rack smell was the sweetish wrongness she'd learned to recognize during one bad winter ferrying a sick trapper's wife to Coldbank too late. Infection. Days of it. Whatever was under that blanket was already past saving, and whatever was in his blood was close behind.

"I've got antibiotics in the plane," she said. "Broad-spectrum. It might—"

"It won't." He said it without drama, the way a man reports weather. "I know what dying looks like. I've watched it enough times in things I shot that I ought to get to know it from the inside." He worked himself up against the headboard by degrees, each inch costing him visibly, until he could see her whole. "What I need is a pilot. Not a nurse."

Zephyrine didn't sit, but she stopped standing quite so ready to leave. "Talk."

"My name is Tobias Wrenn." He watched her face when he said it, and she gave him nothing, because the name meant nothing to her yet. "I prospected the Kessler headwaters for thirty-one years. Before the syndicate sealed the upper river, after, and through all of it. I found what they say isn't there. I found it nine years ago, and I've spent nine years making sure no one else found it first."

"The syndicate doesn't seal rivers," she said. "They file claims."

"They file claims," Wrenn agreed. "And they post patrol boats, and they buy magistrates, and men who find things in the upper Kessler tend to find trouble instead. Ask me how I know." He didn't wait for her to ask. "Ask your own self how you know, Marchetti. Since we're saying names tonight."

The fire ticked in the stove. Outside, somewhere down the valley, a raven said one flat word and quit. Zephyrine became very aware of the door at her back, of the gravel bar behind it, of the Cub sitting on the water with her escape folded up in its wings. Seven years of instinct told her to smile, make a joke about how everyone upriver knew everybody's business, and walk back out into the daylight where the world was simple and measured in miles.

Instead she heard herself say: "How do you know?"

"Because I was camped three miles below the survey site the summer they burned it." His voice had steadied, as if naming the thing had given him back some portion of himself. "August, seven years ago. I saw the floatplane come in low twice. I saw the fire after. And I saw a young woman pilot walk out of that smoke ahead of two syndicate men, and I watched what happened next from a hillside with binoculars, and there wasn't a damn thing I could do about it except remember."

The cabin seemed to tilt slightly, the way a room does when a floorboard gives. Zephyrine put her hand flat against the doorframe without deciding to.

"That woman was framed," Wrenn said. "I've known it seven years. I never said a word, because saying a word would have meant telling the syndicate I existed, and I had a claim to protect. That's the arithmetic of disappearing, girl. Every truth you keep costs somebody something. You just pray the somebody isn't you." He coughed again, shorter this time, angrier. "Well. Now the arithmetic has changed. Now keeping it costs more than telling it."

"Why?" she asked, and her voice came out lower than she intended. "Why tell me now?"

"Because I'm done paying." He pulled the blanket off his leg with a suddenness that shocked her — a motion from a younger man's body, borrowed for one second — and she saw what was underneath and understood everything about the smoke and the four days and the rifle across his chest. He covered it again, gently, like tucking in a child. "A bear got into my cache in June. I went up after it. The slope let go. I crawled home. I've had four days to think, and thinking is the one work I'm still any good at."

He reached under the bunk, moving slowly, and brought out two things. The first was a packet wrapped in oilcloth and tied with waxed twine, soft-cornered from handling, dense as a brick. The second was a roll of heavy paper bound with a leather lace, and when he loosened the lace and spread it across the blanket, she saw it was a map. Hand-drawn, hand-inked, the work of years — the Kessler headwaters rendered in a fine brown line, every bend named, every tributary measured, and along the upper reaches, in a careful architectural hand, annotations in red: elevations, dates, sample numbers, and one mark repeated like a heartbeat — a small circle with a cross in it, drawn maybe forty times, always around the same hidden valley.

"This is thirty-one years," Wrenn said. "Ore bodies the syndicate's surveyors walked past because they were paid to walk past them. Sample assays I ran myself in Coldbank under other men's names. Dates. Depths. Everything they buried when they sealed the river, and the reason they buried it, which is bigger than gold and uglier." He tapped the map once, and his finger trembled, and he let it. "And underneath it all, the ledger page that proves the fraud. Their own numbers, in their own hand, before they cooked them."

"And you want me to take it to the magistrate in Coldbank," Zephyrine said. "Turn it in. Clear—" She stopped herself.

"No," Wrenn said. "I want you to take it to my daughter."

The word landed in the cabin and sat there between them.

"Ruth," he said. "Ruth Wrenn. She runs the ferry crossing at Coldbank. Has run it eleven years, since—" His jaw worked. "Since she was nineteen and I left. She won't want anything from me. She'll throw the packet in the river, likely, or she'll burn it, and I'd deserve the burning. But I made her a promise when she was small enough to believe promises, and I mean to keep the last one late rather than never. There's a letter in the packet. My handwriting. She'll know it. She used to copy it when she was learning her letters, sat right there on my knee in a cabin not so different from this one, and I taught her to draw the letters the way I draw them now, and she'll hate that she still knows my hand, but she'll know it."

Zephyrine looked at the map. The red circles stared up at her, forty small crosses marking one valley, and she knew that valley. She had flown over it a hundred times, had noted the way the syndicate's charts showed it as blank space, had assumed — the way everyone assumed — that blankness meant barrenness. The summer they burned the survey camp had been the summer she learned what blank spaces were for. Blank spaces were where organizations put the things they needed to not exist.

"If I take this to Coldbank," she said carefully, "and Ruth reads it, and anyone learns what's in it — the syndicate will come up this river after whoever carried it. You understand that. You've spent nine years hiding from exactly that."

"I've spent nine years hiding," Wrenn said, "and I can tell you what it costs, since you're already paying the same bill. It costs you every person who might have loved you. It costs you the sound of your own name spoken kindly. It costs you the habit of staying anywhere longer than the season." His gray eyes held hers, and there was no pity in them, only recognition, and that was somehow worse and better than pity. "You fly Tuesdays and Fridays and you don't ask questions at the stops. I've watched you do it for three years, Marchetti. You're the best-disappeared woman on this river, and I am the best-disappeared man, and neither one of us ever asked whether disappearing was the same thing as being free."

The stove ticked. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse moved with the small industry of creatures who had never once considered exile.

"What happens to the map," Zephyrine said, "if I say no?"

"Then I burn it, and the letter, and myself, in that order, and the syndicate keeps its blank space, and you keep your Tuesdays and Fridays." He said it evenly, without threat, and that was what convinced her — a man bargaining with his own death as calmly as if it were a trade goods scale. "I'm not asking you to be brave. I watched you be brave seven years ago and I saw what it bought you. I'm asking you to carry mail. One delivery. To a ferry landing where you already go."

"It's not the carrying I'm weighing," Zephyrine said. "It's what's in the packet. If there's proof in there — real proof — then it's not mail. It's a fuse."

"Yes," Wrenn said, and smiled, and the smile transformed his ruined face into something she could suddenly believe had charmed a wife and raised a daughter once upon a time. "That's rather the point of mail, isn't it? It connects things. Things that were safer apart."

* * *

She went outside to think, because the smell in the cabin had begun to press on her, and because she did her best thinking with wind on her face and her hands busy. She checked the Cub's tie-downs she didn't need to check. She stood on the gravel bar and watched the column of gray smoke stand up against the peaks and thought about the arithmetic of disappearing, and how precisely Wrenn had done the sums, and how long she had avoided doing them herself.

Seven years ago she had been twenty-four years old and certain that the truth would save her — that if she just survived, just endured, just kept flying, someday the facts would surface on their own like drowned things and the world would apologize. Seven years had taught her that facts don't surface. Facts stay where they're sunk unless somebody dives. And divers drown, and the world does not apologize, and the smart play — the play she had made, the play that had kept her alive and free and utterly, immaculately alone — was to let the bottom keep what it held.

But Wrenn had seen. All these years, while she flew her clean little circuits and told herself no one remembered, an old man on a hillside with binoculars had remembered for her. Her exile had been witnessed. Her innocence had been kept, like a lamp in a window, by a stranger who owed her nothing.

She went back inside.

Wrenn had propped himself higher and laid the map flat on the blanket, and beside it the oilcloth packet, and he was looking at them the way a man looks at a life. He didn't look up when she came in.

"Here's my offer," Zephyrine said. "I'll take the packet to Ruth Wrenn at the Coldbank ferry. I'll put it in her hands myself and tell her it came from you, and I won't open it, and I won't read the letter. That part is yours and hers."

"And the map?"

"The map stays with me." She crouched by the bunk so they were level, eye to eye. "Because if Ruth throws it in the river like you say she might, then everything you bled for goes with it, and I didn't crawl out of one fire seven years ago to watch another one eat the evidence. I'll keep it safe until she decides. If she wants it burned, I'll burn it in front of her. If she wants it delivered to a magistrate, I'll deliver it. But I decide nothing about it until she decides, and neither do you, because in four days you'll be past deciding anything."

Wrenn was quiet a long moment. The light through the one window had gone amber; evening was coming down the valley the way it did in the high country, all at once, like a curtain.

"That's a harder bargain than I offered," he said. "You're taking on the risk and giving me less control."

"I'm taking on the risk and giving *her* control. She's the one who gets to choose what her father's name means. Not you. Not me." Zephyrine held out her hand. "Do we have a trade?"

He took it. His grip was astonishingly strong for a dying man, dry and hard as root wood, and he held on a beat longer than a handshake requires.

"There's one more thing," he said. "In the packet. A page I wrote for whoever carried it — instructions, in case Ruth refuses. Because she will refuse, at first. She's her father's daughter, God help her, and we are both of us stubborn as granite and twice as cold." His voice roughened. "Read that page, Marchetti. Only that page. Promise me."

"I promise."

"And one thing more." He lay back against the headboard, spent by the handshake, by the day, by the nine years, and his eyes had begun to shine with the particular brightness she had seen once before, in the trapper's wife, in the last hour. "When you fly out of here tomorrow — stay the night. There's coffee in the tin by the stove and a cot that's cleaner than it looks. Stay the night and talk to a dying man about ordinary things. Tell me about your airplane. Tell me anything that isn't a secret. I haven't had an unguarded conversation in nine years, and I find I miss it more than I miss my leg."

Something in Zephyrine's chest, something armored and load-bearing, shifted a quarter inch.

"All right," she said. "One night. But I'm warning you, I mostly talk about carburetors."

"Heaven," Tobias Wrenn whispered, and closed his eyes, and smiled.

* * *

She flew out at first light, into a morning so clear the peaks stood sharp as cut paper, with the oilcloth packet riding in her jacket's inside pocket, over her heart, where she could feel its weight at every turn of the stick. Wrenn had been alive when she left — had insisted on walking, hobbling, to the edge of the gravel bar to watch her go, upright on his one good leg with his jaw set like a man seeing off a ship. She did not look back at the cabin. She looked forward, downriver, toward Coldbank, and the map rolled in the tube behind her seat, and the red circles rode in her mind like a pulse.

Forty marks. One valley. One daughter who hated her father's name.

By the time the Coldbank water tower rose out of the morning haze, Zephyrine had rehearsed a dozen openings and trusted none of them. She brought the Cub down onto the river road flats beyond the ferry landing, where the wake of the morning crossing was still spreading in long silver lines, and she saw her at once — a broad, dark-haired woman coiling rope on the dock with the economy of someone who had never once wasted a motion, calling orders to a deckhand in a voice that carried across the water like a thrown anchor.

Ruth Wrenn looked up at the sound of the engine, and even at two hundred yards, even across the whole width of the river, her expression was already closed.

Zephyrine took the packet out of her jacket and held it, and found her hands were not entirely steady, and started down the bank toward the woman who was about to learn her father was dying — and that he had sent his last message to a stranger.
