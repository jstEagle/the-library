## 3. Coldbank

The Kestrel came down the lake approach with the wind quartering off the water, and Zephyrine let her settle onto the floats the way she did everything else — precisely, without flourish, without witnesses. Coldbank spread along the shoreline below her: forty buildings, a government dock, a church with a leaning steeple, and the ferry slip where the *Maggie B* sat tied up like a dog that had given up on being called. Smoke rose from a dozen chimneys, straight and thin in the still morning air. Somewhere beyond the tree line, a dog was barking at nothing.

She had been flying into Coldbank twice a month for three years. Medicine for the nursing station, mail, the occasional crate of ammunition or yeast or school supplies. She knew which dock boards were rotten. She knew that Mrs. Aldous at the trading post would try to talk her into staying for lunch, and that she would refuse, and that Mrs. Aldous would press a wrapped sandwich into her hands anyway, because in places like Coldbank refusal was just another word for acceptance delayed.

What she had never done, in all those landings, was stay longer than it took to unload.

Zephyrine taxied to the mooring buoy, killed the engine, and sat for a moment in the sudden silence, listening to the hull tick as it cooled. The lake lay flat as poured tin. On the far shore the mountains went up white and blue into a sky so clean it looked rinsed. It was, by any honest measure, a beautiful morning, and she distrusted it the way she distrusted all things that asked nothing of her.

Seven years of practice had made avoidance into a craft. She could feel the shape of a town's attention before she ever touched the ground — could read it in how long the dockhand met her eyes, whether the nurse at the station asked about her route or only signed the manifest. Most places, she passed through like weather. Coldbank was small enough that weather got remembered.

She gathered the mail sack and the medical crates and stepped out onto the float, and the cold of the water came up through her boot soles like a reminder.

* * *

Ruth Wrenn heard the plane before she saw it — you always did, in Coldbank; sound carried across the water like gossip — and she marked it without looking up from the winch cable she was re-greasing. Floatplane, mail run, nothing to do with her. The *Maggie B* didn't sail until the afternoon tide brought the log boom down from the upper camps, and until then there were cables to dress and a bilge pump that complained and a hundred small betrayals of machinery to forgive.

She worked the way her mother had taught her: alone, thoroughly, and without expecting thanks. Her mother had been dead eleven years now, and Ruth still caught herself doing things because a dead woman would have done them that way. It was its own kind of company.

The pilot came up the dock path around ten, mail sack over one shoulder, and Ruth knew her by reputation the way everyone within two hundred miles knew Zephyrine Marchetti. The bush pilot who kept to herself. The one who'd landed a Cessna on a gravel bar in '39 with a busted strut and walked out carrying a trapper's broken son on her back. People told that story like it was generous. Ruth figured the woman had probably just wanted to be left alone afterward, and the story had made that impossible, and that was why she never stopped anywhere now.

Ruth understood wanting to be left alone. She'd built a whole life inside that want, and it fit her like a well-worn glove fits a hand — snug, familiar, and no use at all for holding anything.

So when the pilot came down the ferry ramp at noon, packet under her arm, and said, "You're Ruth Wrenn," it wasn't the words that put Ruth's back up. It was the name itself, spoken aloud in daylight, in front of Halvorsen's boy who was mending nets twenty feet away and had ears like a bat.

"Who's asking."

"Zephyrine Marchetti." The pilot set the packet on the winch housing, deliberate as setting down something fragile. "I flew up to your father's cabin last week. Tobias Wrenn."

The world did not go quiet. That was the thing Ruth would remember later — the world went on exactly as it had been. The gulls kept working the tide line. Halvorsen's boy kept mending his nets. The winch cable hung there, greasy and patient. Only Ruth changed, the way a house changes when someone dies in it: nothing visible, everything different.

"He's dead, then." Her voice came out flat as slate. Good. Let it be flat. Flat was load-bearing.

"Injured. He didn't make it through the night after I found him." Marchetti's eyes were steady and dark and gave away nothing, which Ruth recognized as a professional courtesy between people who made their living keeping their faces shut. "He asked me to bring you this."

"I know what he asked you to bring me. I'm asking what's in it."

"I didn't open it."

"Course you didn't." Ruth looked at the packet — brown paper, twine, her father's blocky handwriting on the front. *FOR RUTH WRENN, COLDBANK.* He'd written her name like an address, like she was a place instead of a person. Thirty-one years old and he'd still never learned the difference. "How much is the delivery fee?"

"There isn't one."

"Everything has one." She picked up the packet anyway — because refusing to touch it would be a performance, and Ruth Wrenn did not perform — and weighed it in her hand. Light. A few pages, maybe. A letter thirty years late. "You want to know what this costs? It costs me every time. Every time somebody comes down that ramp with his name in their mouth, I pay for it. The last one was four years ago. Trapper, said he'd seen him up the Kessler, alive as you please, asked did I want a message sent. I said send him my address so he can see what he left. Man never came back either."

Marchetti said nothing. That was the thing about her, Ruth was beginning to see. She didn't fill silences with comfort. She let them stand there like weather.

"I don't want it," Ruth said, and set the packet back down on the winch housing harder than she needed to. "Take it back. Take it wherever he is, if he's anywhere, and tell him—" And there her voice did crack, one hairline fracture across the slate, and she hated it, hated him, hated the packet and the gulls and the clean stupid morning. "Tell him whatever he wrote can wait another thirty years. I waited this long. I can wait forever. It's the one thing he taught me."

She turned back to the winch. Her hands found the rag and the grease gun and resumed their work, steady, mechanical, a body that knew its business even when the rest of her was somewhere thirty years ago, standing on this same dock, watching a canoe get smaller and smaller until the lake took it.

After a while she became aware that the pilot hadn't left.

"You can stand there all day," Ruth said without turning. "I've got work."

"I can see that." A pause. "Your father talked about you. At the end."

"Men always do. At the end it's cheap."

"He said you ran the Kessler gorge better than any man on the river."

Ruth's hands stopped. Just for a second. Then they started again. "He say anything else?"

"That you got it from him."

"No." Now she did turn, and let the pilot have the full weight of her looking, the look that had backed down claim-jumpers and customs men and one memorable fisheries officer. "I got it from me. Whatever he gave me, he gave me by leaving. That's the inheritance. That's the whole estate."

Marchetti nodded slowly, like a woman filing something away. Then she picked up the packet, tucked it under her arm, and said, "I'll hold onto it, then. In case."

"In case of what?"

"In case you change your mind." She was already walking up the ramp. "People do."

"Not this one," Ruth said to her back, but the pilot didn't answer, and the gulls went on screaming their idiot joy, and Ruth stood there with grease on her hands and her heart going like a bilge pump with sand in it, hating how light her hand felt now that the packet was gone, and hating herself for noticing.

* * *

Zephyrine spent the afternoon at the trading post waiting on a fuel drum that the barge had short-shipped, and it was there, over Mrs. Aldous's counter, amid the kerosene lamps and the flypaper and the smell of canvas and molasses, that the past stopped being somewhere else.

It happened small. That was the trick of it. Seven years she had kept the survey camp sealed in the back room of her mind, behind a door she'd painted over so many times she almost believed there was no door. And then Mrs. Aldous, making change, said conversationally, "You hear they've got the upper Kessler posted again? Syndicate men came through on the steamer Tuesday. Asking after a pilot, too. Marchetti, the fellow said. You know anyone by that name?"

And Zephyrine, whose whole life was a study in the casual lie, heard herself say, "Common enough name," and watched Mrs. Aldous accept it, and felt the hairline crack run through her practiced ease like frost through a windowpane.

A pilot named Marchetti. Not a warrant — those had burned out of the system years ago, she'd paid a man in Whitehorse to confirm it. Not a description circulated among the Mounties. Just questions. Someone asking. Which meant someone wanted something, and in her experience people who wanted something quietly were more dangerous than people who wanted it loudly.

She carried her tea to the window seat and looked out at the street, at the mud and the boardwalks and the mountains standing witness beyond the rooftops, and she thought about the packet sitting in the Kestrel's baggage compartment, and about the map folded inside it.

She hadn't opened it. She'd told Ruth Wrenn the truth about that. But Wrenn himself, dying, had unfolded it across his own chest to show her what she was carrying — had traced the river with a cracked fingernail, up past the falls, past the narrows, to a place he called the head of provision, and she had looked down at the markings because it would have been cruel not to, a dying man showing you his life's work, and she had seen—

She had seen the survey camp.

Not marked as such. Marked as a bend in the river, a triangle of contour lines, a notation in Wrenn's cramped hand: *old camp, 1931, good water.* But she had known it the way you know your own signature. The horseshoe of the ridge. The creek coming in from the northeast at that particular angle. The flat bench above the river where six tents had stood and where a man named Ellery Roach had died of a blow to the head that Zephyrine Marchetti had not given him, though seven years and a thousand miles of running said otherwise.

Her tea had gone cold in her hands. She drank it anyway.

*Old camp, 1931, good water.*

Wrenn had camped there. Prospected around it, maybe. Walked the same ground where her life had ended, and drawn it onto his map like any other feature of the country, and then — because dying men are honest in ways the living can't afford — he had written something else, small, in the margin near the triangle. She'd only glimpsed it. Three words, maybe four. She hadn't been able to read them from across the cabin floor, and she had told herself she didn't want to.

That was a lie, and she had known it was a lie, and she had packed the map away and flown south with it riding behind her shoulder blades the whole way like a passenger.

Now she sat in Mrs. Aldous's window with the syndicate's questions hanging in the air and the packet fifty yards away in her plane, and she understood that the geography of her life had just rearranged itself. For seven years the worst place in the world had been *somewhere else* — upriver, backcountry, a coordinate she flew around the way you fly around a storm cell. Now it was here. It was on a piece of paper in her own aircraft. It was a day's flight and a hard portage away, and it had a marginal note in a dead man's hand that might say anything, and she was going to have to read it.

Mrs. Aldous came over with the fuel manifest and a second cup of tea unasked. "You've gone pale as a gutted fish," she said. "Sit a minute. Barge won't be in till tomorrow anyhow."

Zephyrine sat. Outside, the light was going long and gold across the lake, and down at the ferry slip the *Maggie B* loaded her evening freight, and a broad-shouldered woman in oilskins worked the deck winch with the economy of someone who had never once been helped and didn't miss it.

"The Wrenn girl," Zephyrine said. "Ruth. Does she have family here? Anyone?"

"Apart from the whole town knowing her business?" Mrs. Aldous snorted. "No. Mother died in '36. Father—" She waved a hand toward the mountains, a gesture that contained thirty years of abandonment in about six inches of air. "There's nobody. Why?"

"No reason."

But there was a reason, and it sat in her chest with a weight she couldn't name yet. Two women on this cold shore, each carrying a version of the same wound — one abandoned, one exiled, both pretending the carrying cost nothing. She finished her tea, thanked Mrs. Aldous, and walked down to the dock in the blue hour, her boots ringing on the planks.

At the Kestrel she untied the baggage compartment and took out the packet. She didn't open it. She held it instead, feeling the map's stiff fold through the brown paper, feeling the three unread words in the margin like a splinter working toward the surface.

Then she carried it aboard, stowed it under the pilot's seat where she could reach it, and sat in the darkening cabin listening to the water lap the floats, and made herself say it plainly, the way she made herself do everything:

*The past isn't somewhere else anymore. It's upstream. And I'm going to have to fly toward it.*

Down at the ferry slip, a lamp came on in the wheelhouse of the *Maggie B*, and a silhouette moved behind the glass, and Zephyrine watched it for a long moment before she banked the cabin heater and settled in to sleep with her hand resting, all night, on the packet beneath the seat.

In the morning she would need fuel, a current chart of the upper river, and a plan. What she got instead, when she walked up to the trading post at first light, was Mrs. Aldous saying, "That Wrenn girl was asking after you. Wanted to know when you'd be flying out." A pause, delicate as flypaper. "Said she'd come by the dock."

Zephyrine stood very still, the morning cold on her face, the packet's weight suddenly very real against her hip where her mind insisted on feeling it.

The past wasn't somewhere else. And now, apparently, it was coming down the ramp to find her.
