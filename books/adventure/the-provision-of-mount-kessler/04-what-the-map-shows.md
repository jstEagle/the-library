## 4. What the Map Shows

The hangar at Three Mile Strip smelled of avgas and cold iron and, faintly, of the spruce boughs Zephyrine had stuffed under the workbench to keep the mice honest. She had not turned on the overhead lights. The single work lamp threw a cone of yellow across the bench, and inside that cone lay Tobias Wrenn's map, weighted flat at the corners with four socket wrenches, like a specimen pinned for dissection.

She had been standing over it for two hours.

Outside, the night did what interior nights did: it pressed close against the tin walls and made the hangar feel like the last lit room in the world. Wind moved through the black timber in long slow breaths. Somewhere down the strip, a fox screamed once and thought better of it. Zephyrine had lived inside that kind of silence for seven years, and she had made her peace with it the way you make peace with a bad knee — not because it stopped hurting, but because you learned which motions spared it.

She pulled her reading glasses from the breast pocket of her flight jacket, blew the dust off them, and put them on. Then she bent closer to the map, close enough that her breath fogged the old paper.

Wrenn had drawn the Kessler headwaters from memory, and from memory the way a dying man draws them — every line carrying weight, every mark made because it mattered and not because it filled space. The river came down off the glacier in a braid of blue pencil, split at a junction he'd labeled *the Widow's Teeth* in a cramped, slanted hand, and ran through a gorge he'd cross-hatched so heavily the paper had nearly worn through. Above the gorge, the valley opened into a basin, and in the basin, drawn in a different ink — newer, darker — was a small square with a flag.

He had written one word beside it: *CAMP.*

Zephyrine's hand, resting on the bench, had gone very still.

She knew that square. She had slept inside it. She had woken inside it, seven years ago, to the sound of boots on gravel and the particular silence of men who had already decided how a story was going to end. The survey camp at the source of the Kessler. The place where a core sample crate had gone missing and a ledger had gone missing and a young field pilot named Marchetti had, by some miracle of administrative efficiency, been convicted of stealing both before the snow even melted off the airstrip.

She took off the glasses. She put them back on. The square did not go away.

*All right,* she told herself. *All right, Zeph. Look at it like a map. Just a map.*

She made herself work outward from the edges, the way Dez — no, not Dez, not yet, that was tomorrow's problem — the way any bush pilot read unfamiliar country. The river's braid was annotated with depths in fathoms and half-fathoms, dated in a running sequence that began in June and ended — she checked twice — eleven days ago. Wrenn had been up that river this summer. Wrenn had been up that river this summer at seventy-one years old, alone, in a boat she was willing to bet had been held together with wire and prayer.

The dates told her more than the depths. They told her the ice. The calving on the Kessler glacier was a clock the whole interior lived by: sometime in the first hard cold of autumn, the serac wall above the source let go, and when it let go, it dropped enough ice into the valley to dam the river for a week and bury the lower basin under a moving hill of rubble. Nobody ran the river above the Widow's Teeth after the equinox. Nobody with any sense, anyway. The window was closing, and Wrenn's dates let her count how fast.

Three weeks. Maybe a little less, if the cold came down out of the north early the way it had two seasons back.

She sat down on the stool and pulled the map into the lamplight and began, mark by mark, to read the rest.

* * *

The notations came in two hands, and once she saw that, she could not unsee it.

The river work — depths, bars, portage lines, the little skull he'd drawn at the head of the gorge with the word *unrunnable* beneath it, underlined twice, then underlined a third time in what looked like anger — all of that was the same cramped slant. But scattered through the margins, in a smaller, neater script, were numbers. Sequences of numbers. Percentages, she realized. Assays.

*Au 14.2 g/t. Au 31.6 g/t. Au 88.9 g/t.*

She read them the way she read weather: not understanding every mechanism, but understanding what they meant. Gold, in grams per tonne, and the numbers climbing as the dates moved up the valley toward the source. She had flown enough geologists and enough ore crates in seven years of honest work to know what an assay sheet looked like, and these were not the numbers of a marginal claim. These were the numbers of a mother lode.

And beside the richest of them, dated in late July, Wrenn had written a single line in the cramped hand, pressed so hard into the paper the nib had nearly torn through:

*They are not sampling the vein. They are sampling the tailings. God knows what the vein runs.*

Zephyrine sat back on the stool. The lamp buzzed. Her heart was doing something she did not authorize it to do.

Because here was the thing she had spent seven years not thinking about, the thing she had buried so deep and so carefully that she'd almost — *almost* — convinced herself it was just the shape of her bad luck, the way a stump is just the shape of a tree. The Kessler syndicate's survey, the one that had put her name on a theft she hadn't committed, had reported the source as *moderately mineralized, commercially marginal.* She remembered the phrase because she had heard it read aloud in a courtroom that smelled of woodstove smoke and wet wool, and she had thought, even then, even as the gavel came down: *marginal. They flew me up there for two seasons and the rock was marginal, and somebody stole a ledger over marginal rock?*

Nobody steals a ledger over marginal rock. Nobody frames a pilot over marginal rock. You only bury a survey when the truth under it is worth more than everything standing on top.

Wrenn had found the truth. Wrenn had found it and carried it down the river and been dying of a ruptured gut in a cabin on the Talisker when she landed, and he had pressed this map into her hands with fingers like frozen rope and made her swear.

*My girl in Coldbank. You give her the packet. You give it to her with your own hands.*

She had sworn. The packet was sitting in her flight bag by the door, and tomorrow she would fly it to Coldbank and keep the swear. But the map — he'd said the map was hers. *Map's yours, pilot. Consider it payment.* And she was beginning to understand that Tobias Wrenn had known exactly what he was paying her with.

Because there, folded into the map's bottom edge, creased so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth, was a single page torn from a ledger.

She had not looked at it at the cabin. There hadn't been time; there had been blood to mop and a fire to build and a man dying by inches on a bunk that smelled of pine pitch, and he had been talking the whole time, low and fast, spending his last hours like a spendthrift. She had folded the page away with the map and told herself she'd look when she had light and quiet, and she had meant it the way you mean to look at a wound.

She looked now.

The page was a survey ledger, the official kind, the syndicate's own stationery — *KESSLER CONSOLIDATED MINING AND DEVELOPMENT, FIELD SERIES* printed across the head in that smug serif font corporations used when they wanted to look like bedrock. It was a core sample log. Date, hole number, depth, recovery, assay result, and the initials of the logging geologist. The dates ran through the same summer as Wrenn's margin notes. The hole numbers matched the locations on the map.

And the assay results were wrong.

Not wrong the way a mistake is wrong. Wrong the way a forgery is wrong. Wrenn had written the true figures in the margins in his neat small script, and beside each one the ledger's printed figure sat like a stranger at a funeral: *Au 2.1 g/t* where the truth was *31.6*. *Au 0.8* where the truth was *88.9*. Hole after hole, sample after sample, the syndicate's own geologist — and she recognized the initials, *R.D.,* and her stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking water — had logged the source as barren while the rock in the crates sang.

At the bottom of the page, in the cramped hand, Wrenn had written a summary that was less a note than a verdict:

*They didn't falsify the survey to cheat investors. Any fool can cheat investors. They falsified it to hide the vein from the government assessment, so the claim would lapse and they could file it private. And when the field pilot saw the real crates come down the river — when she asked why the numbers didn't match — they needed a thief, and fast. She was alone. Alone is easiest.*

Zephyrine read it three times. The third time, her eyes stopped at the bottom of the page, where the paper was torn, and where, just above the tear, in ink that had faded to brown, was a signature she knew.

Not R.D. The other one. The survey director's signature, authorizing the log. *A. Voss.*

The name went through her like a nail through a board. Voss. The man who had testified at her trial with his hat in his hands and his voice full of regret, the man who had said, *I'm afraid Miss Marchetti was in a position to access the sample crates, and I'm afraid the discrepancies began after her flights,* and who had looked at her, once, just once, across the courtroom, with an expression she had spent seven years telling herself was pity.

It hadn't been pity. Sitting here in the lamplight with the page flat under her hands, she finally had a word for what it had been. It had been *inventory.* The way you look at a thing you have already spent.

* * *

She got up because sitting still had become impossible, and walked the length of the hangar and back, her boots ringing on the boards, her breath smoking in the cold air. The Cessna 180 sat in the dark at the far end, a pale shape like a moored animal, and she put her hand on its cowling the way you'd touch a horse's neck, for the contact, for something warm-blooded in the room.

Seven years. Seven years of fuel chits under false names, of landing on strips where nobody asked questions, of sleeping in the plane rather than accept a bed from someone who might remember her face. Seven years of being a woman who had learned to leave before the leaving cost anything. She had gotten good at it. She had gotten so good at it that avoidance had stopped feeling like fear and started feeling like competence, and that was the most dangerous trick she had ever played on herself.

And now a dead man's map lay under four socket wrenches, and on it was a square with a flag, and in the margins of a forged ledger was the arithmetic of her whole ruined life, and the window was three weeks wide and closing.

She thought about what it would cost to go. Not the money — she had eleven hundred dollars under a floorboard and a plane worth more than that in parts, and neither figure was really the point. The cost was the other thing. Going upstream meant going back. Back to the camp, back to the valley, back to the country where her name meant *thief* to every magistrate and mine boss from Coldbank to the coast. And if she went and found nothing — if Wrenn's page was the fever dream of a dying prospector, if the ledger page proved nothing a courtroom would hear — then she wouldn't be a woman hiding from a conviction anymore. She'd be a woman who had gone back for it. There was a difference, and the difference was a cell.

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake, and made herself name what the shaking was. Not the cold. She'd been cold before. It was the fear, finally out of its box after seven years, standing in the hangar with her, and it was saying all the old things in the old voice: *They buried you once. They will bury you deeper. Stay small. Stay moving. Stay nothing.*

She let it talk. She had learned that much — that fear was like weather, and you didn't argue with weather, you read it, and then you flew anyway or you didn't, but you decided with your eyes open.

And then she thought about Tobias Wrenn on his bunk, and the way he had gripped her wrist with a strength that surprised them both, and said: *They took seven years off you, girl. I watched them do it. I was too much of a coward to speak then. This map is me speaking.*

He had watched. He had been up that river, or near it, seven years ago, and he had known, and he had said nothing, and it had eaten him alive — she could see that now, could see that the map was not payment at all. It was penance. He had spent his last summer crawling back up the Kessler to dig out the truth he'd let stay buried, and he had run out of time, and she was what he had run out of time *into*.

She thought about Ruth Wrenn in Coldbank, who would take the packet tomorrow and slam a door, and who was, as far as Zephyrine knew, the only other person alive carrying this old man's blood.

And she thought — this was the thought that turned it, the way a key turns, small and mechanical and final — she thought: *If I don't go, then the lie wins by default. Not by strength. By default.* The syndicate had never needed to be right. They had only needed everyone else to be tired. Seven years of her tiredness had been their whole foundation.

The lamp buzzed. The wind breathed in the timber. The Cessna stood patient under her hand.

Zephyrine went back to the bench and sat down and took off the glasses and looked at the map without them, letting it blur into a wash of blue and black and that one small inked square, and she felt the fear change in her chest the way weather changes — not gone, never gone, but swinging around, the way a headwind swings when you cross a ridge, so that the same force that had been holding you back was suddenly, if you were brave enough and fool enough, holding you up.

Three weeks.

She got a pencil and a fresh sheet from the flight-bag and began to copy the map — river, gorge, basin, camp — and beside the gorge she wrote the word *Ruth,* because there was no running the Kessler gorge alone and no one alive knew that water better than a Wrenn ferrywoman, and beside that she wrote *Dez Calloway,* because if the ledger page was going to mean anything it would need a geologist who could swear to it, and Dez owed the world a swearing-to, and she knew where he drank.

She wrote the two names and looked at them and felt something she had not felt in seven years, something that was not quite comfort and not quite hope but had the shape of both: the beginning of a *we* where there had only ever been an *I*.

Then she folded the copy into her jacket, turned down the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark with the fox-wind and the black timber and the smell of avgas, and said aloud, to the hangar, to the dead man, to herself:

"Three weeks. All right, Tobias. I'm going up the river."

Outside, far to the north, the sky over the Kessler country flickered once with the green breath of the aurora, like something answering.
