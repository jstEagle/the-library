## 5. The River Runner

The fog came off the lake at dawn the way it always did in Coldbank, rolling up the shingle like something alive and hungry, and Ruth Wrenn stood on the deck of her ferry with a mug of coffee going cold in her hands and watched it eat the far shore.

She had been up since four. The *Marten* needed her bilge pump packing replaced before the morning run to the logging camp on the east arm, and there was no one else who would do it, because there was no one else, because that was the whole shape of her life: one boat, one town that tolerated her, and forty years of mornings that began with grease under her fingernails and ended with whiskey in her tea.

She was fifty-one years old and she had made peace with the shape of it. Mostly. On most days. She told herself that while she knelt in the engine bay with a wrench, telling herself was a kind of work too, and maybe the hardest kind she did.

The bell above the dock office door rang at half past seven, which was wrong, because Ruth didn't open the office until eight, and the people of Coldbank knew it. She wiped her hands on a rag that had stopped being white sometime during the previous administration and went to see who was fool enough to ignore that particular fact.

The woman standing on her dock was not from Coldbank. That was obvious before Ruth got the door half open — the flight jacket, worn soft as old paper at the elbows; the boots that had walked gravel strips in three territories; the way she stood with her weight back on her heels, like a person perpetually ready to be somewhere else in four seconds flat.

"You're Wrenn," the woman said. Not a question.

"Office opens at eight."

"I know. I waited until eight." She glanced at her watch. "It's eight-oh-two."

Ruth looked at her properly then. Brown hair going grey at the temples, cut short and practical. Eyes the grey-green of glacier water, and around them the fine map of lines that comes from squinting into sun off wings for years. Somewhere north of forty. Tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"You're the pilot," Ruth said. "The one asking after my father's cabin."

"Zephyrine Marchetti."

The name landed on the dock between them like a dropped tool. Ruth had heard it twice now — once from the trader at Fort Alder who passed along everything worth passing along, once from her own mouth at two in the morning when she'd been talking to the empty kitchen about the stranger who'd come asking questions. A bush pilot. A woman with a reputation that traveled faster than her plane and arrived looking worse for wear.

"My father," Ruth said carefully, "is dead."

"I know. I was there." Marchetti didn't flinch from it. Ruth gave her credit for that; most people found a way to look at the coffee urn or their own boots when they said things like that. This woman looked straight at her, and what was in her face wasn't pity. It was something more like recognition, and Ruth liked it considerably less.

"He died in his cabin," Marchetti went on. "A fall. He'd been hurt some days before I got there — bad leg, infection setting in. There wasn't anything to be done by then. I did what I could to make him comfortable. He lasted about six hours."

"You buried him?"

"There wasn't ground to dig in. I built a cairn above the high-water line and marked it. I can give you coordinates."

Ruth heard herself say, "He would have hated a cairn," and was surprised by the crack in her own voice. She turned away, busied herself with the coffee urn, poured two cups without deciding to. It was easier to move than to stand still in front of this woman and feel whatever was trying to climb up her throat.

"He talked about you," Marchetti said behind her. "At the end."

"He talked about ore," Ruth said. "He talked about claims and assays and the syndicate stealing forty years of his life. If he mentioned me at all it was as an entry in a ledger he never balanced."

She handed over the coffee because her hands wanted something to do, and Marchetti took it, and for a moment they stood on the dock in the thin cold sun with steam rising off two tin cups, two women who had nothing in common except, apparently, Tobias Wrenn, which was a thing neither of them wanted.

* * *

Ruth had been nine years old the night her father left, and she remembered it the way you remember a tooth coming out — sudden, bloody, and leaving a gap your tongue kept returning to.

There had been shouting first. Her mother's voice going up through the floorboards, sharp and getting sharper, and then the sound of the duffel hitting the porch boards, and then the long silence that was worse than the shouting, and then the screen door, and then the outboard coughing to life on the dark water, and then nothing. He hadn't come back for Christmas. He hadn't come back for the funeral, three years later, when her mother's heart quit between one chore and the next, and Ruth, twelve years old, had learned to run the ferry line from a man named Ostrowski who drank and meant well and taught her engines instead of grief, which was the better trade.

Tobias Wrenn came back twice in forty years. Once when she was nineteen, standing on this same dock with snow in his beard, saying *you've got her hands, you know, your mother's hands*, as if that was currency anyone would accept. And once eleven years ago, when he'd left a package with the trader marked FOR RUTH and no letter inside, just a claim stub and a note in pencil: *When you're ready.* She had put it in the bottom drawer of the chart table and not opened it since, and she knew exactly where it was, the way you know exactly where a splinter is.

So when Zephyrine Marchetti set a oilskin packet on the chart table between them and said, "He asked me to bring you this," Ruth looked at it the way you look at a snake you've been assured is dead.

"No," she said.

"You haven't—"

"I know what it is. It's forty years of sorry in an envelope. No."

Marchetti sat back. She didn't argue, which Ruth had expected her to, and somehow that was worse. She drank her coffee and looked out the office window at the *Marten*, and Ruth recognized the look because she wore it herself on slow days: the look of a person measuring distances.

"You want something," Ruth said. "You didn't fly all this way to play courier."

"I want to run the Kessler gorge."

The words sat there. Ruth laughed, short and hard, the way you'd laugh at a dog proposing marriage.

"The Kessler gorge," she repeated. "You've seen it?"

"From the air."

"From the air." Ruth set down her cup. "Let me tell you about the Kessler gorge from the water, pilot. Eleven miles of channel that drops ninety feet. Three rapids with names, and the names are gravestones. The Squeeze runs so narrow in June you can spit across it, and the water through there isn't moving, it's *falling*, sideways, in pieces. There's a hole below the second drop that took a jet boat with four men in '09 and gave back two of them. The rock is granite and it does not negotiate. Nobody runs the Kessler. Nobody has ever run the Kessler. The syndicate's own crews portage around it, and those boys are paid to be stupid."

"So it can't be done."

"I didn't say that." Ruth heard the words come out of her own mouth and could have bitten them back. Marchetti's eyes came up sharp and interested, and Ruth cursed inwardly, because there it was — the old sickness, the one her father had given her along with her hands. Tell me it can't be done, and watch me find out.

"It can't be done *safely*," she amended. "It can maybe be done once, by someone who doesn't care about the odds, in a boat built for it, with a crew that trusts each other, in a window of about ten days when the melt drops the level but hasn't dropped it too far. And every one of those conditions is a coin toss."

"When's the window?"

"Two weeks. Maybe three if the weather holds cold in the divide." Ruth narrowed her eyes. "Why? What's up there that's worth dying for?"

Marchetti was quiet a moment. Outside, a gull argued with another gull about something neither would concede. When the pilot spoke, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of its traveler's ease.

"Your father found something in the headwaters. Ore samples, survey records. Enough to prove the Kessler Consolidated falsified their original assessment — enough to open the whole fraud up. And the fraud they committed seven years ago is the same fraud that put a conviction on my name that doesn't belong to me. I flew supply runs for that survey camp, Miss Wrenn. I carried the crates they later swore contained stolen ore. I've spent seven years running from a crime somebody built around me like a box, and your father spent forty years digging out the proof, and he died holding it, and his last act on this earth was to hand it to a stranger because he couldn't face handing it to you."

The words hit like cold water. Ruth stood very still.

"That's not true," she said, and heard how young she sounded.

"He told me himself. Said he'd written to you a dozen times and burned every letter. Said—" Marchetti stopped. Something moved across her face, some private arithmetic of mercy versus honesty, and honesty won, barely. "Said you were the one thing he'd ruined that he couldn't fix, and he was too much of a coward to try."

Ruth picked up the oilskin packet. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd have bet anything they'd shake. The canvas was stiff with age and wax, tied with a length of copper wire twisted in the neat, fussy way her father had twisted everything. She held it and did not open it.

"Why do you care?" she asked. "About the letter. About any of it. You got what you came for — the map, the proof. Run your river. Why drag me into it?"

Marchetti looked at her for a long moment, and Ruth watched her decide to tell the truth again, and saw how much it cost her, and understood suddenly that here was a woman who paid for every honest word like it came out of a purse with a hole in it.

"Because I read the return address on the outside of that packet," Marchetti said, "and I watched him write your name, and his hand shook, and it wasn't the leg. Because I've got a drawer full of letters I've never sent either. And because I'm going to need a river runner who knows the Kessler, and I'd rather it was someone with a reason to go than someone with a price."

* * *

Ruth didn't open the packet that day. Or the next.

She ran the ferry, replaced the bilge packing, ate supper alone, slept badly, and told herself the packet could sit on the chart table until it rotted for all she cared. On the third night she woke at two in the morning from a dream of the screen door slamming, and lay in the dark listening to the lake lap the hull, and finally lit the lamp and went down to the office in her socks because the alternative was lying there until dawn losing her mind.

The wire came off easy. Forty years of patience in a twist of copper.

Inside: a notebook gone soft at the corners, a folded map drawn in her father's precise draftsman's hand, a small cloth bag that clinked — ore samples, tagged and dated — and a letter, sealed, her name on it in pencil. *Ruth.* Just that. Four letters, and her whole childhood in them.

She almost didn't open it. She sat with it in both hands for a long time, feeling the weight of paper that weighed nothing at all, and thought about burning it unopened, which would have been fair, which would have been justice of a kind, and then she thought about a man dying alone in a cabin six hours' flight from anywhere, spending his last strength making sure a stranger would carry this to her, and she broke the seal because spite, in the end, lost to curiosity, and curiosity lost to something she refused to name.

*Dear Ruth,* it began, and then crossed out, and began again. *Dear Ruth. I have started this letter more times than I can count and I am out of nights now, so it will be a poor one, and you will be right to throw it in the stove.*

*I will not insult you with apologies. You have earned better than words and I have nothing to give but words, so I will give you facts instead, which is the only language I ever trusted.*

*Fact: I left because I believed I would find something in the Kessler headwaters worth more than everything I had, and I was right, and it cost exactly everything I had, and I was too proud to come home empty-handed and too ashamed to come home at all. Fact: your mother deserved better and I have never once pretended otherwise, even to myself. Fact: you have her hands. I said that to you once on a dock and watched you look at me like a stranger, and I deserved that too.*

*Fact: the enclosed ledger proves the Kessler Consolidated survey of seven years ago was falsified from the first page. The pilot they blamed — Marchetti — was innocent. I met her by accident this spring and nearly died of it, because she has her father's eyes and I helped bury her father's good name. What I found in the headwaters clears her. It should have cleared you of me, but there is no assay for that, girl, and I am sorry.*

*The claim is yours. All of it. I registered it in your name twenty years ago and renewed it every season since, and if you never lift a stone from that mountain it is still yours, because you are the only thing I ever found that was worth the finding.*

*Burn this if you want. Keep the claim if you want. But if a woman named Marchetti comes to you asking after the gorge — help her. Not for me. For you. Some rivers you portage around your whole life and they get heavier every year. This is yours.*

*Tobias*

Ruth read it twice. Then a third time, standing in the cold office with the lamp hissing, and somewhere in the middle of the third reading she understood that she was crying, which was an outrage, which she had not done since her mother's funeral, and she let it happen anyway because there was no one to see and the lake kept its own counsel.

Furious. That was the word for it. Furious at him for writing *I am sorry* as though two words could cover thirty years, furious at him for dying before she could say the things she'd rehearsed in the shower and on the water and into her pillow for four decades, furious at him for being exactly the man she'd always known he was — brilliant, stubborn, cowardly, hers — and grieving so hard underneath the fury that the two feelings had fused into a single alloy she had no name for and no tools to work.

And underneath all of it, quiet as ballast: *help her. Not for me. For you.*

* * *

She found Marchetti at the float dock at dawn, checking the tie-downs on a battered de Havilland Beaver whose paint had been patched more times than a trawler's hull. The pilot looked up, and Ruth saw her take in Ruth's face — the red-rimmed eyes, the set jaw — and wisely say nothing.

"I'll run the gorge," Ruth said.

Marchetti went still. "Wrenn—"

"On conditions. My boat, my rules, my route. You don't touch the helm, you don't second-guess a line I call, and when I say portage, we portage, even if the syndicate itself is standing on the bank waving contracts." She stepped closer. "And you tell me everything. Everything he said at the end, every word, in order. You don't soften it and you don't skip the parts where he was a bastard. I've had forty years of other people's softened versions. I'm done with them."

"All right," Marchetti said quietly.

"And one more thing." Ruth reached into her jacket and pulled out the letter, creased now where she'd refolded it, and held it out. "He wrote that you're innocent. That the survey was fixed and they hung it on you. I don't know you, pilot. By reputation you're a smuggler, a liar, and a ghost. But my father could smell a false assay at forty paces, and he staked his last six hours on you." She pushed the letter into Marchetti's hands. "So I'm staking my boat on it. Don't make me regret inheriting his judgment along with everything else."

Marchetti looked down at the letter — her own name visible in the third paragraph, Ruth realized, and felt no urge to take it back — and when she raised her eyes there was something in them Ruth recognized from her own mirror on certain mornings: the expression of a person who has been alone so long that kindness arrives like a foreign currency, beautiful and suspect, and you hold it up to the light anyway.

"Thank you," Marchetti said. It came out rough, like it had been dragged over gravel.

"Don't thank me. Thank him." Ruth turned back toward the office, then stopped, because there was one more thing and she might as well say it while her blood was up. "We leave in five days. I need to fit the *Marten* with a reinforced hull and I need a crew that can pull an oar and keep their heads when the water stands up on end. Do you have people?"

"I'm working on it."

"Work faster." Ruth paused at the top of the dock stairs, and against every instinct she'd sharpened for forty years, she added: "Pilot. There's a boy in Fort Alder — geologist, drunk, disgraced, knows the Kessler formation better than any man alive. Dez Calloway. My father trusted his rock readings, and my father trusted nobody. Might be worth a stop."

Marchetti's eyebrows rose. "You're recruiting for me now?"

"I'm recruiting for the boat," Ruth said. "The boat doesn't care whose redemption it carries. Neither do I."

She went up the stairs and into the office and closed the door, and only then did she lean against it and press the heel of her hand against her sternum, where something had cracked loose and was rattling around like a loose fitting in an engine that had run too long without service. Through the window she watched the Beaver taxi out onto the grey water, gather itself, and climb into the light, and she thought: *five days,* and thought: *the Squeeze,* and thought, with a terror that felt disgracefully like joy: *what in God's name am I doing.*

On the chart table, the bottom drawer stood open, and the space where the packet had sat for eleven years was clean and empty, and the morning came in through the glass and filled it.
