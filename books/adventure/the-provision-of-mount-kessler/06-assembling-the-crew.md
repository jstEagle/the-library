## 6. Assembling the Crew

The trouble with needing people, Zephyrine had learned, was that it started with just one.

She told herself that on the flight down from Coldbank, the map folded in her breast pocket like a splinter working toward her heart. She'd spent seven years perfecting the arithmetic of solitude. One pilot. One plane. One set of hands on the yoke and one name on every manifest, all of them false but her own. It was a clean equation. It balanced. And now here she was, three days after a dying man's bargain, standing in the fuel yard at Meridian Flats with a list of two names she couldn't stop reading.

The first name belonged to a rock.

Not literally — though Dez Calloway had spent enough seasons sleeping against outcrops that the distinction had begun to blur. She found him where the rumor said he'd be: camped behind the assay office in a tent the color of old lichen, surrounded by core samples laid out on a tarp with the tenderness other men reserved for photographs of their children.

He was thinner than the last time she'd seen him, four years ago at an auction in Fort Selkirk, before his name finished rotting. His hair had gone the gray of glacial flour, and his hands — those famous hands, once photographed for a trade journal, *the finest eye for ore in the territory* — were steady as he turned a fist-sized chunk of greenstone in the light. Steady, she noted. That mattered. A drunk's hands shook. Whatever else Dez had lost, he hadn't lost that yet.

"Zephyrine Marchetti." He said it without looking up. "Word travels when you land rough."

"I landed fine."

"You landed loud. Whole town heard you bounce twice on the gravel bar." He set the sample down and squinted up at her, eyes pale as quartz. "Seven years I've wondered what you'd look like if you ever came looking for me. Figured it'd be the law. Or worse."

"Nobody sent me."

"Nobody ever does." He rose stiffly, knees cracking like river ice in April, and studied her face with the same unhurried attention he'd given the stone. "You want something. People who say nobody sent them always want something."

She'd rehearsed this on the flight down. A clean pitch, no history, no debt between them — just a job. But standing in the smell of his woodsmoke and crushed rock, she found the rehearsal falling apart, because Dez Calloway was the only other person alive who knew exactly what the Kessler survey had cost, and they both knew it, and pretending otherwise would insult them both.

"I need someone who can read rock," she said instead. "Upstream. Kessler headwaters."

His expression didn't change, which was itself a kind of answer. Around them the fuel yard clanked and murmured — a loader beeping its reverse warning, two deckhands arguing about a winch — but the space between Zephyrine and Dez went quiet as a snowfield.

"The headwaters," he repeated. "Where the syndicate sealed the source. Where a geologist's signature got forged onto a report that put a bush pilot in front of a judge." He crouched back down and picked up the greenstone again, turning it, turning it. "You know what they took from me? Not the license. Not the reputation. I could live without those. They took the rock. Twenty-six years I read stone like scripture, and now every time I pick up a sample I hear Voss's voice telling me what it says. You can't drink from a well somebody pissed in."

"So that's a no."

"That's a *maybe*, and maybe is more than anyone's gotten out of me in four years." He looked up again, and there was something raw under the quartz-pale calm. "Why you? Why now?"

Because a dying man drew me a map. Because the ledger page tucked inside it has dates that don't match the official survey by eleven days. Because I have perhaps three weeks before the glacier closes the route and I am so tired of running that I could sleep for a year.

What she said was: "Because I'm going anyway. With you or without you. And if the samples are what I think they are, you'll want to be the one holding them when the truth comes out. Not reading about it in a paper written by men who never touched the stone."

Dez laughed — a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge remembering oil. He reached into the tent and came out with a battered leather case, opened it on the tarp, and showed her a single core sample wrapped in waxed cloth. Sulfide-rich, banded, glittering with coarse gold visible even to her untrained eye.

"Picked this up in '19, two miles below the seal," he said. "Never assayed it. Know why?" He didn't wait. "Because I already knew what it would say. And knowing without saying it out loud felt like the last honest thing I owned." He closed the case. "You're asking me to say it out loud. Upstream. In front of whoever's guarding the well."

"Yes."

"And if we don't come back?"

"Then nobody writes about us at all."

He held her gaze for a long moment, and Zephyrine understood that whatever calculation was happening behind those pale eyes had nothing to do with gold or justice or even fear. It was about whether a man could stand to touch the rock again. Whether the well was poisoned all the way down, or only at the top.

"I'll need my own tent," he said finally. "And nobody touches my samples. And if I say a slope is rotten, we go around, even if it costs us a day. Even if it costs us the window."

"Done."

"And Marchetti." He began striking the tent, movements economical, a man who'd packed camp ten thousand times. "This isn't forgiveness. Mine or yours. This is a job."

"Wouldn't have it any other way," she said, and both of them pretended that was true.

* * *

The second name wasn't on any list, because Zephyrine didn't know it yet.

She met Junie Vasquez — though she wouldn't learn the name for another hour — as a pair of boots sticking out from under the engine cowling of a stranger's Stinson at the Meridian public dock. The boots were too big, laceless, and attached to a slight figure wedged into the narrow space between firewall and landing gear with the boneless comfort of someone who'd been living in engine compartments longer than most mechanics had been living in houses.

Zephyrine stood watching for a full five minutes while the figure diagnosed a fouled magneto by ear, one hand inside the cowling, eyes shut, head cocked like a heron listening for fish. When the hand emerged, it held a cracked condenser, and the figure sat up, saw Zephyrine, and froze with the wariness of a cat that has miscalculated the distance to the fence.

"You're leaking," the kid said. Voice high, unbroken, furious about it. "Your left main. Seeping past the grommet. Been doing it since you taxied in."

Zephyrine glanced at her own plane, moored forty yards off. The kid was right. She'd noticed the weep that morning and logged it mentally for Coldbank.

"Who are you?"

"Nobody." The kid slid out, wiped hands on trousers already more grease than fabric, and Zephyrine got her first real look: sixteen, maybe seventeen, sharp-boned, sunburned, dark hair hacked short with what had clearly been a knife rather than scissors. Eyes the color of wet slate and just as likely to freeze over. "Who are *you*?"

"Marchetti. I fly."

"I know. Everybody knows. You're the one who bounced twice on the gravel bar." A ghost of a grin crossed the kid's face and was killed immediately, executed for the crime of showing itself. "Your mixture control sticks in the cold. You should get that looked at."

"It does stick. How do you—"

"I listen to planes. All of them. All day." The kid jerked a thumb at the dock, where a half-dozen floatplanes rocked at their moorings. "That Waco over there's got a bent pushrod, number three cylinder. The yellow Cub burns oil like it's trying to get back at somebody. And your Stinson—" the kid nodded at the stranger's plane, condenser still in hand, "—was going to quit on its owner over the lake tomorrow, and he'd have gone in with it, because he never listens to his own engine. Nobody listens."

Zephyrine crouched, bringing herself level. It was a habit from years of landing at remote strips where children materialized out of the bush to watch the airplane — you got low, you got slow, you made yourself small until they decided you were safe. Though something about this kid suggested safety was not a decision easily made.

"Somebody taught you engines."

"Somebody taught me everything useful and then died." The words came flat, pre-rehearsed, a story worn smooth from telling. "I do odd work around the docks. Fix things. Nobody asks questions if you fix things."

"What's your name?"

A pause. Calculated, Zephyrine could see — the kid weighing names like weights on a cargo manifest, deciding how much identity to declare.

"Junie."

"Just Junie?"

"Just Junie." The slate eyes narrowed. "You going to ask where my parents are? Everyone asks. Then they ask the Mounties, and then I move."

"No," Zephyrine said. "I'm not going to ask."

Something flickered across the kid's face — surprise, quickly buried, but unmistakable. In seven years of practiced avoidance, Zephyrine had become fluent in the language of people hiding, and Junie spoke it natively.

She bought the kid lunch. It took some doing — Junie refused charity with a ferocity that suggested charity had teeth — so Zephyrine framed it as payment for the condenser diagnosis, fair wage for fair work, and Junie ate three sandwiches with the methodical intensity of someone refueling rather than dining. Between bites, Zephyrine learned fragments: a garage in Teslin that burned; an uncle who drank; eight months on the docks, sleeping in boat hulls in summer and, God knew where, in winter. No school since fourteen. Hands that could dress a valve job but hesitated, visibly, whenever anyone stood too close.

It was none of her business. That was the whole architecture of her life — none of it anybody's business, and she nobody's. She had a crew of one to recruit, not two. A geologist and a pilot could run upstream light and fast. A teenager was ballast. A teenager was a liability. A teenager was—

Was looking at her the way she'd once looked at every plane that lifted off without her.

"I leave tomorrow morning," Zephyrine heard herself say. "Upstream. Long trip. Rough country."

"I don't want anything." Too fast. Way too fast.

"I didn't offer anything."

Junie's jaw tightened. The kid looked out at the water, at the moored planes rocking on their reflections, and when the answer came it was aimed somewhere over Zephyrine's shoulder, at nothing, at everything.

"Planes are the only thing that ever went anywhere," Junie said. "I used to think if I could fix enough of them, one would take me with it. That's stupid, right?"

"It's not stupid."

"It's stupid." The kid stood, brushed off trousers that were beyond brushing, and stuck out a greasy hand with the abrupt formality of someone ending a conversation before it could end them. "Thanks for the sandwiches, Marchetti. Good luck upstream."

Zephyrine flew a supply loop that afternoon and thought about nothing else, which annoyed her considerably.

* * *

She was up before dawn, loading the Stinson by lamplight — fuel drums slung, crates lashed, Dez's sample cases crated amidships with his explicit instructions ringing in her ears — when she heard the hangar door.

It moved six inches and stopped. In the gap: a boot. Then the rest of Junie, ducking under the frame, a canvas duffel over one shoulder and an expression of magnificent defiance already locked and loaded, as though Zephyrine had been arguing with the kid for hours instead of sleeping.

"Before you say anything," Junie announced, "I fixed your seep. New grommet, and I re-torqued the baffle bolts while I was in there. Check the logbook, I wrote it up proper."

Zephyrine checked the logbook. It was written up proper. Better than proper — the entry included torque values and a parts note in a careful draftsman's hand.

"I'm not taking passengers."

"I'm not a passenger. I'm crew."

"You're sixteen."

"Fifteen and a half." Delivered like a legal precedent.

"You're fifteen and a half, and I'm flying into country where the syndicate sends patrol boats and strafes camps, and I don't even know what's waiting at the end of it. I can't be responsible for—"

"Don't be responsible for me." Junie dropped the duffel and planted both feet, arms crossed, a stance that said *I have been thrown out of better places than this.* "Nobody's responsible for me. Nobody has been for two years. I fix your engine, I keep your boat running, I cook — I can cook, don't make that face, I cooked for a fish camp of nine men last season and nobody died. You fly, the rock guy rocks, I keep the machinery alive. That's the whole deal. You don't owe me anything and I don't owe you anything and when it's done we shake hands and that's it."

*When it's done we shake hands and that's it.* Zephyrine recognized the phrasing. She'd used it herself, a hundred times, in a hundred towns. *Temporary. Just temporary. Don't unpack anything you can't carry out in one trip.*

The kid had learned her own language fluently, and hearing it spoken back to her did something uncomfortable to Zephyrine's chest.

"Junie." She kept her voice level. "If I say no, will you stow away anyway?"

The silence lasted three seconds. Then, with the terrible honesty of the young: "Yes. Probably in the tail. You'd never feel the weight difference."

"I absolutely would feel the weight difference."

"But you might not check."

Zephyrine looked at the kid — at the grease-stained hands, the knife-cut hair, the defiance stretched thin over something that was not defiance at all — and thought about Tobias Wrenn trading a map for a promise. About Ruth refusing the packet. About how every person she'd met in three weeks was carrying something they couldn't deliver alone.

She thought about the equation. One pilot. One plane. Clean. Balanced. And how, lately, the numbers refused to sit still.

"Rules," she said.

Junie blinked. "What?"

"Rules. One: you wear the headset when I tell you to wear the headset. Two: when I say run, you run, and you don't ask which direction, you run toward my voice. Three—" she pointed at the logbook, "—every entry signed. Your name. The real one, when you're ready to write it. Until then, Junie is fine."

The kid stared at her as though she'd spoken in tongues. "That's it? That's the rules?"

"That's it."

"No curfew? No *where do you think you're going*?"

"Do you see a curfew anywhere within two hundred miles?" Zephyrine gestured at the hangar, the gray dawn, the river fog burning off the flats. "Rule four: nobody out here asks where anybody thinks they're going. We're all going the same place."

From the shadows by the fuel drums, where he'd been waiting with the tact of a man long accustomed to being the third-worst problem in any room, Dez cleared his throat. "The rock guy rocks," he said. "Is that the official job description?"

"It's provisional," Zephyrine said.

"Good." He handed Junie one of the sample cases to carry, testing, Zephyrine realized, not the kid's strength but something else entirely. Junie took it without staggering and without comment, and something passed between the old man and the young one — a brief, gruff nod, two people agreeing not to ask each other anything either.

They loaded in the blue half-light. Zephyrine ran the preflight with Junie shadowing her, absorbing the sequence with that heron-still attention, and when the engine caught — smoother than it had run in months, she had to admit — the kid's whole face changed for one unguarded second, lit from inside like a lamp.

*Don't get attached,* Zephyrine told herself, taxiing out through the mist. *Any of you. This is a job. When it's done, you shake hands.*

Behind her, Dez was already asleep against the sample cases, exhausted by the mere act of hope. Beside her, Junie pressed a palm flat against the window glass as Meridian Flats fell away beneath them — the docks, the moored planes, the boat hulls where a kid had slept through a northern winter — smaller and smaller, and the kid watched it go without waving.

Zephyrine banked north, up the gray ribbon of the river, toward the narrows where the syndicate's patrols ran, and allowed herself exactly one thought about the future:

Three weeks. Two crew. One window.

And somewhere ahead, the first patrol boat was already on the water.
