## 7. Upstream

They launched at first light, when the Kessler ran the color of old pewter and the mist stood on it in columns like the ghosts of trees that had never grown there.

Zephyrine had chosen the hour deliberately. Dawn on a northern river was a kind of camouflage — every boatman knew that the syndicate's patrols kept banker's hours, their crews warm in bunkhouses until the sun cleared the ridgeline and someone with a clipboard decided the day had officially begun. If she could put forty miles of water behind them before that clipboard came out, the rest of the arithmetic might work in her favor.

The boat was Ruth's — a heavy-shouldered river freighter called the *Marta June*, thirty feet of welded aluminum with a diesel inboard that sounded like a man clearing his throat before telling you bad news. Ruth had owned her eleven years and it showed in the way she handled her: no wasted motion, no drama, just the tiller answering Ruth's hand the way a good dog answers a whistle. Zephyrine watched from the bow as they pulled away from Coldbank's fuel dock, and she thought that if the world ever made sense again, she'd like to know a machine half as well as Ruth Wrenn knew this one.

Dez sat amidships among the crates, his long legs folded up like a folding rule, a rock hammer across his knees the way other men carried rifles. He hadn't stopped looking at the banks since they cleared the harbor. Junie was at the stern beside Ruth, ostensibly helping, actually absorbing — Zephyrine could see the girl cataloguing everything, the fuel lines, the bilge pump, the way Ruth feathered the throttle through the wake of the fish tender. Junie learned machines the way some people learned languages: by immersion, greedily, without asking permission.

The floatplane — Zephyrine's own battered de Havilland Beaver, the last thing she truly owned in the world — would shuttle upriver to meet them each evening. It couldn't carry the crew and the cargo both, so the plan was simple: fly ahead, land on whatever stretch of water looked forgiving, wait for the boat to catch up, sleep, repeat. Three weeks of that, if the glacier behaved. Less, if it didn't.

"Fuel dock says there was a patrol boat at Two Mile yesterday," Ruth said, not raising her voice, because on the *Marta June* nobody raised their voice. "Kessler Consolidated colors. Asking after traffic."

"Asking after us, or asking after traffic?" Dez said.

Ruth let the silence answer.

Zephyrine turned her face into the wind coming off the water and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. A patrol at Two Mile meant they were already watching the river mouth. Which meant either routine diligence — possible, but syndicates didn't pay men to be diligent about nothing — or someone had talked. The fuel dock man? The harbormaster? Or simply the fact that Ruth Wrenn, who hadn't run the upper Kessler in six years, had bought enough provisions for five people and told everyone at the chandlery exactly where she was going, because Ruth Wrenn did not lie, and lying to protect an expedition was apparently a skill Zephyrine alone had brought aboard.

She found she wasn't angry about it. There was something almost restful about being hunted openly. For seven years she had been hunted quietly — by paperwork, by rumor, by the slow erosion of being unable to give her real name at a fuel counter. A patrol boat on the water was at least honest about its intentions.

* * *

By midmorning the river began to close around them like a hand.

Below the confluence with the Otter it had been wide and easy, braided through gravel flats where gulls argued over spawned-out salmon. But the Kessler above the Otter was a different animal entirely. The valley walls drew in, spruce crowding down to the waterline, and the current gathered itself into long green tongues that slid past submerged boulders with a sound like silk tearing. Ruth worked the channel with a concentration that bordered on prayer, reading the water the way Dez read rock — the V of a riffle meaning shallow, the smooth glassy tongue meaning something big and patient underneath.

"See that boil," Ruth said at one point, nodding at a place where the surface bulged upward without breaking. "Rock under there, big as a house. Water can't get around it so it gets mad instead." She swung the bow wide. "River's like anything else. You watch what makes it angry, you learn where it lives."

Junie wrote that down. Actually wrote it down, in a grease pencil on the back of her forearm, which was where she kept all her notes, which was why her arms looked like a mechanic's shop manual.

Zephyrine flew the second leg while the others ate cold beans in the boat. From eight hundred feet the river was a gray-green vein threading toward the white massif at the head of the valley, and Mount Kessler itself stood over everything like a judge who had heard all the arguments already and was unimpressed. She'd flown this country for seven years but always around it, never into it — the upper Kessler was syndicate lease ground, posted, patrolled, closed to traffic that couldn't demonstrate a reason to be there. Every time she'd crossed the valley on a medicine run to the villages beyond, she'd given the mountain a wide berth, the way you give a wide berth to a house where something happened once.

Now she banked and looked straight at it. It looked back. Mountains are good at that.

She landed on a long lake below the second canyon and shut the engine down, and the silence that followed was so complete she could hear her own pulse in her ears. Somewhere in that silence, she thought, was a survey camp with her name written all over its ruin. Seven years ago she had walked out of that camp in handcuffs, and the mountains had watched her go, and none of them had said a word.

* * *

They made camp that night on a gravel bar where the river bent north, and it was there, over a fire built small and smokeless out of habit, that Dez finally opened the ore samples.

He'd been carrying them since Coldbank — three canvas pouches from Wrenn's cabin, each holding fist-sized chunks of quartz shot through with metallic threads. He'd been strange about them, handling them like evidence, which of course they were, though he wouldn't say of what. Now he crouched at the firelight's edge with a hand lens screwed into his eye socket and broke a sample open with two taps of his hammer, precise as a surgeon.

For a long time he said nothing. The fire popped. Downriver, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"Galena," he said finally. "Sphalerite. Some native silver — see the wire structures, here, like little frozen lightning bolts." He passed the broken face to Zephyrine. "And this—" he tapped a band of dull rose-colored mineral running through the quartz, "—rhodochrosite. That's your fingerprint."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning this assemblage doesn't occur anywhere else in the drainage. I've sampled every showing between here and Coldbank. Carbonate-hosted silver-lead-zinc with rhodochrosite gangue — that chemistry needs a specific host limestone, a specific intrusion, a specific everything. There is exactly one geological setting in this valley that produces it." He looked up, firelight swimming in his eyes, and Zephyrine saw that he was afraid, and that he had been afraid since Coldbank and had been waiting for her to make him say it. "The source. The sealed zone. Whatever Consolidated has fenced off up there under lease 114 — this rock came out of it. No question. None."

"And the assay figures Wrenn recorded?"

Dez exhaled slowly. "If those numbers are real, it's one of the richest showings in the territory. Richer than anything Consolidated has ever put into production. Which means—" he stopped himself, jaw working.

"Say it," Zephyrine said.

"Which means the deposit they've spent seven years telling the world was marginal — telling investors, telling the government, telling anyone who'd listen that the upper Kessler was a bust worth sealing off cheaply — isn't a bust at all. It's a fortune they're sitting on and pretending isn't there." He turned a sample over in his hands. "You don't seal off a dud. You seal off a treasure and call it a dud until the price is right, or until you've quietly stripped it without paying a cent of the taxes and royalties a producing mine owes. Either way—" he glanced up at her, and now the fear in his face had a different shape, "—either way, the fraud isn't just the survey report. It's the whole story. And the survey report is the part with your name on it."

The fire cracked. Zephyrine fed it a stick and watched the sparks climb.

Seven years ago, she had been the camp cook and occasional pilot for the Kessler Consolidated survey team — a job that paid badly and suited her perfectly, because it required competence, solitude, and no questions about her past. Then the assay office in Coldbank had come up short against the field records, then the field records had come up missing, then a strongbox with her fingerprints on it had come up empty, and the arithmetic had resolved itself the way arithmetic does when powerful people need it to: the pilot did it. The pilot with no family, no history, no one to speak for her. The pilot who, conveniently, had already fled the territory by the time the warrant issued.

She had never known what the survey had actually found. She had assumed, in her bleaker hours, that it had found nothing at all — that she'd been burned as cover for incompetence, a mundane little embezzlement dressed up as sabotage. It had never occurred to her that she might have been burned as cover for *this*. For a mountain full of silver that somebody wanted invisible.

"They didn't frame me to hide a theft," she said slowly. "They framed me to hide the ore."

"That would be my read," Dez said quietly.

Across the fire, Ruth had gone very still, the way she went still when she was reading water. Junie had stopped pretending not to listen and was listening with her whole body, knees hugged to her chest, eyes enormous.

"So they'll come after us," Junie said. It wasn't a question.

"They already are," Zephyrine said.

* * *

The patrol boat found them the next afternoon, or rather revealed itself, because it had probably been finding them since dawn.

It came around the bend below their anchorage at four o'clock — a low black hull with a cabin the color of a hearse and KESSLER CONSOLIDATED stenciled on the bow in letters you could read from orbit. It didn't approach. It didn't hail them. It simply slowed to their speed, half a mile back, and settled into their wake like a wolf settling into a deer's tracks, patient, professional, making sure they knew.

Nobody aboard the *Marta June* said anything for a full minute.

"Well," Ruth said at last, spitting over the side with tremendous dignity. "At least they're not pretending to be fishermen."

Zephyrine stood at the transom and studied the boat through her binoculars. Twin diesels, radar arch, a crew of three she could count, possibly four. Radio antenna. No visible armament, but then neither did wolves carry visible teeth until the moment mattered. She memorized the hull number — KC-7 — and lowered the glasses.

"It'll have a radio," Dez said. "It'll be reporting our position every hour to whoever's upstream."

"I know."

"And we can't outrun it. Not loaded like this."

"I know that too."

"So what do we do?"

Zephyrine took her time answering, because the honest answer was *I don't know yet*, and she had learned that the words *I don't know* were most dangerous when spoken quickly. She watched the black hull hold its distance, obedient as a shadow, and she felt the exhilaration of the morning — the clean speed of the river, Dez's hammer striking truth out of stone, the sense that she was finally moving *toward* something after seven years of moving away — curdle into something colder and more familiar. Being hunted. She knew this country. She had lived in it for seven years. She had simply never had crew before, never had people whose fates were tied to hers, and the weight of that was new and terrible and — she admitted it, in the privacy of her own skull, where admissions cost nothing — not entirely unwelcome.

"We keep doing exactly what we're doing," she said. "We don't run. Running tells them we're guilty of something, and right now we're four citizens exercising a lawful right of travel on a navigable river. We log their position every time we see them. We photograph them. Junie — you got that camera?"

"The one from the wrecked Cessna at Mile Forty? It works."

"Then you're our official photographer. Every time that hull shows up, we document it. Timestamps, positions." She turned to face them, all three, and saw Ruth's eyebrows rise slightly — the closest Ruth Wrenn came to open surprise. "If this ends the way I think it ends, with a magistrate and a stack of paper, then everything Consolidated does between here and the source becomes evidence. Let them follow us. Let them write their own confession in their own logbook."

"And if it doesn't end in a courtroom?" Dez asked softly. "If it ends out here, on the water, with just us and them?"

Zephyrine looked upriver, where the valley narrowed toward the white shoulder of the mountain and the evening light was turning the glacier's face the color of old brass.

"Then it ends out here," she said. "But not tonight. Tonight we eat beans, and Junie shows me how that camera works, and tomorrow we make another fifty miles."

* * *

That night, anchored in a slough behind a willow island with the patrol boat's running lights visible far downstream like a pair of malevolent stars, Zephyrine lay awake in the Beaver's cabin and listened to the river.

It spoke all night, the way big rivers do — a low continuous conversation with itself, gravel shifting, water worrying at the banks, the endless patient argument between current and stone. She had slept beside rivers before, in seven years of bush strips and floatplane anchorages, but this was the first time she'd listened to one and understood that it was going somewhere she was going too. The Kessler ran uphill, in a sense — ran toward the ice, toward the source, toward the sealed camp and the truth buried under its floorboards. Everything in this valley flowed away from the mountain except her.

A sound outside. Soft footsteps on gravel, then a knock on the Beaver's door strut, three taps, tentative.

Junie stood in the moonlight in her oversized jacket, arms crossed, radiating the elaborate casualness of a teenager who has rehearsed a speech and hopes you won't notice.

"Can't sleep either?" Zephyrine said.

"The boat's lights bother me." Junie scuffed a boot. "Also I fixed your landing light. The port one. It had a bad ground."

"You didn't have to do that."

"I know." A pause. The river filled it. Then, in a rush, as if the words had been waiting all day behind her teeth: "When Dez said they'd come after us — you didn't even flinch. You just started giving orders. Like it was a fuel stop or something." Her chin came up, defiant, daring Zephyrine to make something of it. "My mom used to do that. When things got bad. She'd just start organizing, like if she could arrange the cups right the whole disaster would hold together." She stopped, horrified at herself, at how much she'd said, and turned to go. "Anyway. Light's fixed."

"Junie."

The girl paused, silhouetted against the moonlit water.

"Get some sleep," Zephyrine said. "Tomorrow we're going to need that camera."

Junie went. Zephyrine lay back down and stared at the ceiling of her cabin, at the map of water stains seven years of rain had drawn there, and thought about cups arranged in a disaster, and about how a girl who trusted no one had just told her a true thing about her mother, unprompted, in the dark.

Downstream, the black boat's lights held steady, keeping their vigil. Upstream, the glacier waited with its cargo of ice and evidence, calving a little more each day, the window narrowing grain by grain.

Three weeks, maybe less. She closed her eyes and let the river talk her toward sleep, and if her dreams that night were of handcuffs and rhodochrosite and a girl arranging cups in a burning kitchen, well — the river was used to carrying worse things than that.
