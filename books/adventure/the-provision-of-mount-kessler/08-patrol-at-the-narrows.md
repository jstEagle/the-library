## 8. Patrol at the Narrows

Junie felt the other boat before she saw it — a change in the water's rhythm, a deep-throated engine note rolling up the canyon like weather. She had spent two years learning engines the way other kids learned faces, and this one she knew on sight of its sound: a big-block diesel, government-smooth, running just under the speed that would look like a chase.

"Company," she said, and Zephyrine's eyes flicked to the mirror without her head moving at all.

They were in the narrows proper now, where the Kessler pinched itself between walls of gray granite and ran green and fast and mean. The canyon swallowed sound and gave it back doubled — their own wake slapping the rock, Dez's oarlock creaking, the gurgle in the bilge Junie had been pretending not to worry about since Coldbank. The syndicate boat came around the bend below them like something born in the canyon: low, black-hulled, a radar mast and a mounted spotlight and three men in matching rain gear that probably cost more than Junie's whole stolen toolbox.

"Everyone breathe," Zephyrine said. "We're a licensed survey shuttle. We're boring. Bore them."

*We're a stolen toolbox and a forged permit and a girl nobody's supposed to know is aboard,* Junie thought, but she kept her hands loose on the engine cover and did what she'd done her whole life, which was make herself small and useful and forgettable.

Ruth eased the boat toward the gravel bar at the canyon's throat, where the water spread shallow and braided over stones the color of old bone. The syndicate boat swung in behind them with a precision that made Junie's stomach tighten. Not a patrol. A *reception*.

The man who stepped onto the bar first was tall and dry-looking, the kind of dry that had nothing to do with the rain. He wore a canvas inspector's jacket with a laminated badge on a lanyard, and he smiled the way a form smiles.

"Morning. Water Resources Compliance, Kessler District." He had a clipboard, an actual clipboard, which Junie found almost insulting. "Names and permit, please."

"Morning." Zephyrine climbed out onto the stones like she had all the time in the world, and Junie watched her do it — watched her plant her boots, roll her shoulders back, put on a face Junie hadn't seen before. Not the pilot face. Something older and smoother, a face from before the seven years. "Zephyrine Marsh. Running freight and survey support upstream. Permit's in the dry bag."

The inspector's eyes went down the boat. Ruth, arms crossed, jaw set like a river stone. Dez in the stern, gray-faced, hands folded over his bad knee. And Junie, curled by the engine, hood up.

"And her?" the inspector said.

"Deckhand," Zephyrine said. "Local kid. Hired at Coldbank for the portage haul. You know how it is — nobody's boys want to carry gear over scree for a week."

Junie kept her face blank and her heart hammering. *Local kid.* Like she belonged somewhere. Like somebody had bothered to hire her.

The inspector — his badge said HALVORSEN — held out his hand for the permit, and Junie watched Zephyrine pull the folded paper from the dry bag with the unhurried care of a woman who had never forged anything in her life. Junie had watched her make that paper two nights ago in the hangar light, tracing the district seal off a fuel invoice, matching the typewriter spacing letter by letter, cursing under her breath when the *R* came out wrong. It had looked good. It looked, Junie was realizing, good enough to die over, if Halvorsen had a phone signal and a mind to use it.

He didn't have a phone signal. That was the whole point of the narrows.

"Marsh," Halvorsen read. "Zephyrine Marsh." He said the name like he was tasting it. His eyes came up. "Any relation to the Marchetti that used to fly out of Coldbank?"

"Common name up here," Zephyrine said. "Half the interior's named after somebody who ran from something."

Halvorsen smiled again. He had a camera on a strap under his jacket, a chunky thing with a flash, and while he read the permit he lifted it and took a picture of the boat. Then, casually, terribly, he took a picture of Ruth. Then Dez. Then, when Junie shifted her weight, of her.

"Standard documentation," Halvorsen said. "Every vessel transiting the upper district gets logged."

"Since when?" Ruth said.

"Since the district was reorganized." He didn't look at her. He was looking at Junie, and there was something in the look that made her skin crawl — not recognition, exactly. *Inventory.* "How old are you, kid?"

"Sixteen," Junie lied, and was surprised at how easily Zephyrine's voice came in over hers, smooth as river ice.

"She's sixteen, and she's my responsibility until we're back at Coldbank. Her mother runs the chandlery there — Marta. You can check when you've got signal." A beat, easy as breathing. "You don't have signal."

Halvorsen's smile thinned by a millimeter. "No," he agreed. "We don't."

He walked the boat. That was the worst part. He came down the gunwale with his clipboard and his camera and looked into the dry bags, and Junie sat with her hands in her lap and watched Dez's face do something complicated when Halvorsen lifted the tarp over the sample crates. The ore samples, wrapped in burlap, labeled in Dez's careful hand as *aggregate survey — Kessler bench, non-mineralized.* Dez had spent half the night on those labels. Halvorsen read one upside down for a long moment.

"Geologist?" he said.

"Retired," Dez said. His voice came out steady, which Junie hadn't expected. "Bad knee, bad back, good eyes. They pay me to look at rocks and tell them they're boring."

"And are they?"

"Boring as sin," Dez said, and Halvorsen laughed, one dry bark, and put the tarp back.

But he photographed the crates too. Junie counted. Fourteen pictures total, and she counted everything now, it was how she'd survived the group homes — count the exits, count the hands, count the lies. Fourteen pictures, one clipboard, one boat with a radio mast that would reach a repeater somewhere on a peak they couldn't see, and three crewmen in matching rain gear who hadn't smiled once and hadn't stopped watching Zephyrine since the bar.

Halvorsen handed the permit back. "You're clear to transit, Ms. Marsh. The upper district's closed to mineral activity, of course. Survey support only. You'll be out before the calving?"

"That's the plan."

"Plans," Halvorsen said, "are how people end up in the wrong place." He turned to go, then stopped, and looked back at Zephyrine with the smile gone entirely, and what was underneath it was worse — patient, almost kind. "The company appreciates cooperation. It remembers it, too. Both directions."

The black boat pulled away downstream, engine note fading into the canyon's throat, and the river took the sound and gave back nothing.

Nobody moved for a full minute.

* * *

They made two more miles before Ruth found a side channel and put the boat into a cutbank willowed over with alder, out of sight of the main current. Then she killed the engine and turned around and looked at Zephyrine, and Junie pretended to be deeply involved in the bilge.

"Marta," Ruth said. "Marta of the chandlery."

"There's no Marta."

"I know there's no Marta."

"There's a Marta," Zephyrine said. "She ran the chandlery in Coldbank when I was nineteen. She'll be dead or gone, and either way she can't contradict me, and Halvorsen knows it, which is why I picked her." She pulled the permit out and looked at it, and Junie saw her thumb move over the forged seal like she was checking it for a pulse. "He's going to run that name through the district office the second he's in range and find out I'm lying. Which means we've got maybe a day, maybe two, before they stop sending inspectors and start sending Voss."

"You know him," Dez said quietly. It wasn't a question. "The way he looked at you."

"I know the jacket," Zephyrine said. "The company's had inspectors since before I could drive. The cameras are new." She folded the permit away. "New means somebody upstream told them to start a file."

Junie sat with her back against the engine block and listened to them plan in low voices — Ruth saying the portage had to start tonight, Dez saying the samples couldn't be repacked in the dark, Zephyrine saying nothing for a long time and then saying, "We run at first light, and nobody stops," in a voice that closed the discussion like a door.

And the whole time, Junie kept turning one thing over in her head, the way she'd turn a stripped bolt, looking for the threads.

*Local kid. My responsibility. Her mother.*

Zephyrine had lied about her. Not about the boat, not about the permit, not about Dez's boring rocks — about *her.* Had built a whole small false life for her, mother and town and age, in the space of one breath, without blinking, and the lie had been a wall with Junie standing behind it.

Adults lied to Junie all the time. She had a whole taxonomy of it. Lies to get you to sleep, lies to get you into the van, lies about the check being in the mail and the placement being temporary and this time being different. She knew every species. She'd never once been in the category Zephyrine had just put her in, which was: *lies to keep you.*

She didn't know what to do with that. It sat in her chest like an engine part from a machine she'd never seen before, no manual, no diagram.

* * *

They made cold camp in the alders, no fire, cold beans eaten out of the can with a shared spoon while the light went bronze and then blue in the canyon. Dez dozed against a pack. Ruth stood the first watch upstream, a silhouette against the last of the sky.

Junie worked. She always worked. She had the bilge cover off and the little hand pump going, and she'd found what she'd been dreading — a weeping seam where the hull had kissed a rock in the narrows, nothing serious yet, everything serious eventually. She was sanding it clean by flashlight when Zephyrine crouched down across from her, out of earshot of the others, and held out a strip of epoxy-impregnated cloth from the repair kit.

"Marine patch," she said. "Ruth carries it for the ferry. Wet it, wrap it, it'll hold till Coldbank."

"I know how to patch a hull," Junie said.

"I figured," Zephyrine said. "I watched you diagnose that seam before I did."

Junie took the cloth because it was there, not because she needed it handed to her, and worked it around the seam with her fingers in the dark. Zephyrine didn't leave. She just crouched there, forearms on her knees, watching the black water slide past the cutbank.

"You did good today," she said finally.

"I didn't do anything."

"You did exactly nothing, perfectly, in front of a man whose job was to find something. That's a skill. I've spent seven years practicing it and I still hate it." A pause. "I'm sorry I had to put you in it."

Junie kept her eyes on the patch. "You could've left me in Coldbank."

"I could have. I tried to, if you remember. You were under a tarp in the gear locker at the time."

"You could've tied me to a dock."

"I could have," Zephyrine agreed. "And then when Halvorsen photographed that boat, there'd have been a girl on it I couldn't account for, or there wouldn't have been a girl at all and he'd have wondered why the manifest said deckhand." She shrugged, a small motion in the dark. "Easier to make you real. Marta's daughter. Sixteen. Mine."

*Mine.* Junie's hands went still on the wet cloth.

"That's not —" she started, and didn't know how to finish it, because the endings available were all the wrong ones. *That's not what adults do. That's not how it works. Nobody's mine.*

"Get some sleep," Zephyrine said, standing, easy, like she hadn't just said anything at all. "We run at first light, and after the portage there's a week of scree with your name on it, deckhand."

Junie watched her go up the bank to take the watch from Ruth, and sat there in the dark with her hand flat on the patched seam, feeling the river push against it from underneath and the patch hold.

She had a system for adults. It had kept her alive through four placements and two years on her own: count everything, owe nothing, leave first. It was a good system. It had never once included the sentence *she lied to protect me* filed under anything except *run.*

She got her bedroll out and lay down in the willows and did not sleep. She listened to the river, and to Ruth's and Zephyrine's voices trading the watch in low tones, two women who had both clearly spent years being nobody's, and she thought about the photograph. Somewhere downstream, in a file, there was a picture of her now — hood up, face half-shadow, *local kid, Marta's daughter, sixteen, mine.* A false girl with a false mother, filed in a syndicate office, and the strange thing, the thing she turned over and over like a stone with a fossil in it, was that the false girl felt safer than any real one had. The false girl belonged to somebody.

* * *

First light came gray and cold, and with it, far upstream, a sound like a door closing in a very large house.

Junie was up before anyone, standing on the cutbank, and she heard it again — a deep, grinding report rolling down the canyon, followed by a silence that had weight. Dez came up beside her, and his face, when she looked at it, was the color of the granite.

"Calving," he said. "That's the glacier. That's early." He looked upstream, where the canyon bent away toward the white country, and Junie watched him do the math she couldn't do yet. "That's weeks early."

Zephyrine was already at the boat, throwing off the alder branches, her voice carrying flat and clear across the water.

"Then we just lost our margin. Everybody in. We run now, and we don't stop."

Junie jumped down to the engine and put her hands on the starter, and for the first time in two years of leaving first, she found herself hoping — actually hoping — that the boat, the crew, the whole impossible upstream of it, would hold together long enough to get where it was going.

The engine caught on the second pull. The current took them. Somewhere above, the mountain was coming apart, and they were running straight at it.
