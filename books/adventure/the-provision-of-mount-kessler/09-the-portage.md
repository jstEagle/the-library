## 9. The Portage

The gorge announced itself before they saw it, a low grinding in the river's voice that Ruth felt through the hull of the boat like a warning passed hand to hand. By midmorning the Kessler had narrowed to a brown-green fury between walls of granite furred with moss, and Dez stood at the bow with his binoculars and said what they had all been dreading since Coldbank.

"There it is." He handed the glasses back without turning around. "Kettle Falls. Two hundred yards of whitewater dropping forty feet through a slot you couldn't fit a coffin through sideways. Nobody runs it. Nobody ever has."

"Nobody's ever tried?" Junie asked from the engine well, where she was doing something mysterious to the carburetor with a pair of pliers and a look of profound disapproval.

"Somebody tried in '61," Dez said. "They found the boat two miles downstream. They never found the boat's owner."

Ruth watched Zephyrine take the news the way she took all news — a small stillness, a narrowing of the eyes, nothing wasted on her face. But Ruth was learning to read her anyway, the way she could read weather coming down a lake, and she saw the pilot do the arithmetic: two days lost, maybe three, hauling everything overland while the glacier upriver counted down toward its calving.

"We portage at the gravel bar," Zephyrine said. "Dez, how far around?"

"Four miles over the ridge, then down to the pool below the falls. If the trail exists anymore. It was cut for the survey crews twenty years ago. Alder doesn't wait politely."

Ruth swung the boat toward the bar and felt the familiar settling in her chest, the ferryman's calm that came from knowing exactly what a body of water wanted. The Kessler here wanted blood. She gave it none.

* * *

The trail existed the way old promises exist — technically, and only if you were generous. It began as a shelf of packed earth above the bar and dissolved within half a mile into a green chaos of devil's club and alder, each stalk taller than a man and armed like it held a grudge. They went in single file, Zephyrine leading with a machete she'd produced from under the boat's thwart as though everyone traveled with one, and Ruth brought up the rear with the heaviest load strapped to an external-frame pack that had belonged to her father.

She'd carried that pack since she was nineteen. She knew its every complaint — the left shoulder strap that bit, the hip belt buckle that froze if you looked at it wrong. Carrying it up a mountain trail behind four strangers should have felt strange. Instead it felt like the most honest thing she'd done in years.

By noon the first day, the heat had wrung them out. The gorge held the sun like a cupped hand and gave nothing back but the smell of hot stone and crushed alder leaves. Dez had gone quiet somewhere past the second mile, and Ruth, watching him from behind, saw what he was trying to hide: the hitch in his stride, the way he favored the left leg, planting it stiffly, swinging it forward rather than bending the knee.

She'd seen that walk before. Half the broken men who drifted through Coldbank walked that way — old injuries worn smooth by years of pretending.

At the third mile the trail climbed a shoulder of rock, switchbacking through scree, and Dez stopped pretending. He sat down hard on a boulder, pulled off his boot, and Ruth saw the knee — swollen tight as a knot in wood, the skin shining, the joint misshapen where some old break had healed crooked or never healed at all.

"I'm fine," he said, before anyone asked.

"You're fine," Ruth agreed, "except for the part where you're not."

"It's an old fracture. It talks when it rains." He laughed, and it came out thin. "It's been talking since Whitehorse, '09. A drill rig shifted. Pinned me against the core shack. The company doctor set it in eleven minutes because the rig was costing them money every minute I lay there." He flexed the leg and winced. "It works. Mostly."

Zephyrine crouched beside him, and Ruth watched her study the knee with the same attention she gave engines — diagnosis first, sympathy later, if at all. "Can you walk tomorrow?"

"I can walk today. That's the important part."

"It isn't," Ruth said, surprising herself. "The important part is that you don't lie about it. We plan around truth. We can't plan around brave."

Dez looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face shifted — relief, maybe, at being caught. At being allowed to be caught.

So they replanned. Zephyrine redistributed weight with a pilot's ruthless efficiency, and when it came time to divide the loads, Ruth lifted the aluminum case holding the drilling equipment — sixty pounds of steel and ambition — and slung it onto her own pack without asking anyone's permission.

"That's Dez's load," Zephyrine said.

"Dez's knee says otherwise."

"I can carry my own—"

"You can," Ruth told him, adjusting the strap until it settled into the groove her father's pack had worn into her shoulder years ago. "Tomorrow you can carry something lighter and your dignity along with it. Today I've got it." She turned uphill before he could argue. "I run a ferry. Heavy things are my whole profession."

Behind her she heard Junie snort — the kid had been snorting at everything Ruth said for two days, a habit Ruth was beginning to suspect was affection wearing a disguise — and then the crew fell back into motion, a line of five moving up through the green dark of the gorge, one of them limping, one of them carrying double, all of them going the same direction.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the ridge crest, a mossy amphitheater ringed with boulders that held the day's heat like a hearth. Zephyrine got a fire going small and smokeless, the way bush people do, and Junie produced trout from the creek with the smug efficiency of someone who'd been feeding herself since before it was legal, and for a while there was nothing but the business of food — fish sizzling on a flat stone, coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in, the light dying gold behind the western ridges.

Ruth ate slowly and watched the fire. This was the hour she dreaded most on any trip alone: the hour when the work ran out and the thinking started. On her ferry she solved it by staying busy until sleep took her mid-thought. Here there was nowhere to hide.

Zephyrine sat across the flames, cleaning the machete with an oiled rag, methodical as a surgeon. She hadn't talked much all day. None of them had, really — they were still five strangers sharing a fire, polite as hotel guests. But Ruth had spent thirty years reading people who didn't want to be read, and she recognized the set of the pilot's shoulders tonight. That wasn't shyness. That was a woman standing at the edge of something she'd decided not to say, and losing the argument with herself.

Ruth decided to lose it for her.

"My father left on a Tuesday," she said.

The fire cracked. Junie looked up. Dez went very still, the way men do when they recognize a door they shouldn't open but can't help standing near.

"Ferry day. He'd have known that — he built the schedule with me, the year I turned sixteen. First Tuesday of the month, heavy freight, the school run doubled up because the teachers got paid that day." She turned her tin cup in her hands. "He kissed my mother on the forehead, told me the starboard engine sounded rough and he'd see to it Thursday, and he walked down the dock with a duffel bag I'd later find out he'd packed three days earlier. In secret. In my house. Under my roof, packing a bag while I slept."

"He didn't say goodbye," Zephyrine said. Not a question.

"He said *Thursday.*" Ruth heard her own voice go flat and cold, the voice she used on drunks and debtors. "That's worse. Goodbye is at least honest. *Thursday* is a man buying himself a clean exit and making his daughter complicit in it — because for six months afterward I told people my father would be back Thursday. I kept his berth open. I serviced his engine. I did his maintenance schedule for a full season, waiting, because he'd said Thursday and I was sixteen and I believed him." She stared into the fire. "My mother believed him longer. She died believing it, more or less. Not that he'd come back — she was smarter than that — but believing there'd been a reason. Something big enough to make a liar out of a man who'd taught me never to lie about a tide table."

"And now you know the reason," Zephyrine said quietly. "Or part of it."

"Now I know he was sick, or scared, or running from something, and he chose to leave the way cowards leave — clean, early, before anyone could ask questions." She heard the bitterness in her voice and let it sit there. She'd earned it. "And the worst part — the part I can't get past — is that I understand him. That's what keeps me up at night. I know exactly what it costs to disappear, because I've spent thirty years doing a smaller version of it. He just did it bigger."

The fire popped. Down in the gorge the river ground away at its rocks, patient as geology.

"He wrote you a letter," Zephyrine said. "In the packet. Have you read it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because it'll either explain him or it won't," Ruth said, "and I don't know which one I'm afraid of." She looked up, met the pilot's eyes across the flames — grey-green eyes, watchful, and underneath the watchfulness something Ruth had only glimpsed before, a kind of fellow-feeling, two women who'd both been left by the same man in different decades. "You knew him. Flying with him. What was he like out there?"

Zephyrine was quiet long enough that Junie glanced between them, sensing the shift in pressure.

"He was the best stick-and-rudder man I ever flew with," she said finally. "Calm in weather that made me sweat. And he talked about you. Every trip. 'My Ruthie runs the ferry now, she handles her boat better than I ever handled mine.' He carried your photograph in his map case until the case wore through and he had to tape it." Her mouth twisted. "I thought he was proud of you. He was. But I think mostly he was homesick in a way he couldn't fix, because going home meant explaining himself, and he'd decided he'd rather hurt than explain."

"That's not comfort," Ruth said.

"No. It isn't." Zephyrine fed a twig to the fire. "But it's true, and I promised myself I wouldn't give you anything else."

Ruth nodded, and found, to her surprise, that her eyes were burning. Thirty years she'd kept this grief in a dry room, sealed up, inspected annually, never opened. Now it was out in the open air of a mountainside, in front of near-strangers, and it hadn't killed her. It just weighed less. That was the thing nobody told you — that grief shared wasn't divided, it was halved, the way a load shared between two shoulders lets you walk farther than you believed your body could go.

"Your turn," she said to Zephyrine. "Someday. When you're ready. You tell me why a woman with your hands spends seven years sleeping in her plane."

Zephyrine almost smiled. "Deal."

From the darkness beyond the firelight, Junie's voice, carefully casual: "For what it's worth, everybody in this crew has a Thursday story. Mine's just shorter. Everybody I ever trusted said Thursday and meant never." A pause. "So statistically we're all idiots together."

Dez laughed — a real laugh, rusty, surprised out of him — and Ruth found herself laughing too, and the sound went up through the alders and startled something winged into the night, and for a moment the hollow below the ridge felt less like a camp and more like a kitchen.

* * *

The second day was harder, because second days always are. The trail topped the ridge at dawn and dropped through a burn scar where the dead trees stood silver and bare as ribs, and the descent to the pool below the falls was a knee-wrecking scramble over talus that even Ruth's good legs complained about. Dez managed it on grit and Zephyrine's improvised strapping, refusing help with a stubbornness Ruth privately respected and publicly ignored — she took the heavy end of every log they levered, every canoe-haul, every rope-belayed load, and by afternoon Dez had stopped protesting and started simply saying thank you, which cost him more than the weight had.

They reached the pool at golden hour. The boat waited where they'd cached it, untouched, and the river below the falls ran wide and green and — for the first time in two days — reasonable. Across the water, far upstream, the white shoulder of Mount Kessler floated above the tree line, and Ruth saw Zephyrine stop and look at it for a long moment, reading the glacier the way Ruth read water.

Then the pilot frowned.

"What?" Ruth asked.

"The icefall." Zephyrine raised her binoculars, and Ruth watched her face change — the small stillness again, but deeper this time, a crack running through the calm. "The seracs on the east flank. They're hanging further out than they were at Coldbank. The meltwater under the tongue is running high — you can hear it from here." She lowered the glasses. "That's early-season behavior. That window we counted on? It's smaller than the forecast said."

"How much smaller?"

"I don't know yet." Zephyrine turned from the mountain, and Ruth saw her make the decision before she finished speaking it, the way she seemed to make all decisions — fast, total, already in motion. "But we're not turning around. We push upstream tomorrow at first light, and we don't stop."

Junie whooped from the boat. Dez began checking the fuel lines without being asked. And Ruth stood on the bank with her father's pack still cutting grooves into her shoulders, looking at the mountain that had taken everything from both of them, and felt something she hadn't felt in thirty years of solo crossings:

Not fear. Company.

She swung the pack into the boat and took the tiller.
