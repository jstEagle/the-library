## 10. Ice on the Wind

Zephyrine heard the glacier before she understood what she was hearing.

It came in the grey hour before dawn, a sound like the world's largest door groaning on rusted hinges, deep enough that she felt it in her sternum before it reached her ears. She was out of her bedroll and standing at the tree line before her mind caught up with her body, breath fogging in air that should not have been cold enough to fog. The Kessler headwaters lay two miles east, hidden behind a shoulder of granite, but the sound rolled over everything — the river, the pines, the little camp of three tents and one battered boat pulled up on the gravel bar.

Behind her, she heard Ruth's tent flap open. Then Dez's cough, wet and habitual. Then Junie, who slept like a cat and woke like one too.

"That's not normal," Junie said, appearing at Zephyrine's elbow, hair wild, wrench already in hand as though the glacier might be something she could fix.

"No," Zephyrine said. "It isn't."

She had flown the interior for seven years. She knew the vocabulary of ice the way other people knew the vocabulary of grief — intimately, privately, without any expectation of an answer. Glaciers talked all summer: settling, weeping, cracking their knuckles in the heat of the afternoon. But this was different. This was the sound of a mass the size of a mountain range deciding to move ahead of schedule, and every hour of flying experience she owned told her what it meant.

The calving season had come early.

* * *

They gathered around the fire while the light came up thin and reluctant through the smoke haze. Dez spread Wrenn's map on a flat stone, weighting the corners with four pieces of quartz, and Zephyrine watched his hands shake — not from drink, not anymore, but from something older, the tremor of a man reading his own sentence.

"The terminus is retreating faster than Wrenn's notes suggested," Dez said. He tapped the map where the old prospector had penciled dates beside sketched positions of the ice face. "These readings are from six years ago. If the melt rate has accelerated even modestly since then — and that sound last night says it has — then the corridor above the gorge closes in ten days. Maybe eight."

"Forecast said three weeks," Ruth said flatly.

"The forecast was made by people sitting in offices."

Zephyrine crouched at the edge of the map and let herself look at the numbers honestly, the way she'd once looked at fuel gauges in weather she shouldn't have been flying in. Ten days to reach the sealed camp. Two days' travel from here if the river cooperated. A day at the camp itself, digging for whatever Wrenn had cached there. And then the run back down through water that would be rising, thickening, filling with the debris of a glacier shedding its skin.

Eight days of margin, if nothing went wrong.

Nothing went wrong. She almost laughed. Nothing had gone wrong for seven years because she had built her whole life out of never staying anywhere long enough for anything to go wrong. Now she was anchored to a river, to three people, to a promise made to a dying man, and the ice itself had decided to hurry her along.

"We push hard today," she said. "Long hours. We run until the light fails."

Junie grinned, and Zephyrine saw in the girl's face the pure uncomplicated appetite for motion that she herself had once carried everywhere, before it calcified into something more like fear. It hurt a little, looking at it. It steadied her too.

Ruth said nothing, but she was already breaking camp in her head; Zephyrine could see it in the way her eyes moved over the boats, the loads, the water. The ferry operator ran rivers the way other people ran households — with an authority that tolerated no argument and concealed, somewhere far underneath, an enormous tenderness.

Dez folded the map with the care of a man handling scripture.

Above them, faint and white against the pale sky, the summit of Mount Kessler held its ice, and Zephyrine thought she saw — though she would not swear to it — a fresh scar of exposed blue glacial flesh near the peak where no scar had been.

* * *

They made good distance through the morning. The river narrowed as they climbed toward its source, the current quickening, the banks closing in walls of spruce and shattered rock. Zephyrine took the bow of the lead boat with Ruth at the oars behind her, reading water the way Dez read rock — fluently, instinctively, calling the channels before they appeared. Junie rode the second boat alone, running the small outboard with a touch so gentle it seemed less like operation than conversation. They had left the floatplane moored in a side slough two miles back, hidden under cut boughs; after the Narrows, none of them wanted it visible from the air.

That was the thing Zephyrine could not stop thinking about. From the air.

The syndicate patrol boat had shadowed them for a day and a half below the gorge, then turned back at the rapids — but a boat turning back was not the same as interest ending. She kept finding her eyes drawn upward, scanning the strips of open sky between the peaks, and she hated herself for it, hated the flinch, the seven-year reflex of a woman who had learned that the sky was where trouble lived.

At midday they stopped in a wide eddy to eat cold beans and hard bread, and it was there, chewing mechanically, watching the water slide past, that Zephyrine saw the first sign that had nothing to do with sound.

The river had changed color overnight.

Not the green-brown of summer meltwater but a paler tone, a milkiness, fine rock flour suspended in the current — the signature of a glacier grinding actively against its own bed. She'd seen it a hundred times in late August. It was the middle of July.

"Look at the water," she said quietly.

Ruth looked. Dez looked. Even Junie went still, spoon halfway to her mouth.

"That's flour," Dez said. "Heavy suspension. That means the ice is moving." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Moving fast."

Nobody said the rest of it aloud. Nobody needed to. The window Dez had measured in days was a window measured in guesses, and the guesses were getting worse.

They pushed on. By late afternoon the valley opened into a long gravel flat where the river braided into half a dozen silver threads, and Ruth called for camp — not from softness, Zephyrine knew, but because running braided water in failing light was how boats died. They dragged the hulls up high, well above the evident flood line, and set the tents in the lee of a gravel bar crowned with old driftwood.

Zephyrine gathered wood while the others worked. The driftwood up here was silvered and light as bone, and it burned hot and clean, and she carried armload after armload because her hands wanted occupation and her mind would not stop doing arithmetic. Eight days. Maybe fewer. The camp was another day upstream. The ledger, the letters, the proof that would unmake seven years of exile — all of it sitting in some cache Wrenn had dug into frozen ground, waiting for her the way justice waits, which is to say patiently and without any guarantee.

She was kneeling at the fire pit arranging the evening's wood when Junie's voice cracked across the flat.

"Zeph. ZEPH. You hear that?"

Zephyrine froze, head tilted. Above the river-sound, above the wind coming down off the ice, there was something else. An engine. High. Steady. Wrong.

A floatplane engine did not belong in the upper Kessler. No mail route came here. No medicine run, no trapper's charter, nothing legitimate flew this far up a river that officially, on every chart the government maintained, dead-ended in impassable gorge. Whoever was flying up the Kessler valley had a reason that didn't appear on any map.

"Boats," Zephyrine said, and her voice came out level, pilot-calm, the voice she used on passengers when the engine hiccupped. "Everyone in the trees. Now."

They moved. Ruth took the map case and the food bag without being told; Dez grabbed the sample kit; Junie killed the outboard's fuel line and vanished. Zephyrine stayed in the open one moment longer — long enough to kick gravel over the fire's glow and drag a cut bough across the boats — and then she followed the others into the spruce shadow as the engine sound swelled from a whisper to a presence.

The plane came around the bend of the valley at perhaps four hundred feet.

Zephyrine lay prone beneath a spruce's low skirt, needles pressing into her forearms, and watched it through the screen of branches. It was a de Havilland Beaver on floats — a workhorse, an honest aircraft, which somehow made it worse. She knew that airframe the way she knew her own hands. Someone had painted it corporate white and put a logo on the tail she couldn't quite resolve at this angle, but she didn't need to resolve it. There was exactly one organization in the territory with the money to keep a Beaver on the upper Kessler.

It flew the length of the gravel flat slowly. Deliberately. Its floats trailed a faint mist off the river where the pilot dipped them — a show, a flourish, a man letting them know he could land whenever he pleased.

Then it climbed, banked, and came back.

This time, as it passed over the camp, something dropped from the fuselage. Zephyrine's whole body clenched, every story about bombs and strafing runs arriving at once — but what tumbled end over end into the gravel fifty yards from their boats was only a canister, a paint tin by the look of it, bursting on impact in a splash of vivid orange. And as the plane swept past again, climbing away upriver toward the glacier, a loudhailer crackled to life, distorted by distance and echo but perfectly intelligible:

*"THIS RIVER IS CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE KESSLER CONSOLIDATED MINING COMPANY. ALL PERSONS PROCEEDING UPSTREAM ARE TRESPASSING ON LEASED CLAIMS AND WILL BE PROSECUTED. YOU HAVE ONE WARNING."*

The voice paused. The plane banked wide over the braids, and when the loudhailer spoke again, the words were different, and they landed in Zephyrine's chest like a hook set in living flesh.

*"MS. MARCHETTI. SEVEN YEARS IS A LONG WAY TO RUN FOR NOTHING. GO HOME WHILE YOU STILL HAVE ONE."*

* * *

For a long time nobody moved. The engine faded up the valley, around the shoulder of the mountain, gone — but its absence felt temporary, provisional, like a held breath.

Junie came out of the trees first. The girl walked straight to the orange splatter on the gravel and stared at it, then at the burst tin, then back at Zephyrine, and her face wore an expression Zephyrine hadn't seen on her before: not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of someone who had grown up watching adults lie and had just watched one tell the truth in a way that cost something.

"They know your name," Junie said.

"Yeah," Zephyrine said.

"Not your — they didn't say *the fugitive*. They said *Ms. Marchetti*. Like they've got a file. Like somebody typed it up." Junie's jaw worked. "That inspector at the Narrows. The photographs. That's how they—"

"Probably," Zephyrine said. "Or before that. Doesn't matter now."

It mattered, of course. It mattered enormously, and she catalogued the implications with the cold part of her brain even as the rest of her stood very still. They knew her name. Which meant Voss — and she was certain now it was Voss, the enforcer whose reputation traveled faster than the syndicate's barges — had connected the pilot on the river to the woman convicted seven years ago of falsifying survey records and stealing assay data. Which meant this was no longer a patrol responding to trespass. This was personal. This was a man who had helped build a lie being informed that the lie was walking up his river to dig it up.

The orange paint gleamed on the gravel like a wound.

Ruth emerged from the spruce with the map case hugged to her chest, and her eyes went from the paint to the empty sky to Zephyrine's face, and whatever she saw there made her set her mouth in the line that meant a decision had been made. "They'll report our position," she said. "There'll be a boat behind us inside a day. Maybe men overland."

"Yes."

"And you're going to say we keep going anyway."

Zephyrine looked at her. "You already know I am."

"I want to hear it." Ruth stepped closer, close enough that Zephyrine could see the windburn on her cheeks and the fury banked behind her eyes like coal. "I want to hear you say it out loud, so that when my arms are burning tomorrow and the day after, I remember why."

So Zephyrine said it. She stood in the middle of the violated camp, in the smell of orange solvent and spruce resin, with the glacier muttering on the horizon and the ghost of an engine still ringing in her ears, and she said: "We keep going. We leave before first light, we run the braids blind if we have to, and we do not stop until we're standing in that camp. They can photograph me, shout at me, paint their warnings on my grave. Seven years ago they took everything I had by putting their words in other people's mouths. I'm done listening."

Dez had come to stand at the edge of the group, sample kit clutched to him like a shield, and when she finished he nodded slowly, once, the nod of a man confirming a reading he'd already taken.

Junie picked up the burst paint tin, turned it over in her hands, and hurled it into the river with all the strength in her skinny arm. It skipped twice and sank.

"One warning," the girl muttered. "One warning, my ass. That was a countdown."

* * *

They did not sleep much, and Zephyrine did not pretend to try. She sat up through the short northern dark with her back against a boat hull, the loaded rifle across her knees that she'd carried since the Narrows and never once mentioned, and she listened to the glacier talk.

It talked all night. Not the long groaning of the dawn before but a continuous low conversation, cracks and booms rolling down the valley at intervals that were shrinking, she was nearly sure, shrinking the way contractions shrink, and she found herself thinking of her mother, whom she had not thought of in years, and of stories about births and about how the worst pain always arrived just before the moment everything changed forever.

Around what the watch called two in the morning, Ruth came and sat beside her without asking. That was Ruth's way. She handed over a tin cup of coffee strong enough to strip varnish, and they drank in silence for a while, shoulder to shoulder, two women watching darkness where a mountain of ice was busy deciding their future.

"My father used to fly in weather like this," Ruth said at last. Her voice was rough, unslept. "He'd stand out on the dock at Coldbank and look at the ceiling and say, *the mountain doesn't care what the forecast says, Ruthie. The mountain keeps its own books.*"

Zephyrine turned the phrase over. "Wrenn wrote something like that on the map margin. About the ice keeping accounts."

"Did he." Something moved through Ruth's face, grief and anger sharing a doorway. "Man spent thirty years learning that lesson and still died under a rockslide chasing ore." She stared upriver, into the black. "I'm not doing this for him. I want that clear. I'm doing it because those bastards shot at my camp and wrote your name in the sky like they own the air. Nobody owns the air."

"No," Zephyrine agreed softly. "They just rent it to themselves."

Ruth huffed — almost a laugh — and knocked her cup against Zephyrine's, a small tin toast to nothing, to the dark, to the ridiculous fact of the two of them sitting together on a gravel bar at the top of the world.

Far up the valley, the glacier boomed, long and resonant, and this time both of them heard the interval. Shorter. Definitely shorter.

"Ten days," Zephyrine murmured. "Maybe eight."

"Maybe five," Ruth said. "The mountain keeps its own books."

They sat until the sky in the east went the color of dirty pewter, and then Zephyrine rose, stiff and cold, and began waking the others, because the light was coming whether she was ready or not, and somewhere up that river, in ground that might already be moving, a dead man had buried the truth with her name written all over it.

She was cinching the last strap on the supply load when Dez touched her arm and pointed silently at the southern ridge line, where the dawn was just beginning to catch the rock.

Three figures stood against the brightening sky. They did not wave. They did not move. They simply stood, watching the camp, watching her, and even at that distance Zephyrine understood that they had not come to talk.

The overland party had found them. The countdown had begun.
