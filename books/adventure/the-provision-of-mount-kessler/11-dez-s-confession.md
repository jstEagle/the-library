## 11. Dez's Confession

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Dez finally worked up the courage to open his mouth, and even then what came out first wasn't a confession but a complaint about the coffee.

"Junie's grounds are getting worse," he said, turning his tin cup in his hands. "She scoops straight from the bag without measuring. You can stand a spoon in it."

"Then don't stand a spoon in it," Ruth said from across the flames, not looking up from the net she was mending. Her hands moved with the economy of someone who had spent decades fixing things that wanted to fall apart — nets, engines, other people's silences.

Zephyrine sat on the overturned supply crate she'd claimed as her own somewhere around the second day upstream, her boots off, her feet blistered from the portage, watching him with those gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. Or so he'd always believed. Seven years of believing it. Seven years of building a man out of whiskey bottles and borrowed time who could live under the weight of those eyes.

"Dez." Zephyrine's voice was quiet. The river made its low conversation against the stones behind them, and above the tree line the glacier held its ancient blue light, patient as a creditor. "You've been working up to something since the Narrows. I've watched you rehearse it in your sleep."

He almost laughed. Almost. What came out instead was a sound like a cough, or like something breaking loose after a long winter.

"That obvious?"

"You talk in your sleep," Junie said from her nest of tarps near the boat, where she'd been supposedly asleep for an hour. "Mostly about stratigraphy. But yeah. You've been weird."

"Go back to sleep, kid."

"I'm listening."

He looked at Zephyrine. She didn't rescue him from the silence, and he loved her a little for that, and hated her a little too, because a kinder woman would have let him drown in it another night.

* * *

His name had been Desmond Achebe Okonkwo once, before the drinking wore the syllables smooth, before everyone up and down the coast shortened him to Dez as if he were a tool rather than a man. He'd had a doctorate once. He kept the diploma in a storage unit in Coldbank he hadn't visited in four years, behind a dead man's canoe and a box of his mother's dishes, because throwing it away would have been an admission and keeping it was a wound he could visit when he wanted to punish himself properly.

Seven years ago he had been thirty-one years old and hungry in the way young geologists are hungry — not for money exactly, though there was never enough of that, but for *significance*. For a name on a discovery. For the moment when the rock finally told you its secret and you got to be the one who carried it down out of the mountains into the light.

Kessler Source had been that moment. He'd found it. That was the part nobody knew, the part that had eaten him alive in the dark hours ever since: the ore body at the headwaters was *his*. His mapping, his assays, his three summers of grubbing through scree and muskeg while the syndicate's money paid for his beans and his bitterness. He had stood on the moraine below the icefall with a sample bag in each hand and felt the particular joy that only comes once in a working life, and he had thought: *this is mine, this will always be mine, no one can take this from me.*

He'd been wrong about that. They took it in eleven days.

"The assay numbers," he said now, to the fire, because he couldn't look at any of them. "The ones in the final survey report. The ones that got Zephyrine convicted. I ran them myself. And then I signed the report that said different."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the water an owl asked its question and got no answer.

"The real samples showed the vein pinching out below the icefall. Marginal ore. Maybe break-even, maybe not, with the access costs the glacier would demand every single year. It was honest work and an honest answer, and the answer was *no*, not worth it." He turned the cup again. His hands weren't steady; they hadn't been steady in years, but tonight they were worse. "That wasn't the answer they'd already sold to their investors. So they sent a man to my hotel room in Coldbank. Polite fellow. Good suit. He laid out two futures for me on the bed like shirts. In one, I signed the amended report with the inflated numbers, and I got a consulting contract and a quiet life. In the other—" He stopped. Swallowed. "In the other, my sister lost her teaching job, my mother lost her house, and I lost everything, and the numbers went in anyway with somebody else's signature. They just preferred mine. It looked better. A PhD looks credible in court."

"And you signed," Ruth said. Not cruelly. Just stating the geography.

"I signed." The word came out of him like a tooth coming out, root and all, seven years of pressure behind it. "And then the audit came, and the discrepancies came, and they needed someone to hang it on, and they picked the pilot who'd flown the sample runs. Marchetti. Because she was alone up here, because she had no family to make noise, because—" His voice cracked and he let it. "Because I told them she was careful. I told them she logged everything. I told them where she kept her copies. God help me, I sat in that office and I helped them build the case, because they said it would only be fraud charges, civil, a fine, and I believed them because believing them was easier than the alternative, which was admitting what I'd already done."

Junie had gone very still in her tarps. Ruth's mending needle had stopped moving.

"They gave her seven years," Dez said. "She got out in four because of overcrowding, and then she couldn't get work flying, couldn't get bonded, couldn't do anything except run medicine into places where nobody asks questions, and every mile of that has been on me. Every cold night. Every fuel chit she signs with a fake name. That's mine. I did that. I've known where she was the whole time — I used to read the bush telegraph, the pilot gossip, looking for her name, hating myself, telling myself someday I'd fix it, and someday kept being tomorrow, and tomorrow kept being the bottom of a bottle." He finally raised his eyes to Zephyrine. "I drank myself numb for seven years because numb was the only place I could stand to live. And then Wrenn's map fell into your hands, and I heard you were going upriver, and I came along because—" He laughed, wet and ugly. "Because I figured if the ledger's up there, maybe I could steal it and burn it and keep my name clean. That was the plan. That was the actual plan, Zeph. First night out, I lay awake figuring how to do it."

The silence went on so long that the river seemed loud as traffic.

"So burn it," Zephyrine said.

He blinked. "What?"

"If that's still the plan. If that's who you are. There's the fire. The cache copy is in the green dry-bag under my tarp. Go on." She didn't move. Didn't raise her voice. "I'm not going to stop you. I'm not going to say a word. But understand what you'd be burning. Not paper. Tobias Wrenn spent his last months crawling around that camp on a broken hip to hide those pages. My father died looking for them. So if you're going to burn it, look me in the eyes while you do it, and then walk back downriver alone, because I don't travel with thieves."

"Zeph—" Ruth started.

"No. He gets to choose. We all get to choose. That's the whole point of what we're doing." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight carving her face into planes and shadow. "Here's what I know about you, Dez. I know you read rock better than anyone I've ever met. I know you haven't touched a bottle in nine days, and I know what that costs, because I watched my father fight the same war. I know you checked Junie's bootlaces every morning on the portage without being asked. I know you stayed up two nights ago recalculating our fuel margins until they were right. People are not their worst hour, Dez. I would be a hypocrite to believe that, wouldn't I? Given what my worst hour cost me."

"I forged your—"

"I know what you did. All of it. You just told us yourself, which is more than Voss or any of them ever did." She sat back. "So here's how it works up here. There's no magistrate within four hundred miles. No jury. No law worth the name between here and Coldbank. Which means we're the law, right now, this fire, these five people. And the law we keep is simple: you tell the truth, you carry your share, you don't steal from the crew, and you spend the rest of the trip making it right. That's the sentence. Not seven years. Just — the rest of the trip. Starting tomorrow morning, when you take first watch and Junie teaches you the carburetor, because she's been carrying the engine work alone and that's not fair to her."

Junie made a small sound that might have been protest and was mostly astonishment.

"That's it?" Dez heard his own voice as if from far away. "That's — you're not — I handed you the man who destroyed your life and you're giving me watch duty?"

"I'm giving you a chance to be useful," Zephyrine said. "Which is more than the courts ever gave either of us." She stood, stiff-legged, and gathered her cup. "Justice isn't a thing they do to you in a building, Dez. I learned that the hard way. It's a thing you build with the people willing to stand in the cold with you. Tribunal's adjourned."

* * *

He didn't sleep. None of them really did, though they pretended, the way crews do, each private fire banked down to coals behind closed eyes.

Around what he guessed was two in the morning, Ruth rose to check the boat lines and found him sitting up. She crouched beside him in the dark, and for a while neither spoke. The glacier groaned out on the water — a long, deep sound, like a door opening in a house older than houses.

"My father left on a Tuesday," she said, apropos of nothing, apropos of everything. "I was nineteen. He promised he'd send for me by spring." A pause. "I decided for a long time that some things can't be forgiven. Then I rowed this river long enough to learn the difference between forgiving and understanding. Understanding's harder. It doesn't let you off. It just lets you keep going."

"Is that what she's doing?" Dez asked. "Understanding me?"

"She's doing what she does. She's flying through weather other pilots won't look at, because turning back isn't in her." Ruth squeezed his shoulder once, hard, a grip like a cleat hitch, and went back to her bedroll.

Near dawn, Junie appeared at his elbow with two cups of coffee — measured properly, he noticed, dark and strong and honest — and sat down next to him without asking, the way kids do when they've decided something and see no reason to announce it.

"You talked in your sleep again," she said. "But not about rocks this time."

"Oh?"

"You said *I'm sorry* about forty times. Different names each time." She blew on her coffee. "For what it's worth, the forty-first one should probably be to me. You drank the last of the real sugar on Tuesday and blamed the river."

Despite himself, despite everything, Dez laughed — a cracked, rusty sound, the first in seven years that didn't taste like ash. Above the tree line the sky was going pale at the edges, and the glacier caught the first light and held it, blue and enormous and indifferent, and somewhere beyond that ice, two days' hard hike away, the sealed camp waited with its buried truth.

And waiting with it, Dez understood suddenly and completely, was Voss — because the syndicate's floatplane had circled twice yesterday, low and slow, and Zephyrine had seen it too, and said nothing, saving it for morning.

"We need to move today," he said. "Don't we."

Zephyrine was already standing, already lacing her boots, already the pilot again. "Yeah," she said. "We do."
