## 12. The Tributary Trap

Junie felt the engine die before she heard it.

That was the thing nobody understood about machines, the thing she'd been trying to explain to adults since she was nine years old and rebuilt her first carburetor on a kitchen table scattered with her mother's unpaid bills: engines talked. Not in words. In rhythm. A healthy engine had a heartbeat you could feel through the deck plates, up through your boots, into the bones of your feet, and Junie had spent enough hours lying against the housing of the *Kestrel*'s old Gray Marine to know its heart the way you knew your own — the way it stumbled half a beat when cold, the way it smoothed out at cruising throttle like a dog settling down.

So when the rhythm broke, somewhere above the junction where the Kessler met the gray-green slash of the Alder tributary, she was already moving before Zephyrine cut the throttle and the boat began to drift.

"Junie." Zephyrine's voice came back from the wheel, level, but Junie had learned to hear the wire under it. "Tell me."

"It's not the fuel line." She was already kneeling at the housing, wrench already in her hand because it was always in her hand now; Dez had started calling her Wrench instead of Junie and she pretended to hate it. "Fuel line's fine. I checked it this morning."

"You checked it this morning," Ruth repeated from the bow, where she stood with one boot up on the gunwale, scanning the water the way she always scanned water, like it owed her money.

"I check everything every morning." Junie popped the access panel. The smell hit her first — wrong, chemical-sweet under the diesel, and thin. Too thin. "Somebody's been in here."

The boat went quiet in the way boats go quiet, which is louder than any noise. Four people breathing. The river talking to itself around the hull. Somewhere upstream, the glacier cracking its knuckles, that deep artillery boom that had become so constant Junie barely heard it anymore except at night, when it got into her dreams and rearranged them.

Zephyrine came aft. She didn't crowd — that was a thing about her, she never crowded, she stood at the exact distance where she could see what you were doing without making you feel watched, and Junie had decided somewhere over the last week that this was probably a pilot thing, or maybe just a person who'd spent seven years being hunted learning how to give other people room. "Show me."

Junie pointed with the wrench. "Sediment in the filter bowl. That's not river sediment, that's *fine* sediment, that's somebody dumped something in my tank." She pulled the bowl free and held it up. The fuel inside was cloudy, gritted with a pale powder that caught the light like ground bone. "Sugar, maybe. Or that stuff they use for dust control. Either way, it's in the lines by now, it's in the injectors, and if I run it hard it scores the cylinders and then we don't have an engine, we have a very expensive anchor."

"When?" Dez asked. He'd come up from the cargo hold where he'd been cataloguing ore samples, and his hands were shaking the way they always shook until noon, until the coffee and the work burned it off him. "We anchored at the bend last night. Nobody came near us."

"Somebody came near us." Ruth's voice had gone flat and cold, and Junie looked up to see her staring across the water at the mouth of the Alder, where a gravel bar ran out into the confluence like a tongue. There were tracks in it. Boot tracks, two sets, big ones, coming down to the waterline and going away again. "While we slept. While I had first watch." Her jaw worked. "I had first watch."

"You didn't do anything wrong," Zephyrine said.

"I had *first watch*, Marchetti."

Junie kept her head down over the engine, but she listened the way she always listened, cataloguing, filing, because that was what you did when you'd been living on your own since fourteen: you learned the weather of the people around you, and right now the weather off Ruth Wrenn was the kind that preceded something breaking.

"We're not doing this on the water," Zephyrine said. "Ruth, put us in on that bar. Junie, tell me what you need."

What Junie needed, as it turned out, was everything.

* * *

They beached the *Kestrel* on the gravel bar in the long slanting afternoon light, and Junie laid out her tools on a tarp with the care of a surgeon, and then she opened the tank and understood exactly how much trouble they were in.

The contaminant hadn't just sat in the bottom. Somebody had put it in while the fuel was sloshing, at the bend, in the dark, which meant they'd waded out to a boat with a woman awake on watch, or believed themselves unseen, or — worse, and Junie's stomach went tight thinking it — had known the watch schedule. Which meant they'd been seen before. Which meant the patrol boat that had boarded them at the Narrows, the one with the inspector and his camera and his polite questions, had told somebody exactly where they were and exactly who they were, and the somebody had acted.

She drained forty gallons into jerry cans, and the last of it came out gray.

"That's half our fuel," Dez said quietly. He'd done the math faster than anyone, because of course he had, geologists counted things. "Half of what we need to get back downriver even if we find nothing."

"Then we find something," Zephyrine said, "and we worry about getting back after."

It was such a Zephyrine sentence that Junie almost smiled. That was the thing about her — everyone else on this boat treated the future like a wall they kept walking into, and Zephyrine treated it like weather. You didn't argue with weather. You dressed for it.

But dressing for it took parts they didn't have, and this was where the day turned, though Junie wouldn't understand until later how completely it turned.

The filter elements were fouled past saving. The injectors needed flushing. The tank needed scrubbing, which meant someone small enough to actually get inside it, and there was exactly one person on that gravel bar built like a whippet.

"I'll do the tank," Junie said, before anyone could arrange her life for her. "But I need solvent, and I need new filter elements, and nobody's carrying those."

There was a silence. Then Dez said, slowly, "The Alder. There was a placer camp on the lower Alder, ten years ago. It failed, but the equipment sheds might still—" He stopped himself, the way he stopped himself from most sentences that contained hope. "It's a long shot."

"Long shots are the only shots we've got," Ruth said.

They took the canoe — the little folding one lashed to the roof, the one Junie had privately considered dead weight since Coldbank — and paddled a mile up the Alder through water the color of bottle glass, and found the placer camp exactly as promised: three collapsed sheds and a rusted hoist and, in the second shed, under a tarp of mouse-chewed canvas, a drum of mineral spirits gone thick at the top but good underneath, and a crate of filter elements, brass-cased, still wrapped in wax paper, packed by a company that had probably gone under before Junie was born.

Dez stood in that shed holding a filter element like it was a communion wafer, and Junie saw something move across his face that she'd never seen there before, and it took her a moment to name it. It was relief. It was a man who had spent seven years believing the world only ever took things finding, for once, that it gave.

"Good eye," she told him, and he flushed like a boy.

* * *

The work took all of the next day and most of the night, because the tank had to come off, and the tank weighed what a tank weighs, and the bolts had rusted into a language only persuasion could read.

Junie went into the tank twice. It was exactly as bad as it sounds — knees against baffles, arms overhead, scraper in hand, breathing through a rag soaked in spirits, the whole world reduced to the sound of her own pulse and the rasp of the blade against crusted residue. But it was also, she would admit to no one, ever, the happiest she'd been in months. Because inside a fuel tank there was no past and no future. There was only the surface in front of you and whether it was clean.

When she came out the filler neck the second time, gasping, hair plastered to her skull, the light had gone gold and low, and Zephyrine was sitting on the gravel beside the tarp with a wrench in her hands, turning it over, watching.

"How's it look in there?"

"Like somebody's sins." Junie hauled herself upright. "Clean now, though."

Zephyrine nodded. For a while she didn't say anything, and Junie, who had gotten good at silences, let this one sit. Up the river the glacier boomed, twice, close together, like something impatient.

"They knew our watch schedule," Zephyrine said finally. It wasn't a question.

"Yeah."

"And you figured that out in about four minutes, and you didn't say it in front of Ruth."

Junie wiped her hands on the rag, slower than she needed to. "Ruth already knows. She's just mad at herself about it. Didn't seem useful to pile on."

Zephyrine looked at her then — really looked, the way she rarely did, straight on, and Junie felt it like a searchlight and made herself not flinch. "You're sixteen years old," Zephyrine said. "How do you know how to do that?"

"Do what."

"Take care of people without them noticing."

Junie shrugged, and hated herself for shrugging, because it was a kid's answer and she wasn't giving kid answers anymore, not on this river. "Grew up around people you couldn't say true things to," she said. "You learn to say them sideways."

Something crossed Zephyrine's face then, quick and deep, some current under ice, and she reached over and squeezed Junie's shoulder — one squeeze, brief, callused hand — and said, "Get some sleep. I've got the pump till midnight."

And Junie lay in her bedroll under the overturned canoe and stared at the stars through the gap in the gunwales and thought: *that's the first time an adult has touched me on purpose in eight months.* Then she thought: *don't be stupid, it's just a shoulder,* and then she thought about the squeeze again anyway, and slept better than she had since she'd run away from her uncle's shop in Coldbank with a duffel bag and a grudge.

* * *

The engine caught on the third try, at dawn, and the sound it made was clean.

Junie stood at the housing with her hand flat against it, feeling the heartbeat come back — steady, smooth, the stumble gone, the grit gone, everything flushed and fresh and hers — and she was so full of something that she couldn't look at anybody, because if she looked at anybody she was going to cry, and she had a reputation.

"Sounds good," Ruth said, from the wheel, and her voice was rougher than usual, and Junie understood that Ruth knew too, the way women who'd fixed things their whole lives always knew.

"Sounds better than good," Junie managed. "Sounds factory."

They loaded the jerry cans of strained, filtered, triple-decanted fuel — half of what they'd carried, Dez confirmed grimly, doing his counting — and pushed off the gravel bar into the morning mist, and the Kessler took them back the way a road takes back a traveler, without comment.

But something had changed on that bar, and Junie felt it in how they moved around each other. Ruth handed her tools now without being asked, reading ahead of her. Dez had taken to sitting near her in the evenings with his notebook open, explaining rock the way she explained engines, two languages for the same religion. And Zephyrine —

Zephyrine had started saying her name. Not Wrench. *Junie.* Just in passing, just in the ordinary traffic of the boat — *Junie, take the helm a minute; Junie, what do you think?* — and each time it landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed warm.

On the second morning after the bar, running a narrow green reach between walls of black slate, Zephyrine handed her the wheel and went forward to read the water with Ruth, and Junie steered the boat alone, both hands on the wheel, sixteen years old, trusted with the whole weight of everybody's lives, and she caught herself grinning at the river like an idiot and let herself do it, because there was no one behind her to see, and no one ahead who'd take it away.

That was when Dez, up at the bow, went rigid and pointed.

Smoke. Thin, deliberate, a single column rising off the far bank where no camp should be — too neat, too alone, too much like a signal. And below it, tied to a snag, riding low and patient in the current, a boat. Dark hull. Outboard tilted. Empty.

Waiting.

"Junie," Zephyrine said quietly, without turning around, and her voice had the wire in it again, the wire and something under the wire, "how fast can this engine go?"
