## 14. Whiteout

The sky had been lying to them all morning.

Zephyrine had learned to read weather the way other people read faces — the sullen bruise of cloud over a ridge, the way wind went quiet before it turned mean — and everything about the ceiling pressing down on the Kessler headwaters said *wait*. But waiting was a currency they no longer had. The glacier had been calving since dawn, distant reports like artillery, and every hour the ice corridor at the source narrowed was an hour Voss's men gained on them.

So she flew anyway.

The Beaver climbed out of Kessler Bend heavy with fuel drums and gear, Junie wedged in the back between Ruth and Dez with her chin nearly on her knees, the lake water still dripping off the floats as they broke free of it. Below them the river unspooled silver through a country that had never once been kind to anyone who loved it. Zephyrine loved it anyway. That was the whole trouble with her.

"Ceiling's dropping," Dez said from the right seat. He'd been watching the ridgeline to the north for twenty minutes without blinking, his bad hand braced against the frame. "That front off the icefield. It's not going around us."

"It's going through us," Zephyrine said.

"You could put down. Wait it out."

"Where?" She tipped her head at the terrain scrolling beneath them — granite and spruce and white water, none of it interested in holding a plane overnight. "There's no strip inside forty miles that isn't a boulder field. We push for the lake above the moraine. It's wide enough to land blind if I have to."

"And if you don't have to?"

"Then we're two hours closer and I buy everyone dinner in Coldbank when this is over."

Junie snorted from behind her. "You've been promising dinner since the Narrows."

"I keep my promises," Zephyrine said, and felt Ruth's eyes on the back of her neck like a hand. She kept her promises. That was how all of this had started — a dying man's packet, a daughter's fury, a map inked in a shaking hand. Every mile upstream was a promise being kept, and promises, she was learning, were heavier than cargo.

The front came off the icefield the way a wave comes off the open sea: all at once, and then everywhere.

One moment they were skimming under a gray ceiling a thousand feet thick; the next the world simply ended. Snow came sideways out of nothing, fat and wet and blinding, and the wind hit the Beaver like a shoulder slamming a door. The plane bucked, dropped fifty feet in a breath, caught itself with a groan of airframe that Zephyrine felt in her teeth.

"Straps!" she shouted over the engine. "Tighten everything!"

She couldn't see the ridge. She couldn't see the river. She couldn't see her own wingtips. There was only white, churning and alive, and the compass card swinging, and the altimeter unwinding in her gut. Somewhere below was the glacial lake she'd marked on Wrenn's map — flat water, half a mile across, ringed by moraine. Somewhere below was also a wall of granite that would turn the Beaver into scrap and five people into a story told badly in Coldbank bars.

She'd flown blind before. Seven years of medicine runs and mail drops into country where weather didn't negotiate — she'd shot approaches through passes with fog so thick she'd landed by counting seconds and trusting her memory of a shoreline. But always there'd been something. A radio beacon. A break in the murk. A reason to go around.

Now there was only the lake, and the window closing, and the choice she'd already made somewhere back over the Bend without admitting it.

"Zeph." Dez's voice, very calm, the calm of a man who had been in aircraft coming apart before. "Talk to me."

"Lake's at two thousand feet. Moraine on the south shore — I'll come in low over the ice tongue, flare over the beach." Her hands were steady. She made herself notice that. Made it a fact she could stand on. "Everyone brace. If we hit hard, hit bent, not straight."

"What does that even mean?" Junie's voice, thin and furious with fear.

"It means trust her," Ruth said quietly. And that — Ruth Wrenn, who trusted nothing and no one, saying *trust her* in a voice like a hand on a shoulder — that was the thing Zephyrine carried down through the white.

She throttled back. Dropped the flaps. Let the sink take her.

Flying blind onto unknown water is not flying. It is a controlled argument with gravity, conducted entirely by feel — the buffeting of wind on the tail telling you where the gusts live, the sound of the floats' struts in the slipstream, the seat-of-the-pants knowledge of descent rate that no instrument can give you and every bush pilot has bled for. Zephyrine closed her eyes for one full second — one second of pure interiority, of the map drawn on the inside of her skull — and opened them onto nothing, and held her line.

The altimeter spun down through five hundred feet. Three hundred. The snow thickened until it wasn't weather anymore but a place, a white country with its own rules.

Two hundred.

The wind gusted from the left and she corrected, and corrected again, and then — through the roar — she heard it. Water. Not rain. The flat, vast silence-with-sound of a big lake breathing against a shore.

"One hundred feet," she said aloud, though no one needed the number. "Brace."

The floats touched.

They touched hard — harder than she liked, the impact jarring up through the airframe with a crack that did not sound like water alone — and the plane slewed left, dug, plowed a rooster tail of spray that froze midair and came back down as hail against the windows. Zephyrine fought the rudders, full opposite, engine screaming, and for three endless seconds the Beaver tried to cartwheel and she argued it out of the idea, and then they were planing, slowing, sliding across black water into stillness, rocking gently in the white silence like a leaf settling.

The engine ticked. The wind moaned over the wings. Nobody spoke.

"Everyone account for themselves," Zephyrine said finally. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"Alive," said Dez.

"Alive," said Ruth.

"Alive and never doing that again," said Junie, "except probably I will, because apparently that's just what happens now."

Zephyrine ran up the engine, tested both mags, and heard it — a new note in the music, a metallic wrongness underneath. She shut it down and sat for a moment with her hands on the yoke, listening to the wind, doing arithmetic she didn't want to do.

* * *

The damage announced itself the moment she waded out onto the float in driving snow, one hand on the strut, water slopping cold over her boots. The left float had taken the landing's anger personally: a seam split along a third of its length, a strut fitting bent like a broken finger, and — when she hauled herself up to look at the wing root — a wrinkle in the leading edge skin where the gear had flexed beyond its arguments. Nothing catastrophic. Everything disqualifying.

She stood in the freezing spray and let herself feel it for exactly ten seconds. She counted them. Then she climbed back into the cabin, dripping, and told the truth, because these four deserved truth even when it tasted like ash.

"We're not flying out of here."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The snow ticked against the windows. Outside, the world was a bowl of milk.

"How bad?" Ruth asked.

"Float's split. Strut's bent. Maybe fixable with what Junie can fabricate and what's in the survival kit — maybe. But even fixed, I'm not taking off from a lake I can't see the ends of, in a front that hasn't finished arriving, with a load of gear and no margin." She pulled off her gloves and flexed her stiff fingers. "And even if I would, the window won't wait. The calving's early. We've got days, not weeks. Maybe not even days."

"So we walk," Dez said.

It wasn't a question, and Zephyrine looked at him — at the ruined hand, the hip he favored since the portage, the seven years of drink he was slowly sweating out of his blood at altitude — and hated what she was about to say.

"The source is two days' hike over the moraine. Maybe less if the weather breaks. You don't have to—"

"Stop." He said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "I signed the report that buried you, Zeph. I've spent seven years deciding I was too broken to be worth anything. You want to know what carrying Ruth's share of that portage taught me?" He almost smiled. "Broken is a thing you work with, not a thing you are. I'm coming."

"He's coming," Ruth confirmed, in the tone of a woman who had carried him and would carry him again and would deny both under oath.

"I'm coming," Junie said immediately, "and nobody's leaving me on a frozen lake with a broken airplane, because I'm the only one who knows how to keep it from becoming a *very* broken airplane."

"That's actually true," Zephyrine admitted.

"Also I stowed away once already. I'll do it again. On foot. Somehow."

Zephyrine looked at Ruth last. Ruth met her eyes across the small cold cabin, and whatever passed between them needed no words — the ferry operator knew better than anyone what it cost to be stranded, and what it cost worse to turn back.

"Then we walk," Zephyrine said.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of the overturned canoe lashed atop the gear pile, because the Beaver's cabin was too small to heat and too valuable to risk, and because — though no one said it — sitting in the plane felt too much like sitting in a coffin with a view. Junie rigged the camp stove inside a windbreak of fuel drums and cooked oatmeal with raisins, the luxury of someone who understood that morale lives or dies in a pot. The wind screamed across the lake and found them and gave up and screamed some more.

Zephyrine took first watch, wrapped in a tarp at the edge of the windbreak, watching the white darkening into gray-dark. The lake was freezing at its edges; by morning there would be skim ice, and after that, if the cold held, the plane might be locked in until spring. She let that thought sit in her chest and found, examining it, that it hurt less than she expected. The plane had been her shell for seven years. Shells are for animals that haven't decided yet whether they're staying.

Footsteps in the snow. Ruth lowered herself onto the gear crate beside her without asking, the way she'd begun doing sometime after the portage fire, the way people do when they've decided a boundary is now a shared one.

"Can't sleep either?"

"I sleep fine," Ruth said, which was a lie, and both of them knew it, and neither of them bothered with it. They watched the dark together. Somewhere far north, muffled by miles and storm, the glacier spoke — a long grinding boom, like a door closing in an enormous house.

"It's early," Ruth said. "The calving."

"Yeah."

"How early?"

"Early enough that Wrenn's dates might be wrong. Early enough that Voss doesn't need to catch us. He just needs to wait." Zephyrine turned her hands palm-up in her lap, studying them as if they belonged to someone else. "Every plan I've ever had ended with me alone in a cockpit going somewhere else. This is the first one that ends with me walking toward something. I keep waiting for it to feel like a mistake."

"Does it?"

"No. That's what frightens me."

Ruth was quiet a while. When she spoke, her voice was roughened by the wind and by whatever lived underneath it. "My father used to say the mountains don't test you. They just show you what's already there." She pulled her collar tighter. "He said it the night before he left. Last thing he ever said to me, near enough. I've spent thirty years deciding it was a coward's proverb." A pause. "Standing here, I think maybe he meant it."

Zephyrine didn't answer. There was nothing to say that wouldn't have been smaller than the silence they were sharing. She put another stick of wood on the stove, and Ruth shifted her shoulders to block the wind, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough.

* * *

Dawn came without color — a slow brightening of the gray, the storm down to flurries, the wind dropping to a mutter. The lake wore its new skin of ice like a scab. Zephyrine stood at the waterline and looked south, toward the moraine, toward the pass beyond it, toward the sealed camp where a dead man's cache waited with her name inside it.

Two days' hike. Less, if the weather held and the ground cooperated. Behind them, a plane that might fly again in a week or a month or never. Ahead of them, a glacier eating its own edges ahead of schedule.

Dez emerged from the tents, moving better than he had any right to, and began sorting loads with the methodical greed of a man reading rock cores. Junie stripped the Beaver's emergency kit and quarreled cheerfully with it. Ruth broke camp the way she ran her ferry — without waste, without complaint, without permission asked.

Zephyrine shouldered the pack with the map and the ledger pages sewn into its lining, and paused, and looked back once at the little yellow plane sitting locked in the ice, patient as a dog tied outside a store.

"Hold on," she told it quietly. "We're coming back."

Then she turned south, and the four of them started up the moraine together, five sets of tracks stitching across the snow — and it was Junie, ranging ahead on the broken ridge crest, who stopped first, crouched, and swore softly at something half-buried in the drift.

"Hey," she called back, and her voice had gone strange. "Somebody want to tell me why there's fresh boot prints up here? Two days old, maybe three. Heading the same direction we are."

Zephyrine climbed to her side and looked down at the prints — deep-heeled, military-soled, spaced with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly where they were going.

Voss hadn't waited after all.

Voss was already ahead of them.
