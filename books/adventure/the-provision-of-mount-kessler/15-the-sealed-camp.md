## 15. The Sealed Camp

The camp announced itself before they saw it, the way ruins do — a smell of old creosote and rust, then the pale geometry of tarps gone gray with seven years of weather, sagging between poles like the tents of an army that had marched off and never come back. Zephyrine saw it first from the ridge, and for a long moment she couldn't move.

She had told herself she remembered it. She hadn't. Memory had sanded it smooth, made it small, made it bearable. The real thing was larger and meaner: the collapsed core shed with its roof punched in by snowload, the drill platform furred with lichen, the cook tent's frame standing bare as ribs against the glacier's white shoulder. And there, at the edge of it all, half-swallowed by alder scrub, the survey office where her life had ended — where men in syndicate jackets had put handcuffs on her while the radio crackled with a voice reading charges she still could not recite from memory without her mouth going dry.

"You're doing that thing," Ruth said beside her. "Where you go somewhere else."

"I'm here."

"You're not." Ruth shifted the pack on her shoulders. She'd carried most of Dez's share since the portage and never once mentioned it, which was its own kind of eloquence. "It's just buildings, Marchetti. Buildings don't get to keep you."

Zephyrine almost laughed. Almost. "You've never been convicted of something you didn't do."

"No," Ruth said. "But I've spent twenty years being someone's abandoned daughter. It's the same weather."

They came down through the scrub together, boots crunching on frost-brittle grass, and the others spread out behind them with the wordless competence the last two weeks had built into them. Dez went straight to the drill platform, kneeling to read the rock like scripture, his bad leg stretched out stiff behind him. Junie climbed onto the collapsed core shed and began prying at the roofing with the flat of her knife, hunting salvage, because Junie hunted salvage the way other people breathed. Nobody asked what they were looking for. Everybody knew.

The survey office door was swollen shut. Zephyrine put her shoulder into it three times before it gave with a groan that seemed to come up out of the ground itself, and then she was inside, in air so stale it tasted metallic, in the smell of mouse droppings and mildewed paper and — underneath it all, faint but unmistakable, the way a scar is faint but unmistakable — kerosene and fear.

* * *

She worked by the light through the broken window, methodical because method was all she had. The desk drawers had been emptied years ago; whoever sealed this camp had taken everything with evidentiary weight, or thought they had. Filing cabinets stood open and gutted, folders stripped out, hanging tabs labeled in someone else's tidy hand: CORE LOGS, ASSAY RETURNS, PAYROLL, CORRESPONDENCE. All empty. All empty except —

Except the bottom drawer of the third cabinet didn't open. It opened two inches and stopped, and when Zephyrine knelt and looked into the gap with her head sideways, she saw why: a steel plate welded across the interior runners, deliberate, professional. A drawer built to look emptied and refuse to be.

"Dez," she called, and her voice came out strange. "I need your hands."

He came in wiping rock dust on his thighs, took one look, and understood. They pried together, Dez bracing on his good leg, the cabinet shrieking, until the weld cracked and the drawer slid free with a sound like a held breath released. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with waxed twine, lay a packet the size of a hymnal, and beneath it a tin box rusted nearly solid, and beneath that, flat and gray-green with age, a ledger.

Wrenn's initials were burned into the oilcloth. T.W., in letters deep enough to survive anything short of fire.

Zephyrine sat down on the floor. She didn't decide to; her legs simply declined further service. She unwrapped the packet with fingers that would not stay steady, and the first thing she found was a letter, the paper soft as cloth, addressed in Wrenn's cramped, upright script:

*To whoever finds this and has the guts to carry it down.*

Inside the letter, folded around each other like nesting birds: assay certificates in the syndicate's own letterhead, dated the spring of the year everything happened. Originals. Not copies — originals, with the lab chemist's actual signature, showing ore values from the Kessler source that ran four times richer than anything the syndicate had ever reported. And clipped behind them, a page torn from a field ledger in Wrenn's hand, itemizing sample numbers, dates, locations — and beside each entry, in red pencil, the same notation repeated like a drumbeat: *reported value one-quarter actual. Ordered by V. Confirmed by D.A.*

D.A. Dez Ardoin. She knew it before she finished the initials, had known it since the Narrows, maybe since the day she'd watched him limp up her dock and pretend he didn't know her name. But seeing it in ink, in Wrenn's hand, dated — that was different. That was a man's signature on his own damnation, preserved in a dead prospector's cache for seven years, waiting.

And under everything, the last fold of paper, the one that stopped her heart and then restarted it wrong: a carbon copy of the incident report. The one that named her. The original bore corrections — strike-throughs, marginal notes, a second draft in different ink where the sequence of events had been rewritten, where the missing dynamite had been moved from a locked magazine nobody had opened to a footlocker assigned to her care. Someone had drafted the truth first and then written the lie over it, and both drafts had survived, side by side, in the same file, like a confession hiding inside an accusation.

"They didn't even burn it," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away, underwater. "They wrote it down. Both times. They wrote down what they did."

Dez crouched in the doorway, and she watched him read upside down, watch the color leave his face in stages, like a sunset running backward. "That's my hand," he said quietly. "On the second page. I countersigned the revised report." He swallowed. "I told myself it was the sample numbers I was signing. Just the numbers."

"It was me," Zephyrine said. "It was me you were signing."

"I know." He didn't flinch from it, and she gave him that. Seven years too late, but he didn't flinch. "I know exactly what it was."

Outside, Junie whooped over some piece of salvaged copper, and the sound fell into the ruined office and died there, absurd and bright, from another world.

* * *

They laid it all out on the drill platform in the late light, weighted at the corners with stones, and the crew stood around it in a silence that had weight and texture. The ledger. The assays. The report with its two faces. Correspondence — a dozen letters between syndicate directors discussing, in the bland vocabulary of men who never imagine themselves villains, the *necessity of a demonstrable security breach* and the *unfortunate availability of the Marchetti woman*, whose record, one writer noted, *presents a credible subject*. Credible. That was the word they'd used about her. She read it twice and felt nothing, and then felt the nothing itself like a wound.

"So that's it," Ruth said finally. "That's what he carried out of here." She was looking not at the papers but at the burned initials on the oilcloth. "He hauled this over the divide alone. Old man, bad heart, winter coming on. And then he sat on it for seven years because he couldn't make himself walk into Coldbank and face you." Her jaw worked. "Face me. Because I'm in it too, isn't that right? He couldn't deliver it because delivering it meant admitting what he'd let happen to you while he kept his claim staked and his mouth shut."

"He was dying," Dez said. "Men get brave at the end. It doesn't count less, but it comes cheap."

"It counts," Zephyrine said. She surprised herself saying it. "It counts however it arrives."

She should have felt triumphant. She had rehearsed this moment for seven years, in fuel shacks and rented rooms and the long hollow hours between waypoints: the moment the proof lay in front of her, plain as daylight, undeniable. In the rehearsal there was always shouting, always vindication like a struck bell, always the fantasy of marching back into a courtroom with the truth held overhead like a lantern.

What she actually felt was tired. The papers lay on the stones, and they were only paper. The people who wrote them were rich and distant and mostly dead or retired, and the girl they'd destroyed with a paragraph had spent seven years learning to sleep in her boots and trust no lock but her own hands. You couldn't hand that back. There was no restitution column for it. The bell in her rehearsal had been brass and glorious, and the reality was a handful of yellowed pages going soft in the wind, and she stood over them feeling strangely, terribly hollow, the way you feel at the summit of a mountain you climbed to escape something and find only more sky.

Ruth must have seen it, because Ruth always saw it — that was the terrible gift of her, the ferry-woman's eye that read water and women both. She crossed the platform, and without ceremony, without asking, she put her hand on Zephyrine's shoulder. It was a work-roughened hand, cold from the evening air, heavy as an anchor.

"There," Ruth said, not softly, not gently — Ruth did not do gentle, she did true. "Feel that? That's real. The paper's just paper. This is the part that counts. You didn't carry it up here alone, and you're not carrying it down alone either. Whatever happens next — magistrate, courtroom, syndicate lawyers, all of it — that's weather. We're the boat."

Zephyrine looked at her. The evening light came low off the glacier and turned them both the color of old gold, and somewhere behind them Junie was telling Dez, with enormous authority, that the camp's copper wire was worth saving and he was standing on it. And Zephyrine felt the hollowness fill — not with triumph, nothing so clean as triumph, but with something better and harder to name: the sense of standing on ground that held. Of a debt seven years old suddenly shared five ways. Of a hand on her shoulder that weighed more than the whole ledger.

"Okay," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and she let it. "Okay. Then we take it down together."

They were wrapping the cache in its oilcloth when Junie, up on the ridge checking their backtrail out of habit now instead of orders, went very still. Zephyrine knew that stillness. She'd taught it to the girl herself: *when something's wrong, be a stone.*

"Zeph," Junie said, and her voice had dropped its bravado entirely. "There's a boat coming up the river. Two boats. No lights."

Downriver, around the black shoulder of the bend, engines — muffled, throttled low, but unmistakably engines, and climbing toward them against the current through the last of the light.
