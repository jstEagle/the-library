## 16. The Syndicate Arrives

Ruth Wrenn had spent enough years on the water to know the sound a river made when it was lying to you. The Kessler was doing it now—running smooth and silver in the late light, chattering over its stones like nothing upstream had changed, like the glacier hadn't been shedding its skin all week, like the four of them weren't camped at the mouth of a valley that had swallowed her father whole.

She was mending the portage strap when Dez stopped talking mid-sentence.

That was how she knew. Dez never stopped talking. He narrated everything—the rock strata, his own bad knee, the merits of dried apricots versus fresh—and so when his voice simply cut off and his head came up like a bird dog's, Ruth's hands went still on the leather before her mind caught up.

"What," she said.

"Smoke." He tilted his chin toward the western ridge. "Two points of it. Moving."

Junie was already scrambling up the bank with the binoculars, and Zephyrine rose from the fire where she'd been going through the ledger page by page, committing it to memory the way Ruth suspected she committed everything—deliberately, as if memory were a thing that could be defended in court.

Ruth stood too, and the river seemed to go quiet around her, or maybe that was just her own blood in her ears.

*They tracked us,* she thought. *From the Narrows. All this way.*

*We led them straight to it.*

* * *

They had forty minutes, maybe less, and they spent them badly and well at once. Zephyrine divided the ledger into three bundles—one for Junie to cache in the plane's tail section, one sewn into Ruth's coat lining with fishing line and shaking fingers, one that Zephyrine herself would carry, because, as she put it with a flatness that fooled no one, if they took her they should get what they came for. The original correspondence went into a waterproof bag buried under the fire ring's cold ashes, marked by a stone only Dez would recognize as moved.

"You could run," Ruth said, working the needle through canvas. "The plane's patched enough. You and Junie could be over the divide before dark."

"And leave you two on foot?" Zephyrine didn't look up from the ledger. "No."

"That's not an answer, that's stubbornness."

"It's both." A ghost of a smile crossed her face and vanished. "My father ran once. From something smaller than this. I've read his letters now—I know what it cost him. I'm done running from things I haven't done."

Ruth wanted to say something to that—something about fathers, about running, about the letter still folded unread in her breast pocket where it had been for eleven days—but the words snagged the way words always did between them, two women who communicated better in work than in speech. So she just pulled the stitch tight and said, "Then we stand together."

Zephyrine looked at her then, really looked, and Ruth saw something in her face she'd only seen once before, at the fire after the portage: the expression of a woman discovering she had been alone so long she'd forgotten what company weighed.

"They'll want to talk first," Zephyrine said. "Men like this always want to talk. It's cheaper than shooting."

* * *

They came down the western slope at dusk, six of them, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told no by the landscape. Voss walked in front. Ruth had seen him only in glimpses—at Coldbank, years ago, standing on the syndicate dock in a coat too good for the country; and once, terribly, in a photograph among her father's things, younger, smiling, arm slung around Tobias Wrenn's shoulders like a brother's.

He was not smiling now. He was a broad, gray-templed man with the patient face of a butcher, and he stopped twenty feet from their fire as though an invisible line had been drawn there, and behind him his men fanned out with the ease of long practice until Ruth understood, with a cold settling in her stomach, that the line wasn't invisible at all. It was a circle. They were inside it.

"Miss Marchetti." Voss's voice was mild. Almost kind. That was the worst part—that he sounded like a man discussing freight rates. "You've made excellent time. Better than we forecast."

"You've been following us since the Narrows," Zephyrine said.

"Since Coldbank, actually. But the Narrows is when we knew you'd found something worth carrying." He let his gaze travel over the camp—the plane with its storm-battered wing, the boat hauled above the waterline, Junie standing rigid by the tail with her arms crossed like a much smaller, much angrier version of a fortress. "May I sit? My knees are fifty-four years old and this climb was unkind to them."

"Stand," Zephyrine said.

"All right." He clasped his hands behind his back, a man settling in for a sermon. "I'm going to make you an offer, and I'd ask you to hear all of it before you decide anything. Not for my sake. For yours."

Ruth shifted closer to Zephyrine without meaning to. She felt Dez do the same on the other side, his bad leg braced, and she thought: *we've formed a line of our own, we just didn't plan it.* Three people and a teenager, shoulder to shoulder against six armed men, and somehow it didn't feel absurd. It felt inevitable. It felt like the portage, like the whiteout, like every hard mile since Coldbank—they had been practicing for this the whole way up the river without knowing it.

"The survey of '17," Voss began, "was fraudulent. We both know it. The assay figures were altered, the chain of custody was fabricated, and a pilot with no mining background was convicted on evidence she couldn't possibly have produced. I signed off on none of it personally, but I knew. I've known for seven years." He paused, and Ruth searched his face for shame and found something worse: accounting. "The syndicate has changed hands twice since then. The men who built that lie are dead or retired. The current board knows the source exists and knows it must stay sealed—not because of what's in the ground, but because of what's in the paper. What you're carrying."

"The ledger," Zephyrine said.

"The ledger. Correspondence. Enough to void your conviction, yes, but also enough to void eleven years of claims, three hundred jobs, a territorial charter, and the livelihoods of every town downstream that survives on syndicate payroll. Including," and here his eyes flicked to Ruth, deliberate, surgical, "Coldbank. Your ferry contract, Miss Wrenn. Syndicate-subsidized. Did you know?"

"I know exactly what pays for my engine repairs," Ruth said evenly.

"Good. Then you understand this isn't villainy. It's arithmetic." He turned back to Zephyrine, and now his voice dropped into something almost gentle, and Ruth hated him for how well he did it. "Here is the offer. Burn the ledger—all of it, here, tonight, where the ash can't testify. In exchange: the conviction is quietly vacated within the month. Not pardoned—*vacated*, expunged, as though it never happened. Your license restored. Your name clean. A syndicate retainer, if you want it—bush contract, medicine runs, whatever routes you like. And passage out for your companions, no charges, no questions, no photographs in anyone's files." He spread his hands. "Seven years ago they took your life. I'm offering to give it back. All you have to do is hand me the match."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for Ruth to count her own heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

She watched Zephyrine's face, and what frightened her was not that it showed temptation—it was that it showed *recognition*. This was the thing Zephyrine had wanted for seven years; Ruth could see that plainly, the way you can see water in a desert man's eyes. Her name, clean. Her license. The sky given back to her, legally, permanently, with paperwork instead of exile. Every night of those seven years, sleeping in a different hangar, flying under borrowed registrations, flinching at her own surname—Voss was offering to erase all of it with one fire.

And he'd timed it perfectly. They were exhausted. The window was closing. The plane was damaged. Even if they reached Coldbank with the ledger, there was no guarantee any magistrate was beyond syndicate reach, no guarantee the paper would hold, no guarantee of anything except more miles and more risk.

"Say your piece," Zephyrine said finally, "and then I'll say mine."

"I've said my piece."

"No. You've said the syndicate's piece. Now say yours." Zephyrine stepped forward, out of their little line, alone, and Ruth's hands curled into fists at her sides. "You knew for seven years, Voss. You knew the survey was false and you did nothing. Why come upriver now? Why offer me anything? Men like you don't spend syndicate money on mercy."

Something moved behind Voss's mildness—a hairline crack, and Ruth, who had spent a lifetime reading faces across ferry decks in bad weather, saw it clearly. "Because the men who framed you are gone," he said, "and I am tired of carrying what they left. Because I have a daughter who asks why I don't sleep." He paused. "And because if you burn that ledger, I stop being the man who knew. That's my piece. That's the truth of it. I'm offering you absolution because I want some of my own, and I can't afford mine unless you sell me yours."

The honesty of it landed harder than any threat. Ruth heard Junie exhale sharply behind her.

Zephyrine was quiet a long moment. Then she said, "Thank you. That's the first honest sentence anyone from the syndicate has said to me since 1917." She crouched, slowly, deliberately, and picked up a stick from the woodpile, and every one of Voss's men shifted, hands drifting toward coats, and Ruth's heart slammed once against her ribs—

—and Zephyrine fed the stick into the fire and sat down beside it.

"Here's my answer," she said. "You're right that I want my name back. You're right that I've wanted it every single day, and that some nights wanting it was the only reason I got up. If you'd made this offer a month ago, at Coldbank, at the Narrows—" she shook her head. "I'd have taken it. I know I would have. I'd have told myself the ledger would surface someday anyway, that someone else would carry it, that staying alive to fly again mattered more than being right."

"But?" Voss said.

"But you're a month late." She looked back over her shoulder—at Ruth, at Dez, at Junie—and Ruth felt the look like a hand laid flat against her sternum. "A month ago I was a woman with a fuel chit and a fake name. Now I'm—" She stopped, and Ruth watched her struggle for the word, watch her refuse the easy ones. "Now I'm somebody's crew. And you're asking me to burn the only thing that makes what happened to us mean anything. Not just me. Dez signed that report under threat and has been drinking himself to death over it for seven years. Wrenn died getting this evidence out. His daughter carried it up a gorge on her back. A sixteen-year-old rebuilt our engine under patrol guns. If I burn it, I don't buy back my old life. I just tell all of them their part didn't matter."

"Their parts," Voss said quietly, "won't matter either if the syndicate decides they're witnesses."

"Then that's the weather," Zephyrine said, "and we'll fly through it. But I'm not selling them to buy myself. Not even for my name." She stood. "Especially not for my name. Seven years ago you people taught me that a name on paper isn't the same as a self. I finally believe it. You can't give me back what I actually lost, Voss. I found it on the river."

* * *

Voss studied her for a long time. Then he sighed, and the sigh had weight in it, seven years of weight, and Ruth thought: *he came hoping she'd say no.* She couldn't have explained how she knew. She just did. Some men bring offers they pray will be refused.

"Burn it or don't," he said at last. "It changes nothing for you. Do you understand that? The board doesn't need your cooperation. There are six of us and four of you, and the glacier is doing our work for free." He said it without heat, almost sadly, a man reciting terms he wished he didn't have to enforce. "Leave the ledger and walk away. Keep it and be buried with it. Those are the only outcomes left, Miss Marchetti. I gave you the third one because I promised myself I would. It's off the table now."

"Voss." Zephyrine's voice stopped him as he turned. "Your daughter. The one who asks why you don't sleep. When this is over—if you're still standing—go home and tell her the truth. All of it. That's the only absolution that's ever worked. I checked."

For a moment the butcher's patience cracked all the way through, and Ruth saw underneath: a tired man in a good coat, a long way from anywhere, holding a job that had eaten him alive. Then it sealed again, and he gestured, and his men began to close the circle.

Ruth moved without deciding to. One step, shoulder set against Zephyrine's, and Dez on her other side, and Junie sliding in past them all with a wrench in her fist that she held like it might argue on her behalf. Six men against four. Rifles against a wrench. It was the worst arithmetic Ruth had ever stood inside of, and she had never in her life felt less like running.

"Last chance," Voss said.

The mountain answered him.

It started as a sound Ruth felt in her teeth before she heard it—a deep, subterranean groan, the icefield clearing its throat—and then the western wall of the valley let go. Not the small calvings they'd been hearing all week. Something vast, a shelf of blue ice the size of a church shearing away and falling, and the report of it rolled down the valley like God slamming a door, and the snow on the slopes above the glacier's tongue began to move.

Everyone looked up. Everyone forgot the circle.

In the white chaos of the slope, in the thunder, Ruth grabbed Zephyrine's sleeve and shouted the only thing that mattered: "The icefall—it's coming down early—we have to move *now*—"

Above them, the mountain kept falling.
