## 17. The Calving

Junie heard the mountain before she saw it move.

She was under the Cessna's wing with the cowling off, sleeves rolled to the elbow, her hands black to the wrist with grease, when the sound came down the valley. Not a crack, exactly. Not thunder either. It was deeper than both — a groan that started somewhere in the glacier's bones and climbed through the ice beneath their boots until it buzzed in her fillings and rattled the loose bolts in the parts tray beside her knee.

She sat back on her heels. The whole camp had gone quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone says something true.

"Everybody hear that?" Dez called from the tent line, where he'd been sorting ore samples into canvas bags with the tenderness of a man handling eggs.

"Glacier's talking," Ruth said. She stood at the edge of the moraine with binoculars raised toward the icefall above the source, where the Kessler Glacier poured down between two granite shoulders in a staircase of blue seracs. Even from here Junie could see it: the whole face of the lower icefall had shifted since yesterday. New fractures ran through it like veins in marble. "Talking means moving. Moving means—"

"It means we're out of time," Zephyrine said.

She came out of the survey cabin with Wrenn's ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and Junie watched her take stock of the sky, the light on the ice, the smell of the wind — all of it read in about three seconds flat, the way she read weather and engines and people. Seven years of running had built a woman who could calculate an exit before most folks finished noticing there was a door.

"We fly out tomorrow at first light," Zephyrine said. "Everything packed tonight. Ledger, samples, correspondence — nothing stays behind. If the route closes early, we lose the river and we lose everything we came for."

"And Voss?" Ruth asked.

The name landed in the camp like a stone in still water. Voss and his four men had been visible since midmorning, a dark string of figures working up the lateral moraine from the south, closing the distance with the patience of wolves who knew the herd couldn't run much farther. They'd made camp two hundred yards below theirs, close enough that Junie could hear their radio squawking, close enough that Voss had twice walked partway up the slope just to be seen, hands loose at his sides, smiling like a man at a church social.

Zephyrine looked at the syndicate camp for a long moment. "Voss is Voss's problem," she said finally. But something in her voice didn't believe it, and Junie — who had spent fifteen years learning to hear what adults said underneath what they meant — filed that away.

* * *

They worked through the afternoon in the long gold light of the subarctic evening, and Junie threw herself into the work because work was the only language she'd ever trusted. She drained and refilled the fuel strainer. She lashed the sample bags into the cargo pod. She re-cottered the landing gear strut that had taken the hit on the lake landing, hammering the pin home while Ruth held the wrench and Dez read torque values off a scrap of paper he kept in his shirt pocket like scripture.

"You're good at this," Ruth said, not looking up. It wasn't praise, exactly. It was more like an observation, the way you'd note that water was wet.

"My dad taught me. Before he got interested in drinking instead." Junie wiped her hands on her thighs. "Engines don't lie to you. They break for reasons you can find."

Ruth grunted, which from Ruth was practically a sonnet, and went back to lashing.

The light was going amber and long across the ice when the second groan came. This one didn't stop. It built, low and rising, and Junie felt the hair stand up on her arms before her brain caught up with the meaning of it. Down-valley, the syndicate men had stopped moving too. One of them pointed up at the icefall.

"Off the ice!" Dez shouted. "Everyone off the glacier margin — NOW!"

They ran. Junie grabbed the ledger bag because it was nearest, and Ruth grabbed her by the collar and hauled her up the moraine as the world behind them came apart.

It wasn't like the pictures. There was no slow cinematic tumble. The lower icefall simply *failed* — a serac the size of a church shrugging loose from its foundation, then another behind it, then the whole face letting go at once, ten thousand tons of blue ice dropping five hundred feet and detonating against the valley floor. The concussion hit them like a physical blow. Snow and shattered rock came downslope in a rolling white wall, and Junie felt the ground buck under her knees, felt Ruth's grip bruise her shoulder, heard Dez shouting something that the roar swallowed whole.

When the dust settled — and it took minutes, a hanging fog of ice crystals that turned the evening sun into a dim orange ghost — the shape of the valley had changed. The route downriver was gone, buried under a fresh debris field fifty feet deep. And where the syndicate camp had been pitched on the low bench by the meltwater channel, there was nothing but churned white ruin.

"Oh God," Dez whispered. "Oh God, they were right there."

Junie counted. It was automatic, the way she counted everything — bolts, rivets, days, lies. Four figures were scrambling on the high flank of the moraine, alive, climbing away from the debris. She counted again, slower, making sure.

Four.

There had been five.

* * *

The wind carried the sound up to them within the minute: a man screaming. Not words. Just pain, raw and rhythmic, coming from somewhere inside the collapse zone near the buried meltwater channel.

The four survivors had stopped climbing. They stood on the ridge looking down at the debris field, and even at this distance Junie could read the arithmetic happening in their postures. Nobody moved toward the sound. One of them raised a radio; static answered him. Then, one by one, they turned and started down the far side of the moraine, away — abandoning the voice in the ice without so much as a shouted apology, men who served a company that had taught them exactly what people were worth.

"They're leaving him," Junie said. Her voice came out strange and small.

"He'll freeze before morning if nobody digs," Ruth said. "And nobody's digging."

Junie looked at Zephyrine.

Zephyrine stood at the edge of the moraine with the wind pulling at her jacket, staring down into the smoking white field where the man who had hunted her across seven years and half a territory lay pinned under a mountain of her enemy's own making. Junie watched her face do something complicated — watched seven years of fear and fury pass through it like weather, watched the old scar of the conviction, the lost license, the nights sleeping in the plane, all of it rise up and demand its due.

"Zeph," Dez said carefully. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking he's forty feet down and the ice is still settling." Zephyrine was already uncoiling rope from the cache. "I'm thinking the debris field is unstable and anyone who goes in is an idiot." She looped the rope around her waist with quick, practiced turns. "I'm thinking I've been an idiot before. It's worked out."

"You can't go down there alone," Ruth said.

"I'm not asking anyone to come."

"I know," Ruth said. She picked up the second coil of rope. "That's why I'm coming."

Dez swore softly and shouldered the shovel. Junie looked at the three of them — the pilot, the ferrywoman, the broken geologist — and felt something hot and furious rise in her chest, because she understood suddenly that she was watching the exact thing she'd spent her whole life believing didn't exist. Adults choosing the hard thing. Choosing it together. Choosing it for a man who would have sold every one of them to save himself.

"Somebody's gotta anchor," Junie said, before she could talk herself out of it. "And I'm the lightest, so I'm the one who doesn't get buried if the slope lets go." She planted the shovel handle in the scree, wrapped her end of the rope around it, and set her boots. "Go. I've got you."

Zephyrine looked at her — really looked, the way she almost never did, like Junie was a person and not a stowaway to be managed — and nodded once.

"Hold fast," Zephyrine said. "Whatever you hear. Hold fast."

Then they went down into the white.

* * *

Junie held the rope and learned what holding meant.

It meant feeling every shift in the line translate up her arms and into her spine — Zephyrine descending the debris slope in long careful plunges, boot-packing the settling snow, probing with the shovel for voids. It meant listening to the ice groan overhead as aftershocks worked through the fracture zone, each one a reminder that the mountain had not finished deciding things. It meant counting seconds between Zephyrine's signals — two tugs, moving; three tugs, found something — and fighting panic when the count stretched past a minute, past two, the rope slack and silent as a dead thing in her hands.

Three tugs. Then a long terrible nothing.

Then Ruth's voice, muffled by distance and wind: "We've got him! His legs are pinned — slab came down on the channel bank — Dez, the bar here, lever on my count!"

Junie braced and pulled slack through her gloved hands, keeping the line live, and stared down into the blue dusk of the debris field where three small figures clustered around a dark shape half-buried in ice. She could see Voss's face now, or part of it — gray, blood-slicked, eyes open and fixed on the woman kneeling over him with a shovel. He was saying something. Junie couldn't hear it over the wind, but she saw Zephyrine lean closer, saw her shake her head once, saw her keep digging.

*Later*, Junie thought. *Whatever he's saying — begging or bargaining or threatening — later.* Because that was the thing about Zephyrine Marchetti that Junie was only beginning to understand: she didn't negotiate with the past while the present was bleeding. First you dug. First you pulled. The accounting came after, if it came at all.

The slab shifted. A crack ran through the debris field with a sound like a rifle shot, and the whole slope below them sagged six inches. Junie's stomach dropped. She hauled back on the rope and screamed down the line, "MOVE, MOVE, MOVE—" and felt the line go taut as Ruth and Dez dragged Voss free of the pinch and Zephyrine scrambled upslope through snow that was liquefying under her boots, the second collapse chasing her heels like a living thing.

Junie leaned her whole weight backward, boots skidding, arms burning, the shovel handle bowing, and pulled.

They crested the safe ground in a tangle of rope and limbs just as the lower field let go entirely — a soft, grinding subsidence, the ice sighing down into the space where Voss had been buried, erasing the hole, erasing the channel, erasing any argument about what would have happened if no one had come.

For a moment nobody spoke. The four of them lay in the scree gasping, and Voss lay among them — among *them*, inside the circle, inside the rope — coughing pink froth and staring up at a sky he clearly hadn't expected to see again.

"Why," he rasped. It wasn't gratitude. It was genuine bewilderment, the question of a man whose entire cosmology had no shelf for this. "You could have let the mountain do it. No one would ever have known."

Zephyrine sat up. She was filthy, shaking, a ribbon of blood tracking down from her hairline where the debris had clipped her, and Junie watched her look at Voss with an expression Junie would carry for the rest of her life — not mercy exactly, and not forgiveness, but something harder than both. A choice, made and finished, already becoming part of her.

"No one would have known," Zephyrine agreed quietly. "But I would have known." She stood, and looked down the valley toward the four men who had left their boss to die, already small dots on the southern moraine. "Get him warm. Bind his ribs. He comes with us."

"He comes with us?" Dez's voice cracked upward. "Zeph, he's the reason—"

"He's evidence," Zephyrine said. "And he's a man under our rope now, and those are the same thing whether I like it or not." She turned toward the plane, toward the buried river, toward the problem of how in God's name they were getting out of a valley whose exit had ceased to exist. "And somebody get me up high enough to see what's left of the lake. If the calving opened an ice corridor downstream—"

Ruth was already climbing the moraine with the binoculars. She stopped. Went very still.

"Zephyrine," she called down, and there was something in her voice that Junie had never heard there before — something that sounded almost like hope wearing danger's clothes. "You need to see this. The ice — it's opened a channel downriver. Blue water, clear to the bend." She lowered the glasses slowly. "It won't last a day. Maybe not six hours."

Zephyrine looked at the dying window, then at the wrecked plane, then at the man bleeding in the snow — and Junie saw the decision arrive in her the way weather arrives, sudden and total.

"Then we'd better build a boat," Zephyrine said.
