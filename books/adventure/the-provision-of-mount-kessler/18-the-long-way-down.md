## 18. The Long Way Down

The river had opened like a wound.

Zephyrine stood at the lip of the gorge where the calving had torn the icefall apart, and what she saw made her breath go shallow. Yesterday there had been a wall of blue-white ice spanning the Kessler from bank to bank, a hundred feet of compressed centuries, and behind it the river had backed up into a cold green lake that swallowed the current whole. Now the wall was a ruin. House-sized blocks lay tumbled down the channel like the vertebrae of some drowned animal, and between them the water ran fast and silver and furious, threading gaps no wider than her wingspan, plunging through corridors of ice that groaned and ticked as they settled into their new shapes.

The window. The one window. Wrenn's map had promised it in his cramped, dying hand: *when the headwater dam goes, you'll have maybe two days before the channel chokes again with bergs from the upper field. After that, nobody runs it. Nobody ever has.*

Two days ago she would have said the same thing about herself.

Behind her, the camp was wreckage — half their gear buried under the slumped moraine where the icefall's edge had let go, Voss's men scattered up-valley on foot with nothing but their rifles and their shame, and Voss himself sitting against a boulder with his arm bound in a strip of Junie's spare canvas, watching her with the flat, calculating eyes of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like the answer to.

"You can't run that," he said. It wasn't mockery. That was the strange thing. His voice was raw from the dust and the cold, and there was something underneath it that sounded almost like awe. "Nobody runs that."

"Then we'll be the first," Zephyrine said, "or we'll be the last thing this river ever eats." She turned away from him before he could see what the words cost her. "Ruth! Dez! Junie! We're leaving in twenty minutes. Everything that floats, everything that matters, and nothing else."

* * *

They had one boat and half a plane.

The boat was Ruth's — the *Kestrel*, thirty feet of welded aluminum with a shallow draft and an engine Junie had coaxed back to life twice already on this trip, and now a third time, kneeling in the bilge with her sleeves shoved to the elbow, her small hands moving over the fuel line with a tenderness Zephyrine had only ever seen surgeons use. The plane was her own Cessna on floats, holed in the port pontoon by the storm landing, patched with resin and fabric and prayer, good for taxiing and nothing more. So they did the arithmetic every expedition does eventually: the plane rode lashed to the roof of the boathouse of the world, strapped down with rope and hope, and everything else went in the boat.

Voss went in the boat too. That had been the argument, brief and vicious, back at the camp.

"We leave him," Ruth had said. "His own people left *him*. Let the mountain sort it out."

"He'll die," Zephyrine said.

"So?"

Zephyrine had looked at Voss then — at the man who had photographed them at the Narrows, who had strafed their camp, who had put a rifle muzzle within a foot of Junie's face and smiled while he did it — and she had felt the old cold thing in her chest, the thing seven years of running had built around her heart like scar tissue, and she had understood that this was the actual choice. Not the ledger. Not the magistrate waiting in Coldbank. This. Whether the woman who came down off Mount Kessler was the one the syndicate had made, or the one she'd decided to be somewhere above the ice line with four people she loved.

"He rides with us," she said. "Bound, disarmed, and miserable. And if he so much as breathes wrong, Ruth gets to decide what happens next, because I'm going to be busy."

Ruth had stared at her for a long moment, and then something had moved behind her eyes — not agreement exactly, but recognition, the look of a ferry operator reading water. "Fine," she'd said. "But he sits in the bow. First place the river takes if we stuff it."

Now Voss sat in the bow, wrists lashed to the forward thwart, and he did not say another word as they pushed off.

* * *

The first mile was the worst, because the first mile was all guessing.

Dez stood amidships with his bad leg braced against the gunwale, reading the water the way he read rock — patiently, hungrily, looking for the story underneath the surface. He'd been a good geologist once; the river had made him a good one again, because the same instinct worked both directions. Where the water smoothed into a long green tongue, there was depth. Where it piled into a standing wave with a white crown, there was a submerged block and a hole behind it. Where it hissed over something just under the surface, it sang a note, and Dez had started naming the notes out loud, low and steady, like a man calling a hymn.

"Tongue left. Take the left side past the splitter — no, wait—" He cocked his head. "Hear that? High note, thin. Shelf rock under the left tongue. Go right of it, hard right, then back left before the pour-over."

Ruth drove. She drove the way she did everything, without wasted motion and without mercy, and Zephyrine realized within the first ten minutes that she was watching something she'd never seen: Ruth Wrenn happy. Not smiling — Ruth didn't smile, not yet, maybe not for a long time — but lit from inside, some furnace in her finally fed after years of burning damp wood. Her hands were light on the wheel. Her jaw was set. When the bow rose on a wave and slammed down and spray came over the top in a sheet that froze everyone to the bone, Ruth laughed. One short bark of a laugh, startled out of her, and then she hauled the wheel over and threaded the gap Dez called and did it again.

Zephyrine ran the bow line when they needed eyes forward, and Junie ran the engine, and between the three of them and Dez's voice and Voss's terrified silence, the boat became a single animal. That was the thing Zephyrine would remember afterward, in the years when people asked her what it was like — not any single rapid, not the fear, which was real and enormous and sat in her stomach like ballast — but the way five strangers' bodies learned each other. By the second hour Ruth could feel Dez's hesitation in the wheel before he spoke it. By the third, Junie was feeding throttle before Ruth asked, matching some rhythm she couldn't have explained, and Zephyrine, calling the ice, found she no longer needed to shout. She'd say a word — *left, slot, hold* — and the boat would simply do it, the way a hand does what the mind intends without consulting the arm.

They were one body. It shouldn't have been possible. It happened anyway.

* * *

The corridor itself came at midday, and it was worse than anything Zephyrine had imagined, and she had imagined a great deal.

Where the calving had split the icefall, the river ran between walls of ice for nearly half a mile — vertical walls, translucent at their edges so the afternoon sun came through them green-gold, glowing, like running a canyon cut from cathedral glass. The channel in the middle was perhaps twelve feet wide. Maybe less. The water in it moved faster than the *Kestrel* could motor, which meant steering was a fiction; all they could really do was aim the bow and pray the river kept its promise.

"Once we're in," Dez said quietly, "there's no eddy. No backing out. No swimming it — you'd be dead in ninety seconds in that cold. Whatever happens in there, happens."

"I know," Zephyrine said.

"The bergs are still settling. You can hear them." He was right. All along the corridor the ice ticked and cracked and occasionally boomed, deep reports rolling up the valley like distant artillery, and every few minutes a chunk the size of a truck calved off the wall and hit the water with a slap they felt through the hull.

Junie looked up from the engine, and her face was pale, and her chin was up. "I'm not scared," she announced, in the tone of someone who absolutely was.

"Good," Zephyrine said. "Scared is fuel. Burn it."

She looked at Ruth. Ruth looked back, and for a moment neither of them said anything, because there wasn't anything left to say that hadn't been said across a card table in Coldbank and a fire at the portage and a thousand small transactions since. Then Ruth nodded once, toward the corridor, and said, "Your father flew supply runs over this ice for eleven years. He used to say the river always leaves one door open. You just have to be fool enough to be standing there when it does."

Zephyrine's throat tightened. She hadn't known that. There were so many things she hadn't known, and the hermit at Kessler Bend had given her a handful of them, and apparently Ruth had gotten others, and between them they were assembling a father out of scraps the way Junie assembled engines.

"One door," Zephyrine said. "Let's find it."

They went in.

* * *

It was impossible and it was glorious and it lasted nine minutes.

Later, Zephyrine could never make the nine minutes come out right in the telling. People wanted them to be either terror or triumph, and they were both at once, braided so tight you couldn't separate them. The walls closed overhead until the sky was a ribbon. The water ran green-black and smooth as poured glass and then erupted without warning into standing waves taller than the boat. Ice groaned above them, close enough that flakes of it rained onto the deck. At one point a block calved off the right wall directly ahead, and Dez's voice cracked upward an octave — *hard left, HARD LEFT* — and Ruth threw the boat sideways and the hull kissed the left wall of ice with a shriek of aluminum on glass and slid along it for a full three seconds, close enough that Zephyrine, hanging off the bow, could see air bubbles frozen in the wall like pearls, forty feet deep, a winter sixty years ago preserved in blue.

And through all of it, nobody screamed. Nobody froze. Dez called the water. Ruth drove it. Junie fed the engine exactly what it needed, and when the intake sucked a slurry of ice and the motor began to stumble — the single most lethal thing that could happen in that corridor, dead engine, dead steerage, dead crew — Junie was already on it, hand on the primer, voice steady as a metronome: "I've got it, I've got it, give me five seconds," and she had it in four, and the engine caught with a cough and a roar and Ruth hauled the bow straight and they shot through the last narrowing with the walls scraping both gunwales at once, sparks of reflected light flying off the ice like static—

—and then the corridor opened, and the river spread wide and brown-green and comparatively gentle, and they were through, and the boom of their own wake slapping the hull was the loudest sound in the world.

For a moment nobody moved. Then Junie burst out laughing — high, wild, fifteen years old and immortal — and Dez sat down heavily on the cooler and put his face in his hands, and Ruth killed the throttle and let the boat drift, and Zephyrine stood in the bow with the wind tearing at her and laughed until her ribs hurt, laughed the way she hadn't laughed since before the trial, since before the world had taught her that joy was a debt that always came due.

In the bow, Voss was gray. Actually gray. He looked at her with the expression of a man who has watched his entire understanding of the universe get run through an ice corridor and survive.

"You're insane," he said hoarsely. "All of you."

"Yeah," Zephyrine said, still laughing. "We know. Hold on. We're not done."

* * *

They weren't. The corridor was the door, not the journey. Below it the river ran fast for six miles through a landscape still shedding its winter, bergs grounded on the gravel bars like sleeping animals, and twice more they had to thread gaps that Dez read and Ruth took and Zephyrine called, and each time the boat answered as one body, smoother now, the seams gone. In the late afternoon they ran the last rapid — a long, rollicking, honest wave train with no ice in it at all, just water doing what water does — and came out into the widening valley below the gorge where the Kessler slowed and broadened and began, mile by mile, to become the river they knew, the river that led home.

They camped that night on a gravel bar below the gorge mouth, exhausted, soaked, half-frozen, grinning like idiots.

Dez built a fire out of driftwood and the last of their dry stores, and Junie fell asleep sitting up with her back against a log, mid-sentence, still explaining something about carburetors, and Ruth draped a blanket over her without comment and without waking, and Zephyrine saw her do it and saw Ruth see her seeing it, and neither of them said a word, because some things break if you name them.

Voss sat apart, still lashed to a length of rope Ruth had re-rigged around his waist and staked to a boulder. He watched them across the fire for a long time. Finally he said, "Why? The camp. The glacier. You could have left me. Nobody would ever have known."

Zephyrine looked at him across the flames. She thought about the answer for a while, because he deserved a real one, or as real as she could make it.

"Because I spent seven years being what other people decided I was," she said. "A fugitive. A liar. A convenience. And I got so I couldn't tell anymore which parts were theirs and which were mine." She poked the fire. Sparks climbed. "Today I decided. That's all. Today I decided."

Voss looked away, downriver, toward Coldbank, toward the magistrate and the reckoning waiting for him there — his employers' reprisal, his own men's abandonment, whatever was left of a life built on falsified paper. When he spoke again his voice was very quiet.

"They'll come for me, you know. For talking."

"Then you'd better talk first," Zephyrine said. "Talk loud. Talk everything. It's the only shelter left to you, and unlike us, you'll be walking into town alone."

He was silent after that. But Zephyrine, watching him, saw something shift in his shoulders — not gratitude, nothing so clean as that, but the surrender of a man who has finally understood the shape of the trap he built for himself, and who has noticed, with something like grief, that the people he came to destroy are the only ones willing to carry him out of it.

* * *

Near midnight, Zephyrine walked to the edge of the gravel bar and stood looking back up the valley. The gorge was invisible in the dark, but she could hear it — a low, continuous thunder, the sound of the river still tearing itself free, still rearranging its doors. Somewhere up there, under the fresh rubble, lay the survey camp, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth, the proof that would clear her name. Two days downstream, Coldbank waited with a magistrate and a courthouse and a version of her that existed only in syndicate filings.

Seven years she had measured her life in miles. Fuel stops, flight plans, the distance between a name and the nearest person who might use it against her. Tonight, listening to the river, she tried to measure it differently and found she didn't know how yet — but she knew the unit had changed. It wasn't miles anymore. It was these four people asleep around a fire on a gravel bar, and whether they woke warm.

Ruth came and stood beside her. Neither spoke for a while. The stars over the Kessler were brutal and close.

"Coldbank by tomorrow night, if the weather holds," Ruth said at last. "Magistrate Aldous keeps chambers on the north side of town. Syndicate money never bought him — my father told me that much before he was worth telling anyone anything."

"You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else since the Narrows." Ruth's jaw worked. "My name's going in front of that court too, you know. Wrenn. His claim, his ledger, his mess. I spent twenty years pretending I didn't have a father. Tomorrow everybody in Coldbank's going to know I do." She paused. "I find I don't mind as much as I expected."

Zephyrine glanced at her. In the firelight Ruth's profile was all edges, but her eyes were fixed upriver, toward the source, toward the ashes they carried in a tin in the bottom of the gear bag, toward a man neither of them had ever managed to stop loving no matter how hard they'd tried.

"When this is done," Zephyrine said, "when the conviction's voided and the syndicate's exposed — what then? You've got a ferry to run. I've got—"

"Don't," Ruth said. "Don't start planning the goodbye. I've watched you do it since the portage. Every time somebody gets close, you check your fuel and start measuring the runway." She turned, and her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "Whatever you're about to say — whatever speech you've got loaded — save it. Ask me again after the magistrate signs the order. Because I've got a feeling that tomorrow changes what's possible, and I want you answering from the new world, not the old one."

Zephyrine held her gaze. Downriver, dark and patient, Coldbank waited — the courthouse, the truth, the end of seven years of running. And after that, unasked and unanswered, the rest of a life.

"All right," she said quietly. "After the signature."

Ruth nodded once and went back to the fire, and Zephyrine stayed a moment longer at the water's edge, letting the cold come up off the river, letting herself feel the size of what was coming. Tomorrow they would deliver the ledger. Tomorrow the truth would walk into daylight wearing her name.

Tonight, for the first time in seven years, she was not running toward something or away from something.

She was going home with her family, and the river was carrying them.
