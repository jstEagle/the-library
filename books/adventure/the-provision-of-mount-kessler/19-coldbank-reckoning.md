## 19. Coldbank Reckoning

The magistrate's office in Coldbank sat above the ferry landing, in a building that had once been a trading post and still smelled faintly of fur and lamp oil, as though the walls themselves remembered a time when the law out here was whatever a man could carry in one hand and defend with the other. Ruth Wrenn had passed beneath its window ten thousand times. She had never once gone up the stairs.

She climbed them now with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and it seemed to her that the whole town was watching from doorways — the fishmonger, the two boys who pumped fuel at the dock, old Marta Kellerman with her knitting frozen mid-stitch. Word had traveled ahead of them, the way word always did on the river. *Marchetti's come back. Marchetti's come back with proof.*

Ruth was not Marchetti. But she carried what Marchetti carried, and she felt the weight of it settle into her shoulders like a yoke she'd been fitted for without knowing.

Behind her came the rest of them: Zephyrine with her arm bound where the icefall had opened it, walking like a woman who had decided pain was a scheduling problem; Dez hollow-eyed and scrubbed clean for the first time in Ruth's memory, wearing a shirt that Junie had ironed with a kettle because he owned nothing that wasn't creased by seven years of regret; Junie herself, hair braided tight, hands black to the wrist with engine grease she'd refused to wash off — *for luck*, she said, though Ruth suspected it was armor. And behind all of them, wrists cuffed, face the color of river clay, walked Voss between two of his own men who had not abandoned him so much as run out of reasons to stay.

The magistrate was a woman named Odile Ferrant, sixty-odd, with reading glasses on a chain and the unhurried eyes of someone who had spent thirty years listening to men lie about water rights. She cleared her desk with one sweep of her forearm — papers, an inkwell, a taxidermied trout that had watched over Coldbank jurisprudence since before Ruth was born — and said only, "Sit. All of you. Even him."

Voss sat. It seemed to cost him something physical, like setting down a load he'd carried so long his spine had grown around it.

Ferrant unwrapped the oilcloth herself, refusing Dez's help with a small motion, and for a long while there was no sound in the room but paper. The original ore ledger, its columns in Wrenn's cramped hand. The correspondence, syndicate letterhead, dates matching the falsified survey. The affidavit Dez had written at three in the morning by firelight, his handwriting steadier than any of them expected, as though the words had been waiting years for permission to come out straight.

Ferrant read the way Ruth imagined she ran her ferry: no wasted motion, no sentiment, total attention. When she reached the page where Tobias Wrenn had recorded samples from the sealed source — samples taken *before* the survey, samples that proved the ore body the syndicate claimed to have discovered had been known, mapped, and then buried along with Zephyrine Marchetti's name — she took off her glasses and looked at Voss over the rims.

"You're the enforcer," she said. "Kessler Consolidated's man. You were at the camp."

"I was at the camp." His voice was flat, scraped thin. Up close, Ruth could see what the river had done to him — the split lip healing crooked, the frost-nipped fingertips, the look of a man who had been pulled out of a collapsing world and hadn't yet decided whether to be grateful. "I signed the transport manifest for the evidence. I burned the originals. I gave my statement to the territorial police. Every word of it dictated."

"You understand what you're admitting."

"I understand what they'll do to me for admitting it." He almost smiled. It was the first expression Ruth had seen on him that wasn't menace, and it was worse somehow — tired, human, finished. "That's rather the point, madam magistrate. A man facing reprisal has nothing left to protect. I'm told that makes me credible."

"It makes you convenient," Ferrant said. "Credibility we'll establish the slow way." She turned to Dez. "You'll swear your statement under oath?"

Dez's throat worked. "I'll swear it standing on one leg if you want."

"Sit still and speak clearly. That's all swearing ever requires." She made a note. Then, to the room at large: "This will take days. Possibly weeks. There will be a hearing, depositions, wires to the territorial court. The conviction won't be voided today because the law doesn't work today. It works when it finishes working." Her gaze settled on Zephyrine, and something in it softened by a degree that Ruth alone probably caught. "But I've read enough to tell you where it ends, Miss Marchetti. It ends with your name put right. You have my word on it, and my word is the only currency in this building that doesn't devalue."

Zephyrine nodded once. Ruth watched her — watched the way she held absolutely still, the way she did when feeling arrived too large to move through — and Ruth understood that the word *vindication* must feel, right now, less like a door opening than like a weight being set down after so long the arms had forgotten how to hang free.

* * *

They waited four days in Coldbank, because there was nowhere else to go and nothing else to do, and those four days taught Ruth more about the crew than the whole river had.

Junie adopted the ferry dock. By the second morning she had diagnosed the *Marta B*'s starboard engine as "running rich and lying about it" and rebuilt the carburetor over Marta Kellerman's mild objections, and by the third morning half the town's small machinery had found its way into her orbit — outboard motors, a sewing machine, the church organ, which she pronounced "clogged with forty years of dust and somebody's sandwich." She worked constantly and slept badly, and Ruth noticed that whenever Zephyrine came down to the dock, Junie found a reason to be elbow-deep in something, and that Zephyrine always found a reason to stand nearby anyway, saying nothing, until the girl surfaced and grunted at her, and that was apparently the whole conversation, and both of them seemed satisfied by it.

Dez went sober in public for the first time in years and discovered that Coldbank contained people who remembered him from before — students, colleagues, a woman named Ada who had once co-authored a paper with him and who turned up at the boarding house the second evening holding a bottle of cider and twenty years of unfinished argument. He drank the cider. He argued until midnight. Ruth heard him laughing through the wall, a sound rusty as an unused hinge, and lay awake thinking about how a man could carry a thing so long he mistook it for his skeleton.

And Zephyrine — Zephyrine waited the way a held breath waits. She checked the magistrate's office twice a day. She walked the lake shore where her father's old cabin had stood, and came back silent. On the third evening Ruth found her on the ferry deck, feet up on the rail, watching the water do its endless bookkeeping.

"They offered me a deal yesterday," Zephyrine said, without preamble. "Voss's employers. Through a lawyer from the coast. Full pardon, expunged record, back pay for seven years. All I have to do is hand over the ledger and let the survey stand."

Ruth kept her eyes on the water. "What did you say?"

"I said the ledger isn't mine to sell. It's Wrenn's. It's yours." Zephyrine's mouth curved, dry. "Also I may have suggested where they could store their pardon."

"That sounds like you."

"It felt like me. That's the strange part." She was quiet a moment. "Seven years I thought if I could just get clear — new name, new territory, miles between me and that camp — I'd be fine. I built my whole life on distance. And now the distance is gone and I'm sitting on a ferry deck in the town I avoided on every map I ever drew, and I'm—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I keep waiting to feel exposed. I don't. I just feel *here*."

Ruth understood, in the way you understand things about other people that are really things about yourself, exactly what she meant. For eleven years she had run her ferry and refused her father's name and told herself the refusal was strength. It had taken a dying man's map and two thousand kilometers of bad water to show her that strength and stone are not the same material.

"He'd have liked this deck," she said. "My father. He used to talk about running boats before he got the flying bug. I used to think that was just another of his stories."

"Was it?"

"Probably. He had so many." She heard her own voice crack on it and let it. Grief shared was weight halved; grief hoarded was ballast you sank by. She was learning that too, late and slowly, from a woman she'd met as a customer and nearly thrown off her boat. "Zeph. Whatever the magistrate rules. Whatever happens with the name and the record and all of it." She turned, finally, and looked at her straight. "You know none of us needed it fixed to keep you. Right? Dez didn't sign on for a pardon. Junie stowed away for a plane ride and stayed for — well. I ran the gorge because of a letter. We'd have gone up that river for you with nothing but your say-so and a bad map."

Zephyrine looked at her for a long moment. In the dusk her eyes were the color of the lake before freeze-up, dark and deep and holding light anyway.

"I know," she said. "That's the part that frightens me. The pardon I can survive losing. This—" her hand made a small gesture that took in the deck, the dock, the distant sound of Junie arguing with an outboard motor, "—this I couldn't."

"Well," Ruth said. "Then don't lose it."

* * *

On the fifth day, Ferrant sent for them.

The hearing room was the same office, rearranged: a table, chairs, the trout presiding. The territorial assessor had arrived overnight on the mail steamer, a narrow man named Quill with ink-stained fingers and the air of someone who measured his life in certified copies. Ferrant read the finding aloud standing up, which Ruth gathered was unusual, and her voice was ordinary, which made it land harder than any proclamation could have.

"The conviction of Zephyrine Anne Marchetti, entered seven years past on evidence subsequently shown to have been manufactured by agents of Kessler Consolidated Mining, is hereby voided. The record is expunged. The court notes — and I will note it formally, for whoever reads this file after I am dead — that the original evidence against her was fabricated, that the survey underlying it was fraudulent, and that Miss Marchetti's exile was not a failure of justice but a crime committed *by* the process itself." Ferrant set down the paper. "Miss Marchetti. The law moves slow and arrives late. But it arrived. You're free to go anywhere you like."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the ferry horn sounded, absurd and mundane.

Zephyrine stood very straight, and Ruth saw her jaw working, and saw her decide — visibly, deliberately — not to cry in front of the assessor. What broke her instead was smaller: Junie, who had been fidgeting by the door, crossed the room and stood beside her, not touching, just *there*, shoulder an inch from hers, the way crew stands. Then Dez, on her other side. Then Ruth, completing it, and the four of them stood in a rough line before the magistrate's desk like something being christened.

"Thank you," Zephyrine managed.

"Don't thank me," Ferrant said, putting her glasses away. "Thank the dead prospector whose handwriting is better than mine. And him." She nodded at Voss, who stood apart by the window, unshackled now — released into protective custody, which everyone present understood was another word for *witness*. He was looking out at the lake with the expression of a man taking inventory of everything he'd spent and found the sum wanting.

"Voss." Zephyrine turned to him. Ruth braced for hatred, or worse, for graciousness. What she got was neither. "You corroborated everything. You'll testify at the syndicate trial. Why?"

He considered the question honestly, which she perhaps hadn't expected.

"Because you pulled me out," he said finally. "Under that icefall. I had a rifle and orders and you dug me out of the collapse zone with your bare hands and asked me if I could walk." His voice caught on something. "I've worked for men who would have stepped over me. I spent seven years telling myself that was the job. You spent seven years running from what they did to you, and the first chance you got, you chose different." He looked at her then, and it was unbearable and necessary, like looking at the sun through smoked glass. "I can't give you back seven years. Nobody can. But I can make sure the men who took them answer for it. It's not absolution. It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Zephyrine repeated softly. "All right. That I understand."

* * *

They came out of the courthouse into a bright cold afternoon, the kind of day the interior gives you in October as an apology for the ones coming, and the whole of Coldbank seemed to be on the landing. Someone had strung bunting — actual bunting, faded, smelling of the church attic — between the ferry mast and the fuel pump. Marta Kellerman pressed a tin of biscuits into Junie's hands. Two fishermen shook Dez's hand with the solemnity of men greeting a returning captain. A boy of maybe eight tugged Zephyrine's sleeve and asked, with the directness of the young, if she was really the pilot from the story, the one who flew the medicine runs through the pass in the whiteout, and Zephyrine crouched down to his level and said, "That's me. Though the whiteout mostly flew me," and the boy laughed like it was the best joke he'd ever heard.

Ruth stood back from it and watched her crew — her crew, God help her, when had that happened — absorb the town's attention like ground absorbing rain. Junie was showing the boy how the ferry's winch brake worked, already, incorrigibly teaching. Dez was letting Ada steer him toward the boarding house, resisting only decoratively. And Zephyrine stood at the rail with the wind pulling at her hair, her name returned to her like a coat she'd outgrown and somehow fit again, and she was looking not at the town but upriver — north, toward the Kessler, toward the source, toward the mountain that had given all of them more than it had taken, which was not the mountain's habit.

Ruth went to stand beside her.

"They'll want the claim sorted," she said. "Wrenn's claim. The assessor mentioned probate."

"Mm." Zephyrine didn't turn. "There's a packet. Wrenn's letter. Ruth — you still haven't read the whole thing, have you?"

"No." Ruth had read the first page, the night on the river, and had put it away like closing a door on a lit room. "I reckoned I'd know when."

"Do you?"

Ruth looked out at the water, at the far shore where the forest began its long dark argument with the horizon. "I reckon it's tonight," she said. "Whatever's in it. He wrote it dying, and he addressed it to me, and I'm done making him wait."

Zephyrine said nothing, but her hand found Ruth's shoulder — the same gesture, Ruth realized, that had anchored her in the magistrate's office, passed back and forth between them now like a tool they both knew how to use — and squeezed once, and let go.

That night, in the boarding house room with the stove ticking and the river talking to itself below the window, Ruth Wrenn sat down with her father's letter and broke the seal she'd carried two thousand kilometers without opening.

The first pages were what she'd braced for — apology, explanation, the self-justifications of a man running out of time to make them. She read them with her jaw set, granting him nothing, granting him everything, the two impulses grinding against each other like ice in a current.

Then, on the fourth page, her breath stopped.

*"—and the claim itself, the source, the whole of the Kessler headwaters filing — I recorded it in Ruth's name. Years ago. Before I ever got sick. She'll find the paperwork with this letter, registered and current, because I wanted her to have the one thing I ever found that was worth anything, and because I knew — I knew even then — that she'd be the one to hold it right. Not sell it. Not seal it. Hold it. Like a ferry holds a crossing. Not owning the river. Carrying it."*

Below the signature, in fresher ink, a single line added later, shakier:

*"If Marchetti brings you this, trust her with the river. She's the only pilot I ever met who flew like the cargo mattered more than the miles."*

Ruth read it three times. Then she sat for a long while with the paper in her lap, listening to the stove and the water and her own heart doing unfamiliar work, and when at last she rose and blew out the lamp, she already knew what she would say in the morning, and to whom, and it was not the assessor.

It was the pilot.
