## 20. The Daughter's Letter

The magistrate's clerk in Coldbank had stamped the papers twice, once in blue and once in red, and the sound of the second stamp had gone through Zephyrine like a rifle shot. *Voided. Conviction voided.* Seven years of looking over her shoulder, of paying cash under false names, of sleeping with her boots by the door of whatever shack she'd landed in—and it ended with a bored man in a green visor pressing ink onto paper while a ceiling fan turned overhead.

She hadn't cried. She'd stood very straight on the courthouse steps with the wind off the lake flattening her coat against her, and Ruth had stood beside her, and Dez had said something dry about lawyers finally being useful for something, and Junie had whooped loud enough to scatter pigeons off the eaves. It had felt almost like celebration. Almost.

But that night, back at Ruth's ferry house above the Coldbank landing, with the river muttering to itself below the pilings and the lamp turned low, Zephyrine found she couldn't sleep. The name *Marchetti* was hers again—clean, legal, hers—and it sat strangely against her skin, like a coat borrowed from someone else's closet. She lay in the guest room listening to the water and understood that the paper had given her back a name but not yet a life. That part was still unfinished. That part was still hers to build.

It was Ruth who didn't come down to breakfast.

* * *

Zephyrine found her at mid-morning on the ferry deck, sitting on an upturned crate with her elbows on her knees and a yellowed envelope in her hands. She wasn't reading it. She was holding it the way you hold something hot—fingers pinched at the corners, held away from the body.

Tobias Wrenn's letter. It had traveled from his dying hands up the Kessler River, through a sealed survey camp, through a calving glacier, through a magistrate's office, and back down again, and it had not yet been opened all the way through. Ruth had read the first page the night they'd returned—the night before the reckoning—and then folded it closed and put it in her coat pocket, and there it had stayed through the testimony and the verdict and the long evening after, riding against her heart like a stone.

"You don't have to do it today," Zephyrine said from the foot of the ramp.

"I know." Ruth's voice was rough. She was a woman built like the river herself—all current underneath, all gravel where the light hit. "I know that."

The morning was bright and cold, the kind of interior morning that made everything look newly minted. Ice still rode the slow eddies behind the ferry slip. Downriver, a loon called once and got no answer.

"He wrote it seven years ago," Ruth said. "Maybe eight. You can tell by the postmark attempt—he never could spell *Coldbank*, he always put the *k* in the wrong—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "I've been carrying this thing around like it might bite me, and I don't even know what's in it past the first page."

"Then let's find out," Zephyrine said, and came up the ramp and sat down on the crate beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. On the river, the current took a length of driftwood and carried it steadily out of sight.

Ruth unfolded the letter.

There were four pages, written in a hand that started careful and deteriorated as it went, the letters leaning harder and harder to the right like a man walking into wind. Tobias Wrenn had been dying when he wrote it, and he had known it, and he had written anyway, because he was a prospector and prospectors did not stop digging just because the ground got hard.

He wrote about the claim first. He wrote about the color of the ore at the source, how it came out of the seam butter-yellow and went red in the air, how he'd sat alone at the headwaters for a season with nothing but a hammer and a scale and his own bad conscience. He wrote about the syndicate men who had come upriver to buy him out and what he'd told them, which was unprintable, and what they'd told him, which was worse. He wrote about the survey camp downstream, and about a young pilot named Marchetti who had ferried supplies in and out that summer, and about how he had watched from the ridge with his field glasses and seen men carry crates into the assay office at night, crates that had come up from Coldbank full and gone down empty, and how he had understood too late what they were building and who they would need to bury under it.

Then the handwriting changed. Got smaller. Slower.

*I named you in the claim,* he wrote. *Filed it legal at the Coldbank recorder's, spring before last, under your mother's maiden name so nobody would go looking. Wrenn, R., sole holder. The source is yours, girl. All of it. I know you'll say you don't want it. I know what I cost you. But the mountain doesn't know my sins, and neither does the ore, and somebody ought to own a piece of this country who'll take care of it instead of bleeding it.*

*If a pilot brings you this letter, trust her. She flew straight when everyone else flew crooked. They took seven years off her life for something I watched them do. If she reads this over your shoulder, tell her the ledger's real and the map's true and I'm sorry it took a dying man to say so out loud.*

*You don't have to forgive me. Just don't sell the river short.*

Ruth read it twice. Zephyrine knew because she watched her lips move the second time, silently, the way people do when the words won't sit still. When Ruth finished, she folded the pages along their old creases with great care, as if the paper were bone, and held them in both hands in her lap, and looked out at the river for a long time.

"Sole holder," she said finally. Her voice came out flat and strange. "Wrenn, R."

"That's you."

"I know it's me." She laughed—a single hard note with nothing funny in it. "Seven years I hated him. Built a whole life out of hating him. Ferry license, my own boat, my own route, never took a cent from anybody, never—" She stopped again. Pressed the heel of her hand against one eye. "And the whole time he was filing paperwork so the mountain would be mine."

"He didn't do it to buy you back," Zephyrine said quietly. "I don't think he thought it would work. I think he did it because it was true. Because it was yours whether you ever claimed it or not."

Ruth looked at her then, eyes red-rimmed and furious and grieving all at once, the same expression she'd worn on the portage trail and in the whiteout and standing in the ruins of the sealed camp with the wind pulling at her hair. "How am I supposed to hate a dead man who leaves me a glacier?"

"You're not," Zephyrine said. "That's the trick of it. He's made sure you can't."

* * *

They talked until the light went long and gold across the water, and what they talked about, mostly, was what a person does with a mountain.

Junie came down at some point, drawn by the smell of coffee and the unusual sight of both women sitting still, and read the letter over Ruth's shoulder with her chin hooked on Ruth's arm, and went quiet in the way Junie went quiet only about twice a year. Dez arrived later with a string of grayling and pretended not to have feelings about any of it, and failed, because Dez had spent seven years failing to have feelings in front of other people and was getting worse at it, not better.

"It's a working claim," Dez said, turning one of the ore samples from Wrenn's cache in his fingers, watching the afternoon light catch the red oxidation along the fracture line. "Not a bonanza. Wrenn staked it right. There's decades of steady extraction in that seam—not get-rich, get-*kept*. A mine like that feeds a valley. Schools. Medevac flights. Winter fuel." He glanced up at Zephyrine, and there was something old and tired and hopeful moving behind his eyes. "A mine like that needs an air service."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The river said it for them, low and constant, the way it had said everything since the beginning.

"A claim needs supply runs," Ruth said at last, slowly, testing each word like a foothold. "In and out of the headwaters. Ore samples going down, equipment and provisions going up. Nobody else will fly it—the syndicate's got every licensed outfit in Coldbank scared stiff, or bought outright. And the calving corridor opens maybe two months a year, if it opens at all. You'd need a pilot who knows the river now. Who's flown it blind in a whiteout and lived." She turned to face Zephyrine fully. "Who's already carried each of us up it once."

"Ruth," Zephyrine began.

"I'm asking," Ruth said. "Me. Asking. Write it down somewhere, because it won't happen again." And then the gruffness cracked, just slightly, the way ice cracks before it goes out in spring. "He trusted you with his last living act. I've watched you fly that river like you were born in the current. And I—" She exhaled. "I don't want to run that claim alone. There. Said it."

Zephyrine looked at the faces around her. Dez, who had signed his name to a lie and spent seven years drowning in it and had walked back up a glacier to unsign it. Junie, sixteen years old and more competent than most grown mechanics, watching her with that fierce guarded hope she tried to disguise as boredom. Ruth, holding her father's letter like a compass she'd finally decided to follow.

Seven years ago she had been a woman with a clean logbook and a dirty name, measuring her life in miles because miles couldn't betray you. Every departure had been a small escape; every landing, a place not to unpack. She had thought the opposite of exile was home, and home was a thing that happened to other people.

Now she understood it differently. The opposite of exile was this: a river that knew her name, a crew that had bled beside her, a claim that belonged to the daughter of the man who'd saved her, and a question being asked out loud, in daylight, by someone who wanted her to stay.

"When the corridor opens," Zephyrine said, "I'll fly the first run myself."

Ruth nodded once, hard, and looked away fast, out at the water, and did not wipe her eyes where anyone could see, though everyone saw, and no one said a word about it, which was its own kind of tenderness.

* * *

Evening came down the valley the way it did in the high country—all at once, purple and cold. They ate grayling on the ferry deck with their boots propped on the rail, and Junie fell asleep sitting up halfway through Dez's second retelling of the tributary repair, and nobody woke her, and eventually Dez carried her inside without comment, which was the closest Dez had come to a speech in living memory.

Zephyrine stayed out. The stars over Mount Kessler were hard and close, and the glacier at its shoulder caught the last light like a blade laid flat. Somewhere up there was the source. Wrenn's ground. Ruth's ground now. Hers, if she wanted it—not on paper, but in the older way, the way you belong to a place that has nearly killed you and fed you and shown you what you're made of.

She took out the map. Wrenn's map, soft as cloth now from handling, its ink lines crossed and recrossed with his cramped annotations. She wouldn't need it anymore; she knew the river by heart. But she spread it across her knees anyway, and beneath the stars, and thought about the man who had drawn it, and the daughter who had carried it, and the strange arithmetic by which a dying stranger's bargain had become a family.

Her name was hers again. Her record was clean. Anywhere in the territory, any strip, any town, any job—she could go and be *Marchetti* in the open, head up, no more cash under false names, no more boots by the door.

She found, examining herself honestly, that she didn't want any of it.

What she wanted was a hangar at Kessler Bend. What she wanted was a lease with her actual signature on it. What she wanted was a schedule taped inside a cabin door, and medicine runs, and mail runs, and a claim's supply line threading up through the ice corridor twice a season, and these three impossible people waiting at the end of every flight.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she'd ride out to the Bend and talk terms with the trapper about the old hangar, and start the paperwork, and sign things—her name, her real name, over and over, until it stopped feeling like someone else's coat.

She was folding the map when Ruth came back out onto the deck, a lantern in one hand and something else in the other: a second envelope, newer, businesslike, bearing the recorder's stamp.

"Figured you should see this before I file anything," Ruth said. "The transfer papers. Claim holder can name a registered operator for the supply concession." She set the envelope on the rail between them, and the lantern threw their shadows long across the planks. "I haven't filled in the name yet."

Zephyrine looked at the blank line. One line, one pen, one word.

"Sleep on it," Ruth said. "But not too long. Corridor opens in six weeks, and I hear the best pilot in the interior is expensive."

"She works for shares," Zephyrine said. "Shares, and a room at the Bend, and first say in where we scatter the rest of the ashes."

Ruth smiled—slow, real, the first one Zephyrine had seen reach all the way up. "Deal," she said, and held out her hand, and Zephyrine took it, and the river ran on below them in the dark, carrying everything toward the sea except the things that had finally, deliberately, chosen to stay.

Inside the envelope, the blank line waited for morning.
