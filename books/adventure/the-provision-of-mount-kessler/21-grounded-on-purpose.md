## 21. Grounded on Purpose

The plane looked like something a glacier had chewed and spat back out, which was more or less what had happened.

Junie stood in the shallows at Kessler Bend with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her hands still smelling of avgas and river silt, and she made herself look at the damage the way Dez had taught her to look at rock: not as a ruin, but as a record. The left float was stove in along its forward third where the ice had taken it during the whiteout landing. The strut was bent two degrees off true — she'd measured it three times with a length of fishing line and a plumb bob made from a spark plug because nobody had thought to pack an actual level, and honestly that was fine, that was the kind of problem she liked best. The prop had a nick in one blade deep enough to catch a fingernail, and the cowling on the port side was crumpled like a beer can under a boot, and somewhere inside the engine there was a sound that wasn't supposed to be there, a faint dry tick that showed up only when the metal was cold and went away when it warmed, which meant it was real and it was patient and it was going to have to come out.

Two days' hike from the source. Two hundred miles of river behind them. A ledger full of paper that had unmade seven years of somebody's lie, and a magistrate in Coldbank who had read it twice and then said, quietly, *well*. And now this: a wrecked airplane beached on a gravel bar at the edge of nowhere, and nobody going anywhere until it flew again.

Junie had never been happier in her life.

She didn't examine that feeling. Examining feelings was for people with addresses.

* * *

They built a camp first, because Ruth said you didn't work on machinery hungry and cold, and Ruth's word on camp matters had become law somewhere around the portage without anyone voting on it. The trapper's old cabin at the Bend was half-collapsed, its roof sagged under six winters of snowload, but the stone chimney stood straight and the cache platform was sound, and by the second evening there was a fire going, tarps stretched over a lean-to for the engine work, and a drying line hung with socks that Junie privately considered a biohazard but had learned not to mention, because Dez's socks were Dez's business and some things a crew did not police.

It was strange, how fast the shape of them had set. Four people and a prisoner — though Voss was gone now, handed to the magistrate's constables in Coldbank with his arm in a sling and his testimony signed, and good riddance, except that Junie kept catching herself glancing at the empty spot where his tent had been, the way your tongue finds the hole where a tooth was. She didn't miss him. She missed nothing about him. But he had been part of the shape, briefly, the way a splinter is part of a thumb, and now the thumb was healing over and the whole camp felt different in a way she couldn't name and didn't want to.

Zephyrine worked like she flew: total, quiet, no wasted motion. She'd stripped the cowling herself the first morning, and when Junie crawled into the engine bay after her, Zephyrine didn't hover and didn't leave either. She just lay on the gravel beside the float, head half under the airframe, and passed tools before they were asked for.

"Number three cylinder," Zephyrine said, on the fourth day, when Junie finally got the rocker cover off and found the tick. "You hear it cold?"

"I hear everything cold," Junie said. "Cold tells the truth. Everything lies once it warms up."

Zephyrine was quiet a moment, and then Junie heard her laugh — not the polite short laugh she used at Ruth's jokes, but the other one, low and surprised out of her. "Where did you learn that?"

"Learned engines from a man in Terrace who drank," Junie said, working the valve clearances by feel, because feel was faster than gauges and she trusted her fingers more than any dial. "He said machines are honest and people aren't, and I was fourteen and believed him completely, and then I found out he was skimming parts off the charter company and selling them to the fishing lodges, so." She shrugged, shoulder grinding against the firewall. "Machines are honest. People are honest about machines."

"That's the truest thing anyone's said in this camp all week," Dez called from the fire, where he was supposedly cataloguing ore samples and actually napping with his hat over his face.

* * *

The rebuild took eleven days.

Junie kept count in grease pencil on the inside of the cowling, a little tally mark each dawn, and by day four she stopped thinking of the plane as damaged and started thinking of it as hers — not legally, not yet, nothing was ever legally hers, but in the way that a thing becomes yours when you've had your whole arm inside it and know every scar. They straightened the strut with a come-along and a spruce spar and Ruth standing on the far end as counterweight, swearing with a fluency that would have embarrassed longshoremen. They patched the float with sheet aluminum from the trapper's abandoned woodstove, riveted and sealed and ugly as sin, and when Zephyrine ran her palm over the patch she said, "It'll fly," in the tone of someone pronouncing a blessing, and Junie felt that sentence land somewhere under her ribs and stay there.

The prop was the hard part. A nick that deep needed dressing, and none of them had a prop dresser, so Dez — who turned out to have filed metal before, back in his geology field years, back before the drinking, back before — spent two evenings with a bastard file and a lamp, checking balance by hanging the blade on a nail through the hub bolt, and Junie watched him do it and understood something she hadn't let herself understand before: that everybody here was rebuilding something, and the plane was just the one everybody could see.

On the eleventh day the engine ran clean. Cold start, no tick. Zephyrine taxied it the length of the gravel bar and back with the water boiling off the floats in the morning sun, and the sound of it came across the Bend like weather, like something enormous and alive deciding to keep living, and Junie stood in the shallows with grease to her elbows and cried about it, briefly, furiously, facing away from camp, and if anybody saw, nobody said.

* * *

It happened that evening, at the fire.

Junie knew afterward that Zephyrine had planned it, because Zephyrine did not do anything unplanned — she'd learned that much watching the woman talk her way past a syndicate inspector with a forged permit while everyone's heart tried to climb out through their teeth. But at the time it seemed to just happen, the way the best things seem to.

Ruth had cooked something that was technically stew. Dez was telling a story about a moose he'd once mistaken for a boulder, which Junie had heard twice already and would happily hear forty more times, because the way Dez told it, the moose won. The light was doing the thing it did up here in the evenings, going gold and then rose and then a blue so deep it felt private, and the river talked to itself in the shallows, and Junie was thinking, the way she'd been letting herself think for about three days now, that this was the part she'd have to give up soon. That the ledger was delivered, the name was cleared, the magistrate had stamped things, and grown-ups always went back to their real lives when the adventure was over, and Junie was not anybody's real life. She was a stowaway with a tally of eleven marks in grease pencil and a duffel bag that fit in one hand. She'd been planning her exit speech. She was good at exit speeches. She'd written her first one at twelve.

"So," Zephyrine said.

Just that. And the fire popped, and Dez stopped mid-moose, and Ruth looked up from the pot, and Junie's stomach did something complicated.

Zephyrine didn't stand up. She stayed sitting on her heels across the flames, and she looked at Junie the way she looked at instruments — directly, steadily, like something worth reading accurately.

"The Marchetti-Wrenn Air Service needs a mechanic," she said. "Full-time. Year-round. Medicine runs, mail, supply work up the Kessler claim, whatever Ruth's ferry traffic turns into. It's not glamorous. It's cold in winter and the pay is whatever we can afford, which right now is almost nothing, and the hangar leaks, and I will work you harder than anyone has ever worked you, because I don't know how to do less than that myself."

Junie's mouth was open. She closed it. Opened it again.

"And there's a room," Zephyrine went on, and her voice did something small and careful, like a pilot's voice in rough air. "At the Bend. We'll build it out of the cabin — there's room for four if we're honest about it, and Ruth says she's honest about everything, so. An apprenticeship and a room. If you want them."

The silence lasted maybe two seconds. It felt like the whiteout. It felt like the moment between the ice taking the float and the plane stopping moving, when anything could still happen and the only thing you could do was hold on.

"You don't even know me," Junie said, and hated how young it came out.

"I know you rebuilt an engine on a gravel bar with a spark plug for a plumb bob," Zephyrine said. "I know you stowed away because you decided where you were going before anyone asked your opinion. I know you fixed a sabotaged fuel line in a tributary with half the tools gone and your hands shaking, and you did it anyway. That's the whole job description, Junie. You wrote it yourself."

"I'm fifteen."

"In March," Dez said mildly, from his spot against the log. "Which means by the time the flying season opens proper, you'll be sixteen, and apprenticed mechanics younger than that have run half the bush shops in this country since before any of us were born. I checked. I was bored."

"You checked?" Junie said.

"Somebody had to make sure she wasn't breaking the law," Dez said. "Given her history with the law."

Ruth snorted into the stew pot. And Junie sat there with the fire on her face and the thing she'd been bracing against for three days suddenly not there anymore — the exit speech dissolving in her chest like sugar in river water — and she found that what was underneath it was not relief exactly. It was heavier than relief. It was the weight of being wanted on purpose, by name, in front of witnesses, which was a weight she had no practice carrying and which she suspected, looking at Zephyrine's face across the flames, that Zephyrine had even less.

"Why me?" Junie said, quieter. "You could get a real mechanic. Somebody certified. Somebody who isn't —" she gestured at herself, at the duffel bag, at the whole unsupervised fact of her.

"Because I spent seven years being nobody certified," Zephyrine said. "Seven years signing fuel chits instead of leases, sleeping wherever the plane was, keeping moving because staying still meant getting caught. And I got very good at it. And I will tell you the truth about it, since apparently that's what this crew does now: it nearly killed me. Not the flying. The alone." She looked into the fire. "I'm done teaching that curriculum."

Nobody spoke. The river filled the space, talking to itself.

Then Zephyrine reached into her jacket and took out a folded piece of paper, and held it out across the fire, and Junie took it because her hands moved before her brain voted.

It was a lease. An actual lease, handwritten in Zephyrine's square pilot's print, describing a room at Kessler Bend in terms of walls and a window and a stove pipe, with a line for rent that said *in kind, mechanical labor, fair value*, and a signature block at the bottom. Zephyrine Marchetti, lessee. And below it, a second block, blank, waiting.

"First lease I've signed in seven years," Zephyrine said. "Fuel chits, that's all I've put my name to since — well. Since. You sign your name to a chit and it means nothing, you're gone before the ink matters. A lease means you're saying you'll be somewhere tomorrow. And the day after." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and almost cost her something. "Turns out that's the bravest signature there is. Harder than the whiteout. I mean that."

Junie looked at the paper. At the blank line. At the grease still under her own fingernails, eleven days of it, ground in past the quick.

"If I sign it," she said, "I'm not running anymore either. That's what it means. Right? Both of us. Nobody's leaving."

"That's what it means," Zephyrine said.

Junie took the pencil Dez handed her — he'd been holding it ready, the sneaky old rock-reader, he'd known, they'd all known, they'd probably rehearsed it while she was under the engine — and she signed her name on the blank line, JUNIPER VASQUEZ, in letters bigger than necessary, because if she was going to belong somewhere on purpose she was going to do it loudly.

Ruth banged the ladle on the pot rim like a bell. Dez said something about witnesses and consideration and common-law apprenticeship that nobody listened to. And Zephyrine took the lease back, folded it along its crease, and put it inside her jacket, over her chest, the way you carry the papers that matter, and Junie watched her do it and thought: *oh.* Thought: *that's what it looks like from the outside. That's what I look like to them.*

She'd been family for eleven days and had only just been told.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned down to the coals-and-stars hour and Dez was asleep and Ruth was doing her last round of the lines and the cache, Junie sat on the beached float of the repaired plane with her knees drawn up, looking down the long dark throat of the Kessler toward Coldbank and everything downstream.

Zephyrine came and sat on the float's other end. Neither of them said anything for a while. That was another thing Junie had learned this summer: that you could sit next to somebody and not talk and it wouldn't mean anything was wrong. She was still building the muscle for it. It was coming along.

"My dad used to fly out of a strip near here," Zephyrine said eventually. "Before. The hermit told you some of it?"

"He told me your dad died looking for proof for you," Junie said. "Yeah."

"He never met you," Zephyrine said. "But he'd have liked you. He liked anybody who took machines apart to see how they worked, and he especially liked anybody who put them back together better." She was quiet a moment. "That's the inheritance, if you want it. This service. It was always supposed to be that — medicine, mail, second chances, up and down these rivers. He just ran out of runway before he could build it. So we build it. All of us. That's the provision of Mount Kessler, if you ask Ruth, and Ruth's usually right."

Junie turned that over. Inheritance. She'd seen inheritances before — the other kids at the group home got them sometimes, a ring or a photo or a debt, and they carried them like stones. This didn't feel like a stone. It felt like the engine after day eleven: heavy, yes, but running clean.

"Tomorrow," Zephyrine said, standing, brushing gravel off her knees, "we start the cabin. Your room gets the east wall — morning sun, best light for close work. Ruth already has opinions about the stove placement. Strong ones. You'll want to be rested."

She walked back toward the fire, and Junie stayed on the float a minute longer, looking upstream now instead of down — up the dark river, toward the source, toward the glacier that had broken and healed and would break again on its own schedule, indifferent and magnificent, holding the whole year's calendar in its ice.

Downstream was where she'd always been headed. Every plan she'd ever made pointed that way: out, away, gone, the next town, the next shop, the next exit speech.

Upstream was where the room was.

She hopped off the float, picked her way up the gravel bar, and went to see whether Ruth would let her help with the lines, knowing perfectly well she wouldn't, knowing perfectly well she'd ask anyway.
