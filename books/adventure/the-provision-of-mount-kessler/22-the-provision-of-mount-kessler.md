## 22. The Provision of Mount Kessler

The morning the calendar turned to the anniversary of the calving, Zephyrine Marchetti woke before her alarm and lay still in the dark of her own room, listening to a sound she had spent seven years forgetting how to hear: rain on a roof that belonged to her.

Not a fuel chit. Not a bunk in some stranger's hangar with one boot by the door and the other packed. A lease with her name on it — *Marchetti-Wrenn Air Service, Kessler Bend* — signed in ink she'd chosen herself, in a hand that only shook a little when she wrote it. The paper hung framed above the desk now, because Dez had insisted, because Junie had laughed at him for insisting, because Ruth had said nothing at all and simply hammered the nail.

She rose, dressed in the dark out of old habit, and made coffee in the dented pot Junie had rewired twice and refused to replace. Through the window, the lake lay grey and patient under the last of the night, and the Beaver sat at its mooring like a dog waiting for permission. *Ox-Alpha* — no. She smiled at her own sleep-drunk thought. The plane was the *Provision*, painted across her tail in letters Ruth had stenciled freehand, and every morning Zephyrine looked at that name and felt something settle in her chest like ballast set right.

A year. Long enough for the scar on the float to heal into a story. Long enough for the magistrate's stamp to fade from the copy of her voided conviction that she kept — not out of bitterness, but because she had learned that pretending things hadn't happened was just another way of running. Long enough for the interior to learn her name again, and this time for the right reasons.

She flew the medicine run to Halfway Post on Tuesdays. Mail to the trapline cabins on Thursdays, weather permitting, and weather permitting meant Junie standing on the dock with her face tipped up, reading clouds the way other girls read gossip columns. And on the days between — the days that didn't fit any schedule — she flew what the valley had come to call second chances. The woman fleeing a husband with a rifle, carried out over the pass before dawn. The boy from the mining camp who'd taken a fall and needed a surgeon before his spine finished deciding what it would be. The old trapper who wanted, once, before he died, to see the country he'd spent fifty years walking through from the height of the birds. She never charged them all. She kept a ledger page of debts owed and debts forgiven, and if the arithmetic never quite balanced, well. Neither did grace.

* * *

By seven the hangar doors were open and the smell of dope-and-fabric and engine oil filled the space like incense. Junie was already under the *Provision*'s cowling, sixteen now and taller by two inches since spring, her braid tied back with a strip of red flagging tape because she claimed proper hair ties were a scam invented by people who didn't work for a living.

"Number three cylinder's weeping again," she announced without looking up. "I've got it. Don't hover."

"I'm not hovering."

"You're doing the breathing thing you do when you're hovering."

Zephyrine stepped back three paces, ostentatiously, and leaned against the workbench where Dez was hunched over rock samples in trays, his glasses down his nose, his hands steadier than they'd been a year ago though not yet steady. He'd been sober fourteen months as of Tuesday. Nobody had marked it. Everybody had known it. That was the way they'd agreed to do things here — the tribunal had dissolved itself the day they came down off the mountain, and in its place stood something quieter: people who noticed, and stayed anyway.

"Quartz stringers," Dez murmured, half to himself, tilting a core sample toward the light. "Same signature as Wrenn's headwater specimens. It runs the whole ridge. Ruth's claim is going to make her wealthy whether she likes it or not."

"She'll hate that," Zephyrine said.

"She'll hate it beautifully." He glanced up, and there was the old wry ghost of a smile. "You flying up today?"

"Today." She looked out through the open doors at the light coming up the valley, gold sliding down the glacier's shoulder like honey off a spoon. "We all are. If the weather holds."

Junie's wrench stopped mid-turn. "It's holding," she said. "I checked twice."

* * *

Ruth came down from the ferry landing at nine, carrying a tin box wrapped in oilcloth, and the whole bend seemed to adjust itself around her the way water adjusts around a stone. She was broader than she'd been a year ago — not softer, never that, but settled, the way a house settles into its foundation. The claim papers were hers now, notarized and filed, the Kessler source deeded to the daughter Tobias Wrenn had abandoned and then, in the last accounting of his life, remembered. She'd opened the letter forty times in twelve months. Zephyrine knew because Ruth read it aloud sometimes at night in the ferry cabin, to no one, or to everyone, pretending it was practice for something.

"You're staring," Ruth said, setting the box on the dock planks between them.

"You're early."

"Ferries run on time. Pilots don't. One of us has standards." But her voice had none of its usual edge, and her eyes went up the river toward the white shoulder of Mount Kessler, where the calved face of the glacier showed new and raw even from here, a wound the mountain wore openly now instead of hiding behind ice.

"He'd have hated this," Ruth said quietly. "All of us flying up there with his ashes like he's cargo."

"He'd have hated it," Zephyrine agreed, "and then he'd have told stories about it until we begged him to stop."

That almost got a laugh out of her. Almost. Ruth picked up the tin box and held it against her sternum the way you hold something both precious and heavy. "Dez says the ice corridor's open. Same window as last year?"

"Same window. Maybe wider. The melt's been early all spring."

"Then let's not waste it arguing about my father's feelings. He can have those in absentia."

* * *

They went up in the *Provision*, all four of them, which the Beaver could carry but complained about the way an old dog complains about stairs — willingly, loudly. Junie rode copilot with the checklist on her knee, calling items in a voice that had lost its flinch somewhere over the past year. Dez sat behind with the sample kits he no longer needed and brought anyway, because a geologist without rocks was, he maintained, a philosopher, and nobody wanted that. Ruth held the tin box in her lap and watched the river unspool beneath them — the Narrows where the syndicate inspector had photographed their faces; the tributary junction where sabotage had nearly ended everything and a teenage mechanic had saved them with wire, wax, and profanity; Kessler Bend, where a hermit named Aldous Crane had once flown with her father and now waved up at them from his cabin roof with both arms, like a man greeting weather he'd been promised.

Zephyrine flew the route from memory and found the memory had changed shape. A year ago these reaches had been a gauntlet — patrol boats, strafing warnings, the sense of eyes in every cutbank. Now the syndicate's seals were peeling off the survey posts, its patrol boat sold at auction to a fishing cooperative in Coldbank, its enforcer Voss testifying in a courtroom with the hollow look of a man who had discovered his employers' gratitude had a short half-life. The river ran brown-green and indifferent, carrying salmon and silt and the small traffic of people who lived here because they chose to, and the mountains stood over all of it with the patience of things that had watched empires be weather.

"There," Junie said, pointing. "The lake."

The glacial lake where they'd put down blind in the whiteout still showed the pale scar of the landing, already greening at its edges. Beyond it, two days' hike by last year's legs, lay the source: the hidden valley where Tobias Wrenn had chipped ore from forbidden ground, where the sealed camp had given up its ledger and its letters, where the truth about a falsified survey and a framed pilot had waited seven years in a cache box for someone stubborn enough to come get it.

Zephyrine brought the Beaver down on the lake in a long, smooth flare, and the floats kissed the water so gently that Dez looked up from his rocks and said, "Show-off," and she said, "Absolutely," and nobody argued.

* * *

The hike took most of the day, unhurried. Last year it had been a desperate climb against a closing window, packs cutting shoulders, Dez's hip screaming, the storm's wreckage still in their ears. This year they walked it like pilgrims who knew the road — Junie ranging ahead and doubling back, Dez stopping to hammer at outcrops and pronounce geological benedictions, Ruth silent and steady with the box, Zephyrine bringing up the rear and finding, to her surprise, that she wasn't scanning the ridgelines for pursuit. Her body had finally learned what her mind had been trying to tell it for a year: *no one is coming. You are the ones who came.*

They reached the source at late afternoon, when the light went long and amber and the glacier above the hanging valley groaned once, deeply, like a whale sounding — the mountain remembering its own anniversary. The abandoned camp stood as they'd left it, timbers silvered, the cache box empty now, its contents living in a courthouse and a framed conviction-voiding order and, in one copy, folded inside Ruth's letter.

They built no fire. It felt wrong, somehow, to bring smoke to this place. Instead they stood in a crescent at the lip of the outwash plain where the meltwater came off the ice cold and blue-white and certain, and Ruth unwrapped the oilcloth.

Tobias Wrenn's ashes were grey and fine as flour, and lighter than any man's life should weigh.

Ruth spoke first. Her voice didn't break, but it worked hard to stay level, and Zephyrine loved her for the effort. "You were a liar and a coward and you left me hating your name so thoroughly I built a whole self out of the hating." A breath. "And then you walked back into the worst country in the territory, alone, at seventy-one years old, to dig up the truth for a stranger who flew supply runs because she couldn't stop being decent even while hiding from the law." Another breath. "I don't forgive you. I think maybe I understand you, and I'm told that's worth more, though I have my doubts. Here's your mountain, old man. You found it first. Try to behave."

She scattered the ashes into the wind coming off the ice, and the wind took them out over the outwash plain in a long grey ribbon that thinned and vanished among the moraine stones, and Dez said softly, "He's part of the deposit now. Technically he's ore."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's ever said about him," Ruth said, and this time she did laugh, wet and rough, and Junie put an arm around her shoulders and got away with it, which a year ago would have cost the girl a hand.

Zephyrine spoke last, because it was hers to speak. She'd written nothing down. She'd decided the words would come or they wouldn't, and they came now, plainly, the way true things do.

"Tobias. Seven years ago somebody stole my name and I ran from the theft until running was the only thing I knew how to do. You handed me a map and a promise and I thought I was doing you a favor." She looked at the ribbon of ash dissolving into the valley, at the people standing beside her — a disgraced geologist learning to stand straight, a runaway kid who'd become the best mechanic on the river, a ferry pilot with her father's stubbornness and none of his cowardice. "Turns out you were handing me a crew. I don't know if you planned that. I don't think you were smart enough to plan that. But I'm choosing to believe you were kind enough to hope for it. Rest here. We'll take it from the top."

The glacier groaned again, longer this time, and far up the icefall a serac leaned, shivered, and let go — a single clean calving, white thunder rolling down the valley, harmless from this distance, magnificent from any distance. The spray of it drifted over them like a blessing or a baptism, and Junie shouted with pure joy, and Dez raised a sample vial in salute, and Ruth stood with her empty box against her chest and her face turned into the mist, crying without apology.

Zephyrine watched the ice go and felt the last of the old arithmetic leave her. For seven years she had measured her life in miles — fuel stops, safe houses, distances from the law, distances from herself. Every relationship a range calculation. Every kindness a weight allowance. She had flown light so she could leave fast, and she had mistaken the lightness for freedom when it was only hunger wearing a harness.

Now she measured differently. In the number of mugs on the hangar shelf. In the length of Junie's list of engine jobs she trusted herself to do alone. In the weeks Dez had been sober, counted silently by everyone. In the fact that Ruth's ferry schedule now had a blank column where Zephyrine's flights slotted in, pre-printed, permanent. In the sound of rain on a roof that answered to her name.

* * *

They camped one night at the source because Ruth insisted — "He waited seven years; we can wait one night" — and because none of them actually wanted to leave. They ate trout Junie caught with a technique she refused to explain and Dez pretended not to admire. They told the year's stories again, the good ones, the ones that had survived retelling: the forged permit at the Narrows, the portage where Ruth had carried double loads without complaint and then complained magnificently at the fire, the whiteout landing that Zephyrine still dreamed about and no longer woke from sweating. They did not talk much about Voss, except once, briefly, when Dez asked if she ever regretted going back into the collapse zone for him.

"No," Zephyrine said, and found it was simply true. "He was the man I was supposed to become if I'd let them finish making me. I couldn't leave him under the ice. It would've been like agreeing with them."

Ruth had nodded slowly at that, and said, "My father used to say the country provides — provision, he called it, like it was scripture. Food when you needed it, weather when you deserved it." She'd looked around the fire at all of them. "I think he had it backwards. The country doesn't provide. The people you carry up the mountain provide. The country just gives you somewhere to do it."

They flew home in the morning gold, the *Provision* heavy with nothing but themselves, which was the best load she'd ever carried.

* * *

Kessler Bend came up to meet them out of the south, and Zephyrine saw it the way she always saw it now, the way she suspected she would see it for the rest of her life: not as coordinates, not as an escape vector, but as a place with her people in it. The hangar. The dock. The ferry crossing with its cable sagging comfortably between banks. Aldous Crane's cabin with smoke rising. The garden Ruth had bullied into existence behind the ferry house, improbably green against the gravel. And on the dock, waiting, waving — because there was always someone on the dock now, that was the whole point, that was the entire revolution of her life — stood the mail carrier from Coldbank with a sack of letters, and beside her Mrs. Okonkwo from Halfway Post who'd come to arrange a medevac for her sister, and behind them a knot of kids from the trapline who had decided, collectively and without consultation, that Junie was the finest specimen of human being the world had yet produced and followed her around like ducklings.

Junie saw them and stood up straight in her seat, and Zephyrine caught the look on the girl's face — the guarded kid from the wheel well of her old life, watching a dock full of people who had come specifically for her — and said nothing, because some things you let a person discover privately.

She flared the Beaver over the water, touched down smooth as poured cream, and taxied in through the noise and the waves and the shouting of her name — *Marchetti! Junie! Doc Dez! Ruth!* — names called without rank or suspicion, just the ordinary music of a community that had decided they belonged to it.

Ruth climbed out first and was immediately absorbed into argument with the mail carrier about a crate of nails. Dez was mobbed by Mrs. Okonkwo, who had opinions about her sister's heart and wanted them confirmed. Junie dropped to the dock and was swallowed by children demanding to see the trout technique demonstrated, which she still refused to explain, thereby cementing her legend.

Zephyrine stayed in the cockpit a moment longer, hands loose on the yoke, engine ticking as it cooled. Through the windshield she could see the whole bend laid out in the evening light: the planes, the people, the river running easy toward the sea, and beyond it all the white shoulder of Mount Kessler holding its new scar up to the sky like a medal.

Seven years she had measured her life in miles. Fuel burned, distance kept, ground gained on the law and lost on herself.

She unbuckled the harness and looked out at the faces turned up toward her, waiting, impatient, laughing, hers — and understood that the gauge had changed for good. She measured in mugs on the shelf now. In names on the lease. In the weight of a tin box, empty, carried home lighter than it came. In the people at the end of every flight, who were also, she had finally admitted it, the reason for every flight.

Zephyrine Marchetti swung out of the cockpit, dropped to the dock, and walked into the middle of her life.

The mail carrier thrust a letter at her — postmarked Coldbank, official seal, the magistrate's office. Ruth, reading over her shoulder, went very still.

"Well?" Junie demanded. "What is it?"

Zephyrine broke the seal, scanned the first lines, and felt her eyebrows rise. She looked up at the three faces crowded close, at the mountain behind them, at the long gold evening opening out ahead like a runway with no end to it.

"It seems," she said slowly, "the government wants to talk about the Kessler headwaters. All of them. And they've asked for us — by name."

Ruth's mouth curved into something between a warning and a grin. "Us?"

"Us." Zephyrine folded the letter and tucked it into her jacket, over her heart, where the important cargo rode. "Finish tying down the plane, kid. Looks like we're going to need a bigger fleet."
