## 1. Miles, Not Years

The thief had a three-mile head start and a horse that was better than he deserved, which meant Lena Brightwater would catch him by the second salt pan or not at all.

She crouched at the edge of the flats where the hardpan began, reading the ground the way other people read letters. Two sets of hoofprints, one shod and one bare — the bare ones belonged to her own mare, Juniper, who was currently sulking in the shade of the waystation awning because Lena had made her gallop before breakfast. The shod prints were deep at the toe, shallow at the heel. A rider leaning forward, pushing his mount too hard on ground that would eat a careless horse alive.

*Amateur,* she thought, without malice. It wasn't even contempt anymore. Ten years of this work had worn the judgment down to something smooth and neutral, like a stone in a riverbed. She noted it the way she noted the wind direction, the heat shimmer, the faint white crust forming along the print edges that told her the tracks were four hours old, maybe five.

Four hours. At the pace those prints suggested, the horse would be foundering about now. Which meant the man riding him would be walking, which meant he'd be leaving boot prints instead, and boot prints in salt flat country were an apology written in ink that didn't dry.

Lena stood, rolled her shoulders until something in her back cracked like green wood, and started walking.

She measured her life in miles these days. Everyone else she knew counted years — birthdays, harvests, the anniversary of this or that. Lena had tried years once and found them useless containers. A year could hold anything. A year could hold nothing. But miles were honest. Miles added up whether you wanted them to or not, and they never pretended to be more than they were.

She'd done the arithmetic once, drunk on cheap whiskey in a waystation bunk outside Perdition Wells, and come up with a number that frightened her: eleven thousand miles since the night Corvin Hale died. Eleven thousand miles of tracking rustlers and horse thieves and one memorable bank clerk who'd run off with the strongbox and gotten exactly as far as his boots allowed. Eleven thousand miles, and every single one of them pointed away from a particular town with a particular saloon and a particular alley behind it, and if you laid all those miles end to end they would stretch from that alley to somewhere past the horizon, which was precisely the point.

The desert rangers called her the best tracker on the salt side of the range. They said it with respect. What they didn't know — what nobody knew — was that tracking was just running with better posture. Every trail she followed was a trail leading away from something else, and she followed them so well because she understood, bone-deep, the mechanics of flight. She knew how a fleeing man thought because she'd been thinking it for a decade. He looks over his shoulder. He picks soft ground without realizing it. He panics when the terrain opens up and hides when it closes in, always wrong, always choosing cover that shows his silhouette against the sky.

Fleeing men were predictable. That was the joke she never told anyone: she could catch any runner in the territory because she was the best one of all, and she'd simply never stopped.

* * *

She caught sight of him at the second salt pan, exactly on schedule, and felt the small cold satisfaction of being right, and nothing else.

He was leading the horse now, just as she'd predicted, both of them staggering a little. The animal was gray, lathered dark, its head hanging low. The man was young — twenty-five, maybe, with the kind of face that hadn't decided yet what it was going to be guilty of. He wore a stolen coat two sizes too big and boots that were already blistering him, because men who stole horses rarely thought to steal socks.

Lena didn't hurry. Hurrying was for people who weren't sure of the outcome. She walked across the pan with the sun coming up behind her so her shadow stretched out long and thin ahead of her, reaching him before she did, and she watched him see the shadow first and freeze, and then turn, and then understand.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, hell."

"That's about the size of it," Lena agreed.

"I didn't—" He stopped. Started again. "It's my brother's horse. Sort of. I borrowed it."

"Borrowed it at midnight," Lena said, "in a saddle you also borrowed, wearing a coat with another man's name stitched inside the collar."

The boy looked down at the coat as though it had betrayed him personally. To be fair, it had.

"Whose name?" he asked, which was either very stupid or very smart, and Lena had learned you couldn't always tell the difference until later.

"Doesn't matter yet." She came closer, unhurried, and took the horse's bridle. The gray lipped at her sleeve, exhausted and sweet-tempered, and she murmured something to it — she always talked to horses, low nonsense syllables, nothing anyone was meant to understand — and the animal sighed and leaned into her shoulder. "Here's how today goes. You walk back to the waystation, you give back everything that isn't yours, and you tell Magistrate Ochoa the truth, which is going to be embarrassing but survivable. Or you run, and I follow you, and we do this again tomorrow in worse country with less water. Your choice. I've got all day. Well. I've got most of it."

"You'd really chase me again?"

"Son, chasing people is the only thing I've ever been good at." She heard the words come out of her mouth and realized they were truer than she'd intended, and something flickered behind her ribs, old and tired. "Drink some water. You look like a dried apricot."

He drank. He cried a little, quietly, trying to hide it, and she pretended not to notice because pretending not to notice was a kindness, and kindnesses were about all she had left in inventory.

They walked back together, the boy leading his brother's horse, Lena trailing half a step behind out of habit — behind, always behind, watching the back of whoever she was with, because the night Corvin Hale died she'd been in front, and she had opinions about that now, ten years of opinions, none of them useful.

* * *

The waystation sat at the crossroads of nowhere and slightly less nowhere: a low adobe building with a tin roof gone patchy with rust, a water tank, a corral, and a porch wide enough to sleep on, which half the territory's drifters had done at one point or another. Its keeper was a woman named Sefa, built like a doorframe and about as talkative, who kept a pot of coffee going from dawn to well past the point where coffee stopped being a good idea.

Sefa looked up when they came in, took in the boy, the horse, the coat, and Lena's expression, and nodded once, which from Sefa was a full conversation.

"Ochoa's man comes through at sundown," she said.

"Then I've got time for coffee." Lena settled onto the porch rail and let the boy go inside to sit with his shame and Sefa's bread, which was famously terrible and therefore free, punishment and charity in the same loaf.

Juniper was waiting at the corral fence, ears forward, forgiving her instantly in the way horses forgive everything except late dinners. Lena rubbed her nose and let the quiet settle.

This was the part she hated. Not the chase — the chase was fine, the chase was simple, the chase was the only hour of any day when the machinery in her head went quiet and there was just ground and sign and the next mile. It was the after she hated. The catching. Because after came the sitting, and the sitting came the thinking, and the thinking always walked the same road no matter how many other roads she'd walked that day, and the road led back to an alley behind the Gilded Anchor in a town called Marrow Bay, and a sound she still heard sometimes when the wind came off the flats just right.

Not a gunshot. People assumed a gunshot. It hadn't been a gunshot. It had been quieter than that, and stranger, and she had spent ten years being grateful she couldn't forget it and furious that she couldn't remember it properly, both at once, which is the sort of thing that wears grooves in a person.

Corvin Hale had taught her everything. How to read sign. How to read people, which was harder, because people lied with their whole bodies. How to build a lock that would take a master thief a week to defeat and an honest man thirty seconds, because honest men should be able to get into things — that was Hale's philosophy, delivered while his big scarred hands assembled tiny brass mechanisms on the table of whatever room they were renting that month. *A lock isn't a wall, Lena-girl. A lock is a question. You want it to ask the right person the right question.*

She'd helped him build one, once. Just once. A strange little mechanism, nothing like his usual work, all counterweights and a keyway shaped like nothing she'd ever seen before or since. He'd worked on it through the night before he sailed — before the *Coral Triangle* sailed, though she hadn't known the ship's name then, hadn't known much of anything except that Hale had a job, a sea job, a salvage commission that paid enough for him to be cheerful about it, and Hale was never cheerful about money. Cheerful Hale was wrong Hale. She'd known that even at nineteen, and she'd said nothing, because nineteen-year-olds say nothing about the things that matter most.

And then the letter had come, six weeks later. And then the alley. And then eleven thousand miles.

"Lena."

She turned. Sefa stood in the doorway holding out a folded paper, her face arranged in the particular blankness she used for delivering things she considered other people's business. "Rider brought this through an hour ago. Said it was urgent. Said you'd want it fast." A pause, dry as the flats. "I said nobody wants anything fast in this heat, but he insisted."

The paper was good stock. That was the first wrong thing. Nobody sent good paper out here; the mail came on scraps and old wrapping. This was heavy cream-colored stock with a wax seal, and the seal was pressed with a mark Lena recognized from ten years ago and had hoped never to see again: a stylized V, crossed by a harpoon line.

Her hands went cold, which was absurd in that heat, and she broke the seal anyway, because her hands had apparently decided without consulting her.

The handwriting inside was slanted, confident, and infuriatingly neat. She remembered that handwriting from ledgers and contracts and one memorable bar tab that Isolde Vane had disputed on principle, itemizing her objections in this same elegant script while the tavern keeper aged visibly.

*Miz Brightwater,*

*I'll keep this short, as I know you dislike long letters almost as much as you dislike me.*

*You are difficult to find, which I assume is deliberate. It has taken me two years and a great deal of money, and I want you to know I resent every coin. But I have found you, and I am coming, and I will be at your waystation within the day.*

*Bring your memory. Leave your pride. You will need neither as much as you think, and I am bringing something you cannot outrun.*

*— I.V.*

Lena read it twice. Then a third time, standing on the porch with the sun hammering the flats white and the coffee going cold in her hand, and the strangest thing happened, which was nothing. No dread, no fury, not yet. Just a strange ringing clarity, like the moment before a storm breaks when the air goes flat and every sound stands out sharp.

Ten years of miles. Ten years of making sure that no road she traveled ever bent toward the coast, toward the water, toward Marrow Bay, toward the Gilded Anchor, toward the alley. And now a piece of cream-colored paper was telling her that all of it — every mile, every trail, every hollow competent day — had been a circle after all, and circles close.

Inside, the boy asked Sefa something about bread. Juniper blew softly at the corral fence. Out on the flats, the heat shimmered over the salt like the ghost of water, like the sea she'd spent a decade refusing to look at.

Lena folded the letter along its creases, tucked it into her shirt, and discovered that her hands were shaking.

"Well," she said, to nobody, to the flats, to the eleven thousand miles behind her. "Damn."

She went inside to finish her coffee, because whatever was coming would keep until it arrived, and she'd learned that much at least: you can't outrun a letter.
