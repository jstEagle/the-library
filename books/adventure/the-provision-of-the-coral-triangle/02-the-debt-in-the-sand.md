## 2. The Debt in the Sand

The waystation at Dry Fork had exactly three virtues: water that tasted faintly of rust, a roof that mostly kept the sky out, and a keeper named Hobb who asked no questions and remembered everything. Lena Brightwater had been stopping there for six years, long enough that Hobb kept her usual bunk swept and her usual silence unbroken, and she had come to think of it as the closest thing to home a woman could have without actually being caught by one.

She arrived two days after turning in the horse thief, riding a borrowed mule because her own horse had gone lame outside Saltmere, and she was tired in the way that had nothing to do with muscles. Ten years, she thought, watching the sun go down over the salt flats like a coin melting on a stove. Ten years since Corvin Hale died, and she still measured every road against that night. *This stretch is longer than the ride to the harbor. This ridge is where I'd have seen the smoke.* It was a stupid way to live. She did it anyway.

Hobb slid a bowl of stew across the counter — more turnip than anything else, but hot — and said, "You've got a visitor."

"I don't get visitors."

"This one didn't ask permission." He nodded toward the corner table, where a woman sat with her boots up on a second chair, eating with the unhurried confidence of someone who had already decided the place belonged to her. She was broad-shouldered, sun-browned, with a scar running through one eyebrow and hair cropped short enough to be practical rather than fashionable. Her coat was good quality and had been repaired often, which told Lena more about her than any introduction would have: money once, trouble lately, pride throughout.

Lena took her stew to the far end of the counter and ate standing up, the way she'd eaten for a decade, ready to move.

The woman let her finish. That was the first thing Lena noticed and the first thing she disliked — the patience of it. Most people couldn't wait. This one could wait like the tide.

When Lena set down the bowl, the woman rose, gathered both chairs, and carried them to the counter, setting one down across from where Lena stood. An invitation delivered as a fait accompli.

"Ranger Brightwater," she said. "Or do you prefer just Brightwater? You've dropped the ranger part in most of the reports I've read."

"Who's asking?"

"Isolde Vane." She said it the way other people said their ship's name — with ownership and a little love. "Captain of the *Kestrel's Mercy*, salvage and recovery, presently between fortunes."

"I've heard the name."

"I'd be disappointed if you hadn't." Isolde sat down uninvited, which Lena was beginning to understand was simply how she entered rooms. "Stew here is criminal, by the way. Ninety percent turnip."

"It builds character."

"So does debt." Isolde reached into her coat and laid a folded paper on the counter between them, squaring its corners with two fingers, precise as a surgeon. "But we'll get there."

Lena looked at the paper and felt the first cold thread of dread go through her, because she knew what it was before she touched it. Some documents you recognize the way you recognize a grave. Ten years old, oilskin-wrapped, the wax seal cracked but intact. Her own mark pressed into the bottom corner — not a signature; she'd never learned to write pretty — the crossed arrows and broken circle she'd burned into a hundred cattle brands and one catastrophic mistake.

"Where did you get that?"

"Hale gave it to me. The night before he sailed." Isolde's voice was even, but something moved behind her eyes — grief, maybe, or its well-preserved cousin. "He held it in trust. Said if he didn't come back from this run, I should find you and call it due."

"He's dead," Lena said. "Ten years dead. Contracts die with—"

"Not this kind. Not a partnership sworn and never dissolved. There's no death clause, Brightwater, because neither of you imagined needing one. You signed a salvage partnership with Corvin Hale, shares split three ways — you, him, and me, named as executing partner. It was never dissolved. And under coastal law, an undissolved partnership doesn't lapse. It waits." She tapped the paper once. "It's waiting."

Lena stared at the mark she'd made when she was twenty-two and certain the sea would forgive her everything. "That's not legal."

"It's absolutely legal. I checked with three magistrates and a man who owes me his life. One of the magistrates tried to buy it off me, which is how I know it's legal."

Hobb, wiping glasses at the far end of the counter, made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled in infancy.

"What do you want?" Lena asked.

"The Coral Triangle."

The name went through her like a fishhook. She kept her face still — a decade of practice — but inside, everything tilted. *The Coral Triangle.* Hale's last ship. Hale's last run. The wreck they'd never found, off a reef coast she'd spent ten years making sure she never saw again.

"Lost with all hands," Lena said carefully. "Eleven years ago."

"Lost, yes. With all hands, no. Two bodies washed ashore that were never accounted for in the crew manifest, and a hull that went down too clean for weather. But that's not why I'm here." Isolde leaned forward, elbows on the counter, and for a moment the captain's polish slipped and Lena saw the thing underneath, which was hunger — not for gold, Lena realized. For an answer. "I'm here because you're the best tracker on the salt side of the continent, and the reef coast is just tracking with water in it. Channels shift. Scars in the coral tell you where the current's been going for a hundred years. You read ground better than anyone I've ever heard of, and I need someone who can read it wet."

"No."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"There are no terms. The answer's no." Lena pushed the bowl away and finally turned to face her fully. "I don't sail. I don't dive. I don't go near that coast, and I especially don't go near that wreck, and if you say his name again I'll walk out of here and you'll never find me, because finding people is what I do and unfinding them is what I do better."

Isolde regarded her for a long moment. Then, infuriatingly, she smiled — not cruelly, but with the weary understanding of someone who had expected exactly this and budgeted for it.

"You ran for ten years," she said. "I know. I've kept half an eye on you the whole time, partly because I owed Hale, and partly because I wanted to see if you'd ever stop. You haven't. You catch horse thieves and turn them in and feel nothing. Don't look surprised — Hobb talks when he drinks, and I bought the drinks." She glanced down the counter; Hobb became intensely interested in a glass. "You're not living out here, Brightwater. You're hiding, and it isn't working, because you can't outrun a partnership. Believe me. I've tried to outrun things too, and the only thing I ever outran was my own good sense."

"I'm not going."

"You marked that contract yourself. Burned it into the page with your own iron." Isolde slid it across the counter until it touched Lena's hand. "I'm not asking you to break your rule about the sea. I'm telling you the sea has been holding a lien on you for ten years, and it's calling it in. You can come aboard angry — most of my crew comes aboard angry, it's practically a hiring requirement — or you can come aboard furious. Either way, you come aboard. We sail on the morning tide after next."

"And if I refuse?"

Isolde stood, collected the contract, and tucked it back inside her coat with the same surgical neatness. For a moment she looked almost gentle, which was somehow worse.

"Then I file the partnership claim with the magistrate at Port Callow, who seizes your assets — the mule, the tack, the eleven years of bounty wages you've been salting away under the name B. Marsh, which was clever, using your mother's name, right up until it wasn't — and assigns me as executing partner with authority to recover losses. You'd spend the next five years in courtrooms instead of deserts. Or—" she paused at the door, silhouetted against the last of the light, "—you come with me, do the job, and when it's done, I burn this contract in front of you and you never see me again. Your choice. But it's the only choice there is, and we both know you stopped having real choices ten years ago. You just liked pretending otherwise."

She left then, boots ringing on the packed earth, and Lena stood at the counter with her heart going like a drum in a storm and her stew untouched and cooling.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat on the bunk with the door barred and the lamp turned low and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for a decade.

Here was what nobody at Dry Fork knew. Here was what she'd buried deeper than any body: the night Corvin Hale died hadn't been an accident she'd witnessed. She'd been there. She'd done something — or failed to do something — and the difference between those two things was the whole width of the ten years since. She had helped him build something, the week before the *Coral Triangle* sailed. A lock, a mechanism, a secret sealed in brass and whalebone springs, and she had sworn to him she'd keep it, and keeping it had cost exactly what she'd known it would cost, and she had run rather than find out whether she'd chosen wrong.

And now the sea was sending her a bill.

She thought about the contract. Three-way shares. *You, him, and Vane.* She'd forgotten Isolde Vane existed — no. That was a lie, and lying to herself was the one skill she'd never managed to learn. She hadn't forgotten. She'd amputated. Vane had been there that last night in the harbor, laughing at something Hale said, rope-scarred hands and a grin like a knife, and Lena had thought, *there's a woman who'll outlive us all,* and then the ship sailed and the news came back and Lena had put a thousand miles between herself and every person who might say Hale's name out loud.

Until now. Until a woman with a patient smile and a piece of oilskin walked into the one waystation Lena trusted and laid the past on the counter like a fish, gutted and glistening.

*Three ways,* she thought. *He split it three ways. He meant for us to be in it together.* Which meant Hale had planned something, before the end, and whatever he'd planned had died with him, unless—

Unless it was still down there. Sealed. Waiting, like the contract waited.

Lena got up, barred the door tighter than it needed to be, and lay down staring at the ceiling beams. Outside, the wind came off the flats carrying the smell of salt from a coast four hundred miles away, and for the first time in ten years she let herself hear it.

*Six tides,* she would learn later. Six tides before the monsoon closed the reef passage for a season. She didn't know that yet, sitting in the dark with rust-tasting water and a decision like a stone in her chest. All she knew was that Isolde Vane had cornered her with her own hand-marked word, and that the only way out was through, and that the *Coral Triangle* — Hale's last secret, her oldest wound — was sitting at the bottom of the sea with her name somewhere inside it.

In the morning she found Isolde on the waystation porch, cleaning a sextant with a rag and the serenity of a woman who had never once doubted the outcome.

"Terms," Lena said.

Isolde looked up, and the smile she gave wasn't triumphant at all. It was tired, and relieved, and older than her face.

"One condition," Lena went on, before either of them could ruin it. "When this is done — whatever's down there, whatever you're really after — I want the truth. All of it. What Hale told you, what you know about that night, everything. No more pieces."

"That's two conditions."

"Lena." Hobb appeared in the doorway with a fresh mule and a look of genuine mourning. "Your tack's on the gray. I watered her."

"You knew?" Lena said. "You *knew* she was coming?"

"Hobb talks when he drinks," Isolde said mildly, wrapping the sextant. "And drinks when he's paid."

Hobb had the decency to look ashamed for approximately one second. "She said you'd say yes," he offered. "Said you were the stubborn sort that always says yes eventually, just needs the yes dressed up as a no first."

Lena took the mule's lead and swung up into the saddle, and the flats stretched out ahead of her, flat and blinding and familiar, and for the first time in ten years the road ahead pointed the wrong way — east, toward water, toward the coast she'd fled, toward a wreck with her mentor's bones in it and a lock only his hand could open.

A lock she had helped him build.

"Lead on, Captain," she said, and hated how steady her voice came out. "But if I drown, I'm haunting you specifically."

"Get in line," Isolde said, and turned her horse east.
