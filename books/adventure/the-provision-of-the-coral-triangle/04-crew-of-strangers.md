## 4. Crew of Strangers

The *Kestrel's Debt* sat at the end of the harbor like an apology nobody had finished making.

Lena stood on the salt-crusted planks of the pier and studied her while Isolde went ahead to argue with a harbormaster who clearly owed her money — or she owed him; with Isolde it was never clear which direction the bleeding ran. The ship was a coastal salvage brig, sixty feet if she was generous, forty if she was honest, with a hull patched in three places and a name painted over so many times that older names ghosted up through the fresh coat when the sun hit right. Lena could make out at least two: something ending in *-belle*, and before that, faint as a watermark, *Providence*.

"Cheerful," Lena muttered to herself. A ship named Providence that had been renamed twice and now answered to *Debt*. Somebody aboard had a sense of humor, or a grudge, or both.

She'd spent ten years avoiding boats on principle. Water was just sand you couldn't trust — sand that moved in the dark, swallowed your tracks, and gave nothing back. Out on the flats, a man's mistakes stayed where he'd made them. You could read a week-old trail in hardpan and know exactly how tired he'd been, whether he'd looked back, whether he'd known he was followed. The sea kept no such records. The sea kept *everything*, and told you nothing.

And here she was, contract signed under duress — well, signed under leverage, which Isolde insisted was different and Lena knew wasn't — about to sail out over the drowned grave of everything she'd spent a decade outrunning.

Isolde came back down the pier with the smug stride of a woman who'd won an argument by making the other man afraid to continue it. "Harbormaster says we're cleared for the morning tide," she said. "He also says if we sink inside the breakwater again, he's billing me for the crane."

"You've sunk her inside the breakwater?"

"Once. Bram insists it was a docking maneuver." She jerked her chin toward the ship. "Come meet your crew."

"My crew."

"Our crew. Don't flatter yourself into thinking I dragged you across four hundred miles of desert to be a passenger." Isolde's grin had teeth in it, but not cruelty — more like the grin of someone who'd already decided you were going to survive this and found the whole business funny. "You'll want to see what they can do before you decide how much to hate them. It saves time later."

* * *

The first one was on deck before they'd climbed the gangway — a boy, fourteen maybe fifteen, all elbows and sunburn, sitting cross-legged on the foredeck hatch with a slate balanced on his knees. He was drawing lines. Not pictures — *lines*, dozens of them, curving and crossing, annotated in a cramped hand with numbers and little symbols Lena didn't recognize.

"Pell," Isolde said. "Navigator."

The boy didn't look up. "Tide's wrong today," he said.

"Good morning to you too."

"No, I mean—" He finally glanced up, and his eyes were the pale gray-green of shallow water over sand, and they went straight past Isolde to Lena with the frank, unnerving assessment of someone checking a knot. "The tide tables are off by eleven minutes. They always are, this time of year, because the almanac doesn't account for the river outflow after the spring floods. Everybody navigates by the book and everybody arrives eleven minutes late and blames their helmsman." He said it the way other people said *the sky is blue*. Then he went back to his lines.

Lena crouched beside him without asking permission. Up close the slate was worse than she'd thought — it wasn't a chart, it was a *sentence*. Currents written out like handwriting, each line a stroke of the pen, the numbers marking where the strokes thickened and thinned.

"That's the channel approach?" she said.

Pell's head came around slowly, like she'd surprised him. "How do you know that?"

"Because it's a trail." She tapped the longest curve. "Water leaves tracks same as anything else that moves. This one's been walked hard — see how the edge is chewed? Something big pushes through here regular, and it drags the flow sideways every pass. Your line's good until here." Her finger stopped at a junction. "Then it's guessing."

"It is *not*—"

"It's interpolating. Guessing with manners." She sat back on her heels. "Out on the flats, the difference between a guess and a read is whether you're alive at sundown. Show me where your numbers come from and I'll show you what the water's actually doing."

Pell stared at her. Then, without a word, he licked his thumb and scrubbed out half an hour of careful work and started redrawing, muttering, and Lena understood she'd just been tested and passed, though she couldn't have said what the test was.

"He does that," Isolde said quietly behind her. "Redraws everything. Don't take it personally when he erases you too."

* * *

The second one was harder to find, because the second one didn't announce herself.

Lena found her by accident, poking around below decks — partly curiosity, partly the old ranger habit of learning a place by its shadows. The hold smelled of tar and brine and engine oil, and amid the coiled rope and stacked crates there was a woman sitting perfectly still on an overturned dory, hands loose in her lap, eyes closed, breathing slow.

Lena froze. For one absurd moment she thought the woman was dead — she was that still, that complete about it. Then the woman opened her eyes, dark and calm, and looked at Lena the way you'd look at weather.

"Sorry," Lena said. "Didn't mean to—"

The woman tilted her head toward a slate hanging on the bulkhead by a string. On it, in chalk, in a neat round hand: *OSA. DIVER. KNOCK FIRST.*

"You were meditating," Lena said. "I knocked with my boots. My mistake."

Osa picked up a piece of chalk from the dory's bench and wrote, unhurried: *YOU WALK QUIET. FOR A LAND ANIMAL.*

"I'm a ranger."

*I KNOW. ISOLDE TALKS.* Osa's mouth did something that was almost a smile — a small, dry movement, like a fish surfacing. *SHE SAYS YOU READ GROUND. I READ WATER. SAME JOB. DIFFERENT MEDIUM.*

Lena leaned against the doorframe. "Same job?"

*EVERYTHING LEAVES TRACKS,* Osa wrote. *WATER JUST WRITES SMALLER.*

They looked at each other for a long moment. There was something in Osa's stillness that Lena recognized from the mirror — the particular quiet of a person who had decided, at some point, that words cost more than they paid back. Lena had met men like that in the desert. She'd been half that herself for ten years.

"What happened to your voice?" she asked, then immediately wished she hadn't. It was the question everyone asked, and it was none of her business, and—

Osa didn't flinch. She wrote: *LONG STORY. BAD ENDING. ASK ME AFTER WE'RE RICH.*

Then, beneath it, smaller: *OR DON'T. THE NOT-TALKING WORKS BOTH WAYS. NOBODY ASKS A DIVER TO EXPLAIN HERSELF DOWN THERE.*

"Yeah," Lena said softly. "I know how that works."

Osa studied her for a beat, then held out the chalk. Lena took it, puzzled, until Osa tapped the slate — *YOUR TURN* — and Lena laughed, short and surprised, the first real laugh in longer than she cared to count. She wrote: *LENA. RANGER. ALSO LONG STORY, ALSO BAD ENDING.*

Osa took the slate back, read it, and nodded once, solemn as a treaty. Then she erased both messages and hung the slate back on its string, and somehow that felt less like dismissal and more like filing something important.

* * *

Bram she heard before she saw him — a clanging, cursing percussion rolling up from the engine compartment aft, punctuated by what sounded like a wrench losing an argument with a bolt.

"—no, you don't get to seize NOW, you miserable heap, we haven't even LEFT yet—"

Lena leaned in the companionway. The engineer was flat on his back under the engine, only his boots and forearms visible, a smear of grease climbing one cheekbone like a bad tattoo. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, gray-stubbled, with the permanent squint of someone who'd spent years looking at small things in bad light.

"Should I come back?" Lena asked.

"If you're Isolde, yes. If you're anyone else, hand me that rag and keep talking, it helps me pretend this engine respects me."

She handed down the rag. "You must be Bram."

"Bram Halloway, chief engineer, chief cook, chief apologizer to the harbormaster." He wriggled out far enough to look at her, and his eyes were quick and kind and missed nothing. "So you're the desert one. The debt."

"That's me."

"Huh." He wiped his hands with tremendous thoroughness, buying time. "Isolde doesn't collect debts for fun. She collects them when she's scared."

"Bram—"

"I know, I know, none of my business." He hauled himself upright with a grunt and offered a grease-darkened hand, which Lena shook without hesitation, because a man who offered you a dirty handshake was telling you exactly who he was and daring you to mind. "Fair warning, ranger. I owe Vane my life. Two winters back, my last ship went down in the ice straits and she turned around in weather no sane captain sails into and pulled me off a spar I'd been hugging for nine hours. So when she says jump, I check the engines first and then I jump. Whatever she's got you signed into — I'll see it done. That's not loyalty, exactly. It's arithmetic."

"And if the arithmetic goes bad?"

Bram smiled, and it made his face younger. "Then I fix the engines faster."

* * *

They gathered at dusk on the afterdeck — Isolde, Pell with his slates, Osa with her silence, Bram smelling of engine oil and the stew he'd made, which was better than it had any right to be given the galley looked like a brawl in a cannery. Lena sat slightly apart, the way she'd sat slightly apart from every campfire for ten years, and watched them, and realized they were watching her back.

"So," Isolde said, tearing bread. "Introductions are done. Now the part I actually care about." She pointed the bread at Lena. "Tell them why you're useful. And don't give me the ranger speech about tracking horse thieves, I've heard it, it needs work."

"I don't have a speech."

"She has a speech," Isolde said. "It's very grim. Lots of miles in it."

Lena set down her bowl. Fine. If it was a test, she'd take it properly. "Here's what I know," she said. "I've spent ten years reading ground that wants to lie to me. Sand shifts. Wind rewrites. Everything out there is trying to erase its own tracks, and my job was refusing to let it. From what I've seen since I got on this dock, water is the same animal with better manners. Channels silt. Reefs grow. Currents carve new paths every season and the charts are always a year behind and a fathom wrong." She looked at Pell. "Your almanac's off eleven minutes because the land changed and the book didn't. I find what changed. That's the whole trick. That's the only trick I've got."

Silence. Then Pell said, "Show me the reef approach tomorrow. Before we sail. I want to watch you do it."

"It'll be dark."

"Currents don't sleep."

"No," Lena said, and something in her chest shifted a half-inch, uncomfortable, like a boot breaking in. "They don't."

Osa raised the slate: *SHE PASSED. PELL, PAY UP.*

"There's a *pool*?" Bram demanded. "There's a betting pool about the ranger and nobody cut me in?"

"Two coppers each," Pell said, not remotely embarrassed. "Question was whether she'd quit before dinner."

"And the other question?"

Pell and Osa exchanged a look. Isolde suddenly became very interested in her stew.

"The other question," Pell said carefully, "was whether the captain brought you because you're good, or because you're guilty. People who run that far usually have a reason."

The fire crackled. Somewhere across the harbor a drunk was singing badly. Lena felt the old cold move through her, the familiar frost of a decade's practice, and started to build the wall — *none of your business, ask me after we're rich* —

But Osa was watching her, calm as deep water, and Bram had gone still, and even Isolde wasn't grinning anymore, and Lena heard herself say:

"Both. Probably both. Ask me again when we've raised whatever's down there, and I might answer differently." She picked up her bowl. "That's the best offer anyone's gotten from me in ten years. Take it or leave it."

Osa wrote on the slate, held it up: *TAKE IT.*

Bram snorted. Pell almost smiled. And Isolde lifted her tin cup and said, "To the crew of strangers, then. May we stop being strangers before the sea decides to introduce itself."

They drank. And it was warm, and it was almost easy, and Lena distrusted every second of it — and wanted it anyway, which was worse.

Later, when the others had turned in, Pell appeared at her elbow on deck, slate under his arm, and pointed east without a word. Lena followed his finger to the horizon, where the stars above the water had gone soft and smeared, blurred behind a veil of cloud so high and thin it barely counted as cloud.

Except it did count. She'd seen that exact haze a thousand times over the desert rim — the first breath of a storm still days out, lying about its intentions.

"Barometer's falling," Pell said quietly. "Faster than it should. Faster than any season I've charted." He hugged his slate to his chest. "Captain says six tides. I ran the numbers tonight, twice." His pale eyes came up to hers. "I got five."
