## 5. Six Tides to Monsoon

Pell had learned to read the sea the way other children learned their letters — slowly at first, with a great deal of crying about it, and then all at once, so that one morning he simply looked at the water and understood what it was saying.

The tide tables in Isolde Vane's cabin said something he didn't like at all.

He'd spread them across her chart table before dawn, because that was his job and because he wanted to be the one who told her. Navigator's privilege. Bad news travels best when it arrives from someone who has already done the arithmetic of how bad.

Six tides. That was what the moon had left them. Six lifts and drops of the whole ocean before the monsoon came up from the southeast like a drunk uncle at a wedding — loud, enormous, and impossible to reason with — and shut the reef passage for a season. Maybe longer. The old pilots' almanac said *four months, weather permitting*, which was the sea's way of laughing at you.

Pell was seventeen, small for it, with salt-bleached hair he cut himself using a belt knife and a bucket as a mirror, and he had been navigating since he was eleven, when his father's boat went down off the Sable Keys and Pell swam ashore holding the chart case over his head like a crown. The chart had saved his life twice: once by floating, once by giving him something to do with his hands while he grieved. He'd never really put it down since.

Now he stood over the tables with a grease pencil behind each ear — one black, one red, because you never knew when you'd need to mark a thing twice, once as it was and once as you wished it were — and waited for the captain to come below.

She came down at first light, boots first, the rest of her arriving in the order boots suggested. Isolde Vane moved through her own ship like a woman who had built it around herself out of spite and timber. She looked at the tables, then at Pell, then back at the tables, and Pell watched her face do the same arithmetic his had done, only faster and without any of the crying-about-it phase.

"Say it," she said.

"Six tides," Pell said. "Maybe five and a half if the barometer keeps falling, which it is, because I checked it three times and it fell every time, which is its whole personality."

Isolde grunted. It was, Pell knew, the sound of agreement.

"And Rennard?"

"Two tides ahead. If he holds his line." Pell tapped the chart where the reef channels braided together like rivers arguing. "But he won't hold his line. He'll cut inside at the Teeth because Dax Rennard has never once in his life taken the long way around anything, including arguments."

"Which means—"

"He grounds on the Teeth or he beats us there by half a day. There isn't a third option. The reef doesn't offer thirds. That's what I like about it."

Isolde almost smiled. Pell counted it. He kept a running tally of the captain's almost-smiles in the back of his logbook, currently at four for the month, which was a good month, and he suspected this one wouldn't count anyway because it died young.

Footsteps on the companionway ladder — heavy ones, land-heavy, the kind of steps that expected stairs to be where they were and got quietly offended by rolling decks. Lena Brightwater came down into the cabin ducking her head even though the beams weren't low enough to need it, which Pell noted and filed away under *desert habits, many, probably load-bearing*.

The ranger looked better than she had at the waystation, which wasn't saying much. She still had the look of a woman who had been dragged out of sand against her will and hadn't yet decided whether to be grateful. She crossed to the table, read the tide tables upside down — which Pell found both rude and impressive, because most people couldn't read numbers upside down at all, let alone tide tables, which were mostly numbers wearing a disguise — and then turned them around without asking.

"Six tides," she said.

"You can read those?" Pell asked.

"I can read tracks. These are just tracks the moon leaves." She traced the flood lines with one finger. "Everything moving leaves sign. Water included. You just have to know what it looks like when it's lying."

"The water lies constantly," Pell said. "It's my least favorite thing about it. Well. Second least. First is drowning."

* * *

Isolde laid it out the way she laid everything out, flat and hard, like dealing cards onto wet wood.

"Here's the shape of it." Her finger hit the chart. "Rennard's fleet is two tides ahead, here, past the outer shoals. We're here. The reef passage we came through—" the finger dragged south, "—closes first. Monsoon swell comes up from the southeast, piles against the outer bars, and by the sixth tide there's no getting in or out of these waters except through the northern channel, which will be a knife fight even in good weather, and this won't be good weather."

"So we go forward or we don't go anywhere," Lena said.

"We go forward or we spend the monsoon anchored inside the reef watching each other for four months, which I have done once, and I would rather be wrecked."

"That's a very specific preference."

"It comes from experience." Isolde straightened. "Rennard knows the window too. He's not racing us for pride. He wants the Triangle's cargo before the sea closes the door, and after that it doesn't matter who else knows where the wreck is, because nobody can reach it until the dry season, and by then whatever's worth taking will be in his hold."

Lena was quiet a moment. Pell watched her look at the chart the way she'd looked at the tide tables — like sign, like spoor, like a story written by something that could kill you. He decided, provisionally, that he liked her. Provisionally. His likings were provisional on principle; the sea had taught him that everything was provisional, especially ground.

"And behind us?" she asked.

"Closes first," Isolde said again, slower, letting it land.

Pell saw the exact moment it landed. It was a small thing, the moment — Lena Brightwater's hand stopped moving on the table, just stopped, mid-trace, the way a man stops walking when he realizes the trail he's following has circled back to his own campfire. Retreat wasn't dangerous. Retreat was already gone. The channel behind them was a door closing at the speed of the moon, and they were on the wrong side of it, and had been since the moment the keel crossed the bar.

"You knew," Lena said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — assessing. "When you signed me on. You knew the window was this short."

"I knew the monsoon was coming. I didn't know it was coming *early*." Isolde tipped her chin at Pell. "Tell her."

"The tables say six tides," Pell said. "The sky says five. The sky and the tables argue sometimes, and the sky wins, because the sky cheats." He shrugged. "I've sent up the signal kites. The gulls are flying inland already, which they only do when the pressure's dropping faster than the almanac thinks it should. Also Bram says his knee hurts, and Bram's knee has been right about weather more often than I have, which I try not to take personally."

"Bram's knee," Lena repeated.

"Engineer. Big fellow. Owes the captain his life, which makes him loyal in the way of men who've been pulled out of burning water and haven't figured out yet whether they're glad." Pell paused. "You'll meet him properly. Try not to stare at the burn scars; he hates that. Stare at his hands instead. They're beautiful, if you like machinery, which you should, because machinery is honest in a way people aren't."

"Pell," Isolde said.

"Summarizing," Pell said, and shut up, mostly.

* * *

They gathered on deck an hour later, because Isolde believed bad news should be delivered standing up, in the open, where everyone could see the horizon they were being asked to sail into. Pell approved of this. It was theatrical, but the good kind of theatrical — the kind that made fear into a shared meal instead of something each person chewed alone in the dark.

The crew assembled along the rail. Osa stood at the bow end, silent as always, coiling a line with the kind of attention most people gave to prayer. Osa didn't speak — hadn't spoken since childhood, Pell had heard, though no one seemed to know why, and no one aboard asked, because Osa's hands said plenty and were always right. Beside Osa, Bram leaned on the rail like a friendly cliff, his big burned hands folded, his knee wrapped in a rag that he claimed was medicine and everyone else claimed was a rag.

And Lena Brightwater stood apart, arms crossed, desert-worn and sea-unproven, watching Isolde the way a wild horse watches a corral gate.

"Six tides," Isolde said, without preamble. "Then the monsoon seals the reef for a season. Rennard's fleet is ahead of us and cutting for the inner channel. Behind us, the passage we used closes first. So hear me plain: there is no going back. Anyone who wants off does it now, at the harbor, in a shoreboat, with my blessing and a share of nothing."

Silence, except the rigging ticking and the water slapping the hull in that patient, conversational way water has.

Bram spoke first, because Bram always spoke first when the choice was between loyalty and safety, having discovered years ago which one he was built for. "I'm in," he rumbled. "My knee says storm, my knee's usually right, and I'd rather be useful in a storm than comfortable before one."

Osa simply looked at Isolde, then at the southern horizon, then made a short, sharp gesture — flat hand chopping down, then fist closing. *Door closes. We go through.* Pell translated aloud out of habit, though by now everyone aboard read Osa well enough.

That left Lena.

Pell found himself genuinely curious. He'd watched her for two days now, the way he watched everything — currents, birds, the small tells people leaked without knowing. And what he'd seen was a woman built entirely of exits. Every time the conversation turned toward commitment, some part of her shifted weight toward a door. It was in how she stood near railings. In how she never fully unpacked her gear. In how she slept, or didn't — light as a gull, ready to lift off.

A woman made of exits, standing on a ship whose doors had just closed.

"Well, ranger?" Isolde said. "You heard the terms."

Lena looked south, toward the channel they'd come through, invisible now beyond the harbor headlands but present in her face all the same. Pell watched her measure it — not the distance, he realized, but the *cost*. She measured things in cost. He'd seen her do it with words, with favors, with looks. Everything priced.

"The channel closes in how long?" she asked Pell directly.

"Fifth tide starts closing. Sixth, it's suicide." He paused, then added, because navigators deal in precision and precision is a kindness: "If you took a shoreboat now, alone, you might make it. Might. The bar's already getting lumpy."

Something flickered across her face — and Pell, who collected flickers the way other boys collected coins, recognized it. Not relief. Not fear. Something sadder and stranger. The look of a person checking an exit and finding, to their genuine surprise, that they didn't want to use it.

"No," Lena said. "No shoreboat." She turned to Isolde, and her jaw did something stubborn. "But understand me, Vane. I'm not crew. I'm not yours. I'm here because of a contract I signed ten years ago and regretted for nine of them. When this job's done, we settle accounts and I walk."

"Understood," Isolde said, in the tone of a woman who understood perfectly and believed none of it.

Pell hid a smile. He'd seen that tone before. It was the tone Isolde used on hulls she intended to keep.

* * *

The engines turned over at mid-morning.

Pell felt it through the deck before he heard it — a shiver rising from the keel, the ship waking up the way a horse wakes, all at once and slightly offended about it. Bram had spent the night in the engine room coaxing the old plant back to life, and now it answered him with a deep, uneven heartbeat that smoothed out, stroke by stroke, until it sounded less like machinery and more like a very large animal deciding to cooperate.

Harbor lines came off. The *Vane's Fortune* — Isolde had named her salvage ship after herself, which Pell considered either vanity or honesty, possibly both — eased out past the breakwater and turned her bow southeast, toward the reef coast and the drowned ship waiting somewhere inside all that coral.

Pell climbed to the pilothouse roof with his charts and his two grease pencils and began the real work: reading the water ahead, marking the flood lines, hunting the lie in every current. Below and behind him he could hear the others settling into the rhythm of a ship underway — Bram humming tunelessly in the engine room, Osa's feet moving silently along the deck, Isolde's voice giving helm orders in that flat iron voice of hers, and Lena Brightwater standing at the stern rail, watching the harbor shrink behind them.

Watching the closed door, Pell thought. Watching it stay closed.

He understood that better than he let on. He'd lost a father to the sea and a childhood to the chart case, and he knew what it was to measure your life in distances — miles from the wreck site, days from the last port, fathoms from the person you used to be. The ranger measured in miles. He'd heard her do it, talking to Isolde at the waystation: *ten years, ten thousand miles, one mile at a time*. As if distance were armor. As if you could outrun a thing by putting enough ground under it.

But the sea didn't work that way. The sea was round. Every course eventually bent back on itself, and the thing you fled was always, mathematically, ahead of you too.

Pell marked a red X on the chart where the Coral Triangle lay drowned, and a black line showing their track, and noticed — with the small cold pleasure of a navigator seeing truth arrive on schedule — that the two lines were converging. Of course they were. Everything converged. That was the whole joke of navigation.

Ahead, the horizon had gone a strange bruised color at its southern edge, soft and dark, like a shadow falling upward. The monsoon's outrider, still a day and a half out, maybe less. The gulls had known yesterday. The gulls always knew first.

Behind them, the harbor was gone. Ahead, the reef waited with its teeth. Somewhere between, two tides north, Dax Rennard's fleet was making the same run with a head start and worse judgment.

Pell licked the tip of his red pencil and wrote the number at the top of his working sheet, large, where he'd have to look at it:

**TIDES REMAINING: 6**

Then, because the sky had already begun to disagree, he crossed it out and wrote **5½**, and underlined it twice.

Below him, the engines beat on, steady now, committed, carrying them all forward into water that was already beginning to close behind.

* * *

It was near dusk when the lookout sighted the smoke.

Not engine smoke — woodsmoke, greasy and black, smeared along the horizon like a thumbprint. Pell was already in the crosstrees with his glass when the call came down, and he found it fast: a smudge of burning on the water where no burning should be, dead ahead, right where the inner channel bent past the first of the coral teeth.

He glassed it a long time. Wreckage, scattered wide. A hull section, charred. And riding the swell among the debris, shapes that his eye refused for a moment to name and then named anyway: boats. Empty ones. Or nearly empty.

He slid down the shrouds and found Isolde at the wheel, Lena beside her, both already reading his face.

"Report," Isolde said.

Pell swallowed. "Fire ahead, on the channel line. Fresh — hours old, not days. Debris field spread over a quarter mile." He paused, and made himself finish, because navigators deal in precision and precision is a kindness. "Captain — somebody's already met the Teeth. And I don't think it went the way they planned."
