## 6. The Channel of Teeth

The sea, it turned out, was just a desert that had learned to lie about it.

Lena stood at the bow of the *Kestrel's Debt* — a name she'd decided to take personally — and watched the water change color the way sand changed color at dawn: not all at once, but in bands, in seams, in places where something underneath was pushing up or pulling down. The charts spread on the deck box beside her called this stretch of coast nothing useful. Charts never did. A chart told you where things were supposed to be. It didn't tell you what they'd done lately.

"Say that again," Pell said beside her, squinting at her hands like they might be performing sleight of hand.

"I said the deep channel bends east." She pointed with her chin, keeping her eyes on the water. "Your chart has it running straight past the second headland. Your chart is wrong."

"The chart's three years old."

"So's bread," Lena said. "You don't navigate by either."

Pell made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled in its crib. He was sixteen, maybe seventeen, all elbows and salt-cracked lips, with a habit of standing too close to the rail for comfort and reading the current tables the way monks read scripture — aloud, at dawn, with an air of grievance when the sea failed to comply. He'd been doing it since before first light, muttering numbers like penance.

"Show me," he said finally.

So she did. That was the strange part. On land she'd have shown no one; showing people how you tracked was showing them how to catch you. But out here there was nothing behind her to catch. Just open water and a crew of strangers who kept looking at her like she was a tool Isolde had bought at auction.

"See the color off the point? Green going gray-green." She crouched at the rail, close enough to the water to smell it — brine and weed and something faintly rotten, the reef breathing. "On the flats we'd call that hardpan under dust. Ground that looks walkable and isn't. The light color means shallow coral. The dark seam past it — that's your channel. But look at the edge of it. See how it's not clean?"

Pell leaned out. His hair whipped across his face and he ignored it with the discipline of someone who'd been ignoring worse. "It's... ragged?"

"Ragged means moving. Water's been chewing at that edge recently. Fast water, from the northeast, which isn't what your tide table promised you." She straightened. "Desert rule: trust the ground over the map, but trust the scars on the ground most of all. Everything leaves tracks. Sand does. Coral does. Currents are just rivers that learned to swim."

"That's not a real saying."

"It is now. I just said it."

He grinned despite himself, then caught it and put it away, because he was sixteen and grinning at her would have cost him status with whatever invisible tribunal governed teenage dignity aboard ships. Lena understood tribunals. She'd spent ten years avoiding several.

* * *

Isolde Vane found her an hour later, which meant Isolde Vane had been watching her for fifty minutes and had chosen the moment of maximum dramatic convenience, because that was simply who Isolde was as a person.

"You're reading the reef," Isolde said, settling against the rail with the ease of a woman who owned the rail, the ship, and possibly the concept of rails.

"I'm looking at it."

"You're reading it. Bram says you haven't left the bow since dawn. Osa says—" Isolde paused, and Lena had learned enough in two days aboard to know that Osa communicating anything at all was an event worth pausing for. "Osa says you stand like someone following spoor."

"Osa talks?"

"Osa communicates. There's a difference, and if you're smart you'll learn it before she decides whether you're worth the effort of the second kind." Isolde turned her face into the wind. Up close, in honest daylight, she looked older than her reputation — fine lines around her eyes, a scar along her jaw Lena had never asked about and knew better than to. Ten years hadn't softened her. They'd just given the sharpness somewhere to live. "Well? Are we going to die today?"

"Not today." Lena nodded ahead, where the reef line rose out of the water like the spine of something enormous sleeping just under the surface — which, functionally, it was. "But your chart wants us through that gap." She pointed left, where a broad, inviting passage split the coral. "And the gap is lying. There's a bar across it, maybe four feet down. I can see the water piling on it. Any ship that goes through there becomes a very expensive reef decoration."

"And the real way?"

Lena traced it with her finger, a long slow arc that looked, to anyone who hadn't spent a decade reading broken ground, like madness. "East of the headland. Narrow. The current's cut a channel through the coral and it keeps cutting, which means it's deep, which means it's passable — but only on the turn, and only if we go exactly where the water tells us and nowhere else." She looked at Isolde. "It's called tracking, Captain. You follow the trail the maker left. In this case the maker is the sea, and it's left you one path and a lot of ways to be wrong."

Isolde studied the arc. Then Lena. Then the arc again, longer.

"Dax Rennard has sailed these waters twenty years," she said at last. "I've sailed them fifteen. Neither of us saw that."

"Neither of you grew up in country that killed you for misreading it." Lena shrugged, uncomfortable with the weight of what she'd just said and where it wanted to settle. "Give me a desert and a week and I'll find you water nobody knows about. Turns out the skill travels."

Something moved through Isolde's expression — quick, quickly buried, but Lena caught it anyway, because catching things was the other half of the same skill. It looked almost like relief. It looked almost like hope. Both were more dangerous than the reef.

"Take us through, then," Isolde said. "Yours till we're clear."

* * *

The Channel of Teeth earned its name honestly.

From the masthead — Lena went up because she refused to steer blind, and because heights were the one thing the desert had never taught her to fear — the passage showed itself as a winding corridor of blue-black water between walls of coral so pale they seemed lit from beneath. The swells came in long and lazy from the open sea, but inside the channel they behaved badly, stacking and shearing, and the wind carried a sound like grinding, patient stone.

Bram had the wheel. He was a mountain of a man gone soft around the middle, with forearms like dock rope and a permanent expression of mild apology, as though the ocean had inconvenienced him personally and he felt partly responsible. He also, Lena had noticed, watched everything — the sails, the water, the crew, her — with eyes that missed nothing and commented on less.

"Talk me through it," Isolde called up.

"Hard to port after the first marker rock," Lena shouted back. "Then hold center until the water goes green — green means shallow shelf on both sides, don't panic, it's supposed to. Then the channel dog-legs right where the current's eating through. If you see white water ahead, that's the teeth. We do not go near white water. White water is the reef telling you no."

"And if the current disagrees with you?"

"Then the current wins. It always wins. We're just negotiating."

She heard Bram chuckle below, a low rumble. "I like her," he announced to the deck generally.

"Nobody asked you, Bram," Isolde said.

"No, ma'am. Just noting it for the record."

They came in on the turn, as promised, and the *Kestrel's Debt* threaded the gap with six feet of black water under her keel and pale coral rising on either side like the ribs of drowned cathedrals. Fish scattered ahead of the bow in silver sheets. Once, in a deeper pocket, something vast and unhurried turned beneath them — a shadow the length of the ship — and Pell, hanging off the shrouds, made a noise like a kettle and pretended afterward that he hadn't.

Lena stood at the masthead and read the water the way she'd once read washes and ridgelines, calling the turns before they came, feeling the hull answer her voice through Bram's hands on the wheel. And here was the thing she would not admit to anyone, least of all herself, for another hundred miles yet: it felt good. Not competent-good, not hollow-good, the way catching a horse thief felt good, ticking a job off a list in a life that had stopped having lists worth ticking. This felt like the old feeling, the young feeling, the one from before Corvin Hale died — the feeling of being exactly the right person in exactly the right place, of a skill meeting a need like a key meeting a lock.

She hated that it felt good. She held onto the hating, because it was familiar, and familiar was safe.

* * *

They smelled the wreck before they saw it.

A thread of char on the wind, old smoke gone sour, threading through the salt. Lena's head came up like a hound's. Below, Osa — who had materialized at the bow rail without anyone hearing her arrive, as was apparently her custom and her weapon — went very still.

"There," Lena said, pointing.

The lead ship of Rennard's fleet lay where it had died, grounded athwart a coral shelf half a mile short of the true channel, burned to the waterline and beyond. Her stern rose black and skeletal above the surf, ribs exposed, masts snapped and trailing rigging like hair. Gulls had already claimed her. They wheeled off her yards in noisy clouds, indignant at visitors.

The crew gathered along the rail without being ordered to. Nobody spoke for a while. Even the gulls seemed to make more noise than the sailors did.

"How?" Pell asked quietly. He wasn't asking how a ship burns. He was asking how a twenty-year man could end up here, on a shelf, in plain sight of the passage he'd come to run.

Lena studied the wreck the way she'd once studied a campsite abandoned in a hurry — not the ruin itself but the story written around it. "They came in fast and confident," she said. "Night approach, probably, or dawn. Running the wide southern gap because it looks right on a chart." She pointed. "See the disturbed water upstream of the hull? That's their wake, still scarring the coral. They hit the bar I flagged this morning — the one your chart doesn't know about. Punched through it or over it, tore her bottom out, drifted onto the shelf, and burned. Fire from the galley, most likely, or the lamp oil. Once she grounded, nobody was putting it out."

"They lost a whole ship," Bram rumbled, "to a sandbar."

"A coral bar. And to certainty." Lena turned from the rail. "That's the lesson, if anyone wants it. Rennard didn't fail because he's careless. He failed because he trusted a map over the ground. Out here the ground is the only honest thing on the water."

"She's cheerful," Bram noted.

"I'm accurate. Cheerful is extra."

Isolde appeared at her shoulder — she did that, arrived like weather — and for a moment they regarded the wreck together. Lena felt the question coming before it was asked; she'd spent ten years feeling questions coming, usually the ones she didn't want to answer.

"You could have let us find that ourselves," Isolde said. Low, just for her. "Could have played it safe. Let Rennard's loss teach us the lesson at his expense."

"I could have." Lena kept her eyes on the burning-ship bones. "But then we'd have learned it slower, and slower costs tides, and you've told me twice now how many tides we have. Six, minus the ones we've already spent getting here." She paused. "Also, I don't like watching people drown. Even his people."

"Even Dax Rennard's."

"Especially people who aren't me. It's a flaw. I'm working on it."

Isolde laughed — one short bark, surprised out of her, immediately regretted. Lena filed the sound away with the small, growing collection of things about this ship she was trying very hard not to care about: Pell's strangled laughs, Bram's commentary, the way Osa had started leaving the good coffee where Lena could reach it without being asked, which from Osa was practically a sonnet.

* * *

They cleared the last of the teeth at slack water, the *Kestrel's Debt* sliding into a wide lagoon calm as poured glass, and anchored in ten fathoms over sand so white it glowed up through the water like a lamp. Ahead, the outer reef curved away toward the dive site, and beyond it the open sea lay flat and deceptively kind, holding its breath.

The crew worked the anchor in easy rhythm, and Lena found herself part of the rhythm without having decided to be — hauling when Bram hauled, flaking line when Pell flaked it, falling into the wordless choreography of a working deck. It had been years since she'd stood in a crew of any kind. She'd forgotten how it felt: the way labor shared becomes a kind of conversation, the way strangers stop being strangers one task at a time.

She caught Pell watching her again. This time he didn't hide the grin.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. It's just — you steered us through the Channel of Teeth on your first try, and Rennard's best pilot wrecked his flagship in it." He coiled his line with a flourish. "Osa says you're staying."

"Osa says a lot of things."

"Osa said one thing." Pell shouldered the coil and headed aft, then paused, and delivered his parting shot with the deadly precision available only to teenagers and navigators: "Osa's never wrong about people. Ask Bram sometime why he still owes her his life."

He was gone before she could respond, which she suspected was the point.

Lena stayed at the rail as the light went gold and then violet over the lagoon. Somewhere out there, past the reef, the Coral Triangle waited with her dead mentor's secrets locked in her dead mentor's iron, and somewhere behind her, Rennard's surviving ships were regrouping and hungry, and somewhere inside her, a decade of carefully maintained distance was developing, against her will and without her permission, a crack in it shaped exactly like a boy who laughed at her jokes and a diver who shared her coffee and a captain who'd laughed — actually laughed — at something Lena had said.

Belonging, she thought grimly, watching the first stars come out over black water. First sign of trouble.

She was still cataloguing the symptoms when Osa appeared beside her at the rail — silent as ever, pointing not at the stars or the reef but down, at the glowing sand below the hull, where the anchor light reached.

There, printed crisp and fresh in the white sand ten fathoms down, was a track. Long. Deliberate. Moving against the current.

Whatever lived in this lagoon, it had just walked under their ship — and it was heading for the wreck.
