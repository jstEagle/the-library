## 7. What the Wreck Was Carrying

Isolde Vane had told a great many lies in her life, and she had gotten reasonably good at sorting them into categories. There were the lies you told to survive — those were practically currency. There were the lies you told to be kind — those were expensive but occasionally worth it. And then there were the lies of omission, which were the worst kind, because they didn't feel like lies at all until the moment someone stood in front of you asking the right question, and you realized you'd been carrying one around for weeks like a stone sewn into your coat lining.

Lena Brightwater had just asked the right question.

They were alone in the stern cabin, which was less a cabin than a closet that had lost an argument with a ship's carpenter. The *Kestrel's Mercy* rolled gently on the swell at anchor behind the outer reef, and through the single porthole Isolde could see the water going that particular shade of green it went before slack tide — the color of a held breath.

"You've been watching me read charts for four days," Lena said. She was sitting on the supply crate that served as a second chair, arms crossed, boots planted like she expected the floor to try something. "You watch everyone read everything. But you watched me read the reef survey like a woman watching a fuse burn down. So I'll ask plain: what is actually on this wreck?"

Isolde poured two fingers of rum into the tin cup she kept for exactly these conversations. She did not offer Lena any. Offering Lena Brightwater a drink felt like offering a cat a bath.

"Gold," she said. "Specie, plate, some pearls out of the Sulu run. That's what the manifest says."

"That's what the manifest says," Lena repeated, in the tone of a woman testing a plank before putting her weight on it.

"And manifests are written by honest men."

"Are they?"

"No." Isolde drank. "That's rather the point."

She set the cup down on the chart table, next to the survey of the reef coast that Pell had annotated in his small, devout handwriting — he treated tide tables the way other people treated scripture, and Isolde had privately decided that if the boy ever turned to piracy he'd be insufferable about it. Outside, she could hear Bram arguing with the bilge pump in the low, patient voice he used on machinery and children and, once, memorably, on a customs officer. Osa was somewhere below, silent as always, which on this ship meant either working or thinking, and with Osa the two had never been distinguishable.

"Ten years ago," Isolde said, "I ran salvage out of Port Callow with a partner."

"Dax Rennard."

"You've done your reading."

"Lena does her reading," came faintly through the bulkhead, in Pell's voice, followed by the unmistakable sound of Bram shushing him. The walls of the *Kestrel's Mercy* were thin enough that privacy aboard was mostly a matter of politeness. Isolde chose to interpret the eavesdropping as a compliment to her storytelling.

"Rennard and I found things," she went on. "Good things. Honest wrecks — ships that died of weather or war or simple bad luck, carrying cargo that belonged to no one anymore except whoever was brave or stupid enough to go down after it. We were good at it. We were, if I'm being generous with myself, the best pair on that coast." She turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table. "And then we took a contract from a man named Corvin Hale."

Something changed in the quality of Lena's stillness. It was subtle — most people wouldn't have caught it. Most people hadn't spent three years sailing beside Lena Brightwater watching her not-talk about her dead mentor, which was a full-time occupation requiring considerable attention.

"Hale," Lena said. Just the name. Her voice did nothing at all with it, which was how Isolde knew it cost her.

"The Coral Triangle was his ship," Isolde said. "Or his charter, more precisely. He contracted us for a recovery job — insurance work, he said. A vessel lost on the reef coast, cargo to be raised quietly, owners' names kept out of it. Standard quiet-money arrangement. Rennard negotiated the fee; I drew up the dive plan. We never made the dive." She looked at the porthole, at the green water. "The Triangle went down the week before we were meant to sail. All hands lost. Officially."

"Officially," Lena said again.

"There were eleven sailors on that manifest. Eleven families got letters saying their people drowned in a storm that, according to every logbook and weather record Pell has ever been able to dig up, never happened. The sea was flat as a chapel floor that whole week." Isolde let that sit. "Hale paid off our contract personally. Full fee, no dive required. He said the job was cancelled. He shook my hand. He was very courteous about it."

"And Rennard?"

"Rennard didn't shake hands. Rennard counted. He noticed that a cancelled contract doesn't usually come with a bonus, and that bonuses are what men pay when they want you to stop asking questions. He asked questions anyway. Loud ones. The wrong ones, in front of the wrong people." Isolde's mouth went dry around the memory of it. "There was an inquiry. An old charge — smuggling, they called it, though it was really just asking questions with paperwork attached. Rennard took the fall for both of us because he was louder and I was smarter, and I let him. That's the shape of my debt in this story, Brightwater, in case you were wondering why I owe that man anything at all, including the courtesy of beating him to this wreck instead of letting him drown on it."

Lena uncrossed her arms slowly. "So Hale scuttled his own charter."

"I don't know that."

"You suspect it."

"I suspect," Isolde said, "that honest men do not pay cancellation fees with bonuses, and that ships do not sink in flat calm seas, and that Corvin Hale — who I met twice, and who struck me both times as a man who apologized to furniture before moving it — was not the sort of person who did any of this for money." She finally looked directly at Lena. "Which brings us to the question you actually asked, and the answer I should have given you in the sand, before I dragged you onto this ship."

The lantern swung a little with the swell. Shadows moved across Lena's face and settled again.

"The manifest says gold," Isolde said. "But Rennard isn't spending six tides and two ships chasing gold. Gold sits in banks. Gold gets divided by lawyers. What Rennard has — what he bought, bribed, and possibly bled for — is a rumor that the Triangle carried a sealed ledger. Hale's own hand, sealed in the captain's strongbox. And the rumor Rennard believes is that the ledger records the coordinates of a second wreck. Something richer. Something Hale found first and hid."

"And you?"

Isolde laughed, but there wasn't much humor in it, and she suspected even the ship knew the difference. "I think a man who pays bonuses to make questions stop doesn't hide treasure in a ledger. Treasure doesn't need sealing. Treasure doesn't need hiding from the very salvagers you hired." She tapped the chart table once, twice. "Ledgers record debts. Debts owed and debts paid and debts forgiven. If Corvin Hale sealed a ledger inside a ship and then sank the ship, then whatever's written in that book wasn't a map, Brightwater. It was a confession. His, or someone else's. Names. Dates. What happened to eleven sailors on a flat sea."

* * *

Lena didn't speak for a long moment. Isolde had learned, over the past several days, to read the desert ranger's silences the way Pell read currents — there were silences that meant *I'm deciding whether to gut you*, and silences that meant *I'm deciding whether to sleep*, and this one, mercifully, seemed to belong to neither family. This one was the silence of a woman re-counting a decade and finding the arithmetic different than she'd remembered.

"You knew," Lena said finally. "When you pulled me out of the salt flats. You knew Hale's name would land on me like a dropped mast."

"I suspected you'd heard of him."

"He raised me." The words came out flat and fast, like coins spilled from a torn purse. "Eleven years. He taught me to track, to ride, to read country. Everything I am that isn't stubbornness, he put there." She stopped. Reclosed the purse, so to speak. "You could have told me."

"Yes," Isolde agreed. "And you'd have refused the contract, and I'd have lost my only chance at the truth, and you'd have gone back to chasing horse thieves across the salt until the salt took you. I judged that the truth was worth your fury." She spread her hands. "I stand by the judgment. I expect you to hate me for it anyway. Both things can be true; I find that's true of most things."

"Oh, I hate you," Lena said, and there — there it was, under the flatness, a flicker of the dry heat Isolde had been waiting for. "Don't mistake this for anything else. You cornered me with a ten-year-old contract and a debt I can't read, and now you're telling me the prize at the bottom of this reef might be proof that the best man I ever knew was a murderer."

"Or a witness," Isolde said. "Or a man trying to protect someone. Or eleven different things I haven't thought of. That's precisely why it has to come up out of the water, Brightwater. Not for the gold. Not for Rennard's second wreck. Because right now, everyone in this story — you, me, Rennard, the owners, the dead — is living inside a rumor. And rumors are the only wrecks I've never been able to dive."

Lena stood. For a heartbeat Isolde thought she was going to walk out, and Isolde found she minded that more than she'd expected to, which was an inconvenient discovery she filed away for later examination, preferably never.

Instead, Lena crossed to the chart table and looked down at the reef survey. At the little inked channel where the Triangle lay, forty feet down, wrapped in coral and ten years of silence.

"The lock on the strongbox," she said. "If there is one."

"There will be. Hale sealed it himself, per the contract terms."

"If it's a Hale lock, it won't be forced. He built mechanisms like arguments — every way in except the right one leads somewhere worse." Lena's jaw tightened. "I may know something about how he built them."

Isolde raised an eyebrow. "May."

"I said *may*. Don't push it, Vane. I've known you five days and I already regret all of them."

"Four and a half, technically. The half day you were unconscious doesn't count toward our acquaintance; you weren't being interesting."

Despite herself — Isolde watched it happen and enjoyed it immensely — the corner of Lena's mouth moved. It was not quite a smile. It was the geological precursor of a smile, the way a fault line is the precursor of a mountain. On Lena Brightwater, Isolde was learning, that was practically a parade.

Above them, boots crossed the deck — Bram's heavy tread, Pell's quick light ones, and then Pell's voice through the skylight, pitched with the particular urgency he reserved for mathematics: "Captain? Slack tide in twenty minutes, and I need you to look at the tide tables, because I've counted six, and I've counted them twice, and I keep getting six."

"Six what?" Isolde called back.

"Six tides," Pell said, "before the monsoon closes the channel. And Captain — Rennard's fleet left harbor two days ahead of us."

Isolde looked at Lena. Lena looked at Isolde. Somewhere below them, forty feet down in the green dark, a sealed box sat in a dead man's cabin, holding either a fortune or a funeral, and the sea had just informed them, in its usual tactful manner, that the conversation was over whether or not they were finished having it.

"Well," Isolde said, reaching for her coat. "It seems the past has made an appointment. Let's try not to be late — it hates that."
