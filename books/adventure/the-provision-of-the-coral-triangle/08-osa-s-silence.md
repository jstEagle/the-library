## 8. Osa's Silence

The sea had a voice, and Osa had learned to answer it without one.

That was the joke the crew hadn't figured out yet — that the diver who never spoke was the only one aboard who talked all day. To the water. To the fish. To the hull of the *Saltmark* as she groaned through her morning stretches like an old woman complaining about weather. Osa answered everything. Just not out loud.

Words were heavy things. They sank. And once they sank in a person, they stayed down there, turning the water murky, and nobody could see anything clearly anymore. Osa had watched it happen on this very ship. Pell asked questions with his whole face — *are we going to die, are we going to die today, how much are we going to die* — and Isolde Vane answered every single one, and every answer made the boy more afraid than the question had. Words did that. Osa kept hers in a small tin box at the bottom of herself, and the box had rusted shut years ago, and honestly? Best decision she'd ever made.

This morning the water off the reef shelf was green-glass calm, slack tide, the hour when the current forgot which way it was going and let everything rest. Slack tide was Osa's favorite hour of any day anywhere. The sea held its breath. So did everyone else.

She sat on the gunwale with her fins dangling, checking her weights, and felt Lena Brightwater watching her from the deck.

The desert ranger was new. New the way a stone dropped into still water is new — everything rippling around her, nobody sure where she'd settle. Isolde vouched for her, which meant nothing; Isolde vouched for Bram too, and Bram had once tried to fix a bilge pump with a soup ladle and religious conviction. But Osa watched people the way she watched water: patiently, from underneath, waiting to see what moved when they thought no one was looking.

Lena wasn't looking at Osa like the others did. The others looked at Osa and saw a tool that happened to have arms — *send the diver down, the diver doesn't mind, the diver doesn't need anything.* Lena looked at her the way you look at a locked door. Like she recognized the lock.

Osa gave her a small nod. Lena nodded back, careful, like returning something borrowed.

Good, Osa thought. We'll leave each other alone. It's the kindest thing two quiet people can do.

Then she tipped backward off the rail and let the sea take her.

* * *

Down was where Osa lived, really. The *Saltmark* was just where she stored her body between visits.

The light came down in long silver columns, and the reef rose beneath her like a drowned mountain range, which of course it was — coral grows on the bones of old coral, generation on generation, the living building blindly on top of the dead without ever asking what died there. Osa had thought about that before. She tried not to think about it now.

Because somewhere below her, half-swallowed by the reef's shoulder, lay the *Coral Triangle*.

Isolde had told them what to expect: a merchantman, forty years down, broken-backed on the shelf, stripped by decades of salvage crews looking for gold that wasn't there. Rennard's fleet wanted the ledger because they believed it pointed to a richer wreck. Isolde wanted it because — well. Isolde wanted things the way anchors want the bottom, and she didn't explain herself, and nobody aboard was foolish enough to ask twice.

Osa descended along the drop-off, equalizing her ears, kicking in the slow economical rhythm that made her lungs last twice as long as Bram's calculations said they should. Bram had actually argued with her about it once, waving his slate around — *human lung capacity averages five liters, Osa, FIVE* — until Isolde took the slate away and told him to go lubricate something.

The wreck appeared the way wrecks do: first as a shadow with an opinion, then as a shape, then all at once, entire.

And Osa stopped swimming.

The stories were wrong. The *Triangle* wasn't broken-backed. Her spine was bent, yes, but unbroken — she'd settled onto the shelf the way a tired traveler settles onto a bench, bow wedged in coral, stern canted up into the light, and the reef had spent forty years tucking blankets of coral and sponge around her ribs until she looked less like a shipwreck and more like a ship asleep. Fish moved through her open gunports in silver threads. A grouper the size of a barrel regarded Osa from the shadow of the foremast with the deep offended dignity of a cat disturbed from a nap.

*Hello, old lady,* Osa thought at her. *Who tucked you in?*

The outer hull was crusted white and orange, soft corals waving in the faint push of residual current. Osa swam the length of her slowly, running her gloved fingers over the planking, reading the wood the way Lena read sand. Here — a scar from grounding, old, healed over. Here — a patch job done in a hurry, canvas and pitch, someone's desperate night preserved forever under a skin of barnacles. Here —

Here, a name.

*Coral Triangle*, carved into the transom, the letters softened but legible, and beneath them smaller letters that the coral had almost finished eating: a maker's mark, a shipyard stamp. Osa brushed the growth away gently with her thumb, the way you'd brush hair off a sleeping child's face.

She knew that mark.

Not knew it the way you know a thing you've seen. Knew it the way you know a thing that has been written on you.

Her heart picked up, and she made herself breathe slowly, because a diver whose heart runs ahead of her air is a diver making arrangements. She looked at the little stamped sigil — a wave curled around a star — and her body remembered things her mind had packed away in the tin box at the bottom of herself, and the box, for the first time in twenty years, rattled.

*Later,* she told herself firmly. *Look first. Feel later. That's the order.*

She found the captain's cabin by its windows — stern-facing, high, the glass gone but the frames intact, hung now with curtains of sea fan that swayed like something breathing. The door was closed. Forty years underwater and the door was *closed*, which meant the cabin behind it might be sealed, might be dry, might be —

Might be anything.

Osa hooked her fingers around the frame and pulled herself close, and that was when she saw the carving.

It was small. Knee-high on the door, in the lower corner, where a person would put a mark they wanted hidden but not lost. Someone had cut it with a knife point, quickly, in the dark maybe, or in a hurry — four strokes and a curve. A wave curled around a star.

The same mark as the shipyard stamp.

The same mark as the scar on Osa's left forearm, which she had carried since she was six years old, since a night she did not remember and a fire she could not forget, since the harbor town that burned and the boat that left and the family that did not follow. The scar was her oldest possession. She'd had it longer than her name. When the orphanage matron asked where she'd gotten it, six-year-old Osa had looked at her with the flat gray eyes that would someday make grown sailors step back, and said nothing, and been labeled simple, and been left alone, which had suited her fine ever since.

A wave curled around a star. Cut into a dead ship's door, forty fathoms down, by somebody who had stood exactly where Osa was floating right now, alive, with a knife in their hand.

Osa's breath went ragged against the regulator. Bubbles climbed past her face like small startled birds.

*Who were you,* she asked the door. *What did you know. Why does your mark live on my skin.*

The door didn't answer. Doors never do. That was why Osa liked them.

But the grouper had followed her down, and it hovered at her shoulder now, regarding the carved mark with one rolling eye, and then regarding Osa, and then the mark again, in the manner of a creature that understands more than it says. Which, Osa reflected, made them colleagues.

She pressed her palm flat against the door, over the mark, the way you'd press a hand to a grave or a wound. The wood was cold and alive with small things. Somewhere inside that cabin — dry, maybe, dark, certainly — was a strongbox with a lock only a dead man could open, and a ledger full of names, and Isolde Vane wanted those names very badly, and Osa had never once wondered why a salvage captain would chase paper across an ocean.

Paper holds names. Osa knew that better than anyone. Her whole history was a page somebody else had written and she'd never gotten to read.

*Not this time,* she told the door, and meant it so hard that her jaw ached with all the words she wasn't saying. *This time I read it first.*

* * *

Coming up through the columns of light, Osa counted her blessings the way other people counted money — briefly, suspiciously, and with a growing sense that the total couldn't be trusted.

Blessing one: the cabin door was swollen shut but the frame was sound. It could be opened with care, or with Bram's pry bar and a prayer, preferably with care because Bram's prayers had a poor success rate.

Blessing two: slack tide lasted another hour. Enough time to report, plan, dive again.

Blessing three: nobody aboard would ask her how she felt about any of this, because nobody aboard had ever figured out how to ask Osa a question she could answer. For twenty years her silence had been armor. Today, floating in the bright water with a dead ship's secret pressed into her skin, it was also a cage, and Osa noticed — really noticed, for the first time — that the cage door had never actually been locked. She just liked it shut.

Pell's head appeared over the rail as she surfaced, all elbows and alarm. "Osa! You're back early — is it bad? It's bad, isn't it. Is the wreck bad? Did it sink further?" He paused. "Can wrecks sink further? Don't answer that, I don't want to know—"

Osa spat out her regulator, gripped the ladder, and looked up at him.

Pell stopped talking. This was, by crew accounts, unprecedented, possibly historic. He peered down at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him reach down and offer his hand instead of words, which was the most intelligent thing anyone had said to Osa in weeks.

She took it, and came up over the rail dripping, and stood on the deck of the *Saltmark* with the sea streaming off her, and found Isolde Vane waiting with her arms crossed and her eyes narrowed, and Lena Brightwater beside her, also watching, quiet as a held breath.

"Report," Isolde said.

Osa opened her mouth. Closed it. Held up one finger — *wait* — and crossed to the rail locker where the slates lived. She wrote fast, chalk squeaking:

**HULL INTACT. CABIN SEALED. DOOR STUCK NOT BROKEN. CAN OPEN AT NEXT SLACK.**

Isolde read it, nodded, and then, because she was Isolde, leaned closer. "And?"

Osa hesitated. Then she wrote:

**THERE'S A MARK ON THE DOOR. WAVE AND STAR. I KNOW IT.**

Silence on the deck. Even the rigging seemed to lean in.

"Know it how?" Isolde asked, carefully, in the voice she used for things that might bite.

Osa set down the chalk. Then, deliberately, in front of both of them, she pushed up her sleeve.

The scar ran pale along her forearm, old and smooth, four strokes and a curve. A wave curled around a star.

Lena inhaled sharply — the sound of a tracker recognizing sign. Isolde went very still, the way she did when the sea changed under her, and after a moment she said, quietly, "That's a shipyard mark. Corvin Hale's yard used it. Before he ever captained anything."

Something turned over in Osa's chest, slow and enormous, like a whale passing beneath a boat.

Corvin Hale. The dead mentor. The ghost that walked the deck of this ship wearing Lena Brightwater's face every time she thought no one was looking. The man whose lock waited behind a door marked with the same sigil that some frightened hand had cut into a six-year-old girl's arm in a burning harbor twenty years ago.

Osa looked at Lena. Lena looked at Osa. Two women who didn't talk much, understanding each other completely, which was the worst possible luck for both of them.

From the crow's nest above, Pell's voice cracked down across the deck, high and urgent:

"SAIL! Two points off the lee — black hull, no colors flying — it's *him*, isn't it, it's Rennard—"

Osa pulled her sleeve down over the mark, and the sea, which had been holding its breath all morning, finally let it out.
