## 9. The First Tide Lost

Bram had a rule about mornings at sea: if the coffee was good, the day would be tolerable. If the coffee was bad, the day would be survivable. And if there was no coffee at all, the day would be the kind where you wished you'd stayed in port and taken up fishing.

The morning Rennard's second ship came over the horizon, there was no coffee.

"Isolde," he said, standing at the galley door with the empty pot held up like evidence of a crime, "we have a situation."

Isolde Vane didn't look up from the chart table. "We have several. Rank them."

"No coffee."

"That's fourth." She tapped the chart with one finger. "First, we're anchored in a channel that closes behind us in four tides. Second, Rennard's lead ship is a burned hulk on the reef, which means his second ship knows exactly where we are. Third—" she glanced up, and her eyes went to the horizon past Bram's shoulder, "—there she is."

Bram turned. The ship sat low and black against the morning glare, running under full canvas with the particular arrogance of a vessel that had never once worried about her keel. *The Kestrel's Debt*. He'd seen her in harbor two years ago, back when he still drank enough to make poor decisions about cards, and even then she'd looked fast.

"How long?" Pell asked, appearing at the rail with the tide tables clutched to his chest like scripture, which, for Pell, they essentially were.

"Two hours," Isolde said. "Maybe less. She's got the wind and we've got an anchor down."

"Why do we have an anchor down?"

"Because Osa is on the wreck, boy, and I don't leave my divers in the water while my ship sails away without them." Isolde rolled the chart closed. "Bram. Get the swivel gun loaded. Lena — get up here."

Lena Brightwater arrived the way she seemed to arrive everywhere: already there, or nearly. She'd been sleeping on deck since the Channel of Teeth, claiming the cabins smelled like bilge, though Bram suspected it was something else. Desert people, he'd decided, didn't trust roofs that moved.

She took one look at the black sail on the horizon and said, "That's not the same ship that grounded."

"No," Isolde said. "That's the one that did the grounding."

* * *

The next hour was the busiest of Bram's life up to that point, and he'd once rebuilt an engine in a flooding bilge during a squall off the Sable Coast.

He loaded the swivel gun while Isolde shouted orders and Pell recited tide arithmetic like a prayer — *"slack water at midday, monsoon window closes on the sixth tide, we cannot lose a single hour"* — and Lena stood at the stern rail with a spyglass, utterly silent, watching the *Kestrel's Debt* come on.

"You could run," Bram offered, cranking the gun's elevation screw. "Cut the anchor, swing wide, be gone before she closes."

"And leave Osa swimming with the sharks?" Lena didn't lower the spyglass. "No."

It wasn't the *no* that interested Bram. It was the way she said it — flat, immediate, like a woman who had already done the math and found only one answer worth writing down. Ten years he'd sailed with captains who weighed every choice like a merchant weighing coin. This desert ranger had been aboard six days and she was already answering questions before Isolde asked them.

Isolde had noticed too. Bram could tell because Isolde kept glancing at Lena sideways, the way you watch weather coming in.

The *Kestrel's Debt* closed to half a mile. Then a quarter. Then she ran out her own guns — two swivels and a bow chaser, nothing serious, but serious enough — and a voice carried across the water, amplified by a speaking trumpet and no small amount of self-satisfaction.

"*Vane!* You're anchored over my wreck!"

"It's not your wreck, Dax!" Isolde bellowed back, cupping her hands. "You drove your flagship onto it! Finders keepers, sinkers weepers!"

"I have forty men and you have five!"

"Quality over quantity!"

Bram, hauling on the gun tackle, felt someone appear beside him. It was Pell, pale as milk, holding a belaying pin like it might bite him.

"Have you ever been in a fight?" Bram asked him.

"No. Have you?"

"Several. They're all terrible." He checked the fuse. "Stay behind me, stay low, and if anyone boards, hit them with the pin and then run toward me, not away from me. People always run away from their friends when they panic. It's how crews lose boys."

Pell nodded so hard his hat fell off. He picked it up. Put it on. Took it off again and stuffed it in his shirt, apparently deciding that headwear was a liability now.

Out on the water, the parley collapsed — because everyone aboard had known it would — and the *Kestrel's Debt* put a boat over the side.

* * *

There were twelve men in the boarding boat, and they came alongside the *Provision* — Isolde's ship, old, scarred, and stubborn as a mule with opinions — with grappling hooks flying before anyone finished shouting warnings.

What followed was ninety seconds of chaos that Bram would spend the rest of his life describing badly.

The first boarder came over the rail swinging a cutlass and met Lena Brightwater, who was not holding a weapon at all but a boat hook, and who used it the way she presumably used everything: efficiently, and with the expression of someone doing chores. The man went over the side with a splash and a great deal of indignation.

The second and third came together and met Isolde, who fought like a woman settling personal accounts.

The fourth, fifth, and sixth came over the stern, which was where Bram was, which was also where Pell was, because Bram had told him to stay close and the boy had obeyed with the literal-mindedness of the very frightened.

"Pell — down!"

Pell went down. The first man swung at Bram, who caught the blade on his forearm because his hands were full of fuse and powder horn, and the pain was white and total and deeply clarifying. Bram discovered in that moment that he had strong opinions about being cut, and those opinions were loud.

He headbutted the man. It was not elegant. It worked.

The second man grabbed Pell by the collar and hauled him up like a sack of meal, knife at the boy's throat, and shouted something about the anchor chain, and Bram understood — even through the blood running into his eye — what was happening. They weren't here for the wreck. They were here to cost them time. Cut the anchor chain and the *Provision* would drift off station, Osa would surface to open water and no ship, and the tide clock would eat them alive.

"Lena!" Bram roared, because his arms were pinned and his vision was going red at the edges. "The chain!"

He saw her move. That was the thing he'd remember later, in the quiet hours when his arm throbbed under its bandages: not the fight itself, but the way Lena crossed the deck — no wasted motion, no hesitation, three steps and a turn like water finding its level — and put herself between the man with the knife and the anchor capstan without appearing to decide to do it.

"Let the boy go," she said.

"He's my ticket off this tub—"

"Let. Him. Go." She had picked up a belaying pin off the deck — Pell's, dropped in the scuffle — and she held it loosely, the way Bram had only ever seen professionals hold things. "You're Dax Rennard's man. Which means you're paid by the job, not by the grave. Do the sums."

The man did the sums. Sailors usually do, eventually. He shoved Pell forward and went over the rail after his fellows, and the boarding boat pulled away trailing two men who'd been introduced to the sea the hard way, and the *Kestrel's Debt* sheered off with her trumpet-voice captain promising reprisals that everyone knew would amount to waiting them out.

Which was the real wound. Not the arm. The tide.

* * *

They took stock in the aftermath, the way crews do: counting heads, counting blood, counting minutes.

Osa surfaced twenty minutes after the fight, hauled herself up the ladder, took in the blood on the deck, the missing length of anchor chain — they'd gotten halfway through cutting it before Lena's intervention — and the bandage already going up Bram's arm, and raised one eyebrow with such eloquence that no words were required.

"Bram's fine," Isolde said. "Mostly. The chain's half-cut. And we've lost the morning tide."

Osa looked at Bram's arm, then at Pell, then made a short gesture — hand flat, chopping at the air — that Bram had learned to translate as *and whose fault was that?*

"Theirs," said Pell, with surprising heat. "Bram got cut because he stepped in front of me."

Osa considered this. Then she cuffed Bram lightly on his uninjured shoulder, which from Osa was roughly equivalent to a medal ceremony, and went below to change out of her diving dress.

The anchor problem was real. Half a chain meant they couldn't trust the hold; a drifting ship meant losing their position over the wreck; losing position meant losing the dive window, and the dive window was the whole game. Six tides to monsoon. One already bleeding out between the planks.

"We warp back onto station with the winch and a kedge anchor," Lena said.

Everyone turned to look at her.

"It's what we do with wagons stuck in sand," she went on, as if being stared at were a mild inconvenience. "You can't pull yourself forward by the thing that's failing. You throw an anchor ahead, winch yourself to it, repeat. Small moves. Slow. But you don't lose ground."

"That'll take the rest of this tide," Isolde said.

"Yes."

"And Rennard will sit out there watching us do it, grinning."

"Yes."

Isolde studied her for a long moment. Bram, holding his arm against his chest and dripping modestly onto the deck, watched the two women look at each other, and understood that something was passing between them that had nothing to do with anchors — some measurement, some test whose result was being quietly recorded on both sides.

"Do it," Isolde said.

And then, because the moment needed deflating before it swelled into something either of them would have to acknowledge, she added: "Bram, go sit down before you faint on my clean deck."

"It's not clean," Bram said. "There's blood everywhere."

"My clean deck," Isolde repeated, and stalked off to shout at the winch crew, which consisted of Pell and, effectively, Pell.

* * *

Stitches, in Bram's experience, were best administered by someone who disliked you slightly, because they didn't waste sympathy on the process. Lena fit the bill perfectly. She sewed his forearm shut at the galley table with the neat, economical stitches of a woman who'd patched a lot of things that bled — tents, horses, possibly people — and she did not coo over him even once.

"So," she said, tying off. "You stepped in front of the boy."

"He's fourteen."

"Fifteen," Pell called from the doorway, where he had been hovering with the transparent excuse of a pot of tea. "And I'm not a child."

"You hid your hat in your shirt," Bram said.

"That was tactics."

Lena snorted — an undignified sound, quickly suppressed, but Bram caught it, and he suspected she knew he'd caught it, and he filed it away as progress. Six days she'd been aboard, and for six days she'd been a locked room. But rooms opened. He'd spent thirty years around ships; he knew how hulls worked. Pressure from outside made them stronger. It was pressure from inside that broke them, and Lena Brightwater was all interior pressure, and someday — not today, but someday — she was going to crack along a seam nobody had mapped yet.

He hoped, privately, that when she did, the crew would be standing close enough to catch her.

"Drink your tea," Lena told Pell. "Both of you. And Bram — next time, use the pin. Your head is not a weapon."

"It has served me well."

"Your head is a liability with ears." She wrapped the bandage, tight and sure. "But it's a loyal liability. Thank you."

Bram blinked. In six days, Lena Brightwater had said approximately eleven sentences that weren't operational instructions, and this was the first one with warmth in it. He decided not to make a fuss. Fussing, with her, would be like startling a wild horse — technically possible, deeply unwise.

"For the record," he said instead, "I'd have done it for any of you. Even Isolde."

"Especially Isolde," Pell said solemnly. "She signs your pay."

"She does sign my pay," Bram agreed, and drank his tea.

* * *

They warped onto station by nightfall, kedge and winch, small slow moves across the bottom of the channel, exactly as Lena had promised. Rennard's ship sat on the horizon the whole time, black against the last light, patient as a debt.

By the time the kedge bit and the *Provision* swung steady over the wreck again, the tide had turned. The fifth tide was running out beneath them. One left before the monsoon sealed the passage, and the ledger — whatever it truly held — still lay in the dark below, locked in a dead man's cabin.

Bram stood at the rail with his arm bound and a fresh pot of coffee finally in his hand — Pell had made it, and it was terrible, and it was the best cup of his life — and watched Lena at the stern, staring down into the black water as if she could read it.

Isolde joined her. Neither spoke for a while. Then Isolde said something low, too quiet for the rail, and Lena's answer was shorter still, and whatever passed between them settled over the ship like a change in the wind.

Bram drained his cup and went below to check the winch bearings, because tomorrow they'd have one tide to raise a strongbox off a dead man's ship, and somewhere out in the dark, Dax Rennard was counting the same tide table they were.

The sea, Bram reflected, turning in early, had a sense of humor. It just never told the joke until you were already laughing at something else.

Above him, through the deck planks, he heard Pell reading the tide tables aloud to Osa, who could not answer and did not need to, and thought: *strangers, six days ago.*

Tomorrow, the last tide.
