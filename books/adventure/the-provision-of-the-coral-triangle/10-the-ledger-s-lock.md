## 10. The Ledger's Lock

The captain's cabin of the Coral Triangle opened like a held breath.

Osa went first, of course — she always went first — slipping through the hatch with a lantern and a silence that made even the water seem loud by comparison. Lena watched her shape dissolve into the green murk below, tether paying out in slow, patient loops, and tried to breathe around the weight on her chest.

"You're doing the thing," Pell said beside her.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look like you're chewing a lemon and doing arithmetic at the same time." The boy didn't look up from his current tables. He never looked up from his current tables; Lena suspected he slept with them under his pillow like other children slept with knives. "You've been doing it since we sighted the wreck. I counted."

"Counted what?"

"Lemon-arithmetic faces. Eleven this morning alone."

Lena almost smiled. Almost. "You should be a ranger, Pell. That kind of noticing gets people killed out there, but it pays well here."

"It got me a berth on a salvage ship instead," he said, "which is the same thing but wetter."

Below them, Osa's light bloomed against something pale and square. Even through forty feet of monsoon-stained water, even through the hull port and Isolde's brass speaking-tube and Bram's grumbling about pressure differentials, Lena felt it land in her gut like a dropped anchor.

The strongbox.

She'd known it would be there. She had known it for ten years, the way you know a tooth is loose — refusing to touch it, refusing to stop touching it with your tongue.

* * *

Getting it up was a war fought in inches.

The cabin door had swollen shut in the decades since the ship went down, and the corridor beyond was a throat of silt and collapsed shelving. Osa worked the hinges while Bram fed her tools and increasingly creative curses through the tube. Isolde stood at the rail with her arms crossed, saying nothing, which from Isolde was its own kind of weather system — barometric, watchful, promising a storm if the reading came back wrong.

Lena hauled line.

It was honest work, the kind that filled the hands and left nothing over for thinking, and she was grateful for it right up until the box broke the surface.

They brought it aboard wrapped in netting, streaming water, and set it down on the deck planks with the reverence people usually saved for coffins. Which, Lena thought, wasn't far wrong. It was an iron-banded chest no bigger than a sea chest for a child, crusted white with coral, and every inch of it familiar.

Too familiar.

She knew the bands. She knew the way the corner plates overlapped, because she had riveted one of them herself, at seventeen, in a workshop that smelled of oil and lamp smoke, while Corvin Hale told her terrible jokes and pretended not to check her work twice.

Her hands were shaking. She put them behind her back like a child caught at the biscuit tin.

"Well?" Isolde said. "Can you open it?"

Bram was already crouched over it, calloused fingers walking the seams. He was a big man, Bram, built like something meant to hold things together, and he handled mechanisms the way other men handled infants — enormous gentleness, total concentration. "Lock plate's recessed under the forward band. Clever. Saltwater can't get at the works." He whistled low. "This isn't shipyard work. This is a maker's piece. Somebody loved this lock."

"Somebody did," Lena said, before she could stop herself.

Three heads turned. Four, counting Osa, who had surfaced silently at the ladder and was watching with the stillness she brought to everything, water sheeting off her shoulders.

Isolde's eyes narrowed by a degree. On anyone else it would have been invisible. On Isolde Vane it was practically a shout. "You know this lock."

"I know the style," Lena said. "Desert workshops used similar banding. It's common enough."

It was a lie so thin that Pell, who was fourteen and mostly interested in tides, visibly winced on her behalf.

Bram, mercifully or not, was already past it, absorbed. "There'll be a keyway, but I'm betting it's not a key lock proper. See these three apertures? Pressure points. And this dial here—" he tapped a disc of brass no wider than a coin, its face worn smooth, "—that's not numbers. No markings at all. Whoever made it wanted it opened by feel, not by eye."

*By feel.*

Lena's mouth went dry.

Because she remembered that. She remembered arguing about it, actually, standing in Hale's workshop with sawdust in her hair, telling him a combination lock defeated by feel was a fool's design because any thief with patience could read the tumblers through the casing. And Hale had laughed his dry laugh, the one that sounded like paper turning, and said: *That's the point, little wren. A lock you can't see is a lock only memory can open. Memory doesn't get stolen. Memory doesn't get left in a coat pocket.*

He had built it over six weeks. She had helped. She had filed the third tumbler herself, cut the false gate that would defeat anyone who hadn't been there, who hadn't sat across the bench from him night after night while he hummed off-key and taught her the sequence without ever writing it down.

*Never write it down, Lena. A written secret is a dead secret waiting for someone to find the body.*

The sequence was five pressures and a turn. She could feel her hands remembering it now, the pads of her fingers itching with a knowledge ten years deep. Left aperture, two beats. Center, one. Right, held. Left again, soft as a heartbeat. Then the turn, clockwise, stopping at the point where the resistance went quiet — not a number, a *sensation*, the exact moment the tumblers agreed with each other, which she had once described as feeling like a horse deciding to trust you.

Hale had liked that description. He'd carved a tiny horse into the inside of the casing where no one would ever see it, just because she'd made him laugh.

Lena stood on the deck of a salvage ship forty years and half a world from that workshop, and understood with perfect clarity that she could open this box in under a minute.

And that opening it would tell everyone everything.

* * *

They broke for the noon meal, because Isolde ran a disciplined ship and disciplined ships ate on schedule even when sitting on top of a mystery.

The galley was small and hot and smelled of Bram's salted pork stew, which was less a recipe than a geological event. Lena sat with her bowl and pushed it around and listened to the others talk.

"—saying the outer channel shifts a full cable-width on the ebb," Pell was insisting. "I've charted it. Rennard's pilot hasn't, or his lead ship wouldn't be a reef ornament right now."

"His lead ship is a reef ornament because Rennard drives his people like rented mules," Bram said. "No reflection on the channel."

"A reef doesn't care about your feelings either way," Isolde said. "Eat."

Osa sat across from Lena and did not eat. She watched Lena with those dark, unhurried eyes, and after a while she reached across the table and tapped the wood twice — once, twice — then pointed at Lena's hands, folded tight around the spoon.

Lena looked down. Her knuckles were white.

"You're wondering how I know the lock," Lena said quietly.

Osa tilted her head. Neither yes nor no. With Osa, everything was neither yes nor no until you earned the answer.

"I don't," Lena said. "Not exactly. Not the way Bram means it, anyway. I know the *style*. I told you."

Osa held her gaze for a long moment. Then she tapped the table twice more, slower, and this time Lena heard it differently — not a question but a statement. *I don't believe you. But I'll wait.* She picked up her spoon and began to eat, conversation closed, debt recorded.

Pell, bless him, chose that moment to spill his stew, and the table erupted into mopping and mockery, and the moment passed. But it didn't pass. Lena carried it below decks when the meal ended, carried it up to the bow, carried it everywhere, because it had grown too heavy to set down.

* * *

Evening came down over the anchorage like a lid. The Coral Triangle lay beneath them, a shadow among shadows, and around it the reef muttered and clicked with the changing tide — fish, coral, the slow argument between water and stone. Somewhere beyond the outer passage, Dax Rennard's remaining ships were regrouping, and everyone aboard knew they had bought themselves exactly one tide of grace, maybe less.

Lena found Isolde at the stern rail, as she'd known she would. Isolde kept evening watch the way other captains kept ledgers — personally, obsessively, trusting no one else's arithmetic.

"The box goes below tonight," Isolde said without turning. "Double watch on it. If Rennard sends swimmers, they'll find our deck harder to cross than his own was."

"He won't send swimmers yet. He'll send words first. He always tries words before iron."

"You sound like you know him."

"I know the type." Lena came to stand beside her. For a while neither spoke. The water slapped the hull. Lantern light from the galley turned the deck amber and left the sea black.

"Ten years ago," Isolde said at last, conversational, dangerous, "you signed a partnership contract with me and then vanished before the ink was properly dry. I've had a decade to wonder what a desert ranger runs from that requires running *that* fast. Tonight I watched you look at a strongbox like it was a grave marker. So I'll ask plainly, and you may lie to me if you like — I only ask that you make it a good lie, because I'll be checking."

Lena gripped the rail. Below her feet, through the planks, she could feel the ship breathing, the cargo hold where the box now sat under guard, the tumblers inside it holding their five-beat secret the way she had once held hers.

"Corvin Hale built that lock," she said.

Isolde didn't move. Didn't blink. Waiting, the way she waited for tides.

"I helped him build it. I filed the third tumbler. I know the sequence — not a combination, a *feel*, five pressures and a turn, and I could open it right now, tonight, and hand you whatever's inside." Lena's voice came out level, which cost her more than shouting would have. "But I can't. Because the last time I opened something of Hale's, someone died. Maybe because of me. Maybe *because* of me — I've spent ten years and forty thousand miles trying to decide which of those sentences is true, and I still don't know."

The wind shifted. Somewhere aft, Pell laughed at something Bram said, bright and young, and the sound hurt.

"So that's my lie," Lena finished. "Or the truth. I honestly can't tell anymore which one it is. Either way, Captain Vane — you have a lock on board that I can open, and an answer underneath it that I've been drowning in since before you found me in the sand. And tomorrow, when Rennard comes with his words, you're going to need to know which side of me is standing on your deck."

Isolde was silent for a long moment. When she finally spoke, her voice was quieter than Lena had ever heard it, and somehow worse for it.

"Hale," she said. "Corvin Hale. The man whose name has been in my ear since before we sailed — the name Rennard keeps saying like a curse and you keep not-saying like a prayer." She turned, and her eyes in the lantern light were flat and bright as coin. "You helped build the lock on the box we just bled a tide to raise. You've known since the moment it cleared the water."

"Yes."

"And you said nothing."

"Not nothing. I said I knew the *style*."

For one heartbeat Lena thought Isolde might strike her. Instead the captain laughed — short, hard, entirely without humor — and looked back out at the black water.

"One tide," Isolde said. "You have until Rennard's flag shows on the horizon, Lena Brightwater. One tide to decide whether you're going to open that box for us — or whether I let Bram drill it, consequences to the contents be damned, and learn what you're hiding the loud way." She pushed off the rail and moved toward the companionway, then paused, not looking back.

"For what it's worth," Isolde said, "the crew stopped being strangers somewhere around the anchor chain. Whatever's in that box — it lands on all of us now. Best start remembering you're not the only one who can drown."

Her boots faded down the stairs. Lena stayed at the rail, alone with the reef's clicking dark, her hands remembering a sequence she had sworn never to use again — and beneath her feet, patient as the tide, the lock waited for Hale's hand.

It was going to get hers instead.
