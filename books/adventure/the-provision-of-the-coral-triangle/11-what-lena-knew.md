## 11. What Lena Knew

The anchor watch came to her at the second bell past midnight, which was fitting, because Lena had not been sleeping anyway.

She sat in the stern of the *Kestrel's Mercy* with her back against the rail and Hale's lock sitting on the deck between her boots like a confession waiting to happen. It was a small thing, that lock — brass and iron, no bigger than a loaf of bread — and it had ruined more sleep than any storm she'd ever outrun. The moon was down. The reef glowed faintly off the port side, that pale ghost-light coral makes when it thinks nobody's watching, and somewhere below them the Coral Triangle waited with its dead men and its sealed cabin and its cargo of ten-year-old sins.

Isolde Vane found her there, as Lena had known she would. Isolde always knew where people were hiding; it was one of the many irritating things about her.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Isolde said, lowering herself onto the coil of line across from her. She had a tin cup in each hand and pressed one into Lena's grip without asking. It was tea, or what passed for tea out here, which was to say hot water that had once been introduced to a leaf.

"I don't sleep much," Lena said. "Habit."

"Ten years of habit." Isolde blew on her cup. "I've been counting, you know. Not the miles — I wouldn't know how — but the years. Ten since Hale died. Ten since you walked out of my life without so much as a note pinned to the door."

"There was a note."

"There was a *bill*, Lena. For a chart you'd borrowed. That is not the same thing as a note."

Lena almost smiled. Almost. "It felt like the same thing at the time."

They sat with that for a while. The ship creaked around them, settling against its new anchor chain — Bram's replacement rigging, good work done fast under bad conditions, and every link of it a reminder of the tide they'd lost to Rennard's boarders. Four tides left. Maybe five if the monsoon kept its manners, and monsoons never kept their manners.

"You opened the strongbox yet?" Isolde asked, though she knew the answer. Everyone on this ship knew the answer by now, because Lena had spent two days being publicly, elaborately useless about it.

"It's keyed to a mechanism only Hale could open."

"And you helped him build it."

"Yes."

"So you're telling me the one man who can't open it is you."

"I'm telling you I haven't tried."

Isolde set her cup down on the deck with a deliberate little click. "There it is," she said softly. "I've been waiting three days for you to say something true, Brightwater. Go on. I'm listening. And before you start — no, I won't decide anything for you, and no, whatever you say doesn't leave this rail unless you want it to. But you're going to say it, because I watched you look at that lock the way a woman looks at a grave she dug herself, and I have sailed with enough liars to know the difference between a secret and a wound."

Lena looked out at the reef light. It would be easier, she thought, to jump. Not really — she'd grown up in waterless country and trusted the desert over the sea the way a dog trusts its own yard — but it seemed easier, in the way all cowardice seems easy right up until the moment you try it.

So instead she talked.

* * *

"The night before the Coral Triangle sailed," she said, "I was nineteen. You remember that much, probably. You remember everything, it's your worst quality."

"It's my best quality," Isolde said. "Continue."

"Hale came to my room at the harbor house past midnight. He was carrying that lock. He'd been building it for months — everyone assumed it was for the owners' strongroom, some commission, and he let them assume it. But he hadn't slept in days. I could see it. And he put the lock on my table and he said, 'Lena, I need you to help me hide something, and then I need you to forget you helped.'"

Isolde didn't move. Didn't blink. Lena took that as permission to keep going, because stopping now would kill her faster than any reef.

"He told me the Coral Triangle wasn't going to make its run. He'd found papers — manifests, insurance filings, letters he shouldn't have had — and the ship was insured for more than it was worth, and more than its cargo, and the men who owned her had already sold her cargo twice over to different buyers. She was going to go down, Lena. Not by storm, not by reef. By her own owners' arrangement. And the crew — thirty-one sailors, good men, families — were going to be written off as lost at sea, and the payout was going to be enormous, and nobody was ever going to be punished because the men doing it *owned the courts*."

The tea had gone cold in Lena's hands. She drank it anyway, just to have something to do with her mouth.

"He'd assembled everything into a ledger. Names, dates, payments, the whole rotten architecture of it. Proof. And he meant to take it with him, sealed in the captain's cabin behind that lock, because — this is the part I argued with him about until the sun came up — he wasn't planning to expose them first. He was planning to survive the scuttling, come back, and use the ledger as leverage. Or justice. He used both words. I think even he didn't know which one he meant."

"And the lock?"

"The lock was the vault. He built it so that only his hands could open it — a mechanism keyed to pressure and sequence, a thing you had to *feel* rather than know. He showed me the principle. He made me practice the opening motion until my fingers ached, and then he made me swear I'd never use it, because the moment anyone else demonstrated knowledge of the mechanism, the ledger stopped being safe and started being a target. He hid the lock's secret inside my hands and then asked me to bury it." Lena's voice went flat. "And I did. I buried it. Nineteen years old, flattered out of my mind that Corvin Hale trusted *me*, terrified of the men he described, and I swore the oath and I helped him seal the box and I watched him carry it up the gangway at dawn like a man carrying his own coffin, and I said nothing. To anyone. Ever."

"To me," Isolde said quietly. "You said nothing to me. We were — " She stopped. Recalculated. "We were something, then. Weren't we."

"We were something," Lena agreed. "Which is why the next part is worse."

* * *

The reef glowed. Somewhere below decks, Pell murmured tide tables in his sleep like a boy reciting scripture, which, for Pell, it essentially was.

"Eight months later," Lena said, "Hale was dead."

"I know. I was at the funeral. You weren't."

"No. I was in the territory by then, learning to track things across sand." She turned the empty cup in her hands. "Here's what the world believes, what *you* believe: Corvin Hale drowned in the harbor channel, drunk, walking home from the customs house at night. Accident. Open verdict. The constable wrote it up in four lines and everybody shook their heads about wasted genius and moved on."

"That's what happened," Isolde said. "That's what everyone — "

"That's what the *report* says." Lena finally looked at her. "Isolde. I was there. I was the last person to see him alive, and I have spent ten years running across a desert because I cannot stop replaying the last hour of his life and I still don't know — I will never *know* — whether I killed him."

The words came out of her like splinters coming out of a wound, one at a time, each one dragged.

"He sent for me that night. A note, hand-delivered, no signature but I knew the hand. 'Come to the channel stairs, midnight, bring nothing.' So I went. And he was there, at the bottom of the stairs, at the waterline, and he was frightened. I'd never seen him frightened. He said the owners had found out about the ledger. Not that it existed — that it was *aboard the Triangle*, and that the Triangle hadn't gone down where they'd planned, and there were men looking for it, and those men had started with him. He said he'd been followed for three days. He said, 'If anything happens to me, the lock opens for my hands and no one else's, and that has to stay true, Lena. Promise me again.'

"And I promised. And then I heard footsteps on the stairs above us."

Isolde had gone very still. Even the sea seemed to lean in.

"Two men. Maybe three. Big shapes against the lamplight from the street above. And Hale grabbed my arm and said one word — '*go*' — and pushed me toward the water side of the stairwell, the shadow side, and I went. Because he told me to. Because I was nineteen and he was Corvin Hale and when Corvin Hale said jump, the question was only how high." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept talking anyway. "I went down the steps along the wall, into the dark, and I heard them reach him. I heard a short struggle. And then I heard the splash.

"And here is the thing I have never said aloud, not to a constable, not to a priest, not to the sand, not to God: I don't know if they threw him. I don't know if he jumped to lead them away from me. I don't know if he slipped reaching for me in the dark. I was *there*, Isolde. I was six feet away in the black, and I did nothing. I didn't call out. I didn't strike a light. I didn't go in after him, and I could swim, I've always been able to swim. I stood on those stairs with my heart hammering and I listened to the water close over the place where my mentor had been, and then I climbed out the far side and I ran, and I have been running ever since."

Silence. Long enough that Lena heard her own pulse in her ears.

"Ten years," she said. "Every mile I ever measured — thief's trail, salt flat, border ridge, all of it — I was measuring distance from those stairs. I told myself I was a ranger. I told myself I loved the open country. What I was, was a witness who fled, and a keeper of a secret that got a good man killed, and possibly — on the bad nights, and there are a lot of bad nights — the reason he's dead. If I hadn't been there, they'd have had no one to frighten him into silence. If I'd called out, maybe they'd have run. If I'd gone in after him — " She stopped. Shook her head. "Maybe. Maybe. Maybe. Ten years of maybe, and the desert gave me plenty of room to rehearse every version."

* * *

Isolde Vane, to her credit, did not offer comfort. Lena might have struck her if she had. Instead Isolde refilled both cups from the pot she'd apparently brought knowing this conversation would take a while — because of course she had — and waited until Lena's hands had stopped shaking before she spoke.

"Let me tell you what I hear," Isolde said. "Because I think you've had ten years of hearing yourself, and it's rotted the evidence."

"Isolde — "

"No. Listen. You were a child he used as a hiding place. He built a lock into your memory the same way he built one into brass — to protect the truth, whatever the cost to the mechanism. You were the mechanism, Lena. And a mechanism doesn't get to choose. He ordered you onto those stairs' shadow side and you obeyed, because obedience to him was the whole architecture of your young life. That's not guilt. That's design."

"He's still dead."

"He is." Isolde's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "But dead by the owners' men, on the night they came for him — that's murder, not accident, and not your doing. Unless you're going to stand there and tell me you hired those men, in which case I'll want my tea back, because you'd be an entirely different person than the one who just spent three days refusing to touch a strongbox for fear of what it proves."

Lena laughed. It came out broken, but it came out. "Three days of cowardice."

"Three days of a woman deciding whether to trust us with the worst of herself. Different thing." Isolde leaned forward. "Now here's my question, and it's the only one that matters: the letter Rennard wants the ledger for, the second wreck, all of it — does the ledger name the men who killed Hale?"

"The ledger names the owners. All of them. Every name, every payment." Lena swallowed. "And Hale kept records like a man building a gallows. If the men on those stairs were paid, they're in there too. I'd bet the *Mercy* on it."

"Then we're not salvaging a treasure," Isolde said slowly. "We're raising a murder weapon."

"We're raising a confession," Lena corrected. "His. Mine. Theirs. All of ours."

She looked down at the lock between her boots. In the dark it was just a shape, cold and patient, holding ten years of a dead man's handwriting and a living woman's cowardice behind a mechanism her own fingers still remembered.

"Tomorrow," Lena said, "at slack tide, I'm going to open it. Not because I've forgiven myself — I suspect that's a longer voyage than this one — but because I'm done measuring my life in miles from a staircase. Whatever's in that box, this crew deserves to read it in daylight, and so do the families of thirty-one sailors who were written off as weather."

Isolde studied her for a long moment. Then she raised her cup in something that wasn't quite a toast.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the girl who ran was a coward. The woman who stayed to confess isn't. I'd sail with either one, but I'd only trust the second."

Before Lena could answer — before she could decide whether that was the kindest or cruelest thing anyone had ever said to her — the lookout's voice came down from the mast, tight and fast:

"Sail ho! Two points off the lee bow — running dark, no lights, and she's big!"

Isolde was on her feet instantly, cup forgotten. "Rennard," she breathed. "He's not waiting for the truce flag. He knows we have the box."

Lena picked up the lock and held it against her chest like a heartbeat.

"He doesn't know what's in it," she said. "And if I have anything to say about it — he never will."
