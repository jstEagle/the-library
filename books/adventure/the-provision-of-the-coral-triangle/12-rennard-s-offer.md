## 12. Rennard's Offer

The truce flag went up at dawn, and Isolde Vane hated it on principle.

She had known Dax Rennard for nineteen years — long enough to remember when he'd had a full head of hair and a conscience to match, both of which he'd lost in the same season off the Sable Keys. A man like that did not raise a white flag out of generosity. He raised it the way a fisherman raises a lure: something bright and harmless-looking on the surface, hooks underneath.

"Should I answer it?" Pell asked, squinting through the spyglass from the quarterdeck. The boy held it upside down half the time, but he saw more than anyone aboard regardless.

"Answer what?" Bram said, elbow-deep in the winch housing where he'd lived for two days since the boarding skirmish. His bandaged shoulder made him move like a man carrying a sleeping cat he didn't want to wake. "It's a flag. Flags don't talk."

"It's flying from his launch," Pell said. "He's coming over. Alone, I think. Two rowers."

Isolde took the glass herself. Yes — Rennard's longboat cutting across the green water of the anchorage, the white rag snapping above him like an apology he hadn't written yet. Behind it, his surviving ship *Iron Mercy* rode at anchor beyond the reef shelf, patched and sullen after the storm's first blow. And beyond *her*, the outer passage where the monsoon was still chewing on the horizon like a dog with a bone.

Two tides left. Maybe less. The tide tables were lying to them now, Pell had said that morning, his voice very quiet, the way he only spoke when the numbers frightened him.

"He wants to talk," she said.

"People who want to talk send letters," Osa signed, one-handed, from her seat on the rail. Her other arm was splinted to the elbow, and she managed her signs anyway, because Osa managed everything anyway. "People who want something send flags."

Isolde almost smiled. "Both true. Lena?"

Lena stood at the stern rail with her arms crossed, watching the longboat come with the flat, patient stare she used on things she intended to kill or outlast. Ten days at sea had done something to her — softened nothing, exactly, but worn grooves in her, the way water wears stone. She looked less like a woman passing through and more like part of the ship.

"He'll offer you something," Lena said. "That's what he does. He offered me a partnership once, back before either of us knew you." A pause. "I didn't take it. I've wondered sometimes what my life would look like if I had."

"Worse," Isolde said. "You'd have been rich and miserable instead of poor and miserable."

"That is worse," Lena agreed gravely.

* * *

They received him on the quarterdeck, because Isolde refused to receive any man in her cabin — a rule she'd kept since the year a magistrate's inspector had searched it and found the papers that put a charge against her name that had never quite washed off. Rennard came up the ladder alone, as promised, hands open and empty, wearing his best coat, which was to say the one with the fewest burns on it.

"Vane." He said her name like a man testing ice. "You're looking well. Sea agrees with you."

"The sea agrees with no one, Dax. It tolerates us. Sit."

He sat. He looked around the quarterdeck with a professional's eye — at Pell coiling line that didn't need coiling, at Bram leaning in the companionway pretending not to listen, at Osa sharpening a knife that was already sharp, at Lena standing behind Isolde's shoulder like weather about to happen.

"You kept strange company," Rennard observed.

"I keep good company. There's a difference. You should try it sometime; I hear it's restful."

Something flickered across his face — the old wound, the old charge, the thing between them that predated even Lena's decade of running. Nineteen years ago, they had been partners. Then there had been a fire, a lost cargo, a dead supercargo, and an inquiry, and Isolde had walked away from it carrying a name for herself that she hadn't earned honestly and couldn't disprove cleanly. Rennard had walked away carrying the second wreck's coordinates and a grudge shaped exactly like her.

"I'll be brief," he said. "The monsoon closes the outer passage within two tides. You know it. I know it. Neither of our ships makes it out loaded with anything unless we stop wasting each other's time."

"So far this is the most honest thing you've ever said to me. Continue."

He leaned forward. "I have the location of the *Meridian's Pride*. Second wreck. Intact hold, verified by three separate surveys before the owners bought up every chart and buried them. You know what's down there."

Everyone knew the story of the *Meridian's Pride*, the way everyone knew ghost stories: a treasure vessel gone missing forty years back, its cargo manifest sealed by court order, its owners' families grown fat and respectable on the insurance money. A wreck like that wasn't salvage. It was a verdict.

"And in exchange?" Isolde asked, though she already knew. She wanted to hear him say it anyway. There was a pleasure in making a man say a thing out loud that he'd rather have slid across a table in the dark.

"The ledger," Rennard said. "Hale's ledger, from the Triangle. You give it to me, I give you the *Pride*. We both sail out rich, we both sail out clean." He spread his hands. "And Isolde — the ledger names names. The men who scuttled the Triangle, the ones who paid for it to disappear. It also names the people they framed to cover it." His voice dropped, went almost gentle, which from Dax Rennard was the most dangerous register he owned. "It names the supercargo. The one who died in the fire. The inquiry got it wrong, and we both know who they blamed."

The quarterdeck went very quiet. Even Pell stopped coiling.

Nineteen years. Isolde had built a fleet, a reputation, a whole working life on top of that charge the way you build a house on a bad foundation — carefully, and never looking down. *Arson and abandonment of crew,* the inquiry had written, in a clerk's careful hand, and though the conviction had never formally landed, the shadow of it followed her into every harbor, every contract negotiation, every room where money changed hands. *Vane? Vane's reliable enough, considering.* Considering. Always considering.

"They'll clear your name," Rennard said. "Mine too. One document, handed to the right magistrate, and we walk into any port on this coast as free captains. All it costs is a book nobody alive has read."

"All it costs," Isolde repeated.

"You don't even know what's in it. It might be nothing. It might be Hale's grocery lists." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who had rehearsed this. "You'd be trading a maybe for a certainty. That's not even a hard bargain, Vane. That's arithmetic."

* * *

She sent him back to his longboat with wine and courtesy and no answer, because that was how you handled Dax Rennard: you gave him nothing to read.

Then she stood at the stern rail for a long time, watching the white flag come down over on the *Iron Mercy*, and let the arithmetic run itself, because it would whether she watched it or not.

The numbers were simple. That was the obscene part. A name cleared after nineteen years. Harbors that opened instead of narrowed. Contracts signed without the little pause, the little *considering*. And all she had to do was hand over a sealed box whose contents might damn her crew, might damn Lena, might damn a dead man's memory — or might, as Rennard so reasonably pointed out, be grocery lists.

"You're going to do it."

Lena's voice, behind her. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — level. Lena Brightwater delivered bad observations the way she delivered everything else: without flinching.

"I haven't decided."

"You have. You're just deciding whether to tell us yet." Lena came to stand beside her at the rail, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched. Below them, Osa was teaching Pell a knife trick that Bram had expressly forbidden, and Bram was watching from the companionway with the expression of a man who had learned to pick his battles and mostly picked losing ones. "I'd take it," Lena said. "If it were mine. If someone offered me ten years of my life back for one box."

"It isn't yours."

"No. But it's near enough mine that I'm asking anyway." Lena turned to look at her, and her desert-pale eyes were steady. "What's in that ledger matters to me more than it does to anyone else on this ship, and I'm telling you not to trade it. Does that help?"

"It helps," Isolde said, "in the way that a hole in the hull helps. It tells me where the water's coming in."

Lena almost smiled. "Good. Then here's the rest of the leak. Whatever that ledger says about you — whatever it says about the fire, the supercargo, all of it — I'd rather read it with you than have you buy your way around it. Because if you sell it unread, you'll spend the rest of your life wondering. I know how that works. I've been wondering for ten years, and it eats more than a bad name ever could."

Isolde looked at her for a long moment. "You know, when I dragged you out of that sand, I expected trouble. I didn't expect wisdom. It's very inconvenient."

"I'm told I grow on people. Like lichen."

"Like barnacles. Harder to scrape."

Below them, Pell dropped the knife, Osa laughed — actually laughed, a silent shaking of shoulders that was rarer than rain in the salt flats — and Bram threw up his hands. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a crew that trusted its captain to bring them home, and had no idea their captain was doing arithmetic with their lives.

That was the hook of it, Isolde thought. Rennard's offer cost her nothing but a book. But accepting it meant deciding, alone, in the dark of her own cabin, what her crew was worth — and she had spent nineteen years learning exactly what happened to captains who decided things alone in the dark.

"Pell," she called down. "How many tides?"

The boy looked up, and his face did the thing it did when the numbers frightened him. "One and a half," he said. "Maybe two if the wind lies."

One and a half tides to decide whether to buy back her name with a stranger's truth. Isolde turned back to the rail, to the green water and the reef and the wreck below it holding its sealed breath, and found that for the first time in nineteen years, she genuinely did not know what she was going to do.

"Lena," she said quietly. "Wake me an hour before the turn. There's something I need to show you in the hold — and then there's something I need you to help me steal."

Lena raised an eyebrow. "From whom?"

"From Dax Rennard," Isolde said. "His truce flag. He thinks it bought him a conversation." She allowed herself, at last, the beginning of a smile. "It's about to buy him a diversion."
