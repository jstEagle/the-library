## 17. The Rescue at Rennard's Reef

The monsoon came like a debt collector.

Lena had seen storms build in the desert — the slow purple bruising on the horizon, the hour of warning you got if you were smart. The sea gave no such courtesy. One moment the sky was a bruised gray ceiling; the next, the wind arrived with a sound like the whole ocean clearing its throat, and rain came down in ropes, not drops.

"Two tides early," Pell shouted over it, both hands white-knuckled on the wheel. "The tables said two tides!"

"The tables didn't count on Rennard's luck," Isolde called back. She stood at the rail, hair plastered flat, watching the outer reef through the curtain of rain. "Or his bad luck. Look."

Lena looked. Through a tear in the storm she saw them: three ships on the outer reef, and one of them was wrong. It sat too high in the water, canted against the coral like a drunk leaning on a fence post. Even at this distance she could see the shape of the damage — hull breached, masts gone or going, and figures. Small dark figures. In the rigging. On the exposed shelf. In the water between the wreck and the open sea, which was now a churning white field of broken water.

"That's the *Kestrel*," Bram said grimly, appearing at her elbow with a coil of line already over his shoulder. He'd bandaged his own arm from the skirmish days ago and hadn't let anyone look at it since. "Rennard's flagship. I'd know that ugly figurehead anywhere."

"How many souls?" Isolde asked.

Bram squinted. "Forty. Fifty. Maybe more in the water."

The wind shoved the ship sideways. Lena grabbed the rail and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do. The safe window — the passage behind them, the deep channel that would carry the *Vane's Fortune* and her crew and the strongbox sitting in the hold like a sleeping animal — closed with the tide. Their tide. If they turned back for the wreck site, they lost the window. If they stayed the course, they watched fifty people drown in plain sight.

Nobody said any of this out loud. Nobody had to. That was the thing about a crew, Lena was learning. After enough shared misery, you stopped needing words the way you stopped needing introductions.

Isolde turned to face them all, rain streaming off her chin, and Lena braced herself for the captain's calculus — the cold weighing of prize against people that made salvage captains salvage captains.

"We're going about," Isolde said.

Lena blinked. "You're giving up the passage."

"I'm giving up nothing." Isolde's jaw was set in the way Lena had learned meant she'd already fought the battle inside herself and won, ugly as the victory was. "The Coral Triangle has been down ten years. She'll keep. Those people have minutes." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to herself: "And I've been the one in the water before. So has everyone on this deck, one way or another. We go."

* * *

What followed was the worst four hours of Lena's life, and she'd once spent three days buried in a sandstorm eating her own boot grease.

They came upwind of the reef and anchored — what was left of their anchor chain groaning like a condemned man — and began the work. Lines over the side. Osa went first, silent as always, slipping into water that was more foam than sea, and Lena's heart climbed into her throat and stayed there. Pell worked the winches with tears cutting clean tracks through the salt on his face and never once stopped calling the rhythm. Bram hauled. Lena tied knots until her fingers bled, because desert hands knew rope even if they didn't know waves, and because tying knots was better than thinking about the child-sized body that came over the rail blue and limp and then coughed, coughed, *coughed*, and started screaming, which was somehow worse and infinitely better.

They pulled them up one by one. Sailors. Boys, some of them, barely older than Pell. A cook who weighed as much as the ship's boat. A woman with a broken arm who bit Lena, hard, out of pure animal terror, and then wept apologies into Lena's shoulder while Lena wrapped the arm with strips of sailcloth.

Rennard himself came last.

He'd lashed himself to a spar and ridden it toward their lines, and when they hauled him aboard he was half-drowned in earnest — gray-lipped, vomiting seawater, alive by the margin of a coin toss. Lena knelt beside him, rolled him to drain, felt him breathing, and thought with distant surprise: *I'm glad.* Not satisfaction. Gladness. Ten years ago she wouldn't have believed herself capable of it.

Rennard's eyes focused eventually. They found Isolde first, standing over him, and something moved across his ruined face — gratitude, maybe, arriving a half-second before the rest of him caught up.

"You," he rasped. "You *fools*. You've lost the window. The Triangle—"

"Is still on the bottom," Isolde said. "Where she's been since before you learned to swim. She'll keep."

"The ledger—" He tried to rise, failed, collapsed back against the rail. "Do you know what I gave up for that ledger? Do you know what it would have *bought* me? And you threw it away for—" he gestured weakly at the deck, at the shivering, retching, weeping mass of his own crew, "—for *these*?"

"Yes," Isolde said simply.

"They'll hang us anyway! The charge, the owners, all of it — you've saved nothing but a slower death!"

"Then we'll die slower," Isolde said, "together, and dry." She crouched so they were eye to eye. "Get this through your thick skull, Dax. I didn't save you to hear your inventory of regrets. I saved you because fifty people were drowning and I've got a ship and you don't. That's the whole of it. Now shut your mouth before I give you back to the sea, and believe me when I say the sea and I are on speaking terms again after today."

Rennard stared at her. Then he laughed — a horrible, wet, broken laugh that turned into coughing — and put his head back against the rail and let them carry him below.

* * *

By the time the last survivor was aboard, the light was failing and the sea had turned the color of iron. Lena stood at the stern with Isolde and watched the channel close.

There was no drama to it. No wall of water, no thunderclap. Just the reef passage — the deep lane they'd threaded six tides ago, the door home — filling with churning sand and broken coral as the monsoon drove the swell across the shallows. By morning it would be impassable. By tomorrow night, sealed for the season. The Coral Triangle, the strongbox, Hale's lock, everything waiting inside that drowned ship — cut off behind a wall of storm, unopened, unread, unknown.

"There it is," Lena said quietly. "The door shutting."

"Mm." Isolde didn't look away from it. "I've spent my whole life afraid of doors closing. Turns out it depends what's on the other side of you when they do." She glanced back — at the deck crowded with enemy sailors wrapped in blankets, at Pell teaching a Rennard boy the winch, at Osa sitting motionless in the rain letting it wash the reef blood from her arms. "Turn around, Brightwater. Tell me what you see."

Lena turned. She saw a fleet that had become a single ship. Two crews who'd shot at each other nine days ago now sharing a galley that couldn't possibly feed them all, arguing about it in five voices at once. She saw Bram laughing — actually laughing — while he splinted a stranger's wrist. She saw her own hands, rope-burned and steady.

"I see a problem," she said. "Fifty extra mouths and a sealed passage."

"I see a solution," Isolde said. "We winter in the sea caves on the leeward side. There's water there, and fish, and nobody looking for us where nobody can go." Her eyes slid sideways, and there was something in them Lena had never seen before — not calculation, not command. Apprehension. "Which means months. Months in a cave with that strongbox in the hold, and everyone on this ship knowing it's there. Including him." She nodded toward the hatch where they'd taken Rennard.

Lena understood. The storm hadn't just closed the passage. It had locked them all in a room with the truth — with whatever Hale had written, whatever Isolde suspected, whatever Rennard would trade or steal or kill for once he got his strength back. The sea had made the decision for them, in a way. No more running. No more windows. Just time, and proximity, and a box with her mentor's handwriting on it.

"Hale used to say," Lena heard herself say, "that the desert doesn't hide things. It just waits for you to be ready to find them." She swallowed. "Maybe the sea works the same way."

Isolde looked at her for a long moment. Rain drummed on the deck planks between them like impatient fingers.

"Then I hope you're getting ready, Brightwater," she said. "Because come low tide tomorrow, we make for the caves. And I have a feeling that box isn't staying shut much longer."

Below them, faint under the storm, Lena could swear she heard the strongbox shift in the hold — though she knew, rationally, it was only the ship working in the swell. Knew it, and didn't believe it for a second.
