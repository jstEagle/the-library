## 1. Fuel, Rumors, Weather

The engine coughed twice before it caught, which was its way of saying good morning. I'd learned to read the old Wren like a difficult relative — you didn't ask how she was feeling, you listened for the tone of her voice and adjusted your expectations accordingly. That morning the tone said: *I'll fly, but I'll complain about it.*

Good. Complaint was honest. It was the quiet engines you had to worry about.

I was loading at first light because that's when the medicine came up on the mail truck from the coast — two crates of penicillin, a box of insulin packed in dry ice that would start dying the moment the sun cleared the ridge, and a sealed carton nobody would let me inspect that I'd been carrying every second Tuesday for eleven months without asking questions. Asking questions in this country was a luxury. You paid for them in fuel.

The routine went back further than the medicine. Three towns, one circuit, four hundred miles of rock and spruce and river braided so tight from altitude it looked like someone had spilled silver thread on green felt. Kettle Falls got the insulin. Harrow Bend got the penicillin and whatever gossip had accumulated since my last pass through, which was usually more perishable than the drugs and considerably more valuable. Stonebridge got everything else — flour, lamp oil, carbide, the occasional coffin, once a piano that still haunted my dreams and my center-of-gravity calculations.

And gossip ran both directions. That was the trick of it. A pilot who only carried freight was a truck with wings. A pilot who carried news was infrastructure. Old Marta in Harrow Bend told me which families were fighting over fence lines; I told Kettle Falls, and by the time the story made it back around the circuit it had grown two new participants and a lawsuit. Nobody minded. The truth out here was a communal project, like barn-raising, and I did my part by never staying long enough to be assigned a side.

That last bit mattered. One night, any town, no exceptions. I'd kept the rule for six years, ever since a place I don't name and a season I'd rather not describe taught me what happens to a man who lets a town get used to seeing him at the same table twice. People start expecting things. Expectations become obligations. Obligations become history, and history is just cargo you can't unload. My debts were simpler than that — forty dollars to the fuel man in Stonebridge, twenty-two to the co-op in Harrow Bend, and a standing arrangement with Kettle Falls' doctor that I suspected was less debt than charity he was too proud to call anything else. Debts you could pay. History you could only outrun, and I'd gotten good at outrunning.

So when the government survey team requested a supply drop into the high interior — past Stonebridge, past anywhere, up where the map went vague and the contour lines started holding hands — I took the contract the way I took all of them: for the money, against my better judgment, and with a private note that this was exactly the kind of errand that turned a one-night rule into a two-night problem.

The survey team was nine people. I'd met exactly one of them, briefly, at the fuel dock in Stonebridge — a broad man with a surveyor's squint and a way of looking at mountains like he was reading something written under them. Aldous Vane, the manifest said. Lead surveyor. He'd asked me three questions in a row about weather patterns above the tree line, each one more specific than the last, and I remembered thinking: *here's a man who intends to be up there a while.* Then he shook my hand, thanked me for the ice axes I'd hauled in the month before, and walked off toward the government office without looking back.

Nine people is a lot of tonnage. Their camp sat in a bowl below the glacier line, close enough to the calving face that you could hear it go — a crack like God snapping kindling, then a groan, then the sea-green thunder of ice letting go into the meltwater. On a clear day the sound carried thirty miles. I'd been making the run every ten days since spring, and I'll admit the calving had become the part of the trip I looked forward to, if that's the right word for enjoying a noise that reminds you the world rearranges itself whether or not you're watching.

This drop was routine on paper: staples, kerosene, replacement batteries for their radio set, a crate of books somebody's wife had sent, and the mail — actual letters, nine of them, which in the interior counted as an event. I flew in low through the pass as the sun came over the eastern ridge and lit the glacier pink, the kind of light that makes even a man who avoids beautiful things pull his eyes off the instruments for three seconds too long.

Three seconds too long is how near-misses happen, so I corrected, dropped to the bowl, and made my approach.

* * *

The strip was the same scraped gravel shelf it always was, wind sock hanging dead, no movement.

That was the first wrong thing, though I didn't know it yet. There was always movement. Nine people couldn't all be busy at once, not at eight in the morning, not when the plane's drone announced me twenty minutes out the way a church bell announces Sunday. Somebody always came down. Usually it was the cook, a wiry little man named Fenn who waved both arms like he was flagging in a landing that might kill him, then complained about the coffee I brought, then drank three cups of it anyway. Sometimes it was Vane himself, standing apart from the others with his notebook, watching the glacier instead of the plane, which I'd privately decided was either scientific dedication or a man avoiding his own version of whatever I was avoiding mine.

Today: nobody.

I touched down anyway, because the alternative was flying four hundred miles home with nine people's mail and a story I couldn't explain, and unexplained stories cost more than bad landings. The Wren rolled out on the gravel and I shut her down and stepped into air cold enough to make my lungs file a complaint.

The camp was there. Tents staked, radio mast up, the cook tent angled correctly against the prevailing wind. The stove pipe was putting out smoke — thin, but putting it out. Gear lay everywhere in the particular disorder of a working camp: core boxes stacked under tarp, sample bags drying on lines, somebody's boots outside somebody's tent, laces tied together the way you do when you're in a hurry and plan to come right back.

Everything was normal except the silence, and the silence was total. Not sleeping-quiet. Absent-quiet. The difference between a house where everyone's in bed and a house where everyone left.

I stood at the edge of the strip longer than I should have, doing the arithmetic pilots do. Fuel burn. Weather window. The cloud building over the western peaks that meant the snows were maybe two weeks out, maybe ten days, maybe — the way this range liked to do it — tomorrow. I had a schedule. I had debts. I had a rule.

I walked into the camp.

The stove was warm. Not warm-from-this-morning warm — warm enough that whoever had fed it had done so recently, within the hour, maybe less. Coffee sat in the pot, and it wasn't stale. Boots by tents, packs gone. The radio hut stood open, and inside it the set sat clean and powered down, batteries I'd delivered sitting in their crate, unopened, packing straw still tucked around them like eggs.

Nobody leaves fresh batteries unopened. Not out here. Out here the radio is the only voice you have, and you treat it accordingly.

The logbook was on the desk. I didn't touch it — that felt like reading someone's mail, and I had a whole other category of rules about mail — but it was closed, squared to the desk edge, deliberate. Everything in that hut was deliberate. The chairs pushed in. The chart on the wall smoothed flat and weighted at the corners. A camp doesn't tidy itself. A camp tidied by nine people leaving in a hurry looks like a storm hit it. This looked like a room prepared for inspection.

Except outside. Outside, the gear.

Two sledges, fully loaded, tarped and lashed. A third sledge frame, empty. Core boxes — dozens of them, the good wooden ones, the expensive ones — stacked in rows under canvas, labeled in pencil, labels facing out. Sample crates. A case of dynamite, which I mention because I checked on it twice, once professionally and once personally. Rope coils hung on the drying rack. Snowshoes in pairs, seven pairs, hung neat as church pews.

Seven pairs. Nine people.

You don't leave equipment like that behind unless you're coming back for it, or unless you never intended to take it in the first place. And you don't leave it *organized*. Abandonment is chaos. This was inventory. Someone had gone down a list.

I walked to the tree line, because that was the other thing. The bowl's east edge ran up into spruce, and the ground between camp and trees held frost that morning — frost that holds prints the way paper holds ink. There were tracks. Boot prints, a dozen sets at least, walking away from camp in loose order, not fleeing, not marching — walking the way people walk when they've agreed on a direction beforehand. They went up through the trees and thinned as the ground rose, and then they stopped being readable, and I stopped following, because the light was going flat and my fuel arithmetic had turned ugly and some part of me older than my judgment said: *this is not your business, Ashgrove. Walk back to the plane.*

But before I turned, I noticed the last detail, the one that stayed with me the whole flight home and kept me company worse than any passenger could have.

At the tree line, one print apart from the others, deeper than the rest — a heavy man, or a man carrying weight — there was a boot print facing back toward the camp. Just one. Everyone else had walked north into the trees. Someone had stood there, alone, looking back at the tents and the stacked cores and the warm stove, and then he'd walked north too, and the frost had already begun to soften his print by the time I found it.

Fresh. Within the hour. Maybe minutes.

I stood in a stranger's footprint and felt the hair come up on my arms, and I want to be honest about what I felt, because it wasn't fear, or not only. It was recognition. Here was a man doing what I did — stopping at a boundary, looking back at the place he was leaving, weighing whether the leaving would hold. I knew that pause. I'd stood in it a hundred times, in a hundred doorways, in towns I refused to name. The difference between us was that he'd had nine reasons to look back, and I never let myself have one.

I walked back to the Wren. Loaded nothing, because there was nothing to load — the mail I'd brought sat in its sack on the radio hut table, which meant I'd have to tell someone I'd delivered to an empty camp, and telling someone was going to be its own problem. I fired up the engine. She coughed twice, caught, complained.

Climbing out of the bowl I passed over the tree line and looked down once at the frost, at the prints going north, at the single print facing south, already fading. Then the ridge took the view and I put my eyes back on the instruments, because that's the discipline, that's the whole discipline: forward, forward, forward.

By the time I reached Stonebridge the light was going copper and the western clouds had organized themselves into something with intent. I reported the camp to the government office the way you report anything official — briefly, factually, with the flat tone of a man who has learned that tone the hard way. Stove warm. Gear staged. Tracks north. No distress signal. No explanation.

The clerk took it down without expression and said someone would want to speak with me.

Someone did. He arrived within the hour, which surprised me — government moves at the speed of mail out here, and mail moves at the speed of my airplane, and I hadn't landed the news more than ninety minutes ago. He was a compact man in a coat too good for the territory, city boots with no scuffs on them, and he introduced himself as Corven, Ministry of Interior Resources, and he asked if I had time for a few questions.

I had time. I always had time for officials; it was cheaper than having trouble with them.

But I noticed, even before he opened his mouth, that his questions weren't the questions a worried man asks. A worried man asks *are they alive, is anyone hurt, can you go back.* Corven asked me what the gear had been arranged like. Whether the core boxes had been labeled. Which direction the tracks pointed, precisely. Whether I'd touched anything.

Whether I'd touched anything.

I gave him answers, all true, none complete, and watched him write them down in a small book he handled like it contained more than notes. And when he finished, he looked at me with a pleasant, unhurried expression and said the thing that has kept me awake every night since:

"You must have formed an impression, flying in and out of that camp all season. What sort of operation would you say Mr. Vane was running up there?"

And I heard myself answer, "A careful one," and understood, somewhere below the level of words, that I had just answered a question I didn't yet know was being asked — and that the man writing it down already knew more about that camp than I ever would.
