## 2. The Camp With No Tracks

I came back on schedule, which is the first thing I want on the record. Eleven days after the supply drop, same as the manifest said, same as I'd told Corven's little book. The weather gave me a window and I took it, because that's what I do — windows, not questions.

The Kestrel climbed out of the valley town into a sky the color of dishwater, and for two hours I flew north with nothing but my own thoughts for company, which is more company than I usually like. I ran the numbers on my fuel debts instead of thinking about Vane's camp, which is its own kind of prayer. Three towns owed money to, three towns owing me, and the arithmetic only worked if I kept moving. Staying still is how debts catch you. Staying still is how anything catches you.

The survey camp sat in a bowl below the tree line where the mountains start getting serious about themselves. From two miles out I could see something wrong, though it took me another mile to name it.

No smoke.

Eleven days into autumn at that altitude, there should have been smoke standing up from the cook stove like a flagpole. There was no smoke. There was also no one outside, and that was wrong too, because every time I'd come in all season, someone had come out to wave me off or wave me in — usually the young one, Halloran, who waved like he was swatting at bees, like waving was an administrative duty he performed under protest.

Nobody waved.

I circled once. The tents were up. The gear was stacked. Everything looked, from the air, like a photograph of a camp rather than a camp — correct in every detail and entirely without life.

I put the Kestrel down on the gravel shelf they'd rolled flat in June, and I'll admit my hands were doing the landing before my head agreed to it, because that's what hands are for when the head is busy being afraid.

* * *

The stoves were warm.

That's the detail that has never left me since, the one I return to at three in the morning when sleep and I aren't speaking. Both stoves — the cook stove in the mess tent and the little iron heater in Vane's command tent — were warm. Not hot. Not cold. Warm, the way a stove is warm when someone fed it an hour ago and walked away meaning to come back.

There was coffee in the pot. Not old coffee gone to tar — coffee that still had a skin forming on it, half-made, abandoned mid-pour. A mug sat beside the pot, empty, upright, placed neatly by the stove door the way you'd set it if you expected to be back in ten minutes.

I stood in that mess tent and felt the warmth of the stove through my boot soles and understood that I was standing inside a held breath.

"Vane!" I called it anyway. "Halloran! Ashgrove — supply run!"

My voice went out into the trees and came back smaller than it left.

I did what a careful man does. I checked the gear tent first, because gear tells the truth when people won't. And the gear was lying to me, or telling me a truth I didn't want. They'd left things no survey team leaves. Two rifles leaned in the rack, oiled and loaded. The spare radio battery sat in its crate, unopened. Winter boots — four pairs of winter boots, rated for exactly the kind of season that was six weeks out — hung from their pegs like sleeping bats. Nobody walks into a high range in October leaving winter boots behind. Nobody walks anywhere leaving rifles behind, not up here, where the wilderness doesn't hate you but doesn't love you either.

But the footprints were the thing. The footprints were the thing that turned unease into something with edges.

They started at the tree line — six sets, maybe seven, boot prints in the frost-softened ground, spaced like people walking with purpose but not running. Walking like they knew where they were going. The prints went up through the pines, over the frost, across a scree fan —

And stopped.

Not faded. Not scattered by wind or melted by sun. Stopped. At the edge of the scree, where the rock went bare, the last clear print pointed uphill, and above it there was nothing. No sign of passage over stone that would have shown it. No cairn, no blazes, no cut branches. Seven people had walked into the range as if the range had been expecting them, and the mountain had closed behind them like water.

I stood at that tree line for a long time. Long enough for the stove-warmth memory to go cold in me. Long enough to notice the burn pit.

The burn pit was fresh. Ash still held its shape in layers — paper ash, mostly, the delicate gray lace of documents burned fast and hot. Logbooks. I knew the shape of logbook spines in ash; I've burned my own share of records in my day, though mine were fuel ledgers and licensing papers, and I burned them for reasons I'm not proud of. Somebody had burned the survey logs, thoroughly, recently, and then — this was the part that made my neck prickle — raked the pit smooth. Tidied it. You don't tidy your own escape. You tidy for someone who's coming to look.

Seven people hadn't fled that camp. They'd prepared it. Like actors striking a set between scenes, leaving everything in place except themselves.

I flew out of there without waiting for dark, which for me is saying something, and I told myself the whole way south that I was going to report it properly, to the proper authorities, because whatever else Orsen Ashgrove is, he files his paperwork. It's the one clean thing about me.

* * *

Corven received me in the district office in Harrow Bend, a room that smelled of lamp oil and stamp ink, and he was glad to see me in the way a fisherman is glad to see weather.

"Ashgrove." He didn't get up. He had a small book open on the desk in front of him, the same book, and a pen laid across it like a surgeon's instrument resting between incisions. "Sit. You look like a man carrying something heavy."

"I found the survey camp empty."

I said it plainly, because I'd rehearsed plainness the whole flight down, and I watched it land on him the way rain lands on canvas — absorbed, noted, not soaked through.

"Empty," he repeated.

"Warm stoves. Coffee half-made. Gear left behind — rifles, winter kit, a sealed radio battery. Footprints going up toward the scree line and ending at bare rock. And a burn pit full of paper ash, raked flat."

I gave him all of it. Every detail, in order, the way I'd catalogued it in the air. And here is what I want understood: I watched him while I talked, and Corven never once looked surprised. Not at the stoves. Not at the boots. Not at the footprints ending at stone. He wrote, unhurried, pen moving in that small book, and when I finished he sat back and folded his hands and looked at me with an expression I can only call satisfied, the way a man looks at a lock that has just accepted his key.

"That's very thorough," he said. "Very thorough indeed."

"They're missing," I said. "Seven people. In the high range, ahead of the snows, without winter kit. That's not a walk, Mr. Corven. That's a body count waiting to be confirmed."

"Yes." He said it mildly, the way you'd agree about the price of nails. "It does look rather like they intended not to be found."

I waited for him to say something else. He let the silence do his work for him, and it was good work.

"The boots," he said finally. "You're certain about the boots. Four pairs?"

"At least. On their pegs."

"And the burn pit — raked, you said. Smooth."

"Like a garden bed."

He made a note. Then he asked the question I should have recognized as the hinge of everything, the question that in retrospect opened a door I'd been leaning against all along:

"Did you touch anything?"

"No," I said. "Well — the coffee pot. I lifted the lid."

Something moved behind his eyes then. Not alarm. Interest. The specific interest of a man confirming a measurement.

"You lifted the lid," he repeated. "Of the coffee pot."

"To see how fresh it was."

"And how fresh was it?"

"An hour. Maybe less. Skin just forming."

Corven nodded slowly, and wrote, and I sat in that lamplit office feeling the floor tilt a degree beneath me, because I had just realized that none of my answers were informing him. He wasn't collecting what I saw. He was checking it against something he already carried — comparing my testimony to a document only he could read, ticking boxes, finding them in order. Warm stoves: expected. Burned logs: expected. Footprints to the scree: expected. Coffee, one hour: expected.

I was not a witness. I was a calibration.

"Mr. Ashgrove," he said, closing the book at last, and his voice went pleasant and reasonable in a way that frightened me more than any shout, "you've done the district a service. I'll want you to keep this quiet for now. Official channels move slowly, and premature talk breeds panic in the valley towns. Families, you understand. Wives. If word gets out that seven men walked into the ice, we'll have search parties forming before we have facts, and search parties lose people too."

"People are already lost," I said.

"People are accounted for," Corven said, "in ways that don't concern a supply pilot."

He stood, and shook my hand, and his hand was dry and cool and entirely unhurried. At the door he paused, as if remembering something minor.

"One more thing. When you return to the camp — and you will return, on your next scheduled run, because the contract stands until I say otherwise — I'd like you to note whether anything has been disturbed. Whether anyone has been back. You have a good eye, Ashgrove. A careful eye." He smiled. "A careful man notices careful work."

I flew home in the last light with Corven's words riding shotgun, and somewhere over the pass I finally named the thing that had been wrong in that office, the thing beyond the lack of surprise.

He hadn't asked me where they went. Not once. A man reporting seven people missing into the high range asks where they went. Corven asked only what I had seen — the boots, the ash, the hour-old coffee — details, verifications, the inventory of a scene he was preserving in his mind like a man preserving a room against a future inspection.

Which meant Corven didn't need to know where the survey team had gone.

Which meant either he already knew —

— or he knew it didn't matter, because whatever they'd found under the ice, it was never meant to come back down.

I landed at dusk with my hands steady and my stomach in revolt, and I made myself a promise I'd break within the week: file it, forget it, keep flying. Some cargo isn't yours to carry.

Then I saw the woman waiting by my hangar, pack on her shoulder, charter money in her fist, and understood that the mountain had decided otherwise.
