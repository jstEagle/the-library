## 3. A Passenger Named Nobody

The telegram came to me at the fuel office in Kestrel Bend, which is where all bad news finds pilots, because we're the only people in the interior who can't pretend we didn't get it. *Passenger charter, one seat, Lowmark to Harrow Gap. Fare prepaid. Name: V. Solano.* I read it twice, mostly because prepaid fares are rarer than honest weather, and a prepaid fare from Lowmark — a town of forty souls and one saloon that doubles as the morgue — smells like either charity or trouble. In my experience they arrive wearing each other's coats.

I told myself I'd take it for the money. That was true the way most of my statements are true: technically, and only if you don't look too hard.

Lowmark sat on a shelf of rock above the river, and I put the Wren down on its strip with the usual crosswind argument and won, as usual, on points. The saloon keeper, Dree, who owed me eleven dollars and paid her debts in gossip, was waiting with the news before my wheels stopped rolling.

"Your passenger's been here since dawn," she said, sliding a coffee across the counter without being asked. "Been sitting in that corner chair like it's a cockpit. Hasn't touched anything but water."

"Maybe she's careful."

"Maybe." Dree has a way of saying *maybe* that means *no*. "She asked me about you. Asked what kind of man flies medicine into the interior on schedule and never stays past a night."

"What'd you tell her?"

"The truth. That you're either running from something or toward it, and nobody in three towns can tell which." She wiped the counter with a rag that had outlived several governments. "She liked that answer. Wrote it down."

That should have been my first warning. People who write things down are either poets or problems.

Vera Solano turned out to be a young woman of maybe twenty-three, dressed in the kind of canvas jacket prospectors' nieces wear when their uncles buy them clothes and their own hands have never broken a crust of frozen earth. She had a pack that rode her shoulders wrong — too heavy for how easily she carried it, balanced by someone who understood load distribution but not how to fake not understanding it. She shook my hand with a grip that said *survey instruments* or *surgical tools*, something precise, and then let go a half-second early, the way people do when they've caught themselves being competent.

"Mr. Ashgrove?" Her voice was level, trained-level, the kind of calm you learn in a classroom or a crisis. "Thank you for coming."

"You prepaid. I'd have come for less."

"I know. I asked around." She settled into the right-hand seat like she'd been briefed on it — checked the harness twice, knew where the fire extinguisher lived without looking. "Is the weather window good to Harrow Gap?"

"It's a window. Windows close. We leave now."

She didn't argue, didn't chatter, didn't do any of the things nervous passengers do to fill the silence of an engine. She just watched the country go by under us with the expression of someone taking inventory, and I flew, and between us we generated the quietest hour of aviation in the interior that season.

It was somewhere over the divide, banking around a ridge the wind had opinions about, that I saw it. Not the tags themselves — those came later — but the moment she reached into the pack for a canteen and I caught the glint of stitching along the lining seam. Fresh stitching. The kind you do in a hurry, in bad light, with thread that doesn't quite match. Nobody sews new seams into a pack lining unless they're hiding something or changing what the pack used to be. And nobody changes what a pack used to be unless the pack's history has become dangerous.

I said nothing. Rule of the interior: you don't ask questions over open ground, because the ground carries sound and so does the radio, and because a question asked is a debt collected whether you meant to borrow or not.

We landed at Harrow Gap with two hours of light left. She helped me tie down the Wren without being asked, and her knots were textbook, and she caught herself again, and I pretended not to notice her noticing. There's a particular loneliness in watching someone else perform their lie well. It's like hearing your own accent in a stranger's mouth.

At the Harrow Gap trading post, over a meal neither of us tasted, she finally gave me her story. Uncle prospector, up past the Serrano forks, taken ill, needs medicine and company, niece comes out to arrange supplies. It was a good story. It had a sick uncle, which buys sympathy, and a fork in a river, which buys geography, and it was delivered with just enough hesitation in the wrong places — deliberate hesitation, placed like ballast — to make it sound unrehearsed.

The problem was her hands. Prospectors' nieces have hands like tanned rope. Hers had the soft palms of someone whose work is done with fingertips, and small scars on the left thumb and forefinger in the pattern I recognized from my own brief, disastrous attempt at drafting school: the marks of a ruling pen held too long, too tightly. A surveyor's hand. A mapmaker's hand.

And there was one more thing. When she thought I wasn't looking, she took a folded paper from inside her jacket, read it, and put it back, and the paper had the look of a field notebook page — gridded, annotated, creased along lines made by folding and refolding until the creases went soft as cloth.

I've spent nine years building a man named Orsen Ashgrove out of spare parts: a license with a smudge on the medical date, debts in three towns kept deliberately unpaid because creditors remember you kindly longer than friends do, a rule against sleeping anywhere twice. I know forgery from the inside. I know what it costs and how it's maintained and the exact weight of the moment when someone almost sees through it.

So when Vera Solano looked up from her untouched stew and caught me watching her, and neither of us said anything, and both of us smiled the small flat smiles of professionals acknowledging each other's work across an invisible table — I understood that I wasn't flying a passenger out of the interior.

I was flying a fellow liar. And liars, unlike passengers, always want something.

* * *

Mara

He knows. Of course he knows. He's known since the canteen, maybe since the handshake, possibly since the moment I walked into Dree's saloon and asked questions no real prospector's niece would ask, because real nieces don't investigate their pilots, they endure them.

His name is Orsen Ashgrove, and my father said it into a dying radio six weeks ago, and I have built my entire life since then around getting within arm's reach of that name.

I was in the second row of the lecture hall when the transmission came through — patched, degraded, half the words eaten by static, but the operator played it three times and the third time I heard it clean: *"—if anyone receives this, tell Ashgrove. Tell him what we found under the ice. He'll understand why it has to be him—"* And then nothing, and then the official version, which was that the survey team had completed its work early and relocated, and the official version was delivered by a smooth man named Corven who did not once blink during the entire briefing, and I have since learned that this is what guilt looks like when it has government funding.

My father taught me that wilderness doesn't eat people. Institutions do. He also taught me never to trust a story that arrives already smoothed, and Corven's story arrived polished to furniture finish.

So I became nobody. I bought a pack from a dead prospector's estate in Lowmark — God forgive me, I felt the man's ghost approve of the price — and I sewed four equipment tags from the survey team's stores into the lining, because if anyone ever searched me, I wanted them to find exactly enough truth to stop digging. A forged document says *liar*. A genuine tag from a vanished expedition says *witness*. Witnesses get questioned; liars get disappeared. I chose to be questioned.

Ashgrove noticed the stitching within the first hour. I watched him see it — the little hitch in his eyes, the recalculation — and he said nothing, and that silence told me more than interrogation would have. An innocent pilot asks. A guilty one waits. A professional one does both arithmetic at once and keeps flying.

He is not what I expected. I expected the man my father named to be either a hero or a monster, and instead he's tired, watchful, funny in a dry way that seems accidental, like humor leaking out of a sealed container. He ties knots beautifully and pretends his hands don't know things. He has the same look my father got in the last year — the look of a person carrying something heavy and pretending it's luggage.

Tonight at Harrow Gap he watched me read my father's notebook page — I let him see me do it, deliberately, because two liars in one room is a waste of tradecraft — and he didn't ask what was written. Instead he said, "Whatever's in that paper, it's heavier than your pack," and went out to check the tie-downs in weather that didn't need checking.

Tomorrow he flies back to Kestrel Bend, and tomorrow I either tell him everything or follow him forever, and I've done enough following. My father trusted him with his last words. The least I owe that is the truth, even if the truth gets us both killed — though I'm beginning to suspect that Ashgrove would consider that a fair fare.

There's just one thing I haven't figured out yet, and it's keeping me awake in this freezing room above the trading post: the radio message named him. *Tell Ashgrove.* A man he claims he never met. Which means either my father was wrong about him —

or everyone else in this story already knows exactly who Orsen Ashgrove is, and why a dying man eight hundred miles from anywhere spent his final breaths on his name.
