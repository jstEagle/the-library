## 4. The Last Transmission

My father taught me that a lie holds best when you build it out of true things. He was a surveyor, which is to say a professional truth-teller with instruments, and he was also a man who'd spent thirty years working for people who preferred his truths filed, stamped, and buried. So he taught me the craft of the constructed lie the way other fathers teach fly-casting: patiently, with corrections, and always with the caveat that you should never need it if you've planned properly.

I had not planned properly. I had planned for a world where my father came home.

So there I was in the third week of pretending to be Nadia Voss, prospector's niece, sitting in the passenger seat of Orsen Ashgrove's battered supply plane while he flew me out of the valley under a sky the color of dishwater, and every true thing I had built my lie from was rattling around in my pack like loose tools. The equipment tags sewn into the lining. The field notebook written in my father's cramped hand. And underneath all of it, folded twice, the transcript of the last transmission — the one that had named a stranger by name and ruined everything.

Orsen flew the way my father walked a ridge line: constantly reading the ground, never trusting it. His hands were quiet on the yoke but his eyes were busy, flicking between the horizon and the gauges and — I noticed, because I'd been trained to notice — me. Not often. Just enough to tell me he hadn't bought the niece story either.

Good. Liars respect each other's work, but they don't relax around it.

"You're staring at the fuel gauge like it owes you money," I said.

"It does." His voice was dry gravel over something warmer. "Everything in three towns owes me money. The gauge is just honest about it."

I laughed before I could stop myself, and hated how easily it came. Grief does that — it leaves you porous. You spend weeks armoring up as someone else and then one dry remark from a stranger with tired eyes and the armor gaps right where you least want it.

We flew on through weather that was deciding whether to become serious. Below us the valley unspooled — river, road, the little towns strung along it like beads on a wire, each one a place where Orsen Ashgrove apparently had debts and no intention of staying longer than a night. I'd done my research on him in the two days between my father's last transmission and my decision to get on this man's plane anyway. Everyone who knew him said the same things. Reliable. Restless. Doesn't talk about himself. Won't stay anywhere. A man running from something, though nobody could say what, because nobody had ever seen him stand still long enough to look at it.

My father had known. That was the part that kept waking me at night. My father had never met Orsen Ashgrove — I'd checked, gone through twenty years of field logs and Christmas cards and the names in the margins of his maps — and yet the last words he chose to broadcast into the empty quarter were this man's name.

* * *

It had come in at 0340, eleven days ago, on a frequency my father had told me to monitor "if anything ever seems wrong," which was itself an alarming sentence for a man whose idea of small talk was barometric pressure.

The transmission was short. Static chewed its edges. But I have my mother's ear for voices under noise, and I know my father's voice the way you know your own heartbeat — including when it's frightened.

*"Mara, if you're listening — don't come looking. We're going dark by choice. What we found under the ice isn't staying under the ice unless we make it stay. If it goes wrong, find Ashgrove. Orsen Ashgrove. He flies the medicine runs. Tell him—"*

And then the static took the rest, and the frequency went dead, and it has stayed dead, and I have spent eleven days hearing the shape of that unfinished sentence in every silence.

*Tell him what?*

Tell him thank you? Tell him run? Tell him they'll come for him too? Every version rearranged my life differently, and none of them came with instructions.

Here is what I did instead of following the one instruction he gave me — *don't come looking.* I looked. Of course I looked. I am my father's daughter, which means obedience was never among my inherited traits. I found the camp coordinates in his notes. I found the flight schedules. I found that the only pilot flying into the interior that week, the only pilot crazy enough or cheap enough or both, was Orsen Ashgrove, and I understood then why my father had named him.

Not because he trusted him. Because Ashgrove was the door. The only door. Whatever my father wanted finished saying, it had to go through a man who flew supplies into places the government pretended weren't there, and who owed money in three towns, and who never stayed anywhere — a man with no roots to hold hostage and no habits to predict.

My father hadn't named a friend. He'd named a vehicle. And I hated him a little for it, in the way you hate the dead for leaving you their problems instead of their solutions, and I loved him entirely, and both of those things got me onto this plane wearing a name that wasn't mine.

* * *

The weather made its decision somewhere over the second pass. Snow began ticking against the windshield like fingers testing glass.

"We'll be down in Lowmark inside the hour," Orsen said. "Assuming the mountain cooperates."

"Mountains rarely do."

"No," he agreed. "They mostly just wait for you to commit."

He glanced at me again — that quick, inventorying glance — and I decided, somewhere between one gust and the next, that the arithmetic had changed. Because here was the thing about building a lie out of true things: eventually the true things start pressing against the seams. And here was the other thing, the one my father would have appreciated: I needed Orsen Ashgrove to be more than a vehicle now. The transmission had named him. Which meant whoever came looking for my father's trail — and someone would; men like Corven didn't send smooth reassurances to worried pilots out of kindness — would eventually arrive at the same door my father had named.

Orsen was already implicated. He just didn't know it yet. And a man who doesn't know he's implicated makes careless choices, and careless choices get people killed, and I had lost enough people this month to last several lifetimes.

"Ashgrove." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Pull back your throttle a minute. I need to show you something, and I'd rather you not be surprised at altitude."

His hands tightened on the yoke — the first unquiet thing I'd seen them do. "Surprises at altitude are how pilots die."

"Then consider this preventive maintenance." I pulled the pack into my lap and worked open the lining seam with my thumbnail, the one I'd sewn shut four nights ago in a bunkhouse bathroom by candlelight. The tags spilled into my palm: aluminum squares stamped with the survey team's serial numbers, my father's among them. I set them on the console between us, next to the fuel gauge that owed him money.

He looked at them for a long moment. The snow ticked. The engine droned its one patient note.

"Nadia Voss," he said finally, "prospector's niece."

"That was the idea."

"Your hands have held core samples. Your boots are government-issue resoled twice. And you sew a better hidden seam than any niece I've ever invented." He wasn't angry. That was the unnerving part. He sounded almost relieved, the way a man sounds when a weight he's been carrying alone turns out to have a handle. "Who are you?"

"Mara Vane." It felt obscene, saying it aloud after eleven days of exile from my own name — and necessary, like setting a bone. "Elias Vane's daughter. Lead surveyor, interior team."

Something moved behind his eyes. Recognition assembling itself from pieces he'd already collected without knowing why he'd kept them: the burned logbooks, the gear left behind, the footprints ending at the tree line, the official named Corven asking questions a little too precisely.

"They didn't vanish," he said slowly.

"No. They walked out. On purpose. All of them." I unfolded the transcript and laid it beside the tags. "This came in eleven days ago. Listen to the end of it, because I've listened to it a hundred times and I still can't—"

I stopped. Steadied. Grief is a tool like any other, my father would have said. Use it; don't let it use you.

"He says, *what we found under the ice isn't staying under the ice unless we make it stay.* Then he tells me not to come looking. Then he says, *if it goes wrong, find Ashgrove. Orsen Ashgrove. He flies the medicine runs. Tell him—*" I looked up. "And then nothing. Eleven days of nothing. So I'm telling you, Mr. Ashgrove. You're the message. I'm just the delivery method."

Orsen stared at the transcript without touching it, the way you stare at a snake you suspect is dead but aren't willing to bet on. When he spoke, his voice had gone very flat, which I was learning was how he handled fear — ironing it flat so it couldn't wrinkle into panic.

"Why me? He never met me. I never met him. I dropped crates at that camp six times and nobody ever waved me off."

"I don't know what he meant to say. But I know why he picked you." I tapped the tags. "Because you're the only one who goes everywhere and belongs nowhere. Because Corven's people can lean on a man with a family, a license, a home — and you've got debts instead, and debts can't be taken hostage. And because—" I hesitated, then decided he'd earned the whole ugly truth, "—because whoever buried whatever they found will eventually work backward from that camp to every route in and out of it. And there's exactly one route. One plane. One pilot."

The snow thickened. Somewhere below us, Lowmark's strip lights appeared through the murk like coins at the bottom of a well.

"So I'm already in it," Orsen said quietly.

"You were in it the day you flew the first crate. You just hadn't been told." I watched him absorb that — watched the restless competence in him reorganize itself around a fixed point for maybe the first time in years. "There's more. My father's field notes use a route code I haven't cracked yet, but it references a glacier corridor that opens during calving season. Weeks, maybe days. Whatever they found, whatever they're guarding, the window to reach it is closing with the temperature."

He was silent for a long moment. Then, wryly, terribly:

"I had a rule about this. Never carry cargo that talks."

"And now?"

"Now I'm thinking about renegotiating." He banked toward the lights, and his jaw was set in a way that had nothing to do with weather. "Because that man Corven asked me yesterday whether anyone had contacted me since the disappearance. Asked it twice. Worded differently both times." His knuckles whitened on the yoke. "Which means somebody thinks I'm holding something. And I'd very much like to know what it is before they come collect."

I put my hand flat on the transcript between us.

"Then land fast," I said, "and refuel slow. Because I think we're about to find out exactly what my father died trying to keep under the ice — and Corven's offer is coming for you tomorrow."
