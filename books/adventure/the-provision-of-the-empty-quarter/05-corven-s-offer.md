## 5. Corven's Offer

The summons came the way weather comes to the interior — not as a question but as a fact you'd better arrange yourself around. A telegram, hand-delivered by a boy who wouldn't meet my eyes, reading: *MR. ASHGROVE. DISTRICT OFFICE. 0900. R. CORVEN.* No please. No subject line. Men like Corven don't use subjects; they use people.

I read it twice over coffee in the Blue Spruce diner, where the waitress — Della, who kept a ledger of my tab that was longer than some of my flight plans — refilled my cup without being asked and said, "You look like a man going to a funeral."

"Going to an office," I said. "Sometimes it's hard to tell the difference."

She almost smiled. "You want pie for after? In case?"

"In case," I agreed, because a man walking into a room with Corven in it should always pre-arrange something sweet and civilian on the far side of it. A reason to come out. Pilots are superstitious about strange things — never say "fuel's fine," never wash the plane before a storm — but I've always held that the best superstition is dessert waiting for you at the end of a bad morning.

The district office sat above the assay shop, up a staircase that smelled of cold iron and old paper, and Corven received me in a room with one window and too many maps. He was a compact man, gray at the temples, with the kind of stillness that government work beats into a person until it looks like patience. On his desk: my file. I knew it was mine because of its thickness. Honest men have thin files.

"Ashgrove," he said, without standing. "Sit."

I sat. The chair had been placed closer to the desk than comfort allowed, which is a trick I recognized from interrogation rooms and dentists' offices — two professions with more in common than either admits.

"You fly into the interior," he said. It wasn't a question either. Everything this man said was a statement wearing a question's clothes.

"When there's cargo worth carrying and weather that permits."

"And when it doesn't permit?"

"Then I wait. Weather doesn't negotiate."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not annoyance. Recognition. He filed it away somewhere; I could practically hear the drawer close. He opened my file and turned it toward me like a man showing cards he already knew were good.

"Three towns hold debts against you," he said. "Fuel liens in Kestrel Crossing. An outstanding tab at the Blue Spruce — don't look surprised, Miss Delacroix reports her receivables quarterly, it's a condition of her lease. And a licensing matter." He tapped a page. "Or several. Your medical certification lapsed fourteen months ago. You've been flying on a renewal that hasn't been processed because you haven't applied for it. There are also questions about your aircraft's last annual inspection, which appears to have been signed off by a mechanic who, as of last spring, no longer holds a license himself."

"I could explain all of that."

"I'm sure you could. Explanations are cheap. That's why we're having this conversation instead of a hearing." He closed the file, gently, the way you'd close a lid on something sleeping. "Here's what I'm prepared to do. All of it goes away. The liens, the tab, the certifications. Clean slate. You'd be the most legitimate pilot in three districts."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange, you fly one mission. A recovery operation." He slid a paper across the desk. On it, a flight plan already drawn in someone else's hand — my route, my fuel stops, even my preferred altitude through the pass, which was information exactly nobody official should have had. "The survey camp. Government property was abandoned there when the team failed to report. Instruments, records, samples. We need it recovered before the snows make the site inaccessible."

"That's a lot of words for 'we need it picked up.'"

"It's a lot of words because it's an important operation." He folded his hands. "There's also a disposal component. The site may be compromised. Contamination protocols. Anything that can't be flown out gets burned in place. Standard procedure."

"Burning records is standard procedure now."

"Burning *contaminated* records." His voice didn't rise. That was the unnerving part. Most men, caught in a lie, get louder or quieter. Corven did neither. He simply restated the lie with better grammar. "Ashgrove. I'll ask you plainly, because I respect plainness in pilots. What did you see at that camp? On your last supply run. Before they vanished."

And there it was. The odd specificity I'd been chewing on since the day I reported the empty camp and got reassurance instead of alarm. He wasn't asking what happened to the team. He was asking what *I* saw — what evidence existed outside their control, sitting in my memory, possibly in my plane, possibly in my mouth.

"Stoves warm," I said. "Gear left. Boots gone. Tracks ending at the tree line, all heading the same direction, none coming back. Logbooks burned in a fire pit, but the pit was built neat, not panicked. People don't tidy up before they panic."

"Which suggests?"

"Suggests they meant to go. Suggests whoever's looking for them should ask why eleven trained surveyors walked into a mountain range in autumn on purpose."

He considered me for a long moment. Outside the window, the light was doing that October thing, going thin and silver, the whole sky narrowing toward winter. Somewhere below us a truck loaded fuel drums, and the sound came up through the floor like a heartbeat.

"The why isn't your concern," he said finally. "The route is your concern. The recovery is your concern. Fly the plan, burn what needs burning, log nothing you don't have to, and by Thursday you'll be the cleanest pilot north of the capital." He stood, extending his hand across the file, the maps, the whole architecture of my ruin-and-rescue. "Do we have an arrangement?"

Now, here's the thing about me. I've spent fifteen years keeping moving because staying still means staying visible, and staying visible means somebody eventually asks what you ran from. I know the arithmetic of survival down to the decimal. And the arithmetic said: take the deal. Take the deal, fly the mission, burn the camp, and walk away clean into a life where Della's pie costs money instead of forgiveness.

But the arithmetic also said something else, and it had been saying it since a radio transmission named me — *me*, Orsen Ashgrove, a man the lead surveyor had never met — in a dead man's last words. *What we found under the ice.* You don't get named in a dying message unless you're already part of the story. And I had learned, long ago and expensively, that the fastest way to become part of someone else's cover-up is to accept their help getting out of your own trouble.

Corven's hand hung in the air between us, patient as weather.

I thought about Mara — the passenger named Nobody sitting in my hangar right now with survey tags sewn into her pack lining, pretending she couldn't read a topographic map while her calluses said otherwise. I thought about eleven sets of boot prints walking into the trees. I thought about every settlement downstream of that glacier, every cup of water Della had ever poured me.

I took his hand.

"We have an arrangement," I said.

His grip was dry and brief, a handshake like a receipt. "Wheels up Saturday, 0600. Weather window closes Sunday night. Don't be late, Ashgrove. Clean slates expire."

He sat back down and returned to his papers as if I'd been a delivery, which, I suppose, I had been.

* * *

I walked out past the assay shop, past the boy who'd brought the telegram, down the stairs into the street, and stood there a moment letting the cold air argue with the warm sick feeling in my stomach. Then I went to the Blue Spruce and ate the pie. All of it. Della watched me from the counter with the expression of a woman who has fed liars, saints, and pilots, and knows the categories overlap.

"Good news?" she asked.

"Terrible news," I said. "Best pie I've ever eaten."

* * *

The hangar was cold enough that my breath showed when I got there, and she was inside anyway, sitting on an oil drum with her father's field notes open on her knees, pretending she hadn't heard me come in. Mara. Except not Mara — whatever name she was traveling under this week, though she'd stopped bothering to perform it around me somewhere over the second mountain range, which was its own kind of honesty.

"Well?" she said.

"He offered me everything I owe," I told her. "Every debt, every violation, every loose thread in my file. In exchange for flying a suppression run and calling it recovery. Burn the camp. Lose the records. Wheels up Saturday."

"And you said—"

"I shook his hand."

Her face did something complicated — disappointment and calculation fighting for the same real estate — so I held up a finger and crossed to my locker, where I keep the things that matter, which is a very short list. I took out the notebook. My flight logs. Fifteen years of them, every hour, every strip, every load, including a few entries I'd written in a hurry and a few I'd written carefully, knowing someday they might be the only honest witness available.

"Corven keeps my sins in a file," I said. "I keep my truth in these. He thinks he bought a pilot. He rented one. There's a difference, and the difference is Saturday."

Mara looked at the notebooks for a long time. Then at me. "You're going to fly his route."

"I'm going to fly *a* route. His plan has my fuel stops and my altitudes in someone else's handwriting, which means someone's been reading my logs — probably him, probably for months. So he knows how I fly." I tapped the notebook against my palm. "Which means I have to fly the way I don't. Low. Ugly. Through the calving corridor your father marked, the one no sane pilot attempts."

"The one you swore you'd never attempt."

"I swore a lot of things," I said. "Most of them before I understood what they cost." I put the logs back in the locker and locked it, and stood there a moment with my hand flat on the metal, feeling the cold of it, thinking about clean slates and what they're printed on.

Outside, the first flakes of the season came down sideways, small and mean, testing the door seals. Two days early. The mountains were starting their calendar whether Corven's paperwork was ready or not.

"Saturday, then," Mara said quietly.

"Saturday," I agreed. "0600. Bring the notes. And Mara—" I looked at her, and she looked back, and for once neither of us was performing anything. "Whatever's under that ice, we'd both better be ready to carry it. Because after tomorrow, neither of us gets to set it down."

She nodded once, and went back to her father's pages, and I began pulling the cowling off my engine in the falling snow, checking everything twice, hating the price of survival with every wrench turn — and planning, bolt by bolt, how not to pay it.
