## 6. Into the Range Ahead of the Snows

The weather over the Kettle Range had the look of a thing deciding whether to be merciful or not, and I've learned never to bet on mercy.

I'd told Corven I'd fly his recovery run on Thursday. It was Tuesday, and I was already in the air, which meant either I was early or I was a liar, and only one of those kept a man's license intact if it went wrong. The Cessna was loaded wrong for what I'd told the fuel clerk — no burn barrels, no accelerant, just survival gear, rope, and a woman who claimed to be nobody sitting in the right seat with her father's field notes open across her knees like scripture she didn't quite believe in.

"Pass at eleven hundred feet," Mara said. "Then we drop into the valley and stay under the cloud deck."

"The cloud deck is at nine hundred."

"Then stay under *that*."

I liked her. That was going to be a problem eventually, but for now it was useful, because liking someone makes you listen to them, and listening to her father's notes was the only reason we weren't already a smear on a granite face. Halvard Vane had written his survey logs in a code so old-fashioned it was almost charming — bearings disguised as botany, elevations dressed up as bird counts. "Ptarmigan abundant above 2,400 meters" meant the pass was clear to 2,400 meters. "Willow catkins late this season" meant the glacier tongue was still frozen solid, calving hadn't started, the route through the icefall was walkable. A man who spent thirty years writing reports nobody read had apparently decided somebody would read them someday, and read them *carefully*.

"He knew you'd come," I said.

"He knew *someone* would." She turned a page. Her thumb had gone white pressing the paper flat. "He didn't know it would be you. He just knew whoever came would have to be able to fly stupidly low in stupid weather, and there are maybe four people in the territory willing to do that, and three of them work for Corven."

"And the fourth owes money in three towns and has a rule about staying anywhere longer than a night."

"You broke that rule," she said.

"I'm aware."

The cloud came down to meet us the way it always does over the Kettle — not arriving so much as *condensing out of ambition*, gray wool pulled over the summits until the world shrank to a corridor of rock and white water and the occasional terrified pine. I flew by the river. Rivers don't lie about where the low ground is. You follow a river up a valley in weather like this and you live long enough to develop opinions about your life choices.

Mara fed me the notes. "Left fork ahead. He says take the right branch — 'the left fork hosts an ungrateful microclimate.'"

"An ungrateful microclimate."

"That's his way of saying the left valley funnels wind off the ice cap and will fold a wing like wet paper."

"Your father sounds like a delight at dinner parties."

"He never went to dinner parties." Something in her voice closed like a door. I let it stay shut. There's cargo you don't jostle.

We took the right fork. Two minutes later the left fork spat a gust down its throat hard enough that I felt it buffet us even from the next valley over, a shove like a hand flat against the tail, and I put the plane down another fifty feet just because the sky had reminded me it was holding all the cards.

That's the thing about flying into country like this ahead of the snows: fear doesn't leave. It just gets converted. Somewhere around the third hour of threading granite with a ceiling made of wet cotton, the fear stops being a animal in your chest clawing to get out and becomes something more like fuel — cold, clean, burning slow. Your hands stop shaking because shaking wastes attention. Your mouth goes dry and quiet because talking wastes oxygen. You become, briefly, exactly the machine the moment requires, and you understand why some men chase this feeling their whole lives and why most of them die of it.

"Ridge ahead," Mara said. "His note says the saddle is passable at 2,100."

I looked at the ridge. The ridge looked back. The saddle was there, technically, a notch of gray sky between two shoulders of black rock, but the cloud was sitting in it like a hen on an egg, and the wind was combing off the far side in a spindrift plume that said *the air over that notch is moving sideways at speeds we need not discuss.*

"We're not making that saddle," I said.

"The window—"

"The window closes when the snows come, not when the clouds do. We land, we wait, we go when it lifts. Your father's notes say the lake below the ridge freezes flat by now. Does he mention a lake?"

She flipped pages. "Here. 'Good camp on the tarn below the west shoulder. Water sweet. Trout obliging.' That's code for — I think that one's actually just about trout."

"Trout I can work with." I banked along the near shore, looking for the flat white expanse of a frozen tarn, and found it tucked below the shoulder like a coin dropped in a pocket — a mile of lake ice, wind-scoured, blue-white, smooth as poured glass except for a drift line along the eastern shore.

Landing on lake ice is a decision you make once and either get to keep making or don't. I came in over the drift line, flared, and touched down so gently I mostly heard it rather than felt it — a long hiss of skids on ice, the plane settling, the engine idling down into a silence so total it rang.

I sat there for a second with my hands on the yoke, letting my pulse argue itself back down from the ceiling.

"Well," Mara said. "You're showing off."

"I'm decompressing. There's a difference. Show-offs do it on purpose."

* * *

From the outside, I suppose, Orsen Ashgrove looks calm. That's the trick of him — the same trick as the plane, everything rattling internally and presenting a smooth surface to the weather. I watched him sit in the cockpit for a full minute after we stopped, hands still on the controls, breathing like a man doing arithmetic he didn't want anyone to overhear. Then he climbed out, walked twenty feet onto the ice, and stood looking at the ridge as if it owed him an apology.

My father wrote about him once. I found it in the third notebook, buried between a bird count and a complaint about the coffee at the survey camp. *"The supply pilot came today. Ashgrove. Younger than his reputation. Flew out through weather I wouldn't send a dog into, waved at me on takeoff like it was nothing. Either a fool or the best stick in the territory. Asked him which. He said yes."*

I'd read that entry forty times since leaving the coast. I'd built half a person out of it and then checked him against the original standing on the ice in a fur-lined hood, and the original was — close. Close enough that it frightened me a little, how much of a man you can assemble from three sentences written by someone who loved words too much to waste them.

He built a camp the way other people sneeze, without apparent effort or thought. Tent pitched in the lee of the drift line, stove lit, the plane tied down with anchors drilled into the ice and guyed like it might try to escape overnight. He did all of it while the light died, working by feel in the last blue hour, and he never once asked me to help, which I understood — finally, watching him — was not courtesy. It was habit. A man who moves every night learns to do everything alone, because company is just another thing you'd have to pack up.

I helped anyway. He pretended not to notice that I noticed.

Over the stove he melted snow for tea and asked, without preamble, "How long does the window stay open? Honestly. Not the note version."

"Calving usually starts within days of the first real thaw-wind off the cap. Once the tongue starts shedding, the icefall route closes — sometimes for hours, sometimes for the season. My father's last entry says the meltwater was singing under the ice two weeks ago. That means we're late. Days late, maybe."

"Late," he repeated, in the tone of a man filing something away to be afraid of later, at a scheduled time.

"There's a marker. When the glacier calves the first big piece, the sound carries for miles. Everyone at the camp will hear it. They'll know the clock started."

"And Corven's people?"

"They'll hear it too."

He looked at me across the stove flame. In the firelight his face lost the pilot-mask quality and became just a face, tired, thirty-something, with a scar through one eyebrow I hadn't seen before because he keeps the light arranged to hide things. Everyone I've met since my father disappeared has been performing some version of themselves for me. Corven performed reassurance. The officials performed concern. Orsen, I was beginning to understand, performed competence — and underneath it was running the same calculation I was, the one where you add up what you know and subtract what you can survive knowing.

"Why did he name you?" I asked. "In the transmission. He'd never met you before that summer. Why would his last message name a stranger?"

Orsen fed a stick of wood to the stove and took his time answering, which I was learning was his way of telling the truth — slowly, so it couldn't bite on the way out.

"Because I carried his mail," he said. "Every run, I brought the team their supplies and I carried their samples out — sealed cases, chain-of-custody tags, the whole ritual. And every run, your father handed me the cases personally and watched me load them, and one day he said, 'If anything happens to us, these cases matter more than we do,' and I laughed, because surveyors say dramatic things to break up the monotony." He turned the stick in the flame. "Except he wasn't being dramatic. He was handing me the evidence one case at a time and asking me to remember that I'd done it. The transmission named me because I'm the receipt. I'm proof the samples existed and left that camp in plain view, week after week, signed for. Corven doesn't want the team found, Mara. He wants the *chain broken*. And I'm the strongest link he knows about."

The stove ticked. Outside, the wind moved along the ridge and something shifted in the ice beneath us — a groan, deep and structural, the lake settling or the glacier talking in its sleep two valleys over.

Orsen's head came up. So did mine. We listened together, two people hearing the same sound and understanding it differently — him hearing a threat, me hearing a voice.

"That wasn't the lake," I said.

"No," he said quietly. "It wasn't."

Far to the north, muffled by distance and stone, the glacier cleared its throat again — a long, grinding report, like a door opening somewhere under the world.

The calving had begun.
