## 7. What We Found Under the Ice

My father taught me to read a glacier the way other fathers teach their daughters to read scripture: with reverence, and with the understanding that most of what it says is a warning.

So when the ice under our boots began to sing — that thin, high note like a wet finger on a glass rim — I stopped, and Orsen stopped with me, because three days on the icefall corridor had taught him to trust my silences more than his own instincts.

"Calving?" he asked.

"Settling." I crouched and pressed my palm flat against the surface. Cold bit through my glove. "It's moving. Slowly. It's been moving for years."

"Glaciers do that."

"Not this one. Not this fast." I stood and looked up-valley, toward the white shoulder where the ice bent around a granite spur my father had marked in his field notes as *the elbow*. "Something upstream is feeding it heat."

Orsen said nothing, but I felt him doing arithmetic behind me — the same arithmetic I'd been doing since we found the first dead lichen band, bleached gray along the moraine where nothing should have died. Lichen doesn't lie. It grows at the speed of patience and dies at the speed of poison.

We walked on. The wind came down the valley in long, even breaths, carrying the smell of stone and old snow and, underneath it, faint as a rumor, something chemical and wrong. I'd been smelling it for two days and telling myself it was the fuel cans in Orsen's pack. He'd been smelling it too. I could tell by the way he kept checking the caps.

* * *

The camp appeared the way my father had always said real things appear in the mountains: all at once, after you'd already walked past the place you thought they should be.

It wasn't hidden so much as *humbled*. Three tents pitched low against the wind in the lee of the spur, canvas grayed to match the rock, guy-lines strung with strips of cloth so the whole thing read from the air as scree and shadow. A cook stove's pipe vented through a cairn of stones. Whoever built it had spent their life being looked for and had gotten very good at not being found.

Orsen's hand found my shoulder. "Mara."

"I see them."

Two figures at the tent line, standing very still, watching us come. One raised binoculars. Even at four hundred meters I could see the moment the glass lowered and the figure went rigid — recognition traveling down a body like current down a wire.

"That's Voss," I said. "The tall one. He was my father's second."

"You're sure?"

"He carried me on his shoulders at a survey picnic when I was nine. He complained about it for a decade." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Yes. I'm sure."

We closed the distance slowly, hands visible, Orsen calling out names from the team roster he'd memorized off the supply manifests — Voss, Adeyemi, Brandt, the Kettering twins — the way you'd call out to a dog you didn't want spooked. It was Voss who broke first. He dropped the binoculars in the snow and came down the slope at a half-run, half-stagger, and when he reached me he didn't embrace me so much as grip my arms and look at my face like a man checking a ledger against a memory.

"Little Blackwood," he said. "God help us. You look like her."

"Like who?"

"Your mother." His eyes went past me to Orsen, and everything soft in his face shut like a drawer. "You brought a pilot."

"I brought the pilot my father named."

That landed. I watched it land — watched Voss glance up at the empty sky, then back, quick and guilty, and I understood that he knew exactly what a named man meant, because they'd done the naming.

They took us into the largest tent, and there were six of them, not five — six faces hollowed and wind-scoured, six pairs of hands wrapped around tin mugs of melted snow, and every one of them looking at me the way people look at a door they're afraid is about to be knocked on. Adeyemi was thinner than her personnel file photo. Brandt had a burn scar down one forearm he hadn't had in the team photograph. The Ketterings sat hip to hip like bookends, finishing each other's silence.

"My father," I said. "Where—"

"Alive." Voss said it fast, too fast, the way you say things you've rehearsed. "He's below. Resting. He'll want to see you himself."

Everything in that sentence was true except its shape. I let it go. There would be time to take it apart later, and I wanted — God, how I wanted — to see him first and ask questions after.

But Orsen, bless his suspicious, sleepless heart, was already looking around the tent with a surveyor's eye he probably didn't know he had. At the core boxes stacked along the wall, sealed and numbered. At the shortwave radio, disassembled into pieces no larger than a fist, scattered across a sleeping bag like the bones of a small animal. At the rifle leaning against the tent pole, pointed not outward, toward the valley mouth where any threat should come from —

but inward. Toward the ice.

"You're not hiding from searchers," he said quietly.

No one answered.

"You're hiding from your own relief crew." He turned slowly. "Corven sent people. Didn't he? After the camp went quiet. And instead of coming home, you ran up here and buried yourselves."

Voss set down his mug. It took him a long time to do it, and I realized his hands were shaking, and I realized I'd never seen a grown man's hands shake like that outside of hospitals.

"The official story," he said, "is that we were evacuated. That the weather turned and Corven pulled us out before the passes closed. Clean extraction, no casualties, file closed." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "There were four men in the extraction party. They came up the valley in February with orders to bring us down — our logs, our samples, ourselves — and put it all in a room with a stove and a can of fuel oil. We heard them coming two days out. My father—" He stopped. Corrected himself. "*Elias.* Your father. He made the call. We burned our own lower camp ourselves, walked our own tracks into the trees, and came up here. Let them find an empty tent and a cold stove and draw whatever conclusion paid their salaries."

"The footprints ending at the tree line," Orsen said. "I saw that. I reported it."

"Yes," Voss said. "And Corven asked you very carefully what you saw, didn't he. Specific questions. What gear was left. Which direction the tracks ran. Whether anything was burned." He smiled without warmth. "He wasn't investigating, pilot. He was auditing his own cover story. You weren't a witness. You were a proofread."

I felt Orsen go still beside me, the particular stillness of a man re-reading his own life in a new light. I knew that stillness. I'd been living inside it for months.

"Show me," I said. "Show me what he died walking toward."

"He didn't die," Voss said. "He's below. I told you—"

"*Show me.*"

* * *

They took us down onto the ice itself, through a crevasse field crossed on aluminum ladders laid flat like rungs of a fallen ladder to heaven, to a place where the glacier bent around the spur and opened into a blue grotto the size of a chapel. And there, in the blue light, my father stood over a drilling rig with his back to us, arguing with a core sample.

He turned when he heard us, and twenty years fell off his face and then ten more fell back on, and he said my name — my *real* name, the one I hadn't answered to since the funeral — and I crossed the ice and hit him hard enough to knock us both into the rig, and neither of us cared.

When I could speak again, he held me at arm's length and looked at Orsen over my shoulder.

"So you're Ashgrove."

"Sir."

"Elias Blackwood." He shook Orsen's hand with both of his. "You've been flying supplies to my murder for eight months. I'm sorry about the radio call. I knew they'd bury it otherwise. A name gets through where data doesn't."

"What did you find?" Orsen asked. No preamble. I loved him a little for that.

My father led us to the core rack. Six cylinders of ice, each as long as my arm, each banded and labeled in his cramped hand. He lifted one to the light, and deep in the blue clarity ran a smear of gray, like smoke frozen mid-sigh.

"Upstream," he said. "Above every settlement this man has ever flown medicine to. Above the Kettle River towns, above the valley farms, above the school at Harrow Bend. Tailings from the old federal works — the ones that were 'remediated' twenty years ago. They capped it, they photographed it, they gave it a plaque." He set the core down gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping child. "The cap failed. It's been leaching into the headwaters for eleven years. Every town downstream has been drinking it. Slowly. Quietly. At concentrations calibrated to stay just under the reporting thresholds."

"Corven knows," I said.

"Corven *wrote the thresholds*." My father's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about it; it never rose. "Twenty years ago he was the junior man on the remediation project. He signed the cap certificate. Everything since has been him keeping his signature clean. Our survey was routine hydrology until the cores started reading hot. Then we became a problem to be managed." He looked around the grotto, at his hollow-eyed team, at the ladder-bridged crevasses, at the rifle pointed inward. "We didn't vanish, Mara. We *defected*. From our own government, into the only territory it's afraid of. The wilderness keeps no files."

Orsen stood a long moment, looking at the cores. I watched him do the arithmetic I'd already done — every medicine run, every school, every farm, every debt in three towns. The water those people drank. The water he'd been drinking, all these years, flying his careful loops over the source of it.

"The calving window," he said finally. "How long?"

"Four days," my father said. "Maybe five if the wind holds from the north."

"And Corven knows about the window too."

"He wrote the seasonal report," my father said. "Of course he does."

Orsen nodded slowly, and I saw something settle in him — not peace, nothing like peace, but the terrible calm of a man who has stopped weighing options because one of them has begun to weigh him.

"There are six of you," he said. "One plane. Weather closing from the west. And a recovery team that will come up this valley the moment Corven decides we've stayed too long." He looked at the core rack. "I can't carry six people and the evidence."

"No," my father agreed. "You can't."

Down the valley, far off, so faint it might have been wind, might have been imagination, came a sound none of us mistook for either.

An engine.

Orsen's head came up like a bird dog's. "That's not a bush plane," he said. "Wrong pitch. Twin engine, high altitude trim." He looked at my father, and his face had gone the color of the ice. "That's a government airframe. That's *him*."
