## 8. The Window Closes

The engines woke me before the sound did — that particular harmonic of a twin-engine transport running heavy, the kind of note you only hear from aircraft that aren't yours and aren't welcome. I'd been sleeping in the surveyors' cook tent with my boots on, which tells you how much faith I had in the nights up there. I rolled out of the bag, crossed to the tent flap, and looked south down the glacier's throat at two specks descending out of a sky the color of dishwater.

Corven had come by air. Of course he had. Men like Corven don't climb.

"Orsen." Mara was already beside me, and I hadn't heard her move either. She'd gotten good at that in the week we'd been up here — moving quiet, like someone relearning how to be unremarkable. Her father's field notes were wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. "Two aircraft. The big one's a supply hauler. The other one—"

"I know the other one," I said. It was a government utility ship, the kind they issue to men whose paperwork is always in order. "That's his."

Below us, the camp that wasn't a camp anymore stirred. Seven surveyors who had spent eleven days pretending to be dead came out into the grey light carrying rifles they'd taken from their own minders' cache, and there was no panic in it. That was the thing that kept catching me sideways: no panic. They'd rehearsed this. They'd rehearsed being found.

Her father, Aldous Vane — because that was his name, not the name on any manifest I'd ever signed — stood at the ice edge with binoculars and the calm of a man reading a weather report about his own funeral. He lowered them after a long moment.

"They'll land at the lower shelf," he said. "Twenty minutes, maybe less if Corven's pilot is as good as I think he is."

"He is," I said. "I've watched him fly. He flies like a man who's never once wondered what would happen if the engine quit."

"Then he's never really flown," Vane said, and handed me the binoculars, and that small gesture — handing me the glass, trusting my eyes — did something to my chest I didn't have time to examine.

* * *

We worked fast after that, and here is what I learned about the truth: it weighs almost nothing individually and everything collectively. The core samples were sealed in steel tubes no longer than my forearm, each one labeled in Vane's cramped hand — depth, date, grid reference, and one word repeated down the length of every log: *upstream*. Upstream of Halloway's Crossing. Upstream of the mission hospital I'd been resupplying for six years. Upstream of every town that kept me in fuel and debts and gossip, all of them drinking, season after season, from water that a report buried under three signatures said was fine.

The full set was too heavy for my plane with the weather coming in behind it. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it for an hour, which is how mountain people handle arithmetic they don't like.

It was Mara who broke first, or rather it was Mara who stopped breaking. She spread her father's notes across a crate and said, "We split them. Duplicates go out with different carriers. The locals take sets down the valley — Tomas can walk the eastern route, the Reyes family has the mules for the western pass. If some of it doesn't arrive, some of it arrives."

"And the master cores?" one of the surveyors asked. A geologist named Okonkwo, who had hands like a piano teacher and had spent the last eleven days living in a hole in the ice out of principle.

Everyone looked at the master cores. Then everyone looked at my plane, sitting on its skis at the shelf edge with fresh frost feathering the struts.

"No," I said, before anyone could ask me anything. "No, listen. You want me to fly the masters out through—" I gestured at the southern horizon, where the cloud deck had begun its slow, grinding advance off the divide, the front that every pilot in this range knows means the calving window is closing and the glacier is about to spend a week trying to shrug off its own shoulder. "Through that? In calving season? Alone?"

"Yes," said Vane.

"It's suicide with extra steps."

"It's the only route where Corven can't follow," Mara said quietly. "He won't risk his own aircraft in the icefall corridor. He thinks nobody will. He told you that, didn't he? When he offered you the deal."

She had me there. Corven, on the radio strip below Halloway's Crossing, with his soft voice and his folder of my sins — the lapsed medical cert, the fuel debts, the cargo runs I'd made for people whose names went in no logbook — had laid it out like a menu. Fly the recovery, burn the camp, lose the records, and your license troubles evaporate. And when I'd asked what route he wanted me to fly out, he'd smiled and said the corridor was closed this time of year anyway, so I'd take the long way around, the safe way, *his* way.

He'd told me the one route he feared, thinking that was a warning. It was a map.

* * *

They scattered the duplicates through the afternoon while the light died. I watched Tomas Reyes take his sealed tubes and disappear up the eastern scree with the unhurried gait of a man who has been smuggling things past governments since before either of us was born. The mule train went west at dusk. By full dark, the truth about the water was distributed across half the range in pieces small enough to hide in a flour sack, and there was nothing Corven's recovery team — now camped smugly on the lower shelf, their floodlights carving idiot cones into the ice — could do about any of it except fail to find it.

Which left the masters. Fourteen tubes. Eleven kilos. The whole weight of it fit in a duffel bag, and that fact offended me more than anything else about the affair.

Mara walked me to the plane. The wind had come around out of the south, wet and wrong, and somewhere down-valley the glacier made a sound like a cathedral clearing its throat.

"You don't have to do this alone," she said. "Take me. I'm lighter than the fuel margin you're worried about."

"You're your father's only heir and Corven's second target, and I am not explaining to him why his daughter fell out of the sky in the icefall corridor." I cinched the duffel into the cargo net behind my seat, checked it twice, checked it a third time because checking things is the only prayer I know. "Besides. Somebody has to stay here and be alive when the duplicates start arriving in town. Somebody they believe. Corven will spin whatever I say — crashed, stolen, mad. But a surveyor's daughter walking out of the range with her father's notes? That's harder to bury."

"He named you," she said. "In the last transmission. Why do you think he did that?"

"Because he needed a pilot stupid enough to answer."

"Because he read your file," she said. "All of it. Not just the violations. He knew what you did before you came out here, Orsen. He knew why you don't stay anywhere longer than a night. And he decided you were the man who'd carry it, because a man running from something will finally stop running for something bigger."

I didn't have an answer for that. I got in the plane instead.

* * *

Pre-flight in the dark, by flashlight, with the wind moaning through the tie-downs and Vane standing at my wingtip like a lighthouse that had given up on ships. He leaned in over the cowling when I finished.

"The corridor opens at first light for maybe six hours," he said. "After that the calving starts in earnest and the route closes for the season. Corven knows the schedule too. He'll expect you to wait for the safe window behind the front."

"So I leave before it."

"So you leave *now*, in the dark, and hit the corridor entrance at dawn exactly when he thinks you're still on the ground deciding." He put his hand flat on the fuselage, twice, the way you'd steady a horse. "Eleven days ago I ordered my team to vanish rather than hand those samples to the men downstairs. Every one of them could have walked away clean. None of them did. I've stopped asking people to be sensible, Ashgrove. I only ask them to be certain."

"I'm not certain," I said. "I'm angry. It looks similar from a distance."

"From a distance, yes." And he stepped back, and raised his hand, and that was the whole ceremony.

I ran up the engine and taxied onto the shelf with the floodlights of Corven's camp glaring at me from below like a stage crew that hadn't been told the show moved. As I turned into the wind, I saw movement down there — figures spilling out of tents, a radio mast swinging, somebody running for the utility ship. Corven had woken up. Of course he had. Somewhere in the last hour one of his minders had counted sample cases and come up short, or Mara had let herself be seen at the ridge, or the universe had simply decided I'd had enough head start.

The tower frequency crackled in my headset, and then Corven's voice, smooth as poured cream even through static:

"Ashgrove. Whatever you're carrying, it stays in the range. Turn back and we'll discuss your future like reasonable men. Continue, and I want you to understand — there is no route out of these mountains that I don't own."

I keyed the mic. Six years of keeping my head down, of gossip as cargo and debts in three towns and never sleeping twice in the same bed, and it all funneled down to the eight seconds I had before rotation.

"That's the difference between us, Corven," I said. "You own routes. I only need one."

I pushed the throttle through the gate and the shelf fell away beneath me, and ahead, black against the last of the starlight, the icefall corridor waited — the one route in this range I had sworn, in another life, in another cockpit, that I would never fly.

Dawn was forty minutes out. The window was open.
