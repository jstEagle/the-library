## 9. Calving

There's a rule every bush pilot learns before his first solo, and it's not about fuel reserves or weather minimums, though those matter. It's this: the mountain doesn't hate you. The mountain doesn't notice you. Everything that goes wrong up here is arithmetic, and arithmetic doesn't hold grudges.

I repeated that to myself like a prayer while I ran up the engine on the glacier shelf, the master cores lashed into the cargo bay behind me like a coffin I'd volunteered to carry. Below the ice lip, the calving season was doing what calving season does — the glacier shedding a hundred tons of itself into the melt lake with a sound like God clearing his throat. The whole shelf shivered under my wheels. The ice didn't hate me. But it was definitely keeping score.

"Corven's plane is airborne," Mara had said twenty minutes earlier, standing in the wind at the surveyors' camp with her father's field notes flapping in her fist. "Ten minutes behind you, maybe less. He'll take the main corridor."

"Then I won't take the main corridor."

She'd looked at me the way you look at a man who's just suggested swimming the river in April. "The icefall? Orsen, you told me yourself—"

"I know what I told myself." I'd told her, actually, on the frozen lake, in the dark, in the confessional way pilots talk when they think they might die before morning. *I don't fly the icefall. Nobody flies the icefall. The corridor's a slot canyon made of moving glass, and the men who've tried it are all named on memorials.* "But Corven knows the main route like his own signature. He doesn't know I know the icefall exists, because I told everyone who'd listen that I'd never fly it." I'd shrugged. "Turns out a reputation for cowardice is an asset. First one I've ever owned."

Now, engine screaming, tail heavy with sample cores worth more than every debt I owed in three towns, I lifted off the shelf just as the glacier's face let go.

I heard it before I saw it — a crack like the sky splitting along a seam — and then the whole calving wall came down in a white avalanche of house-sized blocks, and the blast of displaced air caught my tail and shoved me sideways like a drunk uncle at a wedding. I fought the stick, wings wallowing, and for three full seconds I was not flying so much as falling with opinions. Then the air sorted itself out, the prop bit clean sky, and I was up. Climbing. Alive.

Behind me, the melt lake was a churned soup of brash ice, and the shelf I'd just left was a memory.

*Arithmetic,* I told myself. *No grudges.*

* * *

The main corridor ran east — wide, forgiving, the route Corven would expect me to fly, the route that led past the government strip where his men would be waiting with handcuffs and a very reasonable explanation about licensing violations. The icefall ran north. The icefall ran through the heart of the range like a wound that hadn't decided to heal: a two-mile slot of seracs and crevasses where the glacier moved fast enough to rearrange itself daily, where the light came down blue and green through walls of ice taller than my hangar, where the turbulence was less weather than geology having an argument with itself.

I'd sworn off it six years ago, after a man named Teodor Hale followed me into it on a dare and didn't come out. I'd never told anyone that part. The icefall was just a thing I didn't do, like drinking before noon or staying in one town longer than a night.

Today it was the only road out.

I brought the plane down to the slot entrance at barely above stall speed, because the entrance was maybe forty meters wider than my wingspan and the wind through it ran downhill at a speed I chose not to measure. The cores shifted behind me — a soft, heavy thud, like a body turning over in sleep. I'd packed them in the surveyors' spare clothing, strapped them to the frame, wrapped the whole bundle in the tent fly. If Corven caught me, I wanted the evidence at least to look boring.

The slot swallowed me.

Flying the icefall is not flying, exactly. It's threading. The walls rose on both sides, blue-green and dripping, close enough that I could count the frozen bubbles in the ice, and the light down there was submarine, dreamlike, the prop wash echoing off the walls like I was flying inside a cathedral that hated me. The glacier groaned around me — long, tectonic complaints, the sound of a mile of ice deciding where to go next. My hands were steady on the stick. My mouth had gone dry as old canvas.

*You swore you'd never do this.*

*Teodor swore too,* I answered myself. *Look how much good swearing did.*

The first serac came at me without warning — a leaning tower of ice on the left wall, calving as I passed, the fall of it filling half the corridor with white thunder. I banked hard right, wingtip maybe three meters from the wall, close enough to see my own reflection in the ice, distorted and pale, a man-shaped smear in ten-thousand-year-old frozen water. The debris fell behind me. I didn't look. Looking is how you die in a slot.

Then the turbulence found me, and it was nothing like sky turbulence. This was the glacier breathing — updrafts off the cold ice, downdrafts off the sun-warmed serac tops, the whole corridor churning like the inside of a shaken bottle. The plane bucked. Something in the tail let out a crack I felt through the airframe and into my teeth. The right aileron cable — I'd know that sound anywhere, I'd replaced it twice — went slack in my grip by half its travel.

I had maybe sixty percent roll authority and a corridor of moving glass and a government plane somewhere behind me, and I did the only sensible thing.

I laughed. Out loud, alone, at stall speed in an ice canyon. Because the alternative was thinking, and thinking was a luxury for men with ailerons.

* * *

Corven's plane found me at the icefall's mouth.

I'd come out the north end into open sky, gray and snowing, the range falling away below toward the long valley that led — eventually, if the weather held, if the plane held, if my nerve held — to Merrow's Field, the little strip outside the valley town where the surveyors had scattered their copies and where the witnesses lived: the schoolteacher, the storekeeper, old Halvard with his radio set and his grudge against every official since the war. Witnesses. That was the whole plan. Not heroics. Just an audience.

Corven came out of the cloud behind me like a debt collector, his twin-engine bird big and black and steady, and I felt his presence the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck. He keyed his radio, and his voice came through the crackle, smooth as ever, the voice of a man who'd never once raised it in anger.

"Ashgrove. Turn back. You're carrying stolen government property through restricted airspace in an unairworthy aircraft. Whatever they promised you, they can't protect you. I can. Turn back, and we'll call this a misunderstanding."

I keyed mine. "Corven. You ever crash-land a plane on purpose?"

A pause. The smoothness flickered. "What?"

"Because I'm about to, and I wanted to know if you'd done it, so you'd understand what happens next." I let that sit. "You can't shoot me down over a valley full of people, and you can't outfly me low, because you fly like a man reading a manual. So here's what happens next: I land short of Merrow's Field, in the flats, in front of everyone, and the cores go into the schoolhouse before your wheels stop rolling. And your flight logs — the ones you've been holding over me for six years — go into the same story."

"Ashgrove—"

"Tell them at the hearing I said hello."

I cut the radio and put the nose down.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about crashing on purpose: it's the calmest flying you will ever do. Every decision is made. Every variable is chosen. There is no weather to second-guess, no mountain to respect, no future to worry about beyond the next thirty seconds. Terror, I discovered, is just indecision with a deadline, and I had made every decision I was ever going to make.

I came in over the valley low and slow, the damaged aileron fighting me on every correction, snow streaking the windshield sideways. The flats below Merrow's Field were white and flat as a fresh page — a bog in summer, a landing zone in winter, and today a stage. I could see the town: the schoolhouse, the store, the antenna mast with Halvard's wire strung from it like a flag. I could see people coming out of buildings to watch the plane that was coming in far too low, far too slow, with black smoke beginning to feather off the engine — because I'd leaned it out on purpose, a rich man's bonfire, something to make the story worth telling.

Corven's twin circled high, impotent, a wolf at the edge of a village with dogs.

The engine coughed. I'd timed the fuel starvation to the second, and it cut exactly where I wanted it, and the silence that followed was enormous. Just wind, and the groan of the airframe, and the flats coming up gentle as a bed being turned down.

I picked my spot — soft snow, short of the strip, in plain view of the schoolhouse windows — and flared with what aileron I had left.

The world went white, then loud, then quiet. The plane plowed a furrow through the drifts and stopped with a shudder and a hiss of snow against hot metal, nose down, tail up, like a bird that had finally decided to sit.

I sat there a moment, listening. The engine ticked as it cooled. Snow hissed. Somewhere behind me, distant but closing, an engine with government money in it was turning for a landing it would be too late to matter.

I unstrapped, turned around, and put my hand flat on the bundle of cores, wrapped in a dead surveyor's tent fly, colder than the ice they'd been drilled from.

"You're going to school," I told them.

Then I kicked the door open into the snow, hauled the bundle out onto my shoulder, and started walking toward the lights of the town — where Halvard was already keying his radio, and the schoolteacher was already coming down the hill through the snow, and where, for the first time in my life, every single person in the valley was about to know my name.
