## 10. The Long Taxi Home

### Orsen

The wreck sat where I'd left it, half-buried in the shallows of a farmer's field forty miles short of the strip, looking less like an aircraft than like something the mountain had spat out and immediately regretted. The left gear was folded under the fuselage. The propeller had stopped being a propeller somewhere over the icefall and become modern art instead. A boy on a bicycle found me sitting on the wing with the core samples stacked beside me like passengers waiting for a connection that would never come.

"You crash?" he asked.

"Deliberately," I said.

He nodded with the gravity of a man who heard that sort of thing every day, and pedaled off to tell everyone, which was the point. Witnesses. That had been the whole flight plan: get the truth down out of the clouds and into reach of people Corven couldn't make disappear. I'd just been the delivery mechanism. The plane was the postage.

I stayed with the wreck that first night because leaving it felt like leaving a body. By morning there were eleven trucks in the field and a woman named Hesta Voss — who ran the co-op in Lowmark and had never once paid me on time — pressing a thermos of coffee into my hands and saying, "About time you landed somewhere."

I didn't have an answer for that. I still don't, mostly.

* * *

The cores went to a lab three hundred miles east, carried by truck, carried by strangers, carried by anyone but me, because anything I touched could be tied back to me and Corven knew my name better than I did by then. For two weeks nothing happened, which is its own kind of weather. Then the story broke the way stories break out here: not all at once but in pieces, a leak here, a hearing there, a minister resigning on a Friday when he thought nobody was watching the news.

Corven held on longer than I expected. He was good at holding on; it was practically his profession. What undid him wasn't the contamination data, oddly enough. It was paper. My paper.

You see, when Corven had offered to forgive my licensing violations — the overweight manifests, the night flights without clearance, the fuel debts logged across three towns like a trail of breadcrumbs leading directly to my bad decisions — he hadn't offered forgiveness at all. He'd offered leverage. He'd kept copies. Every irregularity in my flying record lived in a folder in his office, and he'd shown me that folder once, casually, the way a man shows you a gun he doesn't intend to use yet.

What he hadn't counted on was that folders can be subpoenaed. When the inquiry came for him, they went looking for everything he'd ever used to lean on anybody, and my flight logs were in there alongside a dozen other pilots', surveyors', clerks'. Every quiet favor he'd ever bought or blackmailed, documented in his own tidy hand. He'd built his whole career on keeping records of other men's sins, and it turned out a record-keeper is just a man who's already written his own indictment.

They read some of it aloud in the hearing. I listened on a borrowed radio in Hesta's kitchen. When the clerk read out the entry from the night I flew medicine into Kettle Creek at two in the morning with no clearance and a tailwind I shouldn't have trusted, Corven's voice came through the static, calm as ever, explaining that the pilot in question had been "a known risk." And I sat there in a stranger's kitchen, unemployed, unlicensed, and publicly a known risk, and laughed until Mara came in from the porch to check on me.

"That's the sound of a man getting free," she said.

"It's the sound of a man whose entire personality just got revoked," I said.

She sat down across from me and didn't argue, which was worse.

* * *

### Mara

My father came down off the glacier in October, thinner, grayer, walking like a man who'd spent a summer arguing with stone. The other surveyors came with him, one by one, filtering into the valley towns the way water finds low ground. They testified. They handed over their duplicates. My father shook hands with officials who'd once signed orders to find him, and he did it without smiling, which I think took more discipline than the whole expedition.

People ask me sometimes what it was like, those months hiding in plain sight, traveling under a name that wasn't mine, sewing equipment tags into pack linings so that if I vanished too, someone might eventually do the arithmetic. I tell them the truth, which is that it was like being a ghost with excellent bookkeeping.

What I don't usually say is what Orsen's hangar looked like the day after the hearings ended. He stood in the middle of it with the wrecked plane's wing propped on sawhorses, and he looked around at the tools, the oil stains, the cot in the corner where he'd slept exactly zero nights in nine years of knowing this valley existed — and I watched him realize he had nowhere else to be. Not nowhere in the sad way. Nowhere in the way of a man whose debts are paid and whose ghosts are all accounted for.

"I could rebuild her," he said, patting the fuselage. "Takes maybe a year. Longer, without a license."

"You don't need a license to fix things," I said. "Only to leave."

He was quiet a long moment. "That's either very wise or very cruel."

"With you it has to be both," I said, and handed him a wrench.

* * *

### Orsen

Here is what nobody tells you about losing your license: the sky doesn't close. It just stops being yours. Other planes still cross the valley — the mail run, the medevac out of Lowmark, a bright yellow trainer that flies circuits on Sundays like a dog chasing its own tail. I watch them the way a man watches weather coming in over a range he used to know by heart. It doesn't hurt, exactly. It aches in the specific way of a language you can still read but are no longer permitted to speak.

So I fixed things instead. The wreck first, because she deserved better than the field, and then everything else that rolled, floated, or sulked into the hangar. Hesta Voss's truck. The co-op's generator. A church bell that had gone tone-deaf sometime in the previous decade. Word got around, the way word does, and by spring I was the busiest unemployed man in the valley. Turns out a town full of people who've watched you carry their medicine through weather that would ground angels will forgive you almost anything, including honesty.

Mara stayed. She never announced it; she just kept not leaving, which is how most real decisions look from the inside. She took over the books, because someone had to, and because she was — as established — a ghost with excellent bookkeeping. She catalogued the parts, argued with suppliers, and developed a running feud with the mail pilot over landing fees that I suspect both parties privately treasure.

Some evenings we'd work late, and she'd read me the newspaper coverage of the inquiry, and I'd hold a part up to the light and pretend I wasn't listening. The contamination site was being remediated. Upstream settlements were getting filtration. A lake that had been quietly poisoning three towns for twenty years was finally, officially, a problem somebody had to solve. All of it purchased with a summer's worth of fear and one deliberately crashed airplane.

"Worth it?" she asked me once, not looking up from the ledger.

I thought about it honestly, which is more than I gave most questions that year. "I lost the only thing I was any good at."

"You're fixing a church bell."

"I'm avoiding the question with a church bell."

She set down the pen. "Orsen. You spent nine years making sure no place could miss you. Now there's a town that argues about your landing fees and a woman who knows your real name and a plane in this hangar that will fly again whether or not you ever sit in the left seat. You didn't lose your escape route. You lost your *need* for one. Those aren't the same thing, whatever you tell yourself at two in the morning."

I picked up the bell part again. Held it to the light. Put it down.

"When did you get wise?" I said.

"Somewhere over the icefall," she said. "It was very inconvenient."

* * *

### Mara

The plane flew again in June, fourteen months after the field. Orsen taxied her the length of the grass strip with the town watching from the fence line — Hesta, the mail pilot, the boy on the now much larger bicycle, half the congregation of the newly tuneful church. He brought her around at the far end and shut her down, and climbed out, and that was the whole ceremony. A long taxi home with no destination beyond itself.

"He's not going up?" someone asked me.

"Not today," I said.

"Ever?"

I watched Orsen walk the length of the wing, checking the fabric, running his thumb along a rivet line the way other men worry a rosary. "When there's a reason," I said. "He was never really a pilot. Pilots are just what he called himself while he was running."

That evening he pulled two chairs out onto the hangar apron, which was as close to a porch as the man owned, and we sat while the light went amber over the ridge. Below us the valley did its evening business: dogs, supper smoke, the co-op closing late because Hesta never met a customer she'd turn away.

"There's a supply contract," he said, out of nowhere. "Lowmark to the upper settlements. Ground convoy, mostly. They need someone to organize it. Someone who knows the routes and the weather and which bridges lie about themselves."

"Are you going to take it?"

"I already did." He turned his chair toward me. "It means staying. Years, probably. It means being findable."

"I noticed," I said. "I've been finding you for months."

He laughed — the real one, the one that had come back sometime over the winter along with everything else. Then he went quiet, and I let him, because I'd learned that his silences weren't empty. They were full. They were just full of things that took him a while to carry up.

Finally he said, "There's something I haven't told you. About the last transmission. Your father's last transmission."

"My father made dozens—"

"The final one. After the extraction went wrong. After the minders pulled out. There was a coda, thirty seconds of static and then his voice, and I've never played it for anyone because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because it wasn't for the authorities. It was for me. And I only understood that after I met you."

The evening had gone cool. Somewhere down-valley the church bell rang six, clear and true, in a voice it hadn't had in years.

"What did he say?" I asked.

Orsen reached into his jacket and took out an old cassette, its label written in my father's careful surveyor's hand, and held it between us without letting go.

"He said, 'If you're hearing this, Ashgrove, then you're the one who came back for the rest of it. Don't fly it out. Walk it out. Some truths land harder than they take off.'" He looked at me. "And then he said your name. Your real one. And he said, 'Tell her the coordinates were never the point.'"

The tape sat in his palm, turning slowly in the last of the light, and I understood that my father — methodical, stubborn, a man who labeled everything — had left one final sample in the field, and it was addressed to us.

"Do you want to hear it?" Orsen asked.

The mountains stood dark against a sky still holding its color. The plane ticked softly as it cooled, the way living things do.

"No," I said, and put my hand over his, over the tape. "Not tonight. Tonight I want to stay right here."

And for the first time in either of our lives, staying was the easy part.
