## 1. The Weight of Closed Lines

The spur line died six years ago, but the jungle had been killing it long before that.

Theo Sandoval—Tomas Reyes, on the papers sewn into his shirt collar, Tomas Reyes in the mouths of every laborer within forty miles—stood at the edge of the old right-of-way and watched the morning steam rise off the cut grass like something breathing. The rails ran west from the loading yard at Ambunti toward the interior, twin lines of rust disappearing into green so dense it looked less like vegetation than a decision the world had made. In places the sleepers had rotted to black sponge. In others, strangler figs had taken the embankment whole, their roots pouring over the ballast like wax down a candle.

She had come to measure it. That was the work now: surveying dead things, certifying their deaths, putting numbers on decay so that men in offices far away could file the numbers and forget them. It was honest work in the way that burying is honest work. Nobody asked questions about a man who measured ruins.

She knelt at the first chainage marker and set her level against the cracked concrete. The instrument was German, older than she was, its brass worn to the color of old honey where thumbs had adjusted it across three continents before hers. Through the lens, the crosshairs settled on the stadia rod her assistant held two hundred meters down the grade—a boy called Wem, sixteen maybe, with a scarred lip and the patient stillness of someone who had learned early that stillness was safer than speech.

"Chainage four-one-two," she said, and wrote it in the survey book with a pencil stub sharpened with a machete. "Elevation drop, point-three meters. Drainage channel's taken the east shoulder."

Wem nodded and moved the rod. He didn't ask why anyone would bother surveying a line no train would ever run again. He was the only one of her crew who never asked, which was most of why she kept him.

The truth was that nobody was paying for this particular survey. The truth was that Theo Sandoval paid for it herself, out of the wages she earned doing legitimate work for the plantation interests upriver, because she could not stop walking these lines any more than a penitent could stop visiting the grave. She told herself it was diligence. She told herself that if the company ever came back—and companies always came back; they were like crocodiles that way, patient and cold-blooded and certain of their own territory—she wanted to know exactly what they would find. She wanted to know what the water had taken and what it had left.

What the water had left, mostly, was evidence.

* * *

By midday the heat had thickened until breathing felt like drinking. They worked the western stretch, where the spur climbed toward the saddle between two ridges, and Theo found the washout she'd been dreading since the dry season began. The culvert beneath the embankment had failed—she could see it from thirty meters, the telltale sag in the crown of the fill, the seep of water weeping orange with iron through the ballast. Six years of monsoons working at one weak point, the way water works at everything eventually. Men. Companies. Promises.

She marked it in the book. Culvert 9, collapsed. Fill unstable. Line impassable beyond this point without full reconstruction.

Then she stood very still for a moment, because beyond this point was the junction.

Not the Sepik Junction itself—that lay two hundred kilometers north, deeper in the basin, drowned years ago when the river changed course and took the lowlands with it. But the spur she was standing on had been built to feed that junction, and every meter of it pointed north like an accusation. Follow the dead rails far enough and you arrived at the place where eleven men had gone to work on a Tuesday morning and not come back. Where the company had said, in language polished by lawyers, that the accident was the fault of a surveyor named Daniel Sandoval, who had falsified load reports, who had exceeded safe tonnage, who had died in the disaster along with the crew he'd supposedly murdered through negligence.

Her brother. Dead twice over—once in the water, once in the record books.

Theo closed the survey book and pressed her thumb flat against the cover until the cardboard dented. Wem was watching her from down the grade, rod on his shoulder, waiting. He had learned to read her silences the way other boys read weather.

"Lunch," she said, in Tok Pisin, and her voice came out level, which cost her something. "We eat here. Then we turn back."

"We don't go past the culvert?"

"No." She heard how fast the answer came and made herself slow down. "Nothing past there worth measuring. The fill's gone. We'd be standing on air."

Wem shouldered the rod and started back toward the shade of the fig tree where they'd left the water bags. Theo stayed a moment longer, looking north along the vanished line, at the green corridor the railway had cut and the jungle had spent six years sewing shut. Somewhere under all that growth were the mile posts her brother had set with his own hands, back when the line was new and the future was a thing you could survey. Somewhere under the water of the basin were the barge and the bridge and the eleven men, and the strongbox of original reports that officially did not exist, because official paper said the originals had been destroyed in the accident.

Official paper said a great many things.

* * *

They ate rice and tinned fish in the fig shade, and Wem slept, and Theo took out the map.

It was not a company map. Company maps lived in company offices, stamped and indexed and guarded by men whose job was memory management. This one was hers—drawn in her own hand over six years, on sheets of tracing linen stitched into a oilcloth folder, recording every closed line in the basin: gradients, culverts, washouts, the locations of buried rolling stock, the places where the jungle had swallowed track whole and the places where it hadn't, which were more dangerous. It was, as far as she knew, the only complete picture in existence of what the Sepik Basin railways had been and what remained of them. A man with this map and enough money could reopen half the network. A man with this map and bad intentions could do worse than that.

Which was why, once a year, she took out the oldest sheet—the first one, drawn in the weeks after the disaster, when her hands had shaken and her grief had gotten into the linework, making some of the contours too heavy, too dark—and she read it, and she decided again whether to burn it.

She had decided to burn it six times. Each time she had folded it away instead, telling herself the same lie: that knowledge kept was knowledge controlled. That if it burned, and the company came back anyway, there would be nothing to stand against them except their own records—which were lies. The map was the only true document in the basin. Burning the truth felt like letting her brother die a third time.

Keeping it felt like carrying a lit fuse through a powder store.

Today, though, today she turned to the second page, and this page was different. This page was a copy—not her hand, not her grief. She had made it in her first year in the territory, back when she still believed that important papers should be duplicated, distributed, entrusted to the world. Back before she understood that every copy was a door and every door could be found. She had sent this one to a man in Rabaul, a shipping clerk she'd known slightly, a man who seemed decent, with instructions to hold it unseen. And then she had spent two years of bad nights wondering whether he'd looked, whether he'd shown anyone, whether somewhere in a Rabaul boarding house her brother's death was being traced out in pencil by a stranger's finger.

Three months ago the clerk had written to her—written to Tomas Reyes, care of the plantation office, asking after her health in the tone of a man who wants something. Asking whether she might have further documents of interest. Asking, in a postscript, whether the junction area might be worth developing, since he understood there was salvage in it.

Salvage. As if the drowned junction were a cargo that had fallen off a boat.

She had not answered. But the letter meant the man had looked at the map. Had studied it. Was thinking about it. And a page in a stranger's keeping was a page she did not control, and a page she did not control was a page that could reach Port Moresby, could reach the company's land agents, could reach the kind of men who reopened things.

Theo took out her matches.

She did it methodically, the way she did everything. First she copied the essential figures onto a fresh sheet—the culvert positions, the flood levels, the data that was merely engineering and carried no invitation in it. Then she laid the old page on a flat stone, weighted the corners with pebbles so the wind couldn't take burning fragments into the dry grass, and struck the match.

The tracing linen caught slowly, the way oiled cloth does, the flame moving across her own younger handwriting in a slow orange tide. She watched the contours go. The heavy dark lines her grief had drawn. The little annotation in the margin—D.S. checked these grades personally, March—burning last of all, the ink flaring briefly black before it became ash.

"I'm sorry," she said quietly, to the page or the man, she wasn't sure which. "I can't carry all of you."

Wem stirred in his sleep and didn't wake. The smoke went straight up in the windless heat, a thin gray thread climbing through the fig canopy, and Theo watched it until it dispersed, and then she ground the ash into the stone with the heel of her boot and scattered it into the culvert washout, where the next rain would take it north.

North, toward the junction. Everything in this country flowed north eventually.

* * *

They worked the return leg through the afternoon, and the light was going copper when Theo saw the smoke.

Not her smoke. This was downstream, near the loading yard at Ambunti—a single column, steady, deliberate, the smoke of a camp fire kept by someone who intended to stay. Her heart performed a small cold maneuver that she recognized from six years ago and hated. Strangers on the river were common enough this time of year; traders, missionaries, government patrols. But strangers who came up the Sepik in the dying weeks of the dry season, when the water was too low for anything but local boats, were either lost or looking for something.

And the only things in this country worth looking for were the things that had been buried.

She finished the day's figures by lamplight in the lean-to she rented from the mission, checking each number twice, and while she worked she listened to the night sounds of Ambunti drifting through the mosquito netting: dogs, drums from the village across the water, the river itself muttering over its bars. Somewhere in that soundscape was a stranger building a fire.

At dawn she walked down to the yard to buy kerosene, and that was where she saw him.

He stood at the agent's counter with his back to her, and even before he turned—even before the six years rearranged themselves and put his face back together out of her memory—she knew him by his stance. He had always stood like that, weight on one hip, one hand resting on whatever surface was nearest, occupying space with the easy certainty of a man who had never once been made to feel unwelcome anywhere. Marcus Vane. Older through the shoulders, silver at the temples now, better dressed than the territory deserved, but unmistakably, infuriatingly himself.

He turned as the agent spoke her alias, and his eyes found her, and he smiled.

It was not a surprised smile. That was the worst of it. It was the smile of a man who had walked a long way to be exactly where he was standing, and had known her name—her real name—before his boots ever touched this mud.

"Reyes, is it," Vane said pleasantly, extending his hand as if offering her a contract instead of a greeting. "I've been looking forward to meeting you. I understand you know these old lines better than any man alive."

His grip was warm and dry and perfectly calibrated, and Theo Sandoval stood inside her borrowed name and felt six years of invisibility begin, very quietly, to burn.
