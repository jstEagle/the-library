## 2. A Letter in Wet Ink

The letter came up the river the way bad news always did in the basin—carried by someone smiling.

Theo Sandoval heard the outboard motor a full quarter hour before she saw the boat, a thin insect whine threading through the drone of cicadas along the decommissioned spur. She was kneeling in the gravel ballast of a line that had not carried a train in six years, driving a survey stake with a rubber mallet, and she did not stop. She finished the stake. She sighted along the old alignment to the collapsed culvert, noted the reading in the waterproof book bound to her forearm, and only then straightened and turned to watch the boat come.

It was a company boat. Not a company that officially existed anymore—the concession had lapsed, the subsidiary dissolved, the nameplates pried off the Wewak depot—but the boat had that particular look: new paint over old rot, a hull that had been maintained by men who were paid to maintain it and no one else. It rode low in the brown water, heavy with a cooler, a canvas bag, and one passenger.

Marcus Vane stood in the bow with one hand on the rail, and even at two hundred meters, even through heat shimmer and the curtain of pandanus, Theo felt her body react before her mind could stop it. The old flinch. Six years of being Tomas Reyes—six years of lowered voice, of calloused hands kept deliberately dirty, of answering to a dead man's name—and her body still knew him the way a scar knows rain.

He had aged. That was the first coherent thought she managed. The hair at his temples had gone to iron, and he'd filled out through the shoulders the way men did when they stopped doing physical work and started paying others to do it. But he stood the same way, weight forward, chin slightly down, a man perpetually leaning into the next thing he wanted.

She had time, while he beached the boat and waded the last few meters, to decide who she was going to be. Tomas Reyes, she settled on. Tomas Reyes, assistant surveyor, contract labor, a man with no history worth asking about and no future worth offering. Tomas Reyes did not know Marcus Vane. Tomas Reyes had never signed anything.

"You're the one they call Reyes." Vane stood at the edge of her camp with his hands loose at his sides, and his eyes did the thing they had always done: moved over her face like a man reading a document he'd written himself, checking for alterations. "The crew foreman."

"That's me." She kept her voice low and flat, pitched in the register she'd practiced in front of a cracked mirror in a Wewak boarding house until it stopped sounding like an act. "You're standing on my tarp."

He stepped back, amused, and looked around the camp with a connoisseur's eye. It was a good camp, she knew. She'd built it to be read correctly: the fly stretched between two poles at the regulation angle, the tool roll rolled tight, the kettle on the coals, the surveyor's transit in its case with the brass worn bright. A camp that said: this man is competent, transient, and unremarkable. A camp that said nothing else at all.

"Six years," Vane said, "and you live like a monk."

"Monks have a God. I have a contract." She poured tea into a tin mug and handed it to him because refusing hospitality in the basin was a message, and she wasn't ready to send messages yet. "The company that used to run this concession is a filing cabinet in Port Moresby. If you've come about the spur, the answer's the same as it was for the last three men who asked. It's closed. It stays closed."

"Three men." He smiled, and took the tea, and did not drink it. "You've been turning people away for three years."

"I turn everyone away. It's a specialty."

"And yet here I am." He crouched, unhurried, and set his canvas bag on the gravel between them. He did not open it. That was Vane all over—she remembered it from the years when they had shared a drafting table and, briefly, a bed: he believed that the space around a thing was a weapon, that a document unopened exerted more force than a document revealed. "The concession's been bought. Quietly. New holding company, old money, and they want the basin lines reopened. All of them. Including the spur you're standing on, and including—" he let the pause sit there, fat and deliberate, "—the junction."

The word went through her like a stake driven into wet ground. The junction. She had not heard anyone say it aloud in six years. On paper it was called Survey Point 9, and on the company's maps it was marked with a small, tidy symbol meaning *inundated—no further work*, and in her own maps—the ones she burned, page by page, a ritual of small arson she performed whenever a page grew too dangerous to own—it was marked with nothing at all. She never drew it. Drawing it would have meant measuring it, and measuring it would have meant admitting she remembered every dimension of the place where her brother died.

"Reopening a drowned line," she said, and was proud of how level her voice came out, "is not a survey job. It's a salvage job. You'd need dredges, trestle crews, a marine engineer. Not a foreman with a transit and four men who get paid in cash."

"I'm not offering a survey job." He finally drank the tea, watching her over the rim of the mug. "I'm offering a reopening survey. Route assessment, structural report on the old trestles, waterline mapping of the flooded sections. Six months' work, maybe less. Paid triple whatever Reyes's rate is. Paid in an account you choose, under whatever name you choose." He set the mug down on the gravel, precisely, as if it were an instrument. "I know who you are, Theo."

The name hit the camp like a dropped tool. She did not move. She had trained herself for this too—the stillness, the blank face, the slow blink of a man who has just heard a word in a language he doesn't speak. But inside, everything was moving. Six years of walls, and he'd walked through them in four words, and the worst part, the part that made her hands want to shake, was the relief underneath the terror. Someone knew. Someone finally knew, and she was still breathing.

"Tomas," she said. "My name is Tomas."

"Your name is Theodora Sandoval. You were the junior engineer on the Sepik Basin concession until March, six years ago. You resigned eleven days after the accident at Survey Point 9. You signed the closure order for the line—your signature is on the document, third from the top, under mine." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. He said it the way he read blueprints. "You've been hiding in the one place on earth where a woman with a man's name and a man's trade can vanish completely. And you've done it well. I'll grant you that. It took me two years to find you, and I had advantages."

She looked at him, and let the mask fall—not all the way, but enough. Enough for him to see the fury, because the fury was the truest thing she owned and she wanted him to see it plainly.

"You found me," she said, "to hire me. To reopen the lines I closed."

"I found you because you're the only surveyor alive who knows those lines."

"The lines are closed because the survey crew died."

"The survey crew died," Vane said, "because of what happened at the junction. Yes. I know. I was there, Theo. I signed the same reports you signed."

"You signed the reports that buried them."

"I signed the reports that were given to me." For the first time something moved behind his face—not guilt, she thought, she had seen his guilt once and knew its shape, and this was not it. It was more like the flicker of a man adjusting a position he'd held too long. "That's the difference between us, maybe. You thought the paperwork was the truth. I knew it was a wall. And walls can come down."

He opened the canvas bag.

She had imagined this moment, in the six years of nights when sleep came hard, in a hundred different shapes. A confession. A subpoena. A company man with a warrant. She had imagined every version of the past coming up the river to find her, and in none of them had she imagined it arriving as a single photograph, printed on cheap stock, gone soft at the corners from being carried.

He held it out. He did not stand. He stayed crouched on the gravel with the photograph extended in two fingers, patient as a fisherman, and she understood that this, too, was deliberate—that he wanted her to come to it. To close the distance herself. To make her own choice the instrument of her own undoing.

She did not move.

"March of that year," Vane said quietly. "Eleven days after the barge went down. The company filed the death certificates. Four men, drowned in the collapse at Point 9. Your brother's name was third on the list. You know the list. You can probably still recite it."

"Anselm," she said. Her voice came out wrong, scraped. "Okafor. My brother. Delacroix."

"Delacroix," he agreed. "And this was taken in April. Twenty-six days after the certificates were filed. In Wewak. By a man the company paid to watch the waterfront."

She took the photograph.

She hadn't decided to take it. Her hand simply went, the way a hand goes to a hot stove or a falling child, and then she was holding it and the camp, the river, the six years, all of it receded to the edges of a rectangle of fading silver.

The picture was bad. Taken from distance, from cover, at an angle—the waterfront at Wewak, the fish sheds, a crowd around a supply launch. In the foreground, half-turned, a man in a canvas jacket with his collar up. The face was partly in shadow, partly blurred by the cheap lens and the man's own motion.

But she knew the set of the shoulders. She knew the way he stood with his weight on his left leg, always, since the fall he'd taken off a trestle when he was nineteen. She knew the hands, even blurred—the long fingers, the right thumb hooked in the jacket pocket. She had spent twenty-two years learning those hands across a shared drafting table, across a kitchen table, across the last supper they'd eaten together in the Wewak mess hall the night before his crew went upriver.

"That's not him," she said.

"No?"

"It's a man built like him. It's a trick. You found a photograph and you—"

"Theo." Vane's voice was gentle, and she hated him for it, hated him with a purity she hadn't felt since the funeral-that-wasn't-a-funeral, the closed box, the company's condolences delivered in a memo. "Look at his left hand."

She looked. She didn't want to. She looked anyway.

The left hand was missing two fingers. Index and middle, taken at the second knuckle. She knew exactly how they'd been lost—she'd been standing beside him when the coupling let go, when the chain snapped back across his hand on the Angoram spur, and she'd been the one who tied the tourniquet with her shirt while he laughed, actually laughed, and said *well, that's the guitar finished, then.* She had signed the injury report herself. Her initials were on it. *T.S.*

The blurred man on the Wewak waterfront had two fingers missing from his left hand, at the second knuckle, in exactly the place she had tied the cloth.

The river went on making its sound. The cicadas. The kettle ticking as it cooled. Somewhere down the spur a hornbill called, that absurd, prehistoric laugh, and Theo Sandoval knelt on the gravel of a dead railway holding a photograph that said her brother had walked out of the water that killed him and bought fish in a market twenty-six days later.

"That's impossible," she said. Her voice sounded like it belonged to someone else. "The barge went down. I read the report. I *wrote*—I read the report. Four men in the water at the collapse. The divers went down twice. They found the barge. They found—"

"They found the barge," Vane said. "They found Okafor. They found a body wearing Delacroix's jacket. The company did not ask too many questions about which body was which, because the company had already decided what the story was going to be. And the story needed your brother in it, because your brother—" he paused, and she watched him choose his words, watched him choose them the way he'd once chosen survey lines, for maximum yield at minimum cost, "—your brother had been asking questions of his own. About the load reports. About the tonnage figures on the last three runs. He'd written to the district office. If he'd drowned quietly, a hero of the concession, the questions drowned with him."

The world had a strange, underwater quality now. She was aware of her own breathing, too fast. She was aware that she was still holding the photograph, that her thumb had moved without her permission to cover the ruined hand, as if she could protect him from it, as if she could protect him from anything, six years too late, from a hundred meters of brown water and a lie with her own signature at the bottom of it.

"He was alive," she said. "He was alive and he didn't come to me."

"Or he couldn't," Vane said. "Or he was made to understand that coming to you would be the death of you both. Or—" and here his voice did something she had never heard it do, something almost careful, "—or he's still alive, Theo. That's the part I couldn't verify. A man who survived one death can survive another. The trail went to the highlands, and then it went cold, and then the company's interest in finding it went cold too, for reasons you can imagine."

She looked up at him. The fury was there—it was enormous, it was the truest thing she owned—but it had cracked, and through the crack something else was pouring in, something warm and unbearable, something she had spent six years drowning in the brown water of the basin along with everything else. Hope. Stupid, animal, desperate hope, the kind that makes a person believe a blurred photograph over a filed death certificate, over her own memory, over the closed box and the memo of condolences.

"You want me to reopen the line," she said slowly, "to Survey Point 9."

"I want the junction surveyed, the trestle assessed, the flooded sections mapped. I want a report that says the line can be reopened and what it would cost." He took the canvas bag and stood, and the light shifted, and for a moment he was just a man on a riverbank with bad news in his bag. "The new owners want the basin. What's under the basin is worth more than the basin. You know that. You always knew that—the minerals, the timber rights, the whole reason the line was built. The accident was an inconvenience. The closure was an inconvenience. The new owners are not sentimental about inconveniences."

"And you? Are you sentimental?"

"I'm practical." He smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that had once made her laugh across a drafting table, and now it made her want to put her mallet through his knee. "I'll be at the Wewak anchorage for six days. The contract's in the bag, along with an advance that would set up Reyes and his crew for a decade. You don't have to decide tonight. But Theo—" he crouched one last time, to be at her level, to be kind, and it was the cruelest thing he'd done all afternoon, "—you've spent six years keeping that line closed. Keeping everything closed. And it hasn't brought him back, and it hasn't brought you peace, and it hasn't kept your hands clean, because your signature is on the closure order same as mine. I'm offering you the one thing you've wanted since the day you signed it. A reason to go back. A reason to open it."

She said nothing.

She watched him wade to his boat, watched the outboard catch, watched the wake unspool across the brown water toward the bend where the river took him, and all the while the photograph was in her hand, soft-cornered, cheap, true.

* * *

Night came down on the basin the way it always did, fast and total, the dark arriving like water into a hold. Theo sat by her small fire with the photograph and did not burn it.

That was the thing. That was the whole thing, and she knew it. Six years of discipline, and the discipline had a single rule: nothing of the past survives the night. She had burned maps. She had burned letters. She had burned, in the first terrible year, the last letter her brother had ever written her, because it contained the words *the load reports don't add up* and because keeping it felt like holding a lit fuse. She burned things. Burning was how she survived. Burning was how Tomas Reyes stayed a real man and Theodora Sandoval stayed a dead one.

The photograph sat in her palm, and the firelight moved on it, and she could not burn it.

She tried to make herself. She held it over the coals. The heat came up against her fingers and she pulled it back, and she sat there hating herself with a completeness that felt almost like rest, because at least the hating was familiar.

Because if it was true—if Delacroix had walked out of the water, if the company had buried a living man in a death certificate, if her brother had spent six years alive and silent while she'd lived as a ghost to honor his ghost—then the closure order she'd signed wasn't grief. It wasn't even cowardice. It was the final act of the lie, the last stone laid on the wall, and she had laid it with her own hand, and Vane had been right about the one thing that mattered: the wall had kept her in as surely as it had kept the truth in.

And if it wasn't true—if the photograph was a forgery, if the missing fingers were a trick of light and shadow, if Marcus Vane had spent two years and a fortune finding her just to hang a lure in front of her—then he knew exactly what she was, and where she was, and what she could not refuse.

Either way, he had her. Either way, the river ran one direction, up, toward the junction, toward Survey Point 9, toward the black water where the barge still lay with whatever it held in its drowned hold.

Theo fed the fire another stick and looked at the blurred face, at the collar turned up against a morning six years gone, at the hand with its two missing fingers, and she said, out loud, in her own voice, the first time in six years she had used it where anyone might have heard:

"You're alive. You son of a bitch. You're alive, and you let me bury you."

The basin said nothing. It never did.

She put the photograph inside her shirt, against her skin, where the maps used to go, and she got out the survey book, and she turned to the last clean page, and she began, in a hand that shook only a little, to write a list of names—the men she'd need, the ones who lived off the books, the ones who signed nothing.

She had six days.
