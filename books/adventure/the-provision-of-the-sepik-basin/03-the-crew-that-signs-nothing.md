## 3. The Crew That Signs Nothing

Elio Cruz had a rule about men who paid in advance: they were either desperate or dishonest, and the dangerous ones were both.

The woman who called herself Tomas Reyes was neither, which unsettled him more. She came down to the stink of Wewak's harbor at first light, when the fish buyers were still arguing over ice and the tide smelled like rust and rotting coconut, and she stood at the rail of his boat—the *Mariana*, forty feet of teak and stubbornness, half her planking older than Elio himself—and read his hull the way a priest reads a sinner's face.

She didn't ask permission. She crouched at the waterline where the patch showed, a rectangle of new copper over old rot just above the boot stripe, and pressed her thumb against the seam.

"This was done in a hurry," she said. Not a question.

"Done well, though." Elio came down the gangplank with his coffee tin in hand, steam rising off it into the wet morning air. "Done by me."

"I don't doubt that you did it. I doubt how long it will hold." She straightened, wiped her thumb on her trousers, and looked at him with eyes the color of river water after a storm—brown going gray, patient, unimpressed. "How long?"

Elio shrugged, which was an answer, and she received it as one. That was the first thing he noticed about her: she didn't waste breath pretending. The second thing he noticed was that she'd already counted his crew before he'd counted hers—two deckhands sleeping off last night's cargo under the awning, the boy Sarto coiling line on the foredeck with the quick, showy hands of a man who wanted to be seen working. Her gaze touched each of them and moved on, cataloguing, dismissing, keeping.

"You're Tomas Reyes," he said. "The surveyor. People talk."

"People talk about everyone. It doesn't make what they say true." She pulled a folded paper from inside her jacket but didn't offer it yet. Held it the way a card player holds a card. "I need a boat and a crew for six weeks, maybe eight. Upriver into the basin. No ports of call after Wewak except what I name. No radio traffic. No names logged anywhere."

"No names," Elio repeated. He drank his coffee and considered her over the rim of the tin. Around them the harbor woke up—gulls screaming over the fish tables, a diesel launch coughing itself awake two slips down, the endless green wall of the coast pressing in behind the town like something waiting its turn. "You know what you're asking. A man without papers can disappear in Wewak. A man without papers on *that* river—" he nodded toward the basin, toward all that drowned country westward where the maps went vague and the government boats went never, "—he disappears twice."

"That's why I'm asking men like you."

There it was. Plain as a knife laid on a table between them. Most hirers would have dressed it up—*reliable men, steady men*—and both of them would have pretended not to understand. This woman didn't dress anything. Elio found he respected it and distrusted it in equal measure, the way he respected and distrusted the sea.

"The pay?" he said.

She told him. It was double fair and half of what the job was probably worth, which meant she knew exactly what the job was worth and expected him to know it too.

"And if we hit trouble? If someone asks us questions upriver?"

"Then you tell them the truth," she said. "You're a boatman running cargo. You've never seen me before in your life. I fell aboard at Ambunti and wouldn't get off."

"And if they look at my manifest?"

"There is no manifest. There's no cargo worth logging. Chain, tools, rice, kerosene, quinine." A pause, small as a hairline crack. "And paper. Maps. Nothing anyone would kill for."

Elio looked at her for a long moment. *Nothing anyone would kill for,* she'd said, and her voice hadn't changed at all, and that was how he knew she was lying, or hoped she was lying, or was telling the truth about something so large it made ordinary lies look like mercy.

He should have said no. Six years he'd run this coast clean of trouble, taking the jobs that paid little and asked less, keeping his crew fed and his name out of every book in the territory. He was thirty-four years old and he had survived by being worth nothing to anybody important.

"Six weeks," he heard himself say. "Eight if the rain comes early. And the boy Sarto stays aboard—he's useless, but his mother feeds my crew when we're in port, and I don't leave her son behind."

Something moved behind her eyes—not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of one careful person noting another's carefulness and approving of the architecture.

"Done," said Tomas Reyes, and shook his hand once, briefly, like closing a lock.

* * *

Theo watched them come to her over the next four days the way she'd learned to watch everything: sideways, without appearing to watch at all.

Elio Cruz brought three besides Sarto. Old Mateo, who'd been a fireman on the coastal runs until a boiler scalded half his face and the company decided sixty percent of a man wasn't worth a pension. Benno, young, quiet, missing two fingers on his left hand from a winch that nobody had ever been fined for. And Dario, who claimed to be a cook and cooked adequately and carried a knife he was very good at not showing anyone.

None of them signed anything. None of them asked why. That was the fellowship of the unrecorded—it ran on a currency thinner than trust and stronger than gold: the mutual agreement not to be curious. Every man at her table had a hole in his history shaped exactly like the space where a question should go, and they honored one another's holes the way sailors honor weather.

She fed them rice and tinned beef the first night at her camp above the river road, and she watched them eat, and she thought: *these are the only kind of men left to me.* Men the company had chewed and spat out. Men whose grievances had gone quiet and cold, banked like coals. They would work hard and ask nothing and, if the river turned on them, they would scatter like birds and be impossible to trace—which was what she needed, and also what she deserved.

Because she could not, in good conscience, ask honest men to do this.

The photograph sat in her breast pocket like a coal that wouldn't cool. She'd taken it out eleven times since Vane left her camp, and put it back twelve. Her brother's face, grainy, alive, dated *after*. After the accident. After the company's report. After the funeral Theo had not been permitted to attend because attending meant existing, and existing meant questions, and questions meant the whole architecture of her silence coming down.

*Alive.* The word kept breaking open in her chest like a door swinging on a ruined hinge.

She burned another page of the map that night—one of the middle sheets, the spur lines above the junction, coordinates she alone still held complete—and fed it to the cook fire piece by piece while the crew slept. The ash rose white against the black of the trees. Somewhere across the water a night bird called its idiot question over and over, and got no answer, and called again.

*Tomas Reyes* didn't burn maps. *Tomas Reyes* had no maps to burn. But the woman feeding the fire was neither Tomas nor the person she'd been six years ago; she was something provisional, something held together with wire and routine, and she understood now that Vane had found the wire and was pulling it strand by strand.

She checked the perimeter, checked the boat's mooring, checked the crew's rifles—two, both old, both functional—and went to her tent and lay down with her boots on, as always, ready to move.

Sleep took her eventually, and in sleep there was the river, and in the river there was the barge going over, and her brother's hand coming up out of the brown water—

She woke before dawn with her hand already closed around the knife under her bedroll, and lay listening to the rain begin, far off, a sound like applause from an audience she couldn't see.

* * *

Elio had run the upper Sepik in flood season and dry season and the seasons in between, and he had never once seen a passenger do what the woman Reyes did on the third day out of Wewak.

They were anchored in a side channel, waiting out a squall, and she asked to see his charts. Not borrowed—asked, formally, standing at the wheelhouse door with the rain drumming the canvas overhead. He handed them over expecting her to skim them the way passengers did, hunting for pretty villages and comfortable distances.

Instead she read them. Actually read them—soundings, current marks, the pencil annotations Elio's father had made thirty years ago and Elio had corrected ever since. Her finger stopped on a stretch above Ambunti where the channel split around a mid-river island.

"This mark's wrong," she said. "The eastern passage shoaled out two years ago. There's a snag at the head of it—you can't see it at high water."

Elio took the chart back and looked. His own notation, from a trip eighteen months past, said the same thing. He hadn't told her that. Nobody had told her that.

"How do you know this water?"

"I surveyed it." She said it flatly, without pride, the way a man admits to a debt. "Before."

"Before what?"

The pause lasted three seconds. On the fourth, rain hammered harder on the canvas, and somewhere aft Sarto laughed at something Benno said, bright and careless, and the woman Reyes turned her face toward the sound and Elio saw—just for a moment, just in profile—what lived underneath all that competence and coldness. Fear. Not the general fear any sensible person carried into the basin. Something specific, with an address. She was afraid of a place, or a date, or a name, and whatever it was, it had teeth marks on it.

"Before," she said again, and gave him back his silence like change from a purchase.

That evening he found her at the stern checking the chain locker, and he said, without deciding to say it: "Your patch will hold to Ambunti. Past that, in flood water, I won't promise. If you want my advice, we beach her every second day and I re-tar the seam. It costs us time."

"We don't have much time."

"Then we have a problem, because dead men keep no schedule at all."

She looked at him then, fully, and Elio had the strange sensation of being weighed—not judged, weighed, like cargo whose manifest had been lost. Whatever she concluded, she kept.

"Every second day," she said. "Thank you, Cruz."

"Don't thank me. Tar the seam yourself. My hands are going to be full."

And here was the thing Elio would turn over later, lying in his bunk listening to the river breathe: she did. Next morning, sleeves rolled, hands in the cold tar to the wrist, working beside Mateo without instruction and without complaint, and when a bead of hot pitch spattered her forearm she didn't even flinch—just wiped it and kept the blade moving. The men noticed. Men always notice who shares the misery. By noon Sarto was hauling her gear without being asked, and Benno had started saving her the driest spot under the awning, and none of them knew her real name and none of them cared, because the river didn't care either, and out here competence was the only passport that got stamped.

But Elio noticed something else, something the others didn't have the history to read. When she thought no one was watching, she stood at the bow and looked west—upriver, into the deep green throat of the basin where the old lines ran—and her face did something no surveyor's face should do. It grieved. Quietly, completely, the way a face grieves at a grave.

She wasn't afraid of the journey, Elio realized. She was afraid of arriving.

* * *

They made Ambunti on the sixth day, running easy water under a sky the color of pewter, and tied up at the government station long enough to take on fuel drums and the last of the stores. Ambunti was a hundred buildings pretending to be a town—a patrol post, a trade store, a mission, a sprawl of thatch along the banks where the river widened and slowed and began, already, to feel different. Heavier. Older.

Theo stood on the *Mariana*'s deck supervising the loading, counting drums, checking crates against her list, and felt the basin reach for her the way it always did—not violently, not yet, but with the slow certainty of a tide. Six years since she'd breathed this air. It tasted the same: mud and woodsmoke and green rot and distance.

"Tomas!" Elio's voice, from the shore. "Fuel's aboard. We can clear by the afternoon tide—or there's a supply launch due tomorrow out of Wewak, if you want to wait for—"

"No," she said. Too fast. She caught it, softened it. "No. Today. We go today."

Elio shrugged and turned back to the drums, and Theo exhaled slowly through her nose and told herself the tightness in her chest was only the weather coming down out of the mountains. Rain by nightfall, certainly. The monsoon gathering itself somewhere north like a debt collector.

She was checking the fresh water tanks when she saw the stranger on the wharf.

A woman—that alone was enough to fix the eye, out here, where white and mixed-race women were missionaries' wives or district officers' wives and nothing else, and this one was neither. Younger than Theo by some years, travel-stained, with a canvas pack riding her shoulders like she'd been born under it and a leather document case strapped crosswise over her chest with the possessiveness of a body part. Dark hair cut short and practical. Eyes that were doing to Theo's crew exactly what Theo's eyes had done to them in Wewak: counting, weighing, keeping.

The stranger spoke to Elio. Elio shook his head. The stranger spoke again, longer, and produced something from the document case—papers, or money, or both—and Elio's shrug changed shape, became the shrug of a man whose rules were meeting an exception.

Theo crossed the deck. "We're loaded," she said, loud enough to carry. "Whatever you're selling, we're not buying."

"I'm not selling." The woman turned, and up close her composure had seams in it, fine ones, like the hull patch—new material stretched over old damage. "I'm hiring. Passage upriver, as far as you go. I pay my own way, carry my own weight, and ask no questions I'm not prepared to answer myself."

"Everyone says that last part. Almost no one means it."

"Then we'll find out together." The woman's chin lifted a fraction. "My name is Kiri Anselm. I'm looking for the old junction—the drowned one, on the closed line. I understand you're the only boat fool enough to be going that way in monsoon season."

The word *junction* went through Theo like a spear finding the gap between ribs. She kept her face still because she'd had six years of practice keeping her face still, but inside, everything tilted—because Vane had named the junction, and now a stranger named it, unprompted, on a wharf at the edge of the map, and coincidences that precise were not coincidences. They were design.

"Who told you about my boat?" Theo said.

"A man in Wewak. Big voice, city clothes, pays for information the way other men pay for drinks." Kiri Anselm's eyes narrowed slightly. "Why? Does that trouble you?"

It troubled her the way a loose plank troubles a man standing on it. Vane's hand, everywhere, arranging the board before the game began. Sending her a crew, sending her a witness, sending her—what? A liability? A check on her?

Or bait.

"Passage is fifty pounds," Theo heard herself say, because refusing would raise more questions than accepting, and because a known quantity aboard was safer than one circling in the dark. "Paid in advance. You work if there's work. You keep your cabin tidy and your curiosity leashed."

Kiri Anselm almost smiled. "Leashed," she repeated, as if tasting the word. "Agreed."

She stepped aboard, and the *Mariana* rocked gently under the added weight, and Elio cast off the lines with the unhurried economy of a man who had long ago stopped being surprised by anything passengers did. The current took them. The green walls of the basin slid closer on either side, and the air grew warmer and thicker, and far to the west, beyond the last visible bend, thunder spoke in a language Theo understood perfectly.

She stood at the stern and watched Ambunti shrink behind them, and felt the photograph against her heart, and thought: *the board is set. Every piece placed by someone else's hand.*

Behind her, quietly, Kiri Anselm opened her document case and began, with great care, to read.
