## 4. Terms Written in River Water

The rain came at dusk the way it always did in the basin—not arriving so much as assembling itself, drop by drop out of a sky that had been thinking about it all afternoon. Theo Sandoval sat across the folding table from Marcus Vane and listened to the first fat drops strike the tarpaulin overhead like fingers testing a drumhead.

Between them lay the contract. Three pages, typed on company letterhead that should not have existed anymore, because the company that issued it had dissolved itself six years ago and reconstituted under a new name with the same directors and the same lawyers and the same appetite for other people's silence. The paper was dry. Everything else in the camp was damp—the canvas walls, the bedding, the crates of survey instruments she'd spent years keeping oiled and useless—but the contract was dry, because Vane had carried it inside oilcloth against his chest, close as a heartbeat.

He watched her read. He had always been good at watching; it was one of the things that had made him dangerous before he'd made himself unforgivable.

"Page two has the compensation schedule," he said. "Triple the standard rate for monsoon-season work. Hazard clause covers injury and loss of equipment."

"It doesn't cover loss of men."

"No man is going to be lost, Tomas." He used the name gently, the way a man might handle a bird with a broken wing—careful, proprietary, faintly contemptuous of the thing he held. "The lines are closed, not destroyed. Rails are down there under a meter of silt in most places. We reopen them, we regrade, we run the survey you already know how to run because you ran it the first time."

She turned a page without seeing it. Rain thickened on the tarpaulin. Somewhere beyond the camp's edge, the river spoke in its low continuous voice, the voice it had been using since before there were rails or companies or names worth hiding behind, and would go on using long after.

"The crew," she said. "I choose them."

"Within reason."

"Entirely within reason, or not at all." She set the page down and looked at him directly for the first time since he'd stepped into her camp that morning, mud to his knees, smiling like the intervening years were a corridor they could walk back through together. His hair had gone grey at the temples. There was a new scar through one eyebrow. Otherwise he was exactly the man she remembered: handsome in the way that well-built machinery is handsome, all function and polish, nothing wasted, nothing true. "I don't take orders from the company. Not its shadow, not its letters, not its money. I take a fee. I deliver a report. That's the whole shape of it."

"And the fee buys my discretion about who you were before you were Tomas Reyes." He said it quietly, without cruelty, which was worse. "Yes. That's understood."

The lamp between them guttered as a gust found a gap in the canvas. In the sudden dimness Theo felt the old vertigo rise in her—the sensation, familiar now as her own pulse, of standing on ground that was already moving.

* * *

The night of the accident had been dry. That was the detail no one ever believed when she told it, and she had never told it, so it hardly mattered. Dry, with stars, the kind of hard clear night the basin allowed perhaps twice in a season, as if the sky needed occasionally to show what it was withholding.

She had been twenty-six and certain of everything. Her brother Emilio had been twenty-nine and certain of more. They had stood together on the staging platform above the third cutting while the night crew loaded the ore cars below, and Emilio had been laughing about something—the cook's coffee, she thought, or the foreman's hat—and the laughter had still been in the air, unfinished, when the first car left the rail.

She remembered the sound afterward more than the sound itself: the particular silence that follows a catastrophe in open country, when there is nothing for the noise to echo off of and so the world simply absorbs it, swallows it whole. She remembered running down the switchback path with her lantern swinging wild shadows. She remembered the smell—ore dust and creosote and something else beneath, something iron and intimate that her mind had refused then and refused now to name.

Fourteen men. She had helped pull eleven of them out herself, working until her hands stopped being hands. Three had been beyond pulling anywhere.

And Emilio—Emilio had not been under the cars at all. Emilio had been up on the loading gantry when it went, and by morning he was gone, vanished into the country with two duffel bags and the original load reports in a waterproof case, reports that by noon had become falsified reports, doctored reports, reports the company swore had never existed in their true form. By evening the company had a story: the assistant surveyor Sandoval, negligent, possibly drunk, had altered the tonnage figures to hide overloading, had caused the derailment, had fled to escape justice. The newspapers printed it in three columns. The inquiry confirmed it in nine days. Her brother became a ghost with a warrant attached, and Theo became the sister of a dead man who was legally alive and actually dead, and she learned what it cost to bury someone the world insisted on prosecuting instead of mourning.

She had signed the closure order herself. That was the part that lived in her chest like a swallowed coal. The company had come to her—grieving, pliable, useful—and asked her, as the surviving engineer with the most complete knowledge of the line, to certify the spur closures, to render the whole system unrunnable, unfindable, unrecoverable. Burying the evidence meant burying the rails. She had understood that even then, standing with the pen in her hand. She had signed anyway, because the alternative was to leave the lines open for the company to run again on the same lies, and because some cold practical corner of her had calculated that a closed line kept its secrets better than an open one.

Six years of telling herself it had been the only choice. Six years of knowing it hadn't been, quite.

* * *

"Reyes." Vane's voice pulled her back. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm deciding whether to throw you out of my camp."

"You won't." He leaned back, and the lamplight caught the scar through his eyebrow. "You want to know why? Because you read to the end of page three. A woman who intends to refuse doesn't read to the end."

Theo looked down at the contract. He was right; she had read every word, twice, the way she read everything now—hunting for the trap, the clause, the hinge on which the whole mechanism would swing shut. And she had found it, or rather she had found its absence, and the absence frightened her more than any clause could have.

Because nowhere in three pages did the contract say where the final report went.

"I have a condition," she said.

Vane smiled as if she'd offered him dessert. "Name it."

"The evidence comes to me first." She kept her voice level, kept her hands flat on the table where he could see them, though what he'd see was steadiness and what she felt was the opposite. "Whatever you're holding—the photograph, the papers, whatever it is—you give it to me before we reach the junction. Before the first rail is cleared. Not at completion. Not on delivery. First."

Something moved behind his eyes. It was very fast, and she almost missed it, and she was almost certain it was satisfaction.

"That's unusual," he said.

"It's non-negotiable."

"The company will want—"

"The company can want." She heard her own voice sharpen and let it. "You didn't come here with the company's blessing, Vane, or if you did, you're lying about it, and either way I'm not interested. You came to me because you need someone who knows those lines, and the only person who knows those lines is the woman whose brother your employers destroyed. So we'll be honest with each other for one minute, and then we can go back to pretending. You have proof. Proof of what happened the night fourteen men died and my brother took the blame for it. I want that proof in my hands before I put one boot on your reopened grade. Then I'll do the work, and I'll do it better than anyone alive could do it, and you'll pay me triple rate, and everyone gets what they came for."

The rain filled the pause. It had settled into its working rhythm now, steady and enormous, the sound of the whole sky emptying itself onto the basin, and beneath it the river rose a little in its bed, patient, taking.

Vane studied her for a long moment. She watched him decide something. She had watched him decide things before, six years ago, in another life, when she had loved him and believed that the decisions he made in that particular silence were decisions in her favor. She knew better now. What he decided in the silence was only ever what served him; the rest was theater.

"All right," he said.

Just that. No argument, no counteroffer, no lawyerly haggling over timing or custody or chain of possession. All right, delivered smooth as poured water, and Theo felt the cold move up her spine the way floodwater moves up a channel—quietly, without any visible cause, and all at once much deeper than it should be.

"You agree too easily," she said.

"Do I?"

"A man like you doesn't concede a condition like that unless the condition costs him nothing. Which means either you intend to hand over the evidence regardless—" she shook her head slowly, "—no. Not that. Which means the evidence isn't what you say it is, or it isn't where you say it is, or handing it to me serves you somehow in ways I haven't mapped yet."

Vane laughed. It was a real laugh; that was the terrible thing about him. He could laugh genuinely at the exact moment he was doing you the worst damage, as though the damage delighted some honest part of him that had simply never been consulted about anything important.

"You always did think three moves deep," he said. "It's one of the reasons I—"

"Don't."

"—respect you," he finished, smoothly rerouting the sentence around the wreckage of the one he'd wanted. He gathered the contract, squared its edges, and slid it across the table to her along with the pen. "Sign, and the condition goes in as an addendum tonight. My word and the company's both. Evidence to Reyes before commencement of line work."

"My word against yours, written in ink that means nothing to anyone but us."

"Ink is all anyone has out here." He gestured at the canvas walls, the dark beyond them, the drowned green immensity of the basin pressing in on the little island of lamplight. "Courts are a rumor this far upriver. Law is a rumor. All we have is what we write down and whether we mean it. I'm asking you to believe I mean it."

*That's precisely what frightens me,* she thought. *You know I can't afford to believe you, and you're asking anyway, because a request I must refuse makes me the unreasonable one, and a refusal gives you permission for whatever you do next.*

But aloud she said only: "Addendum first. Then signature."

He wrote it out himself, in a small precise hand, reading each phrase aloud as he set it down—*evidence in full, originals and copies, delivered to T. Reyes prior to commencement*—and Theo watched the pen move and thought about Emilio's hands, big blunt surveyor's hands, the last time she'd seen them reaching up to fix a guy-wire on the gantry, and how those hands had held the true reports, and how the true reports had outlived him somewhere in the world like seeds outliving the tree.

When Vane finished, she signed. Tomas Reyes, in the careful unfamiliar script she'd built like a wall around her life. The letters looked wrong under her hand, a stranger's handwriting wearing her fingers, and she felt the usual small grief of it, and beneath the grief something new and harder: resolve, setting like concrete.

* * *

He stayed the night in the guest tent, refusing nothing, complaining about nothing, and left at first light with the rain still falling, walking out along the muddy track toward the river with two porters and his oilcloth case. At the edge of camp he turned and raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. A marker placed.

Theo stood under the eave of the supply tent and watched him go until the rain dissolved him.

Then she went inside, barred the flap, and knelt beside her cot. She pried up the board at the corner, the one that had loosened and been deliberately not repaired, and drew out the oilskin packet that had traveled with her through six years and four names. Inside, wrapped in waxed cloth, lay her own copy of the original load reports—or rather, the pages of them she'd managed to copy in the week after the accident, before the company sealed the records, before Emilio disappeared, before she understood that copying them had painted a target on her back that she'd been living inside ever since.

She unwrapped them and looked at the initials at the foot of the authorization order. M.V. Countersigned by two directors. Dated four days before the derailment.

Orders to falsify. Signed by Marcus Vane.

She had known it for six years. She had known it the moment he walked into her camp yesterday with his smile and his dry contract, and she had negotiated with him anyway, calmly, professionally, as though he were merely a client—as though her hands weren't shaking somewhere underneath the steadiness where he couldn't see.

He agreed too easily. She turned the phrase over again in the lamplight, looking for the flaw in it, the way she'd once looked for flaws in welds and stress fractures, because finding the flaw was the job, the whole job, the difference between a bridge that stood and one that killed people.

And then she saw it, or thought she did, and the cold went all the way through her.

He hadn't agreed easily because the condition cost him nothing. He'd agreed because the evidence going into her hands first was exactly what he wanted—because a woman holding proof that could hang a director would do anything, risk anything, go anywhere, to protect it. Because the evidence wasn't his leverage against her.

It was his leash.

Outside, the rain kept falling, and the river kept rising, and somewhere downriver a boat was already being loaded with men and supplies for a journey up into the basin—a boat she didn't yet know she would have to hire, with a hull patch she didn't yet know she wouldn't trust, carrying toward her a stranger with a journal and her own reasons for finding the drowned junction.

Theo wrapped the pages, replaced the board, and began, methodically, to make her lists.
