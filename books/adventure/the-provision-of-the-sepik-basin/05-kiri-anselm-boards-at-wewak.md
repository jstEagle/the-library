## 5. Kiri Anselm Boards at Wewak

The rain in Wewak came down like a decision already made, and Kiri Anselm stood under the corrugated awning of the chandlery and watched it hammer the harbor into gray porridge. Six weeks she had been in this country. Six weeks of ferries and cargo decks and sleeping on her father's journal because there was nowhere else safe to put it. And now, finally, the boat.

She had been watching it since dawn—the *Mariana Q*, if that was even its real name, moored at the far end of the fuel dock with its paint peeling in long curls and its hull sitting low in the water, lower than a boat should sit that wasn't yet loaded. A river boat. Shallow draft, flat bottomed, built for water that was more mud than river. The kind of vessel that went where the maps went thin.

Kiri had learned to read boats the way other people read faces. Her father had taught her that much before he taught her anything else: *Look at what a thing is built for, little bird, and you'll know what it's running from.* He'd said it about bridges, mostly. She thought it applied just as well to people.

And the woman who came down the gangplank of the *Mariana Q* at half past nine, alone, in the rain, without an umbrella—Kiri had been watching her for three days now, and everything about her was built for running from something.

She moved like a surveyor. That was the first thing. Kiri had grown up around survey crews, had eaten dinner with them, slept in their camps, listened to their arguments about benchmarks and baselines until the words were as familiar as lullabies. Surveyors walked a particular way, weight forward, eyes doing constant arithmetic with the ground. This woman did it even on a dock, even in rain, her gaze flicking to the waterline marks on the pilings, to the list of the boat, to the sky, cataloguing everything, committing nothing to her face.

She was not beautiful in any way the word usually meant. She was weathered and spare, with dark hair cut short and practical and hands that looked like they had argued with machinery and won more often than not. She wore a canvas jacket gone soft with age and a hat that had been good once. She signed for supplies at the chandlery counter with a name that wasn't hers—Kiri knew this because she had spent forty minutes the previous evening buying rope she didn't need while the clerk, a talkative man named Bruno, filled the silence with gossip.

*Tomas Reyes,* the manifest said. *Engineer. Hired crew going upriver, basin run, don't ask me more because I asked him and he looked at me like I'd asked to marry his sister.*

But Bruno also said—and here Kiri had leaned over the counter and made sure her voice stayed light, curious, harmless—that Reyes had come through Wewak twice before, always alone, always buying instruments, and once, drunk on cheap gin after a long dry stretch inland, he had talked in his sleep in a hotel room next to Bruno's cousin's, and the voice that came through the wall had been a woman's voice saying a name over and over. *Emilio. Emilio.*

Kiri had written the name down that night on the back of a receipt, and then she had sat with it for a long time, because her father's journal had a name in it too, in the margin of the last complete page, in his cramped leftward-slanting hand: *E.S. — she knows. Ask her.*

He never got to ask anyone anything again. That was the whole shape of the disaster, six years old now, worn smooth by official retelling: the survey barge foundered in floodwater near the old junction; four men lost, including the senior surveyor, one Daniel Anselm; the inquiry concluded operator error and negligence on the part of the surveyor himself, whose falsified load reports, the company claimed, had overloaded the barge beyond its rating. Daniel Anselm, dead, could not defend himself, so the company defended itself instead, and the report closed with the tidy finality of a coffin lid.

Kiri had been nineteen. She had spent six years learning to be quiet about it, which was six years longer than she could bear.

Now she watched Tomas Reyes—or whoever she was—finish at the chandlery and stand a moment under the awning, looking out at the rain with an expression Kiri recognized from her own mirror: the look of a person checking whether today was the day the past caught up. Then the woman pulled her hat down and walked out into it, unhurried, as if rain were a country she held citizenship in.

Kiri gave her a count of thirty. Then she bought the rope after all, and followed.

* * *

Getting aboard was the easy part. Boats loading for remote runs took all the hands they could get, and the *Mariana Q*'s deckhand—a wide, sad-eyed man called Ferro who spoke maybe forty words of trade pidgin and none of English—was happy enough to wave a slim foreign woman up the gangplank when she said she had business with the engineer about the upriver contract.

The business was a lie, but a well-built one. Kiri had spent the ferry ride from Madang constructing it plank by plank: she was a medical worker, she said, attached to a mission station up the Karawari, overdue for rotation, willing to pay passage in work. She had a certificate, laminated and genuine, from a first-aid course in Port Moresby two years ago. She had bandages. She had quinine. She had, folded inside her shirt against her skin, wrapped in oilcloth and then in a second layer of oilcloth and then in a strip of her dead mother's bedsheet, the journal.

The engine cover was open amidships when she came aboard, and a small wiry man was buried in it to the shoulders, swearing at it in Spanish with the fluency of long acquaintance. He extracted himself when her shadow crossed the hatch, looked her up and down, and said, in accented but serviceable English:

"You're not cargo."

"I'm hiring on. Medical and general hands. I'm told you're short."

"We're told a lot of things." He wiped his hands on a rag that only redistributed the grease. "Who told you we're short?"

"The dockmaster. The barman at the Belisario. The general opinion of the town." She let a smile touch her mouth and not her eyes. "You're provisioning for a basin run in monsoon season with a crew of five and no medic. Everyone in Wewak knows it. Everyone in Wewak thinks you're either desperate or insane, and they're taking bets on which."

Something moved behind the man's face—not amusement exactly, but a cousin of it. "Elio Cruz," he said. "I own the boat, such as she is. You'll want to talk to Reyes. He's aft, arguing with the chart."

"Arguing with it or threatening it?"

"With his luck, both."

The stern cabin was barely a cabin—a chart table bolted over a bunk, a shelf of instruments in felt-lined cases, a lamp burning despite the daylight because the rain had turned the air the color of dusk. The woman called Reyes stood over the table with both palms flat on a chart of the middle Sepik, and she didn't look up when Kiri stopped in the doorway, which told Kiri she'd heard her on the deck boards and had decided to make her wait.

Kiri waited. She was good at waiting. It was one of the few inheritances her father had left her that couldn't be seized by a company lawyer.

Finally Reyes straightened and turned, and Kiri got the full weight of her attention for the first time, and understood why Bruno's cousin's neighbor had remembered the voice through the wall. It wasn't that the woman's eyes were fierce. It was that they were *careful*—fierce would have been easier to face. These were eyes that measured before they judged, that took in Kiri's boots, her hands, the cut of her jacket, the way she stood balanced against the boat's faint motion, and filed each observation somewhere behind a face that gave back almost nothing.

"You're not my hire," Reyes said. "I didn't post for crew in Wewak."

"No. Your crew posted for you, though. All over town. Which is how I know you're going upriver." Kiri kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice of a reasonable person making a reasonable request. "I'm asking to come along. I can cook, I can pull a line, and I can keep a man alive if the fever takes him or a saw slips. I've done it. There's paper to prove the second part."

"We're not a passenger service."

"I'm not asking to be a passenger. I'm asking to be useful."

"Why?" Reyes said, and the word came out flat and cold as a dropped tool. "Why the basin? Why this run? Nobody comes to this coast asking for the middle Sepik in monsoon season unless they're paid to, and nobody's paying you, because I'm the only contract moving and I haven't hired you."

There it was. Kiri had known the question would come, had rehearsed answers to it on three separate ferries, and every answer she'd built was a lie, and standing here now, under those careful measuring eyes, she found she didn't want to use any of them. Not because she'd grown honest. Because she recognized something. She had spent six years around people lying about her father—company men with their condolences and their settlements and their sealed reports—and she knew the texture of a constructed story the way a seamstress knows cheap cloth. And the woman in front of her was wearing one. It fit her well. But Kiri could see the seams.

So she did the dangerous thing. She told a smaller truth to buy room for the larger one.

"My father worked the basin," she said. "Six years ago. Survey crew."

She watched the change come over Reyes's face, and it was subtle, and it was total. Nothing so crude as a flinch. Just a stillness that spread through her like frost through water, a sudden absolute control of every muscle, the kind of stillness people achieve only by practicing. But Kiri had grown up reading surveyors, and she read this one now, and what she read was: *she knew the name before I say it, she is bracing for the name.*

"Anselm," Kiri said. "Daniel Anselm. Senior surveyor on the basin assessment. My father."

The rain filled the silence, drumming on the deck above, streaming past the porthole in silver ropes. Reyes's hands had gone to the edge of the chart table, and her knuckles were white, and her voice, when it came, was perfectly, deliberately bored.

"The accident," she said. "I remember the reports. I'm sorry for your loss. But I don't see what it has to do with my charter."

*The reports.* Not *the accident*—the reports. As though the two were different things to her. As though she had read them closely enough to distinguish.

Kiri's heart was going hard against the oilcloth bundle under her shirt, hard enough that she half believed the woman could hear it. She made herself breathe. Made herself look tired instead of hungry, grieving instead of dangerous, because grief opened doors and danger closed them.

"It has to do with your charter because everyone says your charter goes up the Karawari toward the old junction," she said. "And my father died at the old junction. And the company that blamed him has spent six years making sure no one goes back there. So either you're working for them—" she let that hang, watched the frost spread deeper, watched something almost like pain get locked away behind the boredom— "or you're not, and either way, I want to see the place where he died. I want to stand on the bank. That's all. That's the whole of it. Passage and a pair of hands, and when we're anywhere near that reach of river, you point me at the bank and I'll get off and walk and rejoin you downstream. I'll sign whatever waiver you want. I'll sign that I was never aboard."

Reyes was silent for a long moment. Outside, someone shouted on the dock, and the shout dissolved into the rain.

"You think the company killed him," Reyes said at last. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't cruel. It sounded almost like a test.

"I think the company wrote a report that made a dead man carry the blame so living men wouldn't have to," Kiri said. "Whether that's killing depends on your definitions. Mine are broad these days."

"And if I say the reports were right? If I say your father made errors and the barge went down for ordinary reasons and there's nothing at that junction but rust and water?"

"Then I'll have stood on the bank and seen it myself, and I'll stop wondering, and wondering is the thing that's killing me. Isn't that worth a berth?" Kiri held the woman's eyes and did not blink. "You want to know why I'm really here. That's why I'm really here. Everything else I'll do on this boat—cooking, lines, medicine—is true too, but it's scaffolding. The building is that I need to see where my father drowned, and I will get there if I have to walk the whole river, and you can either have me aboard where I'm useful or behind you where I'm a stranger in a canoe, and I know which one I'd rather have behind me on a river full of crocodiles."

That earned her something—not a smile, nothing so warm, but a fractional easing around the eyes, the look of a chess player recognizing an opponent who actually plays.

"You argue like a surveyor," Reyes said.

"I was raised by one."

Another silence, shorter. Reyes looked down at the chart, and Kiri watched her thumb move, very slightly, along a blue line toward a place where the ink showed a junction and a spur and, in faded annotation, a symbol Kiri knew: the little triangle-and-cross that meant *structure removed*. Then the thumb lifted, and the moment closed like a door.

"Berth amidships, port side, under the life raft," Reyes said. "You eat last, you stand watches, and you stay off the stern deck when I'm working charts. If you get sick, you get sick quietly. If you ask me questions about the route, I'll put you off at the next mission station, and there won't be another boat for a month. Are those terms acceptable?"

"They're terms," Kiri said. "Acceptable enough."

"Elio will show you the boat. We sail on the morning tide, or what passes for one."

Kiri nodded and turned for the door, and had one foot on the companionway ladder when Reyes spoke again, and her voice had changed—it had dropped its armor entirely, and what was underneath was quiet and rough and very tired.

"Anselm."

Kiri turned.

"Your father's reports," Reyes said, not looking at her, looking at the chart, at the rain, at some middle distance where six years ago presumably lived. "Did the company ever return his personal effects? His field notes, his journals—anything like that?"

And there it was. The question Kiri had crossed an ocean hoping someone would ask, asked by the one person on this coast who must never learn the answer—or must, Kiri corrected herself, must eventually, because the journal's margins said *she knows*, and her father had not been a man who wrote things idly.

"He had a journal," Kiri said carefully. "It went down with the barge. The company listed it among the losses."

Something crossed Reyes's face then, fast, like a fish under brown water—there and gone, unreadable, but Kiri caught the wake of it, and the wake said *liar* as clearly as a shouted word. Whether the lie was Kiri's or the woman's, Kiri couldn't yet tell. Both of them were lying, she realized. Both of them knew the other was lying. And both of them were going to pretend otherwise until the river forced the truth out into the open, the way rivers force everything.

"Shame," Reyes said mildly. "Field journals are worth more than the reports, usually. More honest."

"Yes," Kiri said. "I've heard that."

She went up the ladder into the rain, and Elio Cruz showed her the boat—the bilge pump with its frayed handle, the patched hull section he touched with his toe and avoided with his eyes, the galley that was a stove and a prayer—and Kiri stowed her gear in the berth under the life raft, and lay there in the rocking dark listening to the rain, with the journal against her ribs and the whole conversation replaying behind her eyes.

*Did the company ever return his personal effects?*

Nobody asked that question casually. Nobody asked it at all, unless they needed to know whether the journal still existed—and who had it.

Through the thin bulkhead she heard Reyes come off the stern, heard her low voice conferring with Cruz, heard the word *tide* and the word *fuel* and then, once, briefly, a name spoken so softly Kiri might have imagined it.

*Emilio.*

Kiri closed her eyes and pressed her palm flat against the oilcloth bundle, over the cover her father's hands had worn smooth, and made herself a promise in the dark: *Before this boat reaches the junction, I will know who you are, Tomas Reyes. And you will know what my father wrote about you.*

Above her, footsteps crossed the deck, unhurried, checking mooring lines against the morning tide.

Below her, the patched hull took on the river, drop by patient drop, like a secret being kept.
