## 6. First Water

The basin announced itself before they entered it.

Theo felt it first in the water—a change in the current's temper, the river slowing and widening as if it had begun to forget where it was going. Then the banks changed character: the hard clay gave way to silt the color of old tea, and the forest leaned out over the channel until the trees on either side seemed to be holding hands above them. The air thickened. It was not heat exactly, though the heat was there, but a kind of pressure, as if the whole country were inhaling and refusing to exhale.

She stood at the bow with the chart case open against her hip, one hand shading her eyes, and did not look at Kiri Anselm, who had taken up residence at the rail two arm-lengths away with her father's journal wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like an infant.

"Channel narrows ahead," Elio called from the wheel. "You want to see this."

She did. The chart showed the Sepik proper beginning where the Karawari fed in from the south, and the confluence was marked with the survey company's old notation—*shifting bars, sound annually*—which meant it hadn't been sounded in six years, which meant the river had spent six years doing whatever it pleased. Rivers did that. Rivers were honest that way. They moved what they moved and drowned what they drowned and never signed anything afterward.

That was more than she could say for herself.

"Port side," she said. "The bar will have walked east. It always walks east."

Elio brought the boat around with the unhurried competence she'd hired him for, and the hull scraped something soft and enormous below them—a submerged log, or a bar just forming—and then cleared into deeper water. Behind them the wake folded shut over their passage, and the river looked exactly as it had before they came, as if no boat had ever disturbed it, as if no men had ever died in it.

She checked the depth marks again anyway. Discipline was the only religion she'd kept.

* * *

The first rain came at midday, and it did not arrive so much as descend—a gray curtain walking toward them across the water, visible for a full minute before it hit, the surface of the river pocked and smoking under its advance. Then it was on them and the world contracted to the width of the deck.

"Canvas!" Elio shouted, and the crew moved—Marek and the boy, Dov, hauling the tarpaulins aft while Sarto secured the cargo netting with the exaggerated care of a man performing diligence rather than feeling it. The injured deckhand, Pell, sat propped against the engine housing with his bandaged leg extended, watching the rain come down with the flat expression of a man who had learned not to hope about weather.

Theo got the chart case under cover and stood in the lee of the wheelhouse, water running off her hat brim in ropes, and let herself feel the thing she always felt when the monsoon arrived: relief. Rain meant cover. Rain meant the patrol boats stayed at their moorings and the company's eyes went soft and blind. Six years ago she would have cursed it. Now it felt like a door closing behind her.

"You've done this run before."

Kiri's voice, close, pitched under the rain. She had appeared at Theo's shoulder without crossing any distance Theo could account for, which was going to be a problem.

"I've done rivers," Theo said.

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Theo agreed. "It isn't."

Kiri didn't move. Rain dripped from the end of her nose and she didn't wipe it away, just watched Theo with the patient, terrible attention of someone who had spent months reading a dead man's handwriting and had learned to wait out silences. Her father's journal was under her shirt now, against her skin, and Theo knew because Kiri had told her so on the second night, deliberately, the way you show a stranger your knife—not as threat but as terms.

"The junction," Kiri said. "You knew my father."

"I knew of him."

"He wrote about a junior engineer. In the last entries. Someone who signed the load reports he refused to sign." Kiri's voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it; she wasn't accusing, she was *laying track*, laying it straight at Theo's feet one sentence at a time. "He never gave a name. He just said *she'll understand why I can't*. I've had six years to wonder who *she* was."

The rain filled the pause for her, generous and indifferent.

"Then you can keep wondering," Theo said, and walked forward into it, and hated herself for how easily the mask seated itself, how little friction there was left after six years of practice.

* * *

Elio found the patch at dusk, when the rain thinned to a steady drizzle and the light went the color of pewter.

He came to find Theo where she crouched over the chart in the wheelhouse, marking their position by dead reckoning—the GPS unit was Vane's, and Vane's electronics were a debt she intended never to owe—and his face told her everything before he said a word.

"How bad?"

"Bilge took four inches since noon. Pump's keeping pace." He wiped his hands on a rag that was more grease than cloth. "Barely. The patch I put in at Wewak—I told you it was a stopgap. What I didn't tell you is the planking around it is soft. Rot, or borers, or both. The patch is holding. The *boat* around the patch is giving up."

Theo set down her pencil. "How long."

"Honest answer? Depends on the rain. If we get three dry days, we make the junction and maybe we limp back to Wewak. If the monsoon sets in properly—" He shrugged, a small economical gesture that contained a sinking boat. "I've patched her twice already today. Third time the wood won't take screws."

"Rationing, then. Fuel and food both. And the pump gets priority over everything but navigation."

"Already done." He hesitated, and Elio was not a man who hesitated; hesitation cost him fuel the way idling did. "Theo. You know what's down there. You've known since Wewak. Why are we really going?"

She looked at him. He was fifty-some years of river in a body that had stopped arguing with it, and he had asked the question without accusation, which somehow made it heavier.

"Because someone has to bring up what's down there," she said, "and everyone else who tried is dead."

Elio nodded slowly, as if she'd answered a different question entirely, and maybe she had. "Four inches," he repeated, and went back to his engine.

* * *

They anchored that night in the mouth of a side channel, tied off to two trees that had been drowning for years and stood anyway, gray and patient, their crowns full of roosting birds. Rationing began at supper: half measures of rice, the tinned meat divided with a ruler's fairness, water boiled and doled out by the cup. Dov complained and Marek shut him up. Sarto ate in silence and watched Theo across the fire they weren't supposed to have, and Theo pretended not to see it, and filed it.

Pell's fever was up. Kiri knelt beside him with the lamp and worked with a competence that surprised nobody except, apparently, herself—cleaning the wound, re-splinting, forcing water and willow bark tea into him with the flat authority of a woman who had done harder things than nurse a deckhand through blood poisoning in worse places than a leaking boat.

"You've practiced medicine," Theo said, coming to stand over them.

"I've practiced *not letting people die*," Kiri said. "It's a broader field." She glanced up, lamp-light catching the rain still beaded in her hair. "My father taught me the basics. Field surgery, splints, the fevers. He said a surveyor who couldn't keep her crew alive had no business leading one."

"A surveyor," Theo repeated. Not a question. An examination.

"My father was many things before the company made him a scapegoat. I'm most of them." Kiri tied off the bandage and sat back on her heels. "Your turn. Where did you learn to read water the way you do? Because Tomas Reyes handles a chart like a man who was born to it, and every other thing about him is wrong. The way you stand. The way the crew waits for you to speak first even when Elio's got the wheel. Men don't follow a boatman like that. They follow—"

"Don't."

The word came out sharper than she'd intended, and Kiri heard the sharpness for what it was, and mercifully—for once—let it go. She gathered her kit, settled against the rail within reach of Pell, and closed her eyes as if sleep were a decision she could simply make.

Theo stood at the rail a long time after, listening to the water work against the hull. Four inches. She thought about the planking going soft, the screws losing their grip, the river finding its way in the way rivers always found their way in, patient and impersonal and utterly without malice. The basin didn't hate them. That was the thing no one understood until they'd spent a season inside it. The crocodiles didn't hate them, the rot didn't hate them, the rain didn't hate them. The whole country simply proceeded, and whatever could not proceed with it was removed, quietly, without ceremony, without anyone left behind to hold a grudge.

There were days she envied it.

* * *

The second day the rain held and the channel narrowed, and the navigation became the kind of arithmetic she loved and feared in equal measure: depth against draft, current against fuel, the bar that the chart swore was at marker eleven and the river had clearly relocated. Twice she took the bow line and sounded by hand, the pole punching down through brown water into soft bottom, calling numbers to Elio like a litany. Once the keel kissed mud and slid free again, and nobody breathed until it did.

Kiri appeared at her elbow between soundings.

"The bar's moved east," Kiri said. "Like you told Elio the first morning. How did you know it walks east?"

"Because I helped survey this stretch."

"With my father."

"*Tomas* surveyed this stretch," Theo said, "under that name, six years ago, for a company that erased the survey when it became inconvenient. Ask me something I can answer without digging us both a grave."

Kiri was quiet for a moment. The rain ticked on the canvas overhead. Somewhere in the drowned forest a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"All right," Kiri said finally. "Here's one. When we find the junction—if we find it—what are you going to do when you get there?"

And that, Theo thought, staring at the brown water sliding past the bow, was the one question in the world she couldn't deflect, because she'd spent six years not answering it even to herself. She thought of her brother's name on a company memorial that named the wrong cause. She thought of her own signature on the closure order, ink she could still feel sometimes under her skin like a stain. She thought of a photograph in Vane's coat pocket, a living brother standing in front of a building, dated eight days after the accident that officially killed him.

"I'm going to bring up the truth," she said. "Whatever it costs."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got that doesn't sink us today."

Kiri studied her for a long moment, and then, unexpectedly, nodded—as if *that*, at least, matched something in her father's journal, some note about the junior engineer, *she'll understand why I can't*. "Fine," she said. "But one day soon, Tomas Reyes is going to run out of river to hide behind. And I'd like to meet whoever's underneath before the water does."

She went aft to Pell. Theo stood at the bow and sounded the next reach, and the pole came up dripping, and the numbers she called were true, and the rain came down on all of them equally, and the basin did not care.

* * *

Near evening Elio killed the engine and drifted, listening. Theo joined him at the wheelhouse and heard it a moment later: water, moving fast, somewhere ahead beyond the next bend—a white noise that didn't belong to rain.

"Rapids?" she asked, though the chart said otherwise. The chart said a great many confident things.

"Floodwater coming down from the highlands." Elio's jaw worked. "Early. The monsoon's set in upstream before it's set in here. That means the level's going to rise fast, and everything the charts say about depth and bars is about to become a rumor." He looked at her sideways. "And it means the bilge is going to fight a rising river instead of a flat one. Four inches yesterday. I'll check tonight, but I'm telling you now so you can decide what you decide."

"Which is what?"

"Whether we're going to the junction," Elio said, "or whether we're going to the bottom of it."

Downstream, around the bend, the river spoke in its new voice, low and gathering, and the first stars came out through a tear in the cloud cover and vanished again as if embarrassed to have been seen. Theo stood in the failing light with the chart case against her hip and understood that the wilderness had finished announcing itself and was now simply proceeding—that from here on, every mile would be taken from her, not given.

"We're going to the junction," she said.

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, Kiri stood at the stern rail with the oilcloth bundle in her arms, and she had heard every word.
