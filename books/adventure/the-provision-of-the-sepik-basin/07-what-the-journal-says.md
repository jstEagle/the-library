## 7. What the Journal Says

The rain came down on the canvas awning like a fist on a door that would never be answered. Theo sat with her back against the cargo crates, a tin cup of tea gone cold in her hands, and watched Kiri Anselm unwrap the oilcloth bundle she had carried from Wewak as though it were an infant.

The journal was fat and water-swollen at the corners, its leather cover gone the color of old blood. Kiri held it a moment before opening it, her thumb moving across the embossed initials on the front—*H.A.*—the way a person touches a face they are trying to remember.

"You've had this the whole time," Theo said. Not a question. She had known since the first night aboard, when Kiri slept with the bundle strapped to her wrist like something that might be stolen by the river itself.

"Since they gave me his effects." Kiri's voice was level, but her hands were not. "Six years in a company filing cabinet. They released it when the inquiry closed. Closed," she repeated, tasting the word. "As if an inquiry were a door you could shut."

Elio had rigged the lamp on an ammunition crate between them, its flame bending with every gust that came under the awning. The rest of the crew had rolled into their hammocks or their blankets along the far rail, Sarto nearest the bow, his back turned with a deliberateness that Theo did not trust. The injured deckhand, Pell, slept under a double dose of Elio's cheap analgesic, his bandaged arm propped on a coil of rope. The boat lay against the bank in a side slough where the current couldn't worry the hull patch, and the whole world beyond the canvas was water falling on water.

"Read it," Theo said. "If you're going to read it, read it."

Kiri looked up. In the lamplight her face was all planes and patience, and Theo understood suddenly that this woman had rehearsed this night for six years too—that every mile of river since Wewak had been leading here, to a lamp and a captive audience and a dead man's handwriting.

"I want you to listen," Kiri said. "Not navigate. Not plan. Listen."

* * *

The early entries were almost unbearable in their ordinariness. Load figures. Water levels. A joke about the cook's rice. Her father's hand was small and precise, an engineer's hand, each number set down as if it expected to be audited by God.

*"March 3rd. Barge draws fourteen inches light, twenty-two loaded. Company wants thirty-ton runs on the upper spur. Told Whitmore the trestle at Kilometer 19 won't carry it. He smiled at me. I don't like it when he smiles."*

Theo's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

Whitmore. The name went through her like a fishhook finding flesh. Whitmore had been the basin superintendent—the pleasant, silver-templed man who had signed off on the closure reports six years ago, who had shaken Theo's hand at the memorial service and said, *the company grieves with you*, while his eyes moved over the crowd counting heads.

Kiri turned the page. Rain drummed. Somewhere out in the dark a bird made a sound like a hinge.

*"March 11th. Received memorandum re: revised load certification. Am instructed to record tonnage as certified, not as weighed. Difference on the upper spur runs is roughly forty percent. Asked who authorized. Given a signature. M.V."*

The lamp flame stood very still, as if it too were listening.

M.V.

Marcus Vane. Six years ago he had been a junior operations manager out of the coastal office, hungry and brilliant and already collecting favors the way other men collected debts. Theo had known his initials then the way she knew her own heartbeat—from letters, from requisition forms, from the margins of maps they had once spread across the same table while the monsoon hammered the roof of the survey station and he'd said, laughing, *when this is over, Theo, we'll build lines no one will ever have to close.*

She set the cup down because her hands had begun to shake, and she would not let Kiri see it.

"Go on," she said.

* * *

Kiri read for an hour. The journal's tone changed the way weather changes—not all at once but in a darkening, entry by entry, a man's careful precision curdling into something close to terror.

*"March 20th. Weighed the run myself after hours. Forty-one tons actual, sixty-eight recorded. If the trestle goes under that load it will not bend, it will drop. Wrote to M.V. directly. No reply."*

*"April 2nd. Whitmore has moved my weighing equipment. Says there is no time for duplicate verification. There is always time. That is the entire discipline. There is ALWAYS TIME."*

*"April 9th. The men know. Not the numbers, but the feel of it. Rivas asked me straight out if the barge was safe. I told him yes. God forgive me, I told him yes, because what else does a man say to keep his crew working? What else can you say?"*

Kiri's voice caught on that one, just barely—a hairline crack in poured concrete—and she steadied herself and kept reading, and Theo sat very still and let the words come into her like cold water coming into a hull.

Because Theo knew what happened next. Everyone in the basin knew the shape of it, if not the truth of it. April 17th. The barge *Provision*, overloaded on a run it should never have made, hitting the trestle at Kilometer 19 in rising water. Eleven men. The official finding: pilot error, aggravated by unauthorized excess loading, blame assigned to the survey supervisor—Henrik Anselm—who had, the report said, falsified the weight logs himself to conceal his own negligence.

They had hung it on him. On the one man whose private notes proved he had fought it.

"They never found him," Kiri said now, quietly, closing the journal on her finger to hold the place. "My father. Afterward. The others—they recovered nine of eleven. My father and one deckhand went into the water and the river kept them." She looked at Theo across the lamp. "You were on the recovery crews. That's in your file, what there is of it. Tomas Reyes, salvage laborer, six years in the basin. You pulled bodies out of that channel."

"Yes."

"And you never heard anything? Never saw anything?"

The lie rose in Theo's throat, smooth from long use, and she swallowed it. "I saw the channel," she said. "That's all I'll say tonight."

Something moved behind Kiri's eyes—not anger exactly, or not only anger. Recognition. The look of a woman watching another woman stand at the edge of her own buried thing and refuse to jump.

"There are four entries left," Kiri said. "The last ones. I've read them ten thousand times and I still don't understand them. Maybe you will." She opened the journal again, to pages soft as cloth from handling. "He wrote these the day before."

* * *

*"April 16th. Copied everything tonight. Original weigh logs, the memoranda, the signature page. Mailed one set to my brother in Wewak inside a Bible—no one opens a Bible. Kept one set on my person. If I go down tomorrow, the paper goes down with me or it doesn't, but it will not be in company hands either way."*

Theo's breath left her slowly through her nose. A copy mailed to Wewak. A copy on his body. And somewhere in the flooded dark below Kilometer 19, sealed in the wreck of the barge or sewn into the coat of a drowned man, the third set might still exist—paper surviving where men had not, because paper, unlike men, could wait.

She thought of the strongbox rumor she had chased and dismissed for six years, the story among salvage divers that Anselm had gone down with a locked case chained to his wrist. She had called it a ghost story. Ghost stories were how the basin protected its truths: no one dredges for ghosts.

*"April 16th, later. To whoever reads this. I did not falsify the reports. I refused to falsify them, and so they falsified them without me and put my name on the work. Understand this: the danger was never the river. The river is honest. It was the only honest party in this entire affair. If my name comes to you attached to those deaths, know that the signature above mine on the authorization is the man you should be reading about instead. Initials M.V. Ask him what forty percent of eleven lives weighs."*

The rain filled the silence that followed, vast and indifferent, falling on the slough, on the drowned forest, on the whole breathing green weight of the basin.

Kiri closed the journal. Her hands were steady now; the shaking had passed through and out of her, the way a storm passes and leaves the air scoured. "Initials M.V.," she said. "Say it. Say it out loud, Tomas Reyes, whoever you are. Because I've watched you flinch for two hundred miles of river, and I watched you flinch just now, and I am done being the only person on this boat carrying a name that isn't mine."

"Theo," Theo said.

It came out before she could stop it—six years of discipline undone by lamplight and a dead man's handwriting. Kiri went very still.

"My name is Theo," she said. "Theo Sandoval. I was second engineer on the basin survey. I helped write the closure reports." She heard her own voice as if from underwater, calm and terribly clear. "And I have a copy of the same orders your father described. Signed the same way. I've had it for six years, and I've shown it to no one, and tonight I'm telling you it exists, and that is all I can tell you, because the last person I trusted with the truth used it to hang my brother."

The lamp guttered in a gust. Neither of them moved to shield it.

Kiri looked at her for a long moment, and then, instead of the fury Theo had braced for, she did something worse. She nodded. Slowly, like a surveyor confirming a bearing.

"M.V.," Kiri said. "Vane. The man who hired you."

"The man who hired us."

"Then we aren't running from the company's past." Kiri's voice dropped low, beneath the rain, beneath the hearing of any sleeping man. "We're sailing straight into the middle of it. And he sent us here himself."

Out at the bow, a floorboard creaked—once, softly, the sound of a man shifting his weight in the dark. Sarto's hammock hung empty and swaying, and Theo's eyes went to it and stayed there, and the rope of complicity she had felt tightening around her chest all evening drew one deliberate notch tighter, because whatever Sarto had heard, by morning it would be worth money to someone.

The lamp went out.
