## 8. The Crocodile Bank

Elio Cruz had a rule about rivers: they never lied to you, but they never told you the whole truth either. The Sepik in monsoon season was a liar of a different kind entirely—it told you everything and meant none of it. Brown water moving at the speed of a walking man, carrying whole trees sideways, carrying silt that looked like bottom and wasn't. He'd run this river as a younger man, before his name became something he kept in a tin box under a floorboard in Wewak, and he had promised himself then that he would never again trust water he couldn't see through.

Now he was doing exactly that, with a patched hull, four men who signed nothing, and a woman at the bow who read the current like scripture.

"Left of the snag," Theo called—Tomas Reyes, she was called, but Elio had known within an hour of Wewak that Tomas Reyes was a coat worn over someone else's shoulders. Nobody stood like that. Nobody counted things the way she counted them, her eyes always moving from the waterline to the treeline to the faces of her own crew, measuring, filing away.

He swung the tiller. The boat answered sluggishly, heavier than she should have been with the water coming in slow through the patch aft. He'd stuffed it with tarpaulin and resin in Angoram and told Reyes it would hold to Pagwi. It was holding. That was not the same as holding *well*. Every hour he could feel the give in it, the way a man feels a tooth going bad—not pain yet, just knowledge.

The rain came in the afternoon, the first real weight of it, drumming on the canopy awning until speech became useless. They ran by hand-signals for two hours, Elio reading the channel from the stern while Reyes stood forward with a pole, testing depth where the brown water braided around sandbars that moved overnight. The injured man—no one injured yet, not then—the deckhand Anso sat amidships bailing with a cut kerosene tin, steady as a clock. Sarto smoked under the awning and watched the banks go by with the expression of a man doing sums.

That was the thing about Sarto. Elio had hired him out of Pagwi because he was strong and quiet and knew engines, and only afterward had begun to notice how quiet. Not shy-quiet. Listening-quiet.

The bank came up out of nowhere, which was the Sepik's oldest joke. One moment the sounder said six feet; the next the hull shuddered, ground, and stopped as if the river had closed its hand around them. Elio felt it through his boots—a long grinding crunch, the patch tearing loose somewhere aft with a sound like wet paper.

Everyone came awake at once.

"Silt bar," Reyes said, already moving, already reading the water ahead. "The flood's pushed it across the channel since yesterday. We're on top of it."

"How deep off the starboard side?" Elio asked, though he could guess. The current curled visibly past their beam, maybe three feet deep over the bar, dropping away into darker water beyond. Deep enough. Deep enough for the things that lived in it.

Reyes looked at the water for a long moment. Then at the sky, where the rain was thickening from drumming to roaring. Then at the crew.

"We push her off," she said. "All of us, starboard side, lift and shove on my count. We're light if we're quick."

Nobody moved right away. That was the honest truth of it—nobody moved, because every man aboard had grown up on this river or one like it, and every man knew what the brown water held, and every man was doing the same arithmetic: the boat, the cargo, the pay, the odds.

It was Kiri Anselm who broke the stillness, which surprised Elio. She'd been aboard five days and mostly kept to the shade with her father's journal wrapped in oilcloth, asking questions nobody wanted to answer. Now she set the journal down carefully, took off her boots, and rolled her trousers to the knee.

"The longer we sit," she said, "the more the river piles silt against the hull. By dark we'll be part of the landscape."

"She's right," Reyes said. "Anso, you're lightest—you take the shallows on the bar itself, guide us straight. Everyone else over the starboard gunwale. Elio, keep the engine turning over slow. We want the screw biting the moment she floats."

"And the crocs?" Sarto asked. He said it flatly, not as a joke, and Elio respected him for it even as the question made everyone's stomach drop.

"It's midday rain," Reyes said. "They bask when it's clear and hunt at dusk. Right now they're down in the deep holes staying out of the current. We go now, we go fast, we make noise, and we don't stop for anything." She paused, and Elio saw her decide whether to say the next thing, and say it anyway. "And if anyone goes down, we don't come back for them. You all know what this water is. I won't pretend otherwise and get someone killed trying to be kind."

That was Reyes. That was the cold everyone whispered about. But Elio had watched her long enough to know the cold was load-bearing—it held something up. He put his hand on the throttle.

"On three," she said. "One. Two."

* * *

The water hit him like a fist of warm milk. Waist-deep on the bar, thigh-deep in the scour channel, and the current tried immediately to fold him against the hull. He got his shoulder under the gunwale beside Sarto and Kiri, Reyes at the bow post, Anso out on the bar itself in barely a foot of water, leaning into the hull to steer them straight.

"Push!"

They pushed. The boat ground, shrieked along the silt, gave six inches. Elio's feet sank to the ankle in the bar, and the mud sucked at him, and the current shoved at his chest, and somewhere below the brown surface his mind drew pictures he couldn't stop drawing: the pale snout, the eyes like chips of bottle glass, the way a saltie took a dog off a beach at Angoram when he was nineteen, so fast the dog simply ceased to be a dog.

"Again! All together—*now!*"

The hull groaned, slid, floated free for one glorious second—and then the current took the stern and swung it broadside, and the deeper water on the downstream edge reached up and grabbed them. Elio went under. He came up coughing, lost his grip on the gunwale, found it again. Beside him Sarto was swearing steadily in three languages. Kiri had both arms over the rail, feet kicking in water suddenly too deep to touch, and Reyes was shouting for the engine, and Elio hauled himself half onto the bar and threw his shoulder into the bow and felt the boat begin, at last, to answer.

That was when Anso screamed.

Not a scream of fear. A scream of a man whose leg has been taken to the bone. Elio turned in time to see him go down—not dragged, thank God, not taken, but down, folding into the shallow water with his hands clamped around his left calf, and the water around him bloomed red so fast it seemed impossible, as if the river had been waiting for exactly this color.

"He's hit!" Kiri was already moving, wading toward him through water that Elio's whole body refused to enter, and Reyes moved too, and between them they had Anso up and dragging for the boat while Elio and Sarto pulled it the last few feet into the channel. Anso's calf was opened from knee to ankle, a ragged crescent—glancing blow, a snap and release, the animal deciding he was too big or the water too shallow—and beneath the terror of it Elio registered the luck, the enormous stupid luck, and hated the river for making him grateful.

They got him aboard. They got the boat floating. They got the engine up to full and ran her a half-mile downstream to a high clay bank with no cover on the water side, and there they tied off and knelt around Anso in the rain and did what could be done.

Kiri did most of it. She boiled water on the spirit stove, washed the wound with salt from their stores dissolved in it, stitched with a curved needle from a kit that had clearly crossed oceans before reaching her hands. Her hands never shook. Her face was gray, but her hands were stone. Reyes held Anso's shoulders and talked to him, low and continuous, a stream of words in a language Elio didn't fully catch—Spanish, he thought, old Spanish, lullaby Spanish—and Anso stared up at her and held on to the sound.

Sarto stood apart, under the awning, watching. Not the wound. The women. And then, when he caught Elio looking, the watching shifted smoothly to the rain, as if it had always been the rain.

* * *

By evening the fever had started. Not bad—not yet—but the skin around the stitches had gone hot and tight, and Anso shivered under two blankets in air that made the rest of them sweat. Kiri sat with him, changing compresses, and Reyes stood at the rail looking upstream the way she'd been looking upstream for six days, as if the river were a corridor with a door at the end of it.

Elio found Sarto at the stern, cleaning the engine's fuel filter by lamplight. Good work, careful work. Sarto was good at everything except being invisible, which was the one skill this crew required.

"She knew," Sarto said, without looking up.

"Knew what?"

"What that bar was doing there. Or close enough. She read the water wrong on purpose or she read it right and didn't say." He screwed the filter bowl back on, wiped it. "You've run boats ten years, Cruz. You've seen surveyors' eyes. Hers are surveyors' eyes. She's mapped this basin before."

Elio said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't true, and the true things weren't his to tell.

"And the journal woman." Sarto's voice stayed level, conversational, a man discussing fuel filters. "Her father's name is on the accident report. I read newspapers too, even out here. Anselm. The survey crew that died on the closed lines, six years back. Company blamed the chief surveyor for falsified load reports, and the chief surveyor drowned before he could answer for it." He finally looked up, and his face in the lamplight was open, almost friendly. "So. A disgraced engineer who isn't really an engineer, running a boat upriver to reopen the exact lines where that crew died, hired by a company man who pays triple and shows his face once. And the daughter of the blamed man comes aboard uninvited and nobody puts her ashore." He smiled a little. "I do arithmetic, Cruz. It's how I stay alive. So here's my sum: somebody wants that junction found very badly, and somebody else wants it found first, and we're the shovel in both their hands. I'd like to know which hand holds me."

"You're paid to row," Elio said. "Not to know."

"I'm paid to be quiet. Different thing." Sarto capped the fuel line, stood, stretched. For a moment his silhouette blotted out the lamplight, and Elio saw how big he actually was, how patient. "I'm not threatening anyone, viejo. I'm telling you I can count. And when a man can count, other men find it cheaper to tell him the truth than to let him keep guessing wrong." He patted the engine housing twice, gently, like a horse. "Ask your engineer. Ask her who Vane is, and why the photograph matters, and why we're running flooded channels in monsoon season for a junction that's been dead six years. Ask her tonight. Because tomorrow night, after another day of this river, I'm going to start asking louder—and I'll be asking the crew, not her."

He walked forward along the deck, unhurried, and Elio sat alone with the lamp and the engine ticking as it cooled, and listened to the rain come down on the awning like a crowd that wouldn't disperse.

* * *

Later, near midnight, he brought coffee forward to where Reyes stood the watch alone. She took the cup without surprise, drank, watched the black water slide past the rail. Upstream, lightning worked inside the clouds without ever quite escaping them, silent flashes that lit the basin like a photograph developing: trees, water, trees, water.

"Elio," she said, before he could decide how to begin. "You've been chewing on something since the sandbar. Say it."

He almost laughed. "Sarto's counting," he said. "He's done the sum. He knows who Anselm's father was. He knows the lines are the ones from the accident. He says tomorrow he starts asking the crew instead of you."

She didn't flinch. He'd expected flinch, or fury, or that terrible stillness she did. Instead she nodded slowly, as if a bill had arrived that she'd been expecting.

"Fear does arithmetic," she said quietly. "That's all mutiny is—fear with numbers attached. He's not wrong to count. He's wrong about what the numbers mean." She turned the cup in her hands. "But he'll say it to the others, and the others are tired and scared, and scared men don't want meaning, Elio. They want exits."

"So we give them one?"

"No." She looked at him then, and in the lightning-flash he saw her face plainly: exhausted, resolute, and underneath it all something raw that six years hadn't managed to bury. "We give them the truth. Some of it. Tomorrow. Before he gives them his version." She drained the coffee and handed back the cup. "Wake me at two. And Elio—if Anso's fever climbs, we stop at the next village regardless of schedule. I didn't bring anyone up this river to spend them."

She went aft to her bedroll. Elio stood the rest of the watch alone, listening to the rain and the river and, beneath both, the small persistent sound of the bilge water rising faster than it should, and thought: *one-way vessel.* He'd known it since Angoram. He suspected she'd known it longer.

At two o'clock he woke her, and she rose instantly, fully, as if sleep were just another job she did well—and behind them, unheard by either, Sarto lay awake in the dark, counting.
