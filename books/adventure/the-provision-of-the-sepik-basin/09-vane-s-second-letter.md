## 9. Vane's Second Letter

The supply drop came on the ninth morning, as promised, and it came wrong.

Theo knew it before Elio even finished hauling the oilskin bundle aboard. The arrangement had been specific—Vane's man in Angoram was to leave the crate at the mission dock, weighted and wrapped, and Elio's cousin would ferry it out to the channel marker at dawn. Instead, the bundle had been dropped from the air, or nearly: it lay wedged in the fork of a fallen raintree at the channel's edge, torn at one corner, mud-splattered, as if someone had heaved it from a passing launch without slowing. No note from the cousin. No mark of the mission's wax, which Father Anselm's people had used for every delivery since Wewak.

"Current took it," Elio offered, but he didn't sound like he believed it, and Theo didn't either. The raintree lay upstream of the marker. Nothing floated upstream in the Sepik, not in low water, not in the first flush of the monsoon.

She let the crew break into the food and kerosene first—let them see abundance before she went into the bundle's second layer, the one wrapped in waxed canvas apart from the rest, addressed in a hand she knew better than she wanted to. *T. Reyes.* Not Señor Reyes. Not the informal contraction a stranger might use. Vane wrote her alias the way he had once written her name across the top of a survey report: with a precision that was itself a message. *I know exactly who I am writing to.*

She took the letter to the stern, where the tarpaulin gave her the illusion of privacy, and broke the seal with her thumbnail.

The first letter had been an argument. This one was arithmetic.

*Reyes—*

*By now you'll have seen the channel and understood that my arrangements have changed, as arrangements do when trust is extended and not reciprocated. I want to be plain, because plainness is the courtesy we've always extended one another, even at our worst.*

*The reopened line report—the full survey, the condition assessments, the hydrology, the whole of what you are producing for the company—comes to me. Not through the company's channels. Not through the district office. To me, at the junction, in your own hand. You will understand that I have reasons, and you will not need to be told what they are, because you have always understood how organizations work: the man who carries the paper is the man who owns the paper.*

*If the report reaches anyone else first—if it passes through a company clerk, a district officer, a mission postbox—then the photograph goes to the authorities in Port Moresby within the week, with my affidavit attached. You know what my affidavit would say. You know it would be believed, because I am believed, and your brother is a dead man with a falsified service record, and dead men with falsified records are the easiest kind to convict.*

*I would rather not do this. I remember you as you were, and I find I still prefer that memory to the necessity. But preference is a luxury of men who are not being managed, and neither of us is being managed, Reyes. We are two people who have finally stopped lying about what this is.*

*Come to the junction. Bring the report. Bring the truth, if you can find it, though I suspect you've been carrying it for six years and have only recently admitted the weight.*

*—M.V.*

Theo read it twice. The third time, she didn't read it so much as take it apart, the way she'd take apart a bridge design to find the load path—the way force traveled through a structure, and where it would fail.

*Neither of us is being managed.* That was the sentence. Everything else was scaffolding around it. Vane was telling her she wasn't being managed at the exact moment he was managing her, and the fact that he'd chosen that particular lie told her more than the photograph had. The first letter had offered her a partnership with a knife behind it. This one didn't bother with the partnership. It had dropped the pretense the way the bundle had been dropped from a moving launch—fast, careless of where it landed, certain she'd pick it up anyway.

Because she would. That was the trap's elegance. He wasn't threatening her with the photograph so much as reminding her that the photograph existed, that it sat in whatever strongroom or lawyer's office Vane kept, and that its location was now the center of her life's gravity. Six years of careful orbit around her own secret, and Vane had simply picked up the sun and moved it.

She folded the letter along its creases and looked out at the channel.

The rain had held off three days—Elio called it the monsoon holding its breath—and the basin had used the pause to grow denser, somehow, the sago stands thickening along the banks, the water gone the color of steeped tea, the air so humid that her survey instruments fogged from the inside of their cases. Somewhere ahead, three days' poling if the channels held, lay the drowned junction: rails running into black water, the old trestle rotted to its pilings, and beneath the surface—she was nearly certain now, Kiri's father's journal had confirmed the bearing—whatever remained of the survey barge, and whatever her brother had hidden aboard it before the water took him.

Or before the company did. The two possibilities had stopped being distinguishable years ago.

"You've been reading that letter a long time." Kiri's voice, from the rail. Not accusatory—Kiri Anselm didn't accuse, she observed, and her observations landed like survey pins, precise and impossible to pull out again. "The second reading is always slower than the first. That's when you're not reading it. That's when you're arguing with it."

"Shouldn't you be with the injured man?" Pell had taken a fever off his crocodile-bank wound, low-grade but persistent, and Kiri had appointed herself his nurse with the same unsentimental competence she applied to everything.

"Pell is asleep. Elio is watching him, and Elio watches better than I do, though he'd deny it." Kiri came and stood at the rail beside her, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, not touching. She looked out at the tea-colored water. "Vane again?"

Theo didn't answer, which was an answer.

"Does he know about the journal?" Kiri asked. "About what my father wrote? The initials?"

"He knows the journal exists. I don't know if he knows what's in it." Theo turned the letter over in her hands. "If he knew what was in it, he wouldn't have sent me upriver at all. He'd have sent men to burn the boat."

"Unless burning the boat is what the men are for, and the letter is just to keep you pointed the right direction until they're needed."

Theo looked at her. Kiri was watching the water, her father's journal in its oilcloth slung across her body like a bandolier, and her face was calm in a way Theo recognized because she'd practiced the same calm for six years. It was the calm of a person who had already imagined the worst thing and found she could hold it without dropping anything else.

"You think like an engineer," Theo said.

"I think like the daughter of a man the company needed to be dead. It's the same discipline." Kiri's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "What did the letter say, Theo? Not the words. The shape of it."

*Theo.* Not Reyes. Kiri had started using the real name three days ago, quietly, only when they were alone, and each time it landed in Theo's chest like a struck bell she couldn't stop from ringing. She had almost forgotten the weight of her own name. She had not forgotten how it sounded in a voice that wasn't afraid of it.

"The shape of it," Theo said slowly, "is a funnel. The first letter gave me a choice with a price. This one takes the choice away and keeps the price. He wants the report in his hands at the junction, before anyone else's, and he's tied it to the photograph. And he's—" she stopped, because saying it aloud would make it more true, and it was already true enough. "He's arranged the supply drops. Which means he knows where we are. Which means he's always known where we are."

"Then we've never been ahead of him."

"We've never been *on our own route.* We've been on his." She heard it as she said it, the whole architecture of the last three weeks reassembling itself into something uglier. The contract terms she'd negotiated so carefully in chapter after chapter of argument at Angoram—evidence to her first, the company bypassed, her brother's name cleared before a single rail was relaid—Vane had agreed to all of it too easily, and she'd told herself it was because he wanted the lines open more than he wanted control. She'd told herself a lot of things. The truth was simpler and had been waiting for her the whole time: Vane hadn't agreed because she'd won the negotiation. He'd agreed because the negotiation didn't matter. He had never intended to route the report through anyone but himself, and the letters weren't terms. They were steering. Small corrections, applied early, the way you steered a boat—nothing dramatic, just a hand on the tiller, a few degrees at a time, until the crew looked up and discovered the channel they were in was not the channel they'd charted.

The photograph wasn't leverage. It was a rudder.

"I need to think," she said.

"You need to not burn anything," Kiri said. "That's what you do when a thing frightens you. You burn it. I've watched you look at my father's journal like you're deciding whether it would burn." She said it without heat, which made it worse, and then she did the thing that undid Theo more thoroughly than any argument could have: she put her hand over Theo's hand on the rail, briefly, and took it away, and walked aft to Pell without another word.

Theo stood alone at the stern with the letter and thought about fire.

* * *

She called no meeting. Meetings were how you told a crew there was something to fear. Instead she worked the afternoon as she always worked it—soundings with the pole, notes in the survey book, the small liturgies of competence that kept four frightened men steady—and let the decision assemble itself the way she'd let a bridge design assemble, load by load, until the structure stood or it didn't.

By the time the light went copper over the sago stands, it had stood.

Sarto found her at dusk. She'd been expecting it since the crocodile bank, the way you expect a hairline crack to propagate—Sarto had been asking questions since Wewak, first idly, then not idly, and a man who asked who hired them and why was a man either looking for a cause or looking for a buyer. He came and sat on the cargo hatch across from her, uninvited, and rolled a cigarette with the deliberateness of a man opening negotiations.

"Second letter," he said.

Theo didn't confirm it.

"The first one, you read once and put it away. This one you read three times and then stood at the back of the boat for six hours." He lit the cigarette. "I did six years on the Ramu contracts. I know what letters from men like that look like. They look like that one."

"Like what one?"

"Like a man moving his pieces." Sarto exhaled smoke at the water. "I'm not asking what it says. I'm asking what it changes. Because if it changes nothing, fine. But if it changes what we're carrying, or where, or who it's for at the end—then the men should know, and I'm the one who tells them, because they'll take it from me and they won't take it from you, no offense, Señor Reyes, but you're the one who signed us onto a leaking hull with a stranger aboard and a route nobody's paid to know the end of."

"And if it changes everything?"

Sarto looked at her across the cigarette smoke, and his eyes were flat and patient and older than his face. "Then I'd want to know what the new price is," he said, "and who's paying it, and whether there's a share in it for men who keep their mouths shut and their hands on the pole. Because I've been on jobs where the man holding the contract was never the man holding the money, and the men who figured that out too late are the ones the river keeps."

The words sat between them, and Theo held his gaze and thought: *He's already been bought. He's telling me he's already been bought, or he's telling me he can be.* Vane's man in Wewak—she'd wondered, idly, whether Vane had eyes aboard, because Vane always had eyes aboard; that was the whole lesson of the letter. And Sarto's questions had a direction to them, a current, and currents in the Sepik all ran somewhere.

"I'll tell the men what they need to know when they need to know it," she said. "That's the arrangement. It was the arrangement at Wewak and it's the arrangement now."

"The arrangement at Wewak was made by a man who hadn't gotten a second letter yet." Sarto stood, flicking the cigarette into the water, where it hissed once and died. "Arrangements change, Señor. That's what letters are for." And he went forward to the crew fire, and Theo watched him go and understood that the conversation had not been a warning from Sarto to her.

It had been a report from Sarto to whoever Sarto reported to. *She's read it. She's rattled. She's holding. Ask me again in two days.*

Let him report it, she thought. Let Vane hear that she was rattled and holding. There was a use even for the traitor's arithmetic, if you knew it was being taken.

* * *

Night came down the way it came down in the basin, all at once, the copper light snuffed and the dark rising off the water like something poured. The crew fire burned low. Pell's fever had broken in the evening—Kiri's willow bark and cold compresses, administered with a nurse's flat refusal of drama—and he slept deeply for the first time since the bank. Elio sat at the bow with the pole across his knees, keeping the boat against the current, and Theo went and sat beside him because Elio was the only one aboard who asked nothing.

They sat in silence a while. The frogs ran their endless arithmetic in the reeds.

"He's steering us," she said finally. She didn't have to say who. "The letters. The drops. We're not running our route, Elio. We're running his, and we're running it on his schedule, and I agreed to every degree of it because each one seemed small." She laughed, short and without humor. "Six years I kept myself invisible. I burned a map page rather than let one line of it circulate. And in three weeks this man has turned me into an instrument reading his own hand."

Elio considered this with the unhurried attention he gave to water. "You know what a boatman does," he said at last, "when the current is stronger than the pole?"

"Wait for the bend."

"You don't fight the current. The current wins. You read it. You find where it runs slow along the bank, where the eddies turn back on themselves, where the snags break its force—and you use the current's own water to go where it isn't taking you." He turned his head and looked at her, and in the firelight his face was the face of a man who had survived thirty years of rivers by never once mistaking strength for control. "He thinks the letters are his current. Let him think it. But you're the one holding the pole, señora. And there's one thing his letters can't steer."

"What's that?"

"What's already at the junction." Elio nodded downstream, into the dark. "He can move you like a piece on his board all he wants. But whatever your brother left in that water has been there six years, and it will still be there when you arrive, and he doesn't know what it is. He only knows he wants it. A man who wants something and doesn't know it—" Elio shrugged. "That man is easier to beat than a man who knows. I have seen it. On the river and off it."

Theo looked downstream, into the black, where the channel bent away toward the drowned junction and the barge and whatever her brother's hands had sealed against the water. Vane thought the letters were a tiller. Vane thought the photograph was a rudder. Vane thought she was being managed, and he had even been honest enough to tell her so, in the only sentence in the letter that was true.

But the current didn't know what was under the water it ran over. The current never did.

"Three days," she said. "If the rain holds."

Elio looked up at the sky, where the stars had gone hazy behind a ceiling of cloud that hadn't been there at dusk, and said nothing, and his saying nothing was the loudest answer aboard.

The first drops came an hour later, fat and slow, spotting the survey book open on her knee—blurring the ink of the junction's bearing, the one line on the page she could not afford to lose. She closed the book and held it against her chest, and the rain came on in earnest, and somewhere behind them, far downriver, she imagined Vane reading Sarto's report by lamplight and adjusting his grip on a tiller that was no longer attached to anything.

Let him steer, she thought. The river was about to take the channel charts away from both of them.
