## 10. Night Rain, Open Hands

The storm came down on them like a verdict.

Kiri Anselm felt it first in her teeth—a pressure that had been building all afternoon, the air gone thick and sweet with rot and river, the birds gone silent in the mangroves. Then the sky simply opened. Not rain so much as the river deciding to fall upward, sheets of it hammering the canvas lean-to Elio's men had rigged against two fallen palms, turning the world beyond into a gray roaring nothing.

They had been lucky. That was the word Elio used, spitting tobacco into the floodwater lapping at their boots—*lucky*—because they'd found this shelf of high ground an hour before the channel they'd been navigating turned from brown to white and began swallowing its own banks. The boat was tied to three trees and still groaning against its lines like something alive and unhappy. The hull patch was holding. Barely. Kiri had watched Theo press her palm flat against the wet planking below the waterline for a long moment, as if taking a pulse, before saying nothing at all and walking away.

Now there was nothing to do but wait. The fire hissed under its shelter of split bamboo, half smoke and half light, and the crew huddled in their separate silences—Elio mending net he didn't need mended, the injured deckhand Ferro sleeping off a dose of Kiri's willow bark, Sarto at the edge of the shelter watching the river with an expression Kiri had learned not to trust.

And Theo sat apart, as she always did, knees drawn up, a tin cup of cold coffee untouched in her hands, staring into the rain as though it were a page she was reading.

Six days on the water now. Six days of deflecting questions with a compass bearing or a shrug. Kiri had catalogued every deflection the way her father had taught her to catalogue specimens: *subject avoids mention of the junction; subject reacts to the name Sandoval; subject carries a map she will not unfold in company.* She had come aboard at Wewak certain of one thing—that Tomas Reyes knew what had killed her father—and every mile upriver had only thickened that certainty while telling her nothing she could use.

But tonight the storm had taken something from Theo. Kiri could see it. The woman who moved through the world like a closed ledger sat with her ledger open, and whatever was written inside was bleeding through her stillness.

Kiri crossed the camp quietly, stepping over Ferro's outstretched legs, and lowered herself onto the log beside Theo. Close enough to share warmth. Not so close as to be a claim.

Theo didn't look at her. "You should sleep."

"So should you."

"I don't, much." A ghost of a smile. "Occupational hazard."

"Of which occupation?" Kiri asked softly. "The surveyor's? Or the other one?"

That landed. Kiri watched it land—watched the small muscles around Theo's eyes tighten, watched her set the cup down with exaggerated care, the way a person does when they're buying time.

"You've been waiting six days to ask that," Theo said.

"I've been waiting six years," Kiri said. "I can wait six more. But I'd rather not."

The rain filled the silence between them, drumming on the canvas, streaming off the leaves in ropes. Somewhere across the flooded channel a tree went down with a sound like a door slamming in a very large house.

"My father kept a journal," Kiri said, when she couldn't stand the quiet anymore. "You know that. You've heard me read it. His last entries—the load reports, the initials. M.V." She paused. "There were two sets of handwriting in those reports, did you know that? His, and someone else correcting them. Someone making the numbers lie. He wrote about it the way a man writes when he knows he's already dead and is just deciding how to spend the time he has left." Her voice held steady. She was proud of that. "He died believing the record would save him. That if he wrote down exactly what happened, exactly who ordered it, then someday someone would read it and the truth would be undeniable."

"And instead," Theo said quietly, "the journal spent six years in a trunk."

"Instead the company said he falsified the reports himself. That he was incompetent, or criminal, or both. That the barge went down because of *his* negligence." Kiri heard her own voice crack on the last word and hated it. "They took his name and used it as a lid. Every time anyone asked what happened in the basin, the answer was Anselm. My mother died with that answer in her ears. And the man who actually signed the orders is—" she stopped, because she had never said it aloud, and saying it made it real in a way the journal never had. "Is upriver ahead of us. Waiting."

Theo was looking at her now. Really looking, the way she looked at a stretch of water before committing the boat to it.

"If I tell you something," Theo said, "you'll have it forever. There's no taking it back. No version of events after tonight where you don't know."

"I've spent six years not knowing," Kiri said. "It hasn't been kind to me either."

Theo turned the cup in her hands once, twice. Then she set it down in the mud between them, deliberately, like a woman putting down a tool she no longer trusted herself to hold.

"My name isn't Tomas Reyes."

The rain roared. Kiri waited.

"It was, for about four months, thirty years ago, when my mother remarried and thought a new country needed a new name. But that's not the point." Theo drew a breath, and Kiri saw her gather herself the way she'd seen her gather survey instruments—each piece checked, each piece counted. "My name is Theodora Sandoval. My brother was Rafael Sandoval. And six years ago I signed the order that closed these lines."

The words went through Kiri like cold water finding a crack.

"You—"

"I helped close them. Not just helped. I drew the closure map. I certified the survey that declared the spur unstable, the junction unsafe, the whole eastern basin unfit for traffic. My signature is on the document that turned your father into the official cause of the disaster." Theo's voice was level, but Kiri could hear the cost of the levelness, the way a bridge can be level over a very deep gorge. "Every mark on that map is mine. I burned pages of it for six years rather than let it circulate, and I told myself that was conscience, but it wasn't. It was shame with better posture."

"Why?" It came out of Kiri harder than she intended. "Why would you—your own brother was on that barge. Rafael Sandoval. Everyone said—"

"Everyone said my brother caused it too. Negligent second engineer, poor ballast decisions, the usual." Something moved behind Theo's eyes then, something old and drowned. "Rafael didn't cause anything. Rafael was trying to stop it."

Kiri went very still.

"The load reports were false," Theo said. "Your father found it first—he was honest enough to count twice, which in that company was a fatal virtue. But Rafael found it too, independently. He'd been keeping his own tally of what actually went down the line versus what the paperwork said went down the line, and the difference was enormous. Ore, timber, equipment—declared lost in transit, written off, and vanishing somewhere between the junction and the coast. Somebody was bleeding the line for years, and the reports were how they hid it. Your father confronted the office. Rafael went further. He copied the original reports—the true ones—and sealed them in a strongbox on the survey barge, planning to take them downriver to the regulators himself."

"The barge sank," Kiri whispered.

"The barge was sunk." Theo's jaw tightened. "It went down in weather that shouldn't have troubled it, on a reach every pilot called safe, with Rafael and two of your father's crew aboard. The company report called it an act of God. I was twenty-four years old and I believed them, right up until the night I found the draft of the closure order on my supervisor's desk with the conclusions already written and blank spaces left for the survey data. They weren't asking me to find the truth. They were asking me to sign my name to theirs."

"And you signed."

"And I signed." For the first time her voice failed her, just slightly, and she pressed on through it. "Because the alternative was being the next thing that happened to a Sandoval. Because they showed me what happened to people who counted twice. Because I told myself that if the lines stayed closed, no one would ever go back to the junction, and if no one went back, the strongbox would stay buried, and if the strongbox stayed buried, then at least the truth would survive somewhere, even if it survived in the dark." She laughed, short and terrible. "I built a tomb and called it protection. Six years of invisibility, six years of burning my own work, and I told myself I was keeping the evidence safe. I was keeping myself safe. Those aren't the same thing, Kiri. I'm only just learning they were never the same thing."

Kiri sat with it. The rain hammered. Inside her chest, grief and fury and something dangerously like understanding were all trying to occupy the same space at once.

"You let them blame my father," she said. "You let them blame him for six years while you knew."

"Yes."

"You could have spoken. At any time. One letter, one testimony—"

"To what end?" Theo turned to face her fully, and her eyes in the firelight were wet, whether from rain-spray or otherwise Kiri couldn't tell. "A junior surveyor with no evidence, contradicting a company whose last three witnesses are dead? They'd have buried me beside Rafael and corrected the paperwork by Friday. I told myself I was waiting. I told myself I needed proof. And some of that was even true. But mostly I was afraid, and I dressed the fear up as strategy, and your father's name paid the interest on it every single year." She shook her head slowly. "No. Don't absolve me. I've watched you do the arithmetic in your head for six days and I won't insult you by doing it for you. What I did was wrong. Knowing why doesn't make it right. It just makes it mine."

It was the *mine* that undid Kiri. Not the confession, not even the grief—she had grieved alone in rooms full of sympathetic strangers for six years, and sympathy had always felt like being patted with gloves. But this woman, this closed, competent, infuriating woman, was standing in the open with the worst of herself held out in both hands, refusing to flinch, refusing to explain it smaller than it was.

Kiri realized her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her knees.

"The journal," she said. "My father's journal. The last entry—I've never been able to make sense of it. He wrote: *If I am wrong about the water, forgive me; if I am right about the paper, remember me.* I thought it was a man losing his grip. But it wasn't, was it. He knew the barge was coming. He knew what a sunk barge would mean for his name. He wrote it anyway."

"He was the bravest man on that river," Theo said. "And the company needed him to be the guiltiest one. Those two facts fit together like a key and a lock, and I handed them the lock."

Outside, the storm reached some new register, the thunder no longer following the lightning but living inside it, continuous, the whole sky a struck bell. Elio glanced over once, decided whatever was happening on the log was none of his business, and turned back to his net. Sarto had shifted position—Kiri noted it distantly, the way she noted everything now—closer to the boat, closer to listening range. She filed it away.

"Vane," Kiri said. "M.V. The initials in the margins of the reports. He hired us. He's paying to reopen the very lines he buried."

"He says he holds proof of what happened," Theo said. "A photograph. Evidence that could clear Rafael's name—or destroy it. He showed it to me to bend me onto this boat, and I came, because I am apparently still the woman who signs things she doesn't believe in." Her mouth twisted. "But understand what he's doing, Kiri. He doesn't want the truth out. He wants the truth *priced*. Everything buried in this basin is worth something to someone, and Vane has spent six years being the someone. We're not his partners. We're his crowbar."

"Then we get there first."

"We get there first," Theo agreed, "and we get the strongbox, and we decide—not him, not the company—*we* decide what the truth is worth and where it goes." She paused, and something in her face opened, cautiously, like a shutter testing the wind. "I have a copy of the orders. The falsification orders. I've carried them for six years the way you carry that journal. Two halves of the same crime, sitting ten feet apart on a boat for six days, each of us thinking we were the only one holding anything."

Kiri laughed—it escaped her before she could weigh it, ragged and surprised, half sob. "We're idiots."

"We're thorough," Theo said, and the corner of her mouth lifted, and for a moment, in the firelight and the roar of the rain, she looked young. She looked like someone who had once been easy to love before the world taught her to be legible only in code.

Kiri became aware, suddenly, of the space between them on the log. Six inches of wet wood. It seemed enormous. It seemed like nothing at all.

"You should have told me at Wewak," Kiri said.

"You'd have gutted me and taken the boat."

"I still might."

"No, you won't." Theo said it without arrogance, almost gently. "Because you need me the way I need you. Neither of us gets to the junction alone. Neither of us gets to the truth alone." She hesitated, and then, as if the words cost her more than the confession had: "I'm glad it's you. Of everyone who could have forced their way aboard that boat, I'm glad it was the daughter of the man I helped bury. It means I can't hide from any part of this. It means I have to earn it."

"Do you think you can?"

"I think," Theo said, "that I'm going to spend the rest of my life finding out, and that the finding-out starts tomorrow at first light, and that tonight—" she stopped. Started again. "Tonight I'm tired of being a locked room."

Kiri reached over. Slowly, giving her every chance to move away, she covered Theo's hand where it rested on the log—her fingers cold from the rain-damp, Theo's warm beneath them, both of them steady now, or steadier. Theo looked down at the contact as if it were an instrument reading she didn't know how to interpret. She didn't pull away.

"My father used to say," Kiri murmured, "that the river forgives nothing and forgets nothing, and that the only honest thing you can do on it is tell the truth out loud, because the water hears everything anyway." She tightened her grip, just slightly. "So. The water heard you. So did I. And I'm still here."

For a moment Theo's composure cracked all the way through—not dramatically, not with tears, but with a long shuddering exhale, six years of held breath leaving her body all at once. She turned her hand over beneath Kiri's until their palms met, and held on.

They sat like that while the storm wore itself out against the mangroves, shoulder to shoulder, saying nothing, needing to say nothing. The fire settled. The rain thinned from roar to whisper. And Kiri, drifting at last toward sleep against Theo's shoulder, felt the strangest sensation: that a weight she had carried so long she'd mistaken it for her skeleton had been set down, and that what remained was lighter, and stranger, and terrifyingly like hope.

She was nearly asleep when she felt Theo go rigid beneath her cheek.

Not fear. Something else. Kiri opened her eyes and followed her gaze downriver, where the rain had thinned enough to show the channel—and where, far off through the failing dark, a light was moving. Steady. Deliberate. Too low in the water for a patrol launch, too disciplined for driftwood fishermen.

Someone was coming upriver behind them. Through the teeth of a monsoon.

Beside her, Theo rose without a sound, and Kiri heard her say, barely above the dripping of the leaves:

"Elio. Wake up. We have company."
