## 11. Sarto's Bargain

The rain had stopped an hour before dusk, and the air over the channel lay thick as wet canvas. Theo sat on the overturned supply crate behind the galley tarp, cleaning the grit out of her transit's threads with a rag and a wire brush, because a man who could not trust his instruments had no business trusting anything else. The boat was tied to a stand of flooded sago palms, listing two degrees to port since the grounding on the crocodile bank, and Elio was below with a lamp and a hammer, tapping the hull in slow, diagnostic rhythms that Theo had learned to read like weather: *thud* for sound wood, a hollow knock where the patch was going soft.

She heard Sarto coming before she saw him. That was the thing about Sarto—he never moved quietly when he wanted to be heard, and he never moved quietly by accident. Six years of listening to jungle and river had taught her the difference between noise that meant nothing and noise that meant something, and Sarto's boots on the deck planking meant something tonight.

"Tomas." He came around the tarp with his hands visible, which she noted, and a tin cup in one of them, which she noted harder. "Coffee. It's the last of the good stuff. Thought you'd want it before we're down to the burnt grain."

"Thank you." She didn't take it. She kept working the wire brush through the transit's threads, small precise circles.

He set the cup down on the crate beside her anyway, close enough to be a gesture, far enough to be a courtesy. Then he lowered himself onto the rail across from her, unhurried, and looked out at the water the way men do when they are buying time to arrange their faces.

"The bank today," he said. "That was bad luck."

"Banks always are."

"Bad luck, or bad information." He said it lightly. He turned his head and looked at her with those flat brown eyes that gave away nothing except what they chose to. "We were told the channel ran deep on the eastern side. It doesn't. Somebody knew this water once, or somebody thought they did."

Theo kept her hands moving. Brush, circle, blow the grit clear. Her heartbeat did not change, because she had trained it not to, six years ago, in a company office where a man with a soft voice had explained to her exactly how much trouble a woman could be in if she let her face do her thinking for her.

"Elio knows this water," she said. "Elio's been running the Sepik since before I could read a contour map."

"Elio runs where the money is," Sarto said. "Same as the rest of us."

The tapping below decks paused. Resumed. Somewhere across the channel a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oiling, and then the jungle closed its mouth again.

"What do you want, Sarto?"

"I want to talk about Wewak."

There it was. She had known it would come—had felt it building in him since the second day out, in the way he watched her at the wheel, in the way he counted things: heads, crates, hours, miles. Men who counted were either careful or bought, and Sarto had struck her from the first hour as too careful to be honest and too honest-looking to be safe.

"What about Wewak?" she said.

"You hired us fast," he said. "Too fast. A survey job upriver in monsoon season, off the books, cash against delivery—and you didn't haggle. I've worked for men who didn't haggle. They weren't paying with their own money." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse than anything sharp he might have said. "And you don't move like a Tomas Reyes. Reyes is a good name—it's everywhere, it's nothing—but you hold your tools like they cost you something once. You check the sky every twenty minutes like you owe it money. And when the deckhand went into the water at the bank, you were in after him before Elio finished shouting, and you swam like a woman who'd decided already how she'd die."

Theo set down the wire brush. She wiped her fingers on the rag, slowly, and folded the rag, and put it in her pocket, and all of these motions were real motions doing real work while the rest of her stood very still inside.

"And what do you conclude from my swimming?" she asked.

"That whoever you are, you're worth more than the wage you're paying us." He smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, which somehow made it more dangerous. "And that somebody else is paying too."

The river said nothing. Below decks, the tapping had stopped altogether, and Theo wondered whether Elio was listening or whether the silence was only the hull being honest with him.

"If you've got something to say," she said, "say it."

"I've been writing to Vane since Wewak."

He said it plainly, without flourish, the way a man lays a card face-up on a table when he has already counted the other player's hand. Theo felt the words go through her like cold water through cloth—not pain, exactly, but the sudden full knowledge of how much of her had been soaked in it without her noticing.

She had suspected. Of course she had suspected; she suspected everyone, that was the whole architecture of her life. But suspicion was a wall you built, and confirmation was the moment you learned the wall had a door in it all along, and someone had been walking through it every night while you slept.

"How often?" she asked. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"Every stop where there was a post. Wewak, Angoram, the mission station." He shrugged. "Positions, headcount, morale. What the crew grumbled about. Whether the boat would make it." He paused, and the pause had weight in it, deliberate as ballast. "Whether you'd found anything."

"And what have you told him?"

"That we're behind schedule. That the hull is failing. That you keep your own counsel and sleep four hours a night and sit up reading charts nobody needs to read twice." He tilted his head. "I tell him true things, mostly. Lies are work, and I'm paid to observe, not to invent."

"Paid how much?"

"Enough." He waved it off, as if the figure embarrassed them both. "Look—I'm not here to threaten you, Tomas. If I wanted to hurt you, I'd have sent one letter instead of five. I'm here because I've done the arithmetic, and the arithmetic says you and I want the same thing."

"Do we."

"You know what's at that junction." It wasn't a question. He watched her the way a fisherman watches a line. "Vane wants whatever's there—the papers, the proof, whatever it is that makes a man spend this kind of money chasing a dead line through a drowned swamp. And you—" he gestured at her, at the transit, at the whole careful shape of her invented life— "you wouldn't be upriver in monsoon season unless what's there matters to you more than staying invisible does. Which means it's worth a great deal. Which means there's a share in it."

"There's no cargo, Sarto. There's paper."

"Paper's the most valuable cargo in the world. Ask any government." He leaned back against the rail, arms crossed now, comfortable, a man settling in for a negotiation he believed he'd already won. "Here's my offer. I keep writing my letters. Nice boring letters—positions, weather, morale. Nothing about what we find. Nothing about what you carry out. In exchange, when we reach the junction and you take whatever's there, I get a cut. A real one. Enough that I can stop being a deckhand on other people's secrets and buy myself a life somewhere the mail doesn't find me."

"And if I refuse?"

He spread his hands. "Then I write tomorrow's letter honestly. And Vane—whoever he is to you, and I think he's more than a client—knows everything I know. Which, by then, will include whatever we find." His voice softened again, and that softness was the sharpest thing about him. "I don't want that. You don't want that. Nobody on this boat wants that. So let's not pretend we have a disagreement."

* * *

After he left—unhurried, finishing his coffee on the way, a man with no fear of turning his back to her, which told her he was confident or stupid or both—Theo sat on the crate until the light went out of the channel and the frogs started their evening machinery.

She took inventory of herself the way she took inventory of supplies: coldly, completely, without flinching at shortfalls.

Sarto had been Vane's eyes since Wewak. Which meant Vane had known their position within a day's accuracy for the entire journey. Which meant the supply drops had arrived not because Vane was generous but because Vane was steering them—keeping the boat fed just well enough to keep moving, keeping the crew just loyal enough to keep rowing. She had told herself in Angoram that the ease of their resupply was luck. It had been a leash.

And the grounding on the bank. Had that been Sarto's doing? No—he wasn't navigator. But he'd have reported the delay, the injury, the fraying tempers, and Vane would have known precisely how much pressure the crew could bear before it broke. Every misfortune she'd suffered had been converted, within days, into leverage in another man's hands.

So: the enemy was not ahead of her on the river. The enemy was sleeping eight feet from her, under the awning, snoring gently, keeping meticulous mental notes.

Her first instinct was the old instinct—six years old, worn smooth as river stone. Cut him out. Put him ashore at the next village with his pay and his letters and let Vane lose his eyes. Or quieter: the basin swallowed men. Accidents happened in crocodile water. She could end the problem tonight with a length of rope and the current, and no one would ever—

No. She caught the thought and held it up to the light and looked at it, because she made herself look at every such thought, that was her discipline, that was the tax she paid for what she'd done six years ago. She had already buried one truth in this basin by telling herself it was necessary. She was not going to add a drowned man to the ledger and call it navigation.

Besides—and here was the cold clarity arriving, the part of her that had survived boardrooms and inquests and six years of erasure—Sarto alive was useful. Sarto alive was a channel that ran both ways. He wrote to Vane; therefore she could write to Vane. Every letter Sarto sent was a letter she could shape, if she fed him the right observations. A spy who believed himself undetected was not a threat. He was an instrument, and instruments could be played.

He wanted a cut. Fine. She would promise him a cut. She would promise him the moon and the junction both, in the warm reasonable voice of a woman who had learned from masters how to make agreement sound like friendship. And then she would plan around him the way water plans around a stone—never fighting it, always flowing past it, until the stone woke up one morning standing in dry ground wondering where the river had gone.

What would she feed him? Positions slightly wrong. Progress slightly slow. Let Vane believe he had days of margin he did not have. When they neared the junction, she would give Sarto a false picture of what she carried and where she kept it, so that whatever Vane planned could arrive at the wrong place at the wrong time. And Kiri's half of the journal, and her own copy of the orders—they would travel in no pocket Sarto had ever seen her touch.

She thought of Kiri then, sitting in the bow that evening with the lamplight on her father's journal, reading entries aloud in her low careful voice while the crew pretended not to listen and all of them listened. Kiri, who had boarded at Wewak uninvited and forced her way into this voyage with nothing but a dead man's notebook and a spine like greenheart timber. Kiri, whose father wore the blame that belonged to men with offices and initials.

If Sarto was reporting, then Vane knew about Kiri too. Knew she was aboard, knew what she carried, knew that the daughter of the scapegoat was sailing straight toward the place where the lie had been manufactured. Vane would be factoring her in. Vane might already be planning around her the way Theo was planning around Sarto.

The thought landed in Theo's chest with a weight she hadn't expected, and she examined that too, because it deserved examination. It wasn't just duty anymore. Somewhere between the storm and the confession and the night rain with open hands, this had stopped being a mission with a passenger attached. Love—she didn't name it, wouldn't name it yet, but she knew its weight the way she knew the weight of the transit in her hands—love had become a claimant alongside duty and survival, and now Sarto's bargain had introduced a fourth: the plain animal need to see everyone on this boat reach the junction alive, including the fool who was selling them.

Three claims pulling three directions, and a fourth arriving. She almost laughed. This was how it had been six years ago, too—every loyalty pulling, every choice costing, and her brother at the center of it, believing that the truth could simply be carried to the right people and set down like a parcel. He had believed the system worked. The system had eaten him and blamed a dead surveyor for the meal.

Not this time. This time the truth would travel in pieces, in pockets, in hands Vane couldn't count. This time she would use the man who spied on her to blind the man who owned him. And if it cost her the cut Sarto wanted, or the photograph Vane held, or even the thin gray safety of her own anonymity—then it cost, and she would pay, because she had finally understood that invisibility was just another word for letting the buried stay buried.

Below decks, Elio resumed his tapping. Thud. Thud. Hollow knock. Thud.

Theo picked up the coffee Sarto had left. It had gone lukewarm, and it was, as promised, the last of the good stuff, and it tasted faintly of tin. She drank it anyway. Waste nothing; trust nothing; waste nothing twice.

Tomorrow she would find Sarto alone and shake his hand and give him his cut—a number generous enough to be believable, vague enough to be deniable—and watch his relief, and log his relief as data. Tomorrow she would start feeding the letters.

Tonight she sat with the transit across her knees and listened to the hull being honest, and somewhere upstream, dark and patient, the drowned junction waited with its rusted rails and its black water and whatever her brother had died trying to lift into the light.

Three days, if the rain held. Two, if it didn't.

And in the morning, when she checked the chart case where she always checked the chart case—in plain sight, because the best hiding place was the one your watcher had already catalogued—she would move the strongbox key to the lining of her boot, and smile at Sarto across the breakfast fire, and begin.
