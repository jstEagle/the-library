## 12. The Drowned Junction Ahead

Elio Cruz learned boats the way other men learned prayers—by repetition, in the dark, with something at stake. His father had taught him to read a hull by sound before he'd taught him to read words: press your palm flat against the planking, knock with two knuckles, and listen. Sound wood answered with a round, full note, like a drum wrapped in cloth. Rotten wood answered hollow, or not at all.

He had been knocking his way down the *Santa Rosa* every morning since the crocodile bank, and for six days the answers had been getting worse.

Now, anchored in the lee of a drowned tree island while the crew slept off the midday heat, he knelt in the bilge with a lamp between his knees and both palms flat against the patch—the one he'd sworn was good for another season when Theo Sandoval had looked at it in Wewak with those gray eyes that missed nothing and said nothing either. The canvas-and-tar patch had been laid over a seam split by an old grounding, before Elio's time, before the boat had come to him half-drowned in a debt he'd never fully understood. It had held through two monsoons of coastal work. It was not holding now.

The seam had opened another finger's width. The tar was sweating out of it in long dark ropes that curled in the bilgewater like hair. When he pressed, water came up through the weave in a fine cold pulse, patient as anything in this country. He put his mouth close and tasted it. River, not rain. Coming *in*.

He sat back on his heels and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for three days.

The bilge pump ran maybe four minutes in the hour now to keep them even. That was with the river low between squalls. In open water, in wind, in the channel current they'd be fighting for the last stretch upriver, the boat would ship more than the pump could answer. He knew the numbers the way he knew his own pulse. The *Santa Rosa* would carry them to the junction.

She would not carry them home.

Elio blew out the lamp and climbed up into the light, blinking, and found Tomas Reyes standing at the rail watching him—not the crew, not the river, him—with her arms folded and that stillness she wore like other men wore weapons.

"Well?" she said.

"You knew I was under there."

"I heard you knocking." She came toward him along the gunwale, stepping over the coiled line without looking at it, the way she stepped over everything, as if her feet had memorized the boat before her eyes had. "How bad."

He could have lied. Half his life was lies of convenience, small ones, the kind a man tells so the day keeps its shape. But there were two people on this boat he didn't lie to, and one of them was asleep under an oilcloth aft with a fever, and the other was looking at him now.

"She'll get us there," he said. "Three days, if the rain holds off. Two and a half if we run the night stretches and I don't sleep, which I won't." He wiped his hands on his thighs, leaving dark streaks. "But Reyes. Listen to me the way you listen to everything else. She will not get us back. Not the way she is. Not patched, not nursed, not prayed over. The seam's gone past what tar will hold. We're riding a promise the river already knows is empty."

Theo didn't move. He watched her take it in—the way he'd seen her take in bad news before, like a woman reading a telegram in a language she'd hoped to forget. Her jaw worked once, sideways.

"How far back to the last usable haul-out?"

"Two days. If we turn now. A day and a half if we push and don't care what we break." He gestured upstream, where the channel bent north around a wall of green so dense it looked poured rather than grown. Past that bend, according to the chart she kept folded inside her shirt, lay the drowned junction—rails running into black water, the old barge, whatever truth had been sitting at the bottom of the Sepik for six years like a stone in a boot. "After tomorrow, turning back costs more than going on. After the day after, there's no turning. The swamp closes behind us. You saw what it did to Sarto's shortcut."

Sarto. Even his name sat wrong in Elio's mouth these days. The man had gone quiet since the bank, quiet in the particular way of a man counting something.

Theo turned to face the current. The river here was wide and brown-green and moving with the slow enormous patience of all water in this basin, carrying whole trees on its back like driftwood kings. Rain stood in the air to the west, a gray curtain that hadn't yet decided to fall.

"If I say turn," she said, "will the men go?"

"Rosa will. He wants to go now. The boy—" Elio shrugged. "The boy goes where you point. He thinks you're a saint or a ghost, I haven't decided which he means."

"And Kiri?"

"Kiri Anselm," Elio said slowly, "would swim up this river on her own back before she'd let you turn around. Don't ask me how I know. Ask yourself why you already know it too."

Something moved across Theo's face then, quick as a fish under the surface—there and gone, but he'd been reading water his whole life and he read faces the same way. She wasn't surprised. She was *confirmed*. As if she'd been carrying the knowledge of her own decision for days and had only been waiting for someone else to make it official.

"Elio," she said. "If I told you to turn this boat around, would you?"

He thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to himself was a luxury he'd never been able to afford. He thought about the hull under his palms, the fevered man aft, the money Vane had promised and the kind of man who promised money like that. He thought about the journal Kiri Anselm read aloud by lamplight, her father's careful hand describing orders signed with initials that made the lamplight seem suddenly very thin. He thought about six years of hauling cargo for men who paid cash because cash left no trail, and how tired he was of being a man the world had agreed not to write down.

"No," he said. "I'd tell you the boat's a one-way vessel and that I've known it since Wewak, and then I'd keep steering."

"Why?" It wasn't challenge in her voice. It was something rawer. "You've got no brother in this. No name to clear. You could be in Madang by the end of the month with your pockets full."

Elio leaned on the rail beside her and looked at the water. A log the size of a church pew went by, riding high, trailing roots like a drowned man's hair.

"My father had a saying," he said. "A boat that only ever goes where it's safe isn't a boat. It's furniture." He tapped the rail. "This boat has been furniture for six years. Coastal runs, safe loads, safe water, me keeping her alive and her keeping me invisible, and neither of us going anywhere that mattered." He glanced at her. "You know what that's like. Better than anyone on this deck, I think. Living someplace that keeps you and doesn't count you."

Theo said nothing, but her silence had a different weight than usual. Less armor in it.

"So no," he said again. "I wouldn't turn. I'd want it said plainly first, though. Out loud, to everyone. Because there are five people on this boat and one of them is burning up with fever, and when the *Santa Rosa* finally gives—and she will give, Reyes, it might be at the junction and it might be forty minutes after—we are all going to be standing in the middle of the Sepik Basin with whatever we can carry and nothing else. They should choose that with their eyes open. Same as you're choosing it."

"They'll choose it."

"You're sure."

"I'm sure of Rosa and the boy." She paused. "Kiri chose before she ever boarded."

"And Sarto?"

The pause this time was longer, and Elio watched her decide, in real time, how much to give him. He'd known her eight days and felt like he'd known her eight years, and in all that time she had never once lied to him outright—but she rationed truth like the drinking water, and he'd learned to notice the measure.

"Sarto asked me yesterday who hired us," she said finally. "And what the junction holds. And whether the pay changes if it holds something worth more than survey reports." Her voice stayed level, but her hand had found the rail and gripped it, knuckles pale under the brown. "I told him what I tell everyone. Reports. Salvage rights. Nothing else."

"Do you believe him?"

"I believe a man who asks those questions has already decided which side of the boat he's standing on. I believe he's waiting to see which way the current goes before he jumps." She looked at Elio. "So no. I don't trust him. But I need his arms until the junction, and he needs my pay until the junction, and that arithmetic holds for three more days. After that—" She let it hang.

"After that the boat sinks and everyone's math changes," Elio finished.

"Yes."

* * *

He called them together at dusk, when the light went copper and the mosquitoes rose off the water in their evening columns. Rosa sat against the mast with his arm bound and his fever breaking at last into sweat; the boy, Ferro, crouched at his elbow like a dog that had adopted a dying man and refused to consider the alternative. Kiri Anselm stood apart as always, journal wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and Sarto lounged by the stern with his knife out, cleaning his nails, listening with the particular attention of a man who never admitted to listening.

Elio told it plain, the way he'd promised. The hull. The seam. Three days up, no days back. What it would mean when the water came up through the floorboards for the last time—swimming, wading, carrying, wet bedrolls and wet powder and a hundred miles of country that ate survey crews whole. He watched their faces while he talked. Rosa closed his eyes, not in despair but like a man doing sums. Ferro looked at Theo. Kiri looked at Theo. Sarto looked at the water and smiled at something private in it.

Then Theo spoke, and Elio heard what the others heard: not a speech, not a plea. Just facts, laid out flat, and underneath them the thing she almost never showed—that she would be in the water with them, that she had no exit she wasn't willing to burn.

"We go to the junction," she said. "There's evidence at the bottom of it that six people died to protect and one company spent six years drowning. I'm not asking any of you to care about that. I'm telling you the boat is one-way, the pay is doubled, and the walk out is real but hard. Anyone who wants off at the mission station tomorrow morning can go with my blessing and a month's wages. No questions. No shame."

Silence. The river slapped the hull. Somewhere across the water a bird screamed once and quit.

Rosa opened his eyes. "My village is two days from that junction," he said hoarsely. "My mother's sister married a man who worked the survey. Nobody ever told us what happened to them. Just stopped writing." He shifted his bandaged arm. "I'm coming."

Ferro nodded so hard his whole body nodded with him.

Kiri Anselm said only, "I was always coming," and looked at Theo, and the look between them lasted a half-second longer than looks between strangers last, and Elio filed it away with the other things he'd filed away—her initials in the journal, the photograph Theo burned pages to avoid thinking about, the way the two women stood facing the same direction even when they weren't speaking.

Which left Sarto.

Sarto flipped his knife closed and smiled his easy smile, the one that had gotten him fed at a hundred fires he hadn't built. "One-way boat, eh." He laughed softly. "Crazy gringa and her crazy river. Fine. I'm in. Somebody's got to keep you all alive out there, and clearly it isn't going to be the boatman."

Elio caught Theo's eye. She gave nothing back. But later, when the others had gone below and the two of them stood at the rail in the first spatter of evening rain, she said quietly:

"He stays where I can see him. Forward watch, alone. Away from the strongbox, away from the charts."

"You think he'll jump."

"I think men like Sarto don't jump early. They jump when the price peaks." She turned her collar up against the rain. "And the price peaks at the junction."

* * *

They ran through the night. Elio took the wheel until midnight, then woke Ferro and slept two hours sitting up with his hand resting on the hull, feeling her talk to him through the wood—the engine's tremor, the water's pressure, the seam whispering its one word over and over. *Soon. Soon. Soon.*

Near dawn the rain stopped altogether, which frightened him more than a storm would have. The sky went a strange bruised yellow in the east, and the air lay on the river like a lid. Old-timers called this weather *el silencio del cobro*—the silence before the collection. The monsoon didn't trickle back after a break like this. It arrived like a debt collector.

Theo came forward as the light strengthened, chart in hand, and stood beside him at the wheel.

"Show me," she said.

He pointed with his chin. "That bend puts us in the old navigation cut by noon tomorrow. Junction's half a day past that, if the cut isn't silted worse than your chart says. Three days total, like I told you. But Reyes—" he lowered his voice, though the nearest sleeper was twenty feet away, "—look at that sky. That's not holding off three days. That's not holding off two. When it breaks, it breaks on top of us, and the cut becomes a rapids and the junction becomes a lake."

"Then we run the nights."

"With a fevered man, a hull that's bleeding, and a forward watch you don't trust." He shook his head slowly. "I can do it. I've done stupider for less reason. But understand what you're choosing. There's no version of this where the *Santa Rosa* sees home again. Whatever happens at that junction, we walk out. Everything after today is walking."

Theo was quiet a long moment. Rain-yellow light lay across her face, and for once she didn't look like a woman managing a crew or outrunning a past. She looked tired, and young underneath the tiredness, and certain in the way that costs the most.

"I spent six years making sure nothing I touched could be traced back to me," she said. "No letters. No signatures. No routes twice. I thought invisibility was safety." She looked down at the chart in her hands—at the closed lines drawn in her own careful ink, the marks she'd burned pages to keep private. "It was just a slower way of drowning. At least this way"—she folded the chart and put it away inside her shirt, over her heart, where she kept it—"this way I drown pointing at something."

Elio put his hand on the wheel and brought them around the bend, into the yellow light, upriver.

Behind them, far back along the channel they'd left, a smudge of smoke rose where no camp of theirs was—a thread of gray against the green, deliberate, signal-height. He saw it in the corner of his eye and said nothing yet, only marked it, and marked also that Theo had gone very still at the wheel, which meant she'd seen it too.

Three days to the junction. One-way vessel. And somebody, somewhere behind them, was lighting fires tall enough to be read from a patrol boat.
