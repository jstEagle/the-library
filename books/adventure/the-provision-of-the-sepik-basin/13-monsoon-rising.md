## 13. Monsoon Rising

The storm did not come when Elio said it would. It came early, and it came from the wrong direction.

Kiri felt it first in her teeth—a pressure behind her jaw like a tooth about to abscess—and then in the water itself. All evening the river had been running brown and swollen, but around the second hour of darkness it changed its voice. The low mutter of current against the hull deepened into something with intent in it, and the rain that had been falling all day in a fine gray curtain thickened until it was less weather than substance, a second river falling out of the sky to meet the first.

"Get the cargo lashed," Theo said, and her voice carried through the downpour without rising, which was somehow worse than shouting. "Elio, take us into the channel before it comes up another hand's width. We ride it through tonight or we lose the boat on the bank."

"The channel floods at night," Elio said. He had his hands on the tiller and his eyes on the black water ahead, where the banks had dissolved into an undifferentiated dark that might have been forest or might have been open floodplain. "That's what I told you. That's why we were supposed to be past it by now."

"We're not past it."

"No," Elio said. "We're not."

Kiri crouched under the tarpaulin at the stern, one arm braced against the gunwale, and watched Theo Sandoval work. She had been watching the woman for days now—watching the way she moved through the boat as though she had memorized every plank, the way she spoke to the crew without wasting a word, the way she never once looked back downstream, toward the life she had left on the bank at Wewak. Kiri had come aboard believing she understood this woman: a cold instrument, a keeper of secrets, possibly a murderer by proxy if half of what her father's journal implied was true. She had expected to hate her, or at least to keep her hatred close and warm, like a coal carried in a tin.

What she had not expected was this: Theo Sandoval in the rain, soaked to the skin, hauling crates alongside men who had no reason to love her, because there was no other way through the night.

* * *

The channel announced itself not by sight but by sound—a long, hollow rushing, like breath drawn through a reed—and then the boat swung into it and the world tilted. The current took them sideways before Elio could correct, and for three or four seconds Kiri felt the hull skating across water that was moving faster than they were, the whole vessel slewing like a leaf on a spillway. Somewhere forward, Sarto swore in two languages. The injured man, Pellam, moaned from beneath the tarp where they'd wedged him among the crates, his splinted leg knocking against the timber with each lurch.

"Hold him," Kiri said, and found herself already crawling aft toward the sound of his pain, though every instinct in her body wanted to hold still and let the boat carry her.

Pellam had been hurt nine days ago on the silt bank—the crocodile water, the rope, the way he'd thrown himself under the hull to lever it free rather than watch the boat grind open. The wound in his calf had looked clean enough at first. It no longer looked clean. In the lamplight that afternoon Kiri had seen the red streaks climbing his shin like ivy, and the skin around the gash gone tight and shining, and she had done what her father had taught her to do with field infections when she was twelve years old and trailing him through survey camps: opened it, drained it, packed it with the last of their carbolic, and prayed to nothing in particular that the fever would break.

It hadn't broken. It had climbed. By dusk his skin was hot as a sun-struck rail and he was talking to people who weren't there—his mother, mostly, and once, terribly, a company paymaster, apologizing for a ledger error Kiri doubted had ever existed.

Now, in the storm, with the boat bucking through flooded country, she knelt over him and held his shoulders against the deck while the rain hammered the tarpaulin inches above her head.

"He's burning," she called forward. "He needs to be dry. He needs to be still."

"He needs to be alive," Theo said, appearing beside her out of the dark as though the boat had produced her from its own timbers. Water ran off her chin. Her hair was plastered flat and she didn't touch it. "Can you keep him alive?"

"I can try."

"Then try. I'll get you dry ground for the packing." And she was gone again, forward, into the noise.

Kiri looked after her and felt something shift in her chest that she refused, for the moment, to name.

* * *

They grounded twice in the next hour. The first time was gentle—a shudder, a scrape, the hull settling into mud that had been riverbed a week ago and was now something between. The second time was not gentle. They struck something submerged—a fence post, a deadfall, a drowned cart—and the impact threw Kiri full-length across Pellam's chest, drove the air out of her, and left them both gasping in the dark while the boat slewed and Elio fought the tiller and the rain came down like judgment.

"Off!" Theo's voice, very close. "Everyone off, walk her loose—we're aground on soft bottom, push!"

They went over the side into water that came to Kiri's waist and then, in a hole, to her chest. The current pulled at her legs with hands. The rain filled her mouth when she breathed. She got a shoulder under the gunwale because Pellam couldn't walk and someone had to hold the boat off the snag, and she pushed, and the boat did not move, and she pushed again, and her feet found no purchase in the silt, and for one long moment she understood exactly how a person drowned in a flooded channel at night: not dramatically, but tiredly, one failed push at a time.

Then Theo was beside her in the water—not directing from the deck, not standing on the gunwale giving orders, but hip-deep in the same current, shoulder set against the same hull, her boots lost somewhere in the mud, her face a pale blur in the rain.

"Together," Theo said. "On my count. One—"

They pushed. The boat groaned, slid a foot, caught again.

"Again. One—"

This time it came free all at once, so suddenly that Kiri staggered and would have gone down if Theo's hand hadn't closed on her arm—hard, ungentle, entirely certain—and hauled her along the hull to the stern line.

"Climb," Theo said. "I'll pass him up."

"You can't lift him alone."

"I'm not going to lift him alone. You're going to climb up and pull, and I'm going to push, and then you're both going to stay on that deck until we're through." A pause, barely a beat, and then, quieter, almost lost in the rain: "Please."

Kiri climbed.

* * *

They made the far end of the channel near dawn, or what would have been dawn if the monsoon had permitted such a thing. The sky stayed the color of wet slate. The rain slackened from torrential to merely relentless, and the crew—what remained of them, five bodies and a boat held together by prayer and Elio Cruz's patchwork—collapsed onto whatever dry-ish surface presented itself and did not move.

Except Kiri. Because Pellam was dying.

She knew the signs now the way she knew the signs of a failing bridge abutment, her father having taught her that too: the fever that wouldn't crest, the pulse running thin and fast as a bird's, the lips cracking, the eyes rolling back into talk. The red streaks had reached his knee. When she unwrapped the dressing in the gray light, the smell rose up sweet and rotten, and she had to turn her head and breathe through her mouth and make herself look anyway.

"It's in the bone," she said quietly, to no one, to herself.

"No," said Theo.

Kiri looked up. Theo stood at the edge of the tarp, hair dripping, sleeves shoved to the elbow, and there was something in her face Kiri hadn't seen before—not command, not the flat competence she wore like armor, but a kind of fierce attention, as though Pellam's leg were a problem she intended to solve personally or die trying.

"It's in the leg," Theo said. "The bone is a rumor until it kills him. What do you need?"

"Boiling water. Clean cloth. Alcohol if we have any beyond the carbolic. And—" Kiri hesitated. "And someone to hold him down, if I have to open it deeper. He'll fight me. Feverish men always fight."

Theo turned and gave the orders, and here was the thing Kiri kept turning over afterward, the thing she would remember: Theo did not order anyone to do anything she wasn't doing herself. She built the fire on the one patch of deck they'd covered with sand. She boiled the water. She found the bottle of arrack Elio had been hoarding since Wewak and surrendered it to the cause without negotiation, and when Sarto grumbled that good liquor was wasted on a dying man, Theo looked at him for perhaps two seconds and Sarto found something else to do.

Elio held Pellam's shoulders. Kiri took the knife.

She had done this before—twice, in camps, with her father talking her through it in his level surveyor's voice—but never alone, never in charge, never with the patient's life balanced entirely on her own two hands. Her father had died four years ago with his journal full of other men's crimes, and everything he'd taught her had been sitting inside her ever since like cargo in a sealed hold, waiting for a port that never came.

"Now," she said, and opened the wound.

Pellam screamed. Elio held him. Theo held Pellam's good leg and talked to him—not comfort, exactly; Kiri couldn't hear the words over the rain, only the rhythm, steady as a metronome, a voice for the drowning man to swim toward. Kiri worked. Drainage, probing, the terrible intimacy of it, the pus and the blood and the small white gleam she refused to let herself think was bone. She flushed it with the boiled water, packed it with cloth soaked in carbolic-and-arrack, bound it tight, and sat back on her heels with her hands shaking so badly she had to press them flat against her thighs.

The rain went on. The fire hissed. Pellam, unconscious now, breathing in shallow fast pulls, lay quiet for the first time in two days.

"That's all?" Theo asked. Not doubting—asking. Asking *her*, Kiri realized. Consulting. As though Kiri Anselm, who had forced her way onto this boat with a dead man's journal and a head full of accusations, were the authority on this boat, and Theo Sandoval merely the hands.

"For tonight. If the fever breaks by tomorrow night, he lives. If it doesn't—" Kiri swallowed. "If it doesn't, we should think about what he'd want. He shouldn't die on a boat."

"He won't die on a boat." Theo said it the way she said everything, flat and final, and then she ruined it by adding, softer: "He won't. I've buried enough men in this basin. I'm not burying him."

* * *

Kiri slept two hours and woke to find a blanket over her that she hadn't had when she lay down, and Theo at the bow with her back against the crate of instruments, awake, watching the water rise.

Kiri went forward. There was no reason to; there was nothing to say that either of them would permit themselves to say. She went anyway, and sat, and for a while they watched the flooded forest together—trees standing to their waists in brown water, the whole world become a single slow-moving sheet, the boundary between river and land dissolved as thoroughly as the boundary between what Kiri had believed six weeks ago and what she believed now.

"You held him down," Kiri said finally. "You talked to him. I heard you."

"Somebody had to give him something to hold onto."

"You were in the water. Pushing. You could have directed from the deck. Nobody would have thought less of you."

Theo was quiet long enough that Kiri thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "My brother used to say that a leader who won't put her shoulder to the hull has no business asking anyone else to. He said it about railroad foremen, mostly. He was usually talking about our father." A pause. "He was right about it, too."

Kiri studied her profile—the strong nose, the jaw set against the rain, the exhaustion carved under her eyes. Six years of hiding, Kiri thought. Six years of being no one, of burning maps and burying names, and the first time the water rises, this woman steps into it shoulder-first. Whatever else Theo Sandoval had done—whatever the journal implied, whatever sins were folded into those closed lines—she did not lead from safety. She led from inside the risk, and she had never once asked any of them to stand anywhere she wouldn't stand.

Respect, Kiri thought. That's what this is. Just respect. Name it that and leave it alone.

But her heart was doing something unsanctioned, and the rain was loud enough to hide a great many things, and neither of them moved away.

"How is your father's journal holding up?" Theo asked abruptly. "The paper. This damp will eat it."

"It's wrapped in oilcloth. My father taught me that too." Kiri hesitated. "There are entries I haven't read yet. The last ones. I've been—I've been saving them."

"Why?"

"Because once I read them, I'll know." Kiri watched the water slide past, endless, indifferent. "Everything I've done since Wewak has been built on not knowing yet. Once I know, I'll have to decide what to do about it. And I don't trust myself to decide anything in this rain."

Theo turned her head then, and looked at her, and in the gray light her eyes were the color of the river and just as hard to read. "When you read them," she said, "read them out loud. Whatever they say. I'd rather hear it from you than wonder about it."

"You already know what they say, don't you." It wasn't a question. "You've known since before Wewak. Since before Vane. You know what's in my father's last pages, and you've been sitting on it for six years, and you haven't told me."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere aft, Pellam murmured in his fever, and Elio's voice rose softly to answer him, nonsense words, soothing ones.

"Yes," Theo said at last. "I know."

"And you won't tell me."

"Not in this rain. Not on this boat. Not while your hands are still shaking from the knife." Theo looked back at the water. "You deserve better than a confession delivered like ballast dumped overboard. So does he." A nod aft, toward Pellam. "When the fever breaks—or doesn't—when we're through this channel and this storm, I'll tell you everything. Every word I know. On my life, Kiri. But not tonight."

Kiri wanted to hate her for it. She reached for the old coal of anger, the one she'd carried aboard, and found it had gone cool in her hands somewhere in the last hundred miles, and she didn't know when that had happened, and she didn't know what to do about it.

"Tomorrow, then," she said.

"Tomorrow," Theo agreed. "Or the day after. Or whenever the river lets us stop."

The river, as it happened, did not let them stop. Near midday the rain doubled its fury, the channel walls began visibly to fail, and Elio came forward with his face gray under its tan and said, "Theo. The patch. Come look at the patch."

And when Kiri followed them down into the bilge, she saw it: the seam below the waterline weeping in three places now instead of one, the caulking gone soft as bread, water coming through in threads that were, even as they watched, becoming ropes.
