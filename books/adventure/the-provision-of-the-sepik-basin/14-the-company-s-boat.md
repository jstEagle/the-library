## 14. The Company's Boat

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and in that hour the river went quiet in a way Theo Sandoval did not trust. Six years of living by water had taught her the difference between a river resting and a river listening, and this one was listening. The mist lay on the channel like gauze over a wound. Somewhere upstream, past the drowned snags and the collapsed cutbank they'd passed at noon yesterday, something was moving against the current.

She heard it before she saw it—the low diesel knock, muffled by fog, patient as a heartbeat.

"Kill the lamp," she said to Elio, already crouching at the stern rail. "And the stove."

Elio moved without questions, which was the thing she liked best about him. The galley light died; the smell of coffee hung orphaned in the wet air. Kiri came up from below with her father's journal still open in one hand, a pencil tucked behind her ear like a carpenter's nail, and when she read Theo's face she closed the book without being told.

"Engine," Kiri said quietly. "Upstream?"

"Coming down." Theo lifted two fingers off the rail, pointing into the white. "Against us means it isn't local. No dugout runs the basin at night in monsoon season. No trader either. That's a boat with fuel to burn and orders to follow."

The sound grew. Not fast—nothing about it was fast—but steady, deliberate, the way a man walks toward you when he already knows where you're standing. Theo felt her pulse settle into that old cold rhythm, the one from the survey years, from the night the barge went down, from every moment since when staying invisible meant staying alive. *Count what you have. Spend nothing you can't replace.*

What she had: a patched hull leaking through Elio's best work, four men besides herself, a litter-bound deckhand named Rennick whose fever had broken but whose leg would not carry him for weeks, half a ton of gear, and a route map drawn in her own hand—a map that now existed in more heads than hers.

What she didn't have: time.

"Elio," she said. "The side channel. The one off the oxbow, south bank, maybe two kilometers back. The mangrove throat."

His jaw worked. "That's not a channel, señora. That's a suggestion of a channel. Half a meter at low water, and we draw more than that with the load."

"With the load," Theo said. "So we go in light. Everything that floats goes overboard and gets hauled through by hand. Everything that doesn't float gets lashed to the masthead above the waterline."

"And if the patrol comes down the main stem while we're wallowing in the mangroves like a wounded pig?"

"Then we're a wounded pig they don't see." She turned to the crew, who had gathered at the rail in the grey light—Sarto with his arms crossed, the brothers Okoye silent as posts, young Ferro blinking sleep out of his eyes. "Company boat. Which means someone told them our heading. Which means we are no longer traveling; we are being tracked. Everyone takes this personally."

* * *

They ran the engine soft, half-throttle, back upriver against the current they'd fought all week, and Theo stood at the bow with a sounding pole reading depth by feel, calling numbers to Elio at the wheel. The mangrove throat opened off the oxbow exactly as she remembered it from the survey years—a seam in the green so narrow the banks touched overhead in places, braided with hanging roots and the pale egg-sac clusters of some spider that thrived here in the millions. The water inside was black tea, warm as bathwater, alive with things she preferred not to name.

They went in stripped. Gear over the side into three dugouts the Okoye brothers paddled ahead as tugs, ropes singing wet off the gunwales. Sarto worked the winch without being asked, and Theo noted that too—Sarto had been helpful lately in the specific way of a man currying favor before presenting a bill. She filed it where she filed everything now, in the drawer marked *later*, because the drawer marked *now* was full.

The hull kissed mud twice going in. Both times Elio brought them off with the engine's gentleness rather than power, sweating, swearing under his breath in three languages, and both times the patch held—which surprised Theo less than it should have, because she had stopped believing in luck and started believing in Elio Cruz somewhere around the crocodile bank.

By full light they were buried. The boat sat moored between two great mangrove knees, screened by a curtain of roots and fern so dense that from ten meters out on the oxbow she doubted God himself could find them. The Okoye brothers took the dugouts downstream to watch the mouth of the channel and signal with a mirror if anything came. Rennick was laid out in the shade of the awning with Kiri dosing him. And Theo climbed the mast step with binoculars and waited for the company to show itself.

It took until mid-morning.

The patrol vessel came around the far bend of the main stem at nine o'clock, and even at this distance Theo knew her. *Meridian Star*. Steel-hulled, forty meters, twin screws, the company crest painted fresh on the bow because the company always painted its crests fresh—it was how they announced that whatever had happened last time was behind them now. She'd ridden that boat once, six years ago, in the other direction, with a body bag in the hold and a report in her pocket that she had already begun to falsify herself, line by line, signature by signature.

"They're slowing," Kiri said beside her. Theo hadn't heard her climb up. "Why slow? If they're just running the river, why stop?"

Theo found the answer forming in her own mouth before she wanted it there. "Because they're looking for us. And because—" She lowered the glasses. Through the heat-shimmer she could make out figures on the patrol boat's foredeck. Not many. Eight, perhaps ten. Armed, by the way they stood. And at the rail, apart from the others, a figure in a pale suit who did not need binoculars to be recognized, because Theo had spent six years recognizing that silhouette in her nightmares: the lean frame, the head tilted slightly as though perpetually listening for applause.

Marcus Vane stood on the deck of the *Meridian Star*, coming down the basin, following their route.

"He's not chasing us," Theo said. Her voice came out flat, which was how she knew she was furious. "He's *escorting* us. From behind. At a distance. Like a man walking his own dog."

Kiri stared at her. "Explain that."

"The supply drops. The letters. The changed terms." Theo counted them off on her fingers, each item landing like a stone. "He knew where we were at Wewak. He knew our heading after the second letter. He knew we grounded at the silt bank because the drop that followed included extra dressings for Rennick—dressings sized for a leg wound, Kiri, not general stock. He has known our position within a day's travel for the entire run. We weren't hired to reopen those lines. We were hired to *lead*. To go ahead through the swamp and the flood and the crocodiles and find the safe channel, mark the hazards, burn the fuel, take the risks—and then he follows our wake straight to the junction and arrives with armed men to collect the evidence alone."

"But the contract—"

"The contract says the reopened line report goes to the company." Theo watched the patrol boat resume speed, unhurried, its wake rolling long and oily into the reeds. "Vane doesn't want it to go to the company. Vane wants it to go to *him*. Because the original reports—the ones my brother died carrying, the ones in that strongbox at the junction—don't just prove the accident was falsified. They prove *who ordered it*. And a man who holds that kind of paper doesn't hand it to his employers. He holds it over them. He becomes untouchable. Or he sells it to whoever pays most. Either way, we were never partners. We were the mine detector."

The silence between them lasted long enough for a kingfisher to cross the channel, blue as a struck match.

"You suspected," Kiri said finally. Not accusing. Reading her, the way she read everything, including the journal entries Theo still hadn't let her finish.

"I feared," Theo corrected. "Suspecting is a thought. Fearing is a discipline. I've been fearing this since the second letter, when he agreed too easily to my terms. Men agree too easily when the agreement doesn't matter—when the real arrangement is already made." She looked at Kiri then, and found she could hold it. That was new. That was six days old, dating from the storm, from the confession, from the first tenderness neither of them had named. "I put everyone on this boat in front of me like a screen. Myself included. And he walked us here like sheep."

"No." Kiri's voice was quiet but it had iron in it. "You got us here. Every kilometer of this river, *you* got us here, through water that kills professionals. If Vane thinks he's collecting a delivered package, he's wrong, because the package has opinions." She paused. "What do we do?"

Theo looked back through the glasses. The *Meridian Star* was dropping astern now, falling behind their position, but not leaving—holding station somewhere up the main stem, close enough to come at a day's notice. Waiting. Letting them do the dangerous part.

"We keep going," she said. "We go to the junction, because the strongbox is real and it's the only thing on this earth that clears my brother's name, and I did not drag four good men and one stubborn woman through a monsoon to stop one bend short. But we change the arithmetic." She began counting again, and this time the stones landed differently. "First: nobody hears from Vane again without hearing it from all of us. Second: the route map in Sarto's head stops being complete—I'll feed him a false bearing tonight, a channel that dead-ends in the swamp, and if the patrol shows up down that channel, we'll know exactly who sold us. Third: we reach the junction, we raise the box, and we copy everything before Vane's boots touch mud. Originals stay ours until I decide otherwise. And fourth—"

She stopped. Down in the channel, Elio was looking up at her from the deck, and even at this distance she could see he'd been listening, because of course he had; the man heard everything through a hull.

"Fourth?" Kiri prompted.

"Fourth, we assume the leak in this crew is still leaking." Theo folded the glasses. "Vane reported our route from Wewak. Someone on this boat has been talking since Wewak. And I intend to know who before we're in sight of that junction—because the next thing they sell him won't be our position. It'll be our plan."

Below her, Sarto looked up from the winch at exactly that moment, smiled his easy smile, and waved.

Theo did not wave back.

* * *

They held in the mangrove throat through the rest of the day, letting the patrol boat's distance grow, letting the river close over their absence like water over a dropped coin. Theo used the hours the way she used all waiting hours: working. She redrew the route map twice—once true, once false—and burned the true one's spare copy in the galley stove, watching the ink curl and go. She checked the hull patch with Elio, pressing her thumb to the seam while he pumped the bilge, and they exchanged the wordless look of two people who both know the number of days remaining and have agreed not to say it aloud. Three, maybe four. The boat would die before the journey did.

At dusk she fed the crew the false bearing. "Past the split at the drowned church," she said, tapping the chart laid on the mess table, lamplight swinging with the current's small motion against the hull. "South fork. It reads wider on the survey sheets. We'll make better time."

Sarto leaned over the chart with the others, nodding along, asking the right questions in the right order—*how wide, what draft, any snags*—and Theo answered each one smoothly, warmly, watching his eyes track the pencil line south while her own plans ran north. It was the oldest trick in fieldwork and it never failed for one reason: a man selling information always sells it confidently, and confidence requires believing what you've been told.

Afterward she stood a night watch alone at the stern, and Kiri came to stand it with her, and for a while neither spoke. The basin breathed around them—frogs, insects, the wet slap of fish rising, the mangroves dripping their slow metronome. Somewhere far off, thunder stacked itself up over the ranges, counting down to the next rain.

"My father wrote in the journal," Kiri said at last, "that the worst betrayals never announce themselves. They arrive wearing your own flag." She turned the journal over in her hands without opening it. "You think it's Sarto."

"I think it's whoever signed the supply manifest at Wewak with a name that matches no crew list I ever approved." Theo kept her eyes on the black water. "But yes. Mostly Sarto."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I've insulted an innocent man and lost nothing but his comfort." She glanced sideways. "If I'm right and say nothing, I lose everything. I've done the arithmetic, Kiri. Fear does the sums faster than fairness. That's not a virtue. It's just true."

Kiri was quiet a moment. Then: "My father also wrote that a lie used as a tool is still a shovel. It digs. Just make sure you're digging toward something." She bumped her shoulder against Theo's, brief and warm, and left her to the watch.

Theo stayed until the moon rose thin through the canopy, and in that grey light she allowed herself, for exactly one minute, to feel the whole weight of it: the betrayal confirmed, the enemy riding her wake in a pale suit, the brother whose name hung on a strongbox at the bottom of a drowned junction, the woman below decks who had become, without either of them deciding it, the reason she wanted to survive this. One minute. Then she folded the feeling away with the same care she folded her maps, and went below to plan the approach north—the true approach—through channels that appeared on no chart but hers.

* * *

The answer came in thirty-six hours, and it came exactly the way she'd designed it to come.

They had run north from the mangrove throat at first light, taking the true channel through a drowned forest where the trunks rose out of black water like the masts of a sunken fleet, and Theo had fed Sarto the false bearing the night before departure, casually, over the chart, with the Okoye brothers deliberately out of earshot. South fork. Drowned church. Wider water.

On the second morning, the Okoye brothers' mirror flashed from a bluff behind them—three long flashes, two short. Contact. Company boat, moving.

Moving south.

Elio brought the boat alongside the bluff under cover of overhanging fig, and the younger Okoye came down the bank breathless with the report: the *Meridian Star*'s launch, armed, eight men, running hard up the south fork past the drowned church. Running into dead-end swamp. Running a channel Theo had invented at a mess table by lamplight.

"They bought it," Ferro said, grinning, before he caught the mood aboard and stopped.

Nobody grinned back. Because the arithmetic was finished now, and the sum was ugly: only one person aboard had been given the south fork. Only one person could have sold it. And the launch burning fuel up a dead-end channel meant Marcus Vane had just learned, at the cost of a day and his own patience, that his mine detector had started feeding him gravel.

Which meant he would stop trusting the drip of information—and start applying pressure.

Theo stood at the rail and watched the empty northern channel stretch away ahead of them, black water, drowned trees, three days to the junction if the rain held. Behind them, faintly, carried down the swamp like a rumor, she could hear the diesel knock of the launch turning back, angry, hunting.

"Sarto," she called pleasantly, without turning around. "Winch check, if you please."

Behind her, a pause—one beat too long—and then the rattle of the handle beginning to turn.

Three days. A dying hull. A hunting launch. And somewhere aboard, a man whose smile had just gone one degree colder, calculating what his silence was worth now that his product had proven counterfeit.

Theo gripped the rail and steered them north, into the green dark, and did not look back.
