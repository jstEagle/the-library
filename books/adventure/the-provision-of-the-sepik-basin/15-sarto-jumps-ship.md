## 15. Sarto Jumps Ship

Elio Cruz woke to the sound of a man being quiet.

He had spent thirty years on the water, and the water had taught him every language of the night—the conversation of rain on canvas, the mutter of current against the hull, the long sigh of the basin breathing out its heat. He knew the sound of a man moving carefully through a boat that was not his own. It had a rhythm to it, a held-breath quality, like a thief counting his own footsteps.

He did not open his eyes. He listened.

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and in its absence the swamp had grown loud with smaller sounds: insects stitching the dark together, something large moving through the shallows with the patience of a creature that had never once been in a hurry. The boat lay against the bank where they'd tied up at dusk, in a side channel choked with fallen pandanus, hidden from the main river the way a wounded animal hides from the thing that follows its blood-trail.

Sarto's mat was empty.

Elio let his breath go slow through his nose. He kept his eyes closed and mapped the boat by sound alone: Reyes at the stern, sleeping the way she always slept—on her side, knife under her hand, breath shallow and even, a woman who had trained her own body out of trust. The girl, Anselm, curled against the supply crates with her father's journal in its oilskin wrap pressed to her chest like a child with a doll. Tam, the injured one, fevered and muttering in the bow, his bandaged leg smelling of the sweet rot that Elio did not let himself think about. Four men had signed nothing at Wewak. Tam had paid for the silt bank with his leg. And now—

The soft scrape of canvas. The whisper of a strap drawn through a buckle.

Elio opened his eyes.

Sarto stood at the gunwale with the route map rolled into a waterproof sleeve, and he had his pack on, and he was looking back at the sleeping boat with an expression Elio recognized, because Elio had seen it on men before, in ports where the law was a rumor and every man's loyalty was a currency that devalued overnight. It was not the expression of a traitor. It was the expression of a man who had done his arithmetic and found that the sum of staying was less than the sum of leaving. Sarto was not evil. Sarto was arithmetic.

He swung his leg over the gunwale, and Elio spoke.

"You'll want the east bank," he said quietly. "The west is soft mud to your waist, and there's a crocodile sunning there most mornings that would take you before you got your boots wet."

Sarto froze, half in, half out of the boat, and for a long moment the only sound was the insects and the fevered breathing from the bow. Then he turned, and in the gray pre-dawn light his face was open in a way it had never been in six weeks on the water. Almost apologetic.

"You knew," he said.

"I know boats. Boats tell you everything, if you listen. Men too." Elio sat up slowly, keeping his hands visible, the way you do with a man holding a knife or a decision. "Since Wewak. The way you asked about the junction—not like a boatman asks, wanting to know the depth of the water. Like a clerk asks, wanting to know the value of the cargo."

"Vane pays well." Sarto said it flatly, without shame, and somehow that was worse than shame. "Better than a dead man's promise. You've seen the boat, Cruz. You've seen the hull. We're all dead men on this river; the only question is who gets paid first."

"The girl's leg is rotting," Elio said. "And you're taking the map that tells Vane where we are."

"The map that tells Vane where *you* are." Sarto's voice hardened, and he glanced toward the stern, toward the sleeping shape of Reyes. "I don't know what she's running from, and I don't care. I know what I'm running toward. There's a difference, and the difference is money."

Elio rose. It was not a threat; he was too old and too tired for threats, and Sarto was younger and stronger and armed. It was just the motion of a man standing up in his own boat, in his own domain, and the meaning of it passed between them without a word needing to be said.

"You could wake her," Sarto said. "But then I'd have to wake the others, and there'd be noise, and noise on this river carries. Or you could let me go quiet, and everyone sleeps until morning, and no one bleeds."

It was true, and they both knew it was true, and Elio hated the arithmetic of it because it was the same arithmetic that had put Sarto in the boat in the first place. Six weeks ago they had been five men and two women who signed nothing, bound by nothing but wages and water. Now the wages were spent and the water was rising, and the only things left binding them were things that had never been written down.

"Go, then," Elio said.

Sarto hesitated. For one moment—one long, strange moment—his face did something complicated, something that might have been regret in a man who had never practiced it. "The hull," he said. "The patch on the port side, under the waterline. It's worse than she thinks. I've been bailing twice a night since the silt bank. If you keep her in moving water, you might make it. If you take the swamp channels—" He stopped. Shook his head. "I'm not telling you this for you. I'm telling you because I bailed that boat too."

Then he dropped over the side into the shallows, and the water barely whispered, and he was gone—east, toward the main channel, toward the patrol boat somewhere upstream with its company lights and its armed men, carrying the route map rolled against his back like a spine he'd finally grown.

Elio stood in the empty boat and listened to the swamp, and did not wake anyone.

* * *

He told Reyes at first light, when the mist was still standing on the water in gray columns and the others were awake and the news could not be softened by darkness.

She took it the way she took everything: standing still. But Elio had learned to read her the way he read water, and the stillness was not calm. It was the stillness of a river before the flood crest comes down—a held pressure, a surface that looks like glass and is moving underneath at speeds that will kill you.

"The map," she said.

"Everything. The route, the channels, the timing. He'll be at the patrol boat by mid-morning if the mud lets him move."

"Then we have until mid-morning." She turned to the chart spread on the crate, and her finger came down on the tangled lines of the swamp channels west of the main river. "Vane will expect us to run. Running means the main river, downstream or up, and the main river is where his boat is. So we don't run." Her finger traced a line that made Elio's stomach tighten, a thin thread of blue winding through country marked with the surveyor's oldest and most honest notation: *impassable in wet season.* "We go through the dead swamp. The channels the company never dredged because there was nothing in them worth dredging. No current, no flow—water that just sits. It'll be slow, and it'll be foul, and the hull—"

"The hull will not thank you," Elio said. "The hull will file a formal complaint."

That, unexpectedly, pulled the ghost of a smile from her. "Can the boat do it?"

Elio looked at his boat. Six weeks she had carried them, and she was dying by inches—the patch Sarto had confessed to, the seams weeping at every change of temperature, the wood gone soft in places where the basin's water got into it and never left. He had known since the silt bank, and he had told Reyes a version of the truth, and now Sarto's desertion had stripped even that version away. He thought about lying. He thought about the arithmetic.

"No," he said. "Not the swamp and not the return. She's a one-way vessel now, Reyes. I've known it since the bank. I'm telling you now because you deserve to hear it plain, from the man whose boat it is. Whatever we're doing—wherever we're going—this boat is a ticket, not a return."

Reyes looked at the chart, at the thin blue thread through the dead swamp, at the small cross marking the drowned junction somewhere beyond it. Then she looked at Tam in the bow, fever-bright and fading, and at Anselm, who had come to stand at the chart with her father's journal under her arm, and who said nothing, only watched Reyes with the particular attention of a woman who had decided that this stranger's decisions were her decisions now.

"One-way," Reyes said. "Then we'd better make the trip worth the fare."

* * *

The dead swamp took them at noon.

It was country that the word *water* did not properly describe. The main river had current; this had only memory—floodwater that had come in with the rains and stayed, going nowhere, doing nothing, warming in the sun until it smelled of rot and iron. The trees stood in it knee-deep and waist-deep, their roots exposed like the knuckles of drowned giants, and between the trees the water lay so still that the sky floated in it perfectly, upside down, so that Elio sometimes could not tell, looking over the side, whether the clouds were above him or below.

There was no current to fight and no current to help. Every meter of distance was bought with the pole and the sweep and the engine at its lowest reliable turn, and the engine's voice in that stillness was obscene—a chainsaw in a cathedral—and twice Elio shut it down and they went by hand, in silence, because silence was the only camouflage left to them.

They moved in shifts. Reyes took the pole first, standing in the bow like something carved, reading the waterless water the way she read everything—by what was *not* there. No flow-lines on the surface. No drift of debris. A floating leaf that did not move meant shallow silt beneath; a leaf that turned slowly in place meant a hole, and holes in dead swamp were bottomless. Anselm learned it fast, faster than the deckhands had, and Elio watched the two women fall into a wordless rhythm at either end of the boat, pole and sweep, question and answer, and he thought: *there it is.* Not romance, not yet, maybe not ever in words—but two people moving as one hull, which on the water was the same thing.

Tam drifted in and out of fever. Anselm changed his dressings with hands that did not shake, and Elio, hauling the sweep, understood that she had done this before, somewhere, for someone, and that the journal she guarded was not the only inheritance her father had left her.

They made perhaps six kilometers the first day. It was the slowest water Elio had ever traveled, and he had poled barges through mangrove mazes in two countries. By dusk his shoulders were fire and his hands were raw, and the boat had taken on water all day in a slow, patient seep that matched his own exhaustion, and when they tied up for the night against a fallen trunk, no one suggested a fire.

It was Anselm who broke the silence, in the dark, in the low voice of someone saying a thing they have decided to say and will not be talked out of.

"Sarto will tell Vane everything he knows. But Sarto only knows the route up to the side channel. He doesn't know the dead swamp—he never saw the chart with the swamp channels marked." A pause. "Reyes changed the route after he left. He can't sell what he doesn't have."

"He can sell where we *were*," Reyes said from the bow. "And Vane has a boat with an engine and men with guns, and I have a boat with a hole in it and a crew of four. He doesn't need to know where we're going. He only needs to guess, and he's been guessing about me for six years."

Elio, mending a seam by feel in the dark, said nothing. But he thought about the arithmetic again—Sarto's arithmetic, Vane's arithmetic, the company's arithmetic, all of it the same cold sum—and then he thought about the thing that had happened that evening, the thing that did not appear in any ledger.

It had happened at the second rest, in the worst of the heat. Tam had begun to slide, fever pulling him under, and they had gotten him ashore onto a root platform and Anselm had worked over him, and Reyes had stood in the water to her thighs holding the litter steady, hour after hour, not because she was the strongest but because she would not ask anyone to stand in water that held crocodiles if she was not standing in it herself. No order. No authority. Just a woman in the water, keeping the fevered man's platform level with her own arms, until Anselm said the fever had broken or at least paused, and Reyes had climbed back into the boat with leeches on her legs and said nothing about it at all.

Elio had crewed for captains and owners and one pirate, and he knew every kind of command there was. He had never seen this kind: leadership as shared risk, authority as the refusal to be exempt from the danger. The deckhands had followed her because she paid. What was left of the crew followed her for a different reason, and no one had discussed it, and no one needed to.

* * *

The second day in the dead swamp, the water began to rise.

Elio felt it before he saw it—a change in the boat's behavior, a new strain in the seams, and when he looked over the side, the marks he had cut on the hull at their first anchorage were under water. The flood was coming down from the highlands somewhere, weeks of monsoon arriving all at once, and even the dead swamp was not dead anymore. The still water began, very slowly, to move. Not toward the junction. Not in any direction that helped. Just *moving*, a vast brown exhalation, and the channels they had been threading began to widen and merge, and the trees they had marked as landmarks stood deeper in the water every time Elio looked at them.

"Is this good or bad?" Anselm asked. She was at the sweep, and there was mud to her elbows and exhaustion written into every line of her, and she asked the question the way she asked all her questions now—plainly, without decoration, because the swamp had stripped decoration off all of them.

"Both," Elio said. "The rising water floats us over the shallows that would have stopped us. It also erases the channels. By tomorrow, the chart and the swamp will be two different documents." He looked at Reyes, at the stern, where she stood with the chart in her hands watching the water rise around the trees with an expression he could not read. "How far?"

"Two days," Reyes said. "Maybe three, if the flood confuses us. The junction is on high ground—the old junction house was built on the one ridge in forty kilometers. The water won't take the ridge. But it will take everything around it, and it will take it fast."

"And Vane?"

"Vane is on the main river, with a map that's now two days wrong and a pilot who doesn't know these channels." Something moved through her voice then, cold and bright, and Elio recognized it as the thing that had kept her alive for six years: not hope, not fear—*certainty of purpose*, the coldest fuel there was. "He'll come. Sarto will make sure of that. But he'll come the long way, and we'll be there first, and we'll have the ridge."

They camped that night on the root platform of the largest tree they'd found, all of them crowded together above the waterline that no longer existed, the boat tied below with its seams weeping into the rising flood. Tam slept—truly slept, for the first time in days—and Anselm sat beside him, and Reyes sat apart, as she always sat apart, and Elio found himself beside her without quite deciding to be, the way water finds the low place.

"You knew Sarto would go," she said. It was not a question.

"I knew a man who does arithmetic at night would do it eventually. I told you at Wewak—men who sign nothing can un-sign nothing."

"Does it anger you? Losing the wages, the boat, the—" She gestured, a small motion that took in the swamp, the flood, the whole drowning world.

Elio was quiet for a while. The insects sang. Somewhere far off, thunder spoke in the language of its own country.

"My father fished these rivers," he said finally. "He used to say: the river takes from every boat, and the boats that survive are not the ones with the most aboard, but the ones carrying only what matters. I came on this trip for the money, Reyes. Same as Sarto. But the money drowned at the silt bank, and I'm still here." He looked at her, at the hard profile against the last gray light. "You prune a crew like you prune a tree. What's left isn't less. What's left is what was holding the fruit."

Reyes looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face—the thing that was never quite at rest, the thing that watched every man's hands and every horizon—eased by a degree. Not trust. Elio did not think the word *trust* existed in her language anymore. But something adjacent to it, something a surveyor might mark on a chart as *probable channel: unverified.*

"Four of us," she said. "One boat that won't come back. One ridge. And a strongbox at the bottom of a drowned junction with six years of dead men's names in it." She almost smiled. "That's the whole cargo, Cruz. That's everything we're carrying."

"Then we're carrying only what matters," Elio said.

* * *

They reached the edge of the dead swamp on the third morning, and the water told them they had arrived by finally doing what water was supposed to do: moving. A current, faint but real, tugged at the hull from the west, and Elio read it like a letter from an old friend—the flood spilling out of the swamp into a channel that led, if his dead father's rivers were in him as deeply as he believed, toward the old line and the ridge beyond.

They came around a wall of pandanus at mid-morning and stopped, all four of them, struck silent.

Below them, where the charts promised a channel, the basin had become a single moving sea. The monsoon had arrived in earnest overnight, and the flood had swallowed the low country whole—trees standing to their crowns, the old survey cuts invisible, the entire world reduced to brown water and gray rain and the distant, ceaseless roar of the river in flood somewhere to the north. And through it, faint with distance but unmistakable, a sound that made every hand on the boat tighten: the deep, rhythmic thud of a diesel engine. Not theirs. Bigger. Upstream, on the main channel, moving.

Reyes raised a hand, and Elio cut the engine, and they drifted in the drowned forest, listening.

"Vane?" Anselm breathed.

"Or the company," Reyes said. Her eyes were on the water, on the current, on the arithmetic of distances that only she could see. "Either way, they're on the main river, and we're not. We have the ridge, and the ridge has the junction, and the junction—" she stopped. Her hand went, unconsciously, to the oilskin pouch at her chest where she kept the things she had never shown anyone. "The junction has the truth at the bottom of it. Two days. We can be there in two days if we pole the flooded channels by night and rest by day."

"By night," Elio said, "in flood water, with a hull that's dying, and a fevered man aboard."

"Yes."

Elio looked at his boat. He looked at the three people in it—the surveyor with her dead men's names, the daughter with her father's journal, the boy with his ruined leg who had signed nothing and stayed anyway. Five had come out of Wewak. The river had taken one, and the river had given back something else in its place, something without a name that Elio had seen form in the space of three days and would not try to name now, because names were for things that had finished happening, and this had not finished happening yet.

He put the engine into gear.

"Two days," he said. "Then we'd better not waste the daylight arguing about it."

The boat turned west, into the current, toward the ridge and the drowned junction and whatever waited there—and behind them, far off across the flooded world, the diesel thud went on and on, patient as arithmetic, closing.
