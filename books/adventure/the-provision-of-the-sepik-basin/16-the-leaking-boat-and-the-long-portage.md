## 16. The Leaking Boat and the Long Portage

Kiri felt the boat die before anyone said it aloud.

It was in the rhythm of the bilge pump—Elio had been working it longer and longer each hour, the handle rising and falling like a man praying too fast, and the water coming over the transom had stopped being an insult and become a conversation they were losing. She sat near the bow with her father's journal wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, watching brown water slide past the gunwale only a hand's width below, and she understood that the *Paloma* was not carrying them anymore. They were carrying her.

Theo stood at the stern beside Elio, saying nothing, which Kiri had learned was how Theo said the most important things.

"She'll make the channel mouth," Elio said at last, not looking up from the pump.

"And after that?" Theo asked.

Elio spat over the side, a small useless courtesy. "After that she's a coffin with good lines."

They grounded at midday on a shelf of mud where the channel widened into drowned forest—kapok and sago palms standing hip-deep in moving water, their roots making a lattice that might once have been dry land. The rain had thinned to a fine gray sift, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang, so that everything was wet without anything ever having rained. Theo ordered the boat brought against a fallen trunk and lashed there, and then she stood in the shallows for a long moment with her hands on her hips, looking into the trees the way a woman looks at a bill she cannot pay.

"We walk," she said.

"Walk," repeated Sarto's replacement, a silent Wewak man called Beto who had said perhaps forty words since boarding. He looked at the forest the way other men looked at open graves.

"The junction is nine kilometers northeast," Theo said. "Maybe eleven, the way the water runs. We take what we can carry, we build a litter for Mateo, and we go tree to tree until the ground rises." She turned, and her eyes found Kiri across the flooded deck with a precision that Kiri had stopped pretending was accidental. "Kiri. How is he?"

Mateo lay under the tarpaulin aft where he'd lain since the crocodile bank, his leg splinted from ankle to thigh, the wound beneath the bandage gone from angry red to a quiet, terrible pink. Fever had come in the night and stayed like a guest who paid rent in silence.

"He's lucid this morning," Kiri said. "That's the fever climbing, not falling. I've seen it go both ways from here."

"What do you need?"

"Quinine if you have it. You don't. Boiled water, more bandage, and for him to stop being moved—but that request is now void, isn't it."

"Yes," Theo said simply. "It's void."

There it was again, Kiri thought. The thing about Theo Sandoval—or Tomas Reyes, or whatever name she wore this year—that no one else on the boat seemed able to see clearly because they were too busy being afraid of her. She did not soften bad news. She did not perform grief or resolve. She took the worst fact available, held it up to the light, and built directly on top of it, and somehow that was more comforting than any kindness would have been. Kindness could be a lie. Arithmetic couldn't.

* * *

The litter took two hours to build, and Kiri spent them learning the crew.

Beto cut saplings with a machete and said nothing, but when Kiri asked him twice for the same length of pole he began cutting them all to the second length without being asked, which was its own kind of eloquence. Elio stripped bark for lashings with hands that had done it ten thousand times, humming something tuneless, and talked steadily—not to anyone, just talked, the way some men kept fear at bay by never letting a silence form around them.

"My father ran cargo on the Ramu," he said, knotting a joint. "Boat sank in '09 with a full load of copra and my uncle aboard. After that he told me: the river doesn't kill you, girl's honor. The river just stops helping. Everything after that is your own doing." He tested the knot, grunted approval at himself. "This boat, now—the river stopped helping her three days ago. Everything since has been our own doing."

"You're a philosopher, Elio."

"I'm a man whose boat is dying. It makes everyone a philosopher." He glanced up at her, and his face went uncharacteristically still. "You know she means to carry Mateo herself. Half the portage. Don't tell her I told you. She'd deny it, and she'd be right to, because if the crew thinks the boss is spending herself on one man they'll start doing the arithmetic of abandonment."

"I won't tell," Kiri said. "But I'll watch."

"That's worse," Elio said cheerfully, and went back to his knots.

They rigged the litter between two poles with the tarpaulin slung as a bed, and they loaded themselves like beasts of burden: rice and the medical kit divided into four loads, the tools, the wrapped journal, the coil of fuse and the demolition charges Theo insisted on carrying personally, strapped high across her back like a hump. When Kiri had asked her about them—weeks ago, it seemed now, another life ago—Theo had answered with a look that closed the subject like a door. She hadn't asked again. Some cargoes explained themselves.

Mateo weighed almost nothing. That was the worst discovery of the day. A man who'd been solid as a bollard at Wewak now came up off the deck like driftwood, and he gripped Kiri's wrist with fingers that felt borrowed from someone smaller and said, "Leave me. I'm serious. I'll float down to the mission on the tarp, they'll find me, I'll tell them a very boring story."

"You'll tell them nothing," Kiri said, "because you're coming with us, and you'll be conscious for all of it out of pure spite."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough cost him something visible, and Kiri made a private note of the color of his lips and filed it under words she wasn't going to say in front of him.

They stepped off the boat at four o'clock in the afternoon, into water that came to their thighs, and the *Paloma* settled behind them against her log with a sigh that sounded, to Kiri, grateful.

* * *

The flooded forest was not walking and it was not swimming; it was a third thing, a slow vertical negotiation with a world that had been rotated ninety degrees. Every tree was a decision. Roots rose from the water in buttressed walls slick as oiled stone, and between them the current moved with a hidden agenda, tugging at knees, finding every gap in footing. Above the waterline the trunks were furred with moss and crawling things; below it, Kiri tried very hard not to think about what else lived there.

They went in single file, Theo first, reading the water the way she read maps—with an attention that seemed less like skill than like devotion. She chose their line by signs Kiri couldn't see: the lean of debris caught in branches, the color of the current, the way foam gathered or refused to gather. Twice she stopped the column for no reason Kiri could discern, waited, and then detoured fifty meters around a stretch of open-looking water, and both times, passing the place afterward, Kiri saw the submerged fence of a broken-off snag lying just under the surface, sharp as spears, patient as anything.

The litter went best when four carried it, but four couldn't always stand abreast between the roots, so it went mostly by pairs, rotating every hundred meters, and the rotation became the whole architecture of the day: lift, wade, lower, breathe, lift. Mateo bore it like a soldier, which is to say he complained constantly and precisely, cataloguing every jolt and splash with the accuracy of a man who had decided that complaining was his final contribution to the expedition and intended to make it excellent.

"Reyes," he said, somewhere in the second hour, "if I die out here, tell Vane his money bought the worst boat in maritime history and I want a refund posthumously."

"Noted," Theo said, without turning.

"And tell the girl doctor she splints better than the army surgeon who fixed my shoulder, and I've told that story in six ports."

"You'll tell it in seven," Kiri said. "Hold still."

The light failed early under the canopy, the way it did, going from gray to darker gray to a green-black dusk in which the water and the air became the same substance. They made camp on the root platform of an enormous kapok—a dry triangle of buttress big enough for four bodies if four bodies were friendly—and got Mateo up onto the driest fold of it, and lit no fire because fire meant light and light meant being seen, and being seen, these days, meant Vane.

They ate cold rice and a tin of condensed milk passed around like communion. Somewhere across the black water something large exhaled, once, and did not do it again, and nobody commented, because commenting would have required deciding what to say.

Kiri checked Mateo's wound by feel and the match-flare of a shielded lantern: the pink had deepened toward red at the margins, and the skin around it was hot as a sun-struck rail. She cleaned it, repacked it, dosed him with the last of the willow-bark decoction she'd been brewing from bark Beto gathered, and lied to him about how it looked, and watched him accept the lie with a grace that broke her heart a little, because he knew it was a lie and let her keep it anyway.

Then she sat back against the buttress, and her body presented its bill.

Every muscle from her shoulders to her ankles filed a separate grievance. Her hands were wrinkled white as drowned fruit. There was a leech on her calf that she removed with a fingernail and a lack of ceremony that would have horrified her father, who had believed a woman in the field should remain crisp, and she thought of him then—of the journal against her ribs, of his careful handwriting marching toward the last entry, toward the orders signed M.V., toward the water that had taken him—and she thought that he would have hated this swamp and loved this crew, and that the two facts were probably the same fact.

Across the root platform, Theo was cleaning the pistol with the methodical tenderness other people gave to instruments or children.

"You should sleep," Kiri said.

"After you."

"That's not an answer, that's a ritual."

"It's both." Theo set a part down, picked up a rag. The lantern was hooded to a coin of light, and in it her face was all planes and shadow, younger than she let it be in daylight. "Someone watches. You've been carrying half of Mateo and all of the medicine. I sleep after you or not at all."

"You've slept four hours since the storm."

"You've counted."

"You count mine," Kiri said. "Don't pretend you don't. You always hand me the first watch's coffee."

The rag stopped moving. In the dark, in the small coin of lamplight, Kiri watched Theo arrive at something—watched her circle it the way she circled snags in the channel, testing, refusing the direct line—and then, for once, watched her stop circling.

"Come here," Theo said quietly, "where they can't hear us over the water."

Kiri moved along the buttress until their shoulders touched, which in that climate, in that exhaustion, was a country unto itself. The rain sifted down through the canopy above them, arriving eventually, indifferently, drop by drop.

"I'm going to say a thing," Theo said, "and I need you to let me finish it, because if I stop I won't start again."

"Finish it."

"I have spent six years being no one. It's a discipline, like surveying. You practice it until it holds your weight. And it held—I want you to understand that it actually held, right up until a stranger boarded my boat with my father's sins in her bag and her own lies written all over her face, and I looked at you and thought: *there*. There's the crack in the wall." Her voice stayed level, but barely, the way a bridge stays up. "I don't know what happens at the junction. I know what I intend, and I know the odds, and I've done the arithmetic enough ways that it comes out bad in all of them. So I'm not going to promise you tomorrow, because I've watched men promise each other tomorrows on this river and the river keeps the promises, not them."

"Don't," Kiri said. "Don't give me the speech where you protect me by pushing me out. I've read that document. My father wrote a version of it before his last run."

"I'm not pushing you out." Theo turned then, and the coin of lamplight caught her eyes, and Kiri saw that her hands, resting on her knees, were not quite steady. "I'm telling you the truth, which is the only thing I have left that isn't burned or buried. Here it is: somewhere between the crocodile bank and Sarto's face at breakfast, this stopped being a job I'm surviving. You are the reason I check the perimeter twice. You are the reason I handed you the map page in the storm instead of eating it. When I plan the route now, I plan it around keeping you breathing, and that is either the most dangerous thing I've done in six years or the first honest thing, and I can't tell anymore, and I've stopped trying to." She exhaled. "I love you. Plainly. Without a tomorrow attached. I wanted you to have it before the water takes its share, whatever the water decides to take."

The forest breathed around them. Far off, thunder cleared its throat and thought better of it.

Kiri found that she was crying, which annoyed her intensely, and that she was also, underneath the annoyance, calmer than she had been since Wewak. Grief and love had been tangled in her chest for so long she'd stopped distinguishing the strands; Theo had just gone through and separated them with a surveyor's hands.

"You're wrong about one part," Kiri said. "You think you gave me something. You didn't. You confirmed it. I've known since the night rain, since you told me about your brother with your hands open like that—" she reached over and took one of those hands, folded it closed around her own, "—since then. I was waiting for you to catch up. You're always three days behind your own heart, Theo. It's the surveyor in you. You won't chart a feature until you've sighted it from two positions."

Theo laughed—a real laugh, low and startled out of her, the first Kiri had heard since the basin began—and pressed her forehead to Kiri's temple, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the water said everything, moving endlessly toward places they couldn't see.

"I should warn you," Theo murmured finally. "If we live, I'm going to be difficult. I don't know how to be known. I've had six years of practice at the opposite."

"Good," Kiri said. "Because I've had a lifetime of loving men who hid inside their work, and I'd like to try the opposite too. We'll be terrible at it together. It'll be fair."

Behind them, Mateo shifted and muttered something about refunds. On the black water, the current kept its own counsel. Kiri closed her eyes with her shoulder against Theo's and let herself sleep first, which was its own declaration of trust, and she was nearly under when Theo's hand tightened on hers—not a caress, an alarm.

Theo was sitting up, head tilted, listening.

Downstream, faint but unmistakable, carried flat across the floodwater: engines. More than one. Running slow, running quiet, running their way.

"They shouldn't be able to find us in this," Theo whispered. "Nobody finds anybody in this."

The engines came on, patient, closing.

"Unless," Kiri said, "they already know exactly where we're going."

Theo was already reaching for the charges on her back, her face in the lamplight gone hard and bright as a rail spike.
