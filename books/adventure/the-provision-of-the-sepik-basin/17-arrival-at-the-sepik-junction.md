## 17. Arrival at the Sepik Junction

The channel narrowed the way a throat narrows before speech.

Theo stood at the bow of what remained of their expedition—four people, one litter, and the salvaged gear lashed into bundles that rode on Elio's shoulders like penance—and watched the water change. For six days they had moved through flooded forest, chest-deep in places, hauling the injured deckhand Anselm's stretcher between them on a raft of hollow bamboo, and the whole time the river had been one brown, undifferentiated animal. But here it went quiet. Here the current slowed and spread and darkened, and the trees stepped back as if making room for something they had learned to fear.

She knew this place. She had never been here, and she knew it.

The map she had burned six years ago lived in her hands still; her fingers remembered every contour the way a tongue remembers a chipped tooth. Two miles ahead, the old junction sat where the survey spur met the main trunk line, drowned when the company dynamited the levee and let the basin take back what it had borrowed. The official report called it an engineering necessity. The unofficial truth was simpler: you flood a place, you don't have to explain what happened there.

"Rails," Kiri said.

Theo followed her gaze. Below the tea-colored surface, something caught the last of the afternoon light—a long, straight gleam running away from them into the black water like a vein of metal in stone. The rails. Still true after six years underwater, still pointing at the junction with the dumb fidelity of steel.

Elio set down his bundle and waded forward until the water reached his waist. He spat, ceremonially, which Theo had learned was his way of blessing or cursing—sometimes both. "Company iron," he said. "It doesn't rust honest. It rusts angry."

Behind them, Mateo stirred on the litter. The fever had broken two days ago thanks to Kiri's needle and stubbornness, but he was thin as a fish bone and his leg was wrapped in cloth gone yellow at the edges. "Is that it?" he asked. "The junction?"

"That's it," Theo said.

He was quiet a moment. Then: "My father worked a line like this once. Before it closed." He said it the way men said things they'd been carrying since Wewak. "Nobody ever told us why."

*Somebody will,* Theo thought. *Or nobody will, and I'll have carried it for nothing.* She didn't say it aloud. Words spent too early were words the river could take.

They made camp on the highest ground they could find—a slight rise where an old loading platform rotted under a skin of moss, its pilings still standing like the ribs of something that had died standing up. Theo posted no watch because there was no one left to watch against but the weather, and the weather needed no watching. It would come when it came.

Kiri sat beside her at the edge of the rise, knees drawn up, her father's journal wrapped in oilcloth in her lap. She didn't open it. She just held it, the way you hold a hand.

"You've been quiet since the side channel," Kiri said. "Quieter than your quiet."

"I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"How many ways this can go wrong from here." Theo turned a stick over in her fingers. "There used to be more ways. Now there are only three."

"And?"

"And I don't like any of them." She looked at Kiri then, and found—as she had been finding for days now—that looking at her cost less than it used to. That was either trust or exhaustion, and she suspected the difference didn't matter anymore. "Tomorrow I go down. Into the water. There's a barge under that junction. My brother worked it. And if the company left anything behind when they flooded this place, it'll be chained to that barge, because the man who ran the operation was too proud to trust paper to anyone else's safekeeping."

"The strongbox."

"The strongbox."

Kiri was silent. Then she said, not looking at Theo, "My father wrote about him. Your brother. In the last entries. He said—" She stopped, started again. "He said Sandoval was the only one who counted the loads twice."

Theo's throat closed. She looked out at the black water where the rails vanished, and let herself feel it—one full second of it, no more—before she folded it away. "He did," she managed. "Count twice. It's what got him killed."

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping, and with it the rain—not the monsoon's full fury yet, but a fine persistent sifting, like the sky couldn't decide whether to commit. Theo stripped to her shirt and stood at the water's edge while Elio rigged the rope: tied off to a piling, paid out in coils, the end weighted with a shackle so she could find it in the dark below.

"How deep?" Kiri asked.

"Twelve feet at the rails. Maybe fifteen over the barge." Theo checked the knife at her belt, then took it off and handed it to Kiri hilt-first—an exchange that meant more than any speech either of them could have made. "If I'm not up in twenty minutes, don't come after me. Cut the rope and take Mateo and go. What's in that box is worth more alive than I am."

"I'm not agreeing to that."

"You don't have to agree. You just have to be smart enough to do it anyway."

Kiri took the knife. Her hand stayed on Theo's a moment longer than the transfer required. "Twenty minutes," she said. "Then I start being stupid instead."

Theo almost smiled. Then she filled her lungs, tipped forward, and let the river close over her head.

The world below was another country. Brown light fell in slanted columns through the flooded canopy of the drowned forest, and the cold hit her like a hand pressing her down. She followed the rails. They ran beneath her, furred with silt and algae, and swimming along them felt like swimming down a road into a memory—the kind of dream where you walk toward a house you grew up in and the door is always just ahead.

Fifteen feet. The pressure settled on her ears and she equalized, jaw working, and kept going.

The barge appeared out of the murk all at once, the way large things do underwater—first a shadow, then a shape, then a fact. Forty feet of riveted hull lying on its side in the mud, crusted white with mineral bloom, its deck gunwale pointing at the surface like a raised arm. Six years of river had draped it in weed. Fish scattered from her approach in silver flashes. Somewhere below, in the flooded hold, the dark was absolute.

Her brother had stood on that deck. Had eaten breakfast on it, argued about load counts on it, written the letters home that their mother had read aloud with such fierce pride. And then one night in the wet season the company's numbers hadn't matched, and Emilio Sandoval had counted the loads twice, and eleven days later the survey crew's boat went down in weather that the logbooks swore had been calm.

Theo hung in the cold water above the wreck and grieved. Not the controlled, rationed grief of six years—the other kind, the raw kind, rising through her chest in bubbles she couldn't afford. Her vision blurred and it wasn't the water. She let it happen for three heartbeats. Then she kicked down toward the hold.

The hatch was jammed with silt. She dug at it, fingers numb, working the wheel in short desperate turns while her lungs began their slow arithmetic of complaint. On the third turn it gave, grinding, and a cloud of silt bloomed around her and blotted out even the brown light.

Blind now. She pulled herself through the hatchway by memory and guesswork, into the hold, into the black, one hand on the guide rope and one sweeping the dark. The company's records office had been aft, a little framed-in cubby the survey chief had called the counting house. If the box survived anywhere—

Her hand struck metal.

Not hull plating. Something smaller, angular, bolted down. She traced it: a frame, a lid, a hasp—and there, sealed under six years of river, the stamped plate she'd been promised in a dead man's letter. *Sepik Basin Survey — Original Records — Property of M.V.*

Vane's initials. Vane's property. Even down here, even drowned, the man had signed his name to other people's deaths.

Her chest was burning now. She worked the bolts—two, four, the third seized—wrenched it free with a strength she didn't know she had left, and hooked her arm through the box's handle. Too heavy to swim with cleanly. She'd drag it. She found the rope, gave two sharp pulls—the signal—and began to haul herself up hand over hand into the fading column of light above, the strongbox swinging against her hip like an anchor, like a debt, like a name finally spoken aloud.

The surface broke over her and she breathed in great tearing gulps, and Kiri's hands were under her arms before she'd finished her first breath, dragging her onto the shallow shelf of the loading platform, and Elio was prying the box from her grip, and Mateo was sitting up on his litter with his eyes wide.

Theo lay on her back in the rain and laughed—a short, cracked sound with nothing much of humor in it.

"It's here," she said. "It's all here. Every original report. Every falsified load count. Everything they drowned this valley to bury."

Kiri knelt over her, pushing wet hair from her face, and her expression was something Theo had no word for—relief and dread sharing the same face. "Then we won," Kiri said slowly. "So why do you look like someone who just heard footsteps?"

Theo sat up. She looked out across the black water, at the rails vanishing into the deep, at the drowned junction holding its secret no longer—and there, far downstream, where the channel bent toward the main river, she saw it.

A wake. Long, low, moving fast, cutting the rain-silvered water in a clean white seam.

Coming upstream.

Toward them.
