## 18. Vane at the Waterline

The river told her before anyone else did.

Theo had been standing at the edge of the drowned junction for most of an hour, watching the black water climb the rusted rails one tie at a time, when she noticed the current had changed its mind. All morning it had run hard and brown toward the coast, carrying the monsoon's first offerings—palm fronds, a drowned pig, half a canoe with someone's name still painted on the bow. Now it stuttered. Eddies turned against themselves near the far bank, and a long slick of foam gathered where no foam should gather.

Something upstream was pushing back.

She turned to Kiri, who sat on a fallen trestle beam with her father's journal wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and said nothing, because there was nothing yet to say that wouldn't be a guess. But Kiri followed her eyes to the water, and Theo watched her understand in the same slow way—the way you understand a sound in another room before you can name it.

Elio came up from the boat channel wiping his hands on his trousers. "Current's backing up," he said. "That's not rain. That's displacement."

"Displacement of what?"

"Of a hull." He looked at the water the way he'd looked at his failing patch for two hundred miles, with the resigned intimacy of a man who knew vessels the way priests knew sinners. "Something big, moving slow, coming down-channel."

Theo counted what they had. The strongbox sat under the waterline where she'd raised it an hour before, wedged beneath a collapsed section of rail bed, its contents already divided: half the original reports sealed in Kiri's oilcloth with the journal, half distributed into Elio's waterproof satchel along with the copies she'd made by lamplight over three nights of storm. The injured deckhand, Ansel, lay fevered but breathing under a lean-to they'd built from tarpaulin and wreck timber. The demolition charges she'd found cached on the old trestle—company property, six years old, still dry in their wax casings because the company never trusted anything to the weather—were wired and waiting, though she hadn't told the others yet what she intended for them.

And the river was rising. That was the arithmetic underneath everything else. The junction had been built above the flood line once; the flood line had moved; and every hour the water took another tie, another meter of bank, another argument about how long anyone could stand here at all.

She heard the engines then. Not the stutter of Elio's patched vessel but the deep, even thrum of a company patrol boat, the sound she'd spent six years training herself out of flinching at. It came around the bend downstream—*downstream*, which meant Vane had come up through the flooded channels from the other side, which meant Sarto's map had been better than she'd allowed herself to believe—and slid into view through the drowned trees like something surfacing that had been swimming beneath them all along.

It was white-hulled, company-gray at the waterline, and it carried eight men. She counted twice. Seven stood armed along the rail with the loose, bored posture of men who had done this before and expected no difficulty. The eighth stood at the bow.

Marcus Vane had aged well, which was somehow worse. Six years had silvered him at the temples and put lines around his mouth that could be read as either humor or cruelty depending on the light, and he wore a white linen shirt pressed as if he'd dressed that morning in Wewak rather than slept on a boat. He did not carry a weapon. He carried a leather folder, and he held it the way he'd once held contracts—loosely, at his side, so that everyone understood it was there without being shown.

At the rail behind him, half-hidden among the armed men, stood Sarto. The deserter would not meet her eyes. He looked instead at the water, at the junction, at anything that wasn't the face of the woman whose route map he'd sold.

The patrol boat came alongside the ruined loading platform with a bump of rubber fenders, and a deckhand threw a line to a bollard that hadn't held a rope since before the accident. Vane stepped onto the platform as though stepping onto his own property, which, Theo thought, in every way that mattered, he believed it was.

"Tomas Reyes," he said pleasantly. "Or should we dispense with that now? The river's high, the audience is small, and I've read your handwriting on enough survey reports to know Tomas Reyes doesn't exist."

"Theo Sandoval," she said. "You have me at a disadvantage, Mr. Vane. You know my name and I know your crimes."

"My *alleged*—" He stopped himself, smiled, corrected course. "No. Actually, let's not do that either." He glanced back at his men, then at the flooded rails, and his voice dropped into something almost conversational, almost warm, the register he'd used with her once across a drafting table in Port Moresby when they'd both been young enough to believe ambition was the same thing as purpose. "There's no one here to perform for, Theo. No board, no regulators, no witnesses worth the name. So I'll say plainly what I came to say, and you can decide whether to make this difficult."

Behind her, she heard Kiri shift her weight on the trestle beam. Elio had drifted, without appearing to drift, to a position between Vane's men and the lean-to where Ansel lay. Four of them. Eight of Vane's. The river rising.

"I'm listening," Theo said.

"You always were." He opened the folder—not to hand it over, just to touch it, to remind them both it existed. "Here is what happened six years ago, since you've clearly spent them constructing your own version. The basin lines were profitable only on paper. The load reports were fiction. Everyone senior knew it; the question was never *whether* the numbers were false but who would be positioned to survive when the falsification surfaced. Your brother found out. He was going to talk. And the survey crew was sent into a channel my office had already flagged as unstable, with equipment my office had already certified as sound, on a day my office had already cleared." He paused, letting the machinery of it show. "I didn't kill anyone, Theo. I signed a routing order. The river did the rest. Rivers are so much more thorough than men."

The words landed in her chest like cold water finding a crack. Not because they were surprising—she had suspected every piece of it, assembled it herself in the dark over six years—but because hearing them spoken aloud, casually, by the man who'd written them, stripped away the last comfortable fiction that what had happened had been tragedy rather than policy.

"And me?" she said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later. "The engineer who helped close the lines. Was I part of the routing order too?"

"You were part of the *aftermath*. Which I engineered less successfully." Something moved behind his eyes—regret, or its excellent imitation. "I needed the lines closed cleanly. A closure signed by someone with a conscience, someone the families might actually believe, someone with a personal reason to want the whole basin buried. You were perfect, Theo. You closed the lines to punish yourself, and the company got exactly the tomb it needed." He spread his hands. "I've never had to threaten you. I've only ever had to *offer* you things."

"And the photograph of my brother."

"A tool. Like the map. Like Sarto." His gaze flicked past her shoulder, and Sarto flinched. "Tools get used. Some of them break."

The river rose another tie while he spoke. Theo felt it more than saw it—the platform's edge going soft underfoot, the smell of the water changing as it reached new ground. She let him talk. Every minute he talked was a minute the water climbed, a minute Elio spent moving quietly along the collapsed rail bed doing something she couldn't watch him do, a minute Kiri spent with her hands inside the oilcloth bundle doing something else.

"What do you want, Marcus?" she asked finally. "Say it plainly, since we're being plain."

"The strongbox. All of it—originals, copies, the journal, whatever the girl's been reading aloud at night thinking I don't have ears on this river." He nodded toward Kiri without looking at her. "In exchange: the photograph, your brother's name restored posthumously to whatever dignity the company's lawyers permit, passage out for you and your crew, and my personal guarantee—which you may weigh for whatever it's worth—that the basin stays closed and the dead stay dead. Everyone walks away. The story ends here, at the waterline, where stories about this place have always ended."

"And if I refuse?"

He sighed, genuinely, and for a moment looked tired in a way that seemed real. "Then I take it. There are seven rifles on that boat, Theo, and you have a sick man, a leaking dinghy, and two women. I'd rather not. Violence makes such a mess of the accounting. But I didn't come upriver through monsoon floodwater to negotiate twice."

She looked past him at the water. It had reached the third rail now. The old trestle, where the charges waited, stood hip-deep at its footings, its timbers groaning in the current with a low note she could feel through her boots. Rising water meant rising pressure against the old pilings. Rising water meant the demolition charges' wiring, run along the trestle's spine, was climbing toward the waterline too. Rising water meant—

Rising water meant time was on her side, if she could keep him talking on the platform and off the trestle, keep his boats in the open channel and his attention on her face.

Hatred, she had learned over six years, was useless as fuel. It burned hot and fast and left nothing. But hatred refined—hatred cooled and drawn into wire, cut into precise lengths, wired to a charge and timed—hatred like that could bring down a trestle.

"Let me ask you something," she said, and stepped closer to the platform's edge, close enough that he'd have to look up slightly at her, close enough that his men's rifles tracked her and she ignored them. "The night the crew went in. Did you read my brother's last report? The one he filed the morning of—the one flagging the channel, the one that was pulled from the record?"

Vane's smile flickered. Just barely. "There was no such report."

"There was. I've read it. It's in the box you're asking for." She let that sit. "He wrote it four hours before your routing order sent them in. He named you in it, Marcus. By name, in his own hand, with the falsified figures copied out beside it. He died knowing exactly who had killed him, and the company buried the report in a strongbox at the bottom of a junction, and you have spent six years not knowing whether it survived." She watched the flicker become something colder. "You're not here for the company. The company has lawyers and deniability. You're here because *you* need to see that paper burn with your own eyes. Because a man who lets a river do his killing still can't sleep unless he watches the evidence drown."

"That's enough," Vane said, and his voice had lost its warmth entirely.

"It isn't," Theo said. "Because here's what you've never understood about me, in all these years. You think I closed those lines out of guilt. Guilt is your currency, Marcus—you spend it, you invest it, you understand it. But I didn't sign that closure out of guilt." The river surged against the footings, and she raised her voice over it without hurrying. "I signed it because it was the only way to keep the evidence findable. A closed line gets surveyed. An abandoned one gets forgotten. I kept the map. I kept the coordinates. I spent six years making sure that if the truth ever surfaced, it would surface *complete*—with names, with orders, with the routing document that has your signature on it sitting right now in a waterproof case not ten meters from where you're standing."

She saw it land. Saw the calculation behind his eyes stall and restart, stall and restart—the smooth operator encountering, for perhaps the first time in his career, a variable he had not modeled. He had prepared for a guilty woman. He had prepared for a frightened one. He had not prepared for an engineer who had spent six years building a machine out of her own ruin and had only been waiting for the man who broke it to walk into range.

"You're bluffing," he said.

"The whole basin is a bluff," Theo said. "Ask the river."

And the river answered.

It came as a sound first—a deep, structural groan from the old trestle, louder than any it had made before, and then a crack like a rifle shot as one of the mid-span pilings, undermined by three days of rising flood, shifted against its neighbors. The platform shuddered under all their feet. One of Vane's armed men stumbled, caught himself, swore. Downstream, the patrol boat's fenders squealed against the bollard as the surge rocked it.

Vane turned toward the sound—an instinct, a half-second of divided attention—and in that half-second Theo saw Elio, forty feet down the rail bed, give her a single nod.

The charges were live. The wire ran wet along the trestle's spine to the plunger hidden under wreck timber behind her. The river had risen to within a hand's width of the lowest casing. Everything she had promised him—the complete truth, the routing order, the end of his six clean years—was true, and none of it mattered, because the truth had never been the weapon. The river was the weapon. It had always been the river.

"Marcus," she said, and he turned back to her, and she let him see everything in her face at last—six years of it, unhidden. "My brother's report names you. The routing order names you. The load reports name you. And in about thirty seconds, the only road in or out of this junction is going to be at the bottom of the channel, with your boat on the wrong side of it."

His hand moved toward the folder. Toward the pistol, she realized, that one of his men was easing from under his jacket.

"The photograph," Theo said quickly, loudly, trading the last word she had for the last second she needed. "You still have the photograph. My brother alive after the accident. Give me that, and I'll give you the channel out before the water takes the trestle—"

But Vane was no longer listening. He was looking past her, at the trestle, at the water climbing its legs, and his face was doing the arithmetic she'd already finished—and losing.

Behind him, Sarto dropped the rifle he'd been holding and put both hands in the air.

"Don't," Sarto said, to no one, to everyone. "Don't be on this bridge when—"

The trestle groaned again, longer this time, a sound like a living thing deciding.

Theo's hand closed on the plunger beneath the wreck timber, and the river rose to meet it.
