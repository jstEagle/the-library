## 19. The River Takes Its Evidence

Kiri Anselm had learned to read her father's silences the way other children learned letters, and in the six years since his drowning she had learned to read Theo Sandoval's the same way. So when Theo came up from the waterline at dawn with the strongbox dripping against her hip and said nothing—said absolutely nothing, only set it down on the rotted planking of the old loading platform and looked at it as a woman looks at a coffin she has just opened—Kiri knew that whatever was inside had changed the shape of everything that would come after.

The junction lay around them like the bones of some enormous animal that had lain down in the river and never gotten up. Rails ran out of the black water on both banks, furred with rust and algae, vanishing into the flooded forest where the monsoon had turned the world into a single green murmur. The trestle approach—the great timber spine that had once carried loaded ore cars across the channel—still stood on its pilings for two hundred meters before it simply stopped, sheared off years ago by flood or demolition, its broken end jutting over the current like a snapped rib.

It was toward that broken end that Theo looked now, and Kiri saw something in her face she had seen only once before: the night Theo had confessed on the storm-bound bank, hands open in the rain, telling her about the falsified reports and her brother and her own signature on the closure order. Then, as now, Theo's face had gone very still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"You found it," Kiri said. Not a question. The strongbox answered for itself, its brass fittings green with age, its lock already sprung—Theo must have forced it underwater, down there in the dark among the barge ribs where her brother had spent his last living hours.

"I found it." Theo's voice was rough, as if the river had gotten into it. "Original load reports. Survey logs. And this." She drew out an oilcloth packet, unwrapped it on her knee: papers, browned but legible, and atop them a single sheet bearing the company letterhead and a signature Kiri had memorized from her father's journal without ever expecting to see it rendered in ink so confident, so untroubled. *M. Vane.* Dated eleven days before the accident.

"He signed the order," Kiri whispered. "He ordered the loads falsified."

"He ordered them falsified, and when my brother refused to carry the order through, he let the crew go out anyway. Undermanned. On a barge he'd already condemned in an internal memo he never filed." Theo folded the oilcloth with hands that did not shake, which told Kiri how much it cost her that they didn't. "Everything they needed to hang Anselm's name on the disaster. Everything they needed to clear him. It was all here, under forty feet of water, while I spent six years burning maps to keep people away from it."

Kiri reached out and covered Theo's hand with her own. Theo let her. That small permission, after weeks of deflected questions and careful distances, felt like a door opening in a wall Kiri had been leaning against since Wewak.

"We take it back downriver," Elio said from behind them. He had come up onto the platform quietly, the way he did everything, and stood now with the injured deckhand's bandages bundled under one arm, his eyes moving constantly along the channel the way they always moved, reading water the way Theo read grades and Kiri read journals. "We run for the coast, we copy it a hundred times, we—"

"No," Theo said.

Elio stopped. Even the rain seemed to lean in.

"The patrol boat is upstream," Theo said. "Vane is on it. He knows exactly where we are because Sarto gave him our route, and Sarto will have given him one more thing besides—that I have the box. He isn't coming to negotiate. He's coming to make sure no one else ever reads what's in here, and he's coming with armed men and a boat that draws less water than ours and doesn't leak." She looked down the channel, where the morning mist was beginning to lift off the current, and Kiri watched her do the arithmetic she had been doing since before dawn. "We can't outrun him. We can't outfight him. But we don't have to. We just have to make sure that by the time he reaches this platform, there is nothing here left to capture."

Kiri understood then—or began to—and the understanding went through her like cold water. "You're going to blow the trestle."

"The charges were left on the approach piers when the line was closed. Company demolition, company inventory, logged and then forgotten because no one imagined anyone would ever want the junction back." Theo's mouth curved, and it was not quite a smile; it was the expression of a woman meeting an old debt. "I helped log them. Six years ago. I signed the manifest myself."

* * *

They worked through the morning while the rain came and went in long gray curtains, and Kiri carried charges.

That was the thing she would remember afterward—not the explosion, not the chaos, but the hours of labor before it, the four of them moving along the rotted trestle walkway with crates of wet dynamite and lengths of fuse, Theo directing every placement from memory as though she had walked this structure yesterday instead of six years ago. *Third pier from the shear point. Both faces. Tie the pairs high, above the flood line.* Her voice was calm, almost gentle, and Kiri realized that this was what competence looked like when it had been waiting six years for its purpose. Theo was not brave in the way stories claimed men were brave, standing tall and shouting. She was brave the way water was persistent—quietly, everywhere, wearing stone.

Elio ferried the injured man, Rennick, down to the boat and rigged him into the stern under an awning of oilcloth, and Kiri saw the boatman pause there, one hand on the gunwale, looking at the hull patch that had been failing since the first week. Their eyes met across the water.

"It won't come back," Elio said. Not bitterly. Simply, the way he reported depths and snags. "Whatever happens today, this boat is finished. One way."

"Then we'd better make the way count," Kiri said.

He nodded slowly, and something passed between them that had no words in it—a recognition, maybe, between people who had both spent their lives keeping other people's secrets afloat in leaking vessels.

By early afternoon the charges were set. Theo had wired them in series along two hundred meters of trestle, the main concentration at the three center piers, and she had done something else besides, something Kiri only gradually understood as she watched her work: she had placed the firing point not on the near bank, where Vane's men would land, but on the far side, reachable only by a circuitous path through the flooded forest that Theo had scouted alone at first light. The trap was not for Vane. The trap was geometry itself. Whoever held the junction approach could be cut off from whoever crossed it, and Theo intended to hold the far side.

"She's going to strand herself," Kiri murmured to Elio.

"She's going to strand everyone," Elio corrected. "Including us. That's the part you should worry about."

* * *

The patrol boat came at half past three, sliding out of the upstream mist with the grey confidence of a thing that believed itself inevitable.

Kiri crouched in the fern cover above the far landing with the detonator box on her knees—Theo had put it in her hands twenty minutes earlier with a look that asked and answered a question at once—and watched Vane's vessel nose into the channel below the broken trestle. Armed men lined its rail. Sarto stood at the bow, pointing, and even at this distance Kiri could read the eagerness in his posture, the little dance of a man arriving at the place where betrayal pays out.

Theo stood on the open platform beside the strongbox, alone, visible, her hands empty at her sides. It was a performance, Kiri realized. Every inch of it. The solitary figure, the prize displayed, the posture of surrender—it was theater staged for a man who believed himself the audience.

Vane's voice carried across the water, amplified by the channel's acoustics, warm and reasonable as ever. "Sandoval! Or should I say Reyes? You've made this remarkably easy. Put the box on the platform and step back, and we'll discuss your brother's photograph like civilized people."

"There's nothing to discuss," Theo called back, and her voice did not carry the way Vane's did, but it reached, clear and flat. "You signed the order, Marcus. I'm holding it. Dated eleven days before the accident. Your initials are on the falsification schedule and your memo condemning the barge is in the same box. You didn't come here to buy evidence. You came to bury it deeper than the river did."

Something changed in Vane's posture—the warmth draining out of it, the reasonable man falling away like a coat dropped from shoulders. He raised one hand, and the armed men began to ready the patrol boat's approach to the near landing, below the trestle's broken end.

"Now," Theo said, quietly, to the ferns, to Kiri, to the whole listening basin.

Kiri threw the switch.

The world turned inside out.

She had imagined thunder; it was not thunder. It was the sound of the earth's own bones breaking—six charges speaking nearly as one, a white flash along the trestle's length, and then the great timber spine lifted, buckled, and folded into the channel like a knee giving way. The blast wave hit the water first, a rolling wall that slammed the patrol boat against its own mooring lines and drenched Vane's men where they stood. Then the sound arrived, rolling up the valley, echoing off hills Kiri could not see through the rain, and the trestle came down in three vast sections, planking and piling and sixty years of iron spikes, collapsing directly across the channel between the platform and the near landing.

When the spray settled, the geography had been rewritten. The junction approach was gone. The near bank, where Vane and eight armed men stood ankle-deep in wreckage and churning silt, was separated from the far bank, from the platform, from the strongbox, from Theo, by fifty meters of water choked with floating timber and a current that had been freed by the collapse and now ran hard through the gap.

And Theo Sandoval was already moving.

Kiri had wondered, in the long nights, whether she truly knew the woman she loved—whether the discipline and the coldness were armor over emptiness or armor over fire. She stopped wondering at 3:41 in the afternoon, watching Theo sprint the length of the platform, scoop the strongbox under one arm, and vanish into the tree line, all in the time it took Vane to stop shouting orders at drowned wreckage and understand what had been taken from him.

* * *

They copied the documents by lamplight in a leech-hollow behind the collapsed trestle, and it was the strangest hour of Kiri Anselm's life.

Rain hammered the forest canopy overhead. Downstream, faint through the trees, came the sounds of Vane's men trying to free their boat from the debris field—axes on timber, shouted frustration, once a pistol shot that hit nothing and meant nothing. Upstream, the channel ran loud and new over the grave of the trestle. And in between, in a hollow floored with wet leaves, four people sat around a shielded lamp making copies of the dead.

There were no more pens than hands. Theo wrote; Kiri dried each page over the lamp's heat; Elio folded copies into oilcloth bundles with the same precision he used on knots; Rennick, feverish but lucid, addressed the bundles from a list Theo recited—newspapers in three capitals, the mining regulator, the families of the dead survey crew, a magistrate who had once ruled against the company and been reassigned for it. The original pages went back into the strongbox. The copies went into two bundles.

"Two bundles," Theo said, holding them up in the lamplight. "Listen to me, both of you, because this is the whole design. No one carries everything. If Vane takes me, he gets the originals—which prove nothing he can't already deny, because he'll claim they're forged. If he takes one bundle, the other still reaches the coast. If he takes both carriers—" her eyes went to Kiri, then Elio, and Kiri saw her refuse to finish the sentence, refuse even to spend the words on it— "then the story is already loose. Copies breed. Paper travels faster than patrol boats. That is the entire point. Justice that lives in one body can be killed in one body. So we don't keep it in one body. Any body. Including mine."

"You included yourself," Kiri said.

"I especially included myself." Theo's voice was steady, but her hand, passing Kiri her bundle, held on a half-second longer than the transfer required. "Six years ago I thought the safest place for the truth was nowhere. Buried, burned, drowned. I was wrong. The safest place for the truth is everywhere. In a dozen hands, in a hundred mailbags, in minds that won't fit in any strongbox." She looked at the lamplight, at the rain, at the ruined trestle standing in her memory behind her eyes. "I built my life on the idea that invisibility was safety. It wasn't. It was just a slower way of letting them win."

Elio took his bundle and stowed it inside his shirt, against his skin, wrapped in oilcloth, and Kiri saw him settle it the way a man settles a child against his chest. "Where do I go?" he asked.

"Not with us. Not yet." Theo spread the crude map she'd drawn in charcoal on a scrap of tarpaulin. "The boat is done, but the river isn't. There's a trading post two days downstream—Mabuike's. Rennick needs a doctor and Mabuike owes me a favor from before either of you knew my name. Take him. Take your bundle. Mail it from the coast yourself, hand to hand, no post offices, no intermediaries. Then disappear, Elio. Properly. Go be no one somewhere green."

"And you?"

Theo was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked on the leaves above them.

"Vane is going to get across that channel eventually—tonight, tomorrow, it doesn't matter. When he does, he'll come for the box, and the box will be here, and I'll be here with it." She met Kiri's eyes. "Because someone has to stand at the door while the rest of the truth runs. Because if I run too, he burns the basin looking for all of us, and Rennick dies in a boat, and Mabuike's post burns, and everything we did today becomes just another disaster in a basin full of them. But if he comes for me—if I'm the only target—then he spends his fury on one woman instead of scattering it across everyone I've dragged into this."

"That's not strategy," Kiri said. Her voice cracked on the word. "That's sacrifice."

"It's both." Theo's hand found hers in the dark, closed around it, brief and fierce. "It's the only kind worth doing. Sacrifice without design is just dying. Design without sacrifice is just cleverness. I've been clever for six years, Kiri. Today I'm being useful."

* * *

They parted at the water's edge in the last grey light, and Kiri carried her bundle into the flooded forest with the papers against her heart and the taste of river water and woodsmoke in her mouth.

Behind her, she heard Elio push the dying boat off into the current with Rennick aboard, heard the engine catch, cough, catch again, and begin the long stuttering journey downstream. Ahead of her lay two days of swamp, leeches, and the monsoon, and at the end of it a coast, a post office, a world that did not yet know what she was carrying.

But she did not move immediately. She stood a moment longer in the trees, looking back.

On the far platform, small in the rain and distance, a lantern had been lit. Theo sat beside the strongbox with the lamp turned low, visible to anyone crossing the water, a single fixed point of light in all that drowned darkness—an invitation, a challenge, a promise kept. Vane's men had gotten a boat free of the wreckage; Kiri could see their torches moving on the near bank, gathering, regrouping, and she knew that by morning the channel would be crossed and the reckoning would begin.

Theo had chosen to be the door. Kiri pressed the oilcloth bundle tighter beneath her jacket, turned north into the flooded forest, and began to walk.

Behind her, the river ran loud over the ruins of the trestle, carrying the evidence of everything away in every direction at once—and ahead, somewhere in the dark, the monsoon was rising.
