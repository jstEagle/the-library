## 20. Outlast the Monsoon

The river did not separate them so much as erase the distance between them into something neither side could cross.

Theo stood at the lip of the collapsed trestle and watched the water where Vane's men had been an hour ago. The channel that had run shin-deep along the old approach was now a brown torrent wide as a street, tearing branches off the drowned forest and spinning them downstream like kindling. Somewhere on the far bank—she could not see them through the rain-haze, only hear them, once, a shout carried sideways on the wind—Marcus Vane's party waited out the same storm she did.

Two banks. One flood. And between them, drifting in the strongbox at her feet, the truth.

She had done it. That was the strange part, the part her hands still hadn't absorbed. She had stood on the trestle with the detonator box in both hands and pulled the handle, and the world had cracked open, and the junction approach—the rails, the pilings, sixty years of company iron—had folded into the river like a knee giving way. She had spent six years keeping those lines closed in ink and silence, and it had taken four seconds of thunder to close them forever.

Kiri came up beside her, hooded, gray-faced with exhaustion, one hand pressed to the bandage roll under her coat where she'd split the copied reports into oilcloth packets. Elio crouched behind them with the injured deckhand's litter propped against a fallen trunk, and Mateo—the deckhand, Mateo, she'd finally learned his name properly somewhere around the third day of carrying him—lay breathing shallow but steady, fevered, alive. Alive because Kiri had refused to let him be anything else.

"He can't see us from there," Kiri said. "Not through this."

"He doesn't need to see us." Theo wiped rain from her face with the back of her wrist. "He needs to know we're here. That we didn't drown. That the papers didn't drown with us."

"And then what? You shout across a flood?"

"That's exactly what I do."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the old loading embankment, a shoulder of packed earth that had been built to hold rail cars above the wet season and now held four people, a wrecked boat's worth of salvage, and half a truth apiece. Elio rigged a shelter from the tarpaulin and two paddles lashed to a broken piling. The rain came down in sheets that made the whole basin sound like applause for something terrible.

Theo took inventory by feel: three days of rice, half a tin of oil, the medical kit down to its last clean cloth, the detonator box with one charge left in it—she'd kept the last charge, out of habit or instinct, she wasn't sure which—and the strongbox itself, empty now except for river water and the ghost of everything it had held. The copies were distributed. Kiri wore hers against her ribs. Elio had his sewn into the false bottom of his pack, and he'd shown her the stitching twice without being asked, which was its own kind of oath. A third set, the smallest, the most damning pages—her brother's final load reports, signed in a dead man's hand—Theo carried herself, wrapped in oilcloth inside her shirt, over her heart like a second heartbeat.

No single capture ends it. That had been the design. She'd built it the way you build a bridge pier: assuming the flood will come for it eventually.

"You should sleep," Kiri said, settling beside her under the tarp. "You haven't slept since the trestle."

"Neither have you."

"I slept an hour while you watched the water." Kiri's voice was hoarse from shouting during the collapse, from hauling the litter through flooded forest, from all of it. "I'm asking you to match me."

Theo almost smiled. Almost. "When this is over—"

"Don't say when this is over. People who say that stop doing things now."

So Theo leaned her shoulder against Kiri's shoulder, and they sat like that watching the rain hammer the channel, and for ten minutes—she counted them, because counting was how she kept herself together—it was enough.

Then, across the flood, a light. A lantern raised high on a pole, swinging in a slow arc. Deliberate. A signal.

And beneath the rain, threaded thin through the roar of the water, a voice calling her name.

Not *Tomas*. Not the name she'd worn like armor for six years.

*"Theodora."*

* * *

Elio wanted to go to the bank. He wanted it badly, and he said so with his whole body, standing at the waterline with his fists on his hips like a man arguing with the sea itself. "You can't parley with him. He came here with guns. Sarto told him everything—he knows your route, your crew, your face. He knows you're down to four people and one of them is carried."

"He knows all that," Theo agreed, "and he's raising a lantern instead of waiting for the flood to drop and coming across. Ask yourself why."

Elio asked himself. She watched him ask himself. "Because he can't cross either."

"Because he can't cross either. And because whatever he came here to take, he no longer has a way to be sure he can take it. The trestle's gone. His boats are upstream behind a flooded channel. His men are cold and wet and they've watched their guide desert and their prize burn copies of the evidence in front of them—" she paused, corrected herself, "—*distribute* copies, which is worse. Marcus Vane is a man whose arithmetic just stopped working. Men like that want to talk."

"Men like that want to talk right up until they shoot you," Elio said.

"Yes," Theo said. "Which is why I'll stand where the water stands between us."

Kiri caught her arm before she stepped out. Not gripping—resting, the way you rest a hand on a door you're not sure you want closed. "Whatever you offer him," Kiri said, "remember my father's name is in those papers too. Remember what they wrote about him. If Vane walks away clean while Anselm stays buried—"

"I remember," Theo said. "I've remembered every day since Wewak."

The rain had slackened—not stopped, never stopped, but thinned to a steady gray needle-fall that let sound carry. Theo walked down to the bank alone, hands empty and visible, and stood on the mud at the edge of the roaring channel.

On the far side, perhaps forty meters of white water away, lanterns bobbed. Figures moved behind them. And then Marcus Vane came down to his own waterline, and even at that distance, even through rain, she could see what the flood had done to him.

He looked smaller. That was the first thing. Six years ago he had filled doorways; he had filled rooms; he had filled her entire field of vision until there was nothing else in the world worth looking at. Now he stood hunched in a soaked coat with his hair plastered flat and his shoulders drawn in, and he looked like a man standing in the ruins of his own house.

"Theodora!" His voice fought the river and mostly lost. "You're alive!"

"You sound disappointed!"

A pause. Then, unbelievably, a laugh—short, cracked, real. "No. God help me, no. I'm not disappointed." He came closer to his edge of the water, close enough that she could see him raise both hands, empty, mirroring hers. "I want to talk."

"We're talking!"

"I mean terms! I mean an end to this!" He gestured behind him—at his men, at the flood, at the whole drowned world. "Look at it! Look what it's already taken! I've lost a boat, a guide, and my patience, and the river hasn't even finished with us. Neither of us crosses today. Probably not tomorrow. Maybe not for a week. So we talk, Theodora, or we stand on our own mud and rot."

It was, she had to admit, a fair summary. She cupped her hands around her mouth. "Say your terms first. I want to hear how desperate you are."

Another pause—longer this time. When he spoke again, the performance had drained out of his voice, and what remained was worse than anger. It was honesty.

"They're gone," he said. "The originals. I watched your boatman sew them into his pack like a man sewing up his own coffin, and I understood—I understood right then—that it was finished. Even if I took the strongbox, even if I put a bullet in every one of you, the copies exist. In three places. On three bodies. Scattered into a country where I have no reach." He shook his head, and rain flew off it. "Do you know what I felt when I understood that? Relief. Actual relief. Six years I've carried it, Theodora. Six years of knowing what's in those reports and paying to keep it buried and telling myself it was strategy. It was never strategy. It was a stone in my chest that got heavier every year, and tonight the river is offering to take it off me, and I find I *want* to let it."

Theo said nothing. Her heart was pounding in a rhythm she distrusted—hope has a pulse too, and it lies.

"So here are my terms," Vane called. "The photograph. Your brother's name cleared in full, in writing, with my testimony attached if it comes to that. My silence—permanent, verifiable, I don't care how you verify it, follow me for the rest of my life if it pleases you. In exchange—" and here his voice did something she had never heard it do, something small and stripped, "—you let me disappear. Actually disappear. No trial, no newspapers, no name in anyone's story but mine. I'll go somewhere the company's money can't buy me back and its lawyers can't reach. I'll grow old being no one. Which, I'm told—" and now the laugh again, terrible and thin, "—is a thing you're an expert in."

The rain fell. The river roared. Theo stood very still on her bank and thought about mercy, which is a heavier thing than vengeance, though no one tells you that beforehand.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do since the trestle fell: she could give him to the law. Or what passed for it. She could send the copies to the regulators and the newspapers and let the machine grind him the way it had ground her brother—slowly, partially, with the company's fingerprints all over the gears. She knew how that went. She had watched it go that way for six years. The law had protected the company through the deaths of nine men and the ruin of her family; the law would protect it through Marcus Vane too, and Vane knew it, and that knowledge was the only reason he dared stand on that bank and bargain.

Or she could take his surrender. Take the photograph. Take his written testimony, sealed, dated, witnessed by Kiri and Elio—a confession in a dead-drop envelope, insurance against the day the company came looking for its missing man. And let him vanish into the enormous anonymity of the world, punished by nothing but the punishment he'd already chosen: to be no one, forever, carrying the stone.

"You killed my brother," she shouted across the water. It wasn't a question and it wasn't an accusation. It was a fact being placed on the table between them, where facts belong.

"Yes," Vane said. No flinch. No lawyer's hedging. "I signed the orders that falsified the load reports, and I ordered the survey barge loaded past its rating anyway, and when it went down I let Daniel take the blame because a dead scapegoat was cheaper than a live scandal. Yes. All of it. I'd sign it again in front of God if God would hold the pen steady." His voice broke on the last word and he let it break, didn't try to gather it. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking exile. There's a difference, and I know exactly how wide it is."

Behind her, up the embankment, she heard Kiri come down through the mud—she didn't turn, but she knew the weight of those footsteps now, knew them the way you know weather. Kiri stopped beside her and stared across the flood at the man who had buried her father's name under his signature.

"My father," Kiri called, and her voice carried better than Theo's ever had, hard and clear as a bell. "Anselm. Survey chief. They wrote *negligence* on his grave. Are you going to unsay that?"

Vane was quiet for a long moment. "Yes," he said. "In writing. With my signature. Tonight, if you can get paper across the water, and if the river lets me live long enough to write it."

"It won't bring him back," Kiri said.

"No," Vane said. "Nothing brings anyone back. I learned that the expensive way."

Kiri turned to Theo. Rain ran down her face and she didn't wipe it away, and her eyes in the lantern-light were doing several things at once, grief and fury and a weary, reluctant clarity. "He's broken," she said quietly. "You can see it from here. The river did it, not you. And a broken man's word is worth exactly what you make it worth—so make it worth something. Paper. Signature. Witnessed. Held by us, not traded to him."

"That's what I was going to say."

"I know," Kiri said. "That's why I love you. Go finish it."

* * *

The negotiation, such as it was, took the shape of the river: slow, loud, conducted in fragments.

They couldn't pass paper across forty meters of floodwater, so they improvised. Elio found a length of fishing line in the salvage and weighted it with a bolt; three failed casts and one heroic fourth put the line across the channel, and then a heavier cord, and then—over the course of an hour that felt like a year—a thin rope, sagging into the current, thrumming with the flood. Across it, hand over hand, Elio sent a waterproof satchel containing three sheets of paper, a pencil stub, and instructions in Theo's own handwriting:

*Write it all. Every order, every date, every signature, including yours. Sign it. Date it. Address it to no one. It goes in a sealed envelope that leaves this basin in three different directions, and if any harm comes to any member of my crew, ever, it opens.*

The satchel went across. The lanterns on the far bank clustered. Time passed—two hours, maybe three, marked only by the rain thickening and thinning like breath. Theo sat on the embankment with Kiri's head on her shoulder and did not sleep and did not speak, and it was the most honest conversation they'd had in days.

The satchel came back across the rope near midnight, heavier than it went. Inside: eleven pages, front and back, in Vane's cramped, furious, unmistakable hand. Orders falsified. Loads exceeded. The barge condemned in his own private correspondence months before it sank. Daniel Sandoval blamed by name, with a margin note in Vane's writing—*he was going to talk to the commission, this solves it*—that made Theo's vision blur until she had to press the heels of her hands against her eyes and breathe.

And beneath it all, separately folded, the photograph. Her brother, alive, three days after the accident the company swore had killed him—standing outside a company office in a company hat, and the date stamped on the border like a verdict. Proof he'd survived. Proof they'd known. Proof of whatever happened after, in the gap between this image and the death certificate, the gap where the truth lived and where she would spend the rest of her life digging if she had to.

But that was tomorrow's work. Chapter twenty-two's work. Tonight there was only this: the photograph in her hand, warm from her grip, and the river between her and the man who had made it necessary.

She stood one more time at the waterline. Vane came down to meet her, lantern raised.

"It's done," she called. "Signed, sealed, witnessed. It leaves this basin in three directions the moment the flood drops. You'll never know which three."

"I assumed as much." He lifted the lantern higher, and by its light she saw his face clearly for the first time in six years, and it was not the face of a schemer or a monster. It was the face of a tired man standing in the wreckage of his own decisions, and somehow that was the most damning thing of all. "Theodora. One question. Why? You could have had me hanged—or tried to. Why the mercy?"

Theo looked at him across the roaring water, at the small soaked figure of the man who had taken everything and was now, piece by piece, handing it back, and she gave him the truest answer she had.

"Because the river already sentenced you," she said. "To be no one. Forever. I'm just declining to interrupt the execution."

Vane laughed—that cracked, ruined laugh—and raised the lantern in something that was not quite a salute. "Then we understand each other."

"When the water drops, you go north. We go south. If our paths ever cross again, I won't recognize you."

"No," he said. "You won't. Nobody will. That's the whole sentence, isn't it?"

She turned and walked up the embankment, and did not look back, and the not-looking-back cost her more than the trestle had.

* * *

Near dawn the rain finally eased to a whisper, and the flood began, imperceptibly, to fall. Theo stood watch while the others slept—Mateo's fever had broken in the night, another debt owed to Kiri's stubbornness—and she laid the photograph on her knee and studied it until the light came up enough to see every detail.

Her brother's face. Alive. Three days after the world ended.

There was a story in that gap, and she was going to have it, every word of it, whatever it cost. But first—

First there was a letter to write in her own name, and a map to keep instead of burn, and a woman asleep under a tarpaulin whom she intended to wake gently, and a long river south, and the rest of a life that had just, terrifyingly, become visible.

The water dropped a hand's width by sunrise.

Theo folded the photograph away over her heart, beside the reports, and woke the camp.
