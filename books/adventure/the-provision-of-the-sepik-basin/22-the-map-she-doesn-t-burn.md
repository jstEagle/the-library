## 22. The Map She Doesn't Burn

The ink dried slower than she expected.

Theo Sandoval sat at the long table in the reading room of the Port Moresby gazette office, a sheet of foolscap before her and a bottle of iron-gall ink open at her elbow, and watched the letters of her own name take their time becoming permanent. *Theodora M. Sandoval.* Six years of signing *Tomas Reyes* had made the act feel foreign, like writing with her left hand. The pen wanted to curve back into the old alias; she made it walk straight.

Around her the office smelled of newsprint and lamp oil and the particular staleness of rooms where men argued about other men's reputations. It was late afternoon in the dry season, and through the louvred windows came the sound of the town going about its business—carts, gulls, someone laughing near the wharf. Ordinary sounds. She had spent six years learning to hear ordinary sounds as cover, as camouflage, as the noise that hid her. Now they were just sounds, and she was just a woman at a table, and no one looked at her twice except to wonder why her hand shook.

It didn't shake from fear. That was the strange part. She kept waiting for the fear to arrive—the old familiar dread that had lived under her ribs so long she'd mistaken it for an organ—and it did not come. What came instead was something closer to vertigo, the sensation of standing on solid ground after years aboard a rolling deck.

Below her signature she had written the letter itself, four paragraphs, drafted eleven times on the boat coming downriver and twice more since. It named the company. It named the closed lines—every one, with mileposts, with dates of closure, with the survey numbers that had been falsified. It named the dead: Anselm, Ferro, Okada, Brandt, and Luis Sandoval, surveyor, her brother, killed not by river flood as the record claimed but by a decision made above him, in a room with better furniture, to bury the cost overruns inside a disaster. It named Marcus Vane, assistant director of operations, author of the orders, and noted that Mr. Vane had since resigned his position and left the territory, whereabouts unknown—which was true, because exile was exile even when you negotiated its terms yourself, shouting across forty meters of brown monsoon water while the river ate his boat out from under him.

And it said, in the final paragraph, the thing she had rewritten most often:

*I am the engineer who signed the closure reports. I signed them knowing what I knew and not knowing what I now know, and I have enclosed both sets of documents. Let the inquiry reopen. Let the families read what I read. I make no claim to innocence and ask for none.*

The editor—a heavy, sweating man named Calloway who had listened to their whole story with the expression of a man being handed a live grenade wrapped in ribbon—had offered to soften it. *Anonymous sources protect everyone,* he'd said. *We could run it without your name.*

She had thought about it for exactly as long as it took to drink half a cup of his terrible coffee.

*No,* she'd said. *That's how it worked before. Everyone protected, nobody named, my brother in an unmarked grave with a lie for an epitaph.* She'd set the cup down. *Print the name. Print mine first.*

So here she was, watching the ink dry on the first honest signature she'd put to paper in six years.

Kiri Anselm stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the street. She'd cut her hair shorter since the basin—it grew fast in the wet season and she'd gotten tired of it clinging to her neck in the heat—but she still carried her father's journal everywhere, wrapped in oilcloth, slung across her body like a bandolier. Theo had seen her sleep with it more than once. Some cargo you never set down.

"He's asking if you want a witness," Kiri said, nodding toward Calloway's office. "For the affidavit."

"I've got one." Theo capped the ink. "You."

"You're not supposed to use family."

"We're not family."

Kiri turned from the window. In the lamplight her face did the thing it always did when Theo said something deliberately wrong—half a smile, held in check, waiting to see if Theo would correct herself. Theo didn't. Six months now, and she had learned that some sentences were better left standing wrong.

"Sign the affidavit," Kiri said. "Then come on. We have a map to finish."

* * *

They finished it in the room above the chandlery where they'd been living since the rains ended—a single wide room with a table that had once belonged to a shipping clerk and a window that caught the morning sun, which was why they'd taken it, though neither had said that was why.

The map lay open across the whole table, weighted at the corners with brass fittings Elio had donated from the *Mariana*'s salvaged rigging. It was the original—the one Theo had drawn in her first year in the basin, before the accident, before everything: the full survey of the Sepik lines, every spur and junction and proposed extension rendered in her own precise hand. The same map she had burned page after page of, keeping only this master copy hidden inside the false bottom of her instrument case, telling herself she kept it for navigation, knowing she kept it because burning all of it would have meant admitting there was nothing left to correct.

For three weeks now, working evenings after the day's labor, she had been annotating it.

Not correcting—annotating. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole point. A correction erased. It drew the new line over the old and let the old vanish underneath. What Theo was doing instead was a kind of testimony in cartography: every mark on the map now carried its truth alongside it, written small in red ink, dated, initialed T.S., cross-referenced to the report numbers that had gone out with Calloway's letter and to the pages of Anselm's journal that Kiri had transcribed.

Here, at milepost 41, where the line bent around the drowned forest: *closed 1911, reported cause "unstable subgrade," actual cause: subgrade sound, closure ordered to conceal load-report falsification, see Report 114-C original vs. filed copy.*

Here, at the trestle over the black channel: *charges placed by order of M. Vane, March 1912, demolition executed by T.S. under duress of circumstance, structure unsound prior, see journal, p. 88.*

Here, at the junction itself—the drowned junction, drawn in her old confident lines as though it would ever carry traffic again: *site of barge wreck, 14 October 1911. Five dead. One survivor. Reports recovered from strongbox, distributed. This mark is not an error. This mark is a grave. Treat it as one.*

She had hesitated longest over that last annotation. Kiri had found her at midnight with the pen hovering, and had read the draft over her shoulder, and had said nothing for a long moment, and then had said: *Put the names. All five. Maps remember coordinates. People need names.*

So the names were there now, in red, in a careful column beside the junction mark, and beneath them, in smaller script: *Luis Sandoval died trying to send these truths downriver. He got them as far as the strongbox. His sister got them the rest of the way.*

Tonight was the last night. Tomorrow they went upcountry to start the new survey—the government commission, signed openly, paid openly, Theo Sandoval chief engineer, Kiri Anselm field recorder, Elio Cruz master of transport, and Palu the deckhand, whose arm had healed crooked but who could still handle a chain and swore he'd follow them anywhere there wasn't crocodile water. A real crew, on real papers, mapping a real extension into country no one had surveyed honestly. The irony that the territory government had hired the woman whose letter had embarrassed it into reopening the inquiry was not lost on anyone; Calloway had called it *the smartest thing a guilty institution ever did*, and Theo had decided to accept the work in exactly that spirit—as evidence that institutions could be shamed into motion, which was, after all, the entire thesis of everything they'd sent downriver.

But tonight, the map.

Elio came up the outside stairs at dusk with two bottles of beer sweating in his fists and the unhurried tread of a man who had learned, somewhere in the flooded channels of the middle basin, that hurry rarely improved anything. He set the bottles down, studied the map end to end with his hands on his hips, and was quiet for a while.

"You know," he said finally, "I patched that hull four times between Wewak and the junction. Four. And every time I told myself, one more patch and we float home." He touched the corner weight, the brass fitting off his own boat. "Never once believed it. You know what I mean?"

"I know what you mean."

"The boat was a one-way vessel. So was this." He tapped the map, gently, the way you'd touch a coffin lid or a sleeping child. "Good. One-way things get finished. Round-trip things just get postponed." He took his beer and settled into the chair by the window, which was his chair now, unspoken, the way certain objects in the room had quietly become his. "Finish it."

Kiri sat across from Theo, and for a while the only sounds were the pen, the evening birds, and the distant harbor bell counting the change of tide.

Theo reached the last blank space on the map—the margin along the bottom edge, below the title block, where surveyors traditionally wrote nothing. She dipped the pen. Then she stopped, and looked up, and found Kiri already watching her, because Kiri always knew.

"What are you going to write?" Kiri asked.

"Something that isn't data."

"There's no such thing on a map."

"There is now." Theo bent to the paper. *This map is annotated by Theodora M. Sandoval, engineer, in the presence of Kiri Anselm, daughter of Emilio Anselm, surveyor. Every mark is true. Where the truth was buried, it has been dug up. Where it was burned, it has been rewritten. This document is offered to the public record and to the families of the dead, and it will not be withdrawn, amended, or destroyed by any person, including me.*

She signed it. Her name, third time in a week, and this time the pen didn't hesitate at all.

Kiri read it twice. Then she did something Theo hadn't expected: she took the journal from its oilcloth, unwrapped it, opened it to the flyleaf where her father had written his own name in 1904, seventeen years ago, in a young man's careful hand—and laid the open journal on the map, beside the annotation, so that the two signatures faced each other across seventeen years and one grave.

"They should travel together," Kiri said. "The map and the journal. To the archive, when the inquiry takes them. My father's name next to yours. That's how it should have been from the start—two engineers, one set of lines."

"The inquiry will want them separately. Evidence protocols."

"The inquiry can want what it likes." Kiri closed the journal but left it resting on the map, her hand flat on the cover. "I spent six years thinking the truth was a weapon I had to aim alone. Then I met a woman on a dock at Wewak who lied to me beautifully for two hundred miles, and I learned the truth is more like ballast. It doesn't fire. It just keeps you upright in bad water." She looked up. "You're my ballast, Theo. Don't argue with the metaphor. I'm a surveyor's daughter; I know how boats work."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were going to improve it."

"...I was going to improve it."

Elio snorted into his beer. Outside, the harbor bell rang six, and the lamplighter's boy began his rounds along the street below, and the light in the room went gold and then amber and then soft.

* * *

They left at dawn, because Elio refused to leave at any other hour—"the river gives you the whole day if you steal its first hour, and nothing if you sleep past it"—and because leaving at dawn meant the town saw them go in full light, which was the point. No pre-dawn slipping away. No false names on the manifest. Theo walked up the gangplank of the chartered launch with her instrument case on her shoulder and her face bare to the rising sun, and the harbormaster checked her papers against her face and found them matching, and wished her good survey, and meant it.

The map rode in the case against her hip. The journal rode in its oilcloth against Kiri's chest. The duplicated reports were already gone—out on the mail steamer a week past, to newspapers in Sydney and London, to the colonial regulator, to five families in three countries who would open envelopes and learn, at last, how their fathers and sons had died. Palu had insisted on carrying copies inland himself, to the highland districts where two of the dead crew had been born. He'd shaken Theo's hand doing it, his crooked arm and all, and said, *You gave me a story worth having a scar for,* and Theo had discovered that a person could survive hearing a thing like that, could even stand straighter afterward.

The launch pulled away from the wharf. Port Moresby shrank behind them into its haze of smoke and sail. Ahead the coast ran green and empty toward the river mouths, and beyond the rivers, up through country no honest map had ever covered, the new lines waited—not rails yet, not even routes yet, just terrain, just possibility, just the world before the drawing.

Kiri came to stand beside her at the rail. For a while they watched the water together, shoulders touching, saying nothing, because they had passed the stage where silence needed filling.

"Six years invisible," Kiri said at last. "How does it feel, being seen?"

Theo considered the question honestly, the way Kiri deserved. "Like standing in a current," she said. "You can't pretend the water isn't moving anymore. Everything you do shows downstream." She turned her face into the wind. "I used to think that was the danger. Being legible. Leaving tracks."

"And now?"

"Now I think the danger was the opposite. Six years of leaving no tracks, and the only person who knew where I'd been was me—and I was lying to myself the whole time about what happened at the junction." She looked at Kiri. "A visible life can be corrected. An invisible one just rots."

Kiri nodded slowly, and then, because she was Kiri, she reached into the oilcloth and produced the journal and a pencil, and said, "Then let's start the new record properly. First entry. Date, position, personnel, conditions." She licked the pencil. "Conditions, Miss Sandoval. The official record wants conditions."

Theo laughed—out loud, in daylight, in front of the crew, and nobody flinched and nothing burned. She took the pencil.

"Conditions," she dictated, writing it into the fresh page Kiri held open against the wind: "*Clear skies. Fair current. Crew of four, all willing. Carrying one map, fully annotated, and nothing hidden anywhere aboard.*"

Kiri read it back, approved it with a look, and blotted the page. Up ahead the first river mouth opened in the coastline like a door, and Elio put the helm over to meet it, and the launch leaned into the turn and ran, unhurried and unhidden, into the country they had come to draw.
