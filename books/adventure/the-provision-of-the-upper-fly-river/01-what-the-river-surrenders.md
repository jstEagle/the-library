## 1. What the River Surrenders

The river gave up its dead in the third week of the dry, and Xanthe Fairweather—though no one on the Upper Fly called him that, no one on the Upper Fly called him anything but Tom Reeve—was the one they paid to look at them.

Not people. Not yet. The flood had taken a mission house at Wabia, two longboats, a footbridge, and forty years of silt, and it had coughed all of it back up along the cutbanks in a brown tideline of ruin. The district officer wanted a survey. The mining consortium wanted a report that said nothing was worth saving. Xanthe wanted eleven hundred kina and a helicopter ride out before the wet season closed the gorge, and for six days all three wants had aligned nicely.

He worked alone. That was the point of him. He'd learned the trade honestly enough—two seasons on a salvage crew in the Torres Strait, a year of contract archaeology in the Kimberley under a man who drank but taught well—and he'd learned the other trade just as honestly: how to arrive with papers under a name that had no father attached to it. *T. Reeve, field surveyor.* A name like a canvas tarp. It kept the rain off.

On the seventh morning he followed the cutbank downstream past the drowned mission house and saw the bronze before he understood what he was seeing.

It sat in the mud like a skull. Green-black, furred with river patina, one corner punched up through the silt at an angle that was wrong for a chest that had been swept anywhere. Things the flood carries get tumbled. They get battered. They come to rest face-down in the gravel with their lids sprung and their contents long since scattered. This thing lay square. Level. Lid down, as if set.

Xanthe crouched and didn't touch it for a long time. That was the first discipline they'd taught him, and the one that had saved him most often: the ground tells you when something was placed, and the ground was telling him now. The scour halo around the chest was shallow. The mud above it had layered in annual bands, fine as tree rings, and he counted them with his eyes—ten, twelve, maybe more. The river had been burying it a little every year, patiently, the way the river buried everything, and the flood had simply peeled the blanket back.

Hidden, then. Not lost.

He dug. It took two hours with the hand trowel because he wouldn't risk the mattock, and the sun climbed and the sweat ran and somewhere behind him a hornbill went off like a rusty gate, and when the chest finally came free he turned it over and the seal caught the light and stopped his breath.

It was a stamp, pressed into the bronze lid: a bird of paradise above crossed keys, and beneath it a legend in worn capitals—*ADMINISTRATION OF PAPUA AND NEW GUINEA*—with a date range that ended decades before the chest could have been buried. Colonial issue. Official. The kind of thing that belonged in a museum vitrine in Port Moresby, not under four metres of Fly River silt in a stretch of gorge the maps still labelled with the old word for *no entry*.

He should have photographed it, flagged it, radioed the district officer, and walked away. That was the procedure. That was the life he was pretending to have.

Instead he knelt there in the wet gravel with his pulse going like a bird's, and he understood, in the way you understand a current before you see the crocodile's wake, that he had found the one thing on this river that somebody wanted found by nobody.

The lid was locked. Of course it was locked. But set into a recess beside the hasp, so neatly it might have been part of the ornament, was a round bronze disc the size of a palm, pierced with a keyhole and stamped with the same bird. A medallion. A key. And it was loose—worked loose, maybe, by decades of water, or left loose on purpose.

Xanthe looked up the cutbank and down it. Empty. The gorge hissed and chuckled and went about its business. Somewhere a village dog was barking at nothing.

He took the medallion.

He told himself it was procedure—evidence secured against looters, logged, photographed, to be handed in with the survey. He told himself that while it went into the zip pocket against his ribs, the one that didn't show, and he told himself that while he reburied the chest to the depth of a handspan, scattered the spoil, and dragged a fallen palm frond across the site with a care that had nothing to do with any procedure he'd ever been paid to follow.

Then he climbed the bank to his camp, and the day went wrong in the space of a cigarette.

* * *

The helicopter came in at two in the afternoon, unmarked, white, the kind of aircraft that costs more than the district's annual road budget. It did not land at the mission house. It landed on the gravel bar below Xanthe's camp, and four men got out of it wearing company polo shirts over the kind of bodies that polo shirts are usually hired to disguise.

The tall one did the talking. He had a lanyard with a card and a smile that had been laminated somewhere.

"Tom Reeve?" He didn't wait for confirmation. "Marcus Voss, Kavera Minerals, community liaison." The title sat on him like the shirt. "We understand you're doing the flood survey."

"District contract," Xanthe said.

"Sure." Voss looked at the cutbank, at the river, at the palm frond, and Xanthe watched him look at the palm frond a half-second longer than a man looks at vegetation. "Floods move a lot of stuff around. Old stuff. Mission era, colonial era. If you come across anything like that, it's company land downstream of the pegs, and there are heritage protocols. We'd want to handle recovery ourselves. Chain of custody. You know how it is."

"I know how it is," Xanthe said.

"Great." The laminated smile widened a millimetre. "Anything surfaced yet?"

The medallion lay against Xanthe's ribs, warm as a second heartbeat. He had lied to customs officers, to police, to a woman in Cairns who'd deserved better, and he had never once felt the lie as a physical thing. This one had weight. "Nothing but mission roofing iron and a lot of dead crocs," he said. "You're welcome to the crocs."

Voss laughed, and his men laughed, and none of them laughed with their eyes, and then the helicopter left and Xanthe sat down on a crate with his hands not quite steady and made himself think it through like a survey.

One: they'd come within hours of his find, to a gravel bar in forty kilometres of empty gorge. That wasn't a patrol. That was a destination. Two: Voss had said *old stuff* before Xanthe had said anything at all. Three: the chest had been hidden, deliberately, by people with the skill to hide it from a colonial administration that owned every map and every gun—and hidden so well that it had needed a hundred-year flood to give it up.

Which meant somebody had been waiting. Not for the chest. Nobody waits a century. For the flood. For the one event violent enough to open the grave without opening it themselves, because opening it themselves would have been an admission that they knew it was there.

Xanthe got out the satellite phone he wasn't supposed to have and checked the survey schedule. The consortium's heritage subcontract was listed as a formality: *site assessment, Kavera Minerals, lead consultant M. Voss.* He looked at the name for a long time.

Then he looked at the medallion in his palm, at the bird of paradise and the crossed keys, and felt the whole shape of it turn over in his mind like a boulder in a flood. The chest wasn't treasure. Treasure gets hunted. The chest was a record, and records get *buried*, and the men in the polo shirts hadn't come to recover it—they'd come to make sure it went back under.

He was still working that through when he smelled the smoke.

It came up the cutbank at dusk, thin and wrong, and by the time he reached his camp it was fully involved—tent, crates, the dry season's accumulated paper of a man named Tom Reeve, all of it going up in a column of black that would be visible from the helicopter's next pass. His survey notes. His false passport. His photographs, including the six frames he'd taken of the seal before he'd understood what he was holding.

Arson is a message you send with fire instead of ink, and the message was legible: *You were never here. Nothing was ever here. Go.*

Xanthe stood in the heat with the medallion in his fist and the river talking its endless low talk below the bank, and for the first time since he'd walked out of his father's house seven years ago, he was afraid in a way that had nothing to do with being found.

Behind him, out of the dark at the edge of the tree line, a woman's voice said, "You have something of mine, Tom Reeve. And we have perhaps two days before they come back with more men than matches."

He turned. She stepped into the firelight, and she did not look like a person who had ever once been lost.
