## 2. The Custodian's Daughter

Xanthe heard her before he saw her—the particular silence of someone moving through bush who didn't want to be heard, which was itself a sound if you'd spent two years learning the difference. He set down the billy of tea, kept his hand loose near the machete, and watched the treeline across the creek.

She came out of it like she owned it. Mid-twenties, maybe, canvas pack, a rain jacket gone grey with wear, and eyes that did the thing he hated most in people: they took his measure and found it wanting before he'd said a word.

"You're the one they call Tom Vale," she said.

"I'm the one who answers to it."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give for free." He nodded at the log opposite his fire. "Sit or don't. Tea's shared either way."

She didn't sit. She stood at the edge of the firelight with the water running loud behind her, and she said, "The chest that came up at Kiunga after the flood. You were on the survey crew. You opened it before the consortium men arrived."

"News travels."

"Not news. Arithmetic." She reached into her jacket and drew out a photograph, creased soft as cloth from handling. "My grandfather drowned in 1961, crossing this river with a chest under his charge. The administration said the chest was lost. The administration said a great many things." She turned the photo so the fire caught it: a young man in a colonial uniform, stiff-collared, standing beside a crate stencilled with a seal Xanthe had spent three nights trying to forget—a crowned bird over crossed paddles.

He kept his face still. Two years of practice. "If you're here about salvage rights, talk to the consortium. They own everything now, including the story."

"I'm not here about gold." Her voice didn't rise, but something under it did. "I'm here about what my grandfather died carrying. There was a medallion in that chest. Bronze, the size of a palm, with the same seal. It opens something upstream. And you have it, because you're the only man who touched that chest before Verrin's dogs got there, and because you've been sleeping with your boots on ever since."

Xanthe looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed—short, involuntary, surprised out of him.

"You've been watching me."

"Since Kiunga. You pocketed it left-handed while the foreman was shouting at the flood damage. Left-handed, because you dig right-handed and you thought no one would map the habit." She finally sat, uninvited, on the far side of the fire. "You're not a very good liar, Tom Vale. You're just a very committed one."

The name sat wrong on him tonight, heavier than usual. He poured tea into the second mug—he'd only brought out one, but there was always a second—and pushed it across the dirt with his boot.

"What opens?" he asked.

"A ledger," she said. "And I'll tell you what's in it when I believe you won't run."

* * *

Amara had learned to read men the way her grandmother taught her to read water: not by what they showed but by where their weight sat. The one called Tom Vale carried his weight strangely—all of it forward, all of it ready to move, a man perpetually half-risen from his own life. He'd taken the medallion, yes. But he hadn't sold it, hadn't fled, hadn't traded it to Verrin's people for passage out. He'd stayed in a leaky tent above a creek, digging test pits for wages, keeping a bronze disc wrapped in a sock at the bottom of a dented mess tin.

Guilt kept things. Greed spent them.

"He's not what I expected," she wrote that night in the notebook she kept in Tok Pisin and English both, by torchlight inside her fly. *Expected a thief. Got a man hiding from something bigger than the chest.* She'd traced him through three false names already—Vale, then Kerris on the survey manifest, then nothing at all on the ferry lists, which meant he'd moved like smoke between them. Nobody was that good unless they'd practised. Nobody practised unless they were running.

Her grandfather had run too, Amara thought. All the way into the river.

* * *

They came at dusk three days later, and they came burning.

Xanthe smelled the kerosene before he understood it—sweet, wrong, out of place against woodsmoke and river rot—and then the first flare went through the fly of his tent and the night turned orange. He came out low and fast, ears ringing, and saw four men at the treeline, two with pumps, two with rifles slung casual as umbrellas. The lead one wore a consortium polo shirt under his webbing, which was somehow worse than a mask.

"Vale," the man called, conversational over the crackle. "Or Kerris. Or whatever we decide you are tomorrow. Where's the medallion?"

Xanthe's camp went up in sheets of flame. Two years of careful nothing—his gear, his forged papers, the life he'd built thin as paper—curling into the dark. He felt it go and found, to his own surprise, that he felt almost nothing. That was its own kind of answer.

Then a rifle cracked from the ridge above the creek—not at the men, but into the canopy beside them, deliberate, a warning shot with excellent manners—and all four heads turned. In that turn, Xanthe ran.

He ran downstream along the waterline where footprints die, and a hand caught his wrist at the bend and hauled him into the hollow under the fig roots, and it was her, mud-smeared, breathing hard, the rifle still warm across her knees.

"You shoot?" he gasped.

"My grandmother hunted crocodiles. I shoot."

Above them, torches swept the bank. Voices argued—*he went downriver, check the landing*—and the fire ate his old life behind them, popping and settling like something being cooked.

"They know your face now," she whispered.

"They knew it before. They just wanted confirmation." He lay back against the roots, heart slamming, and laughed again, that same startled sound. "You realise you just burned your cover too. They'll have seen the muzzle flash."

"I counted on it." She looked at him across the dark, and whatever she'd been weighing since Kiunga, he could feel it tip. "So. No more watching each other. My name is Amara Okedi. My grandfather was Joseph Okedi, custodian of the Upper Fly records, and my family has owed a debt to dead people for sixty years. What's yours? Your real one. Because I can't run upstream with a man whose name I don't trust."

The torches were moving away. The river ran loud. Somewhere behind them his tent collapsed inward in a bloom of sparks, and Xanthe Fairweather—who had buried that name under two years of dirt and distance from a father whose face was on magazine covers—heard himself say:

"Fairweather. Xanthe Fairweather."

Her eyebrows went up a fraction. "The novelist's son."

"The novelist's son," he agreed bitterly, "who'd very much like to remain nobody."

"Too late for nobody." She shouldered the rifle and stood, silhouetted against the burning bank. "Verrin's men will sweep the lower river by morning, and your father's expedition holds the gorge mouth. Which means, Mr. Fairweather, that the only direction left is the one everyone forbids."

She started upstream along the black water, and after a moment—because she was right, and because his whole life had been a rehearsal for exactly this kind of mistake—Xanthe followed.
