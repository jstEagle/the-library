## 3. Upstream of Every Law

The gorge did not want them in it.

Amara felt that from the first hour—felt it in the way the river narrowed and darkened, the walls closing overhead like a book being shut. The Fly ran fast here, green-black, cold despite the heat, and every bend delivered them into a corridor of dripping limestone where their paddles echoed like gunshots.

They had stolen the consortium's own longboat from the fuel dump at D'Albertis Point, which Amara considered fair payment for a burned camp. Xanthe had hotwired the outboard with the calm of a man who had clearly done worse things under other names. Now they ran upstream against the current with the throttle open, trading speed for noise, because the alternative was to be caught by the boats behind them.

Three boats behind them. She had counted the engines at dawn.

"Monsoon's early," Xanthe shouted over the motor. He nodded at the sky, where the clouds had stopped being weather and become architecture—towering, purple-bellied, stacked on the ridgelines like siege engines. "Twelve hours. Maybe less."

"Then we don't stop."

"I wasn't going to suggest it."

She almost smiled. Two days ago she'd held a machete on this man in the ruins of his camp and told him she would take the medallion off his body if necessary. He had looked at her—not afraid, exactly. *Interested*. As if she were a sentence he hadn't finished reading. Now he handled her grandfather's bronze key like it was made of eggshell, and she still didn't know what to do with a man who was careful with other people's inheritance when he wouldn't even claim his own name.

*Fairweather.* She'd known since the second night, when he talked in his sleep—a word here, a word there—and she'd pieced it together with the ledger-keeper's instinct her grandmother had drilled into her: names, dates, connections. The famous father. The famous shadow. It explained why he flinched when she said his real surname, and why he didn't.

The gorge bent north. The walls rose. Somewhere above them, hidden in the cloud, was the headwater country the old maps marked *terra incognita* and the new maps marked with a consortium survey grid. Her grandmother had called it by another name entirely, and had died without telling anyone where the cave was—which was why Amara had needed the medallion, and the man who stole it, and three days of running.

Her grandmother had also died without telling her everything. That was the part that sat in Amara's chest like a swallowed stone. *Guard the chest,* the old woman had said. *When the river gives it back, give it to no one.* But she had never said what was in it. Never said why the family had paid for sixty years in whispers and moved houses and silence. Amara had grown up thinking they were poor. They weren't poor. They were *burdened*, and burdens cost more than poverty ever would.

The first rapids nearly took the boat.

Xanthe read the water better than she expected—he chose the tongue of smooth water on the left, threaded the rocks, and came out the far side grinning like an idiot, spray sheeting off the bow. For one stupid moment she thought: *this is what he could have been, if he'd been nobody's son.*

Then the radio crackled. One of the mercenary boats, calling its partner on a channel they'd left open by accident or arrogance.

*"—visual on the target vessel, entering the narrows. Sir says no witnesses past the gorge mouth."*

Amara looked at Xanthe. Xanthe looked at the sky.

"No witnesses," he repeated quietly. "Right."

He opened the throttle all the way.

* * *

They abandoned the boat at midafternoon, wedged it into a side channel under cut palm fronds, and climbed. The gorge wall offered handholds worn smooth by generations of feet that predated every map—the custodian families had used this route, Amara realized, for exactly this purpose: carrying witness upstream, away from men with guns. Her hands fit the holds as if the rock remembered them.

Behind and below, engines. Ahead and above, cloud.

They reached the headwater plateau as the light went strange and yellow, the monsoon's first outriders flickering on the horizon like heat lightning. And there, blocking the trail where it narrowed between two boulders, stood a line of expedition tents—and a man in a pressed field shirt standing in front of them with his arms folded, as if he had been waiting there his whole life.

Edmund Fairweather. Silver-haired, sunburned, famous. Amara knew the face from a hundred magazine covers; she'd have known it anywhere, because it was Xanthe's face thirty years deeper into the world.

"Xanthe." The father's voice carried no surprise at all. "You're a difficult man to find when you don't want to be found."

"You found me anyway." Xanthe stepped slightly in front of Amara, and she hated how much she noticed that. "You always do. That's the whole problem with you."

"We've located the cave system. Seismic survey. There's something in there, son—I need you inside with my team. Whatever it is, it belongs in an archive. In a museum. In *proper hands*." His eyes moved to Amara, cataloguing her, dismissing her. "Who's this?"

"Nobody," Xanthe said. "Like me."

"Xanthe—"

"No." It came out flat and final, and Amara heard in it a decision that had been decades in the making. "You don't get to reclaim me. You didn't lose me—you spent me. Every dig, every documentary, every time you put my face next to yours so the sponsors would smile. I'm not coming home. I was never coming home. I'm just passing through your life on my way to mine."

For a moment the great man simply stared, and Amara saw something pass over his face that no camera had ever caught—something small and old and hurt. Then thunder rolled down from the ridge, and the moment was buried alive.

"The monsoon," Edmund Fairweather said, recovering, gesturing at his team. "We can't move in this. You can't either. Stay in camp—"

But Xanthe was already moving, pulling Amara up the goat-track beyond the tents, and the last thing she heard behind them was the father shouting orders into the wind.

* * *

The cave mouth was exactly where her grandmother's stories had placed it: behind the fall of a dry waterfall, half-hidden by fig roots, breathing cool air into the furnace of the afternoon. Inside, past the first chamber, the walls changed. Handprints. Ochre and charcoal, hundreds of them, layered over centuries—small hands, large hands, some tiny enough to be children's.

"They brought everyone," Amara whispered. "Not just the keepers. Whole families came up here to add their print. So the record couldn't be owned by one person."

At the back of the chamber, set into a socket in the living rock, was a bronze plate bearing a seal—the same seal as the chest, the same seal as the medallion in Xanthe's pocket. He fitted the medallion into it, turned, and a slab swung inward on oiled pivots, maintained by sixty years of silent devotion.

Inside was a stone cist. Inside the cist, wrapped in oilcloth and wax, lay the ledger.

It was not gold. It was not treasure. It was a company journal, 1938, in the immaculate hand of a site manager who had apparently believed, right up until the end, that he was writing a private confession rather than evidence. Amara read by torchlight while the storm arrived outside, and the words went through her like a spear:

*Village of Wamira cleared per instruction. Resistance minimal. Casualties: 214 souls, counted. River used for disposal. Site seeded with crocodile sign to discourage inquiry.*

Two hundred and fourteen. Her grandmother's village. Her grandmother, aged nine, had survived because her mother had pushed her into the reeds and held her under the water until the shooting stopped.

Amara sat down on the cold stone with the ledger in her lap and wept—not softly, not privately, but the way the river below was weeping, in volume, in fury. Xanthe knelt beside her and did not touch her and did not leave, which was somehow exactly right.

"This is what I inherited," she said finally. "Not gold. A debt. Somebody has to be the witness or it never happened."

"It happened," Xanthe said. "And now it's written down, and now two people know, and in twelve hours there will be a hundred more, because—" he glanced at the entrance, where the rain had begun to hammer, "—that storm just closed the gorge behind us. Nobody's following us in tonight. Which means we have time."

"Time for what?"

He looked at the ledger, then at the satellite phone in his pack—the one thing he'd grabbed from the burning camp besides the medallion.

"Time to decide," he said, "who we give it to. Because the moment I send this, my father finds out what his sponsors built their company on. And the moment *they* find out, every lawyer and every gun they own comes up this river."

Outside, the monsoon broke fully over the plateau, and somewhere down the gorge, faint beneath the rain, they both heard engines—many engines, moving fast, arriving anyway.
