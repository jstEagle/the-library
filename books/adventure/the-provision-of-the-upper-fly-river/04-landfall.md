## 4. Landfall

The monsoon announced itself the way a verdict is announced: with a sound you feel in your sternum before you understand it.

We were forty meters inside the headwaters cave when the first true rain hit the gorge. Not the polite afternoon showers we'd been outrunning for six days—this was the sky giving way, a wall of water descending the canyon walls in ropes and sheets, and beneath it a deeper sound, the river itself rising, gathering its argument.

Amara stood at the mouth of the cave with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, watching the light die. Behind us, thirty meters back in the dark, the bronze chest sat open and empty where we'd found it, its false bottom pried up, its secret finally out into the air after sixty years.

"They'll come now," she said. "The rain will drive them in."

"I'm counting on it."

She turned. In the last grey light her face was unreadable, which I had learned over two hundred kilometers of river meant she was deciding something about me. "You've been counting on things since Kiunga," she said. "Tell me this one."

I told her. It was simple, the way most desperate plans are simple. The gorge narrowed half a kilometer downstream at a place the river people called the Throat—a slot of rock no wider than a truck, where the floodwater would pile up like a fist. Above the Throat, on the western wall, an old survey cut ran along the cliff face, a mining adit from the fifties, timbered and rotten, that Amara's grandfather had used as a storehouse in the years he kept the chest. We'd passed its black mouth yesterday without stopping.

"The water backs up behind the Throat," I said. "It always has. Your grandfather timed his crossings by it. When the monsoon hits, the pool below the cave rises fast—but not as fast as they'll come. They'll be caught between the rising water and the cliff. And when the pool reaches the adit—"

"It floods the tunnel," Amara said slowly. "And everything in it."

"Everything in it." I looked back toward the cave mouth, where the rain was turning the world to white noise. "Including them, if they're standing there when it goes."

* * *

They came ninety minutes later, just past full dark, six of them with headlamps stitching the rain to ribbons. We heard them before we saw them—the flat tactical voices, the clatter of gear on wet stone—and we were already pressed into the deep fissure at the cave's rear, the one Amara's grandfather must have used a hundred times, because it was exactly the kind of place a man who spent his life hiding something would prepare.

I watched them through a hand's width of gap. Voss led them, the consortium's chief of security, a man built like bridge cable, and even soaked through and shouting over the river he moved with the unhurried certainty of someone who had never once been late to a killing. He swept the cave with his lamp, found the empty chest, and went very still.

Then he laughed. I felt Amara tense beside me; I put my hand on her arm. Voss turned to his men and said something I couldn't hear, but I could read the shape of it in his shoulders: *they're still here, they can't have gone far, nothing leaves this gorge tonight.*

He was right about one thing. Nothing left the gorge that night. That was rather the point.

They fanned out along the ledge below the cave, moving upstream toward the adit, away from the Throat—which meant Voss knew the terrain too, or thought he did, and believed the high ground was safety. The pool below was already climbing. I could see it eating the ledge in slow bites, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways.

Amara's lips were at my ear. "The fuse," she breathed.

That had been her addition to my plan, and I confess I had flinched from it. Her grandfather's storehouse had been wired once—for blasting, in the old days—and though the timber was rotten, the line ran down through a crack in the adit floor to a junction box above the Throat, and the junction box, when Amara had checked it that morning with the grim competence of a woman who'd grown up around mine workings, still held charge. Enough charge, she said, to bring the weakened roof of the adit down into the throat of the gorge. A plug. A dam made of the mountain itself.

"You're sure about the timing?" I'd asked her.

"My grandfather drowned keeping their secrets," she'd said. "I can do arithmetic."

Now, in the dark, she counted. I watched her lips move. At some number only she knew, she reached out of the fissure and closed the switch we'd rigged from the junction box's guts.

The explosion was smaller than I expected and larger than anything I want to hear again. The mountain coughed. Dust and rock came down the cliff face, and then the river did what rivers do when you insult them: it rose up furious into the wound, filled the adit, burst the rotten timbers, and slammed into the Throat like a battering ram looking for a door.

The door held. The water backed. And the pool below the cave became, in the space of four minutes, a lake.

Voss's men made it to high ground—all but one, whose lamp I watched spiral away downstream into the dark, and I have decided, for my own peace, to believe he swam. The rest stood on a shelf of rock fifty meters above the new waterline, marooned, their radios dead in the wash of spray, shouting at each other across a noise like the end of the world.

Amara took my hand. Her grip said: *enough.* We went out the back of the cave, through the fissure, up the goat track her grandfather had cut into the western wall, and by the time the sky cracked open with dawn we were three ridges east, walking through cloud forest so loud with birdsong it seemed to be mocking the gorge behind us.

* * *

We stopped at noon under a pandanus, and I took out the satellite phone I had carried for eleven days without using it.

Amara looked at it the way you look at a snake. "You said you didn't want to be found."

"I said I didn't want to be *claimed*." I turned the phone over in my hands. It was heavier than it should have been, the way all small decisions are heavy. "There's a difference. I spent four years learning it."

"Xanthe." She almost never used my name—it was still strange between us, my real name, spoken aloud in the bush like a thing uncrated. "If you send those pages out, every court, every journalist, every lawyer my family ever feared will come up this river. They'll come for me. Descendant of the custodian, witness, accessory—I don't know what they'll call me. You can bury the ledger. One more flood, one more landslide. The river keeps secrets better than any archive. We could let it."

"And in twenty years," I said, "some other flood gives it back. Or Voss's employers buy the valley and plow it, and nobody living remembers why the old village site is called what it's called." I looked west, toward the hidden roar of the river. "Your grandfather carried this alone for sixty years. That wasn't justice, Amara. That was just a longer way of drowning."

She was quiet a long time. Rain dripped off the pandanus fronds, counting seconds.

"He wanted it buried," she said at last. "He told my mother: bury it deeper than memory. But my mother told me something else, the night before she died. She said, *your grandfather was a man who hid a fire under his house for sixty years and called it protection.*" She met my eyes. "Send it."

So I did. Photographs of every page, transmitted in batches while the battery died, addressed to a newspaper desk in Port Moresby, a human-rights clinic in Geneva, and—one message, plain text, no photographs—to a number I had sworn for four years never to dial.

To my father I wrote only: *The Upper Fly. Come see what I found. —X.*

Not Xanthe Fairweather, the famous man's missing son. Just X. Let him spend a sleepless night on that.

* * *

The story broke in nine days, which is fast even for the age we live in. By then Amara and I were downriver, guests—not prisoners, whatever the lawyers said—of the village that had sheltered us, sitting on a veranda while the world arrived in helicopters. There were investigators and journalists and, eventually, handcuffs for men further up the food chain than Voss, who had been pulled half-drowned from a tree near D'Albertis Junction and talked for eleven hours without a lawyer.

My father came on the twelfth day. He stood at the bottom of the veranda steps in his expedition khakis, older than his photographs, and for a long moment neither of us spoke.

"You could have stayed lost," he said finally. "You were good at it."

"I know," I said. "That was the problem."

He looked at me—at the man I'd become instead of the shadow I'd fled—and something in his face shifted, settled, like sediment finding the bottom of a glass. "The book they'll write about this," he said. "It'll have your name in it."

"Yes," I said. "First page, I'm told. My own name, spelled correctly, for the first time in my life."

He nodded slowly, and I understood that this, too, was a kind of surrender—the last one, the one that mattered—and that the shadow I had been living under all these years had been cast less by him than by my own refusal to stand in any light at all.

Amara came out with two cups of tea, handed one to me, and ignored him entirely, which I believe he deserved and possibly enjoyed. Below us the Fly ran high and brown with the monsoon's leavings, carrying silt and branches and the season's wreckage toward the sea, keeping nothing, hiding nothing, telling everything it knew to anyone patient enough to listen.

It was Amara who spotted the envelope, three days later, tucked into the doorframe of the veranda hut—no stamp, no return address, hand-delivered by some boat that had come and gone in the night. Inside was a single sheet, typed, unsigned.

*The ledger names fourteen men,* it read. *Your newspaper has twelve. Ask yourself who signed the amnesty in '61, and ask it quickly. Some of us are still upstream.*

Amara read it twice. Then she folded it, put it in the oilcloth with the ledger's remaining blank pages, and looked at me with an expression I was beginning to know very well—the expression of a woman doing arithmetic.

"Well," she said. "It seems the river isn't finished with us."
