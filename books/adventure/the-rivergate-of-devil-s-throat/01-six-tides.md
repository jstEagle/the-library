## 1. Six Tides

The third knock of the morning came at half past seven, and Lena Winterbourne was already gone.

She'd learned the trick years ago, back when her father was alive and the harbor constables still had a sense of humor about a girl who could fold herself into a crab pot like she'd been born in one. Invisibility wasn't magic. It was practice. It was knowing that Mrs. Halloway from the fishmonger's stall would look straight through you if you carried a crate, because crates were furniture and furniture didn't owe anybody money. It was keeping your boots quiet on the cobbles and your face pleasant and forgettable, the kind of face a man described to his wife as "nobody much."

Lena had gotten very good at being nobody much. It was practically a trade.

The trouble with being nobody much, she reflected now, slipping out the back of the boarding house past a landlady whose hand was already cupped for rent, was that nobody much didn't get paid either.

Her debts fit in a ledger she kept in her head, which was safer than paper, because paper could be seized. Fourteen months' board. The diving school fees, unpaid since spring. Old Bramwell's boat repair, which she'd promised in writing because Bramwell had looked at her with those rheumy, trusting eyes and she'd been weak. And underneath it all, like ballast, the loan from the Harbor Fund — three hundred crowns at an interest rate that should have been illegal and probably was, in places with better laws than Rivergate.

Three hundred crowns. She could hold her breath four minutes and forty seconds. She could not hold off Harlan Voss and his collection boys forever.

So when the note came — heavy cream stock, wax seal, delivered by a boy too well-dressed for the dock district — she read it twice before she let herself feel anything at all.

*Mr. Aldous Crane requests the pleasure of Miss L. Winterbourne's company. Crane Shipping House, top floor. Today, eleven o'clock.*

Requests the pleasure. Men like Aldous Crane didn't request pleasure. They summoned it, the way weather summons itself, and you either showed up or you got rained on.

* * *

The Crane Shipping House stood at the mouth of the harbor like a fat black gull on a post, six stories of granite and smoked glass, and Lena felt her debt ledger grow heavier just standing under it. Inside smelled of pipe smoke and money — not the honest smell of coin, but the sweeter, colder smell of paper that had been signed by other people.

Aldous Crane received her in an office with a view of the whole river, and he did not get up when she entered. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, silver-haired, with hands folded on the desk like a man at prayer who no longer believed in anything he was praying to.

"Miss Winterbourne." His voice was warm the way a stove is warm — useful, and dangerous to lean against. "I'll be direct, because I'm told you appreciate directness."

"I'm told a lot of things," Lena said. "Half of it's true."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Approval, maybe. Or the memory of approval, filed away somewhere dusty. He slid a folder across the desk.

"A wreck," he said. "Navy-blacklisted site, upriver. Devil's Throat gorge. There's salvage on it my people need recovered quickly and quietly. Very quickly. Very quietly."

Lena opened the folder. Charts, mostly, and a navy file so heavily redacted it looked like it had been shot. She turned a page. Turned another. "There's nothing here but holes."

"There's everything here but answers," Crane said pleasantly. "You won't need answers. You'll need your gear, a boat, and speed."

"Speed toward what? What am I recovering?"

"Documents. Metal boxes, watertight, stamped with a shipping mark you'll know when you see it." He laced his fingers. "You bring them to me unopened, and you're paid five hundred crowns. Enough to clear every debt you carry and buy a new boat besides."

Five hundred crowns. Lena kept her face still, but inside something small and starving sat up and took notice. Five hundred crowns was freedom. Five hundred crowns was Bramwell's boat repaired and Voss's boys off her street and a year where she didn't have to be furniture anymore.

"And why me?" she asked. "You've got a fleet. Half the harbor works for you."

"My fleet is engaged elsewhere." He said it smoothly, but there was a hitch in it, a half-second where his eyes went to the window and the river beyond, as if the fleet were already moving and he was counting boats. "And you come recommended. You dive deep. You ask few questions."

"That last part isn't a recommendation," Lena said. "That's just poverty."

This time he did smile, briefly, without warmth. "Poverty is the most reliable quality in this business, Miss Winterbourne. Loyalty can be bought away. Fear wears off. But a woman who needs five hundred crowns will swim through fire for them, and she'll do it on schedule."

It was the most honest thing anyone had said to her all year, and Lena hated how much she liked hearing it.

"The catch," she said.

"No catch. One condition." He leaned forward, and the stove-warmth in his voice banked down to coals. "Six tides. My own recovery fleet follows the same chart, six tides behind you. If they reach the site first, the contract voids. No penalty, no pay. So don't dawdle, and don't stop to sightsee."

"You're racing yourself," Lena said slowly. "Your fleet, your contract, your wreck. Why does the left hand need the right hand raced?"

Crane's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a shutter drawn, quick and practiced, over whatever lived in there. "Because I want it done properly," he said, "and my fleet has a habit of doing things loudly. That's all you need."

It wasn't all, of course. Lena had spent her life reading what people didn't say, the way other divers read current — by the pull of it, the cold spot in the warm water. And what Aldous Crane wasn't saying filled the room like floodwater rising: *don't open the boxes, don't talk to my fleet, don't be curious, don't be seen.*

He was paying her to be invisible. Which meant somebody, somewhere, very much wanted this visible.

"Six tides," she said.

"Six tides." He produced a contract already drafted, already waiting, her name printed on it in a stranger's handwriting. That bothered her more than the redactions. He'd known she'd come. He'd known before she did.

She read it anyway, twice, the way her father had taught her — *trust the fine print, Lena-girl, it's where men hide their consciences.* The terms were clean. Suspiciously clean. No liability clause for injury. No clause at all about what happened if she found something in those boxes she wasn't supposed to see.

Most contracts told you what you couldn't do. This one only told her what she must. As if Crane couldn't imagine her wanting anything else.

She signed. Her name — *Elena M. Winterbourne* — looked strange and loud on the page, like a shout in a quiet house.

"Pleasure doing business," Crane said, and this time it almost sounded true, which frightened her more than anything else he'd said.

* * *

She stopped at Bramwell's on the way home, because a debt was a debt even when you were suddenly rich on paper. The old man was patching nets on his porch, and his dog raised its head, considered barking, and decided against it.

"Heard you took a Crane job," Bramwell said, not looking up.

Word traveled fast in Rivergate; word traveled faster than decency. "News travels faster than I sign," Lena said.

"Mm." He bit a thread. "My brother ran supply barges up that gorge, thirty years back. Before the navy closed it." He looked up then, and his eyes were clear and old and sorry. "Men came back from Devil's Throat different. Quiet. Some of them didn't come back at all, and the ones who did wouldn't say why."

"Old man's stories."

"Old men are the only ones who remember anything worth telling." He set down the net. "What's the cargo?"

"Boxes. Sealed."

Bramwell nodded slowly, the way a man nods when a story confirms itself. "Sealed boxes, a race against his own fleet, and a navy file with more black ink than words." He almost smiled. "You always did pick your employers like you pick your dives, girl. Deep, dark, and full of things with teeth."

"I pick what pays."

"So did your father."

The name landed harder than Bramwell could have known — or maybe he knew exactly, old men usually did. Lena touched her pocket, where the compass rode. Brass case, glass cracked at the edge, needle worn smooth from forty years of pointing. Her father's compass. The only thing she'd kept from him, the only thing the creditors hadn't been able to price, because she'd never once let them see it existed.

He'd been a salvage diver too. Died on a job, they said — lost with a crew, lost with a ship, lost somewhere upriver in water the charts marked with a warning symbol instead of a name. She'd been nine. She'd inherited his compass and his silence and, though it took her twenty more years to admit it, his trade.

*Trust the needle,* he used to tell her, turning the brass case in his broad fingers while she watched from the floor. *Not the map, not the captain, not the paymaster. The needle doesn't lie. People do.*

She wondered, walking home along the gray evening water, what the needle would make of Devil's Throat. Whether it would point north like a good honest instrument, or whether it would do what it sometimes did lately — hesitate, tremble, swing a little way toward the river as if remembering something.

Probably nothing. Compasses got temperamental with age. So did divers.

At the boarding house, she packed by lamplight: lines, knives, the good lamp, spare gloves. Downstairs, through the thin floorboards, she could hear the landlady telling someone — Voss's boy, by the voice — that Miss Winterbourne had gone out and wasn't expected back soon.

"Not expected back," Lena murmured to herself, rolling the contract into oilcloth. Well. For once, the landlady was accidentally correct, and Lena intended to profit by it.

Five hundred crowns. Six tides. A gorge full of sealed boxes and a fleet chasing her upriver like her own shadow.

She lay awake listening to the tide turn, and just before sleep took her, she thought she heard it — faint, absurd, impossible — the tick of a compass needle shifting against its glass, two rooms away in her pack, swinging toward the river.

Toward the Throat.

Toward whatever her father had never come home to tell her.

She was on the water by dawn.
