## 2. The Silent Upriver

The fog came off the Rivergate docks in slow gray ribbons, and Lena Winterbourne stood in the middle of it, watching men load her expedition onto a boat she didn't trust.

The *Kestrel's Mercy* was a river tug gone respectable—fresh paint over old scars, a crane bolted to her stern where the fishing nets used to run. She sat low in the water even empty, the way heavy things do when they're carrying secrets. Lena had spent eleven years reading boats the way other people read faces, and this one had a liar's smile.

"Careful with that," she said, as a dockhand swung a crate of dive tanks toward the rail. "If you dent a valve, we're all swimming home."

The dockhand grunted. Everyone on the dock grunted. It was that kind of morning—the kind where the coffee was bad, the money was worse, and the only person who'd offered her work in four months was a man whose smile never quite reached his ledger.

Aldous Crane stood at the end of the pier in a coat worth more than her father's boat had been, hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring his own generosity. He'd arrived an hour ago unannounced, which Crane men apparently did not consider strange. Rich people, in Lena's experience, believed the world was their waiting room.

"You dive like your father," Crane said, without looking at her. "I've read the reports."

"I didn't know I'd been reported on."

"Everything gets reported on, Miss Winterbourne. That's rather the point of reports." He turned then, and gave her the smile he probably practiced in mirrors—the one that said *I am a reasonable man surrounded by unreasonable circumstances*. "Six tides. You understand the terms?"

"I understand them fine." She ticked them off on cold fingers. "I go upriver into Devil's Throat. I find the wreck your navy file is so embarrassed about. I bring back whatever's in her hold. I don't ask questions, I don't talk to journalists, and I don't so much as hum a sea shanty about it for ten years. In exchange, my debts go away and I eat something other than noodles."

"In exchange," Crane corrected gently, "you are *paid*. The debt settlement is separate, and contingent on discretion."

"Of course it is."

He studied her a moment, the way a man studies a horse he suspects might bite. "You have a reputation for asking questions anyway. Quietly. To yourself."

"That's not illegal yet."

"It is on my river."

Lena let that sit. Down the pier, the last crate went aboard with a thud that shook the planks, and somewhere beneath the fog a ship's horn moaned like it had opinions about all this.

She should have walked away. She knew that even then. There's a feeling a diver gets—a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the water—and she'd felt it from the moment Crane's lawyer slid the contract across the table with its suspiciously generous figures and its suspiciously vague cargo description. *Recovery of materials, non-specific.* Nobody paid that kind of money for non-specific.

But the bank owned her father's boat, and the boat was all she had left of him, and hope makes a fool of even the careful ones.

So instead she asked the question she'd been chewing since dawn.

"Where's the advance team?"

Something moved behind Crane's eyes. Small. Quick. A fish under ice.

"Pardon?"

"The advance team. The ones who surveyed the site last week. Your man Voss and his crew." She nodded at the radio shack at the pier's end, where a relay operator was pretending very hard to be busy. "They were supposed to check in every twelve hours. My contract says I'm to coordinate with them on approach. So where are they?"

"They're ahead of schedule," Crane said smoothly. "Deep in the gorge. Radio reception past the narrows is unreliable—limestone eats signal, as I'm sure you know."

"I do know." She'd grown up on this river; limestone and she were old acquaintances. "But they checked in fine from inside the gorge for six days. Then three days ago, nothing. And the last transmission—" she pulled the folded paper from her coat pocket, the transcript she'd charmed out of the relay operator with two cigarettes and a smile, "—ends mid-word. Not mid-sentence. Mid-*word*."

Crane took the paper. He read it the way rich men read things—slowly, as if the words might owe him money.

*"Voss to Rivergate. We've found her. She's not what the file says, she's older than—"* and then nothing. No *over*, no sign-off, no static burst. Just a clean cut, like scissors through thread.

"She's older than what?" Lena asked.

"Probably 'than expected.' Voss was prone to dramatic pauses." Crane handed the paper back, and his voice stayed pleasant, but pleasant the way a frozen river is pleasant—smooth on top, and underneath, current. "Equipment failure. Batteries die, antennas crack, men get distracted by pretty wrecks. The gorge is full of ways for a radio to fail, Miss Winterbourne. That's rather why it needs divers of your caliber."

"And the team itself? Three days of silence doesn't worry you? These are your people."

For just a moment—one heartbeat, maybe less—Aldous Crane looked tired. Not worried. *Tired*, the way a man looks when a bill arrives he'd hoped to defer. Then the pleasantness closed over it like water.

"My people are professionals," he said. "Professionals don't panic, and neither should you. Which brings me to a matter of compensation."

He produced an envelope from his coat. Not a check—an envelope, cash-thick, the kind of payment men use when they don't want a number appearing anywhere with their name beside it.

"Your fee has been doubled."

Lena looked at the envelope and did not take it. "Why?"

"Because the job is difficult, and I value excellence."

"Nobody doubles a fee because the job got *harder*, Mr. Crane. They double it because they want the diver to stop noticing things." She held his gaze. "Is that what this is? Am I being paid not to think?"

The fog drifted between them. Somewhere down the dock, a crate marked FRAGILE fell over, and nobody rushed to pick it up.

"Miss Winterbourne." Crane's voice dropped into something almost kind, which was somehow worse than anything else he could have done. "You're a woman drowning in debt with one asset: the best set of lungs on this coast. I am offering to buy those lungs at a fair price. Twice a fair price. What I am *not* offering is a philosophical seminar. Take the envelope, find my wreck, and let the grown-ups handle the questions."

It would have been smarter to refuse. Smarter to walk back up the hill to her rented room, pack her father's compass, and disappear inland until Crane found some other desperate diver. Lena had survived eleven years in this trade precisely by knowing when a job smelled wrong, and this one smelled like a bilge pump after a long summer.

But she thought of the bank notice pinned to her door. She thought of the auction date circled in red. And she thought—if she was honest, and she tried to be—that her father had died somewhere near Devil's Throat, and no one had ever told her why, and here was a man paying double to keep everyone from looking too closely at exactly that stretch of water.

Curiosity, her father used to say, is just greed wearing a nicer coat. He'd also said it kills more divers than depth does.

Lena took the envelope.

"Wise," said Crane.

"Don't call me wise. I'll start charging extra for flattery too." She weighed the envelope in her palm. Heavy enough to settle the boat loan twice over. Light enough to feel like a bribe, because it was one. "One condition. If we reach the gorge and there's still no word from Voss's team, I surface, I come back downriver, and I keep the fee. All of it."

Crane smiled his mirror-smile. "Agreed."

He said it too fast. That was the thing she noticed later, lying awake in her bunk while the *Kestrel's Mercy* chugged upriver into the dark. He agreed too fast, the way a man agrees when the condition doesn't matter—when he already knows the outcome, or believes he controls it.

She rolled over and pressed her forehead against the cold porthole glass. Below, the river slid black and patient. Ahead, somewhere past the narrows, the gorge waited with its limestone throat full of dead radios and missing men.

Her father's compass hung on a nail above the chart table, brass worn bright at the edges from forty years of thumbs. It swung gently with the boat's motion, steady as ever, pointing north the way good compasses do.

Lena watched it for a long time before sleep took her, and just before her eyes closed, she could have sworn the needle trembled—not north, not any direction at all, but quivering like a dog that smells something on the wind coming down from the Throat.

* * *

Dawn found them at the mouth of the narrows, and the river changed its mind about being friendly.

The banks pinched together. The water went from brown to green to a color that had no honest name, and the current picked up like it had somewhere urgent to be. Tomas—the deckhand, a broad quiet man with forearms like dock rope—leaned on the wheel beside her and said nothing for a long while, which Lena was learning was his way of saying quite a lot.

"You heard from the advance team?" she finally asked him.

Tomas shook his head once.

"Three days, and nobody at Rivergate thinks that's strange?"

Another shake. Then, grudgingly, like each word cost him: "Strange costs extra on Crane's boats. Cheaper to be deaf."

"That's a hell of a company philosophy."

"It works." He adjusted the wheel a degree. "Mostly."

"*Mostly*," Lena repeated, and looked at the water sliding past, green and deep and keeping its own counsel.

Ahead, the cliffs rose out of the mist like the shoulders of something sleeping, and between them the river narrowed to a gray ribbon and vanished into the gorge they called Devil's Throat. The last relay tower stood on the bluff behind them. As they passed out of its range, the radio behind her hissed into static, and in the static, faint as a ghost walking through a wall, Lena thought she heard a voice—two syllables, maybe three, cut off clean.

She spun around. "Did you hear that?"

Tomas hadn't moved. But his knuckles had gone white on the wheel, and he was staring at the radio like it owed him money.

"Limestone eats signal," he said quietly.

"Yeah," Lena said, watching the cliffs close in around them like a decision being made. "That's what everybody keeps telling me."
