## 3. Gorge Water

The cliffs of Devil's Throat announced themselves a full hour before the boat reached them—first as a bruise-colored smudge on the horizon, then as two walls of black stone leaning toward each other like conspirators sharing a secret over a table.

Lena stood at the bow of the *Merry Adeline* and did the thing she always did when approaching water that frightened other people: she stopped talking. Talking was for people who believed the river cared what they thought.

"Beautiful, isn't it," said Callum Bright, the mate, coming up beside her with two mugs of tea and handing her one as if they were tourists on a pleasure cruise. He was sixty, built like a barrel someone had stood on end, and he had been diving wrecks since before Lena was born. "I've always thought it looks like God started carving a cathedral and got called away for dinner."

"It looks like a place where boats go to die," Lena said.

"Well. Yes. That too." Callum sipped his tea. "Mind you, most places are."

Behind them, the deck was organized chaos: Tomas coiling rope with the fierce concentration of a man who found peace in knots; Ruth Okafor's replacement—a wiry young engineer named Pinch who talked to the engine in a low, encouraging murmur, like a jockey soothing a nervous horse; and old Marta at the wheel, who had piloted the upper river for forty years and communicated almost exclusively through grunts of varying pitch, each of which the crew had learned to translate.

The low grunt she gave now meant *trouble ahead*, and everyone pretended not to hear it.

The current hit them at the gorge mouth like a hand shoving their shoulder. The *Adeline* lurched, wallowed, and began, very slightly, to move backward.

"Pinch!" Marta barked—one syllable, rising inflection, meaning *more power if you value your life*.

"I'm giving her everything, I swear—" The engine note climbed to a whine that made Lena's fillings ache. The boat steadied, hung in the water like a fly on a windowpane, and then crawled forward by inches.

"That's the Throat saying hello," Callum said cheerfully. "She'll want to know who we are before she lets us pass."

"And how do we introduce ourselves?"

"We don't. We just keep rowing until she gets bored."

They kept rowing. The cliffs rose on either side until they blotted out half the sky, sheer and wet and streaked white with bird droppings, so tall that gulls wheeled below their crowns. The light turned greenish, filtered through spray, and the air went cold and mineral-smelling, like the inside of a cave that had decided to become a canyon. Somewhere above, water fell from an unseen height and struck the river in long silver threads.

Lena checked her father's compass. It sat in her palm, brass case warm from her pocket, needle steady on north.

*Steady,* she noted, and filed it away. Her father had taught her that a compass was never wrong—it could only be lied about. She'd spent three days trying to decide whether Crane had lied to her yet, or whether she simply hadn't caught him at it. Both possibilities felt equally likely.

The whirlpools began an hour in.

They didn't look like the drawings in the navigation manuals—no neat spirals, no tidy funnels. They looked like the river developing opinions. Great slow dimples would open in the current, ten feet across, twenty, the water sinking into itself with a sound like a giant drinking through its teeth, and then collapse again into boiling confusion. Marta threaded between them with the grunts now coming fast and varied, a whole language of menace, while Callum called depths from the bow and Tomas hung off the rail with a boathook, fending them away from rocks that appeared and vanished like the backs of sleeping animals.

"There's a story," Callum said, conversationally, as a whirlpool the size of a chapel opened off the port side and swallowed a floating log whole, "about a barge captain who came through here in the famine years. Carrying grain. Refused to pay the pilot fee." He nodded at the whirlpool. "They say the river took the grain, the barge, and the captain, and left the fee floating on top."

"Is that true?" asked Pinch, from the engine hatch, sounding genuinely alarmed.

"No idea. But I've never once been tempted to find out, which is the point of stories like that."

Lena laughed despite herself—the first real laugh since Rivergate—and felt the crew relax around her by a degree. That was another thing her father taught her, though he'd never said it outright: fear is contagious, but so is calm, and the person who breathes steadily sets the rhythm for everyone else. On a boat, the quietest person aboard is usually the one in charge.

She just hadn't expected the quietest person aboard to be her.

* * *

Tomas had a system for counting things, because counting kept his hands from shaking.

Fourteen whirlpools passed since the gorge mouth. Nine bloodstains on the rocks—not fresh, but not old enough to be anything except what he feared. Two days of fuel remaining if Pinch kept the engine singing like this. One compass that Lena Winterbourne checked more often than seemed reasonable, though he'd noticed she never wrote down what it told her.

And zero answers from the advance team, whose radio had been dead for six days now, whose last transmission—Crane's people had finally played it for them, grudgingly, when Lena pushed—ended mid-word. Mid-*word*. Not mid-sentence, which might be weather. Not mid-transmission, which might be equipment. Mid-word, like a man reaching for something and having his hand cut off at the wrist.

Tomas counted because his brother lay in a charity ward in Rivergate with a lung full of mill dust, and Aldous Crane held the paper on every coin of his treatment. That was the arrangement. That was the ledger. You did what the man asked, and your people stayed alive, and you didn't ask why the last crew went silent, because asking questions was how you ended up on the wrong side of the arithmetic.

He was coiling line—he'd coiled the same line four times now, just to have something to do with his hands—when he saw the raft.

It was snagged against a rock shelf on the eastern wall, wedged in the calmer eddy behind a spur of stone, riding up and down gently on the swell like something asleep. An expedition raft. Navy-gray hull, salvage-rigged, the kind Crane issued to his advance teams. Even at fifty yards Tomas could see the state of it: one pontoon deflated to a slack gray tongue, gear straps hanging cut rather than unbuckled, and a dark smear along the gunwale that the rain hadn't managed to wash away.

"Marta," he said quietly. "Starboard side. Look."

Marta looked. Her grunt this time was one Tomas had never heard before—low, flat, final. It needed no translation.

Callum brought them alongside with the delicacy of a man lifting a sleeping child, and Lena went down the ladder first, because of course she did, and Tomas followed because someone sensible should be behind her.

Up close, the raft was worse. The blood wasn't a lot of it—that was somehow the worst part. A scrape, a smear, a handprint on the transom. Enough to say *someone was hurt here*. Not enough to say *someone died here*. The space between those two statements was where all the nightmares lived.

"Cut lines," Lena said. Her voice was steady. Tomas counted her breaths without meaning to—she breathed like a woman reading a tide table. "You don't cut your own mooring lines unless you're leaving in a hurry or someone else is doing the cutting."

"Or both," Tomas said.

She glanced at him. Something passed between them—not trust, exactly. Recognition. Two people who had both done the same grim arithmetic and arrived at the same ugly sum.

There was nothing else on the raft. No bodies, no gear worth taking, no note. Just the blood, and a navy-issue flare pistol with one round spent, and—wedged under the thwart, as if someone had hidden it in a hurry—a canvas pouch stamped with the Crane Shipping crest.

Lena opened it with a diver's knife, careful, like it might bite.

Inside: a coil of copper wire, a broken radio valve, and a page torn from a logbook, water-swollen, the ink mostly run. But a few words survived, written in a hurried hand:

*—not the wreck on the file. There are others. Deeper. We're going down to —*

The rest was gone.

Lena read it twice. Then she folded it, put it in her jacket, next to the compass, and climbed back aboard without a word.

Tomas followed, and as he stepped over the rail, he made a decision that his brother's debts screamed at him not to make.

That night, in the lee of the wheelhouse, with the gorge walls black against a sky full of indifferent stars, he found Lena alone at the stern, watching the water.

"You should know," he said quietly, "the fleet isn't six tides behind us."

She turned. Didn't speak. Just waited, the way she did, breathing steadily, letting him set the rhythm.

"I've been listening on the shortwave. Off the books." His hands wanted to count something, so he counted the rivets on the rail instead. "Crane's spotter planes flew over yesterday. And there's a tender working the lower gorge—running lights doused. If it's six tides behind us, it's the quietest ship I ever heard of." He swallowed. "It's more like two. Maybe less."

Lena looked past him, upriver, into the dark throat of the gorge where the current sang against the stones. When she spoke, her voice was calm, and that calm scared Tomas more than the bloodied raft had.

"Then we'd better find what we came for," she said, "before whoever silenced that crew comes back to finish the job."

Above them, far up the cliff face, something moved—a flicker of light where no light should be. There and gone. Like a signal. Or like someone, high on the walls of Devil's Throat, watching them pass.
