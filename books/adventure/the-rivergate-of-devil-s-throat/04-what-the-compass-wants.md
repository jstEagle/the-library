## 4. What the Compass Wants

The compass had never lied to her. That was the whole trouble with it.

Lena Winterbourne sat on the bow rail of the *Magpie's Rest* with the brass instrument open in her palm, watching the needle do something no compass should do at four bells in the morning. It wasn't pointing north. It wasn't pointing anywhere sensible at all. It was pointing—steadily, patiently, the way a dog points at a rabbit it has every intention of catching—at the sheer black wall of Devil's Throat, some forty fathoms of wet granite rising out of the river like the side of a drowned cathedral.

"Still doing that?" Tomas asked, coming up beside her with two mugs of coffee that smelled like it had been brewed from old rope. He handed her one anyway.

"Since we passed the narrows." She turned the compass so he could see. "It wants to go left."

"Compasses don't want things."

"This one does. My father maintained it like a priest maintains his altar. If there was ever a bubble in the fluid, he'd tap it out before I'd finished noticing. He'd sooner have sold his boots." She watched the needle tremble against its glass, eager as a heartbeat. "And now it's telling me there's something in that wall worth dying for."

"Cheerful instrument."

"He was a cheerful man. Mostly."

Below them, the gorge water moved in that slow, muscular way it had—not flowing so much as *patrolling*. The current here didn't race; it circled, coiled, waited for boats to make mistakes. Ruth Okafor had spent the previous evening drawing the whirlpool patterns in her engineer's log with the grim precision of a woman mapping a minefield, and had announced at dinner that they had perhaps a six-hour window each tide when the throat of the river relaxed enough to let divers down without turning them into chum.

Lena had not slept well. She suspected nobody had. The advance team's raft still hung off the stern where they'd towed it aboard, scrubbed twice and still bearing a stain on the gunwale that no amount of lye would lift. Every time she looked at it she thought about the transmission cut mid-word, and about Crane's voice on the radio saying *proceed* as if he were ordering soup.

"You're going down there today," Tomas said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm going down there today."

"The navy file says the wreck is mid-channel. Forty meters, silt shelf, easy marks."

"The file also says the wreck is thirty years old." Lena closed the compass and felt the needle press against the glass, insistent, like a child tugging a sleeve. "My father taught me to read currents the way other people read weather. This channel doesn't behave like a thirty-year-old wreck sits in it. The scour patterns are wrong. The silt's been disturbed too deep for too long. Something down there is older than the paperwork says it is."

"And you trust your gut over an official naval survey?"

"I trust my father's compass over anything stamped with a government seal." She smiled, and it came out thinner than she intended. "Besides, Crane is paying me not to think. Thinking is the one thing he forgot to put in the contract."

Tomas laughed—a short, startled bark—and then looked guilty about it, glancing back toward the wheelhouse where Ruth was running her morning checks. "Careful. Walls have ears, and this particular wall has a payroll."

* * *

They went down on the slack of the morning tide, when the gorge held its breath.

Lena wore her father's old dive rig, patched and re-patched until it was less a suit than a biography. The water took her at ten fathoms and turned the world green-black and silent, the way it always did—the way she loved, if she was honest, more than she loved most dry-land things. Down here there were no creditors, no contracts, no Aldous Crane with his soft voice and his ledger eyes. There was only the pressure of the river on her shoulders like a firm hand, and the rope in her grip, and the small bright circle of her torch cutting through suspended silt.

She followed the channel markers first, because she was a professional even when she was furious. The navy file's coordinates put the wreck mid-channel, and mid-channel, right on schedule, the sonar pings from yesterday had shown a mass: hull-shaped, silted, exactly where the paperwork promised.

She found it in twenty minutes. A freighter's bones, listing hard to port, deck guns stripped, hatches caved. She swam the length of it with her torch, reading the growth on the plates—thirty years of it, give or take, just as the file claimed. Barnacles in their proper generations. Coral starting its slow claim along the rails. Everything orderly. Everything correct.

Everything wrong.

Because the scour trench around the keel ran too deep. Because beneath the thirty-year silt layer, her torch caught edges of timber that hadn't rotted the way thirty-year timber rots. And because, wedged in the channel floor fifty yards upstream, half-buried, lay a second keel—one the sonar had politely ignored, or been told to ignore.

Her air gauge said she had time. Her nerve said otherwise. She checked both, decided to believe the gauge, and swam toward the wall.

That was when the compass happened.

She'd brought it clipped inside her suit—against every regulation her father had ever taught her, because instruments and pressure didn't mix—but she'd carried it anyway since Rivergate, the way some people carry a saint's medal. Now, at depth, she pulled it out and watched the needle swing away from the channel, away from the filed wreck, and point directly, unambiguously, into the rock.

Into a darkness in the cliff face that her torch had skipped past twice without registering—a seam in the granite, narrow, flooded, breathing cold water outward in slow pulses. A cavern mouth. Underwater. In a gorge that appeared on no chart she'd ever seen with any feature but vertical walls and certain death.

Her father's voice arrived in her memory, uninvited, in the tone he used for lessons he expected her to remember past his own death: *A compass finds what it's made to find, Lena-girl. Iron calls to iron. You follow a needle somewhere strange, you ask yourself first—what's the iron?*

"What's the iron?" she said aloud, and heard her own voice come back at her inside the helmet, small and swallowed.

Then she tied off her line, checked her air one more time, and went into the wall.

* * *

The seam widened after eight body-lengths, opening like a throat into a chamber the size of a chapel. Her torch beam climbed a curved iron flank that rose out of the silt and vanished upward into blackness—a hull, intact, preserved by the cold and the dark and the fact that no salvage man in thirty years had thought to look inside a cliff.

She swam the length of it slowly. Reading it. And the longer she read, the colder she felt, and the cold had nothing to do with the water.

The barnacles were wrong. Generations of them—deep, layered, patient. Fifty years of colonization, maybe sixty. The steel showed pitting that took decades. The rigging remnants were pre-war pattern, the kind her father had described from his first commands, the kind that hadn't been manufactured since before Lena was born.

This ship had gone down before the war. Before the file. Before, possibly, Lena herself.

There was a cargo hatch amidships, sprung but not shattered—opened deliberately, from outside, long ago. She eased through it into the hold, and her torch found crates. Dozens of them, stacked in the careful rows of a load that had been stowed by professionals, and every crate bore a brand burned into the planking:

**CRANE SHIPPING — RIVERGATE REGISTRY**

Lena hung in the black water of a dead ship's belly and felt the bottom drop out of her stomach in a way that had nothing to do with buoyancy.

Crane Shipping. Aldous Crane's mark, burned into cargo older than her career, older than her debt, older than the navy file that swore only one wreck lay in Devil's Throat and that it was thirty years young. She touched the brand with one gloved finger, tracing the crisp C, and thought: *someone paid good money to have these marked.* Someone wanted credit. Someone was proud.

She found the manifest plate bolted inside the hatch coaming, corroded but legible, and read the registry number twice to be sure her eyes weren't lying at depth. Then she did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the first crate: if this ship sank sixty years ago under Crane's mark, then either the Crane family had been sinking ships in this gorge for three generations—or Aldous Crane was older than he looked, which was a thought she set aside with a shudder—or, third and simplest and worst, the Crane Shipping of sixty years ago and the Crane Shipping of today were the same company, same ledgers, same hands, and the navy file sitting in her cabin was not mistaken.

It was *edited.*

The 'one wreck' was a lie. The gorge was a graveyard with curated signage.

Her torch flickered—air, time, sense all thinning together—and she backed out through the hatch, pausing once at the hull's stern to sweep her light across the nameplate, silt-furred but readable, and felt the last of her professional detachment go down like a stone:

*MV WINTERBOURNE'S LUCK.*

No. Not that. She laughed into her regulator, a mad little burst of bubbles, because grief at forty meters comes out sideways. But the nameplate read true: *CORVUS STAR*, Crane registry, lost with all hands, sixty-one years ago, cause of loss entered in the company books—she'd have bet her remaining teeth—as *navigational error.*

Her father's compass swung gently in her hand, needle steady now, pointing at the wreck behind her as if to say: *there. That's the iron. That's what I was made to find.*

"My father crewed these waters," she whispered to the dead ship, to the dead company, to the living man who owned them both. "What did you do to his generation?"

The *Corvus Star* said nothing. Dead ships are polite that way.

She ascended slowly, decompressing in stages, and used every minute of it to arrange her face. By the time she broke the surface beside the *Magpie's Rest*, she was Lena Winterbourne, salvage diver, contract employee, woman with no opinions—because the one thing Crane had actually hired her for was invisibility, and she had just discovered she was very, very visible indeed, carrying sixty years of his crimes in her head like ballast.

Tomas hauled her up. Ruth stood at the rail with a towel and a look that said she'd already counted the minutes and knew Lena had gone where the coordinates didn't.

"Well?" Ruth asked.

Lena looked at the compass in her palm. The needle had gone quiet at last, satisfied, pointing north again like an honest instrument—or like one that had delivered its message and was done.

"There's more than one wreck," she said. "And the file's a forgery. And—" she hesitated, because saying it aloud would make it real, would make it *hers*, would mean she could never unknow it— "the hold is stamped with Crane's mark. Sixty years old."

Ruth went very still. Tomas's knuckles whitened on the rail.

"That's not possible," Ruth said carefully. "Mr. Crane's grandfather founded the line during the war. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows what they've been told." Lena closed her fist around the compass. "Ruth—when your advance team found the manifest, did anyone say how many wrecks there were?"

Ruth's face did something complicated and terrible. "The captain asked that exact question on the last open channel," she said quietly. "Right before the transmission cut."

Above them, faint and wrong, an engine sound crossed the gorge rim—high, thin, circling. Not a gull. Not a boat.

A spotter plane, making lazy passes over Devil's Throat, as if someone wanted to know exactly where they were standing.

Tomas looked up. Then at Lena. "Six tides was a lie too," he said.
