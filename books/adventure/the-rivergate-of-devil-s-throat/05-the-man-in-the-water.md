## 5. The Man in the Water

Lena saw the hand first.

It came out of the current like something the river had decided to give back—a pale, splayed thing clutching a broken oar, rising and vanishing in the churn below the cliff shadow. She was on deck checking the mooring lines when Tomas shouted, and by the time she reached the rail he'd already thrown the life ring, and the ring had already been swallowed.

"Downstream," she said. "Everything here goes downstream."

"Everything here goes *sideways*," Tomas said, but he was already hauling the skiff's painter loose.

They took the little boat into the eddy line together, Lena at the oars, fighting the water that wanted to spin them like a coin on a table. The hand appeared again—closer now, attached to an arm, attached to a woman in a Crane Shipping work jacket who was being carried along just under the surface like cargo nobody had signed for. Lena hooked her under the arms and hauled, and for one bad moment the river pulled back, as if it objected to the transaction. Then the woman came over the gunwale in a rush of gray water and lay there coughing up half of Devil's Throat.

She was maybe forty, broad-shouldered, with a burn scar down one forearm and the particular stillness of someone who has recently made peace with dying and is annoyed to have to unmake it. Her lips were blue. Her eyes were not. They were sharp, furious, and open.

"You're Crane's," she rasped.

"We're Crane's," Lena admitted.

The woman laughed—a wet, cracked sound—and then coughed some more. "Then you're late," she said, "and you're lied to. Both."

* * *

Tomas

They got her below into the galley, wrapped in every blanket the boat owned, and Tomas put the kettle on because his mother had raised him right and also because his hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

Ruth Okafor—that was her name, once she could say it—was the advance team's engineer. Third man off the raft when it went over, she said. The other two hadn't come up where anyone could reach them.

"There were four of you," Tomas said quietly. He'd read the manifest back at Rivergate. Four names, four signatures, one radio frequency.

"There were four of us." Ruth stared at the bulkhead. "Now there's me, and I'm only me because the river changed its mind."

He set the tea in front of her and watched her wrap both hands around the mug like it was the first honest thing she'd held in days. Up close, Tomas could see what three days alone in the gorge did to a person: the scraped knuckles, the salt-cracked lips, the way she flinched at the sound of the current against the hull—not afraid of it exactly, but listening to it, the way you listen to a dog that's already bitten you once.

"The radio," he said. "Your team went silent three days ago. What happened?"

Ruth looked at him then, really looked, the way you look at someone to decide whether they're worth drowning for. Whatever she found in his face, she must have liked it, because she sighed—a long, surrendering sound—and set down the mug.

"You want to know why we went quiet."

"I want to know why there's blood on your raft."

"That too." She rubbed her face. "All right. But I'm telling all of it or none of it, and none of it means I go back to pretending, and I'm done pretending. So: all of it."

* * *

Lena

They found the manifest, Ruth said. That was the whole catastrophe, told plainly: they'd found what they were sent to find, and it was worse than anything they'd been sent to find.

"The file says one wreck, forty years old, navy-blacklisted, cargo unspecified." Ruth's voice was flat, reciting. "It's two wrecks. The older one's sixty years if she's a day, and her hold plates are stamped C-S-L. Crane Shipping Lines. And the manifest—the real one, the paper one, sealed in a brass tube in the chart locker—we brought it up. We read it."

Lena sat very still. "What did it say?"

"It said Aldous Crane sank his own ships." Ruth turned the mug in her hands. "Insurance claims. Arms shipments written down as grain and machine parts. And crew lists, Lena—crew lists with names on them, men listed as lost with all hands, except somebody's hands were very much still attached when the scuttling charges went off. There are orders in there. Signed orders. 'Sink at position, secure manifests, report no survivors.' Report *no survivors*, like survivors are a paperwork problem."

The cabin felt smaller. Lena heard herself ask the question anyway, because she had to. "And Crane? When your team reported this?"

Ruth's jaw tightened. "We didn't report it. We tried. The radio crackled once and then we got a reply from a frequency that isn't on any chart. A voice we knew. Crane's own operations man. He said, and I quote, because I've heard it every night since: 'Well done. Hold position. Recovery is en route.'" She smiled without any warmth in it at all. "Recovery. Like we were salvage."

"What happened to the other three?"

"Marsh went into the water first. Night before last. No body, no splash anyone would admit to hearing. Then Pell and Osei decided we should take the raft downriver and find you lot ourselves—go around Crane, tell the story straight. They launched at dawn." Ruth's voice finally broke, just slightly, on the next words. "The raft came back upstream snagged on the rocks. Empty. With blood on it. I went in after them. The river kept me. Maybe it was saving me for something. Maybe it was just playing."

Lena thought of the compass in her pocket, swinging toward the gorge wall, pointing at wrecks that weren't supposed to exist. She thought of Crane across his desk, doubling her fee, saying *no questions* with a smile that had cost him nothing.

"He sent us in after you," she said. "Six tides ahead of his own fleet. He told us the fleet was competition."

"The fleet isn't competition." Ruth leaned forward, and despite everything—the shivering, the exhaustion—there was iron in her now. "The fleet is the cleanup crew. You're not racing them, girl. You're leading them. Every fathom you make upriver, you're finding things for them to bury. Including you."

* * *

Tomas

That evening the wind died and the gorge went quiet in the way it did sometimes, like a held breath, and that was when they heard the planes.

Tomas was on deck with a mug of coffee he wasn't drinking. Two engines, high up, running lights dark, sweeping the canyon walls with a searchlight that flicked across the cliffs like a lighthouse having second thoughts. Spotter planes. Fleet planes. He counted—one pass, two, a third lower than the others—and watched the light slide across the water toward their anchorage and stop just short, as if even the light had been told not to look too closely yet.

Behind him, the hatch opened. Ruth came up wrapped in blankets, moving better now, and stood beside him without a word. They watched the planes together until the sound faded upriver.

"They'll seal the mouth tomorrow," Ruth said. "Maybe tonight. Once the fleet's in position, nobody comes out of this gorge unless Crane says so."

Tomas nodded slowly. His chest hurt in a way that had nothing to do with the cold. Because here was the arithmetic he'd been doing all day, in the galley, while Ruth talked: his brother Emile lay in a charity ward in Rivergate with a lung full of river dust and a bill full of numbers, and every number on that bill had Crane's name behind it somewhere. Tomas owed the man. Not in money—in *gravity*. Debts like that didn't get forgiven; they got collected, and collectors didn't care whose side of a story you were on.

And yet.

He thought about Marsh, who he'd never met, going into black water with no splash anyone would admit to. He thought about the word *recovery*. He thought about the searchlight stopping just short of their boat, patient as a spider.

"Lena asked me to run the radio log tonight," he said. "Regular check-in. Crane's office expects it."

"And?"

"And I haven't decided what the log says." He glanced at her. "You were loyal to him a long time."

"Eleven years." Ruth said it simply, no shame in it. "He paid fair. He remembered my name. He asked after my mother." She looked out at the dark water. "You know how a person goes bad on you slow? It's not one big lie. It's a hundred small ones, each one small enough to swallow. And then one day you're standing in a gorge holding a dead man's manifest, and you realize every swallow was building toward this, and the man you trusted never existed—or worse, he existed, and he was always exactly this, and you were the one lying to yourself." She shrugged inside the blankets. "My loyalty drowned with the others. What's left is testimony."

Tomas looked at the sky, where the planes had gone. He thought about Emile's laugh, which was the same as his own but cheaper, freer, because Emile had never learned to owe anybody.

When he went below to send the scheduled check-in, his hands were steady, and the message he tapped out was three words long:

*Expedition lost. All hands.*

He sent it twice, so it couldn't be mistaken for static, and then he climbed the ladder to Lena's cabin and knocked, and when she opened the door he said, "We need to talk. I've done something, and you need to hear it from me before you hear it any other way."

* * *

Lena

By lamplight, the three of them bent over the chart table—Lena, Tomas, Ruth with her borrowed blanket like a general's cloak—and Tomas laid it all out: the check-ins he'd been sending, the replies, the order he'd received an hour ago, transcribed word for word in his neat deckhand's hand:

*Seal the throat. No vessel exits. Expedition presumed lost.*

"Presumed lost," Lena repeated.

"That's the phrase." Tomas couldn't meet her eyes. "I sent the report that gave them the excuse. I've been feeding that man our position since Rivergate. I thought—I owed him, my brother's bills, I thought if I just kept everyone alive through the job—" He stopped. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, and I'm yours now, whatever that's worth, which I know isn't much tonight."

Lena looked at him for a long moment. Fear was there, and anger, and underneath both something steadier: the strange relief of finally seeing the whole board. Every piece of it fit now—the doubled fee, the silence, the six tides, the planes. Crane hadn't hired a salvager. He'd hired an advance scout for his own funeral procession, and meant to be its only mourner.

"It's worth this," she said. "From this minute, nobody on this boat keeps a secret from anybody else. Not debts, not fears, not stupid hopeful plans. Wages bought loyalty and look where wages got us. From here on we're crew, and crew means truth. Can you live with that?"

Tomas nodded like a man taking an oath, which, she supposed, he was.

Ruth cleared her throat. "Lovely. Now that we're all honest: the manifest tube is in a cache on the north shelf, twenty feet down, marked by a cairn I built. And the primary wreck—the one with the *rest* of the evidence—is another forty feet below that, in water that will kill anyone who dives it carelessly."

Lena touched the compass through her jacket. It was warm, or she imagined it was, and it was pointing, as it had been all day, not north—but deeper.

"Good," she said. "Because we've got five tides, a sealed gorge, and a fleet coming down our throats. Somebody had better teach me the way down."

Outside, far off, the searchlight swept the cliffs again—closer this time.
