## 6. Loyalty's Ledger

Tomas Reyes had three rules for surviving a life spent on other people's boats: never volunteer for the first dive, never eat the cook's stew on a Friday, and never—under any circumstances—owe anyone more than you already do.

He had broken rule three so thoroughly that rules one and two hardly mattered anymore.

The debt was simple in its arithmetic and bottomless in its weight. His brother Mateo's lungs had failed two winters back, drowned slowly by the mill dust of Rivergate, and the only surgeon who'd take him did not accept payment in gratitude. Crane Shipping had. Forty thousand crowns, signed against Tomas's wages, his boat share, and—if the fine print was to be believed—his firstborn child, should he ever be foolish enough to have one.

So when Ruth Okafor started talking in the galley that evening, hoarse from the river water they'd pulled out of her, saying things like *Crane ordered us silenced* and *the manifest is real* and *he sank them, Tomas, he sank his own ships with men aboard*—Tomas kept his eyes on his bowl and thought about forty thousand crowns.

"You're not saying anything," Ruth observed.

"I'm eating," Tomas said. "It's a complicated process."

Lena Winterbourne sat across from him, arms folded, watching him the way she watched everything—like she could see through the hull of a man straight down to the bilge. She didn't say anything either. That was worse. Lena's silences had a way of filling up a room faster than other people's shouting.

Ruth coughed, wrapped the blanket tighter, and pressed on. "I was there. I read the file numbers myself. The *Meridian Star*. The *Corvane*. Ships that went down 'in heavy weather' ten years apart, both insured to the crown, both carrying cargo that was never listed." Her voice cracked, not from the near-drowning this time. "The advance team found the manifest in the forward hold. We radioed it in. And Crane's answer came back inside an hour."

"What answer?" Lena asked quietly.

"'Recover nothing. Return nothing. The gorge is closed.'" Ruth looked at her hands. "We argued. Marlow said we were salvage crew, not grave robbers, and if the manifest existed then it belonged to the families. That night the mooring lines were cut. Our raft drifted. And the radio—" she stopped. "The radio worked fine until it didn't. I swam out because staying meant being quiet forever."

Tomas put down his spoon. His stew had gone cold anyway, which was standard for Fridays.

"Get some sleep," Lena told Ruth gently. "Tomas, walk with me."

* * *

They stood at the rail while the Throat breathed around them—the great slow inhale and exhale of current through the gorge, the whirlpools spinning their patient circles below like dogs circling before lying down.

"He's telling the truth," Lena said. "Or she is. You know what I mean."

"Ruth," Tomas said. "Yes. She's telling the truth."

"And?"

And. There it was. Tomas gripped the rail and looked at the black water and tried to find a version of honesty that didn't cost forty thousand crowns.

"My brother is alive because Aldous Crane pays a surgeon in Vellport," he said finally. "Every month. On time. Like clockwork. You know what else is like clockwork? Crane's fleet. Six tides behind us, maybe less now. If I radio ahead—just a warning, just a courtesy, just *we've had complications*—maybe he goes easy on us. Maybe Mateo keeps breathing."

Lena was quiet for a long moment. "You think Crane goes easy on people?"

"No," Tomas admitted. "I think Crane goes *precise* on people. It's different."

"It is different," she agreed. "Precise is worse."

She didn't argue with him further. That was the thing about Lena Winterbourne nobody understood until they'd worked with her—she didn't push. She just set the truth down between you like a compass on a chart and let it point where it pointed. Then she went aft to check the dive gear, leaving Tomas alone with the rail and the water and the arithmetic.

Forty thousand crowns. One brother. One manifest that could hang a man who owned half the coast.

One radio, sitting in the wheelhouse, warm and waiting.

* * *

He told himself it would be a courtesy.

That was the word he used, turning the dial low, keying the mic past midnight while the others slept. A courtesy. A professional update between a contractor and his employer. *Expedition delayed. Advance team lost. Manifest location unconfirmed.* Nothing that couldn't be printed in a ledger. Nothing that smelled like betrayal.

The channel crackled. A relay station picked up, bounced his signal downriver toward the fleet, and then—

"This is Crane actual."

Aldous Crane himself. At one in the morning. Tomas's stomach dropped through the hull and into the gorge.

"Sir, this is Reyes, deckhand aboard the *Winterbourne* expedition. I'm calling to report—"

"I know who you are, Reyes. I hold your paper." Crane's voice was smooth as oiled chain. "You're calling about the manifest."

"The manifest is—" Tomas swallowed. "Unconfirmed, sir. We haven't located it."

A pause. In the pause, Tomas heard something that made the hair on his arms stand up: the sound of a man not surprised. Not even slightly.

"Reyes," Crane said pleasantly, "do you know what a sealed gorge is worth? Devil's Throat has one mouth, one current, and no neighbors for thirty miles. Whatever your captain thinks she's found down there, it stays down there. Seal the approaches. Nobody in, nobody out. The expedition included."

Tomas's mouth went dry. "Sir—with respect, there are four of us—"

"Three, once Okafor's accounted for. Consider her a rounding error." A rustle of papers, unhurried, obscene. "Your brother's next payment processes Thursday, Reyes. Loyalty is a ledger. Make sure your column balances."

The channel clicked dead.

Tomas sat in the dark of the wheelhouse for a long time, holding the microphone like it had bitten him. Outside, the whirlpools turned. Somewhere below them lay wrecks full of men Crane had written off as rounding errors themselves—and here was Tomas Reyes, deckhand, brother, debtor, who had called those same waters *a courtesy.*

He thought about Mateo, propped up in the Vellport clinic, joking weakly about the food, asking every letter whether Tomas was staying out of trouble. *Staying out of trouble.* Mateo had always been the funny one.

There was no version of the ledger where this balanced. He saw it now, plain as a chart with one coastline missing. Crane hadn't wanted a warning. Crane had wanted confirmation the manifest was still unfound—and the moment Tomas keyed that mic, he'd become the kind of man who sealed gorges with living people inside them.

Thursday could keep.

* * *

He found Lena on deck at dawn, coiling line with the economy of someone who'd done ten thousand hours of it. She glanced up, read his face in half a second, and set the coil down.

"Tomas."

"I radioed the fleet," he said. No preamble. His hands were shaking and he figured he might as well spend the shaking on something useful. "Last night. I told myself it was a status report. Crane came on personally. He ordered the gorge sealed—with us in it. He called Ruth a rounding error." His voice cracked on the last part, which embarrassed him, but there was nowhere to put it except out. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, Lena. I owe him my brother's life and I thought—I thought loyalty was something you could pay in installments."

Lena didn't yell. He'd half expected her to; part of him had braced for it, almost wanted it, the way you want a wound cleaned. Instead she finished tying off the coil, stood, and looked at him with an expression he couldn't name until he realized he'd seen it before—on his mother's face, the day Mateo got sick, when she stopped crying and started planning.

"Thank you for telling me," she said.

"That's it? That's all you have to say?"

"What would you like me to say?" She almost smiled, tired at the edges. "That you're a coward? You're not. Cowards don't confess at dawn—they wait until someone catches them. That you're a traitor? You were, for about six hours. Now you're not. I've known men who stayed traitors for decades and got statues." She stepped closer. "Here's what matters, Tomas. Crane thinks you're his. That makes you the most valuable thing on this boat."

Tomas blinked. "It does?"

"He'll expect reports. He'll expect you to steer us wrong, stall us, sell us out again." Lena's eyes went bright and hard, the way they did over charts. "So we let him expect it. You're going to radio him tomorrow and tell him exactly what he wants to hear—that we're floundering, that the manifest is a rumor, that the captain's losing the crew. And while he's comfortable, we find that wreck and we take everything he buried down there and we make it public."

"That's—" Tomas laughed despite himself, a short disbelieving bark. "That's insane. He'll kill us."

"Probably," Lena agreed cheerfully. "But he'll do it *after* Thursday, and your brother gets paid, and somewhere in the middle of all that we get to be the kind of people who didn't seal a gorge on anybody." She held out her hand. "Wages made you lie to me, Tomas. So let's try something new. No wages. Just allies. Deal?"

He took her hand. Her grip was diver's grip—stronger than it looked, built for holding on.

"Allies," he said. "On one condition."

"Name it."

"When this is over, somebody teaches Ruth to cook. Because if I survive Crane's fleet only to die of Friday stew, that's not a tragedy, that's a farce."

Lena laughed—a real one, startled out of her—and for one moment the Throat felt less like a throat and more like a river.

Then the lookout called down from the mast, voice tight: "Smoke on the water, south approach. Three ships. Running lights off."

The fleet wasn't six tides behind anymore.

It was one.
