## 7. The Second Wreck

The compass had stopped pretending to be a compass somewhere around the third fathom, and Lena had stopped pretending she didn't know why.

It hung from her neck on its braided lanyard, tucked against her chest where her father used to tuck it, and through the faceplate of her dive mask she could see the needle straining—not north, not toward the current, but down and to the left, into the black throat of the gorge where the sonar read nothing and the charts said *unsurveyed* in her father's own cramped handwriting. She'd found that chart three days ago, folded inside the false bottom of his old tool chest, and she had not told Ruth or Tomas about it, because some inheritances you carry alone until you understand what they weigh.

*Deeper than safe,* said the little voice in her head that sounded like every diving instructor she'd ever had, plus her mother, plus the actuarial tables. *Past your limit, girl. Past anyone's.*

"Going past my limit," she murmured into her regulator, because talking to yourself at forty meters was either madness or tradition, and the Winterbournes had never been big on sanity.

The gorge water changed as she descended. Up top it had been green-gray and rude, full of silt and argument. Down here it went cold and formal, like a church nobody attended anymore. Her light carved a cone through it and found only suspended sediment, drifting like snow that had lost its nerve halfway down. The pressure pressed on her ears, her ribs, her teeth. Somewhere above, Tomas would be watching her line counter and hating every meter of it. He'd tied off the anchor at the ledge and given her forty minutes and a look that said he'd personally come down after her if she was late, brother's medical debts or no.

She gave the line two pulls—*all well, descending*—and kept going.

The compass needle swung harder. Left. Down. Toward the wall.

Her father had bought this compass before Lena was born, secondhand, from a river pilot going blind who claimed it had "opinions." He'd used it to find things: drowned barges, lost crab pots, once—famously, in the Winterbourne family canon—a wedding ring dropped off the Rivergate ferry by a bride who cried for an hour and then kissed him so hard his hat fell in the water. *It doesn't point north,* he'd told seven-year-old Lena, turning the brass case in the sunlight. *It points at what matters. Most days, that's the same thing.*

Most days.

She'd spent thirty years believing the wreck they were sent for was where the navy file said it was—a freighter, the *Meridian Star*, gone down in '09, hull number redacted, cargo listed as machine parts. Crane's contract had been very clear: one wreck, one manifest, one payout, and no questions, questions being apparently more dangerous than whirlpools in Devil's Throat.

But the first wreck—the one in the side-cavern, the one stamped with Crane Shipping marks and older than the file claimed—had been a lie with rivets. And lies, Lena had learned in her long apprenticeship of debt, rarely travel alone.

Her light caught something below and out from the wall, and her heart did something complicated.

A hull. Not the cavern wreck. This one lay upright on a shelf of rock at what her depth gauge insisted was fifty-one meters, which her gauge had no business knowing and she had no business trusting. Silt had buried her to the rail line, but the superstructure still stood, and along the bow, half-erased by decades of river, letters the size of coffin lids:

**M E R I D I A N —**

The rest was gone. It didn't need to be there. The *Meridian Star*, exactly where the file said, except fifty meters deeper than any salvage crew could reach without equipment Crane had conspicuously not provided, in a gorge whose currents made diving a polite term for suicide.

*He didn't lose her,* Lena thought, and the thought arrived cold and complete, like ice forming. *He parked her.*

She swam the length of the hull slowly, because you don't rush a grave, even when you're furious at it. Especially then.

The cargo holds had been opened—from the inside. That was the detail that stopped her, that made her hang in the freezing dark with her heart slamming against the inside of her ribs. Scuttling charges blow inward or outward depending on intent; these had blown outward, controlled, deliberate, placed by someone who wanted the ship to settle gently on her shelf like a woman lying down for a nap she knew she wouldn't wake from. But the hold hatches were dogged open from within, and the lifeboat davits were empty, and the davits were empty because—

Because there had been no boats launched. She saw that next. The falls were still rigged, coiled, untouched. Thirty years in the water and the rope-ends were still whipped and neat. Whatever crew had been aboard had stood at those rails and watched the water come up to meet them, and their captain—or whoever gave the orders—had told them the boats were for show.

Lena's father had been a salvage man, not a merchant sailor. But the *Meridian Star*'s crew list, if it still existed, would have names on it, and one of the names in the back of her mind—the name on the insurance claim Crane's own lawyers had filed, the claim she'd pulled from the public registry two nights ago in a Rivergate records office while the clerk pretended not to see her—was *Elias Winterbourne, engineer's mate, lost with all hands.*

Her father hadn't died in a bar fight in Saltmarsh like the story everyone told, the story Lena had grown up on, the story that explained why he drank and why he left and why the compass came to her in a box with no letter. He had died here. Fifty-one meters down, in a ship his own employer had opened like a tin can and let fill.

*Machine parts.* She almost laughed into her regulator, and the sound that came out was ugly. She got her light into the forward hold through the gashed plating, and the silt had shifted just enough, and there they were: crates. Dozens of them, stenciled in faded paint that thirty years hadn't fully eaten: **CRANE SHIPPING — AGRICULTURAL EQUIPMENT**, and beneath the stencil, where a pry bar had opened one crate and the river had done the rest, the shapes of rifle barrels packed in grease like silver eels asleep.

Arms. Shipped under Crane's flag, insured as farm machinery, sunk with witnesses aboard when the ledger started asking questions. And then a second ship—the cavern wreck—to make it look like bad luck instead of murder. Insurance paid twice. Crew mourned once, briefly, in a newspaper notice Lena now realized she'd seen as a child without understanding it, a small gray column listing the lost, her father's name in it, and her mother folding the paper away and never speaking of him again except to say *he chose the river over us*, which was true in a way no one had meant it.

Lena hung there in the cold and let it hit her, wave after wave, grief and fury taking turns, and neither one won, and both of them stayed.

"I'm here," she said into the mask, and her voice sounded small and enormous at once. "Da. I'm here."

The compass against her chest felt warm, which was impossible, and she didn't care.

The manifest was in the master's cabin, because manifests always are, in the safe, because masters are sentimental that way. The safe door stood open—Ruth's advance team had beaten her to it, weeks ago, before Crane ordered them silenced—and inside, wrapped in oilcloth with a care that made Lena's throat tighten, was a canvas document wallet, dry as bone, sealed with wax.

Someone had found it and hidden it again. Hidden it *better*. For whoever came next.

*Ruth,* Lena thought. *You magnificent, stubborn woman.*

Inside the wallet: the *Meridian Star*'s true cargo manifest, initialed by the harbormaster at Rivergate, countersigned by Crane Shipping's own operations director. Machine parts, 40 tons. Then, in a second hand, added later, the amendments: arms, ammunition, field radios, destination a port that officially embargoed everything on the list. And clipped behind it, brittle and brown, a telegram: **SCUTTLE ON MY AUTHORITY. CREW TRANSFER CANCELLED. NO SURVIVORS EXPECTED. — A.C.**

No survivors expected. Except one, once, briefly—a man who clawed out of a sinking ship, swam a gorge that killed stronger swimmers, crawled upriver with a brass compass and a head full of what he'd seen, and lived long enough to father a daughter and teach her that the needle points at what matters.

Or maybe he'd never escaped at all. Maybe the compass had been his before the *Meridian Star*, and the story of Saltmarsh was just the story he'd built to live inside. Lena would never ask him now. That was the theft she couldn't recover, the salvage that was permanently out of reach, deeper than any gauge could measure.

She took the wallet. She took photographs of everything with the camera strapped to her wrist—Crane would want proof, and proof was the one thing Crane couldn't buy back once it existed twice. And then, because her air was screaming and her hands had gone clumsy with cold, she did the hardest thing she'd done since entering the Throat.

She left the compass.

Unhooked the lanyard, wound the braided cord around the safe's handle, and set the brass case inside, needle swinging, pointing—at the manifest, at the crates, at her father, at what mattered. It belonged here. It had always belonged here. It had dragged her across six tides and a hundred miles of hostile river not to be recovered but to be *returned*, and Lena Winterbourne, who had spent her whole adult life keeping herself small and invisible and unclaimed, understood suddenly that you cannot steal your own inheritance. You can only take possession of it.

She gave the line three sharp pulls—*coming up, coming up now*—and rose through the cold formal dark, lighter by one compass, heavier by one truth, and the last thing her light touched was the letters on the bow, and she read them aloud in bubbles:

*Meridian Star. Lost with all hands. Found by one.*

Above her, faint as a rumor, came a sound she'd hoped not to hear for another tide: engines. Many engines, deep-throated and patient, closing on the gorge mouth like a fist deciding to close.

The fleet wasn't coming in six tides.

The fleet was here.
