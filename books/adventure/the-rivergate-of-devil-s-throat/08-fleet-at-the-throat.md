## 8. Fleet at the Throat

The fleet came at dawn, and it did not come quietly.

Lena heard it before she saw it—the deep, patient thrum of diesel engines multiplied by six, rolling up the river like a fist closing. She climbed to the *Magpie's* rail with a mug of tea going cold in her hand and watched them round the bend below: salvage barges, crane ships, a black-hulled tender flying no colors at all, all of them arranged across the gorge mouth with the tidy menace of a hand of cards laid face-up.

"Six ships," Tomas said beside her. "He said he'd send two."

"He said a lot of things." Lena sipped her tea anyway. It had gone bitter, but so had most things lately.

Ruth emerged from the engine room wiping grease from her hands, moving stiffly on legs that still remembered drowning. She looked at the fleet for a long moment, and her jaw did something complicated.

"That's the *Vigilant*," she said. "Third barge. I helped refit her pumps two years ago. Crane gave us all a bonus that Christmas." She laughed, short and joyless. "I keep doing that. Remembering him kindly. Like a bruise you keep pressing to check if it still hurts."

"Does it?" Tomas asked.

"Worse every time."

Below, the lead barge dropped anchor with a chain-rattle that echoed off the cliffs and came back twice. A launch detached itself from the black tender and started upriver toward them, cutting a clean white seam through water that had been, until this morning, entirely theirs.

Lena set down her mug. "Well," she said. "Company."

* * *

The man in the launch was not what Lena expected.

She'd pictured Crane's enforcer as something out of a dockside ballad—scarred, silent, hands like anchors. Instead, the gangway delivered a neat, silver-templed man in a dry canvas coat, carrying a leather case and an expression of mild professional regret, as though he'd come to repossess a piano from someone he genuinely liked.

"Miss Winterbourne." He extended a hand. "Corvus Hale. Operations, Crane Salvage. May I say, your father spoke highly of you."

Something cold slid down Lena's spine and settled in her boots. "You knew my father?"

"I processed his final contract." Hale said it gently, the way one mentions weather. "A fine diver. The company missed him." He glanced past her at the boat—at Ruth, visible through the wheelhouse glass; at Tomas, who had materialized on deck with a wrench he clearly intended as decoration. "Perhaps we could speak privately?"

"We can speak here. My crew has excellent hearing and worse discretion."

Hale smiled as if she'd passed a test. He opened the leather case on the rail, and the morning light did the rest of the work for him: documents, crisp and official-looking, and beneath them a compass.

Her father's compass.

Except it wasn't—she had its twin in her pocket, warm against her hip where it had ridden for three days of gorge current. This one sat in velvet like a relic, brass polished to a shine the real one had never held. A duplicate. A prop.

"A courtesy," Hale said, following her gaze. "Mr. Crane understands sentiment. He also understands arithmetic, which is rarer in this business, and I'd ask you to indulge me in some." He tapped the documents. "Your debts. All of them. The *Magpie's* mooring arrears, the yard fees, the—forgive me—considerable sum owed to the Rivergate Loan Office, who I'm told have been creative in their collection methods. Erased. Signed off before lunch."

"And in exchange?"

"The manifest. Whatever copies exist. And your silence—not a contract, Miss Winterbourne, a courtesy returned. You sail home wealthy in the only currency that matters, which is owing nothing to anyone."

It was seductive. That was the obscene part. Lena stood there and felt the offer move through her like warm water through a wetsuit, filling every cold place. No more creditors at the door. No more diving other people's wrecks for pennies on the pound. No more being invisible, small, forgettable—a woman the world had agreed to overlook because overlooking her was cheaper than paying her.

She thought about the Loan Office man who had waited outside her boarding house three nights running, just sitting on the steps, patient as tide.

"You're offering me my life back," she said.

"I'm offering you the one you already had. Only paid for."

And there it was—the flaw in the jewel, small enough to miss if you wanted to miss it. *The one you already had.* The drowning, the debt, the pen name on every contract because Fairweather divers got remembered and remembered divers got asked questions. Her father's name, sunk in a gorge with his ship, stamped over by a company mark.

"No," Lena said.

Hale didn't flinch. If anything, he looked relieved, the way men do when the pleasant script ends and the real work begins. "Then let me leave the offer open until tomorrow's high tide. Mr. Crane is a patient man regarding money and an impatient one regarding witnesses, and I'd hate for those two temperaments to arrive at different conclusions." He closed the case, compass and all, and inclined his head. "One tide, Miss Winterbourne. Spend it wisely."

His launch receded downriver. Tomas exhaled like a man surfacing.

"One tide," Ruth repeated flatly. "Generous of him. It'll take the fleet four hours to rig a boarding party."

"Then we'd better be gone in three," Lena said, "and gone *through*, not around. Get the charts. We're going to talk about the whirlpools."

* * *

Tomas had grown up on stories of Devil's Throat the way other children grew up on lullabies, and none of them ended well.

Now he knelt on the *Magpie's* deck with the chart spread between his knees, weighting the corners with winch handles while Ruth traced the channel with a grease pencil. The whirlpools marked themselves in ink spirals, five of them, strung along the narrows like a necklace of drowned things.

"They breathe," Ruth said. "That's the trick of it. Old-timers called it the Throat swallowing. Every flood tide, they spin up. Every ebb, they slacken—but never all at once. There's a lag. Fifteen minutes, maybe twenty, between the first one calming and the last."

"So we run the gap," Tomas said slowly, working it through. "We go when the first pool dies, and we pray the fifth holds its manners."

"We don't pray. We weight the bow, double-lash everything that floats, and we put the engine at full ahead through the worst of it and trust the hull." Ruth looked up at him. "You've run rapids."

"River rapids. These are sea rapids wearing a river's clothes." He rubbed the back of his neck. "My brother ran the narrows at Corvo once, cargo run, against advice. Boat made it. His nerve didn't—he never took the helm again." He paused, and the old arithmetic surfaced, the one that had kept him awake since the night he'd keyed the radio: *Crane pays for Mateo's lungs. Crane pays for the medicine. Crane pays, and pays, and what does the paying buy?*

An order to seal the gorge. With them inside it.

"Tomas." Ruth's voice was quiet. "Whatever's turning over in there—I need to know which side of the deck you're standing on before we hit that water."

He thought about lying. It would have been easy; he'd been easy with lies his whole life, small ones, kind ones, the sort that kept wages flowing and brothers medicated. But he'd heard Crane's voice on that radio, calm as a ledger, saying *seal it*, and some door in him had shut that no kindness was going to reopen.

"I radioed the fleet," he said. "Three nights ago. Told them where we were anchored." He met Ruth's eyes, then Lena's, where she'd stopped in the companionway with the chart weights in her hand. "I owed Crane Mateo's debts. I thought—" His voice caught. "I thought loyalty was a thing you could owe. Like money. I know better now."

Silence, except the gorge water slapping the hull.

Lena crouched, set the winch handle down, and looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, "Mateo's debts are coming out of whatever we find at the bottom of this mess. Consider it a loan. From me. At ruinous interest—you'll repay it by never keeping another secret on my boat."

Tomas laughed, wet-eyed and startled. "Yes, ma'am."

"Good. Now help me lash the dinghy, because if we're running the whirlpools at slack water, I want nothing on this deck loose enough to kill us."

They worked through the afternoon—bow weights forward, lines doubled, the manifest sealed in oilskin and strapped beneath Lena's dive vest hooks where it could go into the water with her if the worst happened. Ruth rebuilt the engine's fuel line with the tenderness of a woman apologizing to it. And the sun crawled west, and the tide turned, and somewhere below the fleet's cranes began to swing into position like patient herons.

At dusk, Lena stood at the rail and watched the first whirlpool begin to slow, its spiral loosening by degrees, the way a fist opens finger by finger.

"One tide," she murmured. "He gave us one tide."

Behind her, Ruth killed the cabin lights. In the dark, the *Magpie's* engine turned over—soft, then strong, then hungry.

"Slack water in twenty minutes," Tomas called from the helm. "After that, the Throat starts swallowing again."

Lena took the compass from her pocket. Its needle swung once, twice—and settled, steady and certain, pointing straight down the narrows toward home.

"Hold on to something," she said. "Both of you. And Tomas—if you hear singing, that's the whirlpools. Ignore it. Everyone always ignores it too late."

The *Magpie* gathered way, and the dark water of Devil's Throat opened ahead of them like a held breath.
