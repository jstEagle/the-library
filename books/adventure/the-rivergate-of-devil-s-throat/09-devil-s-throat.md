## 9. Devil's Throat

The gorge woke before dawn, the way it always did—first with a groan of water against stone, then with birds scattering off the cliffs like a handful of thrown dice. Lena stood at the rail of the *Marrow*, watching the light come down in slivers between the walls, and tried to feel brave.

She mostly felt cold. But she supposed that was what bravery was made of down here: cold, and stubbornness, and one last dive.

"They're coming," Ruth said from the wheelhouse, where she'd spent the night with her ear pressed to the radio like it owed her money. "Two hours, maybe less. Crane's fast boats took the lower channel."

"Then we have time for exactly one thing," Lena said.

Tomas appeared with coffee that smelled like it had been brewed over an engine and tasted worse, which meant it was perfect. He pressed a mug into her hands and looked at her with the particular gravity of a man who had already confessed his sins and been forgiven, and still couldn't quite believe it.

"The buoy's rigged," he said. "Ruth checked the beacon twice. I checked it three times, because I'm the one who owes you apologies."

"You don't owe me apologies anymore," Lena said. "You owe me good diving weather."

He almost smiled. Down here, in Devil's Throat, with a fleet of black-hulled ships crawling upriver toward them, that almost-smile felt like a small miracle, and Lena tucked it away the way her father used to tuck away lucky coins.

The plan was simple, which was fortunate, because simple plans were the only kind that survived contact with a whirlpool. The manifest—the real one, the paper that named Aldous Crane as the man who sank his own ships with his own crews aboard—was sealed in a waterproof case. They would rig it to a salvage buoy, send it up through the flooded cavern's blowhole, and trigger the emergency beacon. Every receiver on the coast would hear it. Every radio in Rivergate. There would be no taking it back, no buying it back, no drowning it.

There would be no invisible Lena Winterbourne anymore either. She'd thought about that all night, lying in her bunk listening to the gorge breathe. Invisibility had kept her alive for years. It had kept her small, too, and broke, and alone, answering to men like Crane because men like Crane never noticed the quiet ones until they needed them.

Her father had been quiet too. And Crane had sunk him for it.

"Well," she said, draining the terrible coffee. "Let's go give him something to notice."

* * *

The descent into the side-cavern was familiar now, horribly so. The compass led her down—not north, never north anymore, but inward, toward the wall of the gorge where the water turned cold and green and old. She'd stopped fighting it days ago. Her father's compass knew this place better than any chart did, because her father had died making sure someone could find it again.

The wreck rose out of the dark like a cathedral that had lost an argument with God. Hull plates furred with rust. The hold gaping open. And there, on the stern where the growth had peeled away, the letters she'd traced with her own gloved fingers two nights ago, feeling them like scars:

*WINTERBOURNE'S PRIDE.*

Not a navy file number. Not a ghost ship. A name. His name, given to his boat, the way he'd given her his name and his compass and his habit of humming when he was nervous.

"Hello, Dad," she said into her regulator, which produced only bubbles, but bubbles were honest company.

She swam into the hold. The manifest case was already strapped where they'd left it, lashed to a stanchion near the breach in the hull. All she had to do was clip it to the buoy line, check the seals, and pull the beacon pin. Three tasks. A child could do them. Her hands shook anyway.

That was when the first concussion came through the water.

It rolled through the cavern like thunder heard underwater—which, technically, it was—and the silt jumped off the hull plates in gray ghosts. Lena froze, one hand on the case. Another concussion followed, closer. Then another. Not depth charges. Something blunter. The fleet's ram-bows, punching through the outer shoals, or their winch-anchors slamming down to seal the channels.

They weren't waiting two hours. They weren't even waiting one.

*Move,* she told herself, and moved.

The buoy line ran up through the blowhole shaft, a thin silver thread in her headlamp. She clipped the case to the release collar, checked the seal—good, good, *good*—and put her thumb on the beacon pin. One pull. One broadcast. Everything after that belonged to the coast, not to her.

But her hand wouldn't move yet. Because below her, half-buried in the silt of the hold, wedged beneath a fallen beam, something caught her light and threw it back brass-yellow.

The compass.

No—not *the* compass. She carried that one against her chest, in the pouch over her heart, the way she had since she was nine years old. This was its twin. Her father's working spare, the one he'd taken aboard the *Pride* on its last run, the one the navy file listed as *lost with all hands.*

Lena hung in the green water and understood, with a clarity that hurt more than the cold, what it wanted. What he'd wanted. He hadn't hidden the spare by accident. A navigator who suspected his employer might sink him didn't leave things to chance. He'd wedged it under the beam pointing—not north, never north—inward, toward the evidence, a needle drawn like a signature on a confession.

*Find it,* the compass had been saying for thirty years. *Find it, and say it out loud.*

She could take it. That was the temptation, and it was a soft, sweet one: take the second compass, keep the first, carry both names out of this gorge and let the past stay exactly as buried as it had always been. Take the proof of her father and leave the proof of his murder to the lawyers.

Or she could leave it. Leave it wedged in the wreck of the *Winterbourne's Pride*, holding its post, pointing at the truth, doing the job he'd given it. Let it be what it had always been—not a keepsake, but a witness.

Above her, muffled by forty feet of water and rock, she heard the fleet's engines enter the throat of the gorge. Deep, hungry, closing.

Lena pulled the beacon pin.

The sound was nothing—a click, a hum, a shiver in the line—but she felt it go out anyway, felt it leave her the way a shout leaves the body, and somewhere far above, on every receiver from Rivergate to the salt coast, a signal began to sing. Somewhere, Ruth's rigged transmitter was spitting the manifest into the airwave, page by page, name by name, *Crane* by *Crane.*

She looked at the spare compass one last time.

"You stay," she said. "You've earned the view."

And she kicked for the surface, leaving her father's compass standing watch over his own grave, and let invisibility sink behind her like a coat she'd finally outgrown.

* * *

She broke the surface inside the cavern to chaos, which was at least familiar territory.

Tomas hauled her over the gunwale of the skiff with more enthusiasm than technique, and Ruth was shouting over the engine noise—"It's transmitting! Coast Guard band, commercial band, I even got it onto the fishing frequencies, half of Rivergate is hearing Crane's name read aloud with his shipping numbers attached—"

"How long?"

"Minutes. Maybe less." Ruth's face was lit blue by the radio panel, and she was grinning like a woman setting fire to her own leash. "And Lena? The fleet just changed course. They're coming straight for us."

Of course they were. You didn't spend thirty years building an empire on sunken secrets and let one diver broadcast yours to the entire coast without sending someone to stomp on the radio.

The *Marrow* was already moving. Tomas had her pointed down the throat of the gorge, into the current, because going back meant the blockade and going forward meant the whirlpools, and between those two choices the whirlpools at least had the decency to be honest about wanting to kill you.

The first shell hit the cliff above them and the whole gorge rang like a struck bell.

"Down!" Lena shoved Ruth flat as rock-shrapnel pattered across the deck like angry rain. Through the smoke she saw them: three of Crane's fast boats sliding out of the haze behind, black hulls, searchlights already sweeping, and beyond them the bulk of the salvage fleet filling the gorge mouth like a cork in a bottle.

Except the cork was leaking. Even now, she could see lights changing on the distant bridge of the lead ship—confusion, argument, a searchlight swinging wildly as somebody aboard got the broadcast on their own radio and realized whose name was in it. Crane's own crews, hearing what their wages had been paying for.

"They know," Ruth breathed. "Lena, they *know.*"

"Then let's not die before the paperwork arrives," Lena said. "Tomas! The narrows!"

"The narrows are suicide!"

"The narrows are *narrow,*" Lena said, gripping the rail as the gorge screamed past on either side, "and their boats are wide. Take them."

Tomas took them. The *Marrow* went into the churning water between the cliffs like a leaf over a waterfall, and for ten seconds Lena's entire world was white water, Ruth's whooping, and the compass pouch thumping against her chest like a second heartbeat.

When they came out the other side, smoking and listing and missing most of a railing, one fast boat had followed them in and paid for it—it was wrapped around a rock spur, crew scrambling, its searchlight spinning uselessly at the sky. The other two had turned back. Whether from the narrows or from the news crackling over their own radios, Lena couldn't tell, and didn't care.

Ahead, the gorge opened. Ahead, the river widened, and the dawn came up gold on the water, and somewhere downriver lay Rivergate, full of radios, full of people, full of witnesses.

Ruth killed the transmitter with a flourish. "That's everything. It's out. It's *everywhere.*"

Lena leaned on the rail and breathed. The air tasted like diesel and spray and freedom, which was a strange flavor, and she thought she could learn to like it.

Behind them, deep in the gorge, her father's compass held its post in the dark, pointing forever at the truth. Ahead of them, the coast was waking up to his name—and hers.

"Tomas," she said. "How do you feel about working for a Winterbourne?"

He grinned through the spray. "Depends. Does she pay in apologies or actual money?"

"Both. In advance."

The *Marrow* limped downriver toward Rivergate, toward whatever came next—and on the water behind them, the great black fleet sat dead in the current, its searchlights winking out one by one, like a beast discovering, all at once, that it had been seen.
