## 10. Rivergate

The first thing Lena Winterbourne did when she surfaced was spit out the river and laugh.

It wasn't a pretty laugh. It came out of her in ragged pieces, half sob, half whoop, the kind of sound a person makes when they've been holding their breath for six tides. Ruth Okafor hauled her over the gunwale by the collar like a sack of mail, and Tomas wrapped her in a blanket that smelled of engine oil and river mud, and for a long moment nobody said anything at all. The three of them just sat there in the bottom of the boat, steaming, shaking, alive.

Above them, Devil's Throat went on roaring its indifferent roar. But the sky was empty. The spotter planes were gone. And somewhere down in the black water behind her, wedged in the ribs of a wreck her father had died aboard, a beacon was still singing.

"It's out," Lena managed. "The manifest. It's—"

"We know." Ruth's voice was hoarse. She'd been listening to the radio for the better part of an hour, and her face had a strange, stunned softness to it, like a woman watching weather she'd only ever read about. "It's everywhere. Rivergate harbor authority. The naval station at Cape Merrow. Two newspapers, a magistrate's office, and—" she paused, and something that might have been a grin cracked through the exhaustion, "—a fish-canning outfit in Port Sallow, because Tomas couldn't remember the frequency for the coast guard and just started reading numbers at people."

"They were very polite about it," Tomas said. "The cannery. They said they'd forward it on."

Lena started laughing again, and this time it didn't hurt so much.

* * *

By the time they came down out of the gorge, the river had changed character. Word travels fast on water—faster, sometimes, than boats—and every pilot, fisherman, and barge hand they passed had heard something. They didn't know what exactly. They knew a name, though. *Crane.* They said it the way people say the name of a storm that has finally blown itself out: with wonder, and with a certain amount of relish.

At the mouth of the gorge, they found the fleet.

Or what was left of it. Three salvage vessels sat at anchor in loose, dispirited formation, and even from a distance Lena could see the rot in them. Men on deck arguing. A launch tied up alongside the *Charity Crane* with its engine idling, as though half its crew were considering simply leaving it there. On the lead ship's bridge, a figure stood very still at the rail, watching them come.

Aldous Crane's enforcer. The one who had offered her a fortune and her father's compass back "as a courtesy." He didn't signal them. Didn't raise a hand. He just watched, and then he turned and went inside, and Lena understood that the offer had expired along with everything else.

"Should we be worried?" Tomas asked quietly. "That he's watching?"

"He's watching a boat with no cargo worth taking," Lena said. "Let him watch. It's the first honest work he's done all year."

Ruth snorted. It was not a delicate sound.

They ran the last stretch to Rivergate with the evening light going copper on the water, and when the harbor lights came up—those familiar, shabby, beautiful lights, the ones she'd looked at from the wrong side of the ledger her whole adult life—Lena felt something in her chest unclench for the first time since she'd signed Crane's contract.

* * *

Rivergate had heard the broadcast. All of Rivergate had heard the broadcast.

They came off the boat expecting trouble and found instead a kind of stunned, festive paralysis, like a town that had woken up to find its most feared neighbor arrested in his dressing gown. The Crane Shipping office on the quay was dark, its brass plaque already wearing a film of ash where somebody had thrown something at it. A crowd had gathered outside the harbormaster's station, not angry—*listening*. Every window in the place glowed, and through the glass Lena could see a dozen people crowded around a single radio, and the radio was reading out cargo manifest numbers in a flat, official voice, and every one of those numbers had *C.S. Crane* stamped behind it like a signature on a confession.

"Scuttled his own ships," a dockhand was saying as they passed. "With the crews aboard. I hauled cargo for that man eleven years."

"My uncle was on the *Merrow Star*," someone said, very quietly, and the crowd went still around him, and nobody said anything else, and that silence said more than the broadcast ever could.

The debt collectors, Lena noticed, were conspicuously absent.

This deserved appreciation. For three years, the collectors had been as reliable as tide tables—Mr. Fenwick with his little book and his little smile, the two brothers from the loan house who liked to stand too close, the polite letters that were somehow worse than the impolite ones. She'd rehearsed, on the long run downriver, exactly what she'd say to Fenwick when she saw him. She had a whole speech. It involved the words *war profiteer* and the phrase *I'll be paying you nothing, ever*.

Instead, she found a note tucked under her door. Mr. Fenwick's handwriting, usually so crisp, had gone wobbly at the edges:

*Miss Winterbourne — In light of recent developments, our firm is reviewing its position. No action is required of you at this time. — F.*

"No action is required of you at this time," Ruth read over her shoulder, and then read it again, savoring it. "That's the most frightened sentence I've ever seen a grown man write."

"He's reviewing his position," Tomas said solemnly. "I believe his position is: under a rock."

Lena laughed until her ribs ached, and then she sat down on the floor of her own narrow rooms—her rooms, with the rent owed and the rent now uncollectible—and cried a little, and that was fine too.

* * *

The compass stayed in the wreck.

She'd known it would, from the moment she wedged it into the beam beside the manifest housing, and she'd made her peace with it down there in the dark, in the cold, in the last place her father had ever been. It had never really pointed north anyway. It had pointed at him, all those years, and she had followed it all the way home.

Still. Some mornings she reached for it out of habit, the way you reach for a lamp that isn't there anymore, and her hand found only the empty pocket, and she had to smile at herself.

"You could dive for it," Tomas offered, a week later, as the three of them sat on the quay sharing a paper bag of fried fish that was, for the first time in living memory, paid for without arithmetic. "The wreck's not going anywhere. I'd take you down myself."

"No," Lena said, and found she meant it. "It's where it wants to be. And I know where it is. That's not the same as being lost."

Ruth had taken a room at the Harborside Inn and was, in her words, "resting," a word she pronounced the way other people pronounced *bored out of my mind*. Crane's lawyers had come for her twice and left both times looking like men who'd tried to arrest the tide. She was, Lena suspected, drafting testimony. Ruth did everything in triplicate.

"You know they'll want you to testify," Ruth said now, gesturing with a fish bone at the magistrate's office, where a new notice had gone up that morning: *INQUIRY INTO CRANE SHIPPING — PUBLIC SESSIONS COMMENCE*. "The inquiry. You found the manifest. You broadcast it. You're the whole case, more or less."

"I know."

"They'll ask you about the contract. About the six tides. About why you took it." Ruth's voice gentled, which for Ruth was roughly equivalent to a hug. "You don't have to have a pretty answer."

"I took it because I was drowning," Lena said. "That's the answer. It's not pretty, but it's true, and I'm done trading true things for pretty ones."

Ruth looked at her for a long moment. "Crane used to say something like that," she said. "About trading. Different lesson, though." She stood, brushed the fish grease off her trousers, and added, "I'll be at the inquiry. In triplicate."

* * *

The contract arrived on a Thursday, delivered by hand by a woman named Ilsa Voss, who ran two diving barges out of Port Sallow and had, by her own account, spent the last week "listening to that radio and thinking very hard about who I want working for me."

Lena read it twice at her little table by the window. Fair rates. Shared salvage rights. A clause about crew safety that actually had teeth in it. At the bottom, in the space where the contracting party's name went, she picked up the pen and paused.

For eleven years she had signed *L. Winterbourne*, and before that, on the cheap work and the dirty work, she'd signed other names—names that kept her invisible, unfindable, unownable. *L. Marsh. Lena Wint. Once, memorably, simply "the diver."* A pen name for a pen life. Her father had taught her that: the best way to survive the river was to be nobody on it.

She wrote *Lena Winterbourne*, in full, in careful letters, and signed beneath it, and felt the weight of it settle on her like a good coat.

Ilsa Voss watched her do it and nodded once, as if something had been confirmed. "First job's the *Merrow Star*," she said. "The navy wants her hold mapped before the inquiry sits. They asked for you by name."

"My name," Lena said.

"Your name." Ilsa shouldered her bag and paused at the door. "Fair warning, Winterbourne—the whole coast knows it now. There's no going back to being nobody."

Lena looked out the window at the harbor, at the water going gold with morning, at the far dark line of the gorge downriver where her father waited with his compass in the quiet.

"Good," she said. "I was never any good at it anyway."

She folded the contract into her jacket, over her heart, and went to find her crew.
