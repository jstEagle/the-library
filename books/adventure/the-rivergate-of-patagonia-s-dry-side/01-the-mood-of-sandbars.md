## 1. The Mood of Sandbars

The Rio Seco told you its mood before dawn did.

Noor Thornbury had learned that the way you learn a lover's breathing — not all at once, but in small humiliations and slow corrections, over years. Most pilots read water by color or current. Noor read it by sound. A river going shallow sang differently than a river going fast. A sandbar on the move made a whispering, granular complaint under the hull, like someone turning over in bed who didn't want to be woken. And this morning, three miles below the last sheep station on the lower reach, the *Mariposa* was hearing exactly that whisper — which meant the bar off the eastern point had shifted again in the night, the way it always shifted after a wind out of the west, and if Noor steered the way the old charts said to steer, they'd grind her keel into six feet of Patagonian nothing.

"Port," Noor said quietly, to no one, because there was no one to say it to. The wheel answered with its familiar loose rattle. The *Mariposa* leaned obediently left, and beneath her plates the whisper faded, replaced by the deep, contented hum of honest water.

Noor allowed herself one small, private smile at the empty pilothouse. It was a habit now, smiling at nobody. Like talking to nobody. Like eating breakfast standing up at the chart table because sitting down across from an empty chair had become, somewhere in the last four years, unbearable.

The sun came up over the dry side of Patagonia the way it always did: without ceremony, without mercy, a white coin behind scrubland so flat and so vast that the horizon seemed less like a line than like a suggestion the land had declined to take. There were no mountains yet this far downstream — those came later, blue and improbable, guarding the headwaters like jealous relatives. Down here the country was thorn bush and salt grass and the occasional guanaco standing in the middle of nowhere with the expression of something that had forgotten why it walked over here in the first place.

Noor loved it. That was the trouble.

* * *

By seven the *Mariposa* had made the bend at Punta del Hueso, where the river widened and slowed and the first estancia fences appeared, leaning at angles that suggested their builders had lost interest halfway through. Noor throttled down for the approach to Puerto Árido — a generous name for a settlement of forty roofs, one cantina, a coaling shed, and a telegraph office whose operator, Señora Ibarra, was widely believed to sleep with her boots on.

The dock at Puerto Árido was a wooden tongue sticking out into brown water, and on it stood the usual morning congregation: two gauchos waiting on freight that would come whenever it came, a dog with strong opinions about the dog's territory, and Bruno Ferreyra, Noor's deckhand, holding a coil of rope and wearing the particular face of a man who has news and is deciding whether it can keep until coffee.

It could not. Noor could see that from fifty yards.

"Captain." Bruno caught the bow line Noor tossed and made it fast with the economy of long practice. "Telegram came yesterday evening. Señora Ibarra wouldn't give it up until she'd read it twice."

"She reads everything twice."

"This one she read three times." Bruno handed over the envelope with the reverence he usually reserved for fresh bread. "She says it's official. Government paper. She says—" he dropped his voice into a fair imitation of the telegraph operator's gravel— "*'Tell the captain this is real money, not like the wool brokers.'*"

Noor turned the envelope over. The seal was indeed official, which on the dry side of Patagonia usually meant expensive and rarely meant good. Inside, the telegram itself was brief enough to read twice before the kettle boiled:

> CAPT THORBURY STOP GOVT SURVEY CHARTER REQUIRED RIO SECO UPPER REACH STOP FIVE SURVEYORS ONE CARTOGRAPHER STOP SIX WEEKS PROVISIONED STOP TRIPLE STANDARD RATE STOP REPORT PUERTO ARIDO OFFICE BY 14TH STOP REPLY URGENT STOP

Triple rate.

Noor read it a third time anyway, because triple rates on government paper were the kind of thing that happened to other people, and then folded it along its creases with more care than the document deserved.

"Well?" Bruno said.

"Well what?"

"You're doing the thing where your face pretends to be bored while your eyes count money."

"My eyes are doing no such thing." They were, though. Six weeks at triple rate. Noor did the arithmetic the way Noor always did arithmetic — quickly, greedily, and with a sinking feeling underneath it that had nothing to do with numbers. Six weeks at triple rate was enough to refit the engine properly instead of coaxing it. Enough to pay off the debt in Bahía Blanca. Enough that after, Noor could —

Could what? That was the question that arrived every time, punctual as the tide. After. After the money, after the run, after the next job — then what? Then the next one. Then the one after that. Keep moving. That was the whole architecture of the life, and Noor had built it deliberately, plank by plank, the way you build anything over a hole in the ground.

"Six weeks," Bruno said, reading the telegram upside down with the shamelessness of family. "Upstream?"

"Upper reach."

Bruno whistled low. "Nobody runs the upper reach."

"Nobody's paid triple to."

* * *

The morning proceeded in its usual order: engine checked, cargo manifest argued with, breakfast eaten standing up. The *Mariposa* was a hundred and ten feet of stubborn riverboat, twin-screwed, shallow-drafted, built in Rosario twenty years before for a trade she was never quite suited to and perfectly suited to everything else. Her paint was the green of old bottle glass. Her engine had a knock on the port side that Noor had named — Don Ernesto — and treated with the mixture of affection and suspicion one extends to a relative who borrows money.

At midmorning Noor walked up to the telegraph office to send the reply, mostly because walking to the telegraph office was a thing a person did, and a person needed things to do, and the walk took eleven minutes each way if you let the dog follow you, which it would, having decided years ago that Noor was part of its territory too.

Señora Ibarra received the reply form across her counter with visible disappointment that it contained only the word CONFIRMED and not, apparently, any drama whatsoever.

"That's it?" she said. "'Confirmed'? You're taking a government charter up past the maps for six weeks and your entire reply is eight letters?"

"The rest of my vocabulary costs extra."

"They'll want details. Vessel particulars. Crew list." She was already writing. "You have a crew list?"

"I have Bruno."

"And Pilar."

"Pilar is fourteen."

"Pilar is fourteen and has been running your winch since she was twelve, and if you don't put her on the crew list she'll stow away anyway and you'll be short a wage record when the inspectors come." Señora Ibarra wrote Pilar's name down herself, in the firm hand of a woman who had been correcting other people's decisions since before either of them was born. "There. Now. Upper reach." She looked up, and for a moment the officiousness fell away and something older showed through, something that remembered when the upper reach had names on it instead of blank spaces. "You know what's up there?"

"Water," Noor said. "That's rather the point of a survey."

"I mean before the concession company bought the silence." She stamped the reply with unnecessary force. "My mother's people ran sheep above the gorge. Good wells, they had. Sweet water. Then the company came with its papers, and within two years the wells were dust and everyone said it was the drought, and everyone knew it wasn't." She slid the form into the wire basket. "River remembers who drinks first, captain. My grandmother used to say that. I used to think it was just something old women said."

"And now?"

"Now I'm an old woman," Señora Ibarra said, "and I still don't know. But I'd keep my hand on the wheel up there, if I were you."

Outside, the dog escorted Noor back to the dock with the self-importance of a hired escort, and the whole way Noor thought about sweet wells gone to dust, and about blank spaces on charts, and about how every instinct Noor owned — the ones that had kept the *Mariposa* off a thousand bars — was currently saying *no* in a clear voice while the arithmetic said *triple rate* in a louder one.

* * *

The afternoon went to freight. Wool bales off the punt from Estancia del Medio, crates of nails and flour and one piano, God help them all, consigned upstream to a ranch house whose owner had apparently decided his life lacked sufficient regret. Bruno and Pilar worked the winch while Noor conned the loading from the deck, and Pilar — wiry, freckled, permanently sunburnt at the nose — worked with the fierce competence of a young person who has decided that being underestimated is a condition she intends to cure in everyone around her, one load at a time.

"Captain," Pilar said, swinging a crate onto the deck, "is it true we're going up past the maps?"

"Who told you that?"

"Bruno told Señora Ibarra's nephew, who told the butcher, who told everyone, so yes."

"The butcher is a reliable source of information about meat."

"So it's true."

Noor watched the piano swing aboard and considered, briefly, the futility of information management in a town of forty roofs. "It's true. Six weeks. Triple rate."

Pilar's whole face changed — the careful teenage neutrality dropping away for one unguarded second, revealing underneath it the same hunger Noor carried, the same itch toward the blank spaces. Then the neutrality came back, badly. "I suppose that's fine," Pilar said. "If you need me."

"I need you to mind the winch."

"Yes, captain." But the grin leaked through anyway, and Noor turned away before it could catch.

* * *

Evening came down over Puerto Árido the way it did everywhere on the dry side — sudden, total, and smelling of woodsmoke. Noor ate alone in the pilothouse, as usual, a plate of Bruno's stew balanced on the chart table next to the lower reach, and found the stew going cold because the chart had become more interesting than dinner, which was saying something, because Bruno's stew was genuinely good.

The lower reach ended at the map's edge. Everyone knew that. Below Punta del Hueso the Rio Seco was charted, sounded, tamed; above it the river ran through the gorge country and then into the high plateau, and the charts simply stopped, the way conversations stop when someone walks into the room. Old Tomás had run parts of it once — before. Before the accident. Before the promise. Before the word *before* became the organizing principle of Noor's entire interior life.

Tomás would have taken the charter. That was the thought that arrived with the dark, uninvited, sitting down across from the empty chair the way it always did. Tomás had loved the blank spaces. Had called them *the rivergate*, laughing, tapping the edge of the chart where the ink ran out — *past here, hermanita, the river decides who it likes.* And the river had decided, one bad night four years ago, and it had decided against him, and Noor had lived, and had made a promise over cold water that amounted to this: keep moving. Never stop anywhere longer than a week. Because stopping was where the guilt lived, and motion was the only anesthesia that worked.

Four days in Puerto Árido already. Time to be gone.

Except the charter started here. Except the charter was six weeks.

Noor pushed the plate away and stood at the window a while, watching the town lights — all nine of them — blur in the glass. Somewhere out there in the dark the river whispered over its moving sands, rearranging itself, patient, indifferent, keeping its own accounts. Triple rate. Six weeks. Upstream of maps, into whatever the company had bought the silence about.

"It's just money," Noor said aloud to the empty room, and heard immediately how thin that sounded, and laughed at it, shortly, without much humor.

The lamp hissed. The chart lay open to the edge of the known world. And Noor, who had spent four years perfecting the art of leaving, sat back down and began, slowly, to plan a journey whose only certain feature was that it would make leaving very difficult indeed.

Down at the telegraph office, a light burned late. Señora Ibarra was sending a wire north, confirming passage for five surveyors and one cartographer, arriving on the fourteenth.

Her name, the wire said, was Vasquez.
