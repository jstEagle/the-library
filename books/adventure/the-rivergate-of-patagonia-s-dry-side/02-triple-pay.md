## 2. Triple Pay

The telegram found me at the bottom of a beer glass in Puerto Árido, which is where most bad news finds me.

I'd been nursing it for an hour at the Fonda del Faro, watching the harbor light swing its slow arc over water the color of old pewter. The bartender, a woman named Chela with forearms like mooring cable, had stopped asking if I wanted another. She just refilled when the glass got low and charged me when I left, and between us that was a whole civilization of trust.

The boy who brought the telegram was maybe twelve, barefoot, breathless from running the length of the pier road. "Captain Thornbury? *La capitana* of the *Tomás*?"

"That's the boat's name," I said. "I'm just the fool who steers her."

He didn't laugh. Boys that age never do; they haven't yet learned that a joke is how you survive a thing. He held out the envelope like it might bite him, took his coin, and ran back into the wind.

I let it sit on the bar for a while. Telegrams on the Rio Seco come in two flavors: somebody dead, or somebody wanting something. Sometimes both at once. I finished my beer first, because whatever was inside, I wanted to meet it with a clean palate.

*TRIPLE RATES STOP GOVERNMENT CHARTER STOP SURVEY PARTY UPSTREAM STOP REPORT HARBORMASTER OFFICE TOMORROW EIGHT OCLOCK STOP REPLY REQUIRED.*

Triple rates. I read it three times to make sure the arithmetic wasn't lying. On the lower river, a season of hauling wool bales and salted fish and the occasional stubborn tourist doesn't leave much after coal and crew and the endless war against rot. Triple rates meant I could pay off the *Tomás*'s hull debt by Christmas. It meant I could tell Chela goodbye without owing her money. It meant — and this is the part that got me, the part I should have recognized as the hook going in — it meant I could keep moving. Upstream, downstream, anywhere but still.

"Bad news?" Chela asked, wiping the bar in front of me though there was nothing on it.

"Money," I said.

She nodded like she understood. She didn't. Nobody does until they've tried to outrun their own shadow and discovered the sun follows you down every river.

* * *

The harbormaster's office smelled of ink, mildew, and the particular despair of men who work for governments in towns nobody chose on purpose. Puerto Árido earned its name honestly: the wind comes off the plateau carrying grit from a hundred miles inland, and it polishes everything — windows, gravestones, patience — down to the bone.

The harbormaster himself was absent, or pretending to be, so it was a clerk who walked me through to the back room where the survey party had established itself among crates and rolled charts. Five men sat around a trestle table looking like five different opinions about what a gentleman looks like when he's been on a boat too long. And standing apart from them, at the window, was a woman with a leather tube slung across her back and ink stains on two fingers, watching the harbor as if she were already drawing it.

"Captain Thornbury." The man at the head of the table rose. He was long and dry as a fence post, with a mustache that looked recently argued with and a collar that hadn't surrendered to the climate the way the rest of him had. "Calloway-Hurst. Ministry of Public Works. You received our wire."

"I did. I have questions."

"Naturally." He gestured to a chair as though granting me a favor. I stayed standing. There's a kind of man who offers you a seat so you'll feel smaller getting out of it, and Calloway-Hurst had that look about him — the brittleness of porcelain that knows it's been glued once already.

"Triple rates," I said. "For a survey. What are we surveying?"

"The river."

"It's a river. I've seen it. Mostly wet, occasionally not."

A couple of the other men smiled before catching themselves. Calloway-Hurst did not smile. He drew a chart from the stack beside him and unrolled it with the care of a priest handling relics, weighting the corners with inkwells. "The upper reaches. Beyond the last official sounding. The ministry requires accurate measurement of flows, depths, seasonal variation. Water is becoming a matter of national consequence, Captain. Concessions expire. New allocations will be made. Accurate data is—" he paused, choosing the word the way a man chooses which lie to stand on, "—valuable."

"And why me? There are bigger boats on this river. Boats with engines newer than the idea of engines."

"Because," said the woman at the window, without turning around, "the bigger boats draw too much water past the Salta Bend shallows, and every pilot who's taken a government contract upriver in the last ten years has either refused the gorge or wrecked in it. And because your boat is named for Tomás Ferreyra, and everyone on this stretch of water knows what he did, and what you've done since, which is run this river alone, in all seasons, without losing so much as a crate of eggs."

Now she turned around, and I got my first proper look at Elena Vasquez. Dark hair cut short in a way that announced it had been decided rather than neglected. Eyes the brown of river water in October — clear, and moving faster than they appeared. She had the particular stillness of people who watch for a living, and I felt watched the way you feel weather change.

"You know my boat," I said.

"I know boats. It's my trade." She tapped the leather tube on her shoulder. "Maps. Which means knowing what floats where, and why, and who can be trusted to take you there. You came up the lower reach last Tuesday in a southwester that put two trawlers on the mud. I checked."

"You checked."

"I check everything. It's the job." She said it lightly, but underneath the lightness was something load-bearing. A woman who checks everything has usually learned the hard way what happens when nobody does.

Calloway-Hurst cleared his throat, displeased that his meeting had developed a second voice. "Señorita Vasquez is our cartographer. Her enthusiasm for detail is—"

"Professional," she said.

"—thorough," he finished, and the two words sat together in the room like cats in a sack.

I looked at the chart. It was wrong. Not subtly wrong — wrong in the way of a map drawn by someone copying another map, each hand adding its small polite errors, until the river on paper bore the same relation to the real one as a portrait bears to a corpse. The Salta Bend shallows were marked a full kilometer below where they actually lived. The gorge was drawn straight; the gorge is anything but.

"This chart's fiction," I said.

"It is the official chart," Calloway-Hurst said.

"Yes. That's what I said."

One of the five men laughed outright this time — a big, red-faced fellow with hands like hams, who introduced himself as Osgood, the team's engineer, apparently delighted that someone had finally said aloud what the rest of them had been thinking for a week. Beside him, four more gentlemen of varying degrees of dampness: a thin nervous pair named Whitlock and Prue who kept consulting a ledger between them like it was a shared prayer book, a heavy silent man called Brand whose eyes never stopped moving toward the windows, and a young one, barely shaving, whom the others called Denny and who looked at me with the naked hope of someone who has signed on for adventure and begun to suspect the paperwork says otherwise.

"Captain," Calloway-Hurst said, and here his voice changed — dropped its officiousness and showed the wire beneath, the way a chair shows its frame when the upholstery wears through, "we sail in three days. We require a pilot who will not turn back at the posted boundary. The ministry's authorization extends beyond it. There is additional compensation for that portion of the route."

There it was. I'd been waiting for it since the word *upstream*, the way you wait for the third sandbar in a channel you know. Posted boundaries on the Rio Seco belong to the concession companies, and government papers that override them are either very new law or very old lies, and either way the men who enforce those boundaries don't stop to read fine print. They stop to ask questions, with rifles.

"How much additional?" asked the greed in me, in a voice I pretended wasn't mine.

"Enough," said Calloway-Hurst, "that you needn't take another contract for a year."

A year. A year of motion. A year in which the *Tomás* would always be somewhere else, always under way, always with a horizon ahead doing its honest work of standing between me and everything behind. I thought of the promise I'd made over Tomás Ferreyra's grave, or near enough to it — the river keeps its dead, so we made do with a cairn and a bottle — *keep her moving, Noor, keep her moving and nothing catches up.* I'd kept that promise the way some men keep faith: obsessively, joylessly, and without ever once asking whether the god I served had ever actually asked for it.

And I thought of triple rates, and then of triple rates plus enough-for-a-year, and I confess the arithmetic drowned out the alarm bells for a good while. Greed is like that. It doesn't argue with instinct. It just talks louder, and it has better stories.

"I'll want half in advance," I heard myself say. "Paid to Chela at the Fonda del Faro, since I trust her books more than any bank between here and the sea. My crew of three stays on at charter wages. And I decide what the boat carries and where she moors. If I say a reach is closed, we anchor, and no ministry signature moves us an inch further. Agreed?"

Calloway-Hurst's jaw worked. I had the sense of a man recalculating, and not liking the new total. But the clerk of his pride stamped the approval anyway. "Agreed."

He pushed the contract across the table. I read it — all of it, twice, because Elena Vasquez was watching me read it and I found I minded what she'd think — and the clauses were mostly ordinary. Except clause nine, which said the ministry assumed no liability for loss of vessel, cargo, or life above the last surveyed point, and which was written in a hand noticeably different from the rest of the document. Added later. Added by someone in a hurry.

I signed anyway.

I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the only cargo I've never lost: I signed because of the money, and the money stood for motion, and motion stood for the only kind of peace I believed I could afford. The alarm bells were ringing by then. I could hear them fine. I signed the way a man steps off a ledge he's been eyeing all evening — telling himself the fall is really a kind of flying.

Elena Vasquez watched me sign, and when I looked up she was wearing an expression I couldn't file anywhere. Not approval. Not contempt. Something closer to recognition — the look of one gambler watching another place a bet she'd have placed herself, and knowing exactly what it costs.

"Captain Thornbury," she said, as the others began gathering charts and the room dissolved into the organized chaos of departure, "a question, professional to professional."

"Cartographer to pilot. Ask."

"When you looked at that chart — the official one — you knew it was wrong immediately. How?"

"Because I've run that water since I was nine years old, and the river doesn't care what the ministry believes about it."

"Mm." She slung the leather tube higher on her shoulder. "And when a map and a river disagree, which one do you steer by?"

"The river. Always the river."

Something in her face settled, like a compass needle finding north after being jostled. "Good," she said. "Then we'll get along. The men steer by the paper. I've watched them do it for a week now, and it makes me nervous in a way I can't yet explain."

"You'll explain it," I said. "On this river, everybody explains themselves eventually. The water just speeds up the process."

That earned me the first almost-smile, gone as quick as a fish rising. Then she was gone through the door after her colleagues, and I stood alone in the harbormaster's back room with the smell of ink and the sound of wind worrying the shutters, and I asked myself the question I should have asked an hour earlier:

What kind of government survey needs a pilot willing to cross a posted boundary, assumes no liability for dying, and hires its maps drawn by the only person in the room who seemed to wish the whole enterprise would sink?

The answer arrived on schedule, the way answers do — too late to be useful, early enough to be dreaded.

But triple rates, I told myself, walking out into the grit-bright afternoon. Triple rates and a year of never stopping.

Chela, when I told her, poured me a beer on the house and studied me over the foam with those eyes that miss nothing on her stretch of bar the way I miss nothing on my stretch of river. "You've got the look," she said.

"What look?"

"The look of a man who's just sold something he doesn't know he owns yet."

I drank the beer. Outside, the harbor light swung its slow arc, and far upstream, past the shallows and the gorge and the posted signs, the river waited — patient as debt, and just as certain to collect.
