## 4. Upstream of Maps

There is a moment every river pilot knows, though none of us admits it in company: the moment when the chart in your hand stops being a promise and becomes a rumor.

For the Rio Seco, that moment came at noon on the second day, at a bend marked on my chart with a small, confident ink arrow and the words *last surveyed, 1911*. The surveyor who drew it had been dead longer than I'd been alive, and whatever he'd seen from his mule had been rearranged by ninety years of wind and water since. Beyond that bend, the paper went blank — not empty exactly, but blank the way a room is blank when someone has carefully removed all the furniture.

I stood at the wheel with the chart folded back to its edge and felt the old familiar itch between my shoulder blades. Most people call it fear. I'd learned to call it attention.

"You're smiling," Elena Vasquez said from beside me. She had taken up a station at the rail that morning as if she'd been assigned to it, notebook open, pencil moving. "Why are you smiling? We've just run out of map."

"I'm not smiling."

"Your mouth is doing a thing."

"That's just how my face sits."

"It wasn't sitting that way an hour ago." She tapped her pencil against the rail. "In Puerto Árido you looked like a man being paid to do something unpleasant. Now you look like a man getting away with something."

I adjusted the wheel a degree for no reason the river required. "The river's different up here," I said, which was true, and which was also a way of not answering. "Downstream it's wide and lazy and it lies to you about how deep it is. Up here it's narrow and honest and it will kill you quicker. I prefer honest things."

"Is that why you live alone on a boat?"

"Miss Vasquez—"

"Elena."

"—Elena. Is there a reason you ask questions like a customs officer?"

She considered this with what I can only describe as professional pleasure. "Yes," she said, and went back to her notebook.

* * *

The *Mariana Q* — Tomás's boat, my boat now, though some mornings I still reached for the rail expecting to find his hand already there — was built for water like this. Shallow draft, steel-shod hull, a winch on the bow that could drag us over a sandbar like a stubborn mule over a fence. I'd need all of it. By mid-afternoon the channel had begun to misbehave.

A river is never the same river twice. That's the first thing they teach you and the last thing anyone believes. Downstream, where the Rio Seco spread out and got bored, the changes were slow — a bank cut here, a bar grown there, nothing that would surprise a pilot who'd run the reach within the season. But upstream of the last survey, the river changed its mind overnight. Literally overnight. A sandbar that blocked the channel at dusk could be gone by dawn, moved fifty meters downstream by wind and current, sitting in your path like a cat that has decided your chair is now its chair.

"Explain to me again," said Mr. Calloway-Hurst, appearing at the wheelhouse door with his collar buttoned to the top despite the heat, "how one navigates a river that refuses to hold still."

"One doesn't navigate it," I said. "One negotiates. There's a difference. Navigation assumes the other party has agreed to something."

He did not appear to find this comforting. He was a thin man, Calloway-Hurst, the kind of thin that comes from tension rather than fasting, and he had spent the entire voyage so far in a state of low-grade offense at the landscape — the dust, the thorn scrub, the way the light came off the water flat and white as tin. His four surveyors were worse. They treated the river like a delay between them and their real work, complaining about the food, the flies, the smell of the engine. Only Elena seemed to understand that the river *was* the work, that everything upstream of the maps began right here, in the negotiation.

"Soundings," I called down to Bruno, who leaned over the bow rail with the lead line, swinging it in slow arcs. Bruno was a big, quiet man from up-country who sang to the engine when he thought no one could hear, and who had once told me, with complete sincerity, that the river talked back if you listened long enough. I didn't disbelieve him. I'd heard it myself, some nights.

"Two fathoms!" Bruno called. "One and a half! One!"

"Starboard a shade." I eased the wheel. The bottom rose to meet us, pale gold through the brown water, close enough to count the ripples the current wrote across it.

"One fathom!" A pause. "Captain — sand ahead. Big one. It's moved."

Of course it had moved. I'd anchored below that bar the night before with a hundred meters of clear water showing on the lead. Now the bar sat squarely in the channel like a bill presented without warning.

"All right," I said. "Everyone off the bow. Pilar, get the kedge ready."

Pilar — sixteen, all elbows and opinions, the youngest deckhand I'd ever hired and possibly the loudest — came scrambling aft with the kedge anchor over one shoulder like a man carrying a drunk friend home. "It's the same bar from yesterday, Captain. I'd swear it's the same bar. It just walked."

"Bars don't walk."

"This one does. I watched it."

"You watched the current move it, which is worse, because the current doesn't leave footprints." I brought the *Mariana Q* head-to-current and held her there, engine turning just enough to keep her nose upstream, while Pilar and Bruno rowed the kedge out in the dinghy and set it above the bar. Then the winch took up the strain, cable singing, and we hauled ourselves over the sand the way you'd haul a heavy trunk up a staircase — inch by inch, the hull grinding, the whole boat shuddering like a dog shaking off water.

Calloway-Hurst watched this operation from the rail with his hands clasped behind his back. "This is how you travel?" he asked. "By dragging yourselves?"

"It's how you travel when the river won't carry you," I said. "You'll get used to it. Everyone does. Eventually they even start to like it, though they're usually ashamed to admit it."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh in a man with better circulation.

* * *

We camped that night on a gravel shelf beneath a bluff the color of dried blood, moored stern-first with two anchors out because I trusted nothing upstream of the maps, including my own habits. The crew cooked; the surveyors ate; the fire cracked and spat. And it was there, while Bruno was telling Pilar a story involving a condor and a wedding party — I caught only fragments, but the condor appeared to have opinions about the bride — that I saw Calloway-Hurst take out his notebook.

He thought he was unobserved. He had positioned himself at the far end of the gravel shelf with a lantern, and he'd turned his body into the dark the way men do when they want privacy, which is to say: in the single most conspicuous manner available. I have spent thirty years reading water and twenty of those also reading men, and the second skill is easier, because men always think they're being subtle.

He opened the notebook. He read something. His shoulders went stiff — I saw it even at forty meters, the lantern light catching the line of him. Then he tore out a page, held it to the flame of the lantern, and burned it.

Not crumpled and discarded. Burned, deliberately, holding the corner until the fire climbed to his fingers, then dropping the last scrap onto the gravel and grinding it under his heel. Twice, to be sure.

Elena appeared at my elbow. She did this often — arrived silently at precisely the moment I'd concluded I was alone — and I was beginning to suspect she did it on purpose, the way cats sit on the one book you're reading.

"You saw," she said quietly.

"I saw a man burn his own notes."

"People don't usually burn their own notes unless someone else wrote them."

I glanced at her. "That's a very specific theory."

"I photograph manifests for a living. Well — I map coastlines for a living. But I read documents the way you read water." She nodded toward the far end of the shelf, where Calloway-Hurst had closed the notebook and was now staring into the fire with the expression of a man rehearsing an apology he hadn't been asked to give yet. "He's frightened, Captain. Not of the river. Of the pages."

"Fear keeps a man honest."

"Fear keeps a man quiet. Those aren't the same thing." She hugged her arms against the evening chill, though the night was barely cool. "You knew him before this voyage?"

"No. First time."

"But you took the charter anyway. Triple pay, five strangers, and a route with no charts." She wasn't accusing me. Her voice had something almost gentle in it, which was somehow more uncomfortable than accusation. "You don't strike me as a gambler."

"The money was good."

"The money is never the whole answer with people like you."

"And what kind of person am I, Miss Vasquez?"

"Elena."

"Elena."

She looked at the fire for a while. Somewhere up the bluff, a fox screamed once and went silent. "Someone who's running," she said finally. "But running *toward* something, which is rarer than you'd think. Most runners are only running away." She turned and left before I could decide whether to be insulted, which I suspected was also on purpose.

* * *

The third day, the river turned strange.

I want to be careful with that word, because pilots use it loosely — strange water, strange weather, strange luck — the way sailors use *story* to mean anything from an anecdote to a lie. But this was strangeness in the older sense. The sense that the place itself had noticed you.

It began with the sound. Rivers make noise, obviously — riffles, rapids, the grind of sediment — and a pilot learns to read that noise the way a doctor reads a heartbeat. Every sound means something specific. But that morning, rounding a bluff of pink granite, we heard a sound that meant nothing. A whistling, high and thin, rising and falling with no relation to the current or the wind. It came from the rocks themselves — hollow stones along the waterline, worn through by centuries of sand, breathing when the breeze crossed them. The locals downstream had a name for reaches like this. I'd always assumed the name was superstition.

By noon the superstition was harder to dismiss. The channel narrowed until the bluffs pressed close enough that Bruno could have thrown a stone across. The water ran green instead of brown, cold and glassy, and the soundings stopped making sense — fifteen feet, then three, then twelve again, as if the river couldn't decide how deep it wanted to be. Twice we passed side channels that my instinct swore should flow into the main stem and instead simply... ended, sinking into gravel with no fanfare, water going into the earth like coin into a pocket.

"Where does it go?" Pilar asked, watching a whole arm of the river vanish into the bank.

"Underground," I said. "Karst. The rock's full of tunnels. Some of this water probably comes back out fifty miles from here, or doesn't come back at all."

"That's not right," Pilar said, with the moral seriousness of the young. "Water shouldn't just disappear."

"Tell it that."

But I confess the sight bothered me too, and I've never fully sorted out why. Perhaps because a disappearing river is a broken promise. Perhaps because I recognized something in it.

Calloway-Hurst stood at the rail through all of it, and I noticed — because noticing is my trade — that he had stopped taking readings. Two days of diligent instrument work, barometers and sextant and the little aneroid he consulted like a priest with his breviary, and now the instruments stayed in their case. He watched the banks instead. He watched the water. And once, when the whistling stones sang particularly clearly, I saw his hand go to his breast pocket, to the notebook, and then come away empty, as if he'd remembered there was nothing in it worth saving.

* * *

That evening we tied up below a gorge whose walls went up and up until the stars sat in a slot above us like coins at the bottom of a well. Supper was beans and goat and Bruno's story about the condor, reprised at Pilar's request with new details that improved it considerably — the condor, it emerged, had attended the wedding uninvited and had judged the cake harshly. Even two of the surveyors laughed. Calloway-Hurst did not. He ate standing up, quickly, and then excused himself to his tent, and the lantern inside it burned long after the rest of the camp slept.

I took the middle watch, as I always did — midnight to four, the hours when the river does its private thinking. Elena came out around one, wrapped in a blanket, and sat on the hatch cover near the wheel without asking permission, which by then I'd stopped minding.

"Third night he's burned daylight over that notebook," she said.

"You've been counting."

"I count everything. It's the job." She pulled the blanket tighter. "Tomorrow we reach Estancia Salada. The supply stop. He told the team it's a fueling layover, half a day, in and out. But I checked the manifest. We're taking on enough stores for six more weeks."

"Maybe he likes beans."

"Nobody likes beans that much, Captain."

"No," I admitted. "Nobody does." I let the wheel rest in its neutral line and watched the black water slide past, carrying its cargo of stars. "What is it you think he's afraid of, Elena? Truly."

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "I think he came up this river knowing something he shouldn't know. And I think every mile takes him further from the people who told him to forget it, and closer to the thing itself. And I think a man in that position starts doing arithmetic." She looked up at the slot of sky. "Forty days until the concession expires. That's what the port agent said, isn't it? Forty days."

"It's what everyone says. The whole coast is counting."

"Then we're all doing arithmetic." She stood, gathered her blanket, and paused at the wheelhouse door. "Goodnight, Captain. Wake me if the river says anything interesting."

"It rarely talks to me before dawn."

"It talked to Bruno."

"Bruno listens better than I do."

She smiled — I caught it in the lamplight, brief and real — and went below, and I stood my watch alone with the whistling stones singing somewhere upstream in the dark, and the arithmetic ticking in all our heads like water into a pocket.

At dawn we cast off for Estancia Salada, and the first thing the morning gave us was a sandbar where the chart promised deep water — and the second thing, half-hidden in the reeds at the bar's upstream tip, was a rowboat. Old, painted government grey, registration number scraped away, sitting empty.

Nobody in the camp said anything about it. But I noticed that Calloway-Hurst looked at it for a long time, and that afterward he burned another page.
