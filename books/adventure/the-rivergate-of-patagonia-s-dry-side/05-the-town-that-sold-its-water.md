## 5. The Town That Sold Its Water

The town announced itself by smell before it announced itself by sight.

Elena was on deck with her coffee, sketching the river's bend in her field book, when the wind shifted and brought her the scent of lanolin and dust and old wood smoke — the particular perfume of a sheep town that had stopped pretending it was going anywhere. She looked up from the page. There it was: a scatter of tin roofs and whitewashed walls on the western bank, a church with a bell tower leaning like a man listening hard, and behind it all, the brown hills folding away toward the gorge they'd been told not to think about.

"Estancia Salada," Noor said, appearing at the rail beside her. Elena was beginning to suspect the captain could materialize at will, like weather.

"I thought salt was a coastal thing."

"Everything here is a coastal thing, if you go back far enough. The whole valley was a seabed once. The town got its name from the wells — the water came up tasting of it. Not enough to kill you. Just enough to remind you the land keeps accounts." Noor's eyes moved over the town with an expression Elena hadn't learned to read yet. "We'll take on water and firewood. Half a day."

"Half a day for a town that size?"

"Half a day because Señora Ibarra will want to talk, and it's rude to rush her." Noor said it lightly, but there was something underneath it — the tone of a person paying a debt they didn't discuss. "She's the one who taught me the lower river, years ago. Before your Mr. Calloway-Hurst's employers arrived."

Elena noticed that. *Your* Mr. Calloway-Hurst. She filed it away with the other things she'd been filing away since Puerto Árido — the crates, the manifests, the drilling equipment that no honest survey required, the photographs sleeping in the false bottom of her instrument case like a loaded gun. She was getting quite good at filing things. It was, she reflected, what she'd been trained for: her whole life had been an exercise in putting what she actually wanted into a drawer and closing it quietly.

The boat came alongside a stone jetty that had been handsome once. Two men unloaded crates while Pilar — the youngest of the deckhands, all elbows and earnestness — went off with the water barrels, walking with the exaggerated care of a boy carrying something heavier than water.

Elena took her sketchbook and did what she always did in new places: she walked.

* * *

Estancia Salada had perhaps sixty residents left, and Elena suspected she saw a third of them in her first ten minutes, because a stranger walking through town was clearly the day's entertainment. They watched from doorways with the polite, unblinking patience of herons. A dog followed her for two blocks, lost interest, and was replaced by a different dog with identical opinions.

The town was clean. That was what struck her first — not picturesque decay but a fierce, stubborn tidiness, streets swept, fences mended with wire and willow and what looked like the remains of a wagon. These were people who had decided that if they were going to be poor, they would be poor with dignity, and heaven help anyone who suggested otherwise.

The second thing she noticed was the water.

Or rather, the absence of it. In every yard there was a cistern, and every cistern had a lock. At the town's center, where a fountain stood — a lovely old thing, a stone maiden pouring a stone jug into a stone basin — the basin was dry, and someone had planted geraniums in it. They were thriving. Elena stopped to sketch them, because there was something in the image that her hand understood before she did: a fountain growing flowers because it had forgotten how to be a fountain, and making a better job of it than anyone expected.

"You're the cartographer."

Elena turned. An old woman stood behind her, small and upright as a fence post, wearing black from collar to boot with the authority of a uniform. Her hair was white and pinned with what appeared to be a fishhook. Her eyes were the pale grey of the sky over the gorge, and they went through Elena the way a sounding line goes through water.

"Señora Ibarra," Elena said. "The captain mentioned you."

"The captain mentions me the way a guilty man mentions a priest." She said it without malice, and Elena found herself smiling before she could decide whether it was permitted. "You draw the geraniums. Good. Everyone photographs them. Photographs lie — they make dying look pretty. Drawing tells the truth, if your hand is honest. Is your hand honest?"

"I'm trying to teach it to be."

The old woman considered this for a long moment, then nodded once, as if a contract had been signed. "Come. You'll want to see the wells. Everyone wants to see the wells, once I tell them about the wells. It's the only entertainment this town produces now, and I don't charge for it, which is why the town disapproves of me."

She set off at a pace that made Elena's longer legs work to keep up.

* * *

The wells were at the edge of town, three of them in a row behind a low stone wall, each with its windmill — two of them motionless, their blades feathered and still, and the third turning with a slow, complaining creak, like a door in a house where nobody lives.

"Salada had water," Señora Ibarra said, standing at the wall with her hands folded. "Not sweet water. Salt water, but water. Enough for sheep, enough for gardens, enough for a fountain with a maiden in it who has better things to do now. Then, eleven years ago, the concession company came up the valley. Señor — what was his name — Reyes, a young man then, very polite, very clean boots. He had papers. He had a survey. He said the aquifer under our land was being 'inefficiently exploited,' which I believe is a legal way of saying we were drinking it wrong."

She said *Reyes* the way you'd say the name of a snake you'd once stepped on and were careful never to step on again. Elena kept her face still and her hand moving, sketching the dead windmills, and waited.

"They drilled," the old woman went on. "Not here — upriver, in the high country where nobody goes, where the river is born. They put their straws into the aquifer and they drank first. And the wells here—" she gestured at the motionless mills "—went down. First one, then the other. The third holds on. It gives us enough, if we're careful, for drinking and a little washing. The sheep go to the river, when the river has water in it. Which is also less than it was. But you'd know that. You've been reading the gauges on the boat. I can see it in your face — the face of a person doing arithmetic she doesn't like."

Elena looked up from the page. "The river's dropped. Two centimeters a week since Puerto Árido, in a season when it should be rising."

"And you've been told you're surveying it." Señora Ibarra smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Child, I've watched boats come up this river for sixty years. I know a survey crew the way I know a ewe from a ram. Your five gentlemen measure nothing. They ride. They write in little books. They look upstream the way a man looks at a woman he intends to marry for her money. And they brought drilling crates, which I'm told are still in your hold, because the boy Pilar talks, and I listen."

The wind creaked through the last turning mill. Elena felt the ground shift under her — not literally, though she'd learned on this river not to rule that out, but in the way the ground of a conversation shifts when someone shows you they've known everything all along.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked.

"Because you draw instead of photograph." The old woman turned and looked upriver, toward the brown hills and the hidden gorge beyond them. "And because you'll go up there eventually. Everyone does. The river pulls. And when you go, I'd rather you went knowing something."

* * *

She took Elena back to her house, a low stone place with a garden that was less a garden than a manifesto — everything in it edible or medicinal, arranged with the ferocious efficiency of a woman who had decided that if the land wouldn't provide, she would provide for herself out of spite. Inside, the room smelled of wool and dried herbs and lamp oil. On the wall hung a framed photograph of a young couple beside a full fountain, the stone maiden pouring water that caught the light.

"My husband," Señora Ibarra said, following Elena's eyes. "Dead thirty years. He never saw the wells fail. He was a lucky man and didn't know it, which is the definition of a lucky man."

She went to a chest in the corner, unlocked it with a key from around her neck, and drew out a roll of oilcloth. When she unrolled it on the table, Elena caught her breath.

It was a map. Hand-drawn, ink on butcher's paper, the work of years by the look of it — annotated, corrected, annotated again, the margins crowded with notes in a small fierce hand. It showed the river from Estancia Salada to its source, every bend, every rapid, every sandbar with dates beside them showing how the bars moved. And above the point where every official chart Elena had ever seen simply stopped — the blank space the surveyors called, with a certain nervous poetry, *the uncharted reach* — the old woman's ink went on, confident and detailed, into a gorge marked with names like *Grito de Piedra* and *El Silbador*, and past it, at the top of the sheet, a small blue shape with a careful label: *La Naciente*.

The Source.

Elena had seen the concession maps in Calloway-Hurst's cabin, the ones he thought she hadn't seen. The blank space on those charts had been circled in pencil. Somebody wanted very much to know what was inside it.

"Where did you get this?"

"I made it. My husband and I, over forty years. He walked the ridges; I took the boat when the river let me. The river doesn't always let you." She tapped the gorge. "The Silbador — the whistling stones — that passage has eaten three boats in my lifetime, and one of them was carrying people who deserved it. And here—" her finger came down on a reach above the gorge, drawn in a different ink, darker, as if traced from memory or confession rather than observation "—is where the charts stop and the company's signs begin. *Prohibido el paso*. They posted it eleven years ago. Before that, my family grazed sheep up there every summer. There is a lake up there. A spring-fed lake, cold as a widow's heart, that has never once been dry. Not in drought. Not ever. Do you understand what I'm telling you, child?"

Elena understood. In a country where every other water source was failing or had failed — where the wells were locked and the fountain grew geraniums — a never-dry lake at the head of the river was not geography. It was a fortune. It was the only fortune left in the valley, and the company had posted signs around it and gone very quiet.

"The permits," Elena said carefully. "The concession on the middle river. I've heard they expire soon."

"Forty days, more or less. And everyone with paper and a gun is suddenly interested in the upper gorge, because whoever holds the source when the permits fall holds everything downstream. Every estancia. Every town. Every well." Señora Ibarra rolled the map with great care, tied it, and put it in Elena's hands. "So. You will take it."

"Señora, I can't—"

"You can and you will, because I'm old and stubborn and you'll hurt my feelings, and my feelings have survived worse than you." She pressed the roll into Elena's grip and closed the younger woman's fingers over it with hands that were dry and strong as tree roots. "Your captain knows this river's moods but not its memory. You, I think, are in the business of memory. Draw it. Write it. Put it somewhere the company can't burn."

Elena looked down at the map in her hands, and felt the weight of it settle into the same place the photographs lived — that quiet, accumulating ledger she'd been keeping since Puerto Árido, the one that was getting harder and harder to call anything but evidence.

"Señora," she said. "Why did the wells really fail? The company drilled upstream, yes, but—"

"Child." The old woman looked at her, and for a moment the fence-post uprightness bent, and Elena saw underneath it something tired and old and frightened, something that had been waiting eleven years for someone to ask. "They didn't drill the aquifer. They *dammed* it. Up at the source, in the dark, where no survey would ever look, because every survey is paid to look elsewhere. The river didn't dry up on its own. It was stolen. And everyone downstream has been drinking the company's leftovers and calling it weather." She straightened again, and the fear went back into its drawer, and the iron came back into her spine. "One more thing, and then you'll go, because your captain will be pacing the jetty by now and Noor paces like a cat in a sack. When you go up the gorge — and you will go — remember this."

She took Elena's chin in her root-dry hand, gently, the way you'd steady a compass.

"The river remembers who drinks first. It has a long memory and it keeps its own accounts, and it always, always settles them. The company thinks it bought the river with papers. Papers burn. The river is patient. Make sure, when the settling comes, that you are standing where the river can see whose side you were on."

* * *

Elena walked back to the jetty with the map against her ribs and the old woman's words walking beside her like a fourth passenger. The town had gone about its evening; smoke stood up straight from the chimneys in the windless air, and somewhere a woman was singing to a child in a voice worn smooth as river stone. It was, Elena thought, a beautiful place. It was a place that had been robbed so politely, so gradually, with such excellent paperwork, that most of the people in it probably still believed they were simply unlucky.

That was the part that frightened her. Not the enforcers, not the gorge, not the blank space on the charts. The part that frightened her was how easy it was — how a whole valley could be drained while everyone watched, as long as the thieves arrived with clipboards and called it a survey.

Noor was on the jetty, and — as predicted — pacing.

"You met her," the captain said. It wasn't a question.

"She gave me a map."

Noor stopped pacing. Something moved behind those river-colored eyes — not surprise exactly; more like the look of a person watching a door they'd assumed was locked swing open on its own. "She gave you the map. The whole map?"

"The whole map. Including the part that isn't on anyone's charts."

Noor was quiet for a moment. Down the deck, Calloway-Hurst emerged from the cabin, saw the oilcloth roll under Elena's arm, and went very slightly grey before composing his face into its usual brittle courtesy. He asked no questions. That, Elena thought, was its own kind of answer.

"We moor tonight below the gorge," Noor said at last, turning to give the order to cast off. The boat's engine woke with its familiar asthmatic cough. "It's a bad stretch ahead. Best done in daylight, and we'll want an early start."

Elena stood at the stern rail as Estancia Salada fell away behind them — the leaning bell tower, the geraniums in the dry fountain, the three windmills, one still turning its slow complaint against the sky. She thought about the old woman's hand closing over hers. *The river remembers who drinks first.*

Behind them, the last light caught the town and made it briefly golden, a place that had sold its water without ever seeing the bill.

Ahead, the hills drew together, and the river narrowed, and the gorge opened its dark mouth to take them in.
