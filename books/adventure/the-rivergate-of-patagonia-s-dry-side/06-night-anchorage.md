## 6. Night Anchorage

The gorge announced itself before we saw it. You could hear it the way you hear a stranger's argument through a hotel wall — a low, continuous grumbling somewhere ahead in the dark, getting louder the way arguments do when they decide to involve you.

I brought the *Mariposa* in below the last bend and put her against the eastern bank where an old oxbow had cut itself a quiet pool, deep enough to float us and sheltered enough that the wind coming down off the plateau couldn't reach her hull. Twenty-two years on this river and I could find water like this with my eyes shut. Sometimes I suspected I did.

"Is it always like this?" Elena stood at the rail beside me, her hands wrapped around a tin mug of coffee she hadn't drunk. She'd been holding that mug for an hour. It was a prop, and I knew props — I'd been carrying one of my own for two decades.

"Like what?"

"Like the river is talking about you behind your back."

I laughed before I could stop myself. It came out rough from disuse, like an engine turning over after a winter.

"That's not the river," I said. "That's the gorge. The river's just what falls into it."

She looked at me over the rim of the mug. Elena Vasquez had a way of looking at things that made them feel inventoried. Not unkindly — more like she was adding them to some private chart, drawing in coastlines other people didn't bother to notice. I'd been watched by surveyors for three days now, and none of them looked at anything the way she did.

"You know these waters well," she said. It wasn't flattery. It was data collection.

"Well enough to be afraid of them."

"And are you?"

We were moored below the Gorge of Whistling Stones, though nobody aboard called it that yet because nobody aboard had heard it whistle. The men were ashore setting their camp — Calloway-Hurst pacing out the perimeter with his little notebook, the others pitching tents with the cheerful incompetence of gentlemen who'd been told camping would be "character-building." Pilar was below decks oiling something that didn't need oiling, which was his way of being near a conversation without admitting he wanted in on it.

"No," I said, and then, because lying to her felt suddenly like wasting good rope: "Yes. Both. Fear's just respect that's done its homework."

* * *

Dinner was mutton, hard bread, and Calloway-Hurst explaining to no one in particular that the upper reaches of the Rio Seco had been "inadequately surveyed by previous expeditions, whose methods I will charitably describe as enthusiastic." He said it the way a man says something he's rehearsed. The other four laughed at the right places. They always laughed at the right places. I'd started keeping count.

Elena ate quickly and quietly, the way people eat when eating is fuel rather than pleasure, and afterward she climbed back up to the rail with her mug and her notebook and stood looking upriver at the black wall of the gorge until the firelight got too sociable for whatever mood she was carrying.

I gave it twenty minutes. Then I told Pilar I was going to check the anchor lines, which was true in the sense that all my best lies are true — I did check them, eventually, but first I went and stood next to her.

"The wind drops after midnight," I said. "Then the gorge starts whistling. Some nights it sounds like singing. Some nights like a man calling for help. Nobody agrees which is worse."

"Which do you think?"

"I think it doesn't matter what it sounds like. It matters that you don't answer it."

She turned that over. Above us the sky was doing its Patagonian trick — more stars than darkness, as if someone had spilled the whole inventory. Down south there's so little between you and everything else that the stars stop being decoration and become geography.

"Tomás answered it once," I said.

I hadn't planned to say it. That's the thing about night anchorages — the river takes the day's noise away and leaves you alone with the cargo you've been hauling underneath everything else. I'd carried Tomás for eleven years, and I'd never once set him down in front of another living soul. But Elena was standing there with her notebook closed, and the gorge was muttering, and my mouth simply went ahead without filing the proper paperwork.

"Who was Tomás?" she asked. No surprise in her voice. As if she'd known all along that everyone on this river was carrying somebody.

"My deckhand. My friend." I said *friend* the way you say a word you've worn smooth from handling. "He crewed with me six years. Knew the Seco better than I did, if I'm honest. He used to say the river kept a ledger, and one day it would call in our accounts, and he only hoped he'd have paid enough by then to cover me."

"What happened?"

"It was a flood year. April. We were running light down through the middle reaches, and a side channel took us wrong — the sandbars had shifted in the night, the way they do, and I misread it. I've never misread it since." I looked at my hands. Pilots' hands. They hadn't done anything wrong that night except exist on the wrong boat. "We hit a snag. Boat started shipping water fast. I went for the pump. Tomás went for me."

"He pushed you clear."

"He held me under the gunwale while the water took the deck out from under us. Kept me down so the current couldn't have me. And when I came up, he was gone." The gorge muttered. Somewhere above, a stone let go and clicked down the rock face like a closing door. "They found him three days later at the Estancia Salada bend, forty miles down. He looked peaceful. That's the part I can't forgive — that the river can take a man and make it look gentle."

Elena said nothing for a long time. She didn't fill the silence with comfort, which is the mistake most people make, offering words like sandbags against a flood that's already past. She just stood there and let the silence be big enough to hold him.

"So the promise," she said finally.

"You know about the promise?"

"Pilar talks. When you're asleep."

Of course he did. Eleven years old and already fluent in the language of watching adults.

"I promised him I'd keep the boat moving," I said. "His boat, after — he'd bought half of her, saved up for years. I promised I'd never let her sit still long enough for the guilt to catch up. Because guilt moves slow, see. Guilt walks. And as long as the boat runs faster than guilt walks, I'm—" I stopped. Heard how it sounded. "It's not a philosophy. It's a superstition with a schedule."

"A week in any port," Elena said softly. "Never longer than a week. Pilar mentioned that too."

"Eight days, once. In Puerto Árido. Worst eight days of my life."

"Why?"

"Because on the eighth day I caught myself thinking about staying. And staying means the ledger comes due." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "You wanted to know if I'm afraid of the gorge. I'm afraid of every place the boat stops. Every town is just a sandbar that doesn't shift."

* * *

She was quiet for a while, and I thought that was the end of it, and I was already composing my retreat — check the lines, Noor, you came up here to check the lines — when she spoke again.

"My family has arranged a marriage for me."

I confess I turned around fully. Of all the cargo I expected her to unload tonight, that crate wasn't even on the manifest.

"They've been arranging it since I was sixteen," she went on, in the same level voice she used for coordinates. "A cousin of my mother's family. A man who owns land I've never seen and opinions I've heard plenty of. Every year the arrangement gets more... load-bearing. More relatives standing on it. And every year I find another expedition, another contract, another reason to be exactly here instead of exactly there." She looked up at the stars. "I took this job because Calloway-Hurst's telegram arrived the same week as my mother's letter. One said *come home*. The other said *the far end of the map*. It wasn't a difficult choice. It was only a difficult week."

"Elena—"

"Don't." She smiled, small and sharp. "Don't tell me I should choose for myself. I know. I've known since I was sixteen. Knowing and choosing turn out to be different rivers entirely. My duty isn't a cage, Captain. It's a current. And I have spent my whole life swimming in it pretending I'm steering."

The gorge whistled then — low, rising, a note that bent in the middle like a question. We both listened. Neither of us answered it.

"Tomás would have liked you," I said.

"Why?"

"Because he also talked about currents like they were people. And because he never once asked me why I couldn't stay still. He just made sure the boat was ready whenever I needed to run." I looked at her. She was lit half by starlight and half by the distant fire, and I noticed — not for the first time, but for the first time *admittedly* — that noticing her had become a habit, and habits on a boat are dangerous things. "And because he'd have taken one look at your situation and said, *that woman's rowing upstream, and she'll make it.*"

"Is that what I'm doing?"

"Isn't it? A marriage arranged by duty, and you sign onto a government charter to the one stretch of river no chart admits exists. That's not downstream behavior, Señora Vasquez."

"*Señorita,*" she corrected, "and it's *cartographer*, not *woman fleeing a wedding*, thank you very much." But she was smiling when she said it, and the smile stayed longer than smiles usually do on her face, like weather that had decided to settle in.

* * *

We stood there until the fire ashore burned down to orange eyes and the cold came up off the water honest and clean. At some point her shoulder found the rail near mine. Not touching. Adjacent. On a boat, adjacent is a country all its own, with borders you cross at your peril.

"I should check the lines," I said, for the third time that night, meaning nothing of the kind.

"You checked them an hour ago. I watched you."

"You watch everything."

"It's my job." She closed her notebook, which she'd opened at some point without writing anything in it — the first blank page I'd ever seen her keep. "Besides, someone on this boat has to keep track of the captain. Pilar says you sleepwalk toward the engine room."

"Pilar says a great many things for a boy who still thinks salt pork is a food group."

"He also says you talk in your sleep. Names, mostly."

The cold moved through me then, quick and tidal. "What names?"

"Tomás. Mostly Tomás." She said it gently. "And sometimes — I don't know how to translate it. *Todavía no.* *Not yet.* Like you're answering someone."

Above us the gorge sang its bent note, and the stars wheeled their slow inventory, and eleven years of running caught up with me all at once, right there at the rail, because guilt doesn't walk after all. Guilt rides. Guilt rides the current, patient as water, and it had followed me two thousand miles to this pool below the stones, and it had sent ahead a cartographer with kind eyes to hold the lantern while it arrived.

But it didn't feel like drowning. That was the strange thing. It felt like ballast — weight settling low in the hull where weight belongs, making the boat steadier.

"Not yet," I said aloud, to whoever was listening. "But soon."

Elena didn't ask what I meant. She just stood there, adjacent, while the gorge whistled and neither of us answered it.

We turned in late. I slept without dreaming, or dreamed without names, and woke before dawn to Pilar shaking my shoulder with a face gone white as river fog.

"Captain," he whispered. "Wake up. The camp — Captain, the tents are empty."
