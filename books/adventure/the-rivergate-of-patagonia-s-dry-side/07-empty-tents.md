## 7. Empty Tents

I have a rule about dawn on the Rio Seco: you don't trust it. Dawn down there is a con artist. It comes in pink and gentle over the eastern mesa, promising you a soft day, and by nine o'clock it has stripped the paint off your hands and filled your boots with sand. I've been conned by that dawn more times than I can count, and still every morning I fall for it a little.

That morning I fell for it completely.

I was up before the kettle, as always, standing at the rail of the *Mariposa* with my coffee going cold in my hand because I like the first cup better as an idea than as a beverage. The river ran low and glassy past the mooring, the color of old pewter, and the gorge above us was still holding its night shadow like a man refusing to open his hand. Somewhere upstream a bird was making a sound like a hinge that needed oiling. Everything was exactly as it should be.

Except it wasn't. It took me a while to notice what was wrong, because what was wrong was an absence, and absences are slow to announce themselves. A riverboat at anchor makes noise — lines creaking, water slapping the hull, Bruno snoring through the deckhouse wall like a man being paid by the decibel. And camp, on the bank, made its own noises too: the surveyors' tent flaps, their little spirit stove, Mr. Okafor's astonishing singing, which he did while shaving and which sounded like a donkey being introduced to religion for the first time.

None of it. Silence from the tents. Not even the stove.

I told myself they'd gone for an early walk. Survey men do that; they get up at ungodly hours to poke at rocks and argue about contour lines. I finished my coffee, boiled water for the second cup — the one I actually drink — and only then, when the light had come up enough to show me details, did I walk down the gangplank and across the shingle toward the camp.

The tents were all standing. That was my first wrong note. Four tents, pitched in a neat row the way Calloway-Hurst liked them, and every one of them shut up tight as a bank vault. But the fire pit was cold. Not banked — *cold*, the ash gray and dead, no warmth in it at all if you held your palm over it, and I did. Nobody had fed that fire since midnight at the latest. Four grown men do not skip their morning coffee on the dry side of Patagonia. I've known men who would trade their wives' portraits for a cup at dawn.

"Calloway-Hurst," I called out, because his tent was the fifth one, set slightly apart the way leaders always set themselves apart, close enough to supervise and far enough not to smell the men. "Your boys are playing games."

Nothing.

I stood there with my heart doing something quiet and unpleasant, and then I went to the nearest tent and put my hand on the flap.

Inside: bedrolls folded. Boots paired beside them, laces tucked in. Kit bags squared away. A tin plate washed and stacked. It looked less like a camp and more like a shop window — everything arranged, everything *displayed*. Men who walk out at night to relieve themselves or chase a stray mule do not fold their bedrolls first. Men who are taken in their sleep do not fold them either.

Men who expect never to come back might.

I checked the other three. Same story, same terrible tidiness. In the third tent I found Mr. Okafor's razor laid out on its wrapping paper, dry, the strop coiled beside it like a sleeping snake. A man does not strop his razor and leave it dry unless he believes tomorrow is somebody else's problem.

By the time I got to Calloway-Hurst's tent he was already coming out of it, and I will say this for the man: he could dress fast. Waistcoat buttoned, collar pinned, hair combed, looking like he'd stepped out of a Buenos Aires hotel instead of a canvas coffin on the Rio Seco. He looked at me, and then at the four cold tents behind me, and his face did something I want to describe accurately, because it mattered later.

It did nothing.

"Ah," he said. "So they've gone."

"Gone where?" I said. "There's nowhere to go. We're two hundred miles past the last charted reach, and the nearest thing to a road is a goat track that died of loneliness."

"They were restless men." He said it mildly, folding his hands. "Government men often are."

"Mr. Calloway-Hurst." I kept my voice level, which cost me something. "Their boots are in their tents."

He looked at me for a long moment, and behind his eyes something moved — quick, furtive, like a rat crossing a pantry — and then it was gone and he was just a tired middle-aged administrator again. "Then perhaps you should ask your cartographer. She was up in the night. I heard her."

That was a lie. I knew it was a lie the way you know a bad knot — Elena Vasquez slept aboard, in the forward cabin we'd cleared for her, and the gangplank creaked like a church organ whenever anyone crossed it. Nobody had crossed it in the night. I'd have heard. I hear everything on my boat; it's the one sense I've never managed to switch off.

But I didn't call the lie. Not yet. There's a time to catch a man in a falsehood and a time to let him think it landed, and I'm enough of a river pilot to know you read the current before you commit the boat.

"I'll ask everyone," I said pleasantly, and walked back to the *Mariposa* to wake my crew.

* * *

Bruno took it badly. Bruno takes most things badly; it's part of his charm. He stood on the bank staring at the four neat tents with his big hands hanging at his sides and said, "Capitán, this is wrong. This is wrong like a fish with fur."

"That's very poetic, Bruno."

"My mother says things like that."

"Your mother sounds like a wise woman. Go check the mooring lines and stop looking at the tents. They'll still be creepy after breakfast."

Pilar, who at nineteen notices everything and pretends to notice nothing, came to me quietly while Bruno stomped off. "Capitán. The dust."

"What about the dust?"

"There's no dust on anything. Look." She pointed at the ground around the tents, the fine pale silt that the wind lays down along the Seco overnight, thick as flour. "Nobody walked out of here. Nobody walked anywhere. If four men left in the night, they left footprints. There aren't any. Not going out, not coming back. Nothing."

She was right. I'd noticed it myself and hadn't wanted to name it, because naming it made it real. The ground around that camp was smooth as a fresh-made bed. Whatever had happened to those four men had happened without weight.

Elena came down the gangplank while Pilar and I were standing there, and she was already dressed, already sharp-eyed, her notebook under her arm like a weapon she hadn't decided whether to draw. She looked at the tents, at the dead fire, at the untouched dust, and I watched her arrive at the same place Pilar and I were standing, only faster, because Elena arrives everywhere faster.

"Their gear's intact," she said. Not a question.

"All of it. Instruments, papers, boots."

"Instruments?" Something flickered across her face — and I mean really flickered, like lightning inside a cloud. "All of them?"

"Every crate accounted for, far as I can see."

"Hm," said Elena Vasquez, and wrote something in her notebook, and I remember thinking: *that woman knows more about why these men are here than the men do.*

Calloway-Hurst came down to join us then, insisting on organizing a search party as though the men had wandered off to pick flowers, and I let him organize it, because a man organizing a search cannot simultaneously prevent one. We cast about in widening arcs through the scrub — me, Bruno, Pilar, Elena, and Calloway-Hurst himself, who walked with his hands clasped behind his back like a vicar inspecting a garden.

It was Elena who found it.

She was maybe three hundred meters upslope, where the shingle gives way to a steep scree fan running up toward the gorge wall, and she called out once, just my name, "Noor," in a voice with no decoration on it at all, and I was moving before the echo settled.

She was crouched over the scree. Boot prints. Two sets, side by side, heading uphill — Okafor's wide hobnails and a narrower pair, probably young Ferreyra's. The prints were crisp, unhurried, spaced like men walking, not running. Walking uphill at night, together, in the dark, toward nothing.

And then they stopped.

Not faded. Not lost among the stones. *Stopped.* Mid-slope, mid-stride — one print planted full and deep, the next one simply absent, as though the man had been lifted off the face of the earth between one step and the next. Both sets ended the same way, within a meter of each other. After that, nothing but scree undisturbed as far as the eye could follow.

We stood over it for a while. Even the wind seemed to be waiting.

"Noor," Elena said quietly. "People don't levitate."

"No," I agreed. "They don't."

"And yet."

"And yet."

Behind us, gravel shifted. Calloway-Hurst had followed us up — of course he had; he wouldn't want us finding things unsupervised — and he stood looking down at the prints with his hands still clasped behind him, and his expression was still that awful mild blankness, and I understood suddenly, completely, the way you understand a chart error only after you've touched the sandbar, that this man was not worried about his missing surveyors.

He was *relieved*.

"You see?" he said. "Nothing here. Some geological curiosity, perhaps. Wind-scoured stone holds prints oddly." He smiled at Elena. "Miss Vasquez, you're a cartographer. You know how treacherous loose ground is for evidence."

"Intimately," Elena said, and her voice was warm as a knife. "Which is why I know these prints weren't wind-scoured. Wind doesn't erase the second half of a stride, Mr. Calloway-Hurst. It erases all of it or none."

For a moment — just a moment — the mask slipped, and what was underneath wasn't guilt exactly. It was fear. Old fear, worn smooth, the kind a man carries so long he's stopped noticing the weight of it. Then he straightened his waistcoat and said, "Well. We shall report them lost when we reach a telegraph office. The expedition continues. Time is money, Captain, and yours is expensive."

He walked back down the slope. We watched him go, picking his way carefully, precisely, a man who never misstepped because he always knew where the edges were.

"He knew," Pilar said. She'd come up behind us without a sound, the way she does. "Capitán. He knew they were going. He didn't even look surprised."

"I know."

"So what do we do?"

I looked at the prints ending mid-slope. I looked down at the river, silvering now in the full morning, running north toward the sea with all the patience in the world. I thought about Tomás, which I try not to do before noon, and about promises, and about the fact that I had signed on with triple pay to carry five men up a river and four of them had just evaporated into the night, and that the fifth was lying to me with the calm of a man reading his own eulogy.

And I thought about Elena, standing beside me with her notebook open, drawing the boot prints in precise, unforgiving detail — date, bearing, spacing, termination — documenting the impossible because documenting was what she did, and because somewhere under all that competence she had decided, apparently without consulting herself, that these men deserved a witness.

"We do our job," I said. "We keep going upstream. That's what we were paid for, and it's also the only direction the truth lives in."

Elena snapped the notebook shut. "You believe that? That the truth is upstream?"

"Captain Thornbury believes the truth is wherever she's pointing the boat," I said. "It saves wear on the engines."

Something happened to her face then — the first crack, small as a hairline fracture in good porcelain. She almost smiled. "And if the truth is dangerous?"

"Then it can wait its turn in the queue like everybody else."

But we both knew I was joking, and we both knew I wasn't. Because as we walked back down toward the camp and the cold fire and the four tents standing empty as held breath, I caught sight of the *Mariposa* at her mooring, and beyond her, far downstream, a smudge on the water that might have been haze and might have been a hull, and the morning that had promised me a soft day finally showed its true colors.

Fear is a funny thing on a river. It starts in the feet, in the soles, where the boat talks to you through the deck. By the time it reaches your head it's not fear anymore. It's resolve. It's the difference between reading the rapids and running them.

I reached the rail and put my hand flat on the wood, and the *Mariposa* talked to me, and I listened.

"Pilar," I said. "Get the engine warm. We're making way within the hour."

"Aye, Capitán." She hesitated. "Upstream?"

"Upstream," I said. "Wherever it is those men went — whatever lifted them off that hillside — it's above us. And I don't intend to sit here waiting for it to come down."

Elena was already aboard, standing at the bow rail, facing upriver into the gorge's shadow, her notebook open against the wind. She didn't look back. She didn't need to. Some decisions make themselves the moment the alternative becomes clear, and hers had been made sometime between the cold coffee and the boot prints ending mid-air.

I rang the engine bell twice, and the *Mariposa* answered, and we turned our bow toward the source of everything.
