## 8. What the Manifests Said

Elena had learned long ago that the truth about men was kept in their paperwork. Husbands kept theirs in ledgers; governments kept theirs in filing cabinets that smelled of dust and self-regard; and Mr. Calloway-Hurst kept his in a leather document case he slept with the way other men slept with a Bible — close to the chest, one hand resting on it even in dreams.

She had photographed his manifests on loading day at Puerto Árido, crouched in the shadow of the fuel drums with her little folding camera held low against her skirt, and she had spent the four days since then turning the negatives over in her mind like stones on a riverbank, looking for what lived underneath.

Now, moored below the gorge with four surveyors vanished into thin air and a fifth man drinking coffee as though nothing were wrong, it seemed a reasonable time to stop turning stones and simply pick one up.

She found Noor Thornbury at the stern rail, watching the water with the particular stillness of a person listening to something no one else could hear. The captain had been like that since dawn — since the empty tents, since the boot prints that ended mid-slope as if the earth had swallowed four grown men whole. Elena had watched Noor check the lines twice, walk the deck twice, and stand at the rail for an hour without moving, and she understood that this was not calm. This was a woman holding very carefully onto the edge of herself.

"Captain," Elena said. "I need to show you something. Not here."

Noor's eyes came around slowly. "If it's about the tents—"

"It's about the crates."

Something shifted behind Noor's eyes. She pushed off the rail. "My cabin."

* * *

The cabin smelled of engine oil and cheap tobacco and, faintly, of the river itself, which had gotten into everything the way river gets into everything eventually. Noor cleared a space on the chart table by sweeping a coil of rope into a hammock of its own making, and Elena laid out her photographs in a row, weighting the corners with whatever came to hand: a brass compass, a tin of matches, Noor's own coffee cup, commandeered without apology.

Noor looked at them upside down first, which told Elena something about how the woman's mind worked — always reading the current from the far bank before committing to it.

Then Noor turned the photographs around, and Elena watched the understanding arrive.

"You said you took these," Noor said quietly. "Loading day. I remember you crouching down there like a heron."

"I photograph everything. It's my job. Maps are just photographs that have been made to tell the truth." Elena tapped the first image. "This is manifest one. Drilling equipment. A diamond-core rig, two hundred meters of casing, a portable compressor. Captain — no hydrographic survey needs a core drill. You don't drill holes in a river to measure it. You measure it and *then*, if you're going to dam it, you drill."

Noor's jaw tightened. "Go on."

"Manifest two." Elena moved her finger. "Survey stakes. Four hundred of them. Aluminum, pre-stamped. And look at the stamping."

Noor leaned closer. Her lips moved as she read the tiny stamped codes, and Elena saw the moment they stopped being codes to her and became coordinates.

"That's not the lower river," Noor said. "Those numbers are high country. That's—" She stopped. "That's past where any chart stops pretending."

"The source," Elena said. "Every stake is numbered for the upper gorge and the headwater basin. Manifest three is the interesting one, if you enjoy being insulted. It lists 'geological sampling consumables' — which is crates of cement, detonators, and forty rolls of galvanized wire. Captain, you do not take cement and wire to *survey*. You take cement and wire to *claim*. To mark boundaries. To build small weirs and diversion heads and make a claim physically real on the ground so that when the concession office stamps your papers, the paper agrees with the dirt."

The cabin was very quiet. Through the hull came the patient sound of the river working at the mooring line, the way it worked at everything, all night, all day, asking the same question forever.

Noor straightened up. "Whose concession?"

"That's the beautiful part. That's the part I've been chewing for four days like gristle." Elena pulled out the last photograph, the one she'd almost not taken because taking it meant believing it. "The permits are issued under the name of the government survey. But the equipment orders — the purchase requisitions underneath — those carry the letterhead of the Compañía de Aguas del Sur. The same company whose wells dried out Estancia Salada. The same company that owns every drop between here and the coast. They aren't helping the government survey, Captain. They *are* the government survey. They hired themselves, dressed themselves in the flag, and sent themselves upstream to stake the source before the rival concessions expire — forty days, Mr. Calloway-Hurst told us at dinner our first night, forty days and then everything unclaimed opens to bid. He wasn't telling us the deadline. He was telling us the schedule."

Noor sat down slowly on the edge of her bunk, as if her legs had submitted a request her pride had only just approved.

"And the four men," Noor said. "Petrov, Okafor, Lindqvist, the young one — Marsh."

"Gone."

"Or staked out somewhere along the high ground doing the company's work while we sit here like a ferry for thieves." Noor rubbed both hands over her face. When they came down, her expression had gone somewhere flat and cold that Elena suspected was older than the rest of her face. "I carried their crates. I winched their rig aboard with my own hands. I fed them my coffee."

"I'm sorry," Elena said, and meant it more than the words could hold. "I should have shown you sooner. I kept thinking I'd misread it. I wanted to misread it."

"Why?" Noor asked, and it wasn't hostile; it was genuinely curious, the way she seemed genuinely curious about most things once she decided you weren't lying to her. "You didn't sign on to police anyone."

"No. I signed on to draw a map of a river nobody had bothered to map, which is the closest thing to being born that I know how to arrange." Elena gathered the photographs back into their folder, then stopped, holding them. "But I also signed on because a man offered me triple pay and a story to tell afterward, and I let the money quiet the part of me that noticed the crates. So did you. So did your crew. We all took the pay and agreed not to look too hard. That's how it works, I think. That's how it always works. Nobody has to be wicked. Everybody just has to be busy."

Noor huffed something that was almost a laugh. "You'd have made a fine river pilot. Same philosophy exactly."

"I'll add it to my list of careers I'm fleeing into."

* * *

They found Calloway-Hurst where he had been all morning: seated on a crate at the shaded end of the deck with his notebook open on his knee, writing nothing, turning pages he had already written as though hoping they would rearrange themselves into innocence. He was a brittle man, Elena thought — not weak, precisely, but built like good china, all surface and firing cracks, made for a cabinet and now out in weather.

He looked up at the two of them approaching and, to his credit, did not perform surprise.

"Captain. Señora Vasquez." He closed the notebook. His hand stayed on it. "You have the look of people arriving at a conclusion."

"We have the look of people who read manifests," Elena said.

For a long moment Calloway-Hurst studied the middle distance, where the gorge walls rose brown and gold into a sky scrubbed clean of clouds. Then he laughed — a short, airless sound with no humor in it whatsoever, the laugh of a man hearing the punchline of a joke played on him.

"Of course you did." He set the notebook aside and folded his hands. "I wondered which of you would get there first. My money was on you, señora. Petrov bets on everything, you know. He had two pesos on the deckhand."

"Mr. Calloway-Hurst," Noor said, in the voice Elena imagined she used on rivers — level, patient, and absolutely without mercy available to it, "where are your men?"

"They're alive." He said it quickly, and it had the ring of truth, or at least of a man clinging to one true thing with both hands. "I want that understood before anything else. Whatever else I am, they are alive. Reyes has no interest in dead surveyors. Dead surveyors can't be produced later to testify that the survey was genuine."

"Reyes," Elena repeated. "Inspector Reyes. Of the Compañía."

"Of the Compañía," Calloway-Hurst agreed. "Officially he's a government inspector of concessions. Unofficially he is the government, insofar as the government extends to this river, which is exactly as far as the company's launch can steam." He looked down at his hands. "I requested this posting, you know. I fought for it. A survey of the upper Rio Seco — do you know what that means to a man who has spent twenty years mapping other people's rivers? The blank space on the map. The last white patch in the province. I thought I would put my name on it. Elena Vasquez, I imagine you understand that better than anyone on this boat."

Elena did understand it. Understanding it felt like a hook behind her ribs.

"And instead," Noor said.

"And instead I received my instructions in Puerto Árido, before we ever loaded." His voice had gone dry and careful, a man walking on ice he already knew was thin. "Stake the basin above the gorge. Mark the diversion sites. Complete the work inside thirty days, before the rival permits lapse, and file everything through the company office. The government seal would follow. It always follows. I was ordered to let it happen, Captain. I was ordered to *do* it, and to let my crew believe we were measuring a river while we fenced off its throat."

"And your crew believed it?"

"Petrov knew. Okafor guessed — he's not a fool, he asked why a survey needs detonators, and I lied to him badly, and he accepted the lie because men accept lies that come with triple pay. Lindqvist and Marsh never suspected anything. They're good men. They think in contours and flow rates." He smiled briefly, terribly. "It's the honest ones who make the best cover, isn't it? Nobody audits a man who loves his work."

The river said nothing, but it kept asking.

"So why tell us now?" Noor asked. "Why not keep lying? We're three days from anywhere, Mr. Calloway-Hurst, and you outnumber us."

"Do I?" He gestured vaguely at the deck, at the boat, at the whole brown-gold world. "Captain Thornbury, I watched you bring this vessel through the Narrows at midnight with a headwind and half a rudder's authority. I have spent six days learning what you are. And this morning four of my men walked up a slope and stopped existing, and I understood that the company no longer needs me either. I am not telling you because I've grown brave. I'm telling you because I've done the arithmetic, and the arithmetic says the safest place left on this river is standing next to you."

Elena heard it — the exact shape of the cowardice, honest down to its bones. She found, to her own irritation, that she didn't despise him for it. Despising him would have been easier.

"The manifests," she said. "The originals. Where are they?"

His hand went, involuntarily, to the document case at his side — the one he slept with, the one he'd carried aboard like scripture. He looked at it. He looked at the two of them.

"If I give you these," he said, "there is no version of events in which I go home respectable. There's prison, or there's exile, or there's the bottom of the river, and I confess I've become attached to the idea of dry land."

"There's another version," Noor said. "There's the version where the true survey gets published and the whole rotten arrangement dies in daylight, and the man who handed over the proof is the man who helped kill it. Governments forgive that. Governments *love* that. It lets everyone pretend the system worked."

Calloway-Hurst stared at her. "You believe that?"

"No," Noor said honestly. "But I believe the river's going to decide this before any government does, and I'd rather you bet on us than on Reyes."

Another silence. Somewhere overhead a hawk turned in the thermals above the gorge, riding the rising air in slow circles, patient as interest accruing.

Calloway-Hurst opened the document case. He took out the manifests — the true ones, the requisitions with the company letterhead, the stamped coordinate schedules, and beneath them a telegram flimsy that made even Elena's practiced eye go wide: instructions in the company director's own abbreviated style, dated eleven days before they'd ever left port.

He laid them on the crate between them like a man laying down cards he'd been cheating with.

"There," he said. "Now I'm either a witness or a corpse, and I find I'd like to be consulted on which."

Elena picked up the telegram. The paper trembled slightly in her fingers, and she hated that it trembled, and let it anyway. Everything she had photographed, everything she had suspected, was now confirmed in ink and official seals: the survey was a mask, the source was the prize, and the clock — the forty-day clock, now thirty-six, now thirty-five — was ticking toward a theft dressed as a discovery.

"Captain," she said quietly, "we need to talk about what's downstream of us."

Noor was already looking that way. Downstream, around the last bend of the lower gorge, lay open water and, somewhere on it, a black-hulled launch with a courteous man aboard who had been promised a woman and some maps.

"Reyes won't have heard yet," Calloway-Hurst offered. "The company doesn't know I've talked."

"He will," Noor said. "Men like that always hear. It's the one thing they're better at than anyone." She stood, and rolled her shoulders, and Elena watched the fear move through her and get put away somewhere, neatly, like gear lashed down for rough water. "Get below, all of you. If we're going to be witnesses, we're going to need this boat pointed the right way when they arrive."

"And which way is that?" Elena asked.

Noor looked upstream, toward the gorge, toward the whistling stones and the blank space on every map ever printed.

"Away," she said. "Fast."

* * *

By late afternoon the *Rivergate* — Tomás's old boat, Noor's inheritance of guilt and diesel — was coaled, watered, and breathing hard at her moorings, ready to run a passage at dusk that Elena suspected no sane pilot would attempt. Elena spent the hours at the chart table copying the manifests' coordinates onto her own field sheets, building, line by line, the map that would hang somebody.

It was Pilar who spotted it first — the youngest deckhand, sharp-eyed as a gull, calling down from the wheelhouse roof in a voice pitched carefully casual:

"Captain. Smoke. Downstream. Two bends."

Elena climbed up beside her and looked, and there it was: a smudge against the evening haze, moving, deliberate, unhurried. Something coming upriver with all the patience in the world.

Behind her, she heard Noor climb the ladder, and stop, and go very still.

"Well," Noor said softly. "Right on schedule."
