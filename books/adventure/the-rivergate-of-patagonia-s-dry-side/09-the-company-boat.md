## 9. The Company Boat

I have a rule about rivers: they tell you everything if you listen, and they lie about half of it. The Rio Seco that morning was telling me we were being followed.

It said it in the birds first. The herons that had stood on the mudflats all week like bored clerks suddenly lifted off in a body, all of them at once, heading upstream — which herons never do unless something downstream has offended them. Then the water itself changed its mind about the current, a long slow shudder running under the hull like a horse flicking off a fly. I'd felt that shudder twice before in my life. Both times it had been a big engine working hard against shallow water, and both times I had ended up having a very bad week.

"You feel that?" I asked Pilar, who was coiling line on the foredeck with the exaggerated care of someone pretending not to be seasick.

"Feel what?"

"That." The shudder came again, longer this time, and Pilar's hands went still on the rope.

"Engine," Pilar said. "Big one."

"Big one," I agreed. "Downstream. Coming up fast."

Elena appeared at the cabin door with two cups of coffee, took one look at my face, and set both cups down on the chart table instead, which told me she understood that coffee was about to become a luxury. "How fast is fast?"

"Fast enough that the herons quit arguing about it." I climbed to the wheelhouse roof and put the glasses on the reach behind us. The river bent south around a gravel island, and for a moment there was nothing but light on water and the dust-colored hills, and I let myself believe I'd imagined it. Then the bend gave up its secret.

A launch. Black hull, black superstructure, white trim painted so clean it looked like teeth. It was riding heavy in the stern, which meant men and equipment, and it was coming up the channel at a speed no sensible pilot would use above Puerto Árido, because above Puerto Árido the river moved its sandbars around at night the way a sleeper moves his legs.

"Well," Elena said beside me. She hadn't needed the glasses. "That's not a fishing boat."

"No."

"It's not a navy boat either. Navy boats are ugly on purpose. That one's ugly on purpose *and* expensive."

I handed her the glasses. Through them the launch resolved further: figures at the rail, four or five, standing in that particular stillness that hired men stand in when they're being paid to be still. And at the bow, alone, a man in a pale suit, hands folded behind his back, looking upstream directly at us as though he could see us without glass, which for all I knew he could.

"Do you know him?" Elena asked.

"I know the suit," I said. "Everybody on this river knows the suit. That's the Concesión del Sur's idea of a greeting card."

* * *

His name was Reyes. I learned it an hour later when we'd put enough distance between us to fool ourselves, and then he put it back again in twenty minutes flat, because whatever engine that launch carried had been built by people who believed in overdoing things.

We were tied up at midday taking on water from a spring seep near an old sheep crossing — you can't run the upper reaches dry, no matter how much you want to outrun your problems — when the black launch rounded the bend below us and slowed, and slowed, and came alongside with the smoothness of a hand closing around a wrist.

There were six of them aboard besides the man in the suit. Four stayed seated on deck crates doing nothing whatsoever, which is the most frightening thing armed men can do. One held our lines. And the man in the suit stepped onto my deck as if he owned the plank, which, given the state of my debts, he may briefly have considered.

"Captain Thornbury." He pronounced it carefully, the way you handle something you've been told is fragile. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with the kind of tan that comes from standing in shade watching other people work. "Inspector Emilio Reyes. Concesión del Sur, Department of River Affairs."

"There's a department of river affairs?"

"There is now." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was the smile of a man who had practiced smiling at people who hated him until even hatred got tired. "You have a government charter. Survey team, hydrographic work, upper reaches. Very exciting. Very unusual."

"The government doesn't explain itself to me. It pays me not to ask."

"And yet here I am, asking nothing, explaining everything. A courtesy." He turned a slow circle on my deck, taking in the winch, the stacked fuel drums, Elena's charts drying on the rail, Pilar frozen mid-coil with a rope in his hands like a man caught stealing it. "Your papers, Captain, if you would."

I gave him the charter. He read it more slowly than it deserved, and while he read, the four men on the crates watched me, and I watched the water, because the water was telling me something else now. It was telling me their draft. A launch that size, riding that heavy, drawing that much — she couldn't follow us past the Narrows. Whatever Reyes intended, he intended it below the gorge, and everything above the gorge was going to be a footrace on foot, or nothing at all.

"Charter's in order," Reyes said at last, handing it back with both hands, another courtesy. "The ministry is thorough. Though I confess some confusion about your cargo manifests. Drilling equipment, Captain? For a survey?"

"You'd have to ask Mr. Calloway-Hurst."

"Oh, I intend to ask everyone everything eventually. That's the whole of my profession." He looked upstream then, at the haze where the hills closed together and the river went into the gorge, and for just a moment the smile went away entirely, and what was underneath it was patient the way a trap is patient. "Forty days, Captain. Did you know? Forty days left on every concession on this river, and after that, everything gets renegotiated. Men get nervous before renegotiations. Nervous men do untidy things." He turned back to me. "My advice, offered freely: finish your charter quickly. Stay in charted water. Go home to Puerto Árido and spend your triple pay somewhere the air is thick and the drinks are cold."

"That's three pieces of advice."

"I'm feeling generous." He stepped back across to his own deck, paused, and added, almost gently: "The river remembers who drinks first, Captain. But it also remembers who muddies it. Good day."

The launch pulled away downstream — downstream, note, not up — and sat out in the middle of the channel, black and patient, like a chess player who has already seen the endgame and is simply waiting for you to notice.

"He knows about the manifests," Elena said quietly, at my shoulder.

"He knows something. Whether it's the manifests or just that Calloway-Hurst sweats like a church candle, hard to say."

"What do we do?"

I looked upstream. The gorge mouth was two days' run, maybe less if I pushed, and past the Narrows his beautiful black boat might as well have been a piano. "We do what rivers taught me," I said. "We go where he can't fit."

* * *

Here is the thing about the passage above the Narrows at night, and I will say it plainly because nobody else ever has: it cannot be done. Every pilot on the Rio Seco will tell you so. The channel braids through gravel shoals that move with every windstorm; the banks are soft clay that sheds whole slabs into the current; and the markers, such as they are, were placed thirty years ago by a government surveyor who, judging by his notes, was drunk, optimistic, or both. By daylight it is a gamble. By moonlight it is a prayer. On a moonless night it is a story old pilots tell young pilots to make them stay home.

Which is why, when the new moon rose — that is to say, did not rise — I started my engines at dusk and ran for the Narrows with the last of the light.

"You're joking," Elena said. She had figured it out before I said it, because she'd spent the afternoon studying my charts the way she studied everything, which is to say completely and slightly hungrily.

"I never joke about channels. Only about everything else."

"Noor. The sandbars shift overnight. You told me that yourself. You said—" 

"I said the lower bars shift. Up here they don't shift so much as *migrate*. But they migrate slow, and I ran this reach in March, and I remember where the river was rude to me." I checked the fuel gauge, the lamp oil, the lead line coiled at the wheelhouse door. "Also, I have something Reyes doesn't."

"A death wish?"

"Tomás." I heard myself say the name and kept going before it could catch on anything. "He ran this stretch eleven times. He used to count strokes of the oar between landmarks instead of trusting the markers. Said the river lies but arithmetic doesn't. I've got his numbers in my head, and tonight I'm going to find out whether grief makes a good chart."

Elena looked at me for a long moment. In the dying light her face was all shadow and decision. "What do you need from me?"

"Your eyes. All of them. And Pilar on the lead line, and Bruno on the searchlight only when I say, and everybody else holding onto something bolted down."

* * *

We entered the Narrows at full dark with the stars out and no moon, and I will try to describe what the next six hours were like, though description is a poor boat for that water.

Imagine steering by memory and arithmetic through a room where the furniture rearranges itself whenever you blink. Imagine knowing that somewhere ahead is a bar called the Widow's Teeth — Tomás named it, of course, Tomás named everything grimly and laughed while doing it — and that the Widow's Teeth has moved since March, and that finding its new address is the difference between breakfast and eternity.

For the first hour we crept. Half throttle, soundings every ten seconds, Pilar swinging the lead and calling depths in a voice that started steady and got smaller as the dark got bigger. "Seven! ... Six and a half! ... Seven!" The clay banks slid past like sleeping animals. Once a slab of bank calved off fifty yards ahead with a sound like a door slamming in an empty house, and we all stood absolutely still until the ripples reached us and rocked us gently, almost apologetically.

"Starboard light," I called softly, and Bruno swung the beam out, and there: the pale seam of moving water over the bar's shoulder, exactly where Tomás's arithmetic said it ought to be, give or take a boat-length. I turned us through the seam with the delicacy of a woman threading a needle in gloves, and the keel cleared by what Bruno later swore was a hand's width and I later swore was a full fathom, and neither of us has conceded since.

Somewhere in the second hour Elena came up beside me at the wheel. She didn't speak for a while. When she did, it was barely above the engine noise.

"Reyes will have guessed."

"Reyes guesses everything. Guessing isn't fitting. His boat draws nine feet. This channel draws five, and lies about it."

"You could have taken the road out. Paid off the crew, walked away, kept the triple pay for the days worked. Nobody would have blamed—"

"Elena." I kept my eyes on the water. "If I wanted blame I'd have stayed married to it in Puerto Árido years ago."

She made a sound that was half laugh, half something else, and put her hand briefly on my shoulder — brief enough to deny, long enough to mean — and went forward to spell Pilar on the lead.

At the third hour the river played its trick. The channel split three ways around a shoal that wasn't on any chart, wasn't in Tomás's numbers, hadn't existed in March. Three doors, all dark, all promising. I brought us to a stop, engine ticking, drifting, and let the boat tell me. Current leans toward truth on a river; the false channels eddy and curl back on themselves, but the real one pulls. I felt the pull faintly, port side, and we went, and the depth under the keel went eight feet, nine, twelve, and the Widow's Teeth — wherever she'd moved to — clicked her jaws shut behind us on empty water.

And I confess it here, because the logbook deserves honesty: partway through that fourth hour, threading the last reach by starlight with the whole crew silent as congregation, I was afraid, and tired, and grieving a man eleven years drowned, and I was also — God forgive me — *happy*. Not comfortable. Nothing about that night was comfortable. Happy the way a struck match is happy. There is a version of me that exists only at moments like that, and she had been locked in a closet for years, and that night she was flying the boat.

* * *

We came out of the Narrows at dawn into the long lake-broad water below the gorge, and anchored in the lee of a gravel point, and ate eggs, because Pilar insisted that surviving the impossible requires eggs, and I have never once argued with that theology.

It was Bruno, up on the roof with the glasses, who saw it first.

"Captain." His voice had gone careful. "Downstream. At the Narrows entrance."

I took the glasses. There, sitting in the mouth of the reach no sane pilot would attempt, black against the morning mist, was the launch. Anchored. Patient. Her white trim catching the first sun like a knife laid on a table.

They'd come all the way up in the dark, hugging the edges, burning fuel, risking their pretty hull — because Reyes had guessed too, and guessed right, and had raced us to the door only to find it too narrow for him. He couldn't follow. So he'd done the next thing a patient man does.

He'd posted himself at the exit.

Elena lowered her own glasses slowly. "He's not chasing us anymore."

"No."

"He's waiting for us to come back down."

I looked upstream instead, at the gorge walls standing shoulder to shoulder, at the haze of the high country beyond where a river kept a secret worth fortunes, and at the smoke of a campfire that didn't belong to us, thin and gray, rising from a bend two miles up where no chart showed anyone living.

"Then we'd better make sure," I said, ringing the engine telegraph to Ahead Slow, "that by the time we come back down, there's nothing left for him to be waiting *for*."

Behind us, the black boat sat motionless on the bright water, and ahead, the gorge opened its throat — and from somewhere inside it, faint on the morning wind, came a sound none of us could name yet: a low, rhythmic knocking, like stone speaking to stone.

Pilar heard it too, and asked the question we were all thinking:

"Captain — what's *up* there?"
