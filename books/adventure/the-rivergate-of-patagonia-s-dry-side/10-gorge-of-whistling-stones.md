## 10. Gorge of Whistling Stones

I had made maps for eleven years before I understood that a map is an argument. It says: *this is how the world is arranged; you may trust me.* I had never once been on a river that argued back.

The Rio Seco argued.

We entered the gorge at first light, because Noor refused to enter it any other way and refused, also, to explain why beyond a shrug that said *light through a gorge lasts ninety minutes and I intend to use all of them.* The walls rose on either side of us like the covers of a book closing — red stone streaked black where centuries of rare rain had written their thin signatures — and between them the river ran fast and green and full of opinions.

"Everyone listen," Noor said, standing at the wheel with one boot up on the rail in that way that managed to be both casual and absolutely commanding. "In about two kilometers the channel narrows and drops. There are rocks in it that sing."

"Sing," repeated Bruno, who was coiling rope with the concentration of a man who had learned everything he knew from watching other people do it wrong first.

"Whistle. Wind comes off the ice fields upstream, funnels through holes in the stone. If you can hear the stones whistling, the water's high enough to cover the worst of them. If they go quiet—" Noor paused, and I watched the pause travel down the deck like a cold draft, "—you pray quietly and do exactly what I say."

"And if we can't hear anything over the engine?" Pilar asked. She was sixteen and had been aboard four days and already asked better questions than the entire survey team combined.

"Then we're already dead, so it won't matter." Noor smiled when she said it, which somehow made it worse and better at once.

I stood near the bow with my notebook, because that was what I did. That was who I had decided to be somewhere around Puerto Árido: the woman with the notebook, the observer, the documenter. A person who watched things happen and wrote them down afterward, safely, in ink. It had seemed like a fine identity. It had even been true, more or less, for years.

I want to record, here, honestly, that it stopped being true sometime in the next forty minutes, and that I felt it stop the way you feel a rope go taut.

* * *

The gorge narrowed the way a fist closes.

One moment we had fifty meters of water and room to breathe; the next, the walls came in to perhaps twenty, and the river, denied its width, took its payment in speed. The *Estrella del Sur* — Tomás's old boat, though I only learned her history later — lifted at the bow and began to run, and I heard Noor say, very calmly, to no one in particular:

"There they are."

And the stones began to whistle.

It is a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, I suspect, every time I stand near moving water. Not a whistle exactly — more a breath drawn across the lip of a bottle, dozens of bottles, all different sizes, all singing slightly out of tune with each other, a chord held by the wind and the stone while the river hissed underneath it. It should have been beautiful. For about four seconds, it was.

Then the first wave hit us sideways.

I have read accounts of rapids by men who ran them for sport, and they always describe the water in terms of power, of force, of tonnage. They are wrong, or they are lying, or they have forgotten. What the water actually does is *decide*. It decides what you are worth. It picked up our stern as if weighing us, found us acceptable, and threw us forward into the standing waves, and the whole boat became something alive and panicking beneath my feet.

"Hard left!" Noor shouted, and the wheel spun, and we swung so hard I went to my knees on the deck and slid until the rail caught me under the ribs. Bruno grabbed the back of my collar without looking, the way you'd catch a falling cup, and hauled me upright, and said, conversationally, over the roar:

"You should hold onto something, señorita."

"I'm holding onto you!"

"Yes, but I might need both hands."

He let go of me and took the line Pilar thrust at him, and the three of us — cartographer, deckhand, girl — braced against the gunwale while Noor fought the channel alone, reading water the way I read contour lines, except faster, and with consequences measured in seconds instead of errors.

The gorge did not let up. Wave after standing wave, each one taller than the last, the whistling stones shrieking now above us as the wind found its full voice in the holes of the cliff. Spray came over the bow in sheets. My notebook was soaked through in my pocket and I remember thinking, absurdly, *the survey data* — and then thinking, less absurdly, *I don't care about the survey data, the survey data was a lie anyway,* and then not thinking at all, because the river dropped us into a hole.

Not a metaphorical hole. An actual hole in the river, a place where the current poured downward into itself and came up boiling, and the *Estrella del Sur* went into it bow-first and did not come out the way boats are supposed to come out of things.

We stalled. That's the word for it, though nothing about it was still. The bow buried, the stern lifted, the engine screaming at water that wasn't there, and the whole world tilted until the cliff wall on our left was below us and the sky was a gray ribbon overhead, and I understood with perfect clarity that we were going over.

People talk about time slowing in moments like this. It doesn't slow. It *separates*. Everything happens at once and also each thing gets its own complete moment, its own little room. In one room: Noor's voice, flat and certain, saying "Bruno, the starboard line, NOW." In another: Bruno letting go of the rail. In another: the boat coming over.

Bruno didn't make the line.

The boat rolled — not fully, not all the way, but enough — and the river reached up and took him off the deck the way you'd take a coat off a hook. One moment he was beside me, big and warm and complaining; the next there was just green water where he'd been, and a boot, one boot, spinning away downstream.

I did not think. That is the part I keep returning to. Eleven years of careful observation, of watching, of writing things down after they were safely over — and in the moment that mattered, I did not observe, I did not document, I did not hesitate to calculate risk. I heard myself shout "PILAR, THE LINE, TIE IT TO ME," and I was already climbing the rail.

Here is what I knew in that instant, with a clarity I had never achieved in any survey office: the river was carrying Bruno toward the right-hand wall, where the water funneled through a gap between two boulders, and on the downstream side of those boulders the current curled back on itself, and if he went past the curl he would be gone — not drowned dramatically, just *gone*, absorbed, filed away, another name the river kept. And I knew it because I had spent the last hour doing the only useful thing I know how to do: paying attention. Reading the water. Making the map in my head.

All those years of drawing lines, and it turned out they were training for this.

"Noor!" I screamed, and Noor looked at me — one second, half a second, the boat wallowing half-sideways in the boil — and whatever Noor saw in my face was apparently sufficient, because the throttle slammed forward and the *Estrella del Sur* lunged out of the hole and swung downstream after a drowning man, listing badly, taking water, Noor's hands doing four impossible things at once on the wheel.

Pilar got the line around my waist. Her hands were shaking and her knots were terrible and I loved her forever for both.

"Bruno!" I yelled. The gorge ate the word. The stones whistled their idiot chorus. And then, between two waves, I saw him — an arm, a head, dark hair plastered flat, twenty meters off and closing fast, and his face wore an expression I recognized from loading day, the expression of a man watching himself do something wrong and being unable to stop it.

I went over the side.

The cold was total. It wasn't temperature, it was *opinion* — the river's opinion of me, delivered instantly and at length. It closed my throat, emptied my lungs, turned my hands into someone else's hands. The current took me the way it takes everything, without malice and without mercy, and I understood in about one and a half seconds why Noor had said *pray quietly.*

But I had tied myself to the boat, and Pilar — sixteen years old, four days aboard, terrible knots — paid out the line hand over hand and *followed*, leaning out, keeping the slack right, keeping me from being snapped like a kite string when the line came taut. I found out later she'd done it purely on instinct, having never once handled a rescue line. Some people are simply built from the right material and don't find out until the testing.

I got an arm under Bruno's chin. He was heavier than a door and fighting me the way drowning men fight everything, including salvation, and we went under together twice, and the second time I felt the line snap tight around my waist and the whole world jerked, and for one long gray moment I was a pendulum swinging between the river's will and the boat's, and I genuinely did not know which would win.

Then hands. Many hands. Noor had brought the boat alongside in water no sane pilot would bring anything alongside in, and Bruno and I came over the gunwale in a tangle of rope and limbs and river, and the *Estrella del Sur* staggered upright and ran on, and the gorge — the absolute nerve of it — simply continued, as if nothing had happened, its stones whistling their tuneless hymn.

* * *

We came out of the narrows into a wider reach where the water lay down again, exhausted from its own performance, and Noor finally dropped the throttle to idle and put both hands flat on the wheel and stood there breathing like a bellows.

Nobody spoke for a full minute. The engine ticked. Water ran off us in ropes. Somewhere upstream, faintly, the stones were still singing to nobody.

Bruno broke the silence, because of course he did.

"I want it noted," he said, teeth chattering, wrapped in the blanket Pilar was strangling him with, "that I have now been saved from drowning by a cartographer. A *mapmaker*. I am a sailor. This is the most humiliating day of my life and I include the incident with the goat."

"The goat?" Pilar said instantly.

"The goat is not part of this story."

"What goat?"

"There is no goat! Forget the goat!"

I laughed. It came out of me sideways, cracked and gasping, the laugh of a body releasing terror it had nowhere else to put, and once it started I couldn't stop, and then Pilar started, and then even Noor — dry, wrung-out, white-knuckled Noor — made a sound that was almost a laugh, one short bark, quickly suppressed, like a person coughing up something precious by accident.

I sat down hard on the deck, still dripping, and discovered my hands were shaking so badly I could not have held a pencil. My notebook, when I pulled it out, had become a brick of pulp. Every page. Three days of soundings, sketches, notes on the gorge's bearing and width — dissolved into gray porridge.

And here is the strange thing, the thing I have thought about many times since: I did not mourn it.

I looked at that ruined brick of paper, at eleven years of professional habit pulped beyond recovery, and I felt — light. Unburdened. Because I understood, sitting there in a puddle of my own making, that I had crossed some line in the water back there in the narrows, and there was no mapping my way back across it.

I had spent my life documenting the world. Watching it. Writing it down *afterward*, from the safe bank of the page. But when Bruno went into the river, I hadn't reached for the notebook. I had reached for the rail. And the difference between those two gestures, I realized, was the difference between my whole life before and whatever life came next.

A documenter watches. A witness commits. And I was, God help me, committed — to this boat, to this impossible river, to the shaking girl holding the blanket, to the chattering sailor, to the captain at the wheel who had trusted me, in that half-second look, with the only thing Noor had left: the boat, and the memory inside it.

"Noor," I said.

Noor glanced over. Tired eyes. Guarded, always guarded, but less than before. Less every day, in fact, in increments so small you'd need one of my instruments to measure them.

"When we get to Cobre Muerto," I said, "I'm going to need a new notebook. And after that—" I looked upstream, where the gorge bent away northward into country no chart admitted to, "—after that I'm going to need you to teach me the river. Properly. All of it."

Noor looked at me for a long moment. Something moved behind those eyes — surprise, maybe, or the beginning of an answer to a question neither of us had asked aloud yet.

"You nearly died back there," Noor said.

"Yes."

"And your first request is *lessons*."

"My first request was a notebook. Lessons are my second. I prioritize."

Noor shook her head slowly, and the corner of that mouth — the mouth that kept so much locked behind it — twitched upward despite itself. "You're going to get us all killed, Vasquez."

"Probably," I agreed. "But memorably."

* * *

We made camp that night on a gravel bar below the gorge, under a sky so crowded with stars it looked like a spilled cargo. Bruno slept like the dead, snoring with the deep contentment of a man who has very recently been extremely alive. Pilar sat up late copying the current charts by lantern light, mouthing the symbols to herself, and when I corrected her grip on the pencil she didn't argue, just adjusted it, and drew the next line straighter.

Noor stood at the bow for a long time, looking north up the dark river.

I went and stood alongside. Neither of us said anything for a while. The river talked enough for three.

"They'll have seen us run the gorge," Noor said finally. Quietly. "Reyes's launch. There's no other way through. Which means they know exactly where we are, and exactly how much fuel we burned getting here."

"Then we shouldn't waste the night," I said.

Noor turned. In the starlight I could see the question form — *why are you still here, Vasquez? You could still walk out. The road crosses the valley ten kilometers east. You could be safe by Thursday.* I saw Noor decide not to ask it, and I answered it anyway.

"I'm not documenting this anymore," I told the river, or Noor, or both. "Whatever's at the source — whatever they built up there, whatever Calloway-Hurst was too frightened to put in his reports — I'm going to see it. And I'm not going to write it down afterward from a safe distance. I'm going to be standing in it when it counts."

The wind came down the gorge and passed through the stones far above us, and for a moment the whole cliff face breathed its strange, tuneless music into the dark.

Noor listened to it. Then: "Get some sleep, Vasquez. We run at first light."

And far downstream, so faint it might have been imagination — though I have never believed it was — I heard the low diesel knock of a black-hulled launch, patient as the river itself, following us into the dark.
