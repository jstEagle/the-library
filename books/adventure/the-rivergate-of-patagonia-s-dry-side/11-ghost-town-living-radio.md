## 11. Ghost Town, Living Radio

The river told me about Cobre Muerto before I ever saw it.

It told me the way it tells a pilot everything, if you've spent enough years listening — not in words, of course, but in the way the current leaned left where it had no business leaning, and in the smell that came off the water around midday: rust and old smoke and something mineral, like licking a battery. Copper country. I'd run this stretch twice in my life, both times at night, both times because I wanted to be past it before daylight showed me what was there.

Now I brought us into it slow, with the sun high and nowhere to hide, and let it show me everything.

Cobre Muerto sat on a shelf above the east bank like a town that had stood up from its own funeral and forgotten to lie back down. The mine workings gashed the hillside above it — three dark mouths, timbered, silent. Below them: a church with no roof, a row of company houses with their doors swinging, a smelter stack leaning against the sky at an angle that suggested it had given up arguing with gravity years ago. Wind moved through the empty windows and made a sound I didn't care for. Not moaning — nothing so honest as moaning. More like breathing.

"There," Elena said beside me at the wheel. She'd been quiet for an hour, working on her charts, but she came alive the way she always did when there was something to look at properly. "Noor. Look at the landing."

There was a landing. A wooden one, rotted gray, but standing — and tied to it, half-sunk, a rowboat. Government issue. Stenciled numbers on the transom.

I knew those numbers. I'd loaded those crates myself in Puerto Árido, back when this whole business still looked like money instead of what it actually was.

"That's the surveyors' boat," Pilar said from behind us. The kid had a gift for stating the obvious with the reverence of scripture.

"It is," I said.

"Then where are the surveyors?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Four men gone from our camp two nights ago without tracks, without gear, without so much as a cold cup of coffee to show for themselves. And now their boat, here, forty miles upstream of where they'd vanished, tied neatly to a dock in a dead town — which meant somebody had rowed it upriver, or somebody had carried it, and either answer was worse than the other.

I put us alongside the landing gentle as a kiss. Old habit. You don't bang into things you might need to leave from in a hurry.

* * *

Cobre Muerto in daylight was worse than Cobre Muerto would have been in the dark, because in the dark your imagination does you the courtesy of blurring the details. In daylight every detail stood at attention. Boots by a doorway, pairs of them, sized for men. A tin plate on a table through one window, with a fork laid across it neat as Sunday. A child's chalk drawing still ghosting on a schoolhouse wall — a horse, badly done, loved anyway — fifty years faded and refusing to leave.

Bruno went first with the crowbar he'd taken to carrying since the gorge, and I let him, because Bruno was built like a door and doors are meant to go first. Pilar kept to the middle. Elena walked where she pleased, which was everywhere, sketchbook out, photographing the town like it owed her money.

The surveyors' belongings were in the largest of the company houses, arranged too carefully to be accidental. Bedrolls folded. Instruments wrapped in oilcloth and stacked square. Calloway-Hurst's spare spectacles sitting on a shelf next to a tin of tobacco, exactly where a man would keep them if he expected to come back for them.

Or exactly where someone else would put them if they wanted him to think so.

"They didn't leave," Elena said quietly. She was holding one of the bedrolls, and her hands were very still. "Nobody leaves their glasses, Noor. Nobody folds their bedding before they're taken."

"I know."

"And their boat's outside."

"I know that too."

"Say something more than *I know*."

I looked at the spectacles. Small, wire-rimmed, patient. Somewhere behind my ribs, Tomás cleared his throat the way he did whenever I was being dense on purpose, which was his way of loving me and insulting me in the same gesture. *You know what this is,* he said. *You've known since the coffee was cold.*

"This is a larder," I said. "Somebody's keeping them. Or keeping something of them. And whoever it is wanted us to find this house, because there's only one reason to arrange a room this tidy in a town nobody's lived in since before I was born."

Elena set down the bedroll. "Which is?"

"To make sure we stopped."

* * *

We found the radio an hour later, and that's when the day turned from bad to instructive.

It was in the mine office at the top of the row — a proper set, army surplus by the look of it, with a fresh aerial strung from the chimney to a dead poplar. Fresh wire. New solder joints. Somebody had maintained this thing with love, in a town of ghosts, and that arithmetic only worked one way.

Pilar found it first, because Pilar found everything, having been raised by curiosity and fed on it. "Captain! There's power here — look, the dial lamp—"

"Don't touch it."

Too late. The kid's hand was already on the switch, and the lamp glowed amber, and out of the speaker came a voice so calm and so reasonable that my skin tried to leave my body and go on ahead without me.

"—repeat for Station Four. The woman Vasquez and all cartographic materials are to be secured intact. Intact, do you understand, gentlemen; paper burns and memory doesn't, but we prefer the paper. The pilot Thornbury and any crew are expendable. Sink the vessel at your convenience. It's an old boat. The river won't miss it." A pause, courteous as a handshake. "Inspector Reyes, transmitting. Out."

Static. Then silence, except for the wind doing its fake-breathing through the broken houses below.

Nobody said anything for a while. Bruno set the crowbar down very gently, as though it might overhear us.

"Well," I said finally, because somebody had to, and in my experience the captain is elected to say the stupid thing first so everyone else can feel brave. "At least he thinks well of the river."

"Noor." Elena's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a person measuring a distance. "He knows your name. He knows mine. He knows the boat's condition."

"He's had a radio longer than we've had a river."

"He called you *expendable*."

"Yes," I said. "He did. And do you notice what he didn't call you? *Intact.* That's the word he spent his breath on. Whatever Reyes wants, he wants it in your hands and your head, and he wants it undamaged, and that makes you the most valuable cargo on this deck. More than the maps. Maps can be redrawn." I looked at her. "You can't."

She held my gaze the way she held everything — straight on, no flinch, a woman who'd grown up being appraised and had decided to be a nuisance about it. "Then perhaps you should reconsider what you're carrying."

"I've reconsidered plenty. I keep arriving at the same answer."

"Which is?"

"That I'm a terrible businessman," I said, "and triple pay was always a lie I told myself so I could stop counting the cost of things. Cost of Tomás. Cost of the crew I keep losing to better sense. Cost of—" I gestured at the radio, the town, the whole dry cursed slope of the world. "Cost of this."

Pilar, bless the child, chose that moment to be practical. "Captain, if they're relaying orders on this frequency, could we... listen? Learn what they're planning before they plan it?"

I could have kissed the kid, and I said so, and Pilar went the color of the copper the town was named for, and Elena laughed — one short, real laugh, the first in two days — and for about four seconds Cobre Muerto felt almost warm.

Then the radio spoke again.

Not Reyes. A different voice, younger, nervous, transmitting from somewhere downstream. "Station Four confirming. We have visual on the target vessel. They've stopped at Cobre Muerto. Request instruction."

A pause long enough to pour tea in.

And then Reyes, unhurried, pleasant, a man reading a menu: "Let them stop. Ghost towns are restful. Wake me when they reach the narrows — that's the last place the river will do our work for us. Until then, Station Four, you may watch them. Consider it educational."

Click. Static. Silence.

Elena and I looked at each other over the glowing little dial, and I watched her arrive at the same place I'd already been living for some time: we weren't running anymore. We were being *allowed*. Every mile of open water between here and the source was a corridor someone else had measured, and at the end of it waited the narrows, where the river — patient, obedient old river — would do the company's work for free.

"They're watching us right now," Elena said. "Possibly through glass. Possibly from the ridge."

"Probably." I crossed to the office window, took off my hat, and gave the eastern ridge a small, formal bow. "Good afternoon, gentlemen. Lovely country. Terrible hospitality."

"Noor—"

"If they're watching, let them watch a confident pilot. Fear travels. So does its absence." I put the hat back on. "Besides, I meant it. Terrible hospitality. They could have left a kettle."

* * *

We stayed the night, because the alternative was running the narrows in the dark with a company launch somewhere behind us, and even I have limits, though Elena has begun to suspect otherwise.

I posted watches. Bruno took first, Pilar second, and I took the graveyard shift alone, sitting in the mine office with the radio murmuring static like a cat that wouldn't settle, going through Calloway-Hurst's effects one more time looking for anything the man himself might have hidden rather than lost.

I found it just after two. Taped inside the false bottom of his instrument case: a field notebook, water-stained, half the pages torn out — the same page, I'd have sworn, that I'd watched him burn on the second night out. What remained was coordinates. Repeated over and over, in a hand that deteriorated down the pages like a man writing while walking, or while running. Coordinates for a lake that appears on no chart I've ever seen, above a reach that every map politely pretends is desert.

And beneath the last entry, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*They are not lost. They are kept. Tell the woman — the source is dammed. Tell her it was never a survey. Tell her I'm sorry. Tell her—*

Nothing. The sentence just stops, mid-word, the pen dragged sideways off the page.

I sat with that for a long time. Outside, the wind breathed through the empty town, and the radio hissed, and somewhere downstream a man named Reyes slept the sleep of the patient and the paid.

When I came down to relieve the watch at dawn, the first thing I noticed was the light — wrong, thin, the kind of morning that arrives already apologizing. The second thing I noticed was Bruno, standing at the foot of the ladder, and the expression on his face, which was the expression of a man who has counted heads twice and gotten the same terrible answer both times.

"Captain," he said. "It's Calloway-Hurst. His bedroll's gone. His boots are gone."

I felt something in my chest turn over — hope, of all the traitorous organs, hoping the man had run, had swum, had sprouted wings.

"And Captain," Bruno went on, quieter, "the rowboat's untied."

We went down to the landing together. The government boat was loose against the pilings, bumping soft and idiotic in the current, empty. No oars missing. No note. Just the gray morning, the breathing wind, and a trail of wet boot prints leading away from the water's edge — heading not downstream, not toward the road, but up. Into the hills. Toward the coordinates. Toward the lake that wasn't on any chart, and the dam that wasn't on any survey, and whatever was left of a man who had burned one page of the truth and then, at the end, taped the rest of it shut and walked into the high country alone.

Elena came down the bank at a run with the notebook I'd left under her pillow for safekeeping already open in her hands, and she read the last line, and she read the stopping point of it, and she looked up at me with her jaw set the way it sets when she's decided something the world hasn't approved yet.

"The narrows," she said. "Reyes said wake him when they reach the narrows. So we don't give him the courtesy."

I looked upstream, where the river bent out of sight between walls of red stone, and I thought about Tomás, who had taught me that the river takes what hesitates, and I thought about a broken man limping into the fog with half an apology in his pocket, and I heard myself say the thing I'd been circling for eleven days like a moth around a lamp:

"Pilar. Wake the engine. We're going through."
