## 12. Two Against the Current

The road out of Cobre Muerto was a scar of white gravel climbing away from the river toward a pass that, on clear days, let you see all the way to the coast. Pilar had found it on the second morning, standing on the rail with one hand shading her eyes.

"There's a truck," she said. "Or there was. There are tire tracks, anyway."

Elena climbed up beside her and looked. The tracks ran from the ghost town's collapsed loading dock, over two hundred meters of broken ground, and out through the pass. Somewhere at the end of them was a telegraph office, a bus line, a train, a life with walls around it and a door that locked. Somewhere at the end of them was everything sensible.

"Someone could walk it in a day," Pilar said. "Maybe two, with the wind."

"Two," Elena agreed.

Below them, Noor was down in the engine well with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder, elbow-deep in the guts of the *Mariposa*, swearing at a fuel line in three languages. The two paid-off crewmen — Sosa and Ferreyra, who had taken their wages in silver because they no longer trusted paper — were already an hour gone up the road, their duffels bouncing on their backs, not once looking back at the boat they'd helped keep alive for eleven years. Noor hadn't watched them go either. She'd counted out the coins, shaken each man's hand, and then walked straight to the engine well as if the engine were the only thing in the world that had ever asked nothing of her.

Three of them now. A captain, a cartographer, and a girl of seventeen who had signed onto a government survey expecting steady meals and had instead gotten gunrunners' manifests, a haunted pilot, and a river full of men who wanted them sunk.

Elena looked at the tire tracks a long time.

"Two days," she said again, mostly to herself.

* * *

She spent the middle of the day doing what she always did when she couldn't decide: drawing. She sat in the wheelhouse with the chart table cleared and worked on the gorge survey from memory — the whistling stones reach, where Bruno had gone over the side and come back by the width of a hand; the narrows below Cobre Muerto where the current ran green and silent and deep. Her pencil moved without her permission. It had been doing that since Estancia Salada, since the old woman's map, since the words *the river remembers who drinks first* had settled into her chest like a stone into a well.

Noor came up the ladder at midday, wiping her hands on a rag that had given up being clean sometime the previous decade.

"The fuel line's held together with optimism and copper wire," she announced. "Which is more than I can say for most things on this boat." She dropped into the chair across the chart table, and her eyes went immediately to what Elena was drawing, and then to Elena, and stayed there a moment too long. "You've been sitting here four hours."

"I've been working four hours."

"You've been deciding four hours. There's a difference. Your left foot does this thing—" Noor tapped her own boot against the deck twice, a small nervous drum, "—when you're arguing with yourself. You've been drumming since breakfast."

Elena put down the pencil. "And what does your left foot do?"

"My left foot," Noor said with dignity, "has never argued with anyone in its life. That's my problem exactly."

It should have been easier than this. Elena had made hard decisions before — leaving Buenos Aires, taking Calloway-Hurst's money, photographing those manifests in the hold while the crew sang work songs overhead. But every one of those decisions had been *away* from something. This one was *toward*, and toward-choices weighed differently. Toward-choices you had to carry.

"Pilar found the road," Elena said.

Noor's face did something complicated and then settled into careful blankness, which was how Noor said the important things. "Good road?"

"Good enough."

"Well." Noor stood, crossed to the window, looked out at the dead town with its rusted headframe and its streets full of blowing sand. "Then I suppose this is where I say the sensible thing. The company launch is somewhere downstream with Inspector Reyes aboard, and we have eleven days of concession left, and a field book describing a dam that isn't supposed to exist, and a crew of three, one of whom is legally a child. The sensible thing is that you take the road. You take the field book pages that matter — I can copy them tonight — and you walk out, and you get to a telegraph, and you do the part of this fight that doesn't require drowning."

"And you?"

"I go upstream." Noor said it lightly, the way she said most true things. "Somebody has to see it. Somebody has to stand at that lake and look Reyes in the eye and know what he built. Even if nobody believes us after. Even if—" She stopped. Adjusted something on the window frame that didn't need adjusting. "Even if it goes the way rivers usually go, for me."

"You mean the way it went for Tomás."

"I didn't say that."

"You were about to."

"Elena." Noor turned from the window, and for once she didn't deflect, didn't joke, didn't hide behind the boat or the river or the manifest of her own guilt. She just looked at Elena with those gray eyes that had seen too much water. "I have spent nine years moving so fast that grief couldn't catch me. And it has been — I want to be honest with you, because you'll catch me lying anyway — it has been the easiest thing in the world, these last weeks, to stop running. Do you understand what that costs me? To tell you to leave? Every part of me that has wanted anything in nine years wants you on this boat going upstream, and that is precisely why you should take the road. Because if you stay and the river takes you, I will not survive becoming the person who let that happen. Tomás was enough. You would finish me."

There it was. Not a declaration — Noor Galloway did not declare things, she bled them out sideways — but underneath it, plain as bedrock under sandbar: *I love you, which is why I am trying to give you away.*

Elena picked her pencil back up. Her hands were steadier than she expected.

"Let me tell you about the safe life," she said. "Because you keep offering it to me like it's a gift, and I don't think you know what's actually in the box."

* * *

What was in the box was this: a house in La Plata belonging to her uncle Rodrigo, who had arranged her marriage to a man named Ernesto Olazábal, who owned half the wool presses in the south and whose conversation, in the three times Elena had endured him, had consisted entirely of the prices of things. What was in the box was a wedding her mother had already begun sewing for. What was in the box was a life in which Elena Vasquez drew maps that other people signed, in rooms with good light, and never saw the places on them, and grew old being told she was fortunate.

"I took this job to run from that," Elena said. "You know that. I told you on the night anchorage, and you told me about Tomás, and we agreed — didn't we — that running and escaping are different animals."

"We agreed," Noor said warily.

"So hear me. If I walk out that pass tomorrow, I'm not escaping anything. I'm just running in a prettier circle. I go to La Plata, I marry the wool presses, I spend forty years drawing other people's rivers, and every night I lie awake knowing that twenty kilometers upstream from a town called Cobre Muerto there is a lake that doesn't appear on any map, and men with rifles guarding it, and a woman on a rotten old boat who taught me that a person can refuse." Elena stood. The wheelhouse felt suddenly small, the way it did before weather. "You want to protect me from the river. Fine. But you don't get to protect me from myself. I'm not cargo, Noor. I'm crew."

For a long moment Noor said nothing. Then, very quietly, with the ghost of the smile that Elena had been cataloguing for weeks like a rare bird:

"Crew gets paid worse."

"Crew gets a vote."

"Crew," Noor said, "drowns at the same rate as captains. It's one of the few egalitarian features of the profession."

"Then we'll drown equally. How modern of us."

Noor laughed — a short, surprised sound, wrung out of her like water from a rope — and then pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes, and Elena understood that she was laughing so she wouldn't do something else entirely. When Noor lowered her hand her face was open in a way Elena had never seen it, unguarded as the river at dawn.

"All right," Noor said. "All right. Upstream. Both of us. God help Pilar, having to watch this."

"Watch what?" said Pilar, from the top of the ladder, where she had absolutely been standing for some time.

"Nothing," they said together, and Pilar grinned like a cat.

* * *

They told her properly that evening, over the galley stove, with the tin plates out and the last of the good coffee. Pilar took the news the way she took everything — first practically, then ferociously.

"So it's three of us," she said, counting on her fingers. "Against a company launch, a dam, an inspector, and whatever happened to five grown surveyors who vanished off solid ground."

"That's the shape of it," Noor said.

"And Sosa and Ferreyra took the road."

"They did."

Pilar chewed on this. Elena watched her — the youngest of them, the one who still folded her wages into her boot, who had cried the night Bruno went overboard and denied it ever after. She expected fear. What she got instead was the girl setting down her cup with great deliberation and saying:

"Good. Fewer people to split the glory."

"Pilar—"

"No, listen, Captain. I've thought about it. I thought about it all day, actually, while you two were up in the wheelhouse having your—" she waved a hand vaguely at the space between Noor and Elena, "—whatever that was. My mother says a person who turns back at the halfway mark remembers the turning back longer than she'd have remembered the drowning. She says it about sheep dogs, but I think it applies." She shrugged, suddenly shy. "Besides. Someone has to keep the engine alive, and you two would kill it inside a week arguing about whose fault the fuel line is."

Noor opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked at Elena, who raised both eyebrows in perfect innocence.

"She's not wrong about the fuel line," Elena said.

"The fuel line is *fine*—"

"The fuel line is held together with copper wire and prayer, Captain, I read the maintenance log."

"The maintenance log is a *suggestion*—"

And just like that the wheelhouse-galley was warm again, the three of them bent over charts and engine diagrams, arguing and laughing, and Elena felt the strange lightness that comes after a decision is finally made — not the absence of fear, but the discovery that fear and purpose can share a body without tearing it apart.

Later, when Pilar had gone forward to her bunk, Elena stayed at the chart table finishing the gorge survey by lamplight. Noor came and stood beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and studied the penciled lines.

"What will you call it?" Noor asked. "When you publish. The reach past the boundary. The forbidden one."

Elena had thought about this for days. She wrote it now in the margin, small and certain: *Vasquez Reach.*

"After yourself? That's not modest."

"It's not modesty I'm after." Elena capped the pen. "Maps lie by omission, Noor. Whole valleys disappear because nobody was willing to put their name on the truth. Well — somebody is willing now. Let the name sit there where the company lawyers can see it. Let them know exactly who witnessed them."

Noor was quiet a moment. Then she reached over and, with the pencil, added a single word beneath Elena's inscription, in her blunt pilot's handwriting: *witnessed.* And beneath that, almost too small to read: *and kept.*

Elena looked at her. "Kept?"

"The map. The names. The ones the river took." Noor's voice was low. "Tomás. Bruno nearly. Those five men in the tents. Somebody has to keep them, Elena, or the river keeps them instead, and the river keeps nothing — it just moves things along. That's what I finally understand. I spent nine years thinking staying still was death. But there's more than one way to stop moving. You can stop running, or you can stop carrying. I'd like to try the first one. With you. After."

"After," Elena said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a promise either, because neither of them knew yet what lay between here and the source, and honest people don't promise past the next bend. Still, Noor heard what was in it. Her hand found Elena's on the chart table and stayed there, rough palm against ink-stained fingers, while the lamp hissed and the river muttered against the hull in the dark.

* * *

They cast off at first light, when the fog still lay on the water like a held breath. Pilar took the bow with the sounding pole; Elena stood at the rail with her notebook open, mapping as they went, because that was the vow now — witness everything, lose nothing. Noor stood at the wheel, and there was something new in the set of her shoulders. Not lighter, exactly. Aimed.

Behind them, the white scar of the road climbed away over the pass, empty, going nowhere they needed anymore.

Ahead, the river narrowed and turned north into country no chart admitted to, and after an hour Pilar's voice came back along the deck, tight with something between alarm and wonder:

"Captain! There's wire across the water ahead — and a sign nailed to a post, fresh paint!" She squinted, reading. "It says *Prohibited Reach — Concession Property — Entry Forbidden by Order.*"

Noor didn't slow the engines. If anything, Elena thought she heard them deepen, the old *Mariposa* leaning into the current like a horse that smells home.

"How fresh?" Noor called back.

"Paint's barely dry!"

"Good," Noor said, and Elena saw her grin — sharp and bright and utterly without fear, the grin of a woman who had finally stopped running and started arriving. "That means they're worried. Nobody paints warnings for people they aren't afraid of."

The fog thickened. The wire came on, gleaming wet out of the white.
