## 13. The Permit Clock

Eleven days. I counted them the way a man counts coins he knows are short — twice in the morning, once at night, and always coming up the same number short.

The concession expired on the twenty-eighth of the month. That was printed on the notice Elena had photographed off Calloway-Hurst's manifest, and Elena had it by heart anyway, because Elena kept numbers the way other people kept grudges: close, warm, and ready to use. Eleven days to run the last hundred and forty kilometers of the Rio Seco, past the posted boundary, into a reach that no chart admitted existed, and find whatever it was the company had built up there in the dark of the headwaters.

Pilar asked me once, scrubbing rust off the winch drum, whether I was counting too.

"I don't count," I said. "Counting is for people who intend to arrive."

"And you?"

"I intend," I said, "to keep moving until the river decides otherwise."

That was the kind of thing I said back then. It sounded like freedom if you didn't look at it too long. Pilar looked at it too long — sixteen-year-olds always do — and then went back to the rust without another word, which is more mercy than most people my age deserve.

* * *

The lock at Paso Herradura was the last working piece of government between us and nowhere. The river above it narrowed into the gorge country; below it lay three hundred kilometers of brown water, sheep estancias, and the slow rot of towns that had sold their water and were only now learning the price. The lock itself was a concrete throat cut into the step of the plateau, forty meters of lift, hand-cranked gates, and a lockkeeper named Ferreyra who had held the post so long that the government had forgotten he existed, which he considered the highest form of job security.

We came down to it in the late afternoon with the light going amber on the water and the wind doing what the wind does on the dry side of Patagonia, which is everything it wants.

"Gate's open," Ferreyra called from the catwalk, before we'd even thrown him a line. "Been open since noon. You're expected."

I felt that sentence go through me like cold water through a boot.

"Expected by whom?" Elena asked, coming up beside me with her chart case under her arm. She'd been drawing the gorge approaches all morning, bent over the table in the wheelhouse with her pencil making small, certain sounds, and she had that look she got when the world confirmed something she'd already suspected — not surprise, exactly. Inventory.

"Man came yesterday," Ferreyra said, cranking the gate wider with an ease that suggested the mechanism got more exercise than his conscience. "Black launch. Polite fellow. Left word that the *Santa Rosa* would be along and should be received courteously." He looked down at us, and there was something in his face I didn't like — not fear, exactly. The memory of fear, worn smooth like a stone carried in a pocket. "He paid the lock fee. For you."

"He paid our lock fee," Elena repeated.

"Three years' tolls," Ferreyra said. "Said you'd be in a hurry and shouldn't be delayed over money." He shrugged, a big slow shrug that moved his whole body like a hillside settling. "I took it. A man would be a fool not to take it."

"A man might also wonder why," Elena said.

Ferreyra smiled with half his mouth. "Señora, I've wondered about this river for thirty years. Wondering never once changed where the water went." He waved us in. "Come on, then. Chamber's filling. You've got maybe an hour on the lift, and your courteous friend said he'd call again tonight."

* * *

The lock chamber is a strange place to spend an hour. You sit in a box of concrete while the river is lifted around you, and the walls sweat, and the water rises in silence except for the seep singing through cracks, and you have nothing to do but think, which on my boat has always been the most dangerous cargo aboard.

Elena used the hour properly. She laid out her charts on the foredeck table and weighted them with brass fittings and worked, pencil whispering, transferring the old woman's hand-drawn map of the upper gorge onto proper survey paper — the contours, the side channels, the place marked with a small fierce X and the words *aquí se esconde el agua*, here the water hides itself. Pilar sat cross-legged nearby, holding the corners flat against the wind, watching Elena's hands with the total absorption of someone being taught without being told they're being taught.

"You see how the contour lines crowd here?" Elena was saying. "Crowded lines mean steep. Steep means the current stacks up against the outside of the bend. So you read the lines and you know where the river will try to kill you before you ever see the water."

"The river tries to kill you everywhere," Pilar said.

"Yes," Elena agreed pleasantly. "But it's polite enough to announce where. Only nobody taught you the announcements." She tapped the map. "This is the announcement. Learn to hear it and the river becomes almost honest. Almost."

I stood at the rail and watched them and did not count the days, and thought about Tomás, who had taught me the river's announcements one at a time, patiently, the way you teach someone a language you love, and who had then gone into the water at the flood at Las Juntas and stayed there. Keep moving, I'd promised him. That was the whole promise. Not survive it. Not make sense of it. Just keep moving, so the guilt couldn't catch up.

And now here I was, sitting still in a concrete box, rising.

* * *

They came at dusk, when the chamber had lifted us level with the upper pool and the first stars were out over the plateau. The black launch slid out of the shadow of the far bank without lights, quiet as a rumor, and tied off alongside with fenders already placed, as though its crew had known our freeboard to the centimeter.

Inspector Reyes stepped aboard without asking. He was a compact man in a coat too good for the river, gray at the temples, with the unhurried movements of someone who had never once needed to hurry because everyone else always did it for him. He removed his hat. He complimented the boat.

"She's older than she looks," he said, running a gloved hand along the rail. "Or younger. Hard to tell with riverboats. They age from the inside." He turned to me. "Captain Thornbury. We corresponded, in a manner of speaking. I paid your tolls."

"I heard."

"Do you object?"

"It seemed rude, since you'd already done it."

His smile was genuine, which was the worst thing about it. "Good. I admire economy. May I?" He gestured at the deck chairs, and sat in one without waiting for the answer, arranging himself as though the chair had been built for him and the boat merely happened to be nearby. Elena came forward from the chart table and stood at my shoulder, arms crossed, and Pilar drifted near the wheelhouse door with a boathook held loosely, hopefully, like a boy pretending not to hold a weapon.

Reyes noticed all of it. He noticed everything; you could see him noticing, filing, and being untroubled by any of it.

"Eleven days," he said. "You've been counting. Everyone counts. It's human. The permit expires on the twenty-eighth, and after that, this entire reach reverts to open concession — which is to say, to us, cleanly, legally, permanently. Anything found above the boundary after that date belongs to the company by right of grant. Anything found before—" he spread his hands, "—is a complication. Complications are expensive. For everyone."

"We're a survey team," Elena said. "Government charter."

"Madam Vasquez." He inclined his head to her with real courtesy. "Your government charter consists of five men, four of whom appear to have evaporated, one of whom has confessed in writing to fraud, and a crate of drilling equipment no honest survey ever needed. Your government, I regret to inform you, has been informed of none of this, and will be informed of considerably less. Buenos Aires is nine hundred kilometers away and thinks of this river the way one thinks of a toothache — occasionally, and with a wish that it would simply stop." He turned back to me. "So. Captain. Let us speak plainly, because I can see you're a plain woman, and I confess I'm tired of talking to men who aren't."

"Talk," I said.

"I am authorized to offer you amnesty. Full amnesty — the boat, the girl, yourself, all records of your involvement dissolved. In addition: ten thousand pesos, cash, tonight, and a concession of your own. A ferry license on the lower river. Puerto Árido to Estancia Salada, exclusive rights, in perpetuity." He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; you could hear him doing it. "A fixed route, Captain. A fixed home. No more sandbars shifting under you in the night. No more running. You'd stop moving at last. Isn't that what you want? My people tell me you've never stayed anywhere longer than a week."

The chamber seeped and sang around us. Somewhere below, the water we'd risen on went on without us.

"They tell you a lot, your people," I said.

"They earn their wages." He leaned forward slightly. "Consider what's above you. The gorge. The whistling stones. The dead town with its radio. And beyond the boundary, a reach that isn't on any chart, guarded by men who are paid precisely to ensure it stays that way. You have eleven days, a failing boat, a crew of two, and a cartographer whose value to you is entirely dependent on her remaining alive and free — which, I'll note, is also her value to me, and I pay better." He rose, unhurried, and put his hat back on. "Take the offer, and tomorrow morning you wake up rich, safe, and finished. Refuse, and tomorrow morning you wake up exactly where you are — which is inside my patience."

"And how long does your patience last?"

He smiled again, that genuine smile, and looked up at the stars as though consulting them.

"Longer than eleven days, Captain. That's rather the point." He tipped his hat to Elena, nodded to Pilar — who gripped the boathook tighter and achieved, to her credit, a glare of real hostility — and stepped back onto his launch. At the rail he paused. "One courtesy more. There's a lock keeper downstream who talks too much, and a radio in Cobre Muerto that works better than it should. We'll attend to both. You'll want to hurry. Not because I'm chasing you." He settled the hat. "Because I'm not. The river above does my chasing for me, and it has never once accepted a bribe."

The launch pulled away into the dark, running without lights, and the sound of its engine faded until it was indistinguishable from the wind.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while. The chamber held us at the top of the lift, and Ferreyra's voice came faintly across the water: *"Gates opening! Mind your approach!"*

Elena broke the silence first, in the tone of a woman setting out facts like dishes.

"Ten thousand pesos."

"I heard."

"An exclusive ferry license."

"I heard that too."

"A fixed route." She looked at me sideways. "Noor. That's everything you say you want. He wasn't lying — that part I'd swear to. He could have made that offer true by Thursday."

"That's what frightens you?"

"What frightens me," Elena said, gathering her charts into their case with quick precise motions, "is that I watched your face when he said it, and for one full second you weren't here. You were on some lower river, moored at Salada, and it was winter, and you were *still*." She snapped the case shut. "I need you here, Captain. All of you. Whatever's left after Tomás — I need it upstream."

The gates opened onto the upper pool, black water breathing under the stars, and the gorge mouth beyond it, a darker darkness, waiting.

"Ferreyra!" I called across to the catwalk. "How deep's the pool above the narrows?"

"Deep enough to drown a company man," he called back cheerfully, "if you bring him up this far."

"Good enough." I took the wheel. "Pilar — steam. Elena — get your light on the channel markers, such as they are. We run the narrows tonight, while his patience is busy elsewhere."

Elena paused at the wheelhouse door. "You refused him."

"I did."

"Why?"

I thought about giving her the noble answer, and then thought better of it, because she'd have seen through it in a heartbeat and respected me less for offering.

"Because he was right about one thing," I said. "I do want to stop moving. And I'll do it when I choose, on water I choose — not bought off the backs of four drowned surveyors and a town with dry wells." I eased the throttle forward and the *Santa Rosa* shuddered awake beneath us, old heart hammering. "Tomás gave this river everything so I could keep moving. Seems poor thanks to sell the stopping to the man who killed him."

Elena looked at me a moment longer than she needed to. Then she went forward to the lamp, and the beam swung out over the black water, and the narrows opened ahead of us like a held breath.

It was only later — much later, in the dead hours, with the gorge walls closing in and the engine running hotter than it should — that Pilar came to me quietly and said the thing I'd been refusing to think:

"Captain. The engine. Listen to it."

I listened.

Under the familiar knock of the old motor there was a new note, thin and wrong, like a voice trying to sing through gritted teeth. I knew that sound. Every river pilot on the dry side knows it, and prays never to hear it in the dark, in a gorge, with eleven days left and a patient man somewhere behind.

Salt. Salt in the engine.

Above us, the whistling stones began to sing.
