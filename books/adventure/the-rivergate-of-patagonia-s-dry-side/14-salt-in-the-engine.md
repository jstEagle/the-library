## 14. Salt in the Engine

The engine died on a Tuesday, which Pilar considered a personal insult, because Tuesdays were supposed to be the boring day of the week.

One moment the *Rosa del Río* was pushing upstream through a reach so wide and flat it might have been a lake that had gotten lost, and the next moment there was a sound like a sheep being stepped on, a shudder that ran up through the deck plates and into Pilar's boots, and then silence. Not the good kind of silence, either. Not the Sunday-morning kind. The kind of silence that has a price tag attached.

"No," said Pilar, to the engine, to the river, to God, and possibly to the heron standing on the bank watching them with the smug expression herons reserve for other people's misfortunes.

"No what?" called Elena from the bow, where she had been bent over her charts with a pencil behind her ear.

"The engine."

"Ah." Elena came aft with the unhurried stride of a woman who had already decided the world was mostly trouble and saw no reason to be surprised by a new installment. She peered down into the engine well. "Is it supposed to look like that?"

Pilar looked. The engine — Bruno's engine, the captain's engine, the engine Pilar had been learning piece by piece the way you learn a difficult relative — was wearing a crust of something white and crusty along the water line, like a man who'd fallen asleep on a beach.

"That's salt," Pilar said. "That's — we're a hundred and fifty leagues from the sea. How is there *salt*?"

"Because the river is dying of thirst," Elena said, "and what's left of it is getting concentrated. Like soup left on the stove." She said it lightly, but her pencil had stopped moving, and Pilar had learned by now that when Elena's pencil stopped moving, the news was bad.

From the wheelhouse came the captain's voice, flat and calm in the way that Pilar had learned to read as the exact opposite of calm: "Pilar. Tell me what you see."

Pilar told her. Noor appeared at the engine well, looked down, and said nothing for a long time, which from Noor was a sermon.

"Salt in the jackets," Noor said at last. "It's fouled the intake and it's eating the pump. I can strip it and clean it, but not running. Not today, maybe not tomorrow."

"Dead water," Pilar said. "We're in dead water. The current's nothing. We'll just sit here."

"We'll just sit here," Noor agreed, and something in the way she said it made Pilar's stomach do a small, cold roll. The captain had a talent for agreeing with bad news in a voice that made it worse.

* * *

They drifted for three days.

That was the thing about dead water that nobody warned you about, Pilar thought: it didn't feel like danger. It felt like nothing. The river spread itself thin across a basin the color of old pewter, so flat that the sky lay on it twice over, and the *Rosa* turned slowly on her own reflection like a leaf deciding whether to commit to falling. The heat came down and didn't leave. The horizon got so familiar Pilar felt she'd memorized it, and then felt insulted that it never changed.

They fished. That was the plan, such as it was: fish, ration the stores, fix the engine. The fishing was the part that went wrong.

"Are those the fish?" Elena asked on the second day, holding one up by the line. It was the length of Pilar's hand, silvery, with an eye like a dropped bead of glass.

"That's a fish," Pilar said.

"It's an *appetizer*."

"It's a whole fish. In the old days at home, my mother would have made three of these feed six people."

"Your mother," Elena said, "was a magician, and I honor her." But she cleaned them anyway, with the quick precise hands she used for everything, and salted them from the small tin Noor guarded like a relic — irony being, as Elena observed, apparently the river's favorite seasoning — and they ate them with hard bread, and they were hungry again by mid-afternoon.

The rationing was Noor's department, and Noor ran it like a customs house. Two biscuits at morning, one at night. Water measured out with a tin cup and a level eye. Pilar caught herself watching the captain pour and thinking, not for the first time, that there was something in Noor that understood scarcity the way fish understood water — from the inside, without ever having to be taught.

"You're staring," Noor said, without looking up.

"I'm learning," Pilar said. "That's different."

"Is it?" Noor handed her the cup, exactly full, no more. "In my experience they look the same from the outside."

Pilar didn't have an answer for that, so she drank her water.

* * *

It was on the third day, with the engine half-stripped across the deck like a patient on an operating table and the sun doing its patient, terrible work, that Elena sat down beside Pilar on the hatch cover and said, "Show me what you see."

Pilar looked at the river. "Water."

"Wonderful. A cartographer's salary, wasted." Elena unrolled one of her charts — a working chart, soft as cloth now from being handled, with the pencil ghosting over it in Elena's small, upright hand. "No. What do you *see*. Not the water. The water's just the messenger. What's it telling you?"

Pilar squinted. The dead basin lay around them, featureless, and she said so.

"Featureless," Elena agreed. "And yet." Her pencil touched the chart. "Here. See this line? That's not a shore. That's where the reeds change color. Reeds change color when the bottom changes. And here —" the pencil slid left — "the water's the same color as everything else to your eye, but it's warmer. Feel it? Warm water comes off shallow ground. Cold water comes out of channels. You can't see the channel, but you can taste it on the air, if you're paying attention. The river is always telling you where it's been. Most people just don't listen."

"And you listen."

"I draw," Elena said. "Listening is the captain's job. Drawing is mine. Every pilot has their own way of keeping the river in their head. Noor keeps it as a feeling. I keep it as a picture. You'll have to find your own."

"What if my way is just being scared?"

Elena laughed — a real laugh, quick and unguarded, and it did something warm to the air. "Then you'll be in excellent company. Fear's the oldest navigation instrument there is. It's just a matter of calibrating it." She put the pencil in Pilar's hand. "Here. Mark where you think the deep channel runs. Doesn't matter if you're wrong. Wrong is how a chart gets honest."

Pilar drew a line. It was, she was fairly sure, a stupid line.

Elena looked at it for a long moment. Then she took her own pencil and made one small correction — one, no more — and said, "Huh."

"*Huh* what?"

"Huh, you were nearly right, and the part where you were wrong, you were wrong in an interesting way." She tapped Pilar's mark. "You put the channel against the far bank. Everyone puts it midstream, because midstream feels deep. But in a dying reach like this, the current hugs the cut bank where the old flood did its carving. You read the *shape* of the land, not the habit of rivers." She said it the way a person reports a strange weather event: with surprise, and a little respect.

Pilar tried very hard to keep her face neutral and knew she was failing. "Do that again."

"Do what?"

"The thing where you say something nice and then pretend you didn't."

"I said nothing nice. I said you were interestingly wrong. Ask anyone; that's an insult in three languages." But Elena was smiling down at the chart, and she didn't take the pencil back.

* * *

On the fourth day the fish stopped biting, and hunger stopped being an idea and became a roommate.

It was a polite roommate, at first. It sat quietly in the corners of the day. But by the afternoon it had opinions about everything, and Pilar found herself irritable at the heron, at the sun, at the chart, at Elena's humming, at Noor's silence, at the engine most of all. Her stomach had developed a language of its own, a slow grinding dialect, and it spoke constantly.

"We could row," Pilar said.

"With what?" Noor asked. "We have two oars and a boat that draws a meter and a half. We'd row for a month."

"We could walk."

"Into that?" Noor nodded at the shore, where the scrub stood gray and brittle as wire. "There's no water out there. There's no anything out there. The river's the only road, and the road's closed for repairs."

"Then we sit here and starve politely."

"No," said Elena, from the engine well, where she'd been handing Noor tools all day. "We sit here and starve *impolitely*. There's a difference, and the difference is the pump." She emerged, wiping her hands, and held up a small brass fitting, gleaming where she'd scoured it with sand and vinegar from the stores. "The intake's clear. The jackets are flushed. If the pump seals hold — and I've cut new gaskets from the cork lining of the chart case, which the charts will forgive me for, eventually — we might turn her over tomorrow."

"You fixed it?" Pilar said. "You're a map person."

"I'm a person," Elena said, "who spent two years of an arranged engagement being taught domestic chemistry by a aunt who believed a woman should be able to do everything and admit to nothing. The engine is just a stubborn stove that moves." She looked at the fitting in her palm, and something moved across her face — not sadness exactly, more like the shadow of a door closing gently somewhere far away. "She'd have liked this boat, my aunt. She'd have liked the captain, too. She had no patience for people who couldn't sit still, though. She'd have said Noor runs from things."

"And what do you say?" Pilar asked, before she could stop herself.

Elena was quiet. Out on the flat water, the evening came down like a held breath.

"I say," she said at last, "that some people run from things, and some people run *toward* things, and from the outside, at speed, they look identical. Ask me again when we get where we're going."

* * *

The engine turned over at dawn on the fifth day.

It coughed twice, caught, and settled into a rhythm so rough and so ugly that Pilar wanted to apologize to it, but it turned, and the *Rosa del Río* leaned her shoulder into the current and began, at a speed a grandmother could outwalk, to move upstream.

Noor stood at the wheel and did not smile. Pilar had learned that about her too: the captain's joy came out sideways, as a loosening of the shoulders, a longer stride, a second biscuit at breakfast "because the stores are getting light anyway and we may as well enjoy the fall." But she stood at that wheel with her hands easy and her eyes forward, and Pilar thought: there. That's what happy looks like on this boat. Learn it. It's rare.

They ate the last of the fish — two of them, small ones, split four ways with the heron getting nothing, though Noor did mutter something about throwing it "a share of our good fortune" and Pilar couldn't tell if she was joking. Probably not. That was the thing about this boat, Pilar was beginning to understand. It was a strange little nation of three, with its own laws and its own weather, and somewhere in the last five days of drift and hunger and salt, without anyone signing anything, Pilar had been made a citizen. Not crew. Not hired help. The kind of person whose wrong answers got corrected with one pencil mark instead of a lecture. The kind of person whose biscuit was counted.

She sat down next to Elena at the bow with the working chart spread across both their knees, and Elena put the pencil in her hand without being asked.

"Your turn," Elena said. "Mark the channel."

Pilar studied the water ahead. The basin was narrowing now; the banks were beginning to lean in; somewhere past that bend, Elena's charts said, the dead water ended and the river remembered how to be a river. She could feel it, almost — a coldness on the air off the upstream reach, the taste of moving water. She thought about reed color and warm shallows and the shape of cut banks, and she drew her line, and it came out confident, and she was surprised to find that she mostly believed it.

"Better," Elena said. "Much better. You're wrong, of course."

"Of course."

"But you're wrong *closer*." Elena smiled and shaded the correction in with two strokes, and Pilar watched the chart become true under her hand and thought that this, too, was a kind of navigation — that maybe this was what Elena had been doing to all of them since Puerto Árido, marking where they were, correcting by small degrees, never saying the nice thing out loud.

They rounded the bend in the late morning, and the dead water fell away behind them, and the current took the *Rosa*'s hull in its old familiar grip.

And there, standing on the shingle at the head of the next riffle, thin as wire and swaying like something the wind had carried in, was a man in the rags of a gentleman's suit.

Pilar heard Noor's breath stop at the wheel. She heard Elena say, very quietly, "That's impossible," in the voice of a woman who knows exactly who it is.

"Captain," Pilar said. "Who is that?"

Noor brought the boat toward the bank with hands that were not quite steady, and said, "That's a dead man. That's Calloway-Hurst. He walked out of the high country — or something did."
