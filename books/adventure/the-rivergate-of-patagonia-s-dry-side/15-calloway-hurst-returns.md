## 15. Calloway-Hurst Returns

The wind came down off the high country that evening carrying a smell I hadn't smelled since I was a boy on my father's boat — the smell of snowmelt, of real water, cold and mineral and old. It shouldn't have been possible. We were still forty miles below the source by Elena's best guess, camped on a gravel bar where the Rio Seco ran thin as spilled tea over stones the color of old bone.

"That's glacier smell," I said to Elena, who was sitting on the deck rail mending a net she had no use for, because her hands needed something to do the way other people's hands need cigarettes.

"Or it's the smell of a place that's lying about itself," she said. She'd taken to talking like that lately. The river does it to people. It did it to me twenty years ago and never gave me back.

Pilar came up from the engine hatch wiping grease on a rag that had started life white and was now more black than cloth. "She'll turn over," Pilar announced, with the solemnity of a doctor delivering good news about a tumor. "Eventually. When she feels like it."

"She's a woman of principle, then," Elena said.

"She's a rusted-out relic held together by prayer and baling wire," I said, "and I'll thank you not to insult her in her hearing." I patted the deckhouse. The *Tomás* — named for a man who'd have laughed himself sick at what we were doing with his boat — shuddered agreeably, or maybe that was just the current pushing at her hull.

We'd been beached two days waiting on the fuel pump, and I will admit that for the first day I'd paced like a cat in a sack. Two days stopped, with Reyes somewhere behind us and eleven days on the concession clock bleeding out like a stuck pig. But there's a thing the river teaches you if you let it: sometimes dead water is just the river telling you to sit down and listen. By the second day I'd almost managed to hear it.

Then the dog started up.

We didn't have a dog. That was the first strange thing. The sound came from upstream, from the willow scrub at the top of the gravel bar — a thin, cracked barking, the kind of noise an animal makes when it's been doing it too long and can't stop. Pilar went for the lantern. Elena put her hand flat on my arm, which is a thing she does instead of saying *wait*, and I've come to understand it as one of the most eloquent sentences in any language.

The shape came out of the scrub sideways, the way men walk when their legs have stopped taking orders and are simply executing the last command they received. It dropped once, got up, dropped again. The barking, I realized as the lantern light found it, wasn't a dog at all. It was a man laughing. Or trying to. What came out was somewhere between laughter and the sound a hinge makes.

"Calloway-Hurst," Elena said.

He looked like a scarecrow that had been left out through winter and half of spring. His boots were gone — he'd wrapped his feet in something, canvas, strips of tent fly, and beneath the wrappings his feet were the color of meat left too long in a freezer. His lips were split. His eyes had gone past wild into some country beyond it, a calm, terrible clearness, like a man who has seen the bottom of everything and found it closer than expected.

I got to him before he fell the last time. He weighed nothing. A big man once — he'd had the soft bulk of a desk surveyor when he'd boarded my boat back in Puerto Árido, all tweed and pomade and secrets — and now there was hardly enough of him to fill my arms.

"Water," he said. "Not river water. You don't understand. Not river water."

"We've got river water," Pilar said practically, holding the canteen to his mouth.

He drank like a man who'd learned the hard way how much a throat can take, small sips, disciplined, and then he grabbed Pilar's wrist with a grip that surprised us all, considering. "Listen. Listen to me. I have to say it while I can still say it in order."

"You can say it inside," I said. "By the stove."

"No fire." His voice cracked on it. "No smoke. They watch the valley at night. I walked three days without—" He stopped himself, swallowed whatever the rest was, and said, in the tone of a man laying cards face-up on a table, "I burned my notebook page in chapter four of this disaster, Captain, and I have regretted it every hour since. This time you get the whole book."

* * *

We got him into the deckhouse, propped among the charts, and Elena worked on his feet with snowmelt we'd been saving in tins, warming them slow, the way you're supposed to and nobody ever does. He talked while she worked. Maybe that was the point. Maybe he knew that if he stopped talking, the order of things would leave him and never come back.

"They took us at the moored camp," he said. "Four of us. They came at night, no lights, quiet as church mice with rifles. Reyes himself shook my hand. Shook it! Asked after my health. Put us in the launch and took us up past the posted boundary, into the reach that isn't on any chart, and I'll tell you why it isn't on any chart, Captain, because I drew part of that chart myself, two years ago, and I was told what to draw and what to leave off."

Elena's hands went still on his feet. Then they kept working, gentler.

"There's a lake," he said. "Up there. Spring-fed, cold as a sin, deep as a cathedral. Blue-green, the color of bottle glass. The maps show dry ground. The maps show *nothing*. And behind it — they built it in eighteen months, Captain, they built it in secret, with men brought in from the coast who signed papers and don't ask questions — there's a dam. Concrete. Ninety feet if it's an inch. And a diversion tunnel cut clean through the shoulder of the mountain, running the headwaters west, over the divide, into their own concession territory."

"The wells at Estancia Salada," Elena said quietly.

"The wells everywhere on the east slope." He laughed again, that hinge-sound. "The river hasn't been dying of drought, Mrs.—Miss Vasquez. The river's been robbed. Robbed at the throat, above the gorge, where nobody could see. Forty days from now the old concessions lapse, and the new permits — the ones based on my forged surveys, showing seasonal flow insufficient for irrigation claims — go through unopposed, and the company owns every drop from the divide to the sea. Legally. With stamps on it. That's the beauty of it. That's the *artistry*."

"And the four men?" I asked. "Your team?"

"Alive." He said it fast, like he was afraid the word would spoil. "That's the one decent thing. Reyes keeps them alive because dead witnesses make inquiries and living ones can be managed. They're housed at the dam site. Fed. Bored out of their skulls. One of them — Okafor — has taken up whittling. He whittles little sheep. There are forty sheep now." His voice wobbled between horror and something dangerously close to fondness. "They're quite good sheep."

Pilar, who had been listening from the doorway with the expression of someone watching a storm roll in, said, "And you? How'd you walk out?"

Calloway-Hurst looked at his wrapped feet. For the first time since he'd staggered into our lantern light, the clarity in his eyes flickered, and underneath it I saw the thing that had actually kept him alive on that walk: not courage. Shame. Good old-fashioned shame, which has carried men farther than courage ever has.

"I signed the false surveys," he said. "Two years ago. I told myself everyone signs things. I told myself the numbers were close enough to true. And then they took us up there and showed me what my signatures had bought, and Reyes offered me a desk in Buenos Aires and a raise, and I said yes. I said yes, and I shook his hand, and I ate their dinner, and I slept in their bed, and at two in the morning I climbed out the latrine window in my socks." He held up the ruined wrappings on his feet like evidence. "These used to be socks."

Nobody laughed. It wasn't funny. And yet something in the way he presented the socks — the sheer absurd dignity of it — cracked the dread in the deckhouse open just enough to let a little air in. Even grief, I've learned, needs ventilation.

"The field book," Elena said.

He fumbled at his shirt with fingers that didn't want to work, and Elena helped him, and from against his chest, where he'd kept it warm enough that his body heat hadn't let the pages freeze solid, he brought out a oilskin-wrapped book swollen with damp and stained with what I chose to believe was mud. He held it a moment before giving it up. A man holds a thing like that a moment. It was the last piece of himself that mattered.

"Coordinates. Elevations. The tunnel bore, the spillway, the penstock — I measured what I could see, and I remember the rest, and I wrote it down at night under a blanket with a pencil stub." He pressed it into Elena's hands and then covered her hands with both of his, and his grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "You'll know what to do with it. You're the only person on this river who draws lines because they're *true* rather than because they're paid for."

"That's not entirely—" Elena began.

"It is entirely," he said, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was the smile of a man setting down a weight he knows he won't be picking up again.

* * *

He lasted until a little past midnight.

Elena stayed with him the whole time. I sat outside on the rail where I could see the stars and hear the murmur through the deckhouse wall — his voice getting slower, hers steady, asking questions in a low even tone, mapping him the way she mapped everything, right down to the end. Pilar sat next to me on the rail, close enough that our shoulders touched, which neither of us remarked upon, because that's how Pilar and I communicate most comfortably.

"He's dying, isn't he," Pilar said. Not a question. Fifteen years old and already fluent in the grammar of the river, where everything arrives eventually.

"Yes."

"Because of his feet?"

"Because of everything. Feet, cold, three days walking, and mostly because a man can only carry so much before he sets it down." I turned my cup in my hands. The coffee was terrible. Pilar made it. Pilar will always make terrible coffee; I've decided to find this endearing rather than criminal. "Some men set it down and keep walking. Some set it down here."

Pilar was quiet a while. Downstream, the river whispered over its bones. Somewhere in the dark a fox screamed once and thought better of it.

"Captain," Pilar said finally, "when we get up there — to the dam. What are we going to do?"

I looked at the kid. In the starlight Pilar's face was all angles and stubbornness, and I thought: *this is what Tomás would have looked like at fifteen, if Tomás had been born fifty years late and twice as brave.* The thought hit me in the sternum like a winch handle.

"We're going to do what the river does," I said. "We're going to keep going downhill until we find the sea. Only first we're going uphill."

"That doesn't make sense."

"Most true things don't, at first pass."

From inside, Elena's voice rose slightly — not alarm, just emphasis, a question repeated — and then there was a long silence, and then no more questions at all.

* * *

We buried him at dawn on the bluff above the gravel bar, where the wind came down smelling of snowmelt. There was no priest within two hundred miles, so Elena read from the field book — not scripture, but his own words, the careful coordinates, the elevations, the notes in his cramped schoolmaster's hand: *Spillway crest approx. 28.4 m. Tunnel bore bearing 262°. Concrete pours observed weekly, Thursdays.* It was, I thought, exactly the kind of eulogy a surveyor would want: precise, useful, true. Pilar stacked stones. I dug, because digging is the captain's job on my boat, always has been, and my hands needed the work more than my pride needed the rest.

Elena marked the grave with a flat stone and penciled on it in her neat cartographer's letters: *A. CALLOWAY-HURST, SURVEYOR. HE DREW THE TRUTH TWICE.*

"Twice?" Pilar asked.

"The first time wrong, because he was afraid," Elena said. "The second time right, because he stopped being afraid. Most of us don't get the second drawing." She closed the field book and held it against her chest, and I watched her stand there a long moment looking north up the valley, toward the reach no chart admits exists, and I saw something settle in her face the way sediment settles in still water — slowly, completely, and for good.

Grief is a strange cargo. It weighs nothing and sinks everything. I'd been carrying mine for twenty years, ballast hidden below the waterline, and I'd convinced myself the listing was just how boats ride. But standing over that shallow grave, watching Elena hold a dead man's truth against her heart, I felt my own ballast shift — felt the guilt over Tomás, the promise, the endless moving, all of it slide to one side — and the *Tomás* — no, not the boat, me, *me* — come level for the first time in years.

Level, and pointed upstream.

"How many days left on the permit clock?" I asked.

"Nine," Elena said.

"And how far to the source?"

"By the river, five days, if the engine cooperates." She glanced back toward the deckhouse, where the sound of Pilar arguing lovingly with the fuel pump drifted out like birdsong. "By land, over the divide — three, maybe. But we'd lose the boat."

"We're not losing the boat," I said, and heard how it sounded, and corrected myself, because Calloway-Hurst had earned honesty with those ruined sock-feet. "I mean — we're not losing it *yet*. There's a difference. Ask any captain."

Elena almost smiled. Then she opened the field book to the last written page and turned it toward me, and the smile died.

At the bottom, beneath the final coordinates, in handwriting gone shaky and huge, pressed hard enough to tear the paper, Calloway-Hurst had written one last line:

*Reyes expects you at the lock. He does NOT expect you at the falls. Nobody uses the falls. Nobody has run the falls since 1911.*

I looked up the valley. The wind came down off the high country, cold and mineral and old, carrying the smell of stolen water, and for the first time in twenty years of running rivers I felt something I couldn't rightly name — not fear, though fear was in it, and not peace either, though peace was in that too.

Purpose, maybe. Grief with a heading on it.

"Pilar!" I called toward the deckhouse. "How's our lady of principle feeling this morning?"

There was a clang, a curse, and then the engine caught — rough, ragged, alive — and Pilar's head popped out of the hatch wearing a grin and half the grease in Patagonia.

"She says," Pilar announced, "that she'll run the falls if we buy her a new fuel pump in the next town."

"Elena," I said, stepping over the rail to the wheel, "how do you feel about lying to a fifteen-year-old?"

"I'm a cartographer," she said, taking the field book forward to navigate. "Lying is the whole profession. We just call it projection."
