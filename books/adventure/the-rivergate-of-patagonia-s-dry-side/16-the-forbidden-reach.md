## 16. The Forbidden Reach

The sign was older than I expected.

I had imagined something official — enameled steel, a government crest, the kind of notice that implies lawyers and telegraph lines behind it. Instead it was a plank of quebracho wood, nailed to two crooked posts at the river's edge, the letters burned in by hand and then half-erased by years of sun:

*PROHIBIDO EL PASO — CONCESIÓN RÍO SECO S.A.*

Below that, someone had scratched a second message into the grain with a knife, so faint I had to put my fingers on it to read it: *el agua no es de nadie.* The water belongs to no one.

"Someone disagreed loudly," Pilar said, running her thumb over the scratches. She was seventeen and had the crew's habit of touching everything, as if the boat were an extension of her own hands.

"Someone who's probably dead," Noor said, but not unkindly. Noor stood at the rail with the field book open against one forearm — Calloway-Hurst's field book, its water-swollen pages already soft as cloth. He had died holding it like a man passing a baton, and Noor had not let go of it since. "Or someone who was right, which is worse for whoever nailed this up."

We were past the last estancia, past the last fence, past the last thing any map of mine admitted existed. My own atlas — the one I'd carried since cartography school in Buenos Aires, its spine held together with tape and stubbornness — showed nothing here but blank paper and a printed apology: *zona sin levantar.* Unsurveyed zone. A polite way of saying: *we were never allowed to look.*

I took out my pencil and wrote in the margin, small enough to deny later if anyone ever asked: *Day 1 above the boundary. Fog. Standing water. The river is wider than it has any right to be.*

Then we went through.

* * *

The fog did not roll in so much as rise up out of the river itself, as though the water below us were breathing. One moment I could see both banks — low, gray-green scrub, the bones of an old sheep trail cut into the slope — and the next the world contracted to the length of the boat and the sound of the engine working patiently against a current that had gone strange.

Strange is a surveyor's word. What I mean is: the current stopped arguing with us. For eleven days since the lock, since Reyes had smiled at us from his black launch and let us go like a fisherman returning something too small, the Rio Seco had fought us every meter — shallow braids, gravel bars that shifted between morning soundings, reaches where the channel narrowed until the willows brushed both rails. Now, above the boundary, the river went wide and slow and deep, and the silence that came off the water was so complete I could hear Pilar swallow.

"It's standing," Noor said quietly, at the wheel. "Feel it? We're not pushing current anymore. We're pushing pond."

Noor said *pond* the way other people say *blasphemy*. A river that stands still is a river being held back by something, and neither of us needed Calloway-Hurst's dying words to guess what that something was. But knowing a thing and seeing it are different animals, and the fog gave us the courtesy of delay.

Pilar broke the silence the way she broke most silences, which was cheerfully and slightly sideways. "My mother says fog is the sky coming down to check on people."

"What does she say it finds?" I asked.

"Usually disappointment." She grinned, then sobered, because even Pilar could feel what this place was doing to us. She went forward to the bow with the sounding pole, and her calls came back muffled and rhythmic — *doce pies... quince... quince... veinte* — each number deeper than the last, the bottom falling away beneath us like a staircase into a cellar.

I worked. That is the honest truth of what I did in that fog, and I have never been ashamed of it: I worked. I had the plane table lashed to the chart desk under canvas, and every time the fog thinned enough to show me anything — a bend, a terrace, a drowned fence post standing in a meter of water with its wire still strung — I fixed it. Bearing, distance, depth, note. *Drowned fence line, bearing 214°, posts at 40-meter intervals, wire intact. Land flooded within living memory.* Then another entry. *Water level above all seasonal expectation. No inflow visible. Source must lie ahead, impounded.*

An act of witness. That is what I told myself it was, when my hands shook. Not trespass. Not theft. Witnessing is the oldest work there is: you stand where you are told not to stand, and you write down what is true, and you carry it back to people who were kept from seeing. Everything else — the permits, the concessions, the forty-day clock ticking down to nine now, eight — all of it was built on the assumption that no one would ever write it down.

So I wrote it down.

* * *

The drowned town appeared at midday, or what passes for midday inside a fog bank, which is to say the light went from gray to a paler gray and Pilar announced lunch.

It was not on any list. I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only religion I have left. It was not on the concession maps Reyes's company filed, not on the provincial surveys, not in the colonial archives I'd searched in Puerto Árido before we signed aboard. A settlement of perhaps a dozen buildings, stone-footed, timber above, sitting in thirty centimeters of still water as though the whole street had knelt down to drink and forgotten to stand up.

Noor brought us in slow, backing the engine, and we drifted down what had clearly once been the main road. A church bell hung from a gallows-frame at the corner of the flooded plaza, green with age, silent. Doors stood open on empty rooms. On one windowsill, sheltered from nothing because there was nothing left to shelter from, sat a clay pot with a geranium long dead in it, the stalks bleached white as bone.

"They didn't leave," Pilar said softly. She had gone quiet in the way she did around graves. "Look. They left *slowly*. Things are stacked. Furniture's piled neat in the doorways."

"Flooded out a little at a time," Noor said. "Year after year. Each spring a bit higher. You don't flee a thing that rises a hand's width annually. You stack your furniture and tell yourself next year you'll go." Noor's voice did something complicated on the last word, and I looked up from my board and caught it — that flicker, the one I had learned to read by now, the ghost of Tomás moving behind Noor's eyes. People stay too long in places that are killing them slowly. Boats do too. Captains know this better than anyone alive.

I fixed the town on my sheet. I named it — not officially, not yet, but provisionally, the way you hold a place in trust: *Pueblo Sumergido (nombre por confirmar).* And underneath, because the old woman's voice was in my ears and had been for weeks, I added a note that no proper survey would contain: *The river remembers who drinks first. Someone drank first here.*

We ate lunch aboard, drifting, and the fog pressed close around us like a curtain drawn by considerate hands, and for twenty minutes nobody spoke, and it was — I record this honestly — almost peaceful. There is a version of this story where the three of us were afraid every minute, and that version is false. There were minutes, whole minutes, when we were simply three people on quiet water sharing bread and canned peaches, and the terror was a thing waiting politely outside the fog like a caller who knows you're not ready.

Pilar finished her peaches and licked the syrup off her fingers and said, "When I have a boat, I'm going to name it *Canned Peaches*, so that every time things go wrong I remember they can also go right."

"That is either very wise or very stupid," Noor said.

"I've been told those are often the same boat."

And Noor laughed — actually laughed, short and rusty, the laugh of a hinge unused for years — and I felt something in my chest turn over, and I bent lower over my board and drew a contour line with unnecessary care.

* * *

The fog lifted at dusk the way a confession lifts — all at once, and leaving you exposed.

One moment gray; the next, air so clear it rang. And there it was ahead of us, filling the valley floor from escarpment to escarpment: the standing water, kilometers of it, flat as poured glass, going violet and copper with the sunset. And rising out of it, far upstream, dark against the burning sky, a shape no natural river makes.

A wall. Straight-edged. Man-made. With, along its crest, a thin silver thread of spillwater catching the last light — and beside it, smaller, uglier, a concrete throat where a diversion tunnel drank from the headwaters and sent them away somewhere the river would never see again.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The engine idled. Somewhere out across the flat water, a bird called twice and got no answer.

"There it is," Pilar finally whispered. "The whole war. It's just — sitting there."

"No," Noor said. I turned. Noor wasn't looking at the dam. Noor was looking at me, and there was something in that weathered face I hadn't seen before — not the restlessness, not the guilt, not the running. Something settled. "There it isn't. That's the point. That's the whole crime in one photograph, Elena. Every map says this valley is dry. Every permit says the water rights downstream are worthless because there's nothing up here to take. And meanwhile—" Noor gestured at the drowned town behind us, at the dead geranium, at the estancias whose wells had gone salt, "—meanwhile they've had their hand on the tap for years, and everyone downstream is blaming the sky."

I set up the plane table right there in the failing light, because some fixes cannot wait for morning. I shot the dam's bearings from three stations as Noor moved the boat in a slow arc — Noor understanding without being asked, holding each position steady while I sighted, the two of us working like we'd done it for years instead of weeks. I measured the spillway crest against the horizon. I sketched the tunnel intake. I wrote, in my best hand, the sentence I had been carrying since Estancia Salada like a stone in my boot:

*Headwaters impounded by unauthorized works, Concesión Río Seco S.A., prior to and concealed from all licensed survey. Witnessed this day by Elena Vasquez, cartographer, at coordinates recorded herein.*

Witnessed. It sounded small for what it was. It sounded exactly right for what it was. Most true things do.

Pilar stood watch at the bow with the binoculars, and after a while she said, very quietly, "Elena. Lights."

I looked up. Far across the flat water, at the base of the dam where the tunnel mouth drank the river, lights were moving. Not fixed lights — lanterns, carried. Three of them, maybe four, swinging in a rhythm I recognized from a hundred survey camps: men walking a perimeter.

And then, closer — much closer — off our port beam, where the fog had been, a single lantern came alight on the water itself, and a voice carried across the still surface with terrible courtesy, the way certain voices can:

"*Señora Vasquez. Capitán Thornbury. You are on private concession waters.*"

Reyes. Of course it was Reyes. He had let us through the lock because he had known all along there was only one door, and he had been waiting behind it with the lamp lit and his manners polished.

Noor's hand found mine on the chart desk — briefly, hard, a whole conversation in two seconds — and then Noor reached for the throttle, and the engine note rose into the violet silence like a held breath finally let out.
