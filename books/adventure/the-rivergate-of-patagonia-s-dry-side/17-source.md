## 17. Source

The fog did not lift so much as it gave up.

It had ridden with us for two days past the posted boundary — the boundary itself a joke, a strip of tin painted red and nailed to a dead coigue that had no business growing this far east of the mountains. Elena had photographed the sign from three angles, then photographed Pilar standing beside it for scale, and I remember thinking that if we all drowned in the next hour, at least there would be evidence that someone had once stood in this place and laughed about it.

Now, on the third morning, the fog thinned like butter spread too far across hot bread, and what it revealed made even Pilar stop talking.

A lake.

Not a river. A lake, sitting in a bowl of gray stone at what every chart in Argentina insisted was a dry saddle. It was the color of old bottle glass, green-gold where the first light came through, and so still that the mist lying on it looked less like weather than like the lake's own breath, held and not yet released. The water came up out of the ground itself — you could see the springs along the northern shore, a dozen pale scars in the rock where water welled without sound, without current, as if the earth had been keeping this to itself for ten thousand years and had only just now been caught.

"Captain," Pilar said, in the hushed voice people use in churches and morgues, "the charts say there's nothing here."

"The charts," I said, "say a great many things."

I brought us in slow. The *Tomás* — my boat, Tomás's boat, the name on her stern worn nearly to nothing by sun and salt — slid across water so clear I could count stones eight meters down. Somewhere beneath us, I knew, ran the diversion tunnel Calloway-Hurst had described with his dying breath: concrete throat swallowing the headwaters whole, feeding them south into company canals while the estancias downstream drank dust. The lake didn't look robbed. That was the obscenity of it. It looked abundant. It looked like a place that could give forever, which is exactly the kind of place men build fences around.

Elena stood at the bow with her notebook open against her chest, not writing. Just looking. I have seen pilgrims look at shrines with less on their faces.

"There," she said finally, and pointed.

On the far shore, half-hidden behind a shoulder of rock: timber barracks, a crane, the raw scar of the dam face where it met the stone. And below the barracks, small with distance but unmistakable —

Men. Four of them. Moving between two structures with the slow, shuffling economy of prisoners doing chores under a guard's eye.

I put the glasses up. My hands were steady. I'll say that much for myself; whatever else the river has taken from me over the years, it left me steady hands.

Four men. Bearded past recognition, dressed in rags that had been survey khaki once. One of them paused in his shuffling, looked out across the water at our boat — a speck, surely, nothing more — and raised one arm straight up over his head. Not waving. Holding something aloft. Even at that distance I could see it catch the light: brass. A survey instrument, held up like a signal fire.

"That's Okafor," Elena said quietly. "The transit is his. He wouldn't let anyone else touch it." Her voice did something complicated on his name. "He's alive."

"They're all alive," I said. "Count them."

She counted. Four. The fifth — Delgado, the youngest, the one who'd written home about buying his mother a stove — was not among them, and none of us said his name, because some absences are too heavy to lift twice.

Pilar said, "Do we go in?"

And here is the thing I want set down honestly, because history will be written by Elena's pen and she is kinder to me than I deserve: I wanted to run. Every instinct I own, every hard-won rule of survival that has kept my skin on my bones for thirty years on this river, said *turn around, go back down the gorge, let the government sort it out*. There were rifles over there. There was Reyes over there, somewhere, patient as a lock waiting for its key. And there were four witnesses who, if we took them aboard, would turn my shallow-drafted riverboat into a slow, overloaded coffin.

But then I looked at Elena, and she wasn't looking at me at all. She was looking at the man holding the transit above his head, and her jaw was set in the particular way it sets when she has already decided something and is merely waiting for the rest of the world to catch up.

I've been catching up to that woman for weeks now. I expect I always will.

"We go in," I said.

* * *

We went in the way you approach a sleeping animal: slow, broadside, hands visible. Pilar flew nothing. We carried no flag because we had none, and because flags are just cloth until someone shoots at them.

The guards met us at a crude landing of planks and oil drums. Two of them, young, bored, rifles slung with the casualness of men who had never once needed them. Behind them, the four surveyors stood in a line, blinking at us like creatures dragged up from underground. Which, in a sense, they were. They'd been kept in a windowless store shed, Calloway-Hurst would later tell us — fed, unharmed, forbidden to leave or signal, because dead witnesses are a scandal and live ones are merely an inconvenience.

The older guard consulted a clipboard with tremendous seriousness, as though our arrival were a scheduled delivery.

"You're expected," he said.

"I'm rarely expected," I said. "People who expect me are usually disappointed."

He didn't smile. Neither did the younger one, though I saw something flicker at the corner of his mouth, and I thought: *there's a boy who'd rather be anywhere else.* You collect those details the way Elena collects photographs. You never know which one will matter.

They walked us up the slope toward the dam crest, the four freed surveyors trailing behind us like ghosts attending their own exhumation. Okafor gripped Elena's hand once, hard, and said nothing, and she said nothing back, and somehow it was the loudest conversation I witnessed all day.

Reyes was waiting at the top.

I want to describe him properly, because the man deserves description the way a storm deserves a name. He stood at the edge of the dam crest in a pressed field suit, gray as the stone, hands clasped behind him, surveying his illegal empire with the contentment of a landowner watching rain fall on his own fields. Beside him, a folding table. On the table, a white cloth. On the white cloth, a coffee service — actual porcelain, actual silver spoons — and a plate of facturas, the flaky kind, dusted with sugar.

He had set out breakfast for us. That was Inspector Reyes entire: he would murder you, but he would do it after offering you the good coffee, and he would genuinely be wounded if you refused.

"Captain Thornbury." He inclined his head. "Señorita Vasquez. And — forgive me — the young deckhand. Pilar, isn't it? You've grown since Puerto Árido."

"Everyone does," Pilar said, and I could have kissed that kid, except that Reyes's eyes moved to me with mild reproof, as though I'd brought a badly trained dog to a dinner party.

"You found the source," Reyes said. It wasn't a question. He turned his palms outward, presenting the lake, the springs, the whole impossible secret of the dry side of Patagonia, as if unveiling a gift. "Look at it. Do you know how long men have guessed at this? Since the Conquest. Half the expeditions that went looking died of thirst within sight of these hills. And here it sits, enough water for a province, enough water to make deserts bloom from here to the coast — and the government that owns it can't even find it on a map."

"It's not theirs to find," Elena said. "It belongs to the estancias. To the towns downstream. The permits—"

"The permits expire in nine days," Reyes said pleasantly. "Yes. I know. I wrote the schedule." He gestured to the table. "Please. Sit. The coffee is from Buenos Aires; I don't trust anything grown locally anymore, and neither should you."

Nobody sat. He sighed, the sigh of a reasonable man surrounded by unreasonable ones, and poured anyway, four cups, setting each at an empty chair with unhurried precision.

"Let me save us all the theater," he said. "You have photographs of forged manifests. You have the testimony of five surveyors — six, counting poor Mr. Calloway-Hurst, wherever he's got to now; the high country eats men like him. You have Señorita Vasquez's maps, which I will admit are beautiful work. And I have rifles, a radio, forty kilometers of gorge with no road out, and the patience of a company that has owned this valley longer than Argentina has owned this valley." He smiled. It reached his eyes, which was the worst part. "So you see, the arithmetic favors me. But I am not a mathematical man by nature. I am a practical one. And practically speaking, killing you creates paperwork."

"How considerate," I said.

"Isn't it?" He lifted his cup, found it too hot, set it down again. "Here is my offer, and I make it once, so listen as though your lives depend on it, because in a sense they do — though I'd argue, Captain, that your life stopped depending on things years ago. You strike me as a man who has been running from a debt. Am I wrong?"

I didn't answer. He hadn't asked a question that required one. That was another of his talents.

"Señorita Vasquez." He turned to her, and his voice changed — softened, dropped into the register men use when they believe women require persuasion rather than terms. "The new concession forms in eleven days. Two hundred thousand hectares, water rights in perpetuity, and the surveys must be true ones this time — the old forgeries won't survive scrutiny forever, and the company needs a cartographer whose name carries weight. Your name will carry weight. You'd be chief of survey. Your own office. Your own staff. Your family's debts settled by Christmas — yes, I know about the debts, and about the marriage they arranged to cover them; I told you, I read everything." He spread his hands. "You could spend your life fighting us from the outside, and lose, and be forgotten. Or you could stand inside, hold the pen, and make certain the maps are honest. Think of it. The truth, drawn by you, signed by you — with a salary that lets you sleep at night."

I watched Elena take this in. I watched her look at the porcelain cup, at the sugar-dusted facturas, at the lake glittering behind him like a bribe made of light. And I confess a shameful thing: for one full second, I hoped she'd take it. Because if she took it, she'd be safe. She'd be in an office in Buenos Aires with her name on the door instead of standing on a dam crest next to a broke riverboat captain with a dead man's name on his stern and a rifle somewhere behind every rock.

Then she laughed.

Not cruelly. That was the remarkable part — it was almost warm, almost sorry for him. She stepped forward and picked up one of the facturas and ate it, deliberately, chewing while she spoke.

"Inspector. I grew up watching my father sign things. Contracts, letters, confessions — whatever kept the family comfortable. He had beautiful handwriting. Everyone admired it." She brushed sugar from her fingers. "I became a cartographer because maps are the one document that can't lie politely. A map either matches the ground or it doesn't. Yours don't. And you're asking me to make my living the way my father made his — signing things that match nothing." She shook her head slowly. "No. But thank you for the pastry. It really is excellent."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind Reyes's eyes that was not patience. Something quick and cold, a fish turning under ice. Then it was gone, banked like a fire, and he was courteous again, courteous as a knife laid on clean linen.

"Well," he said. "I did promise to offer once."

"And we promised to refuse," I said. "So nobody's lied today. Rare, on this river."

"Captain." He picked up his coffee at last and drank, unhurried. "Do you know why I let you pass the lock at the gorge? Eleven days ago. I could have taken you there — you know it, I know it, we both pretended otherwise. I let you go because I wanted you to see all of it. The lake. The dam. The tunnel. I wanted you to arrive at the top of the world and understand that the view belongs to us." He set the cup down with a small click of porcelain on porcelain. "What I did not anticipate — and I confess this freely, it will keep me awake tonight — is that you would bring the surveyors' testimony down off this mountain no matter what I did. So I find myself forced into the arithmetic after all. And the arithmetic, Captain, is that the *Tomás* cannot carry six passengers, two crew, and a story like yours back down my river. Not all of it. Something has to stay behind."

Behind him, without any order being given that I saw, the two young guards shifted their rifles forward off their shoulders.

Okafor moved first — the surveyors, bless their ink-stained hearts, had clearly worked out escape plans in that store shed for months, the way prisoners do, plans they never believed they'd use. He threw himself sideways behind the crane housing, dragging Delgado's brother-in-law with him. The other two scattered toward the barracks. Elena grabbed Pilar's collar and pulled, and I was already moving, because thirty years on a river teaches you exactly one lesson perfectly:

When the water breaks, don't stand on the bank admiring it.

"Down!" I shouted, and the first shot cracked the morning open like river ice going out in spring, and below us the lake lay green and gold and utterly still, holding its breath, remembering — as the old woman in Estancia Salada used to say — exactly who had drunk first.

Reyes did not shoot. I heard him shouting orders, calm, precise, furious in the way of a man whose tablecloth has been stained. And over the noise of boots and stone and Pilar's breath tearing beside me, I heard the other sound, the one that turned my blood to river water:

From the dam's base, deep in the concrete throat of the diversion tunnel, came a groan. Long. Low. The sound of something enormous being asked to hold more than it was built to hold.

Elena, flat against the rocks beside me, looked at me with wide eyes and said the words that ended the argument before it began:

"Noor. The spillway gates. Someone opened them — and the tunnel's blocked. The lake's rising."

Above the springs, above the barracks, above the porcelain and the rifles and the whole rotten architecture of the concession, the bottle-green water had begun, very quietly, to move.
