## 18. Water Remembers Who Drinks First

### Noor

The lake at the top of the world was the color of a glacier's eye — pale green, unnervingly still, and ringed by concrete that had no business existing this far past the edge of any map.

Noor stood in the shallows of the spillway channel and let that fact settle like silt. Behind the dam, water waited. Forty years of it, maybe more, held back by a wall the concession company had built in secret while the estancias downstream watched their wells go dry one by one. The river hadn't died of drought. It had been robbed.

And Reyes knew they'd found it.

The surveyors were alive — five of them, thinner and grayer than the men who'd boarded the *Mariana Delia* in Puerto Árido, but alive, kept in a timber barracks near the works camp as witnesses nobody intended to let speak. A guard walked the dam crest with a rifle slung loose, the way men carry rifles when they've never once needed them and know it.

Reyes had met them at the landing with coffee.

That was the thing about Inspector Reyes that no one ever quite believed until they saw it: he was *courteous*. He had chairs brought down to the shore. He apologized for the state of the road. He offered Elena a partnership with figures so precise they could only be real, and when she declined — politely, which Noor would remember forever — he had simply nodded, as if she'd declined a second cup.

Then he'd said: "You'll want to stay the night, Captain. The current below the dam is treacherous after dark." And he'd smiled like a man quoting scripture.

Now it was full dark, and Noor crouched behind a stack of cement bags at the edge of the works camp, doing arithmetic.

The company launch lay moored in the spillway channel — a black-hulled brute of a boat, twin screws, a radio mast, and enough fuel in her tanks to run the upper river for a month. She was Reyes's pride. She was also, Noor realized, sitting directly above the spillway gates, in water barely two fathoms deep, with a hull that had never once been asked to forgive anything.

"You're smiling," Pilar whispered beside them. The boy had a crowbar across their knees and an expression of pure religious terror. "Captain. You're *smiling*. That's worse than the rifles."

"I'm not smiling."

"Your voice is smiling."

Noor looked out at the launch riding her mooring lines, gentle as a sleeping animal, and admitted — privately, in the ledger where such things were kept — that Pilar wasn't wrong. There was a plan forming, and it was a bad plan, and bad plans always made Noor smile, because Tomás used to say the river forgave good plans and drowned careful ones, so you might as well enjoy yourself.

"Here's the shape of it," Noor said. "Elena gets to the radio shack. Reyes keeps his set tuned to the relay at Cobre Muerto because he's vain enough to want every station on the river hearing his orders in his own voice. Tonight we give him what he wants — his voice, saying things he never said."

"And you?"

"Me and the launch are going to have a disagreement about buoyancy."

Pilar stared. Then, slowly, they began to grin — the first grin since Bruno went overboard in the gorge, since the engine died, since the whole long hunger of the dead-water drift. It was a lopsided grin, seventeen years old and scared half through, but it was there.

"The spillway gates," Pilar said. "You're going to open them under her."

"I'm going to open them *slowly*, under her, which is different, and much more insulting."

### Elena

The radio shack smelled of kerosene and cigarette smoke and, faintly, of ink — the smell of power, Elena thought, or at least of paperwork pretending to be power.

She had counted her steps from the barracks shadows: forty paces along the dam access road, twelve across the gravel yard, past the guard whose attention belonged entirely to a cigarette and the far shore. Reyes ran his camp the way he ran everything — on the assumption that no one would dare. It was his single flaw, and it was beautiful.

Inside, the transmitter sat on a scarred desk beneath a shelf of ledgers. Elena did not look at the ledgers. Looking at things was how people got caught; she had learned that from watching Calloway-Hurst burn his own notebook page by page somewhere north of Estancia Salada. Instead she touched only what she needed: the key, the frequency dial, the microphone.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Somewhere between the gorge and the ghost town and the eleven days of permit clock, her hands had stopped asking her permission for anything.

She keyed the relay frequency — Reyes's frequency, the one his orders traveled on, the one every station from Cobre Muerto down to Puerto Árido listened to at the top of the hour because the company paid the operators to listen. The clock on the wall said nine minutes to the hour.

In her coat pocket: her photographs of the manifests. Her true survey, drawn night by night up the forbidden reach. And folded soft as a love letter, the old woman's hand-drawn map of the upper gorge, its ink gone brown, its margin carrying four words in a shaking Spanish hand:

*El agua recuerda quién bebe primero.*

The river remembers who drinks first.

Elena had spent her whole life being told what she was for — her family's duty, her family's arrangement, her family's name written neatly into another man's future. She had taken this job to run from that. She understood now that she had not run at all. She had walked, step by patient step, upstream toward the one sentence that was hers to say.

She pressed the transmit key and began to read.

### Noor

Getting aboard the launch was insultingly easy, which Noor chose to take as the river's blessing rather than the company's negligence.

The deck watch was a boy younger than Pilar, dozing against the rail with his rifle propped like a fishing rod. Noor came over the stern quiet as fog, put him gently face-down on the deck with a knee between his shoulders and a finger to his own lips, and took the man's knife and boots for good measure.

"Nothing personal," Noor murmured. "You're employed by thieves. Consider this a career opportunity."

Below, the engine room was warm and humming, and the spillway gates stood closed beneath the hull like a held breath. Noor had spent thirty years learning what water does when you stop arguing with it. Water is patient. Water is fair. Water takes exactly what it's owed, with interest, and never gloats.

The gate winch was manual — old machinery, older than the concrete around it, some engineer's honest work buried inside the company's dishonest one. Noor put both hands on the wheel and began to turn.

The first sound was small: a click, a groan, a thread of green lake water finding the gap beneath the launch's keel. The second sound was the hull shifting on her mooring lines, complaining in the language of boats that have discovered the floor has opinions.

Above, faintly, carried down the channel, Noor heard the radio shack crackle to life — and then heard Elena's voice coming out of it, clear and level and utterly fearless, reading coordinates and claim numbers and the words *forged survey, forged permits, stolen headwaters* to every ear on the river.

Noor grinned in the dark and turned the wheel faster.

* * *

### Elena

"—and I am reading from the field book of Mr. Alistair Calloway-Hurst, senior surveyor, government charter, whose team was detained at the source by personnel of the Río Seco Concession Company—"

The door opened.

Reyes stood in it with a lantern in one hand and no expression at all, which from Reyes was the equivalent of another man screaming. Behind him, the guard from the yard, rifle up, uncertain — because the voice on the air was a woman's, and the woman was unarmed, and the voice was already halfway to Puerto Árido.

"Señora Vasquez." Reyes set the lantern on the desk with terrible gentleness. "You have been busy."

"Nine minutes of airtime," Elena said, without lifting her fingers from the key. "I've used six. Would you like to hear the part about the diversion tunnel? It's very well documented. Your own engineers signed it."

Something moved behind Reyes's eyes — not anger. Arithmetic. Elena watched him do it: the stations listening, the operators logging, the testimony already loose in the world like water from a broken tank, running downhill toward places he could never pump it back from.

He reached for the transmitter's power switch.

Elena did the only thing left. She swept the lantern off the desk into the kerosene heater.

Flame went up the wall like a startled bird. The guard shouted. Reyes turned — one heartbeat, no more, but a heartbeat was all the river had ever needed from anyone — and Elena wrenched the microphone free of its cable, dove past him through the smoke, and ran.

Behind her, Reyes's voice rose over the fire, giving orders in that courteous tone that had finally, finally cracked:

"Flood it! Open everything — drown the evidence, drown the *camp*, I don't care what's in the channel —"

And beneath the dam, something vast and old and patient began, at last, to move.

* * *

### Noor

The launch was sinking beautifully.

There was no other word for it. Noor had opened the gates a hand's width, then a forearm's width, and the green lake water had come up through the spillway channel with the unhurried malice of a creditor, filling the black hull's belly while her mooring lines sang. The deck watch — released, barefoot, and sprinting for shore with the wisdom of the young — had not even stopped to curse.

Noor stood at the winch and felt the whole structure begin to speak.

Not the launch. The *dam*.

It started as a hum in the boot soles, then a shudder in the handrail, then a sound Noor had heard once before, years ago, on a flooded river in the north — a deep, grinding note, like a giant clearing its throat before deciding whether to sing or swallow. Concrete made that sound when it was being asked a question it couldn't answer.

The gates were meant to release water gradually. They were not meant to be opened wide, at night, by a fugitive with a crowbar and a grudge, while a forty-year head of pressure leaned on the other side.

"Noor!" Pilar's voice, high and thin from the shore. "Captain! The wall — the wall's *talking*!"

"I hear it!"

"Is it supposed to talk?!"

"No!" Noor gave the wheel one last savage turn and felt the mechanism seize, jammed open past all sense, and then it was time to stop being an engineer and start being a swimmer.

The launch settled onto the spillway sill with a sigh, rolled, and went under stern-first, her radio mast — Reyes's radio mast, Reyes's proud black boat — the last thing to vanish, winking once in the moonlight like a hat tip.

Noor hit the water on the downstream side and let the spillage carry them. Cold didn't describe it; cold was a polite word. The current took Noor sideways, tumbling, and for one white moment the whole world was roar and green foam and the memory of Tomás's hand letting go —

— and then Pilar's arm, hooked under Noor's collar, hauling, both of them coughing onto the gravel shallows with the dam roaring behind them like a struck bell.

They lay side by side in the wet stones, gasping.

"Captain," Pilar wheezed. "If we live, I'm learning a trade on land."

"Noted."

"There's no *boats* in my future. Write it down."

"Written," Noor said, and then the dam spoke again — louder, longer, a note with a crack running through it — and neither of them laughed at all.

* * *

### Elena

Elena ran through the burning camp with the microphone clutched to her chest like an infant, and it was absurd, it was ridiculous, she was fleeing a man with a rifle carrying nothing but a piece of brass and wire — and she had never in her life felt less like running away.

Because the microphone was the map. The photographs. The truth, in her voice, already traveling at the speed of light down every wire on the river. Reyes could shoot her; he could not recall her.

She reached the waterline where the *Mariana Delia* lay moored — battered, patched, her engine salted and her decks scarred, the ugliest beloved thing on the river — and found Noor and Pilar sprawled in the shallows like drowned cats.

"You're wet," Elena said.

"You're on fire," Noor said, nodding at the glow behind her. "We match."

"The radio—"

"Gone with the launch, I'd guess." Noor sat up, wringing a sleeve, and looked at the microphone in her arms, then at the dam, which had begun — unmistakably now, audible even over the roar — to groan in long, structural, dying syllables. "Though I suspect Reyes is about to solve your transmission problem permanently. Along with several others."

The spillway was a white cataract now. The gates jammed open. The lake behind the wall dropping, leaning, pressing its whole ancient weight against concrete poured in secret by men who had measured everything except consequences.

And Reyes stood on the dam crest in the firelight, small and precise, shouting orders to open the emergency sluices — every valve, everything, drown the evidence, drown the records, drown the *witnesses* if it came to that — and the surveyors in the barracks were already spilling out into the yard, unguarded, unguarded because every guard was staring up at the wall.

"He's flooding it," Elena breathed. "He'd rather destroy the valley than let the truth float."

"He's been drinking first for forty years," Noor said, standing, water streaming off them, and there was something in Noor's face Elena had seen only once before — the night they'd talked about Tomás, the guilt worn smooth as river stone. "The river's just now collecting."

The dam groaned again — a sound like the world clearing its throat — and a seam of dust jumped from the crest, catching the firelight, hanging in the air like a question.

Noor looked at the barracks. At the surveyors stumbling free. At Pilar, soaked and swearing. At Elena, holding the truth in her two hands.

"Pilar — get everyone to high ground, the cutbank above the works. Now. Run."

"And you?"

Noor was already turning toward the *Mariana Delia*, toward the mooring lines, toward the last boat afloat in a channel that was about to become a waterfall.

"I'm going to go say goodbye," Noor said, "to an old friend."

The dam screamed.
