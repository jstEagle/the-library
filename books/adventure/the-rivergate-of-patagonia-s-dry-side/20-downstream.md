## 20. Downstream

The river did not carry us so much as pour us.

I had spent my life believing water was patient — that it wore away stone in increments too small for grief or triumph, that it kept its own time the way old women keep their own clocks, out of stubbornness and spite. But the flood taught me otherwise. The Rio Seco, given back its headwaters after forty years of theft, came down the dry side of Patagonia like a debt collector who had finally found the address.

We rode it on nothing.

That is not quite true. We rode it on what remained of Noor's boat — half a hull, most of a deck, an engine Pilar coaxed back to life twice before admitting she was performing miracles rather than maintenance — and we rode it on the company's own launch, which Noor had scuttled in the spillway and which now sat half-sunk in the shallows below the broken dam like a whale that had made a very poor decision. And we rode it, mostly, on the flood itself: a brown, muscular, singing thing that took the boat in its hands and ran.

"Sit down," Noor told me, for perhaps the hundredth time since the dam broke.

"I am sitting."

"You're sitting *wrong*. You're sitting like a woman writing a letter. Sit like a woman whose life depends on staying in the boat."

"My life does depend on staying in the boat."

"Then sit like you know it." Noor's hands never stopped moving — wheel, throttle, the long pole they used for reading depth by feel when the instruments drowned. There was blood on one knuckle and river mud to the elbow, and I remember thinking, with the strange clarity that terror lends, that I had never seen anyone so entirely where they were. Noor was not remembering Tomás. Not yet. That would come later, downstream, in the quiet. For now there was only the water and the work, and Noor belonged to both completely.

Pilar stood at the bow with a boathook, looking seventeen years old and six feet tall, shouting depths in a voice cracked from cold. "Two meters! Meter and a half! *Shoal* — hard left!"

Noor went hard left. The boat went hard left. I went sideways, caught the rail, and discovered I was laughing — actually laughing, high and unhinged, into the spray.

"You've gone mad," Noor said without turning around.

"Probably."

"Good. It's easier to steer mad people. They don't argue with the river."

* * *

The flood ran ahead of us and behind us and all around us, and everywhere it went it left evidence.

That is the phrase I keep returning to, months later, trying to write this honestly: *evidence*. Because that is what the water carried — the diversion tunnel's timber shoring, torn out whole; the company's filing cabinets, their drawers blooming open like startled fish, spilling forged survey sheets across the flooded flats; the boundary posts with their painted warnings, snapped off at the base and floating past us like fence pickets in a windstorm. Forty years of paperwork pretending the upper river didn't exist, all of it going downstream at last, exactly as rivers insist everything must.

A sheet of it slapped against the hull near Puerto Árido, and I picked it up because picking things up is what I am. It was a concession map in official hand, showing the headwater reach as *terreno improductivo* — unproductive land. Blank. A lie drawn in beautiful ink. I smoothed it against my knee and looked at it for a long time.

"Elena," Noor said quietly. "Let it go."

"No."

"No?"

"No. This one I'm keeping." I folded it and put it inside my coat, against my chest, where the old woman's hand-drawn map of the gorge had ridden for three weeks. "Someone drew this blank space on purpose. Someone got paid to draw it. And somewhere downstream there's a tribunal, or a magistrate, or failing that a room full of angry sheep farmers, and I intend to put this piece of paper in front of them and say: *here. Here is the crime. It has a border.*"

Noor looked at me then — really looked, the way Noor rarely allowed, because looking at people for too long was a form of stopping and Noor did not stop. "You know," Noor said, "when I hired on with your survey team, I thought the dangerous one was Calloway-Hurst."

"He was dangerous. He died telling the truth."

"Yes. Well." Noor turned back to the wheel. "You're dangerous in a more permanent way. He just wanted to confess. You want to *file*."

I have decided to take this as a compliment. I have decided this firmly and repeatedly, usually late at night, and it works about half the time.

* * *

We came down through the drowned country slowly, because the river made us slow and because, for once, no one was chasing us.

Reyes was gone. I want to write that plainly, because for eleven days his name had been a weather system over our lives, and then the dam broke and he simply — wasn't. Whether the flood took him, whether he walked out over the mountains toward the coast and a new name, whether he sat down in the fog above the spring and let the water decide — none of us ever learned. The tribunal sent inquiries. The company, dissolving, answered none of them. Pilar said once, in her flat practical way, "Men like that don't drown. Men like that are *transferred*." She was probably right. But some nights, when the wind came down the valley carrying the smell of wet stone, I permitted myself the other version, and slept well on it.

The four missing surveyors — alive, half-starved, imprisoned at the source as witnesses to their own silence — rode down with us as far as the first road out of the gorge. Their leader, a geologist named Ferrán with a beard full of twigs, shook my hand at the landing with both of his.

"The maps," he said. "You mapped the forbidden reach? As you went?"

"In pencil. In rain. On the backs of manifest pages."

"Better than anything the government ever paid for." He almost smiled; his face had forgotten how, but it tried. "They'll ask you to testify."

"I know."

"They'll also ask you to be reasonable. To round things off. To accept that the company's lawyers will need six months to review—" He stopped himself, and looked upriver, toward the fog country. "Don't be reasonable, Señora Vasquez. Reasonable is how the blank spaces get drawn."

I promised him. It is the only promise I have ever made that I have kept without any struggle whatsoever, because it turned out that being unreasonable is simply what happens when you have watched a river come home.

* * *

The tribunal sat in Puerto Árido, in the customs house, because it was the largest room and the only one with functioning windows. It was not grand. It was, in fact, rather shabby — a magistrate with ink-stained cuffs, two assessors, a stenographer who fell asleep twice, and a gallery of estancia families who arrived smelling of sheep and woodsmoke and stayed all day every day, standing along the walls when the benches filled, because their wells had been dry for a decade and someone had finally written down why.

I testified for four days.

I brought the photographs of the manifests — the drilling equipment disguised as survey crates, the claim-staking kit, the schedule of permits timed to expire while rivals slept. I brought Calloway-Hurst's field book, recovered from his body on the high trail, its final entries in a hand gone shaky with cold: *Saw the dam today. Saw the tunnel. God forgive me, I signed the maps anyway.* I brought the old woman's map of the gorge, drawn in pencil on flour sacking, and I told the room her name — Doña Mercedes Aguirre, of Estancia Salada — and I watched her grandson in the gallery stand up straighter than he had stood in years.

And on the fourth day I brought the blank map, the one that had slapped against the hull, and I laid it beside my own chart of the upper river, and I asked the stenographer to record the following:

"The concession company did not fail to find the source of the Rio Seco. It found the source, dammed it, tunneled it, and sold the water east while the western estancias went dry. Then it paid cartographers — including, briefly, me — to draw the place as empty. I am here to un-draw it."

The magistrate asked, gently, whether I understood that my own contract with the survey team could expose me to charges.

"I counted on it," I said.

He blinked. "You *counted* on it?"

"Señor Magistrate, I accepted employment from men I suspected of fraud, aboard a vessel piloted by a person running from a ghost, carrying cargo that breathed, into a gorge that whistles. If I were capable of avoiding trouble, do you think I'd be standing here?" A laugh went through the gallery — the tired, disbelieving laugh of people who have been thirsty for ten years and have just heard the word *rain*. Even the magistrate's mouth twitched. "Charge me or don't. But put my map in the record first."

He put it in the record.

* * *

The concession collapsed the way bad buildings collapse — not all at once, but in a series of decisions by people who had been waiting years for permission.

First the banks withdrew. Then the eastern buyers, discovering that the water they'd purchased was stolen from a river with a name and witnesses, withdrew. Then the directors began resigning in the peculiar order such men resign — fastest those furthest from the actual crimes, slowest the ones who'd signed the blank maps, until the last signature belonged to a junior clerk who wrote a confession of eight pages and fled to Chile, thereby doing more for the truth than the entire board had done in a decade.

It was quiet, all of it. That surprised me. I had expected thunder — arrests at dawn, headlines, Reyes in irons on the customs house steps. Instead there were notices in the trade papers, small ones; a liquidation notice nailed to the company office door in Puerto Árido, curling in the damp; and one gray morning, a crew of government engineers arriving at Estancia Salada with instruments and polite voices, asking permission to test the wells.

Doña Mercedes's grandson — Tomás Aguirre the younger, though everyone called him Toto, which caused Noor a private pain I only later understood — walked the engineers out to the old well himself. I went along. So did Noor, who had business in Salada that Noor described as "none of yours," which meant it was entirely mine and I pretended otherwise.

The engineer lowered the sounding line. We waited. Wind moved the dust around the yard the way it always had, indifferent, ancient.

"Twelve meters," the engineer said. He frowned, checked the figure, checked it again. "Water at twelve meters. Last recorded level was—" he consulted his folder— "dry. Completely dry, nine years."

Toto made a sound I will never forget — not a cheer, nothing so organized. Just a small noise, like a man who has been holding a heavy door shut for a decade and has finally been told he can set it down. Doña Mercedes, ninety-one and blind in one eye, reached out and touched the well casing with her palm, flat, the way you touch a horse's neck.

"The river remembers who drinks first," she said. "It seems it remembers who drank *last*, too."

* * *

The wells rose all that autumn. Not everywhere, not evenly — justice, I have learned, is a groundwater phenomenon: it arrives, but it seeps, and some fields wait longer than others, and a few, unfairly, wait still. The eastern diversion works were dismantled by court order, but dismantling takes seasons, and the lawyers of the dissolved company proved more durable than their dams. Two of them are appealing still. Let them appeal. Every season they delay is a season the record grows heavier, and records, unlike directors, do not flee to Chile.

In the spring I published the map.

I had offers, of course. A Buenos Aires firm wanted it, with their imprint and their corrections and their suggestion that "Vasquez" be softened to something with more consonants of consequence. An academic journal wanted it, provided I frame the upper reach as "contested hydrology" rather than, as I insisted, "a crime scene with contours." And one gentleman — smooth, cologned, representing interests I will leave unnamed — offered me a sum that would have bought three boats, if I would agree to publish the source's location only approximately.

Approximately. As if the river were a rumor.

Noor, when I told this story at the kitchen table of the Salada ferry house, laughed until Pilar had to thump Noor's back. "Three boats," Noor wheezed. "You could have had three boats."

"I have one boat," I said. "It's enough."

"It's *my* boat."

"It's the boat I chose. There's a difference, and the difference is worth more than two boats."

The map went out under my name alone — Elena Vasquez — with a dedication line I fought the printers for and won: *For C.H., who saw it and signed anyway, and told the truth with his last warmth.* And beneath the title block, in the blank space where forty years of official charts had written *terreno improductivo*, I printed the old words in small capitals, where every pilot, every lawyer, every schoolchild tracing the river with one finger would eventually run across them:

**THE RIVER REMEMBERS WHO DRINKS FIRST.**

* * *

There remains one thing to say about that autumn, and I have been circling it for pages the way the flood circled the bend below the dam, and I might as well say it plainly, because Noor certainly won't.

I stayed.

Not the way travelers stay — a week, a season, until the money runs out or the memory gets close. I stayed the way a river stays in its channel: because the channel fit, and because leaving would have been a kind of drowning. I told myself at first it was temporary. I told myself it was for the map, for the testimony, for the tribunal's endless follow-up depositions. I told myself a great many things with a straight face, and Pilar watched me tell them with the expression of a seventeen-year-old who finds adults hilarious, and winter came down off the meseta, and I did not leave.

Noor noticed, of course. Noor notices everything except the obvious.

"You could winter in Buenos Aires," Noor said one night in September, not looking up from the engine manual, which was a technique. "Your family writes. Your mother writes *weekly*. There's a marriage waiting in that correspondence like a fish under ice."

"There is no marriage waiting. There is a *negotiation* waiting, and I have sunk it in deep water."

"Deep water sinks most things."

"Not this. I weighted it properly." I turned the page of my own book, which was also a technique. "Besides, someone has to teach Pilar the current charts properly. She reads them like poetry. Poetry is lovely but it doesn't get you past the shoal at Kilometer 40."

"She got us past the shoal at Kilometer 40."

"She got us past it *once*, on adrenaline, with divine assistance. I'm talking about craft."

Noor looked up then, and for a moment neither of us employed any technique at all, and the stove ticked, and outside the wind walked down the valley smelling of rain that hadn't fallen yet but intended to.

"You're staying," Noor said. Not a question. A man checking his soundings.

"I'm staying," I said. "Unless you intend to throw me off the boat."

Noor considered this with great seriousness. "The boat's barely seaworthy. Extra weight might be the thing that saves it."

"That is the least romantic sentence ever spoken in South America."

"I'm a riverboat captain. Romance is above my draft line." But Noor's voice had gone rough, the way it did when the guilt came close and was turned away again, and Noor reached across the table and put one muddy hand over mine, briefly, like a stamp on a document. Filed. Witnessed. Done.

Outside, far off toward the hills, I heard it — faint, unmistakable, impossible to mistake for anything else.

Rain.

* * *

We went down to the landing the next morning to see it ourselves, because in Salada rain is news, and news travels faster than the mail. Toto was already there, hatless, letting the drops hit his face like a fool, grinning like a bigger one. Pilar stood in the doorway of the ferry shed with her arms crossed, pretending to be unmoved, getting steadily wetter.

And coming up the road from the south, walking easy, coat collar up, was a figure I knew by the set of the shoulders before I could make out the face — someone who had walked out of the high country once already and somehow, impossibly, was walking again.

Noor went very still beside me.

"Noor," I said carefully, "isn't Calloway-Hurst dead?"

"No," Noor said slowly, eyes fixed on the road. "Calloway-Hurst is dead. But that walk..." The rain thickened, silvering the air between us and the figure. "That walk belongs to somebody else I buried years ago."

The figure raised one hand — a wave, or a warning, I couldn't tell — and kept coming.
