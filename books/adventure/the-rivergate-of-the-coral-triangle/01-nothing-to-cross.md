## 1. Nothing to Cross

The salt flats at midday were a lie told in white.

Rosalind Okafor had crossed them eleven times in her career as a ranger of the Karst Reach, and every time they tried to convince her the world ended somewhere out past the shimmer. No trees. No shade. No horizon worth trusting — just a glare that ate the sky and a crust of salt that crunched under her boots like old porcelain. The flats didn't want travelers. They wanted bones, politely arranged.

She walked anyway, because walking was the only thing she'd ever been good at that nobody could take from her.

*Inventory*, she thought, the way her training officer had drilled into her twenty years ago. *When you're lost, count what you have. When you're found, count it again.*

Water: four liters, two skins, sipped by schedule and not by thirst, because thirst was a liar worse than the horizon. Rope: thirty meters of braided desert hemp, coiled across her chest, good for climbing, better for dragging a bound prisoner if it came to that. It would come to that. Rations: hardtack, dried figs, a strip of salted goat she was saving for a bad day. The bad day was today, technically, but she was saving it anyway on principle. A ranger's principles were mostly stubbornness wearing a uniform.

And then there was the rest of it. The part that didn't fit on any inventory sheet.

A debt.

She turned the thought over as she walked, the way you'd turn a stone to see what lived underneath. Three months ago her brother Theo had died face-down in the red dirt outside Perdition Wells with a knife between his shoulder blades and his hand still curled around an empty space where his compass used to be. She knew it was empty because she'd pried the fingers open herself, gently, the way you do when there's nothing left to be gentle about. His palm had been printed with the ghost of a shape — a circle worn into the skin, the way a ring leaves its mark after forty years. He'd carried that compass since before she could remember. Longer than he'd carried anything else, including, she sometimes suspected, his own name.

The thief's name was Kess. That much she knew. Tall, narrow, quick hands, a face like a man perpetually listening to a conversation happening one room away. He'd passed through Perdition Wells two days before Theo died, bought nothing, sold nothing, asked odd questions about the old coast road, and left a debt of four coppers at the Waystation Inn that Rosalind had paid off herself, because even a man you intend to drag back in chains deserves a clean ledger. Then Theo had gone into the hills to meet someone about something — Theo was always meeting someone about something; he'd been a fixer, a finder, a quiet middleman for other people's secrets — and come down again without his compass and without his life.

Kess had been seen heading south. Toward the flats. Toward the coast.

So here she was.

* * *

By late afternoon the glare softened into something almost kind, and Rosalind allowed herself the luxury of sitting down against her pack and eating three figs instead of two. In the distance, a mirage had assembled itself into a lake so convincing that her mouth watered out of pure spite. She'd learned long ago not to hate the mirages. They were just the flats being the flats. You couldn't hold geography accountable for its personality.

What she could hold accountable was a thief.

She'd chased thieves before. Eleven years a ranger, and the arithmetic came out to something like forty recoveries: stolen horses, stolen water rights, a stolen wedding dowry that had turned out to be eloped rather than stolen, which had made for an awkward report. Thieves were a category. You tracked them, you caught them, you took back what they took, and everyone went home slightly poorer but legible. The world made sense afterward. That was the whole job, really — making sense afterward.

This was different. This was the first time anyone had stolen something that couldn't be replaced, and she didn't mean the compass itself. She meant what was inside it. Because Theo's compass didn't point north. It never had. She'd figured that out when she was nine, spinning it on the kitchen table while Theo pretended not to watch, and it had swung lazily, insistently, toward the southeast — toward the coast, toward the sea, toward some fixed point that wasn't on any map she'd ever owned. She'd asked him why, once, and he'd smiled his closed-door smile and said, "It points where I need to go," and she'd been young enough to find that romantic instead of suspicious.

Her brother had needed to go southeast his entire life, apparently, and had spent fifty years not going.

Now the compass was gone, and with it the only honest thing Theo had ever owned — because everything else about him had been negotiable. His name changed depending on who was asking. His history rearranged itself like sand. Even his laugh had a rental quality to it. But the compass was constant. It pointed southeast through wars and droughts and their mother's funeral, and Rosalind had built, without ever admitting it, a small private faith on top of that needle: whatever else her brother was, wherever else he'd been, there was one true direction in him.

Someone had taken it. Someone named Kess, currently somewhere ahead of her on this white hell, leaving footprints that the afternoon wind was already tidying away.

She stood up, brushed salt from her trousers, and kept walking.

* * *

She caught his trail at dusk, where the flats began their slow argument with the first low dunes.

He was good. She'd give him that, and she gave very few people things. Most fugitives ran in straight lines, panicked lines, lines you could read like a confession. Kess moved like a man who'd studied how trails die. He'd walked the spine of a salt ridge so the wind could erase him. He'd doubled back through a patch of crust thin enough to swallow a boot, and she'd found the crack where it had nearly done exactly that. He'd even — and this was the detail that made her snort despite everything — paused to eat, because there was a neat little circle of disturbed salt where someone had sat cross-legged for ten minutes, and beside it, pressed carefully into the crust, a single dried fig stem.

He'd eaten a fig. On the run. With a ranger behind him.

"Either you're calm," she said to the empty flats, "or you're insane, and I've arrested both kinds."

But there was something else in the trail, something that kept snagging her attention like a burr in a sock. His line wasn't random. Fugitives meandered; that was the nature of fear. Kess's line bent, gently, consistently, southeast. The same bearing the compass pointed. As if he weren't running from anything at all, but following something — following it with the confidence of a man reading instructions off the inside of his own eyelids.

She crouched over the fig stem and let herself think the ugly thought she'd been keeping in a locked room at the back of her skull for three months.

*What if the compass wanted him?*

No. Compasses didn't want. Compasses were brass and glass and a magnetized needle, and grief made people believe otherwise. Her mother had believed rivers remembered. Her brother had believed — well. Her brother had believed in whatever the room required. Rosalind believed in tracks, water math, and the weight of a body in a grave she'd dug herself.

Except.

Except that when she'd finally opened Theo's effects, all two crates of them, she'd found the strangest thing: no map to the southeast. A man who owned a compass pointing at one fixed spot for fifty years ought to have maps. Charts. Notes. Something. Theo had nothing. Not a scrap. As if the destination were something you weren't supposed to write down. As if the compass weren't a tool at all but a promise — or a leash.

She pocketed the fig stem, absurdly, and moved on.

* * *

Night on the flats was a different country. The heat surrendered all at once, the way a crowd goes quiet, and the cold came up out of the ground like something surfacing for air. Rosalind made camp in the lee of a salt ridge, wrapped in her blanket, and permitted herself the nightly ritual she'd never admit to anyone: talking to her brother.

"You'd laugh at this," she said to the stars. "You'd say, Roz, sweetheart, the man is three days ahead of you and the flats will kill you before he does. Write it off. Grieve properly. Buy a new hat."

Theo had always recommended hats for unsolvable problems. It was one of his hundred small evasions dressed up as wisdom.

"But you didn't have a new hat," she went on. "You had a compass, and somebody pried it out of your dead hand, and I keep doing the arithmetic where I catch him and take it back and put it on your grave, and none of the sums come out right. Because it won't bring you back, and we both know it, and I'm doing it anyway. That's not justice. That's just me refusing to let the story end wrong."

The stars declined to comment. Stars were worse than mirages that way.

She rolled onto her side. Somewhere out in the dark, a kilometer maybe, maybe less, a man named Kess was also lying on salt, also awake, probably also eating something he shouldn't. She found she wasn't angry at him tonight, which annoyed her. Anger was fuel, and she was running low, and sympathy for the target was a leak in the tank.

Because here was the thing she'd pieced together from the innkeeper and the well-keeper and the old woman who sold water at the flats' edge: nobody had a bad word for Kess. Forty recoveries, and she'd never once tracked a thief whose witnesses described him with *affection*. The innkeeper said he'd fixed her leaking roof unprompted. The well-keeper said he'd pulled a child out of a cistern. The old woman said he'd listened — actually listened, both ears, no wandering eyes — to forty minutes of her complaints about her knees, and then shown her a stretch for them that worked.

"He's not a thief," the old woman had said, squinting at Rosalind's badge. "Whatever he took, I'd wager he was carrying it for someone."

Carrying it for someone. Rosalind turned that phrase over now, in the dark, and hated how well it fit next to the empty circle printed in her brother's dead palm.

*Stop,* she told herself. *Tomorrow you corner him. Tomorrow you ask your questions and he answers or he doesn't, and either way the compass comes home. One job. Simple.*

She slept badly, which was standard, and dreamed of the kitchen table, which was new. In the dream the compass spun and spun and Theo watched it with an expression she'd never seen on his waking face — not the closed-door smile but something rawer, something like homesickness — and he said, in a voice that was almost not his, "It doesn't point where I need to go, Roz. It points where I'm *from*." And then the dream flooded white, salt to every horizon, and she woke with grit in her teeth and the certain, unshakable feeling that her brother had been afraid of the truth for fifty years, and had died still holding the door shut.

* * *

Dawn came up rose-gold and merciless, and with it, the sea smell.

That was the hook of it, the thing that had pulled her across three hundred kilometers of salt: the air changed. You smelled the coast before you saw it — iodine and wet stone and something green underneath, something alive, the opposite of everything behind her. Rosalind crested the last salt ridge at first light and stopped, pack straps biting her shoulders, and looked at the edge of the world.

The flats ended in cliffs. Below them, the ocean lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky just deciding whether to be blue. And along the base of the cliffs, exposed by a tide so far out it looked like a mistake, stretched a shelf of black rock studded with tide pools, each one holding its own small perfect sky.

And there, half a kilometer out along the shelf, a figure sat on a rock with his elbows on his knees, facing the water.

Not hiding. Not running. Sitting, in the open, in plain view, like a man waiting for a ferry.

Rosalind stood very still and did the professional arithmetic. Distance: five hundred meters. Cover: essentially none — the shelf was bare black stone, and any approach would happen in full view. Escape routes available to the target: the sea, the cliff caves, or nowhere. Energy reserves: hers adequate, his unknown. Probability that a man with his demonstrated skill had failed to notice the only ranger within a hundred kilometers cresting the only ridge in sight: effectively zero.

Which meant he was waiting for *her*.

Every instinct she owned filed a complaint. She overruled them, because the alternative was standing on a ridge forever, and started down. The switchback trail was old and slick, cut by hands unknown, and halfway down she noticed the strangest thing: the rock was carved. Faintly, worn nearly smooth, but unmistakable once seen — spirals, fish, waves, and repeated over and over, small as a signature, a circle with a needle through it.

A compass mark. Chiseled into coastal stone older than any nation she worked for.

Her mouth went dry in a way the salt flats hadn't managed in three days.

She reached the shelf. The tide pools ticked and breathed around her boots. Fifty meters out, the man on the rock heard her coming — of course he did — and turned, and it was him: tall, narrow, quick-eyed, with the face of a man listening to a conversation one room away. Up close, he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. He looked, she realized with a jolt of professional offense, *relieved*.

"Ranger Okafor," Kess said. His voice was hoarse, like he'd been arguing with himself for days. "Three days behind me. I'm impressed. Most people turn back at the flats."

"You know my name."

"I know a lot of things." He looked back at the water, and she followed his gaze, and saw what she should have seen from the ridge: far out along the shelf, where the cliffs dipped to meet the sea, the retreating tide had uncovered a cave mouth. Black stone, wet-lipped, breathing fog. And deep inside it — she blinked, sure the glare was playing tricks — a faint glow. Blue. Patient. Like a lamp lit in a house that had been empty a very long time.

"The compass wants to go home," Kess said quietly. He patted his coat, where something round and heavy hung against his chest. "I've carried it for three years, Ranger, and it has never once pointed north. It points *there*. And the tide only opens the door twice a year." He stood, and turned to face her fully, and spread his empty hands — the gesture of a man surrendering to something larger than her. "I was going to run. I had a boat lined up and everything. But it wouldn't stop pulling, and I got so tired, and then I thought — she'll come anyway. She's her brother's sister. So I'll wait, and I'll show her the door, and she can decide what kind of fool she wants to be."

Rosalind's hand rested on her oath-knife. Every rule she'd sworn by said: bind him, take it, walk home, file the report, make sense afterward.

The blue light in the cave pulsed, slow as a sleeping heartbeat.

"Why," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, "would a thief lead his pursuer onward?"

Kess smiled, and for the first time it reached all the way to his eyes, and it was the saddest smile she'd ever seen on a living man.

"Because," he said, nodding at the glowing dark, "you can't arrest what's down there. And neither can I. Come on. The tide's going out, and it isn't going to wait for either of us to be brave."
