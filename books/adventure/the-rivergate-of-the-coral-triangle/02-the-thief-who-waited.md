## 2. The Thief Who Waited

The sea announced itself before Rosalind saw it — a low, mineral smell riding the wind off the flats, like wet stone and old salt. She had been three days on Kess's trail by then, and in those three days she had learned a great deal about him, most of it against her will.

She learned that he chewed a root that stained his spit red, because she kept finding little crimson flecks where he'd rested. She learned that he slept cold, because his fire rings were always generous, built for warmth rather than concealment, which was either arrogance or exhaustion. She learned that he was left-handed, that he favored gullies over ridgelines, and that once — only once — he had doubled back along his own trail to look at something behind him.

That last one had bothered her more than she liked to admit. Thieves didn't look back. Thieves ran forward with their ears full of their own heartbeat. But this one had stopped, crouched at the top of a dune, and stared back the way he'd come — the way *she* had come — for a long moment before moving on.

Ranger training said: he's checking your position. Ranger instinct, the older and less tidy voice, said something else entirely, and Rosalind had spent the better part of a day telling that voice to shut up.

Now the salt flats gave way to black volcanic rock, and the rock gave way to sand, and the sand ended at the sea — a long, gray-green sweep of it under a sky the color of an old bruise healing. The tide was out so far that the water seemed embarrassed about it, sulking a quarter mile from where any honest high-tide line would sit. Tide pools glinted like dropped coins. Gulls argued over something dead and unidentifiable.

And there, standing at the water's edge with his boots off and his trousers rolled to the knee, was Kess.

He wasn't running. That was the first thing her body registered, before her mind caught up — the animal part of her that had been coiled for three days expecting a chase went briefly, stupidly blank. He was just *standing there*, ankle-deep in a tide pool, looking down into it as if it owed him money.

Rosalind's hand went to her knife out of pure habit. Her other hand checked the strap of her pack, the coil of rope at her hip, the water skins — two full, one half. The inventory she performed every morning without thinking, the small arithmetic of staying alive. Water, rope, knife, oath-knife, and the debt. Always the debt.

"Kess," she called, and her voice came out flatter than she intended, scraped thin by three days of wind.

He didn't startle. That was the second wrong thing. He turned around slowly, squinting against the light, and when he saw her his expression did something she had never seen a cornered thief's expression do.

It relaxed.

"Well," he said. "You're faster than I expected."

"I'm persistent. Faster is just what persistence looks like from behind." She came down the rocks carefully, never taking her eyes off him, choosing each foothold the way her instructors had beaten into her: *the ground is always trying to kill you; be polite but don't trust it.* "Give me the compass."

"No," he said, pleasantly.

The flatness of it — no fear, no bluster, no bargaining — knocked her stride sideways for half a step. She covered it by stopping, planting her feet in the wet sand, and letting the silence do some work. Silence was a tool. Half of rangering was knowing when to let a silence stretch until the other person filled it with something useful.

Kess did not fill it. He just looked at her with mild, patient interest, like a man waiting for a bus he knew was late.

"You've been leading me," she said finally. It wasn't a question, and it surprised her that she hadn't said it sooner. Three days of generous fires. Three days of a trail that was almost too legible, as if someone were leaving signposts for a reader instead of tracks for a pursuer. "You could have lost me at the salt pans. You didn't even try."

"Would you have stopped following?"

"Yes."

"Liar," he said, with such good humor that it took her a moment to feel insulted. "You'd have followed the ghost of my trail. I watched you read sign outside Harrow Bend. You read ground the way priests read scripture — like it's personally addressed to you." He nodded back the way she'd come, toward the desert. "No. If I'd shaken you, you'd have spent the rest of your life wondering. And she—" he touched his shirt pocket, where the compass rode, "—doesn't want that."

"She," Rosalind repeated. "It's a compass."

"It's a *she*," Kess said, with the serene confidence of a man who had clearly had this argument with himself many times and won. "And she wants to go home."

Something cold walked down Rosalind's spine, despite the warm wind. "Compasses point north. That's not wanting. That's magnetism."

"This one points north," Kess agreed. He drew it out of his pocket then — and her whole body leaned toward it before she could stop herself, hungry, the way you lean toward a window when you hear a familiar voice in the street. It was smaller than she remembered. Smaller than it had any right to be, for how large it loomed in her life. A disc of dark metal gone soft-edged with handling, a glass face clouded like breath on a winter pane, and under the glass the needle — trembling now, in Kess's open palm, straining seaward with a kind of eagerness that made her stomach hurt.

Because it wasn't pointing north. It was pointing at the ocean. Straight out, past the tide pools, past the sullen gray water, at nothing.

"That's broken," she said.

"That's *pointing*," Kess said. "There's a difference." He crouched and held the compass near the surface of the tide pool, and the needle swung, slow and deliberate, adjusting itself to aim at some fixed point beneath the water. "Three years I've carried her. Bought her off a dying man in a border town who couldn't pay his room and paid with this instead. And every day since, wherever I am, she leans. Not north. *Seaward.* Like a dog at a door." He looked up at her, and for the first time the pleasantness fell away and she saw what was underneath it, which was tiredness — a deep, worn-smooth tiredness, the kind you can't sleep off. "I stole plenty of things before her, Ranger. Real thefts. I'm not proud, but I'm honest about it. This is the first thing I ever took that I think might have been taking *me*."

"Poetry," Rosalind said. "From a thief. Give me the compass, Kess. It belonged to my brother. It's all that's left of him, and I am going to have it back if I have to carry you to the magistrate in pieces."

"And what will the magistrate do with it?" Kess asked, genuinely curious, which was somehow worse than mockery. "Melt it down? Lock it in a drawer? Put it on a shelf between someone's confiscated dice and a smuggler's false-bottomed trunk?" He shook his head. "You don't want justice. You want her back because she's the last piece of him that still *moves*. I understand that. I do. It's the reason I haven't sold her, and believe me, Ranger, people have offered." He stood, brushing sand from his knee, unhurried. "But she doesn't belong in a drawer, and I'm starting to suspect she didn't belong to your brother either. Not originally."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means," Kess said, "that a dying man gave me this compass and made me swear to carry it somewhere, and I've spent three years failing to figure out where, and then three days ago she started pulling so hard I couldn't sleep, and I realized she wasn't pulling anymore. She'd arrived. We'd arrived." He turned and pointed — not at the sea generally, but at a specific place in it, a quarter mile out where the water lay strangely flat and dark between two black headlands. "There."

Rosalind looked. There was nothing there. Rocks, water, the bruised sky. Nothing.

"There's nothing there."

"Not right now," Kess agreed. "But the tide's going out further than it should. Look at the pools. Look at the wrack line — see how high the kelp sits? The water's been dropping all morning like it's been told to." He rolled his trousers down and sat on a rock to pull his boots back on, and the sheer domesticity of it — sitting down to lace his boots while his pursuer stood over him with a knife — was so absurd that Rosalind actually laughed, one short bark of a laugh that the wind took away.

"You're insane," she said.

"Probably." He tied the laces. "But you're going to wait with me anyway."

"I am going to arrest you."

"After," he said. "Arrest me after. I'll even walk to the magistrate myself, hands out, very cooperative. But not yet. Because if I'm wrong, you get to say 'I told you so' for the rest of your natural life, and if I'm right—" he patted his pocket, where the compass had gone quiet, "—then you'll want to see it. Whatever it is. Because it's hers, and she was his, and you've been following both of us across a desert on the strength of that."

Rosalind opened her mouth to tell him exactly what he could do with his reasoning.

And then the sea moved.

Not a wave — waves she knew, waves had grammar and rhythm. This was different. The whole gray sheet of the ocean seemed to inhale, drawing back another fifty yards with a sound like a giant sliding a table across a floor, and the gulls went up all at once, screaming, wheeling inland as if they'd gotten news from somewhere. The tide pools shrank. Fish flopped in the shrinking shallows, silver flashes of panic. The air changed too — cooler, tasting of stone and something greener, something that had no business being anywhere near a coast.

"Rip current," Rosalind said automatically, because naming things was how she stayed calm. "Undertow. Freak tide. There are explanations—"

"There are always explanations," Kess said, standing. "My favorite one is: *she said she wanted to go home.*"

Between the two black headlands, where the water had withdrawn, the seabed lay exposed — and in the seabed, a cave mouth.

It shouldn't have been visible. It was below the low-tide line, below anything sensible, a ragged arch of black rock that the retreating water unveiled inch by inch like a stage curtain. And from inside it came light. Faint, wrong, beautiful — a blue glow, deep as evening, pulsing gently, the way a lantern pulses when someone behind it is breathing.

Rosalind's ranger mind supplied facts, usefully and far too late: bioluminescence, algae, phosphorescent rot, a dozen mundane explanations, all of them plausible, none of them accounting for the way the light seemed to keep time with her own heartbeat, or the way the compass in Kess's pocket — she could hear it, faintly, a fine metallic shivering through the air — had begun to sing against its casing.

"Why," she said slowly, "would a thief lead his pursuer onward?"

Kess smiled, and it was a strange smile, crooked and scared and hopeful all at once, the smile of a man stepping off a ledge and trusting the air. "Because maybe I'm not a thief," he said. "Maybe I'm a courier who's three years late. Come find out."

He picked up his pack and started walking toward the glowing cave mouth, boots crunching on wet sand, not looking back to see whether she followed — which was, she thought wildly, either tremendous confidence or the single most irritating thing anyone had ever done to her.

Rosalind stood at the tideline with her hand on her knife and her brother's debt in her chest, and felt the first crack run through the solid wall of her certainty. Thieves ran. Thieves hid. Thieves did not wait at the edge of the world with their boots laced, inviting arrest, talking about compasses like they were widows. Everything she had told herself for three days — *he's clever, he's cruel, he's baiting you* — was still possible. But it was no longer comfortable, and comfort, she was beginning to suspect, had been doing a lot of load-bearing work in her anger.

The blue light pulsed. The sea kept withdrawing, groaning, like something enormous making room.

"Kess." Her voice cracked across the flats.

He stopped but didn't turn.

"If this is a trap," she said, "I want you to know I'll be extremely unpleasant about it."

"Noted," he said. "For the record — if it's a trap, we're both in it. That's rather the point of coming together."

Which was, she would think later, the exact moment she decided to follow him. Not because she believed the compass wanted to go home. Not because of the impossible light. But because he had said *we*, casually, as if it were obvious, as if three days of pursuit and a knife at the ready were just weather between them — and because some stubborn, buried part of her, the part that had been alone since her brother died, heard that word and wanted it to be true.

She checked her pack straps. Two waterskins full, one half. Rope. Knife. Oath-knife. Debt.

"Wait for me, then," she muttered, and went down onto the wet sand after him, toward the cave mouth and its breathing blue light, while behind them the sea stood aside like a held breath — and ahead, in the dark of the arch, something ancient and patient began, very softly, to hum.
