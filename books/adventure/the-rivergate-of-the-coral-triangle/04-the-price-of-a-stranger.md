## 4. The Price of a Stranger

The cell was not what Rosalind expected of a prison.

For one thing, it had a window. For another, the window had a view — and what a view. Beyond a sill worn smooth by generations of elbows, the gorge of Rivergate opened like a book someone had left face-down mid-sentence: terraced stone houses clinging to both walls, bridges strung between them like stitches, and below it all the river itself, green-gold and muscular, sliding through the city with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once been late.

Rosalind sat on the cot — also not what she'd expected; it had an actual mattress stuffed with something that smelled faintly of river-grass — and took inventory, because taking inventory was what she did when the world went strange. Water: one clay jug, refilled that morning by a guard who hadn't spoken. Food: bread, dense and sweetish, with a crust that fought back. Rope: none, which stung. And the debt she owed her brother's memory: incalculable, non-refundable, currently locked in a gatekeeper's strongbox three floors down in the shape of a brass compass.

She'd been counting hours by the light that crawled across the far wall. Six since they'd pulled her, coughing and half-drowned, out of the whirlpool pool at the city's edge. Six since a woman in slate-gray robes with a voice like a closing door had said the word *assessment* and made it sound like a diagnosis.

"Assessment," Rosalind said now, to the room. "Of what? My posture?"

The room declined to answer. It was very good at that.

* * *

The guard came at what Rosalind judged to be midday, judging by the angle of light and the growl of her stomach. He was young, broad, and moved with the careful politeness of someone who had been instructed that prisoners were people but hadn't entirely believed it yet. He set down a tray — more bread, a bowl of fish stew that smelled better than any prison food had a right to — and turned to go.

"Wait," Rosalind said. "Questions."

He paused in the doorway without turning around. That, she noted, was a technique. She'd used it herself on skittish informants.

"One question," he said.

"Why am I here?"

That got him to turn. He looked at her the way you'd look at someone who'd asked why water was wet. "You came through the Gate."

"I fell through the Gate. There's a difference. Falling is involuntary."

"Not to the river." He said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "Everyone who comes through owes passage. Strangers pay in truth or labor. The Gatekeepers assess which."

"Truth or labor," Rosalind repeated. "Like a toll booth run by a tax auditor."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — suppressed, but there. Warmth under the procedure. She filed that away. People who suppressed smiles were people who could be reached.

"How long does an assessment take?"

"However long it takes." He hesitated, then added, as if paying for the smile he hadn't given, "Ask your window. Windows tell more than guards do."

Then he was gone, and Rosalind was alone with the stew, which she ate completely, because a ranger who turns up her nose at prison food deserves whatever happens next.

* * *

The window told more than the guard did. By the second day, Rosalind had stopped pacing and started watching, and by the third day she had found a sliver of charcoal — a broken piece of a writing stick some previous occupant had wedged into a crack in the sill, either carelessly or as a gift across time, and she chose to believe the latter — and began to draw.

Not art. Mapping. The difference mattered to her the way the difference between water and drowning mattered.

From her window she could see perhaps a third of the city. She worked methodically, the way her brother had taught her to read country from a ridge: don't look at things, look at *relationships*. The bridges first — seven of them visible, each a different design, which meant different builders or different eras, which meant history, which meant politics. The two great staircases cut into opposite walls of the gorge, one wide and public, one narrow and guarded. The cluster of slate-roofed buildings near the upstream end where gray-robed figures moved in patterns too regular to be anything but ritual. The market terraces, loud even at this distance, color spilling down the steps like paint knocked over.

And always, always, the river. Running from the upstream dark beneath the cliffs — where, the guard had finally admitted on day four, in exchange for her silence about his almost-smile, the source lay sealed behind stone older than the city — down through the gorge and out through the drowned gates at the seaward end.

"The only way out is up," the guard had said. "Through the source. And the dry season seals it." He'd held up his hand, fingers spread. "Forty days. Thirty-six, now."

Thirty-six days. Rosalind had written the number in charcoal at the top of her map and underlined it twice, hard enough to snap the stick's remaining nub, so that she'd had to wedge it back into its crack and feel foolish about it.

* * *

On the fifth day, the woman with the door-closing voice came to see her.

Her name, she announced, was Vesh — no title offered, none needed, apparently — and she entered the cell the way weather enters a valley: completely, and without asking permission. She was older than Rosalind had guessed from across the assessment hall, silver at her temples, eyes the flat bright green of shallow water over rock. She looked at the walls, the cot, the window, and last of all at the wall beside the window, where Rosalind's map had grown to cover most of the available stone.

Vesh studied it for a long moment.

"You've been busy."

"It passes the time."

"You've marked the guard rotations." Vesh touched one of the small x's Rosalind had scratched along the narrow staircase. "There are no guard rotations on that stair. Those men are masons. They've been repairing the same landing for a month."

Rosalind kept her face still, which cost her. "Then your masons are very slow."

"They're thorough." Vesh turned from the map and looked at her directly, and Rosalind felt the look the way you feel a current under calm water — nothing dramatic, just the steady knowledge that the surface was lying to you. "You count exits. You befriend guards. You eat everything you're given and complain about none of it. You are assessing *us* while we assess you."

"That seems fair."

"It would, if you were any good at hiding it." But there — again — the ghost of amusement, quickly shuttered. Vesh crossed to the window and looked out at her own city as if checking it for damage. "Do you know why we ask strangers for truth or labor?"

"Because you can?"

"Because Rivergate survives on what flows through it." Vesh's reflection in the glass was unreadable. "Water. Goods. Gossip. And truth. A stranger who pays in labor gives us her hands for a season. A stranger who pays in truth gives us something we cannot get any other way: the view from outside. What we look like to someone who didn't grow up believing our walls hold up the sky." She glanced back. "Which will you pay?"

"That depends on what you're buying."

"Everything, eventually. That's what an assessment determines — the price." Vesh moved toward the door, then paused with her hand on the frame. "Your companion pays differently. Did you know that? He has been in assessment five days and has answered every question except the ones that matter. He lies beautifully. It's almost a craft."

"He stole from me," Rosalind said. "Lying is the least of it."

"Mm." Vesh looked at her a moment longer than was comfortable. "One more thing, ranger. You should know that the compass your friend carried — the one the Gate refused when he tried to pay with it —"

"It's mine," Rosalind said, too fast, too sharp. "It was my brother's."

"Yes," Vesh said quietly. "That is what interests me. Because the Gate refuses payment that isn't honestly owned. And it refused him." She let that sit in the cell like a stone dropped in still water, then inclined her head. "Think about what that means. Assessment concludes tomorrow. Rest well."

* * *

Rosalind did not rest well.

She lay on the too-comfortable mattress and stared at the ceiling and turned Vesh's parting words over and over, looking for the trick in them. *The Gate refuses payment that isn't honestly owned.* Kess had held out the compass at the gate — she'd seen it, seen the gatekeepers' faces change, seen the refusal land on him like a verdict. At the time she'd thought: good, the thief's stolen goods rejected, justice with a sense of theater.

But if the Gate only refused what wasn't truly owned — whose was it, then?

Her brother's. Her brother's, obviously. He'd carried it across a desert and died holding it, and she'd chased it across another one, and if some ancient river-gate's magic had opinions about ownership, then surely —

Surely it was hers by inheritance. Surely.

Except the compass had never pointed north. Not once, not in all the years Tomas had carried it, not in all the months she'd chased it. It pointed where it wanted, and lately what it wanted was upstream, into the dark under the cliffs, toward a source that would seal itself in thirty-five days and take the only exit with it.

Rosalind got up, crossed to the window, and looked out at the sleeping city. Lamps burned low on the bridges. Somewhere below, the river ran on in the dark, patient as a creditor.

"Thirty-five days," she told it.

The river, being a river, did not answer. But far downstream, at the drowned gates where the water met the sea, something boomed — deep, rhythmic, like a heart heard through a wall. The tide, coming in. Sealing the sea-cave behind her. Closing doors behind while the ones ahead counted down.

She went back to her map and, by the light of the lamp they'd left burning — another kindness that felt like a leash — added what she'd learned today. The masons' stair. The assessment hall's position relative to the strongbox. A small, bitter note beside the upstream cliffs: *EXIT — 35 DAYS.*

Then, because the charcoal was in her hand and honesty was apparently the local currency, she added one more line underneath, small enough that only she would ever read it:

*Whose compass is it really?*

Tomorrow, assessment concluded. Tomorrow, a price named. Tomorrow, presumably, a choice between her hands for a season and her secrets laid open on some gatekeeper's table — and Rosalind Okafor had spent her whole life learning that secrets were the only currency that never devalued.

She blew out the lamp and lay back down.

Sleep came slowly, and just before it took her, she heard footsteps in the corridor outside — soft, unhurried, stopping briefly at her door before moving on. Not Vesh's step. Lighter. Almost familiar, in a way she couldn't place and didn't like.

In the morning, she learned, she would find out what she was worth.

In the morning, a girl with river-mud on her boots and her father's ferry rope coiled over one shoulder would be waiting outside the assessment hall, chewing a stem of sweetgrass, about to become the first person in Rivergate to treat Rosalind like a person instead of a payment.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was only the map, the countdown, and the sound of the river running through the dark toward a door that was already beginning to close.
