## 5. Kess Explains Nothing

The cell they put Kess in was nicer than most rooms he'd paid good coin for, which he found insulting on principle.

It had a window — a real one, cut high in the gorge wall, framed with river-polished stone — and through it he could see a slice of Rivergate: terraced gardens clinging to the cliff like green stitches, footbridges strung between towers, and everywhere the water, moving with the unhurried confidence of something that had never once been late. Lanterns were being lit along the lower walkways, one by one, so that the whole city looked like it was slowly deciding to glow.

Kess sat on the bench — also stone, everything here was stone, the city had clearly never met wood and didn't want to — and tried to work out how a simple job had gone this wrong.

Simple jobs were supposed to go wrong in familiar ways. You got caught by a magistrate, you talked your way out or you didn't, you did your time in a cell that smelled like other people's regrets. That was the trade. What you were not supposed to do was follow a stolen compass into a glowing cave, get swallowed by a whirlpool, and end up in a city that existed on no map he'd ever seen, being held for *assessment*.

Assessment. The word kept snagging on him like a burr in a sock.

He'd been in the cell perhaps two hours when the door opened without a sound — stone hinges, oiled, expensive — and a woman came in carrying a small table. Not a chair. A table. She set it down in the middle of the room as though this were the most natural thing in the world, and then a man came in carrying two stools, and then a third person came in carrying nothing at all except an expression of vast, patient authority, and Kess understood that he was about to be interrogated by furniture.

"You're the tall one," said the woman with the table.

"I'm the only one," Kess said. "There was a ranger chasing me, but I imagine she got her own table."

"Rosalind Okafor," the patient one said. It wasn't a question. "Desert ranger. Sworn."

"That's her. If she offered you her oath as payment, don't take it. It's worth more than she thinks."

The three of them arranged themselves around the table. The patient one sat facing him; the other two flanked, taking notes on slates with bits of soft white stone. Up close, Kess could see they all wore the same gray-green sash, and that the sashes were embroidered with a pattern of interlocking waves that seemed to move if you looked too long. He made himself stop looking. He'd learned young that some patterns are doors, and you don't knock on doors you can't close.

"I am Vesh," said the patient one. "Gatekeeper, second circle. You entered our river without payment. Do you understand what that means?"

"Honestly? No. I've been meaning to ask."

"It means," Vesh said, "that the river carried you here, and the river does not carry anything for free. Strangers pay in truth or in labor. Most pay in labor. Truth is faster." He folded his hands. "But truth is also weighed. A half-truth weighs more than a lie, in this city. A lie is a stone; we can set it aside. A half-truth is a stone with a hook in it."

"That's very poetic," Kess said. "Did you practice that?"

"Every morning," Vesh said, without a flicker of humor, and Kess decided he liked him slightly and trusted him not at all, which was roughly his default setting for authority figures.

* * *

They asked him his name first, and he gave it, because there was no point lying about a name they'd already pulled off him — they'd taken his pack at the gate, and his knife, and the little tin of lockpicks he'd had since he was nine, and he'd felt each loss like a tooth coming out. Then they asked where he was from, and he told them that too: a port town two hundred leagues west, salt-stained, honest about its dishonesty, the kind of place where everyone had three trades and none of them were discussed at dinner.

Then Vesh leaned forward, and the two note-takers stopped writing, and Kess knew they'd arrived at the actual question.

"The compass," Vesh said. "Tell us about the compass."

And here was the thing. Here was the whole rotten, beautiful thing.

Kess had been rehearsing this story for six years. Not for interrogators — for himself, mostly, in the dark, in the way a man rehearses an alibi until he half-believes it. The story went like this: I bought it fair. A dying sailor needed coin for medicine, I had coin, he had a strange brass compass that didn't point north. Fair trade. Sad story. Nothing to see.

It was even mostly true. That was the trouble with it. It was true right up until the part where it mattered.

"He was a sailor," Kess said. "Old. Sick — the wet kind of sick, the kind that gets into the lungs and stays. He'd run cargo up and down these coasts for forty years, he said. He had the compass wrapped in oilcloth inside a false bottom of his sea chest, and he told me—" Kess paused, because the pause was part of the performance, and performance was the one craft he'd never been ashamed of. "He told me it wanted to go home. And I laughed at him, because sailors say things like that about figureheads and lucky coins, and then I looked at it, and it wasn't pointing north. It was pointing east-southeast, steady as a sermon, out over open water. Toward here, though I didn't know that then. Nobody knows that. There's no *here* on any chart."

"And you bought it."

"For more than it was worth," Kess said, which was true, and let them draw their own conclusions about why a man buys a broken compass at a premium. Let them think sentiment. Sentiment was safe. Sentiment explained everything and implicated no one.

Vesh watched him for a long moment. The note-takers wrote nothing, which somehow felt worse than if they'd written everything.

"What was his name?" Vesh asked.

And there it was. The hook under the stone.

Because the dying sailor had a name, and Kess had known it within a day of meeting him, because the man had said it himself, feverish, on the third night, gripping Kess's wrist with a strength that shouldn't have been left in that body: *If anyone ever asks — if anyone ever comes asking — tell them Teodor Halloran sent it home.*

Teodor Halloran. Which was not the name on the papers of the dead ranger's brother. Which was not the name Rosalind Okafor had been shouting across three deserts and a coastline while she chased him. But Kess had done his research after — of course he had, he always did his research *after*, it was a character flaw — and he'd found the grave, and the sister, and the grief, and he had understood, sitting in a tavern with cheap wine going warm in front of him, that the dying man had given him a message wrapped in a name, and that delivering it would cost him everything he'd built his quiet life out of.

So he'd said nothing. For six years. And the compass had pointed east-southeast every single day, patient as tide, and he had told himself that a man could ignore a direction forever if he simply never turned to face it.

Until a desert ranger cornered him on a cliff with murder in her eyes, and the tide fell away from a cave mouth like the sea opening a door, and the compass in his pocket pulled toward it hard enough to bruise.

"A name I never caught," Kess said. "He was delirious half the time. Sailors don't give their names to buyers anyway. Bad luck."

The lie came out smooth. It always did. That was the problem with being good at this — the better you got, the fewer people there were who could catch you, and the lonelier the truth became.

Vesh studied him the way a jeweler studies a stone for the flaw that tells you it's been cut twice.

* * *

They kept him another hour. They asked about the cave — he told the truth, because the truth there was stranger than any lie he could build. They asked about the ranger — he told the truth again, and found, to his mild alarm, that talking about her steadied something in him. *She's not my enemy,* he heard himself say. *She thinks she is. She has excellent reasons. Her brother died holding that compass, or near enough, and I'm the man who walked off with it. If our positions were reversed I'd have chased me across three deserts too.* The note-takers wrote that down. He wondered which part of it they underlined.

When they finally rose to go, Vesh paused at the door.

"The assessment will take some days," he said. "You'll be fed. You'll be treated fairly — Rivergate is fair, stranger, whatever else it is. But I'll tell you something for free, since free things confuse your kind." He turned, and the lantern light caught the wave-pattern on his sash, moving, always moving. "The compass you carry was forged in this city before your grandfather's grandfather drew breath. It is not a tool. It is a *key*. And keys are not bought, and keys are not sold, and keys do not fall into the hands of dying sailors by accident." His eyes were the gray of river fog. "So when you decide which parts of your story to keep, ask yourself what the river already knows. It was there."

The door closed behind them without a sound.

* * *

Kess lay on the stone bench and stared at the ceiling, which was carved — he'd noticed this earlier and was now trying very hard to un-notice it — with the same interlocking waves, so that lying in this cell was like lying at the bottom of a very calm sea.

*A key.* Well. That tracked. Six years of the thing dragging him east-southeast through ports and deserts and one deeply regrettable season in a swamp, and *key* explained the pull better than *cursed* or *haunted* or any of the other words he'd tried on. Keys wanted locks. That was the whole nature of them.

And the lock, presumably, was upstream. Somewhere past those drowned temples he'd glimpsed from the ferry landing, dark shapes beneath the green water, old beyond old.

He thought about the dying man's grip on his wrist. *Tell them Teodor Halloran sent it home.* Not *sold it*. Not *lost it*. *Sent it home.* As if the man hadn't been a seller at all but a courier — as if the compass had been passing through hands the way a letter passes through hands, each carrier knowing only their leg of the route.

And the ranger — Rosalind — had a brother who died holding it.

Kess closed his eyes. He was not, by temperament, a man who connected dots voluntarily. Connecting dots led to conclusions, and conclusions led to obligations, and obligations were how a reasonably comfortable thief ended up in a stone cell in a city that wasn't supposed to exist, being assessed.

But the dots were connecting themselves now, whether he liked it or not. A dying courier with a false name. A dead brother with a real one. A key forged here, stolen — *stolen first*, Vesh had implied, and the word *first* had landed in Kess's chest like a dropped anchor — and a sister who had crossed a desert on the strength of nothing but grief and a debt.

She was in a cell somewhere in this city too. Probably mapping it from her window already. She struck him as the type. He'd known her for four days, three of which involved her trying to arrest him, and he already knew she was the type.

"They're going to weigh your truths," he said to the ceiling, quietly. "I hope you packed extras."

Above him, the carved waves went on turning, patient as tide, and somewhere downstream — or upstream, he genuinely could not tell anymore — the river moved through its gorge in the dark, carrying everything toward the sea, keeping everything it carried.

In the morning, he decided, he would ask for paper. If they were going to assess him, they could assess the whole ledger: the sailor, the purchase, the pull, the years. Everything except the name. The name he would keep. Not for his own sake — for hers. Because there were things a sister should hear from a friend, if she ever managed to become one, and things she should never hear from a Gatekeeper's slate.

He was still deciding which category the truth fell into when the lantern outside his window guttered, and he heard, faint and far below, the sound of someone singing on the water — a ferry song, low and looping, in a language he almost recognized.

The compass was locked in the Gatekeepers' strongroom. He couldn't feel it anymore. And yet, lying still in the dark, Kess could have sworn the pull was still there — east-southeast, steady as a sermon, drawing him onward into a city that already knew more about his story than he'd ever told anyone.

Including, he suspected, the part he'd just lied about.
