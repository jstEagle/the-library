## 6. The Ferryman's Daughter

They released Rosalind on the ninth morning with a wooden token, a bundle of dry clothes that smelled of river reed, and a sentence.

"Assessment complete," said the gatekeeper at the cell door — a round-faced woman whose name tag, if Rivergate had such things, would have read something like *I Have Judged You and Found You Mostly Harmless*. "You'll serve labor until your debt is weighed. Ferry line, third landing. Report to the pilot."

"And if I don't?"

The woman smiled the way a lock smiles at a key it has already decided not to accept. "Then you'll owe more. Everything here is owed, stranger. The trick is choosing what you owe it for."

Rosalind had spent nine days in a stone cell with a window the size of a loaf of bread, and she had used every hour of them. From her bunk she could see a slice of the gorge: the river running green-gold between cliffs stitched with stairways, bridges slung like necklaces, houses stacked on houses with their laundry flags snapping in the spray-wind. She had drawn the city in her head three times over — the landings, the weirs, the temple roofs drowned to their shoulders in the shallows upstream — because a ranger who could not map her prison was already lost.

Now they handed her the map's first street-level detail: a ferry token stamped with a coiled fish.

"Third landing," she repeated. "And my compass? My pack?"

"Held in assessment," the gatekeeper said, and shut the door on the question before it finished being asked.

* * *

The third landing was a platform of black timber cantilevered off the gorge wall, half of it always wet, all of it loud. Ferries came and went on ropes strung across the current — flat-bottomed boats with woven canopies, piloted by people who stood at the stern poles as casually as other folk stood in doorways. Cargo went downriver: fruit, salt bricks, coils of copper wire. People went upriver rarely, and when they did, the whole landing watched them go with the particular silence of a town watching someone open a door everyone else had agreed to leave shut.

"You're the new debt," said a voice behind her.

Rosalind turned. The speaker was perhaps nineteen, wiry, sun-browned, with hair cropped short for practicality and a grin that suggested practicality had lost several recent arguments. She wore a pilot's oilskin much too big for her, sleeves rolled six times, and she was eating a skewer of grilled fish with the unselfconscious commitment of someone who intended to finish it regardless of conversation.

"Ilo," she said around the fish. "Ferry pilot. You're assigned to me, which means you haul rope, bail water, and don't touch the pole. Ever. Touch the pole and I'll feed you to the weir, and then I'll have to fill out forms, and I hate forms."

"Rosalind Okafor." She offered her hand. Ilo looked at it, considered, shook it with the hand holding the skewer, and immediately regretted the grease transfer.

"Sorry. That's — yeah. Welcome to Rivergate." She gestured broadly with the skewer at the gorge, the river, the noise, the smell of woodsmoke and wet stone. "Best city in the world, worst employer. Questions?"

Rosalind had a great many questions, and she had learned in nine days of captivity which ones got answers. "How long is a work day?"

"Sunup to the second bell. Sometimes past, if the current's moody." Ilo hopped onto the gunwale of the nearest ferry and balanced there like it was a curb. "Current's been moody all week. Dry season's coming. You'll hear about that from everyone, constantly, whether you want to or not."

"The source seals," Rosalind said. "That's what they told me. Forty days."

"Thirty-one now." Ilo's grin dimmed by one degree — the first honest thing about her so far, and Rosalind noted it the way she'd note a change in wind. "Yeah. Everyone counts. Kids count it in chalk on the stairs. When the source seals, the river drops, the upper ferries stop running, and the city gets quiet and mean until spring. So." She clapped her hands, all brightness again. "Rope. Bail. Don't touch the pole. Let's see if you're worth the bread they're feeding you."

* * *

It turned out Rosalind was worth the bread.

She had hauled rope across desert wadis, winched supply sleds up canyon walls, and once dragged a dehydrated surveyor two miles through soft sand by his collar, so a ferry rope across a hundred yards of current was, frankly, a rest day. What she hadn't done was do it in rhythm with anyone, and that was the part that caught her off guard. Ilo called the pulls in a singsong cadence — *heave-two-three, hold-two-three* — and somewhere in the second crossing Rosalind stopped counting her own breaths and started counting the boat's, and the work became almost pleasant, the way a march becomes pleasant when the column finds its stride.

"You've done this," Ilo said at midday, tossing her a rice ball wrapped in banana leaf. They sat on the landing with their legs dangling over the water while the ferry took on cargo. Below them, fish the length of her forearm hung in the current, nosing at dropped grain.

"Sled teams. Desert work. I'm a ranger — was a ranger — back east. Salt flats and canyon country."

"A ranger." Ilo chewed thoughtfully. "Like a soldier?"

"Like a shepherd, but the flock is the land and half of it wants to kill you."

"So a soldier."

Rosalind laughed — actually laughed, she realized, for the first time since the salt flats, maybe since before. It startled her enough that she covered it with the rice ball.

Ilo watched her with open, unhurried curiosity, the way kids watch weather. "What'd you do to get assessed? Nobody gets labor-servitude for nothing. Usually it's smuggling. Sometimes it's worse. Once it was a man who insulted the Gatekeepers' singing, which, fair, their singing is terrible."

"I came in chasing a thief," Rosalind said. "He stole something of mine. We both got pulled through the cave mouth together, and now the Gatekeepers say we both owe passage."

"What did he steal?"

"A compass."

Ilo stopped chewing.

It was a small thing, a half-second hitch, but Rosalind had spent eleven years reading faces across campfires and interrogation tables, and a hitch was a hitch. "You know it?"

"I know the word." Ilo resumed chewing, slower. "Compasses are common enough. Trade goods from the coast. But nobody comes into Rivergate chasing one. And nobody gets pulled through the cave mouth by accident — the tide decides who comes in, and the tide doesn't waste itself on brass trinkets." She pointed the skewer at Rosalind. "Your thief. What's his name?"

"Kess."

Something moved behind Ilo's eyes — recognition, or its careful absence. "Huh," she said, which was the least informative syllable in any language, and stood up, brushing rice from her oilskin. "Break's over. Rope."

* * *

By the fourth day, Rosalind had learned three things about Ilo.

First: she was the ferryman's daughter in name only, her father having drowned at the second weir when she was eight, after which the ferry line had simply absorbed her the way a river absorbs rain — no ceremony, no paperwork, just inclusion. She spoke of him the way Rosalind spoke of her brother, which is to say she didn't, except in the specific gravity of certain silences.

Second: she knew everything. Not guessed, not suspected — *knew*. Which clan elder was feuding with which weir-master; how many casks of Highland ale had come downriver marked as vinegar; which Gatekeeper took bribes in dried eel (all of them, allegedly, but one in particular). The ferry line, Ilo explained, was the city's bloodstream, and gossip rode it the way silt rode the current — settling everywhere, informing everything.

"People tell pilots things," she said, poling the ferry through a slack stretch below the market terraces. "You don't look at them. You look at the water. It's like confession, except free, and I keep the good parts."

"And the bad parts?"

"The bad parts are usually the good parts."

Third: Ilo was afraid of exactly one subject, and Rosalind found it on the fifth day, when a Lowland Clan barge tied up at the third landing and its crew came aboard to inspect cargo.

They were broad men and women in oiled leather, wearing river-pearl armbands, and they moved through the ferry with the proprietary boredom of people who had never once been told no. Their leader, a heavyset woman with a scarred jaw, lifted a tarp, looked under it, dropped it, and pronounced the boat clean. Then her eyes found Rosalind — found the stranger, the outsider, the wrong-colored clothes and wrong-cut hair — and stayed there a moment too long.

"New debt," the woman said. Not a question.

"Assigned," Ilo said lightly. "Good hands. Best puller I've had since Da."

"Mm." The woman's gaze crawled over Rosalind like a hand checking pockets. "Keep it away from the upper river. Outsiders go upriver, things go missing. Always have." She said it loudly enough for the landing to hear, and the landing heard it the way a congregation hears a familiar verse — nodding along, no one surprised.

They disembarked. The barge pushed off. The normal noise of the landing resumed, and Rosalind noticed that it resumed *around* the space where the words had landed, the way water resumes around a stone.

"Charming," Rosalind said.

"They're not wrong, exactly." Ilo was coiling rope with unusual attention, not looking at her. "Lowland Clan keeps the old grievances. Generations back — before my grandmother's grandmother — something was stolen from the river. From the source, some say. A relic. The story goes an outsider took it, and the Lowlands swore an oath on it: no stranger passes upstream while the theft stands unpaid." She snapped a coil tight. "It's why the cave mouth only opens on the lowest tides. Why strangers get assessed instead of welcomed. Why the Gatekeepers ask what you'll pay. Everyone's still collecting on a debt nobody alive remembers taking out."

"What was stolen?"

Ilo's hands went still on the rope.

For a long moment the only sound was the river and the creak of the mooring lines, and Rosalind watched the girl decide — visibly, weight by weight — how much truth this stranger had earned. Five days of hauling rope. Five days of rhythm. One laugh, honestly given.

"A needle," Ilo said finally, quietly. "That's what the old songs call it. The Lodestone Needle. Forged in the source itself, back when the Gatekeepers were priests instead of clerks. It didn't point north. It pointed *home* — to the source, always, from anywhere in the world. The river's own heart, cut loose." She looked up, and her grin was gone, and without it she looked much younger and much older at once. "My da used to sing about it. Said whoever holds the Needle can find the source blindfolded, in the dark, from the far side of the world. And that when it comes home, the river wakes up."

The words went through Rosalind like cold water through cloth.

*A compass that points home.* She thought of Kess on the salt flats, turning the stolen thing over in his hands with something that had never looked like greed. She thought of him at the coast, saying *it wants to go home*, pointing at the sea — and the freak low tide opening the cave mouth like a held breath finally let out. She had filed all of it under *thief's tricks*. Thieves lied. It was the defining feature of the profession.

But thieves didn't buy compasses that pointed to hidden rivers. Thieves didn't walk willingly into whirlpools with their pursuers on their heels.

"How old is the story?" she asked. "The theft. Exactly."

"Older than the clans' feud. Older than the Gatekeepers' office. The songs say—" Ilo paused, frowning, doing arithmetic she clearly didn't enjoy. "The songs say the exile who took it left during the last great sealing. That's... four generations. Maybe five."

Five generations. Sixty, seventy years.

Kess had told the assessors — and told Rosalind, in the one fragment of honesty he'd allowed himself — that he'd bought the compass from a dying man *years ago*. Years. Not decades. Not generations. Whatever Kess was carrying, whatever had sung to him across the salt flats and opened the tide for them both, it could not be the relic the Lowland Clan mourned.

Unless it was.

Unless it had come back before — carried by another dying man, another exile, another pair of hands passing the debt along like a hot coal — and gone out again, and come back again, circling home and fleeing home for five generations while the clans sharpened their grievance and the river waited.

Rosalind stood very still on the wet black timbers of the third landing, and felt the shape of her chase change underneath her. She had crossed a desert to arrest a man. She might have crossed it to deliver a message she didn't know how to read.

"You've gone strange," Ilo observed.

"I'm thinking."

"Don't hurt yourself." But Ilo said it gently, and then, quieter, in the voice she used for the bad-good parts: "Whatever your thief took — if it's what I think it is — the Lowlands will kill for it. Not figure of speech. There's a standing bounty older than the ferry line. And the Gatekeepers let them keep it, because a grievance is cheaper than a war." She tossed Rosalind the rope end. "So. Heave-two-three, hold-two-three. And Rosalind?"

Rosalind looked up.

"If you're planning to go upriver — and you've got the face of somebody planning to go upriver — don't do it without me. I know the water. And nobody gossips about a ferry pilot's passengers." The grin came back, crooked and bright. "Besides. You're the best puller I've had since Da. I'm not losing you to a weir."

Across the gorge, upriver, past the drowned temples where the water ran fast and green, the afternoon light caught the high cliffs near the sealed source, and for half a heartbeat Rosalind could have sworn the rock face glimmered — faintly, briefly — blue.

Thirty days, said the chalk tallies on the stairs.

She took the rope, and pulled.
