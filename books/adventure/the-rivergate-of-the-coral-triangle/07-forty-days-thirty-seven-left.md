## 7. Forty Days, Thirty-Seven Left

The cell had one window, and the window had a view of everything.

That was the joke of it, Rosalind decided on her ninth morning in Rivergate — that they'd lock her up somewhere with a better vantage point than most rangers got in a lifetime. The gorge wall fell away beneath her in terraces of pale stone and hanging gardens, and beyond them the river ran silver-green through the city like a vein through a leaf. She could see the ferry docks. The market canopies, striped red and yellow. The drowned temple's crown poking out of the water upstream, black and patient as a sleeping animal.

She could also see, if she pressed her cheek to the stone and looked straight down, the assessment yard where strangers were put to work hauling silt baskets. Kess was down there this morning. She could tell by the way he carried the baskets — wrong, deliberately wrong, like a man performing labor rather than doing it. He'd been a thief too long to be good at anything honest.

"Thirty-seven days," she said to the window, because saying it out loud made it real. "Give or take."

The dry season would come, the Gatekeepers had told her, and when it came the river would shrink and the source far upstream would seal itself behind stone until spring. The only way out of Rivergate was through the source. Which meant the only way home was upstream, past the drowned temples, past whatever waited there, in less time than it took a desert well to go bad.

Rosalind Okafor did not enjoy waiting. Waiting was for people who believed things worked themselves out. In the salt flats, waiting got you dead; you moved at dawn or you baked where you stood. Nine days of sitting in a comfortable cell with excellent food and a spectacular view had nearly driven her out of her skin.

So on the morning of day nine, when the door opened and Gatekeeper Vesh stepped through it, she was already standing, already dressed, already wearing the expression she used on smugglers who thought she hadn't noticed the extra crates.

Vesh was tall in the way of someone who had been tall since childhood and had never once been surprised by it. His gatekeeper's robes were grey-green, river-colored, and his face was smooth and pleasant and gave away absolutely nothing, which Rosalind respected professionally and distrusted personally.

"The stranger Okafor," he said. "You've been counting days."

"You can hear me through the walls?"

"I can hear you through the *window*. You talk to it." He glanced at the view with mild interest. "It doesn't answer. I've tried."

"It's early," Rosalind said. "I'm not at my best before the sun clears the rim."

"No," Vesh agreed. "You're at your best right now, actually. I've watched nine days of you, Okafor. You pace less each morning. You eat more slowly. You've stopped testing the bars." He folded his hands. "You've stopped planning an escape and started planning something else. I'd very much like to know what."

*Careful*, said the part of her brain that had kept her alive through eleven years of desert work. But another part — the part that had spent nine days staring at that river and mapping every dock, every weir, every stretch of whitewater between here and the source — said: *he came to you. Use him.*

"A trade," she said.

Vesh's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Strangers don't usually propose trades. They usually propose begging."

"I'm not built for begging. Bad knees."

"Your knees look fine."

"They're liars. Everything about me is a liar except my offer." She turned to face him fully. "Guide me toward the source. Get me passage upstream before the dry season seals it. And when I'm out — when I'm standing on the other side of that cave mouth with my compass back and my thief in custody — I'll come back. I'll serve Rivergate a full year. Labor, whatever you want. My oath on it."

Silence. Somewhere below, a ferry horn sounded, low and mournful, and a flock of white birds lifted off the water all at once, like a page turning.

Vesh studied her for a long moment. Then, to her considerable surprise, he smiled.

"Yes," he said.

Rosalind blinked. "Yes?"

"Yes. A year of service from a ranger of your skills is a generous price. I accept."

"That's it? No haggling? No counteroffer?" She'd prepared for haggling. She'd prepared three fallback positions and a dramatic speech about honor. The dramatic speech was going to waste inside her like an unopened letter.

"You expected me to grind you down."

"Everyone grinds. It's how I know a deal is real."

"Then let me reassure you." Vesh moved to the window, hands still folded, looking out over his city with the ease of a man looking at property. "Rivergate has wanted someone with your particular talents for some time. Someone who reads water and stone. Someone who isn't afraid of what lives upstream." He turned his head just enough to catch her eye. "A year of your service is not a price, Okafor. It's a bargain I'd have paid double for."

And there it was — the small cold drop of unease, sliding down her spine like water finding a crack in stone.

Because that wasn't how bargains worked. That wasn't how *anything* worked. In the salt flats, when a buyer agreed too fast, it meant one of two things: either you'd asked for too little, or they knew something about the goods that you didn't.

"And what exactly," she said slowly, "does Rivergate need from me upstream?"

"Passage. Protection. An escort through waters we no longer travel." Vesh's smile didn't move but something behind it did, some calculation completing itself. "The clans have quarreled. The old routes are contested. A neutral party — an outsider with nothing to gain and a debt to pay — is precisely what the situation calls for."

"You're sending me into a clan dispute."

"I'm sending you toward the source. What you meet on the way is the river's business, not mine." He inclined his head toward the door. "You leave tomorrow, at first bell. There's a pilot who's volunteered — a ferry girl, Ilo. You may know her. She's been leaving bread on your windowsill against regulations."

Rosalind had wondered who was responsible for the bread. It had appeared every second morning, wrapped in oilcloth, always slightly stale, always accompanied by something absurd — a carved fish, once, and once a note reading *the guards' soup is worse, count yourself lucky.*

"She's a smuggler," Rosalind said.

"She's a *ferry pilot*," Vesh corrected mildly, "with smuggling as a hobby. You'll get along."

He moved to the door, and Rosalind felt the window of opportunity — the actual, literal window behind her — pressing at her back. Thirty-seven days. One guide. One chance.

"One more thing," she said. "Kess comes with me."

Vesh paused with his hand on the door. "The thief."

"He knows the compass. He's held it for years — he knows its weight, its moods, whatever a stolen thing has instead of a history. If it matters at the source, I'll want him there." She crossed her arms. "In chains, if you like. Chained is fine."

"Oh, chains will be provided," Vesh said, and there was something in his voice then, brief as a fish flashing under the surface, that made her stomach tighten. "Consider it done. Rest tonight, Okafor. Tomorrow the river gets interesting."

The door closed behind him.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she did best, which was taking apart a conversation the way she'd take apart a suspicious lock — gently, patiently, piece by piece, until it showed her what it was hiding.

He'd agreed too easily. That was the splinter she couldn't dig out.

She paced the cell — four steps, turn, four steps — cataloguing it the way she'd catalogue tracks around a poisoned well. First: he'd known about her plan before she offered it. *You've stopped planning an escape and started planning something else.* Second: he'd wanted her upstream specifically, and had wanted an outsider specifically, which meant the clans wouldn't take Rivergate's own people where he needed them to go. Third —

Third, and worst: he'd accepted a year of skilled service without blinking, which meant the year was worth less to him than whatever he expected her to do in thirty-seven days. Nobody traded a fortune for a favor unless the favor was bigger than the fortune.

"I'm not a guide," she told the window. "I'm a tool. He's sending me upstream to do something he can't ask anyone else to do."

The window, as usual, declined to comment. But the light coming through it had gone gold and long, and down on the river the evening ferries were lighting their lamps, one by one, so that the whole waterway looked like a string of embers being pulled downstream.

There was a soft scrape at the sill.

Rosalind crossed the cell in three strides. On the ledge sat a bundle of oilcloth, and inside it: bread, a strip of dried fish, and a note in a quick, slanting hand.

*Heard you bought your way out. Congratulations and condolences. Ask yourself why a man who locks strangers in cells agrees to anything in under a minute. — I*

Rosalind read it twice. Then she looked down at the assessment yard, where the last workers were stacking their silt baskets, and found Kess among them, looking up. Even at this distance she could see the question on his face — the same question she could feel forming in her own chest, cold and patient as deep water.

*What did you just agree to?*

Below, a guard barked at Kess to keep moving. He shouldered his basket and went, glancing back once.

Rosalind tucked the note into her boot, ate the bread, and lay down on the cot to watch the last light leave the gorge.

Tomorrow she'd be on the river. Tomorrow, upstream, past the drowned temples, toward the source and whatever Vesh actually wanted there. She'd made her bargain and she'd keep it — a deal was a deal, and her word was the one thing the desert had never managed to take from her.

But she fell asleep composing, carefully, the list of questions she intended to ask Ilo before first bell. Starting with the simplest one, the one that had been ticking under everything since Vesh smiled:

*What's really waiting upstream?*

And why did a man who'd won his bargain look, for half a heartbeat, like a man who'd just set something in motion that even he couldn't steer?
