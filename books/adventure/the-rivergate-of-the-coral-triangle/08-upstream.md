## 8. Upstream

The boat Ilo piloted was called the *Marrow*, and she loved it the way other people loved their children.

"She's ugly," Ilo admitted, patting the gunwale with genuine affection. "She leaks in three places I know of and probably four I don't. But she's honest. A beautiful boat lies to you about the water. The Marrow tells you everything."

Rosalind looked at the flat-bottomed ferry with its patched sail and its cargo of caged river-hens and thought: *of course it does.* Everything in Rivergate told you everything, usually at a price.

Kess sat amidships with his wrists chained to a ring bolted into the keel — Vesh's arrangement, not hers, though she hadn't argued hard against it. He'd made one joke about it since dawn, something about finally traveling first class, and then gone quiet in a way that Rosalind was beginning to recognize as his version of thinking. It looked a great deal like sulking, but there was machinery underneath.

"Temple water today," Ilo said, hauling on the tiller as the current took them. "You'll want to keep your hands inside the boat and your opinions to yourself. The drowned temples don't care what you think of them, but they'll drown you anyway if you're rude."

"How far?" Rosalind asked.

"To the Weir? Two days if the river's kind." Ilo grinned, white teeth in her brown face, a scar splitting one eyebrow that she'd refused to explain for three days running. "The river is never kind. But it's fair, sometimes, which is better."

They slipped out of the city gorge in the grey half-light before proper morning, past the terraced gardens where laborers were already wading thigh-deep among the rice paddies, past the fisher-children who waved at Ilo with both arms because one arm wasn't enough for how they felt about her. Rosalind watched the city shrink behind them — the hanging bridges, the lantern-poles, the great stone faces of the Gatekeepers carved into the cliff walls with their eyes closed, as if the whole civilization were asleep and only pretending otherwise.

She had been in Rivergate thirty-one days. She had twenty-six left before the dry season sealed the source, and she'd spent most of those thirty-one days learning the city from her cell window and then, after Vesh's bargain, from the deck of this leaking boat. She could draw the gorge from memory now, every stair and sluice-gate. What she couldn't draw was the feeling she got at night, lying in the ferry-house loft, that the river was counting her. That it knew exactly how many days she had left, and was keeping score somewhere in its green depths.

"You're doing the face," Ilo said.

"What face?"

"The desert face. Like the water owes you money."

Rosalind almost smiled. "The water might. Everything else here does."

Ilo laughed and steered them around a bend, and the world opened up.

* * *

The drowned temples were nothing like Rosalind had imagined, mostly because she hadn't been able to imagine them at all. She had pictured ruins — broken columns, sad stones. What she saw instead was architecture mid-drowning, or perhaps mid-rising; it was impossible to tell which. Great stepped pyramids rose out of the river at angles, their upper tiers crowned with trees, their lower tiers furred with weed and lit from within by some phosphorescent life in the flooded halls. Between them, the river braided itself into channels, some wide and slow, some narrow enough that the water hissed through like a held breath being released.

"People lived here," Kess said quietly. It was the first thing he'd said in an hour.

"People live everywhere until they don't," Ilo said. "Highland Clan built these, back when the river ran higher. Then the river changed its mind. Rivers do that. Best not to take it personally." She pointed with her chin at a pyramid whose peak broke the surface like a whale's back. "That one's the Temple of the Second Mouth. Pilgrims used to row inside during flood season and come out the other side. Some of them came out the other side of living, too, so we stopped advertising it."

Rosalind found herself leaning forward despite herself. "What's inside?"

"Carvings." Ilo's voice lost some of its bounce. "Old ones. About the source, and the compass, and who holds what. You'll see more of them upstream, if we get that far." She glanced at Kess, chained to the keel, and her grin came back but thinner. "Which depends on him behaving, and on the Marrow holding together, and on the river deciding today is a fair day."

"And which do you put odds on?"

"Him behaving? Terrible odds. The Marrow? She's never let me down." Ilo said it lightly, but her hand rested on the tiller the way Rosalind's hand had once rested on her brother's shoulder — the way you touch something you've already decided to protect with your life.

The thought of Tam rose unbidden, the way it did every day, usually at the worst moment. Tam, who had taught her to read stars and lie to customs officers and whistle through a blade of grass. Tam, dead in a ditch outside Kaladesh with a compass in his fist and no explanation anyone would give her. She had chased Kess across four hundred miles of salt and scrub because grief needs a direction or it eats you alive. Somewhere in the last month, without noticing the exact moment, the chase had become something else. She still didn't have a word for what.

"Your face again," Ilo said gently.

"I'm fine."

"Liar. Good liar, though. Desert people always are. Too much sun, not enough witnesses." Ilo steered them into the widest channel, and the temples slid by on either side, dripping and glowing, ancient and patient. "My mother ferried pilgrims through here. Her mother before her. The Marrow's hull has more of my family in it than the family graveyard does. So when I say she tells you everything the water thinks—" she patted the gunwale again, "—believe me. She's related to it."

* * *

They made camp that night on a shelf of stone beneath the Third Temple, a broad terrace halfway up its flank where the flood line had left a beach of pale silt. Ilo built a fire from driftwood that burned blue-green at the edges, and they ate smoked eel and river-plums, and for an hour nobody talked, which was its own kind of conversation.

It was Kess who broke it.

"Ilo. The carvings in the Second Mouth. Have you ever seen one of a robed figure holding a compass?"

Rosalind went very still.

Ilo fed a stick to the fire. "Everyone's seen those. There are dozens. The Pilgrim with the Star, we call it. Robed figure, round instrument, chain on the wrist." She shrugged. "Highland Clan says it's their ancestor carrying the relic home. Lowland Clan says it's a thief caught in the act. Same carving. Funny thing about stone — it doesn't argue. People do the arguing for it."

"Why a chain on the wrist?" Rosalind asked.

"Depends who you ask," Ilo said, and grinned, and refused to elaborate, which Rosalind was learning was a Rivergate art form.

Kess lay back against the keel of the overturned boat they were using as shelter, chains pooling around him, and looked at the stars. "In the desert," he said, "the stars are just decoration. Pretty, useless. Here they feel like they're watching."

"They are," said Ilo comfortably. "Everything here watches. That's why I talk to my boat. Boats don't repeat what they hear." She stood, stretched, and pointed at Rosalind. "First watch is yours, ranger. Wake me when the moon clears the temple. And wake him—" a nod at Kess, "—never. Chained men sleep badly enough without help."

She rolled into her blanket and was snoring within minutes, with the shameless efficiency of someone who had spent her whole life sleeping outdoors.

Rosalind sat with her knees drawn up, the oath-knife across her lap, and watched the water glow below the terrace. Deep down — deeper than any lamp could reach — lights moved. Fish, or something else. Once, she could have sworn the lights arranged themselves into a pattern: concentric rings, like ripples frozen in place, like the face of a compass.

She shook herself. Thirty-one days underground will do that to a person.

Across the fire, Kess was watching her.

"Don't," she said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You were about to say something noble and self-flagellating. Save it. I'm not in the market."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "It's strange, isn't it. All I wanted, all these years, was to bring it home. And now that we're actually going — every mile upstream feels less like arriving and more like being swallowed."

"That's because you are being swallowed," Rosalind said. "We both are. Slowly. By a river." She turned the oath-knife over in her fingers. "For what it's worth, I understand the feeling. I've been chasing something for a year that keeps getting bigger the closer I get. Started as a thief. Became a mystery. Now it's—" she gestured vaguely at the glowing water, the drowned temple looming above them, the whole impossible country, "—all this."

"And when you catch it? The truth, I mean. What then?"

Rosalind looked at him for a long moment. In the blue-green firelight he looked younger than he had on the salt flats, and tireder, and more afraid, though he hid it the way she hid things — badly, if you knew where to look.

"Then I pay for it," she said. "Rivergate taught me that much. Nothing true comes free."

* * *

The second day, the river stopped being fair.

It started well enough. Ilo took them through the temple channels with the easy confidence of long habit, reading the water the way Rosalind read dunes — small signs, invisible to strangers, meaning everything. Where the surface wrinkled in a certain pattern, there was a submerged wall. Where the glow beneath the hull dimmed, the channel deepened. She narrated half of it, partly to teach, mostly because silence made her itch.

But by midday the channels narrowed, the banks steepened, and the sound of the river changed. Rosalind heard it before she understood it: a low, continuous roar ahead, like the sea, except they were two hundred miles from any coast.

"Rapids," Ilo said. All the humor had gone out of her voice. "The Squeeze. Every pilot runs it twice a year, no more. We can portage, but it'll cost us a full day, maybe two, and we don't have two." She chewed her lip. "Or we run it. The Marrow's done it before. Once. With my mother at the tiller, and my mother was better than me."

"Then we portage," Rosalind said.

"With him chained?" Ilo jerked a thumb at Kess. "Portaging means hauling the boat over rocks for a mile. He'd slow us to a crawl. Or we cut his chains, and then Vesh has our hides, and honestly I'd rather face the rapids than Vesh."

"I could swim the portage alongside," Kess offered.

"You'd drown," Rosalind and Ilo said together.

He spread his chained hands. "I'm just saying, options exist."

Ilo studied the water for a long time. Rosalind watched her read it — the standing waves, the exposed fangs of rock, the foam-lines that marked where the current folded over on itself. She watched her make a decision the way you watch someone step off a ledge.

"We run it," Ilo said. "Left channel, past the Split Rock, hold hard right at the Weir's throat. If I shout 'down,' everyone gets flat and prays to whatever they've got. Ranger, you bail. Thief, you sit on your hands and stay a passenger. Nobody touches the tiller but me." She looked at each of them in turn, and her grin came back — fierce now, defiant, a flag planted. "The Marrow's never let me down. Let's not give her a reason to start."

They ran the Squeeze at midafternoon, and for the first ninety seconds it was magnificent.

The river dropped away beneath them in a series of ledges, and the Marrow rode them like a living thing, plunging and rising, spray sheeting over the bow while Ilo laughed out loud — actual laughter, wild and delighted — and the drowned temples fell behind and the gorge walls closed in, red stone streaked with white bird-guano, and Rosalind bailed with a wooden scoop and felt, absurdly, briefly, happy. Grief had a direction, and for once the direction was forward.

Then the river changed its mind.

It happened between one ledge and the next. A wave rose where no wave should have been — Ilo swore later it was a backwash off a submerged slab, a wave that belonged to no map and no memory — and it took the Marrow amidships and swung her sideways, and sideways was death in the Squeeze, because sideways meant the current could get its whole hand around you.

"DOWN!" Ilo screamed.

Rosalind went flat. The world became noise and cold and the taste of the river. Through the chaos she heard Ilo fighting the tiller, heard her saying — not screaming, saying, conversational, terrible — "no no no, not now, sweetheart, not now—" and then wood cracked somewhere below the waterline, a sound like a bone breaking, and the Marrow began to die.

They hit the Split Rock. Rosalind never saw it; she felt it, a blow through the hull that threw her against the keel beside Kess, and then the boat was folding around them and the river was coming in and Ilo was shouting the last order of her life:

"THE RING! GET HIM OFF THE RING!"

Rosalind understood one heartbeat too late. The chain. Kess was chained to the keel, and the keel was going down, and the ring bolt would take him with it.

She got the oath-knife out. The chain links were thick — forged gatekeeper iron, meant to hold — but the padlock staple was thinner, and her hands were shaking, and the boat was under them now, and the water was at her chest, her shoulders, her ears.

The staple snapped. Kess came free. The river took all three of them like a hand closing.

* * *

Afterward, Rosalind could never assemble those minutes into order. There were fragments only: tumbling in green roaring dark; surfacing gasping between stone walls; catching a rope — the Marrow's painter, torn free, trailing like a severed tendon — and hauling herself and Kess toward an eddy behind a boulder. She remembered coughing the river out of her lungs. She remembered shouting Ilo's name until her voice tore.

She remembered seeing her, once.

Ilo surfaced forty yards downstream, in the white heart of the rapids, and for one instant her head was up and her eyes found Rosalind's across all that chaos, and she didn't look afraid. She looked furious — furious at the river, at the wave, at the unfairness of it — and then the current folded over her and took her down, and the glow-lights deep beneath the water swirled together where she'd been, concentric rings, like ripples frozen in place.

Like a compass face.

They found the wreckage at dusk, wedged in an eddy a mile downstream: a shattered hull, a caged hen paddling in circles, one oar, and Ilo's scarf, snagged on a root, streaming in the current like a flag of surrender.

Rosalind sat on the bank with the wet scarf in her hands and could not make herself move. Kess stood behind her, chains dangling from one wrist where the broken staple still hung, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say. The river went on roaring past them, indifferent, enormous, keeping its score.

Three days ago, Ilo had laughed at her own ugly boat and smuggled gossip and warned her about clans and shared her plums. Three days ago she had been alive, and her only crime was ferrying a ranger and a thief upstream on a quest that was never hers.

Rosalind's quest. Her debt. Her cost, paid by someone innocent.

The grief that came was worse than the grief for Tam, because Tam had chosen his road, whatever secrets he'd kept, and Ilo had simply been kind.

Kess crouched beside her, slowly, the way you approach something wounded. "Rosalind."

"Don't." Her voice came out gravel. "Whatever it is. Don't."

He didn't say anything. He just stayed there, close enough that she could have hit him, and after a while she found she didn't want to.

It was full dark when she finally stood, wound the scarf around her wrist, and looked upstream, where the roar of the Squeeze faded into the deeper silence of the drowned temples and, beyond them, the source.

Twenty-five days left. One boat destroyed. One friend in the river.

And on the far bank, half-hidden in the reeds, torchlight — many torches, moving downstream along the cliffs, spreading out in a search pattern that had nothing to do with looking for survivors.

Kess saw them too. "Those aren't searchers," he breathed.

"No," Rosalind said, pulling him down into the reeds. "Searchers look for bodies. Those are looking for us."

The torches crossed the water toward their bank.
