## 9. The Drowned Temple

**Rosalind**

The river did not give Ilo back.

Rosalind stood knee-deep in the shallows where the boat had come apart, calling his name until her voice went raw, until the only answer was the water slapping against the drowned stones and Kess, chained at the ankle to a piece of wreckage, saying quietly, "He's gone. Rosalind. He's gone."

She knew it. She had watched the river take him — one moment his hand on the tiller, the next a swirl of brown water and a single oar spinning away downstream like a thing that had decided to leave without them. But knowing a thing and accepting it were two different countries, and she could not cross from one to the other just yet.

"He's a ferry pilot," she said. "Ferry pilots don't drown."

"Everyone drowns," Kess said. "Some of us are just late about it."

She rounded on him, ready to spend grief as anger, the way she always had — but he was sitting in the shallows with his wrists still bound, soaked to the bone, shivering so hard his teeth clicked between words, and there was nothing in his face to fight. Only the same hollow look she felt behind her own ribs.

So instead of hitting him, which she wanted to do, she waded over and cut his wrist bindings with her oath-knife. The ankle chain she left. Not out of cruelty. Out of arithmetic: one of them needed to sleep, and she wasn't going to be the one who couldn't.

They were stranded on a shelf of black stone at the base of cliffs no climbing gear in the world would forgive, three days' walk upstream from the source by Ilo's reckoning, and downstream was rapids that had just eaten their boat and their friend. The sun was dropping behind the gorge walls, and the temperature with it, and the river was rising — she could see it eating the lower steps of the stone shelf, inch by patient inch.

"We need shelter," she said. "High ground."

"There." Kess nodded across a narrow spit of rock to where the cliff face opened into a dark archway, half-drowned, its lintel carved with shapes the water had worn smooth. "Temple. Or tomb. At this point I'm not picky."

"You're chained to a wreck."

"I noticed." He looked at the chain, then at her, then at the knife in her hand, and had the decency not to ask twice.

She cut him loose. It cost her something to do it — not the metal, but the principle — and she made sure he saw what it cost. He took the chain and coiled it over his shoulder anyway, which she found she respected. A man who kept his own leash within reach understood something about himself.

The archway's floor sloped down into black water. Inside, the air was cool and smelled of wet stone and something older — incense, maybe, or the memory of it, soaked so deep into the rock that a hundred floods hadn't rinsed it out. Their footfalls echoed wrong, doubled, as if someone else were walking a few paces behind them, matching their steps exactly.

"Don't do that," Rosalind said.

"Do what?"

"The echo thing."

"That's not me doing anything. That's the temple doing it." He lifted a hand, and the echo lifted a hand too, a half-beat late, from somewhere deeper in the dark. "See? It's friendly."

"It's creepy is what it is."

"It can be both. Most holy places are."

* * *

**Kess**

Kess had been in temples before. He'd slept in a dozen of them across four territories, because temples were dry, defensible, and — if you were respectful about it — unlikely to be disturbed by anyone who mattered. He knew the grammar of them: the raised thresholds, the lamp niches, the way the floors tilted subtly toward an altar or a pool.

This one spoke a dialect he didn't know.

The water inside was clear — impossibly clear for a river that outside ran the color of steeped tea — and when they got far enough in and the last grey light through the doorway caught it, he stopped walking so fast he nearly went into it.

The floor was carved. All of it. Every submerged stone, as far as the light reached, was covered in figures: processions of people, schools of fish, coils of current rendered in lines so fine they seemed to move when you weren't looking directly at them. And running through all of it, repeated, larger than everything else, was a shape he knew better than his own hands.

A circle. A needle. A housing worked with waves.

"The compass," Rosalind breathed beside him.

"Not a copy of it," Kess said. His voice came out strange. "It's *the* compass. Look at the housing. The wave-work. That's not decoration, that's the same pattern, line for line."

She crouched at the water's edge, and the light moved across her face as the carvings caught it, and he watched her go very still in the way he'd learned meant she was either about to understand something or refuse to.

"Follow it," she said. "The figure holding it. Follow the procession."

They did — along the flooded floor, wading now, the water at their thighs, cold enough to make speech difficult. The robed figure appeared again and again, always central, always holding the compass aloft or presenting it or — in one panel that made the back of Kess's neck prickle — pressing it into a socket in the earth while the river around the figure's feet changed from chaos to calm.

And every time, the figure was built the same. Tall. Broad-shouldered. A particular set to the jaw beneath the hood that Kess would have known anywhere, because he'd spent three years watching that jaw argue, laugh, and finally go quiet forever.

He glanced sideways at Rosalind. She was staring at the largest panel, water to her waist, one hand hovering above the carving without touching it.

"He's left-handed," she said. "Look. The compass is always in the left hand. Everyone else carries things in their right."

"Yes," Kess said.

"And the build—"

"Yes."

She turned and looked at him, and the water dripped off her braids, and her eyes in the dim blue light were the flat silver of a blade being drawn.

"How old are these carvings, Kess?"

"Old," he said. "Centuries, maybe. The stone wear says—"

"*How old.*"

He should have lied. He was good at lying; it was practically a trade skill. But standing chest-deep in a drowned temple with her brother's face carved into the walls a hundred times over, wearing robes older than any nation he could name, some part of him simply refused to add one more falsehood to a room already this full of impossible truth.

"I don't know how," he said. "I swear to you, Rosalind, I don't know how. When I bought it from the dying man, he told me it was old. Older than Rivergate, he said. He never mentioned—" he gestured helplessly at the walls, at the tall left-handed figure, "—*this*."

"But you recognized the build. Just now. You looked at me before I looked at you."

Because of course he had. Because he'd spent three years carrying a dead man's compass and listening to a dead man's voice in his head, and the first time he'd seen Rosalind Okafor across a salt flat with a ranger's rifle and murder in her posture, he'd thought: *there you are. You look just like him.*

He didn't say that. Some truths were load-bearing, and you didn't pull them out to show people in the dark.

"I've seen the compass a long time," he said. "You get to know what goes with it."

* * *

**Rosalind**

They found a dry chamber above the flood line — a small one, low-ceilinged, with bench-niches along the walls that might have been beds for sleeping priests once. Rosalind got a fire going from driftwood and the sad remains of their supplies, and they ate hardtack softened in river water, which tasted like punishment but counted as food.

Her brother's face was on the walls.

She kept circling back to it, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. In the desert, grief had a shape she understood: Danny had died on the flats, alone, holding a stolen compass, and the story of his death had been simple. He'd fallen in with bad company. He'd taken something that wasn't his. She had spent two years carrying that story like a stone in her pack, heavy but familiar, and she had hated Kess for making her carry it.

Now the walls of a temple older than memory said the story was wrong. Said Danny — or someone shaped exactly like Danny, left-handed exactly like Danny — had held the compass the way priests hold holy things. Had used it to calm a river. Had been, panel after panel, the center of everything.

"People don't get carved into thousand-year-old temples," she said aloud, to the fire.

"No," Kess agreed. He was sitting across from her, rubbing warmth back into his chained ankle, and for once he didn't dress it up. "They don't."

"So either these carvings aren't a thousand years old—"

"They are. I know stone. These are old."

"—or my brother has been dead for a thousand years and also died eight months ago on the Saltreach flats, which presents certain theological difficulties."

Kess snorted. Despite everything — despite Ilo, despite the cold, despite the fact that she still didn't trust him further than the length of his own chain — the sound startled a laugh out of her, brief and cracked, and it hurt her ribs in a way that felt almost good.

"Certain theological difficulties," he repeated solemnly. "That's going in my memoirs."

"You don't have memoirs."

"I'll write them. Chapter one: everyone drowns eventually, some of us are just late about it."

"Stop quoting yourself."

"It was a good line."

It was a good line. That was the problem with Kess. Underneath the evasions and the lies she could smell on him like smoke on a coat, there was a person she might have liked, in another life, in a world where he hadn't run off with the last thing Danny ever held.

She fed the fire and asked, carefully casual, "The dying man. The one you bought it from. What did he look like?"

Silence. Long enough that she looked up.

Kess was staring into the flames, and his face had done something complicated — guilt and fear wrestling for the wheel.

"Why?" he said.

"Because I've spent two years chasing a thief, and today I found out the thief might be a priest, and my brother might be both, and I am tired, Kess. I'm tired and cold and my friend is probably dead, and I want one straight answer tonight. Just one. Describe him."

Kess looked at her for a long moment. The firelight moved on his face. And she watched him decide — she actually watched it happen, the moment the lie lost and something else won, or mostly won.

"Tall," he said. "Broad shoulders. Left-handed — he signed his trade-mark with his left. Brown skin, desert-born, like yours. He called himself Maro." A pause. "Said he was from somewhere he could never go back to."

Maro.

Danny's childhood name — the joke name, the name their father used when he was teasing him, *come here, Maro, you left-footed disaster*. A name Danny had never used with anyone outside the family. A name that existed nowhere on any document, any register, any wanted poster.

Rosalind discovered she was standing. She didn't remember getting up.

"You knew," she said. "You've known since the salt flats. You let me chase you for two years and you *knew*—"

"I suspected!" He was up too, chain dragging, hands out. "I suspected. There's a difference. I had a name and a build and a compass that pointed at the sea, and none of that adds up to your brother, Rosalind, it adds up to a coincidence with excellent posture—"

"Danny is *dead*, Kess. He died holding that thing, and you—"

"I was *there*." It came out louder than he meant it to, echoing off the temple walls, doubling, tripling, the friendly creep of the acoustics turning suddenly merciless. His voice dropped. "I was there. I held him. He put it in my hands and told me to take it home, and I didn't even know where home *was* until the needle started pulling. So yes. I ran from you. I'd run from you again. Because you want someone to punish for how he died, and I am *tired*, Rosalind, and I am not going to be your stone."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, the river rose another inch against the carvings, patient as a debt collector.

Rosalind sat back down. Her legs simply declined to keep holding her.

"My brother," she said slowly, "was a ranger. Like me. Desert-born, oath-sworn, honest to the point of stupidity. He was not a priest in a drowned temple and he was not a man named Maro and he did not die handing treasure to smugglers."

"No," Kess said quietly. He lowered himself back onto his bench, and when he spoke again, all the fight had gone out of his voice, leaving something worse underneath. "He wasn't. And he was. Both things were true of him, the way a river is true of itself upstream and down. You knew the downstream version. I met him the week before the source closed over him."

"What does that mean? *What does that mean?*"

But Kess only shook his head, and pulled the chain up onto the bench beside him like a child with a blanket, and said, "Sleep. We'll need our arms tomorrow. The water's still rising, and I don't think this temple intends to let us leave by the front door."

* * *

**Rosalind**

She didn't sleep, not really. She drifted in the grey country between, and in that country the temple talked to her.

Not in words. In pictures — the panels she'd seen, rearranging themselves behind her eyelids. The robed figure calming the river. The robed figure sealing the socket. And in the deepest panel, the one the floodwater had only just uncovered, the one she'd glimpsed for half a second before the light failed: the robed figure *falling*, the compass flying from his left hand, the river rising around him like a mouth.

Danny falling. Danny sinking. Eight months ago, or a thousand years, or both.

She woke before dawn with her heart slamming and the fire down to coals, and Kess was already awake, standing at the chamber's inner door, looking back into the deep of the temple.

"What," she said. "What is it."

"The water dropped," he said. "Two feet, maybe three. While we slept. And Rosalind—" he turned, and the coal-light caught something in his expression she'd never seen on him before. Not fear. Something closer to recognition. "It didn't drop everywhere. It dropped *inward*. Down the main hall, toward the altar pool. Like the temple's breathing in."

They stood together at the top of the flooded stairway, and looked down into the great hall, where the dawn's first light fell through some hidden shaft and lay across the water like a road. The carvings emerged from the retreating flood one by one, dripping, gleaming — fish, processions, the tall left-handed figure — and at the far end, in the widest and oldest panel of all, the robed figure stood with the compass pressed into the earth, and beneath it, in characters older than any script Rosalind knew but shaped, somehow, like the wave-work on the compass housing, a single line of text.

Kess read it aloud, haltingly, the way a man reads a language he's never been taught but has dreamed in all his life.

"'The river keeps what is given,'" he translated. "'And gives back what is kept.'"

Below them, the altar pool began to glow — faintly, steadily, the exact blue of the cave mouth on the coast, the blue of the whirlpool that had swallowed them both.

And from somewhere beneath the water, unmistakable, impossible, came the sound of a bell.
