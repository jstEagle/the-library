## 10. Clan War at the Weir

The trouble with being captured by raiders, Rosalind decided somewhere around the third hour of marching uphill with her wrists lashed to a carrying pole, was that nobody warned you about the walking.

She had imagined capture as a single dramatic moment — a shout, a net, maybe a knife at the throat. She had not imagined that the Highland Clan's idea of prisoner transport was essentially a very long hike with extra weight, conducted in cheerful silence broken only by the occasional argument about whether they should have taken her boots.

"They're desert boots," said the one called Brannoc, for the fourth time.

"She'll need them when we throw her back," said the one called Mave, who seemed to be in charge and seemed to resent it.

"Throwing people *back* is wasteful," Brannoc said. "Boots are boots."

Rosalind kept her eyes forward and her mouth shut, which was a discipline she'd learned on twenty years of patrol: the less you gave them, the more they invented on their own, and what they invented was usually more useful than anything you could have told them. Behind her, she could hear Kess breathing — quick, shallow, the breath of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

They had been taken at dawn, or what passed for dawn this deep in the gorge, where the sun arrived late and left early like a guest with somewhere better to be. One moment Rosalind and Kess had been picking their way along the high trail above the drowned temples, arguing — quietly, wearily, the way two people argue when they've run out of energy for real fighting — about which fork of the river would take them upstream fastest. The next moment the mist had produced six armed figures out of nothing, the way mist does when it's been paid to.

Kess had frozen. That was the thing she couldn't stop turning over. Not reached for a weapon, not run — frozen, staring past the raiders' spears at something behind them, and then saying, in a voice she'd never heard him use before, "*You.*"

And the tall raider with the braided beard had looked at Kess and said, "After three generations, you come *walking* up our river?" — and that had been that.

Now the trail climbed through terraces cut into the gorge wall, each one green with crops Rosalind didn't recognize, tended by people who stopped their work to watch the prisoners pass. They didn't look hostile, exactly. They looked like people watching a storm come in over land they needed.

"Your friend is bad at this," Mave said, without turning around. She walked like a woman whose spine had opinions.

"My friend?"

"The thief." Mave jerked her chin backward without looking. "He keeps saying things under his breath. *I'm not who you think I am. There's been a mistake. You've got the wrong line.*" She snorted. "In my experience, the wrong line always says exactly that."

"He's not my friend," Rosalind said. It came out too fast, and Mave's eyebrows went up in a way that suggested Rosalind had just confirmed several things.

* * *

The weir announced itself before they saw it: a low, constant thunder that Rosalind felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears. Then the trail bent around a shoulder of black rock and there it was — a wall of fitted stone laid across the entire gorge, older than anything she'd ever seen built by human hands, with the river pouring over its crest in a single unbroken sheet of white.

It wasn't a dam. Dams held water back. This did something else — it *stepped* the water down, in long descending pools, and the pools were full of fish traps and waterwheels and children leaping between the stones with the casual fearlessness of children everywhere who had grown up on top of something dangerous.

Above the weir, carved into the cliff face itself, were houses. Hundreds of them, stacked and cantilevered, connected by stairs and rope bridges, the whole cliff city hung like a wasp nest of pale wood and dark stone. Smoke rose from vents. Goats stood on rooftops, judging everyone.

"Oh," Rosalind said, before she could stop herself.

Mave glanced back, and for a moment something almost kind crossed her face. "Aye. Everyone says that."

"It's like—" Rosalind searched. "Like someone built a city into a promise."

"That's near enough to what the elders say." Mave's kindness withdrew as quickly as it had come, folded away like a good knife. "Which makes it worse, doesn't it. Strangers coming to see it."

"I'm not—"

"You climbed three days up our river following a thief who carries a stolen relic. Whatever you are, you're not *nothing*."

They took the prisoners down a stair cut so steeply into the rock that Rosalind's legs burned, across a rope bridge that swayed with insulting confidence, and into a hall built from a natural cavern at the weir's base, where the thunder of the river became a heartbeat you stopped noticing after a while and then noticed all at once, every time.

Inside, the hall was lit by lamps burning something that smelled faintly of fish oil and pine. Elders sat in a crescent on stone benches worn smooth by generations of sitting. And in the center of the floor, on a raised stone plinth under a cloth, sat an object that made Rosalind's chest go tight before she understood why.

The cloth was pulled off by a young woman with ceremonial gravity, revealing a socket — a round depression in the plinth's surface, ringed with carvings, empty. The carvings spiraled inward like water going down a drain, and at their center, where the compass should have rested, there was only smooth stone and a fine skin of dust.

Three generations of dust.

An old man rose from the center of the crescent. He was small and straight-backed, with eyes the color of river fog, and he looked at Kess the way a hawk looks at a field mouse that has made the mistake of standing still.

"Say your name," the old man said.

Kess opened his mouth. Closed it. Rosalind watched him do the arithmetic again — she could practically see the numbers moving behind his eyes — and she knew, with the sick certainty of a ranger who has spent her life reading faces, that whatever he was about to say, it was going to be the wrong answer delivered badly.

"Kess," he said. "Just Kess."

"Of the line of Sarrow?"

"No," Kess said. Then, immediately, because he apparently could not help himself: "I mean — yes. No. There isn't really a *line*, as such—"

"There is a line," the old man said, and his voice didn't rise at all, which was somehow much worse. "There has been a line since the Breaking of the Weir. A line that took the Stone Compass from its socket and carried it out of Rivergate, and left us with an empty plinth and a river we can no longer read. Every child here learns the name. Sarrow. Say it back to me."

"Sarrow," Kess repeated, in the tone of a man agreeing to a hostage demand.

"And you carry the compass."

"I — bought it," Kess said. "From a dying man. Years ago. Fairly! Mostly fairly. There was a transaction."

"A transaction." The old man turned to the crescent of elders. "He says there was a transaction."

Someone in the crescent laughed, briefly and without humor.

Rosalind stepped forward, which was stupid, and knew it was stupid, and did it anyway. "He's telling the truth about the purchase. I've tracked him across two hundred miles of salt flat and I can tell you he didn't steal it — he *carried* it, like it was pulling him. If you want the truth of how that compass left this place, it's older than him. Older than both of us."

The old man's fog-colored eyes moved to her for the first time. Up close they were unsettling, because they didn't seem to look *at* her so much as *through*, taking measurements.

"And who are you, stranger, who defends thieves on another clan's stone?"

"Rosalind Okafor. Desert ranger, out of the eastern flats." She hesitated. The oath-knife at her belt felt suddenly heavy — the knife she'd used to cut them free of the weir downstream, the blade that had cost her a vow. "I'm chasing the same truth you are."

"Chasing it *upstream*," the old man said softly. "Toward the source. Toward the place where the compass belongs." He let that sit in the thundering silence. "Interesting, how the guilty and the curious walk the same road."

"I'm not guilty of anything except poor company," Rosalind said.

Behind her, Kess made a small wounded noise.

* * *

They put the prisoners in a storeroom — because the Highland Clan, Mave explained with some embarrassment, hadn't needed a proper jail in living memory, and the closest thing was a root cellar with a new lock. It was dry, it was warm, and it smelled overwhelmingly of fermented fish, which Rosalind chose to regard as a tactical disadvantage for her captors rather than herself.

They sat against opposite walls, knees nearly touching in the narrow space, a single lamp between them.

"So," Rosalind said. "'There isn't really a line, as such.'"

"Don't."

"'There was a *transaction*.'"

"I said don't."

"You froze when you saw them. You said '*you*' like you recognized the bearded one. And then, given one question to answer honestly, you managed to make 'no' sound like a confession." She leaned her head back against the stone. "Kess. In the temple, you told me you bought the compass from a dying man. You told me you'd never been to Rivergate. Which of those was true?"

Silence. The lamp flame leaned and recovered.

"The dying man was real," Kess said finally. "That part was true."

"And the rest?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. When he did, his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man laying cards face down.

"When I was a boy — before I ever saw the compass — I dreamed about this place. The gorge. The stair. A room with a spiral carved in the floor and something missing from the middle of it. I dreamed it so often I could draw it." He looked up at her, and for once there was no smirk anywhere on him, and that frightened her more than the raiders had. "I told you I'd never been to Rivergate. That was true. But Rivergate has clearly been to me."

Outside, footsteps crossed the landing above — two sets, unhurried, pausing by the door. A murmur. Then Mave's voice, low, through the wood:

"Ranger. You awake?"

"Barely."

"The elders want to see you. Alone." A pause. "They say the thief stays here."

"Why me?"

Another pause, longer, and when Mave answered, Rosalind heard something in it that she hadn't heard from any Highlander yet — not hostility, not suspicion, but the careful neutrality of a messenger carrying news she didn't like.

"Because the old man asked you where you learned to defend strangers on clan stone. And then he asked us to check your knife." The latch rattled. "Ranger — where did you get an oath-knife with a Rivergate maker's mark on the tang?"

Rosalind looked down at the blade at her belt — her brother's gift, the one thing of his she'd carried every day since the funeral — and felt the floor of the world tilt, gently, like a boat taking water.

"I didn't know it had one," she said.

"Yeah," said Mave, opening the door. "That's what worries them."
