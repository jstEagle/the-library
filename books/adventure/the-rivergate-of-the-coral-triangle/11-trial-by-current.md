## 11. Trial by Current

The Highland Clan did not believe in prisons. They believed in rope.

Kess learned this within an hour of capture, when a broad-shouldered woman with river-mud braided into her hair cinched his wrists behind him with a knot so elegant it was almost apologetic. "We don't waste walls on liars," she said, tightening the last loop. "Walls are for people worth keeping."

"And what am I?" Kess asked, because he had never once in his life had the good sense to keep his mouth shut at the right moment.

"Pending," she said, and shoved him toward the weir.

* * *

The weir sat where the gorge narrowed to a throat of black stone, a ladder of timber and stone steps rising across the river's spine. Even this far upstream, the water ran loud — not the polite murmur of Rivergate's channels but a full-throated roar that climbed through the rock and into Kess's teeth. The Highlanders had built their judgment-place here deliberately, he realized. You couldn't hear yourself think at the weir. You could only hear the river.

They bound Rosalind first.

He watched them do it from ten paces off, arms lashed to a timber post driven into the lowest step, water breaking white around her shins. She didn't struggle. That was the thing about her that kept wrong-footing him — a desert ranger, hauled half-drowned out of wreckage, chained for days, and she stood at that post like it was a duty roster she'd been assigned. Chin up. Eyes moving. Cataloguing exits even now, even with no exit in existence except straight down the rapids.

The clan elder was a small man made almost entirely of beard and grievance, and he addressed the assembled raiders in a voice pitched to carry over the water.

"Generations ago," he called, "a thief came up from the lowlands and took what was ours. The Compass of the Source. Our grandmothers sang the theft into us before we could walk. And now—" he turned, one knotted finger finding Kess like a compass needle itself, "—the relic walks back into our reach, carried by a stranger who cannot say where he got it."

"I *can* say where I got it," Kess shouted over the current. "I told you—"

"You told us a dying man gave it to you." The elder's beard quivered with contempt. "A dying man. A nameless dying man, on a road, far away, beyond any witness. Do you know what we call such a story at the weir?"

"A story?"

"We call it *water*," the elder said. "It flows wherever it wants and holds nothing."

There was a rumble of assent from the raiders. Kess caught Rosalind's eye across the churning shallows. She gave him a look he was beginning to know well — the look that said *you are making this worse* — and then, infuriatingly, the smallest twitch at the corner of her mouth that said *but keep going anyway.*

"The river judges," the elder went on, raising both arms. "That is the old law. Not blades, not votes, not the whisper-politics of the Gatekeepers. The river. We bind the accused at the weir at high turn, and we leave them through three risings of the water. If they are innocent, the river will not take them. If they are guilty—" he spread his hands, as if the outcome were self-evident and merciful, which it very much was not, "—the river keeps its own."

"Three risings," Kess said flatly. "How long is a rising?"

Nobody answered him. Somebody laughed.

* * *

They tied him to the post beside Rosalind as the light went copper and then gray. The rope went around his chest, his waist, his ankles — thorough, workmanlike, and, he noted professionally, tied by someone who genuinely loved knots. His left arm stayed free enough to feel his own pulse, which was currently doing something undignified.

The water came to his knees. Then, as the high turn swelled, to his thighs. It was cold in the way that deep places are cold, cold that didn't come from weather but from somewhere older underneath.

"So," Rosalind said, when the raiders had withdrawn to the bank to build their judgment-fires. Her voice was level, but he'd spent enough days beside her now to hear the effort holding it level. "Any thoughts? You're the one who's been drawn back here your whole life. You must know the old laws."

"I know stories about the old laws," Kess admitted. "Mostly that nobody comes back from the weir innocent. The river doesn't check paperwork, Roz. It just takes whoever's still standing there when the third rising crests."

"Wonderful." A pause. Water shoved at their legs, patient as a debt collector. "Kess. If we get out of this—"

"When."

"—*if*," she repeated, with the stubbornness he was starting to find less annoying and more like ballast, "you're going to tell me the truth about that dying man. The real truth. Not the version you told me on the temple steps, and not whatever you told Vesh, and not whichever lie you're still sitting on because you think it protects someone."

Kess looked downstream. The drowned temples were somewhere below them in the dark, and below those, Rivergate, and below everything, the sea, and somewhere between all of it a truth he'd been carrying so long it had grown into his skeleton like a splinter.

"If we get out of this," he said, "I'll tell you everything. Even the part you'll hate."

"That's a large category."

"Yeah," he said. "For me it mostly is."

She almost smiled. Then the second rising came, and there was no more room for talking.

* * *

The second rising reached their waists and did not recede all the way. The Highlanders had chosen their season well; the river was climbing toward some argument with the moon, and each surge arrived higher than the last, dragging silt and broken reeds against their legs. Kess's fingers had gone numb inside the ropes. He worked them anyway, the way you worry a tooth — not because it helps, but because stopping would mean admitting how bad it was.

"Rosalind."

"Mm."

"There's something I should probably mention."

"Now's a great time."

"The oath-knife," he said. "Your ranger's knife. The one you swore on. They took your pack but they didn't search your boot, because Highlanders don't wear boots and it didn't occur to them anyone would hide a blade in footwear."

Silence, except for the river. Then, very quietly: "You saw that."

"I see everything. It's my worst quality."

"It's a *terrible* quality." But her voice had changed — sharpened, recalculated. "You want me to cut us loose."

"I want you to consider cutting us loose."

"You know what that knife is." Her tone wasn't angry. It was worse than angry — it was careful, the way she got when something mattered too much to shout about. "I swore my oath on that blade when I was nineteen years old. Every vow I've kept since has run through it. If I use it to break custody — if I use it to run from a lawful judgment, even a barbaric one — then every arrest I ever made gets a question mark after it. Every oath I ever swore becomes a thing I broke the first time it cost me something."

The water surged. It hit them at the ribs this time, and Kess heard her breath catch and steady.

"Rosalind," he said. "Look at me."

She turned her head. In the firelight from the bank her face was wet and set, and younger than she pretended to be, and older than she deserved to have to be.

"In three risings, the river decides," he said. "And the river doesn't decide anything. It's just water. It doesn't know you. It doesn't know whether you kept your vows or broke them or whether you're the most stubborn, decent, impossible person I've ever been chased by. It will drown us both and the Highlanders will sing about justice, and your brother's compass will sit in their shrine until somebody else steals it, and none of it — *none of it* — will have been about guilt. It'll just be about standing in the wrong place at the wrong turn of the tide."

"That's not the point—"

"It's exactly the point!" The words tore out of him louder than he meant, swallowed instantly by the roar. He lowered his voice, dragged it close. "You told me once, on the flats, why you chase thieves. You said it's because the law is the only promise the desert ever keeps. I understood that. I respected it. But Rosalind — the law isn't here. There's no desert charter at this weir. There's a bearded man with a grudge older than his grandmother, tying strangers to posts because his ancestors needed someone to blame. Your oath was sworn to protect people from exactly this. Don't let the oath kill the person who swore it."

Her jaw worked. The water climbed another handspan.

"And what about yours?" she asked suddenly. "Your promises. The ones you keep lying around. If I cut us free, you owe me — not a story. The whole of it. Whatever you've been carrying since before we met."

Kess felt the splinter shift in his chest.

"You'll hate it," he said again.

"Probably."

"But you'll cut us loose anyway?"

She was quiet long enough that two surges passed between them. When she spoke, her voice had gone soft in a way that scared him more than the river did.

"My brother died holding that compass," she said. "I've spent a year telling myself that if I could just get it back, I'd get *him* back somehow — the version of him I grew up with, the one who taught me stars and knots and never lied to me. And every mile upstream, that version gets smaller and something truer gets bigger, and I hate it, and I can't stop. So yes. I'll cut us loose. Because I'm not losing you to a river full of other people's grudges before I get the whole truth. That's not loyalty to an institution, Kess. That's just me being selfish."

"That's the least selfish thing I've ever heard."

"Don't ruin it."

* * *

The third rising began under a sky rinsed clean of stars by cloud, and the fires on the bank threw long orange shadows across the water. The Highlanders had gathered to watch — dozens of them now, silent, their faces lit from below like masks. The elder raised his staff.

Kess felt the rope tighten as the water swelled, felt the first real fear of the night arrive, cold and specific. This was no longer thigh-deep politics. The river was coming for the posts themselves.

"Rosalind," he said. "*Now.*"

He heard rather than saw it — the small scrape of a boot heel, the whisper of a blade leaving leather. Then her hand found his bound wrists in the dark, and the oath-knife slid through the ropes around his chest like they were made of morning.

The sound of it did something to him he hadn't expected. It wasn't relief. It was closer to grief — the sound of a woman cutting herself loose from the only fixed point she'd ever trusted, and choosing, in the same stroke, to trust *him*, of all the people in all the world.

His arms came free. He tore at his ankle bindings while she sawed through her own, and the water hit them at the chest, and somewhere on the bank the elder's voice cracked open in fury —

"*The river sees! The river sees what you've done!*"

"Tell it to send a letter!" Kess shouted back, which was, in retrospect, not the moment for wit, but he'd never claimed otherwise.

Rosalind grabbed his collar. For one heartbeat, in the firelight, he saw her face clearly — terrified, resolute, and freer than he'd ever seen it, like a woman stepping off a ledge she'd been standing on her whole life.

"Together," she said.

"Together," he agreed, and meant it more than he'd ever meant anything he'd said to her.

They jumped.

* * *

The river took them the way it took everything — completely, indifferently, without malice or mercy. It rolled him end over end, filled his ears with thunder, scraped him along stone that had been waiting centuries for something to scrape. He lost Rosalind's grip in the first instant. He lost direction in the second. He lost, briefly, the entire concept of up.

But he did not lose consciousness, and he did not lose the strange, stubborn certainty that had been growing in him since the cave mouth — that the river knew them. Not judged them. *Knew* them. It had pulled them both through the whirlpool into Rivergate; it had wrecked Ilo's boat at the temples; it had carried the compass across a continent and back. And now it held them in its fists and considered.

Then it spat them out.

Kess came up coughing in slack water beneath an overhang, black and quiet, the roar of the weir muffled to a heartbeat behind him. He clawed toward the stone, found a root, hung on. A moment later a hand closed on his ankle — he yelped — and Rosalind surfaced beside him, gasping, hair plastered to her face, the oath-knife still clenched in her fist.

"You're alive," she wheezed.

"Statistically surprising."

"Shut up." She hauled herself onto the shelf of stone beside him, and for a long moment they just breathed, side by side, dripping, alive, criminals.

Then she started to laugh. It was a ragged, disbelieving sound, and it cracked halfway through into something else, and Kess had the grace not to look at her while she pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes.

"I broke my oath," she said finally. "Nineteen years old to tonight. Gone. One knife-stroke."

"Do you regret it?"

She thought about it honestly, which was the most Rosalind thing about her. "Ask me tomorrow," she said. "Tonight I'm too busy being glad you're not dead."

High above, torches moved along the rim of the gorge. Voices drifted down, thin with distance — the clan spreading out along the banks, hunting. They couldn't stay. Downstream lay the drowned temples and, beyond them, whatever waited at the source. Upstream lay ropes and fires and a bearded man's righteous fury.

Kess pushed himself upright, joints complaining, and looked at Rosalind — oath-broken, soaked, alive, watching him with an expression that said *your debt is due.*

"You said you'd tell me everything," she said quietly. "Even the part I'll hate."

"I did."

"And the dying man. His name. The real one."

Kess looked downstream, into the dark where the water ran smooth and deep toward the drowned temples, and felt the splinter finally begin to move.

"His name was Emeric Halloran," he said. "But everyone who knew him called him Em. And Rosalind — there's something you need to understand about why he sent the compass home."

Her face went very still.

"*Home,*" she repeated. "You said home. You told me he was a stranger on a road."

"I lied," Kess said. "About one detail. Just one. But it's the one that changes everything — because the man whose compass your brother died holding wasn't taking it anywhere."

He met her eyes.

"He was bringing it *back*. To Rivergate. Because your brother stole it from here first — and he stole it to save the world."
