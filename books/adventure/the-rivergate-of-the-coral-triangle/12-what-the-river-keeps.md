## 12. What the River Keeps

The river spat us out the way a cat spits out something it regrets tasting.

I came up coughing in a backwater eddy where the current had dumped an entire museum of debris — half a canoe, a fishing net gone green with age, and enough driftwood to build a chapel. Kess surfaced three feet away, sputtering, one hand still knotted in the rope that had bound his wrists before I cut him loose at the weir. The rope had saved him. It had also nearly drowned us both, tangling our legs together like two flies in one web, so that for thirty terrible seconds downstream we had been not enemies, not allies, but a single four-armed creature with too many opinions about which way was up.

"Rosalind." He said my name like a man checking whether a coin was still in his pocket. "Rosalind. Are you —"

"Alive," I managed. "Barely. Ask me again when I've stopped swallowing the river."

I dragged myself onto the driftwood shelf of the bank and lay there on my back while the sky wheeled gently overhead. My oath-knife was still in my hand. I stared at it.

I had cut that rope. I had cut the vow woven into it — the ranger's knot every desert ranger ties at their swearing-in, the one that says *my word is iron, my bond unbreakable*. You don't cut it. You don't ever cut it. There are rangers who have died rather than cut it, and their names are carved into the cistern wall at Fort Meridian, and someday mine might be carved under them with a footnote: *also broke her oath to save a thief from being judged by a river*.

Except he wasn't a thief. That was the thing my hands kept returning to as they shook against the wet stone. The clans at the weir had called him the descendant, the relic-taker, the thief of generations — and he had denied it badly, exactly as badly as he'd lied to me in that interrogation room, and now I finally understood why he lied badly. He lied badly because lying wasn't in him. Whatever the truth was, it was worse than theft, and he'd been carrying it alone for years.

And Ilo had gone into the water at the rapids trying to help us, and I did not know if she was alive.

That thought sat on my chest heavier than the river had.

Kess hauled himself up beside me, dripping, and for a long moment neither of us spoke. The backwater murmured around us. Somewhere upstream, faint as memory, the clans would be discovering the empty weir and the cut rope, and working themselves into a fury about it.

"We should move," Kess said finally. "When they find the rope—"

"I know."

"They'll follow the banks. We should get into the trees."

"I know, Kess." I sat up, wrung out my hair, and looked at him properly for the first time since the weir. He looked terrible. He looked like a man who had been bound to a weir and left for a river to judge, which he was. "But first you're going to tell me what you didn't tell me. All of it. Because I broke an oath for you, and I'd like to know what I bought."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. And then, blessedly, honestly, said: "I don't know all of it. But I know more than I told you, and you deserve it. Just — not here. Not on an open bank with Highland raiders coming."

It was a fair point, and I hated fair points made by people I was furious at, so I stood up without answering and started walking into the treeline.

* * *

We found shelter in the ribs of a wrecked ferry hull, beached decades ago and half-swallowed by strangler fig. Inside, it was dry-floored and dim, hung with old river-glass beads that someone had strung across the beams — a shrine, maybe, or just decoration left by some crew that never came back. Kess built a tiny smokeless fire with the competence of a man who had been homeless more often than not, and I watched him do it and thought: *a dying man gave him a compass, and he has been walking toward this river ever since.*

"All right," I said. "Talk."

So he talked.

He told me about the dying man — not the name yet, I noticed, though I marked the omission and let it lie — but the rest. A desert town on the far edge of the salt flats. A hospice room that smelled of camphor. A stranger with river-grey eyes who had paid for his own bed by telling stories no one believed, about a city inside a gorge where the water ran uphill twice a day, about gatekeepers who charged strangers in truths instead of coin.

"He knew he was dying," Kess said, turning his hands over the little fire. "Not sick-dying. *Chosen*-dying. He said the river keeps its own ledger, and his page was full. He asked me to carry something home for him. Not sell it. Not use it. Carry it home. He said—" Kess's voice caught, and he steadied it with visible effort. "He said the compass isn't a compass. It's a key. And keys are only safe in the lock they were made for."

"And you took the job," I said. "For pay?"

"For pay," he admitted. "I was broke and he was generous and I told myself it was easy money. Walk west, find a cave, drop a thing in a whirlpool. What could go wrong?" He laughed, short and bitter. "Then I got to the coast and the compass started *pulling*. Have you felt it? Of course you haven't, you're not — it doesn't pull everyone. It pulled me. Every night it turned in my pack until it pointed the way I'd already decided to go, and after a while I couldn't tell anymore whether I was carrying it or it was carrying me. So when you caught up with me at the tide caves, I stopped running. Because I was tired, and because some part of me knew running from that thing was like running from weather."

I fed a stick to the fire and said nothing, because the next question was the one that mattered and I wanted to ask it with my eyes open.

"The dying man," I said. "His name."

Kess looked at me across the fire. And I saw him decide — saw the exact moment the last wall came down, the moment he chose truth over whatever thin safety the lie had bought him.

"He went by Tam," he said. "Tam Oduya. But that wasn't his real name. He told me that much himself, near the end. He said if anyone ever asked after him — if anyone ever came looking, following the compass — I should give them his real name, because they'd earned it and he hadn't." Kess drew a breath. "His real name was Halloran. He said it like it hurt. *Halloran.* Like a word in a language he'd sworn off."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a bird complained about the weather.

My brother's name was Daniel Halloran. Daniel had run off to the coastal towns eleven years ago, chasing some dream of the sea, and written twice a year, and come home thinner each time, and died — the rangers' report said *drowned, circumstances unclear* — holding a compass in his fist that our mother swore she'd never seen before.

Daniel, who taught me to read stars. Daniel, who called me Roz and stole the last sweet potato off every plate we ever shared. Daniel, who had been *Tam Oduya*, who had been a Rivergate exile, who had known this gorge and these gatekeepers and this river well enough to die by its ledger.

"He was my brother," I heard myself say. It came out very flat, very calm, the voice I used for reporting deaths to families. "Daniel Halloran was my brother. And he sent a stranger to carry his key home instead of telling me any of it. Instead of telling *me*."

Kess flinched. "Rosalind—"

"Eleven years." I was standing now; I didn't remember standing. "Eleven years of letters, and not one word. He let me think he was a fisherman. He let me think he *drowned*. He handed the most important thing he owned to a stranger he met in a hospice, and he let his own sister chase that stranger across the salt flats believing—" My voice cracked, damn it, cracked right down the middle. "Believing her brother's ghost needed avenging."

"Maybe," Kess said carefully, "he was protecting you. From exactly this. From the gorge, and the clans, and Vesh, and everything that wants that compass."

"Then he protected me *badly*," I snapped, "because here I am."

And that, absurdly, was what broke the tension — because Kess snorted, and then I snorted, and then we were both laughing in the wrecked ferry hull like a pair of lunatics, the exhausted laughter of people who have nearly drowned twice in one day and broken an oath and discovered their whole history was a locked room. It lasted maybe ten seconds. Then I sat back down, and the silence that followed was a different kind — lighter, somehow. Shared.

"I'm sorry," Kess said quietly. "About Daniel. About all of it."

"He should have told me himself."

"Yeah. People should say a lot of things." He poked the fire. "Doesn't make them liars. Makes them cowards with good intentions, which is worse, honestly."

Outside, the light through the fig leaves was going amber. And that was when we both heard it: the low, rhythmic knock of an oar against a hull, coming up the backwater channel.

I was on my feet with the knife drawn before my brain caught up. Kess blew out the fire with one breath. Through a gap in the wrecked planking, I watched a small boat slide into view — a ferry skiff, patched a dozen times, riding low — and at the oar, wrapped in sodden blankets with her hair plastered to her skull—

"Ilo," I breathed.

She looked *different*. That was the first thing. Ilo the gossip, Ilo the smuggler of rumors, Ilo who narrated the whole river like a festival announcer — that Ilo had been bright as a struck bell. This woman poling slowly through the backwater was quiet in a way that seemed to have been installed from the inside. Her eyes moved over the water like she was reading it. When she spotted the wrecked hull, she didn't call out. She simply turned the skiff and brought it alongside, and said, in a voice scraped raw:

"You'd better get in. And you'd better listen, because I went down where the carvings are, and I need to tell someone what I saw before it eats a hole in me."

* * *

She wouldn't talk while poling. She talked once we were a mile downstream, moored in the lee of a drowned temple terrace where the water ran slow and green and only knee-deep over the old flagstones. She made us eat first — dried fish and hardtack from her stores, pressed on us with the fierce hospitality of someone who needs her hands busy. Then she sat back against the mast, looked at the water for a long time, and began.

"When the boat broke up at the rapids," she said, "the current took me under. Not the surface current — the *under* one. There's a whole other river down there, moving slower, colder, going places the top river's forgotten. I got rolled through a gap in the temple foundations, and I shouldn't have survived it, except the under-river put me in an air pocket. An old ceremonial chamber, mostly dry. And the walls—"

She stopped. Started again.

"The walls were carved, floor to ceiling. I had light — my lamp survived, sealed. I wish it hadn't. Some things you see and you can't unsee, and this was—" She rubbed her arms. "It was the story of the source. The whole story, in pictures, older than any carving I've seen in the upper temples. And in the middle of it, there's a chamber. A socket. And a compass-shaped key going into it, and rivers — dozens of them, branching out from the chamber in every direction, feeding everything. Fields. Cities. The delta. Everything drinks from that one room."

"That matches what the clans believe," Kess said. "That the compass opens the source."

"No." Ilo's voice came down hard, flat as a slab. "That's what Vesh *wants* everyone to believe, and it's a half-truth, and half-truths are how that man does all his business. Listen. The carvings show the key going in *twice*. Once to set it, ages ago, by the founders. And once more — later, in the newest panels, carved rougher, by different hands — showing the key being *turned wrong*. And when it turns wrong, the carvings show what happens. The rivers don't open. They *obey*. They bend. They flood one valley and starve another, on command. There are figures downstream drowning while figures upstream harvest double." She looked at us both, and her eyes were terrible. "The compass isn't a door-key. It's a *rein*. The founders forged it to lock the source against exactly this — so no one could ever turn the river into a weapon. The lock isn't there to keep people out of the source. The lock is there to keep the source out of people's *hands*."

The backwater lapped against the hull. Somewhere above us, water dripped from the temple stones with the patience of centuries.

"So whoever holds it," I said slowly, working it through, "holds the only thing that can either keep the source sealed true — or break the seal and aim the river like a spear."

"Now you understand," Ilo said. "Now you understand why your brother ran."

The words landed in my chest like a dropped anchor. *Your brother.* She'd guessed, or Kess had told her while I slept off the weir — it didn't matter. She said it plainly, kindly, and it undid me more than the carvings did.

Because suddenly every piece slid into place and the picture was nothing I had ever imagined. Daniel hadn't stolen the compass. Daniel had been *given* it — trusted with it, by Rivergate or by whatever faction within it understood what Vesh was building — and he had carried it out into the world and hidden it in the desert and died holding it rather than let it fall into the wrong hands. He hadn't abandoned us. He'd been a garrison of one, stationed at the edge of the map, holding a fort nobody at home even knew existed.

The thief I had chased across the salt flats.

The guardian. He had been a guardian, and I had spent eleven years thinking he'd run away from us, and he had actually been standing watch.

"Rosalind." Kess's voice, gentle. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're breathing like a woman planning to punch a waterfall."

"I'm considering it."

Ilo watched us both with her new, deep-water patience, and then she leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the grey in her soaked hair, and she said the thing that turned my grief into urgency:

"There's more, and you need to hear it fast. The carvings showed a third panel. Rougher than the others — recent, within a generation. It shows the seal weakening. The founders' lock fraying, the source starting to slip its socket on its own. That's why the dry season matters, why the Gatekeepers count the days, why Vesh is so hungry. The lock is failing, and when it fails completely, the source goes wild — floods, droughts, the whole delta at the mercy of chance. Unless the key is seated true before the dry season seals the channel." She held up her hand, counting. "Thirty-seven days were left when we launched. Two are gone. And Vesh knows what the compass is now — I'd stake my ferry on it, it's why he agreed to guide you so easily, why he's been herding you upstream like sheep toward a shearers' shed. He doesn't want the compass returned. He wants it *turned*."

I looked at Kess. Kess looked at me. Between us, unspoken, passed the same thought: *Vesh agreed too easily. Vesh always agrees too easily.*

"Then we stop talking," I said, "and we start rowing."

Ilo almost smiled — the ghost of the old bell-bright Ilo surfacing for one instant like a fish rising. "There's my girl," she said. "One condition. When we reach the source, and the Gatekeepers demand payment in truth — and they will, they always do — you tell them *all* of it. Your brother. The exile. The theft that wasn't. All of it, out loud, in front of everyone." Her eyes held mine, kind and merciless. "Because the river keeps what's true, Rosalind. And right now, the only person carrying your brother's truth is you."

I looked downstream, toward the darkening gorge, toward the source I could not see but could suddenly feel — the way Kess must have felt it every night, the compass pulling in his pack like a second heartbeat.

Thirty-five days.

"Hold on tight," I said. "Row hard. And Ilo — tell me everything else you saw down there. Every carving. Leave nothing out."

The oars dipped. The skiff swung upstream, into the current, into the gathering dark — and the river, keeping its secrets, carried us toward the one place none of us were meant to go.
