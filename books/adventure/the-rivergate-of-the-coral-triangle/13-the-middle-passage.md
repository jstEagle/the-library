## 13. The Middle Passage

**Rosalind**

The river changed its mind about them somewhere past the third drowned temple.

For eleven days it had shoved them downstream, spun them, swallowed Ilo, spat them onto a weir, bound them to a beam of timber while highland raiders argued over whose grandfather had been robbed — and then, the morning after they cut themselves free and crawled ashore half-drowned, it went calm. Glass-calm. The kind of calm that felt less like mercy and more like the river deciding it had made its point.

Rosalind sat on a flat stone at the water's edge, wringing out her second-worst shirt, and watched Kess do the same three stones down. He was shivering. He was always shivering; she'd begun to suspect it was less about cold than about a body that had spent years running and didn't know what to do with stillness.

"We could split up," he said, not looking at her.

"We could."

"You'd catch me in an hour."

"Half an hour," she said. "You're wet."

He laughed — a short, surprised sound, like he'd stepped on it. Then he pulled his knees up and looked at the water for a long time, and when he spoke again the humor had gone out of him like heat from a stone.

"I'm tired of lying to you, Ranger."

"Okafor," she said automatically. "I broke my oath. The title's retired." She said it lightly, but her chest did something complicated around the words. Eleven days ago she'd sawed through her own vow-knife's binding cord to free him from the weir, and the knife hadn't spoken to her since. Rangers' knives weren't magic — she'd have sworn they weren't — but hers had hummed against her hip for nine years, a faint brass note whenever she kept faith. Now it hung silent as a spoon.

"So you're just a woman chasing a thief across a drowned country for no reason at all."

"I'm a woman walking upstream with a thief because the river keeps trying to kill us separately and failing."

"That's practically a marriage," Kess said.

She threw the wet shirt at him. He caught it, which she hadn't planned on, and held it, looking faintly pleased with himself, which she definitely hadn't planned on either.

* * *

They walked. The gorge narrowed and the temples gave way to terraces cut into the rock, ancient and empty, their stairways furred with moss. Someone had farmed these walls once, centuries ago, before the water rose. At midday they found an orchard gone wild — small sour plums hanging over a collapsed wall — and ate until their stomachs ached, sitting in the ruins like a pair of crows.

It was there, with plum juice on his chin, that Kess started talking.

"You asked me once where I got the compass. On the salt flats, with your knife at my throat. You remember what I told you?"

"You said you bought it."

"I said I bought it," he agreed. "That part was true. What I left out was everything else." He turned one of the plums over in his fingers, not eating it. "Seven years ago. A river town called Sallow's Rest, two hundred miles downstream of here, though I didn't know then that 'here' existed. There was a man dying in the ferry house. Fever. Nobody would sit with him because he talked in his sleep in a language nobody knew, and the ferrymen thought he was cursed."

"And you sat with him."

"I sat with him because nobody else would and because I'd been paid to sweep the floor and sweeping around a dying man is easier than sweeping around gossip." A crooked smile. "I'm not going to pretend I was noble. I was nineteen and hungry. But he grabbed my wrist on the third night and he knew my name somehow, which I hadn't given him, and he said — " Kess stopped. His jaw worked. "He said, *'You'll carry it home for me.'* Not ask. Say. Like it was already decided, like he'd known me longer than I'd known myself."

Rosalind felt the hair rise on her arms. "The compass?"

"He made me swear an oath on it. Not to sell it, not to open it, and never — never — to let anyone from Rivergate take it back without hearing his whole story first. And then he made me memorize a name." Kess looked up at her, and his eyes were steady and miserable. "Because he said someday someone would come asking after the compass, and if that person was honest, the name would mean something to them. *Tobin Vale.* That was the name he gave me. Tobin Vale, of Rivergate, exile."

The world tilted.

Not dramatically — Rosalind didn't fall off the wall or drop her plums. It was smaller and worse than that: the orchard stayed exactly where it was, the light stayed golden, the river went on murmuring its one long syllable below them, and none of it noticed that something inside her had come loose.

*Tobin Vale.*

Her brother's voice, teaching her stars on the roof of their father's station house: *That one's the Ferryman, Roz. Follow him home.* Her brother's handwriting in the margins of her schoolbooks, correcting her geography with jokes. Her brother, dead on the salt flats with a stranger's compass clenched in his fist, and every night of her childhood he had kissed their mother goodnight and lied to her face with a name she'd worn like a coat.

"Tobin," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened. "His name was Daniel Okafor. He was my brother."

Kess went very still. "I know," he said quietly. "I've known since the cave, when you said his name. I should have told you then. I told myself I'd tell you when we were safe, and then safe kept not happening."

"How long?" she heard herself ask. "How long did you know?"

"Since the whirlpool."

"Eleven days." She stood up. Her legs felt borrowed. "Eleven days you've known my brother sent you — that my brother *was* your dying man — and you let me chase you across the flats thinking you were a common thief."

"What was I supposed to say?" For the first time since she'd met him, Kess raised his voice. "'Hello, ranger, funny story, the man you're mourning hired me'? While you had a knife on me? While I thought you might be exactly the kind of person he warned me about?" He stopped, dragged a hand through his wet hair. "He told me someone would come. He told me some people come for the compass and some people come for the truth, and only one of those can be trusted with both. I didn't know which one you were."

"And now?"

"Now I think you're the second one," he said. "But I've been wrong about people before, and being wrong about you would have gotten me killed twice already."

* * *

**Kess**

She didn't forgive him. He hadn't expected her to, and he noted, with the detached interest of a man watching his own funeral procession, that he respected her more for it.

What she did was walk. She picked up her pack, checked the silent knife at her belt with a gesture so automatic it clearly wasn't a decision, and set off up the terrace stairs at a pace calculated to make conversation impossible. Kess followed, because following was the only job he'd ever been any good at, and because the alternative was standing alone in a haunted orchard with a belly full of sour plums and a conscience doing laps.

By late afternoon the terraces ended at a shelf of rock above a long, slow pool, and she finally stopped — not because she'd forgiven him, he suspected, but because even grief needed a place to put itself down.

"My mother named him Daniel," she said, to the water rather than to him. "Our father was a desert ranger too. Died when we were small. Danny used to say he'd be a ranger like Dad, and he did, and he was good, and then one day he just — left. Said he'd found work guiding caravans east. Wrote letters. Came home twice a year." Her laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "Twice a year, Kess. For how many years? How many times did 'Tobin Vale' walk through my mother's kitchen and eat her cooking?"

"I don't know what he did here," Kess said carefully. "Only what he told me at the end. He said he was born on this river. He said he took something that wasn't his to keep, and that keeping it was the only way to keep it safe, and that he'd carried the weight of it so long he'd forgotten which hurt more — the carrying or the lying." He hesitated. "He cried, near the end. Not from the fever. He said his sister was the honest one, and that she'd hate him if she ever learned the truth, and that he hoped she'd hate him anyway rather than learn it."

Rosalind sat down slowly on the rock. For a long moment she said nothing, and Kess, who had survived weir-judgments and clan raids and a whirlpool with more composure than this, discovered he could not bear the silence.

"He also said," Kess added, "that if you ever came asking, I should tell you he kept a drawing of you in his pack for twenty years. He showed me. A little girl with braids, holding up a fish. He'd redrawn it so many times the paper was soft as cloth."

"Don't." Her voice cracked clean through the word. "Don't be kind to me right now. I will fall apart, and I refuse to fall apart in front of a man who lied to me for eleven days."

"Twelve," Kess said, "if we're counting today."

Something happened to her mouth — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, dragging itself upright under terrible conditions. "Was he a good man? Tobin. Daniel. Whoever he was."

Kess thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to her had stopped working somewhere around the weir. "I only knew him for four nights. But he spent his last strength making sure a stranger wouldn't fail him, and he spent his last words worrying about his sister. I think he was a good man who did a hard thing badly, and then spent twenty years paying for it in installments."

"In installments," she repeated. She looked at the pool, at the drowned country all around them, at the whole vast secret her brother had swum in alone. "He paid with his whole life."

"He paid with yours too," Kess said, before he could stop himself. "And mine. And Ilo's, maybe. That's the thing about secrets this size — they don't stay bought."

She turned and looked at him then, really looked, and Kess braced himself for the knife, literal or otherwise.

Instead she said: "Tell me the rest. All of it. The oath, the warning, everything he made you memorize. Tonight, by the fire, start to finish. And Kess—" she pointed at him, and her hand was shaking, and her eyes were dry as the salt flats she'd caught him on, "—if there's one more thing you're holding back, one more name, one more sentence, you tell me now or you spend the rest of this river explaining yourself to the clans instead of me."

"There's nothing else," Kess said. And then, because apparently he was constitutionally incapable of letting a moment stand: "Except that your brother snored like a landslide and accused me of stealing his boots, which I had not yet done."

The ghost-smile came all the way back this time, wobbling. "You stole his boots?"

"After he died. They fit better than mine. It seemed like the thing to do."

* * *

**Rosalind**

They made camp in the shell of a watchtower, one of a chain marching up the gorge, and Kess kept his word. He told it all — the fever nights, the oath sworn on cold brass, the warnings delivered in a dying man's cracked whisper: *the Gatekeepers are not all honest; the source can be turned; whoever holds the compass when the dry season comes holds the river's throat.* Rosalind listened with her arms around her knees and let the story rebuild her brother in front of her, piece by piece, into a man she had never actually met.

It hurt. It hurt like setting a bone. But underneath the hurt, something else was settling — a horrible, clarifying relief. Twenty years of small wrongnesses, of Danny glancing at doors, of letters that never mentioned where he'd been, of a compass he'd never explained — they all clicked into place at once, and even a broken explanation was better than none.

Near midnight, when the fire had burned to coals, she said, "Vesh wants the compass to turn the source. To control the dry-season flow. Hold power over every clan downstream."

"Yes."

"And my brother died keeping that from happening. And I chased the man he trusted with it halfway across the Coral Triangle to drag it back toward—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I've been Vesh's errand girl. I've been the exact person Danny was afraid of."

"No," Kess said, with a firmness that startled her. "You cut us off a weir with your own oath-knife. Whatever you were when you walked into that cave, you're not that anymore. Your brother bet on who you'd become, not who you were. I think he bet right."

She lowered her hands. Across the coals, Kess was watching her with an expression she was beginning to recognize and didn't yet trust — the look of a man who had decided something and was waiting for permission.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. Just — tomorrow we pass the last of the drowned temples. After that the gorge climbs, and the air changes, and you can smell the source before you see it. Cold and green, like crushed leaves. My dying man told me about that smell. He said it was the first thing he remembered and the thing he missed most." Kess poked the coals. "Two days, maybe three, and we're there."

"Where Vesh will be waiting," Rosalind said. "He let us go too easily at the weir, Kess. Raiders don't just happen to release prisoners upstream of the thing they want. He's herding us. We're not travelers anymore — we're deliveries."

Kess was quiet for a moment. Then he smiled, and it was the first smile she'd seen on him that didn't look like armor.

"Well," he said, "then let's make sure we arrive late, dirty, and thoroughly uncooperative."

Above the watchtower, the stars wheeled — and there, low over the gorge's northern rim, the Ferryman climbed the sky, faithful as ever, pointing the way home to a home that had never been hers and somehow, now, was.

Rosalind pulled her blanket to her chin and slept without dreaming for the first time since the whirlpool.

She woke at dawn to Kess standing rigid at the tower's edge, staring downriver.

Smoke," he said. "A lot of it. Coming fast."
