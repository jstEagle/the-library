## 14. Vesh's Bargain

The trouble with rivers, Rosalind had learned in her nineteen days upstream, was that they only ever went two directions, and everyone who wanted to find you knew both.

She had been careful. She'd thought she had been careful. They'd left the drowned temple at first light, kept to the high bank where the ferns grew thick enough to muffle their footfalls, and doubled back twice through the shallows the way her brother had taught her to erase a trail in sand country — walk in the water, leave no story. For three days it had worked. She'd even begun to let herself believe it.

Which was, she reflected later, exactly the mistake. Believing you were careful was how deserts killed people. Believing you were careful was how rivers did it too.

They had stopped at midday in a hollow above the river bend, a green pocket of shade where a fallen strangler fig made a bench the width of a wagon. Kess sat with his back against the trunk, working a strip of dried fish with his teeth, and Rosalind was crouched at the waterline filling their skins when she saw it: a single boat, painted the grey of river fog, sliding out from behind the far bend with no one poling it.

No one visible, anyway.

"Kess," she said quietly.

He looked up, followed her gaze, and stopped chewing. "That's a Gatekeeper hull," he said. "I know the paint. It's mixed with ash from the assessment fires. Vesh's own boat."

"How can you tell whose—"

"Because it's the only one in Rivergate that never hurries." Kess stood, brushing fish crumbs from his knees with the air of a man tidying up before a funeral. "Everything else on this river runs from something or toward something. That boat just arrives. It's a message in itself. *You cannot outrun the administration.*"

"Charming."

"He'd want you to know it was charming. He practices."

The boat touched the bank below them without a sound, and a figure stepped out onto the mud as lightly as a heron. Vesh was not a large man — that was the first thing that unsettled her every time she saw him. She kept expecting power to be bigger. He was narrow and dry-faced, with the neat grey beard of a scholar and the still hands of someone who had never once needed to grab at anything, because everything came to him. He wore the slate-grey sash of the Gatekeepers, and over it, absurdly, a pair of spectacles with one lens dark and one clear.

"Ranger Okafor," he called up the bank, pleasantly. "And the ferryman of lost property. Do come down. I promise I haven't brought soldiers. Soldiers leave footprints, and footprints are a form of lying."

Rosalind glanced at Kess. He was doing arithmetic with his eyes — the bank, the water, the treeline — and she could see the sum come out badly. The fig hollow sat in a bend; the river had them boxed on two sides and Vesh's unhurried certainty on the third.

"We can still run," Kess murmured.

"We could," she agreed. "And then he'd know we have something worth running with, and he'd stop being polite."

"You think he's being polite now?"

"I think he's being *patient*, which is worse, but survivable."

She slung the water skins over her shoulder and started down the bank. After a moment, Kess followed, because — she was learning this about him — for all his lying, he hated being left out of a bad decision.

* * *

Vesh received them at the water's edge as though they were an appointment he had graciously squeezed in. He had a small folding stool with him, which he did not sit on, which Rosalind understood was the point: the stool existed so that they would notice he didn't need it.

"Nineteen days," Vesh said. "Give or take. You've made better time than I predicted. I had you wrecked at the third weir, honestly. The river's temper is not what it was."

"The river's temper," Rosalind said, "killed a friend of mine."

Something flickered across Vesh's face — not guilt, she thought. Recalculation. "The ferry pilot. Ilo. Yes, we heard. The river gives and the river takes; the Gatekeepers have never pretended to govern it, only to keep its accounts." He turned his clear lens on Kess. "You have it with you."

It was not a question, which was its own kind of answer. Rosalind felt Kess stiffen beside her, felt his hand drift — casually, badly, the way a child pretends not to look at the cake — toward the inner pocket of his coat where the compass rode against his ribs. She'd seen him check it eleven times a day. She'd stopped counting.

"You have sharp eyes," Kess said.

"I have a ledger," Vesh said. "The compass was entered into the Gatekeeper registry four hundred years ago. It was entered out of it the same year, under a seizure order signed by the source-wardens of the Lowland Clan. Every Gatekeeper since has carried the debt of it. When your boat went through my assessment, Ranger, I felt it pass like a cold draft under a door." He smiled thinly. "I have been waiting nineteen days to stop being patient."

"Then stop," Rosalind said. "Say what you want."

"What I want." Vesh took off his spectacles, polished the clear lens on his sash, and put them back on — a little ritual, she realized, a man buying himself three seconds. "What I want is a dry season that doesn't kill people. Have you seen the lowlands in the dry months, Ranger? Of course you haven't. You've only come *up* the river. Downstream, the channels braid out into a hundred silver threads, and every year there are fewer of them, and every year the clans who live on those threads fight over the ones that remain. Last dry season, the Lowland Clan dammed the Sorrowfork and the Highland Clan sent raiders, and forty-one people died over water that, I promise you, was flowing past them the whole time — up here, where the source keeps it, behind a lock that only one instrument in the world can open."

Rosalind's mouth had gone dry, and not from the climb. "The compass."

"The compass." Vesh said the word the way a priest says a name of power, and she hated him a little for it. "It is not a toy, whatever the dying exile told his courier. It is a key. The old wardens forged it to *seal* the source — to hold the river to one honest channel and keep any clan from turning the flow into a weapon. A noble idea, four hundred years ago. But the world has changed, and the lock has not. The river sits behind that seal like water behind a fist, and every dry season, downstream, people die of the fist."

"And if the key turns," Rosalind said slowly, working it through, "the source opens. The dry-season flow doubles. Maybe triples."

"Conservatively," said Vesh, with the modest satisfaction of a man who had done the sums.

"Then turn it."

"Ah." He held up one dry finger. "A key that opens a door can also lock it. Turn the compass one way and the source gives. Turn it the other, and it *keeps* — and the Gatekeepers, who alone would hold the key, decide which clans drink and which clans learn humility." He spread his hands, and for a moment he let her see it, the whole architecture of him, the ledger and the lens and the patience: *I am not a villain, I am an administrator, and administrators are only villains with better handwriting.* "I don't want to starve the lowlands, Ranger. I want what every reasonable person wants. A river that behaves."

Rosalind looked at Kess. Kess was looking at the water, and his jaw was set in a way she'd learned to read: he was holding something behind his teeth, and it was heavy.

"And the clans?" she asked. "The Highland Clan believes Kess is the descendant who stole their relic. The Lowland Clan blames outsiders for a theft committed generations ago. If you turn that key, every clan in this gorge will know who holds it."

"The clans," Vesh said, "will adjust. Clans always do. It's the individual who breaks." He turned to her then, fully, and the dark lens made his face half-shadow, half-light, like a coin on its edge. "Which brings me to the actual reason I've come. Not for the courier — he's a means of transport, nothing more. For you."

"Me."

"You are a woman of the open country. A ranger. You have an oath, a dead brother, and — if I read the ledger of your face correctly — a growing suspicion that you've been lied to by nearly everyone you've met since you walked onto my salt flats." He smiled, and it was almost kind, which was the most frightening thing he'd done yet. "You don't belong here, Rosalind Okafor. You never have. The river knows it. That's why it keeps trying to drown you."

"It hasn't tried very hard."

"It's been *testing*. There's a difference." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice, and the lowering was itself a bribe — the intimacy of it, the sense of a door closing on the two of them alone. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it once. Give me the compass. Not for me — for the Gatekeepers, for the registry, for the debt. In exchange, I will open the assessment in your favor. No year of labor. No trial by current, no weighing of your broken oath. A boat, provisions, a guide to the cave mouth at the next high tide — three days from now — and you walk out of this gorge and home to your desert with your name clean and your brother's memory intact."

The words went through her like cool water through a crack, and she hated how good they felt. *Home.* The salt flats at dusk, the long violet shadows, the small honest work of a ranger's life — tracking lost goats, marking wells, sleeping under a sky with nothing hidden in it. Nineteen days of wet rope and drowned temples and a man she couldn't decide whether to trust, and here was a dry-faced stranger offering to hand her back to herself.

"And Kess?" she heard herself ask.

Vesh's smile didn't move, but something behind it did. "The courier stays. He entered Rivergate on a false declaration; the assessment will take its course."

"The assessment takes a year. You told me that yourself."

"The assessment takes what it takes." He said it lightly, but she heard the ledger underneath: *one of you leaves, one of you pays, and the accounts balance.* "He stole from a dying man's legacy, by his own admission. He can serve it out. Rivergate is not unkind to those who serve honestly."

"He didn't steal anything."

"Then he'll have an easy time proving it," said Vesh, "in a year or so."

* * *

They let her walk a little way up the bank alone — Vesh had suggested it, delicately, the way you suggest a thing you've already arranged — and she stood under the strangler fig with the water skins heavy on her shoulder and tried to think like a ranger instead of like a tired woman.

Ranger thinking was simple. Ranger thinking was: *what do I owe, and to whom, and in what order?*

She owed Kess nothing, by the old arithmetic. He had taken her brother's compass off a dead man's body — no, not even taken; *carried*, if his latest story was true, and she still didn't know which of his stories were true, that was the whole trouble with Kess, he was honest the way a river was honest, truthfully, in every direction at once. But he had run from her. He had let her chase him across the salt flats for six days with grief in her teeth. Whatever his reasons, he had made her a pursuer, and pursuers don't owe the pursued.

Except.

Except that he had waited for her at the coast. Except that he'd walked into a whirlpool beside her rather than let her go through alone. Except that when the weir had them bound and the river rising, he'd shouted at the Highland raiders — *take me, she's nothing, she's just a ranger* — and she had cut them both free with her oath-knife and felt her whole old life snap with the cord.

And she owed her brother. That was the oldest debt, the one she'd started this whole chase carrying. But the debt had changed shape out here, the way things do in water. It wasn't *avenge* anymore, or even *recover*. It was *find out*. Her brother — steady, stubborn, terrible-at-goodbyes Tam — had lived a whole secret life she'd never suspected, had died holding a key to a river he'd apparently been born beside, and the only person alive who might explain it was currently standing down the bank negotiating his own imprisonment with a smile he thought she couldn't see.

If she gave Vesh the compass, Kess went into the assessment for a year, and the truth went with him. Her brother's secret would stay a secret — *intact*, Vesh had said, and the word had been chosen, everything Vesh said was chosen — but intact for whom? For her? A clean memory was just a lie she didn't have to watch being told.

And the clans. Forty-one dead last dry season, and Vesh's arithmetic said the key could save them, and Vesh's arithmetic also said the key could starve them, and Vesh held the ledger. She thought of Ilo's laugh, Ilo's gossip, Ilo going down into the brown water at the weir and the boat spinning away empty. She thought of the carvings in the drowned temple: the robed figure with her brother's build, holding the compass up to the source like a lamp. *Forged to seal it against weaponization.* Against exactly this. Against a patient man with a ledger and a lens, standing at the waterline, explaining reasonably why the river ought to behave.

Her brother had died holding that compass. Not lost it. Not carried it idly. *Held* it, the way you hold a door shut.

"No," she said.

She walked back down the bank. Vesh watched her come with the stillness of a man who had already entered her answer in the ledger under *probable*, and was merely waiting for the ink.

"No," she said again, out loud, so it was on the record. "I won't give it to you. And before you adjust your offer — it's not a price problem. You haven't offered enough, but there is no enough. I've seen what that key was made to prevent. I've seen it carved in stone under forty feet of river, and the stone was older than your registry."

Vesh took off his spectacles. Polished the clear lens. Put them back on. "Your brother's memory," he said gently, "is worth a great deal to you. I understand. But memory is not food, Ranger, and the lowlands are hungry."

"Don't," Rosalind said. "Don't dress it in the hungry lowlands. If the flow was all you wanted, you'd have asked the clans to witness the turning. You'd have wanted everyone to see. You came alone, with a stool you didn't sit on, because whatever happens at that socket, you want the only hands on the key to be Gatekeeper hands." She let that sit. His face did not change, and the not-changing was confirmation. "You can take it from us, I suppose. You have soldiers after all, whatever footprints they leave."

"I could," Vesh agreed. "But the river judges takers, and I am a patient man, and patience has never yet needed to." He inclined his head, a small precise bow, the bow of a clerk closing a file he intended to reopen. "The offer stands until the source. After the source, Ranger, the assessment takes its course — for both of you. I'd think about the dry season. It's coming either way."

He stepped into his grey boat. It slid from the bank without a sound, without hurry, around the bend, and was gone, and the river closed over the place where it had been as if it had never been there at all.

Kess let out a breath he'd apparently been holding since the boat appeared. "Well," he said. "That went better than I expected."

"Better?"

"He didn't search us. I had the compass in my *sock*, Rosalind. Do you understand the risk I took for that sock?"

She stared at him. "You had it in your *sock*."

"Left sock. It's a good sock."

She laughed — it came up out of her sideways, exhaustion dressed as humor — and then stopped, because down the bend, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that did not belong to the afternoon: voices. Many voices. And beneath them, a drum, the deep two-beat rhythm she'd heard once before, at the weir, the night the Highland raiders came.

Kess's head came up. The humor drained out of his face like water out of a cracked pot.

"That's not a hunting drum," he said. "That's a *war* drum. Both clans are on this stretch of river." He looked at her, and for once he wasn't lying, wasn't performing, wasn't checking the compass in his sock. He just looked scared, and honest about it. "Vesh didn't come alone after all. He came *ahead*. Whatever he offered us, he's offered the clans something else — and I don't think it was a boat home."

Rosalind shouldered the water skins and turned upriver, toward the sound of the drum, toward the source, nineteen days of river behind them and — if the drum meant what she feared — not many days of anything left.

"Then we'd better get there first," she said.
