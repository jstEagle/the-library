## 15. Nineteen Days

The trouble with being hunted by two clans and a Gatekeeper, Kess reflected as he pressed himself flat against the wet stone of the aqueduct, was that they never hunted at the same speed.

The Highland Clan came fast and loud — torches, dogs, someone always shouting about honor. You could hear them coming from a mile off, which was generous of them. Vesh's gatekeepers were worse. They came quiet, in threes, moving like water finding cracks. Kess had spent nine years around Rivergate's edges and he still couldn't hear them until it was too late.

He counted heartbeats. Ten. Twenty. The torchlight below the aqueduct swung away toward the weir, and a dog whined, confused by the smell of the river.

"Clear," he whispered.

Above him, Rosalind Okafor lowered herself down the aqueduct wall with the unhurried competence of a woman who had once rappelled into canyons for a living and found this particular cliff insulting rather than challenging. She landed beside him without a sound.

"You're getting slow," she said.

"I'm getting *sensible*. There's a difference. Slow people live longer."

They moved along the aqueduct's spine, crouched beneath the lip of stone where the old engineers had carved channels to carry water up to the terraces. The city glittered below them — ten thousand lamps strung along the gorge like a net of fallen stars — and every one of those lamps, Kess knew, sat in a window belonging to someone who would turn him in for the price of a good dinner.

Nineteen days. That was what Rosalind had said last night, counting on her fingers by the light of a stolen candle stub. Nineteen days before the dry season sealed the source, and with it their only way out. Nineteen days to travel upstream through territory held by people who wanted his blood, pursued by a Gatekeeper who wanted something worse than blood.

Vesh wanted the compass.

And Kess had known, since the drowned temple, since he'd seen the robed figure in the carvings and felt the floor drop out of his stomach — he had known that eventually he would have to tell Rosalind the truth. All of it. Not the version he'd fed the interrogators, not the fragments he'd rationed out like a miser counting coins.

The whole truth.

He'd been putting it off for eleven days. He was running out of days to put things off into.

* * *

They holed up past midnight in a boathouse on the middle terraces, one of Ilo's father's old properties, abandoned since the ferry line had been seized. Rosalind built a fire no larger than a cupped hand, using driftwood so dry it burned without smoke, and Kess watched her do it and thought, not for the first time, that she was wasted on the desert. She belonged to rivers. She just didn't know it yet.

She also belonged to a brother who had lied to her his entire life, and Kess was about to make that worse.

"Eat," she said, tossing him dried fish wrapped in oilcloth. "You've been making faces at me all day. Whatever it is, it'll keep better if you're chewing."

"I haven't been making faces."

"You've been making faces since the temple. You're a very expressive liar, Kess. It's your worst quality."

"It's my only quality," he said, "and I resent the slander." He chewed. The fish was terrible. He ate it anyway, because she was right, because the faces weren't going to stop, and because there was a limit to how long a man could carry a thing like this before it started carrying him.

The fire crackled. Somewhere above, a night bird called across the gorge, three notes falling like water.

"When you hired me," Rosalind said quietly, "in the salt flats. Before I knew your name meant anything. You told me you bought the compass from a dying man."

Kess's hands went still.

"That's what you told the interrogators too," she went on. "A dying man. No name. No place. Just bad luck and a purchase." She turned to look at him, and her eyes in the firelight were the exact color of the river at dusk, which was a thought he firmly put away. "I've been chasing thieves most of my adult life, Kess. Thieves lie about everything except the shape of the theft. And the shape of yours has been wrong from the start. Thieves don't run *toward* the thing they stole. They don't stand at a cave mouth and say it wants to go home. Thieves don't look at a compass like it's a debt."

The fire popped. He didn't say anything. She waited, and she was very good at waiting — desert patience, horizon patience — and finally he understood that she wasn't going to fill the silence for him, and that this, more than anything, was why he had to speak.

"He wasn't dying when I met him," Kess said.

Rosalind went very still.

"He was already dead." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I'm sorry. I should have — there's no good way to say this, so I'll say it badly and you can hate me for the packaging. Your brother hired me eight months before he died. In a port town called Sel, three hundred miles east of here, under a name he'd been using for years. He called himself Corren Vale."

Her breath caught. He heard it — a small, sharp sound, like ice cracking on a pond — and he made himself keep going, because stopping now would be the cruelest thing he could do.

"He paid me half up front and half on delivery. The delivery was simple, he said. Carry this compass back to a cave mouth on the coast of the Coral Triangle. Wait for the lowest tide of the year. Go through. Give it to the Gatekeepers and tell them the exile sends it home with an apology." Kess looked at the fire instead of at her. "That was the whole job. One package. One apology. I asked him what was in it for me besides money, and he laughed and said, 'Nothing. That's the point. Nobody can follow a courier who wants nothing.'"

The silence stretched so long that the fire burned down a full finger's width.

"Say the rest," Rosalind said. Her voice was level. Too level. The levelness of a woman standing on a ledge deciding whether the ground is real.

"The rest is that he died before the tide came. Fever, fast, ugly. I was holding his hand when—" Kess stopped. Started again. "When he gave me the second half of the payment early. And a message. And then I did the one thing couriers are never supposed to do."

"You ran," Rosalind said.

"I ran." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "Eight months of a clean job, and at the end I stood at the cave mouth with the compass in my bag and I couldn't make my feet go through. Because I'd spent those eight months asking questions I shouldn't have. About Rivergate. About the source. About what happens to strangers who walk in carrying relics. And every answer I got was worse than the last. So I told myself the job was void. Dead employer, contract broken, finders keepers. I told myself I'd sell it in some city far from any river and be rich and never think about him again." He finally looked at her. "And then you walked into my camp with your brother's name in your mouth and your brother's eyes in your face, and I ran again, because I am, as previously established, a coward with excellent taste in escape routes."

Rosalind didn't move.

The firelight moved on her face instead — shadows sliding over cheekbones gone sharp from nineteen days of hard travel — and Kess realized, with the peculiar clarity of a man watching a disaster he built himself, that he had prepared for her anger. He had rehearsed her anger. Anger he could survive.

What he hadn't prepared for was the way she looked at the fire and said, softly: "Corren Vale taught me to read stars. When I was nine. He said he'd learned it from a friend who lived near the sea."

* * *

She didn't cry. Kess had expected crying, or shouting, or the oath-knife — she still carried it, even broken-vowed, even exiled from her own ranger oath, and he had genuinely considered the possibility that she might use it on him.

Instead she sat with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, and after a long time she said: "How much did he tell you? About why."

"Not why. He was careful about why." Kess turned the compass over in his hands — he still carried it; he'd been carrying it for over a year now, and the weight of it had stopped feeling like theft somewhere around the drowned temple and started feeling like something else entirely. "But he talked around it. The way careful men talk. He said the river was a door that somebody wanted to turn into a weapon. He said the compass was the key to the lock, and locks don't care whose hand turns the key. He said—" Kess hesitated. This part he'd kept even from himself, mostly. "He said if anything happened to him, the compass had to go home before anyone came looking for it. Because someone *would* come looking. Someone inside."

"Vesh."

"Vesh. Or whoever Vesh answers to. Your brother knew the dry season could be controlled. He knew what opening the source by force would mean for every clan downstream — flood in the wet months, famine in the dry, and every clan begging whichever Gatekeeper holds the key." He shook his head. "He didn't steal it, Rosalind. He took it out the way you'd take a lit lamp out of a room full of gunpowder. And then he spent the rest of his life trying to carry it back without letting anyone see the flame."

"And he never told me." Her voice cracked on the last word, just barely, and she pressed her mouth shut until it steadied. "Twenty-nine years. He was my brother. He taught me to track, to read water in dry country, to find north when the stars were hidden. He built his whole life around keeping secrets from the people who loved him, and he died in a port town under a false name with a stranger holding his hand, and I—" She stopped. Breathed. "I spent a year hunting the man who killed him. A year. And the man who 'stole' his compass was carrying out his last request."

"I know," Kess said. "I let you chase me. That's on me. I could have—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't apologize yet. I'm not done being angry, and if you apologize now I'll forgive you before I'm finished, and then I'll have to start over, and neither of us has time for that. Nineteen days, remember?"

Despite everything — despite the grief sitting in the boathouse between them like a third person — Kess felt his mouth twitch. "Nineteen days."

"So here's what we're going to do." She unfolded herself, and the ranger came back into her spine, vertebra by vertebra, the woman who had crossed salt flats on half a skin of water. "We're going to sleep four hours. Then we're going upstream through the Highland terraces, because Vesh will expect us to hug the low water and the Highlands will expect us to stay out of their territory, and everyone is wrong about us roughly half the time. And when we reach the source, you're going to put that compass exactly where your contract says it goes, and I'm going to watch you do it."

"And then?"

"And then," Rosalind said, "we're going to figure out what to tell the Gatekeepers about the exile. Because I am not letting my brother be remembered as a thief, Kess. Not even to save myself. But I'm also not letting Vesh use his secret as a lever." She looked at him across the small fire, and something in her expression shifted — not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground where forgiveness might someday take root. "You held his hand. At the end. Nobody else did. That counts for something. I haven't decided how much."

"That's fair," Kess said. "I'd offer you the second half of the payment, but I spent it on a boat."

"You spent my brother's blood money on a boat?"

"A very nice boat."

"The boat sank."

"*One* of them sank," Kess said, and was rewarded — barely, grudgingly, at the very corner of her mouth — with the first almost-smile in eleven days.

* * *

They slept in shifts, as they always did, and Kess took first watch because he knew he wouldn't sleep anyway. He sat in the boathouse doorway with the compass in his lap and watched the lamps of Rivergate burn across the black water.

His confession sat in his chest like a splinter finally drawn — the wound still there, still raw, but no longer poisoning. She knew. She knew all of it, or nearly all, and she hadn't used the knife, and she had said *we*, twice, without appearing to notice she'd done it.

We. On the run from two clans and a Gatekeeper, with nineteen days left and a mountain between them and the source. We.

He heard her breathing change behind him — the shift from sleep to waking that desert rangers apparently did faster than other people — and then her voice, low, from the dark:

"Kess. The message. When he died. You said he left a message."

His stomach dropped. There it was. The one piece he'd held back — not from cowardice this time, or not only — but because it wasn't his to give, and because he'd been hoping, foolishly, that she'd never ask.

"He did," Kess said.

"Was it for the Gatekeepers?" A pause. The water lapped against the pilings. "Or was it for me?"

Kess looked down at the compass in his lap, its needle trembling faintly, pointing — as it always did now, as it had since the temple — not north, but upstream.

"For you," he said. "It was for you. And I think you'd better hear it before we climb another step toward that mountain."

Behind him, in the dark, Rosalind sat up.
