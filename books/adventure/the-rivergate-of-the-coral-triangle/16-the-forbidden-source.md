## 16. The Forbidden Source

The river told us we were close before anything else did.

For three days it had been shrinking — a broad brown shoulder of water narrowing to a stream, the stream to a creek, the creek to something you could shout across without raising your voice. Now it moved in fits and starts, pooling in dark basins and then spilling over lips of black stone, as if it were gathering its courage for each small journey. Kess walked ahead of me along the bank, and every time the water hesitated he glanced back at me like a man checking whether his companion had noticed the floor of the world tilting.

"You keep doing that," I said.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at me when the river does something strange. As if I'm going to explain it."

"You're the ranger," he said. "You're supposed to know things about terrain."

"I'm a *desert* ranger. My expertise is sand. Sand is honest. It sits where you leave it." I stepped over a seam of wet stone, testing my weight before committing. "This river has been lying to us since the ferry wreck."

"That's not fair. The river saved our lives twice."

"The river drowned Ilo once, too. I'm keeping score."

He didn't laugh at that, and neither did I, really. But the shape of the joke sat between us the way jokes do between people who have been walking together too long — a small warm thing carried against the cold.

We had climbed out of the gorge proper by then, into a country of terraced stone so old the steps had softened into waves. Moss grew in green velvet ropes along the walls. The air was cooler here, and heavier, and it tasted faintly of iron and something sweeter underneath — like blood and honey in the same breath. I had been tasting it for half a day before I understood what it meant: we were breathing the river's beginning. Whatever was ahead of us, it was already in my mouth.

Kess stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into him.

Ahead, the canyon closed. Not narrowed — *closed*, walls meeting in a wall of stone that no flood could shift, smooth as poured glass and dark as deep water. And set into that wall, at the height of a man's chest, was a circle.

I knew the shape before my eyes finished reporting it. Of course I did. I had spent eleven months chasing it across salt flats and down whirlpools and through the drowned temples of a civilization I hadn't known existed. The compass face. The ring of eight points, the hollow center, the proportions exactly right — except this one was three feet across and cut from the same black glass as the wall around it, worn shallow, empty.

A socket.

"It's real," Kess said. His voice came out strange, flattened, like a man reading his own eulogy. "The dying man told me about it. I thought — I don't know what I thought. That he was raving. People rave at the end."

"What did he say about it?"

"He said the river is born behind that wall. He said the compass isn't a compass." Kess turned and looked at me, and in the dim green light his face was younger than I'd ever seen it, and more frightened. "It's a key. Or a lock. He said it depends on who turns it."

* * *

We made camp in the lee of the wall, because there was nowhere else and because neither of us said out loud what both of us were thinking: that whatever happened next deserved a night's sleep before it, or wouldn't get one after.

I built the fire small. Habit. In the desert you build fires the size of your secrets, and mine had been getting smaller all year.

Kess sat with the compass in his hands, turning it over, and I watched him do it and felt the old anger rise and settle, rise and settle, like weather. Eleven months ago I would have taken it off him at knifepoint. Six months ago I would have taken it off him and thrown him in a cell. Two months ago, on the weir, with the clans binding us both to the timbers to let the current judge us, I had cut him free instead of saving myself — and broken my ranger's oath to do it, the oath I'd spoken at seventeen with my brother watching from the front row, proud as a peacock.

My brother. Who had a name here. Who had lived and died under an alias I now knew, and whose compass — whose *key* — had been forged in this gorge generations before any of us were born.

"Do you feel that?" Kess asked.

I listened. Under the fire-crackle and the small busy sounds of the water, there was a rhythm. A pulse. Slow and vast, like a heartbeat heard through a wall — which, I realized, was exactly what it was. The river behind the stone, breathing.

"The whole gorge has a pulse," I said. "That's not ominous at all."

"You joke when you're scared."

"You count things when you're scared. We've established each other's sins."

He smiled, briefly, and went back to turning the compass. And I looked at the socket in the wall, and let myself think the thought I'd been refusing for three days.

If we seated the compass, the source would open. That much everyone agreed on — Vesh, the clans, the carvings in the drowned temple, the dying man's last instructions. What nobody agreed on was what opening it *meant*. Restore the natural rhythm, Ilo had said, surfacing from the deep with river-water still ringing in her ears and truth in her mouth: the compass was forged to lock the source against weaponization, and turned rightly, it would give the river back its own seasons. Turned wrongly — or turned by the wrong hands, with the wrong machinery waiting downstream — it would hand every clan in the Coral Triangle to whoever held the valve.

Vesh wanted it. Vesh had offered me safe passage home for it, in that soft reasonable voice of his, as if he were doing me a kindness. And Vesh knew where we were going. He had agreed to guide me toward the source far too easily, back when I bargained with him, and I had known even then that I was a piece moving across someone else's board.

"We should sleep," I said. "In shifts. If he comes, I want to hear him coming."

"He'll come," Kess said quietly. "Men like Vesh always come. They can't stand for a door to exist that they don't hold the handle of."

* * *

I took first watch, which meant I took no watch at all, because I couldn't stop looking at the wall.

The firelight reached it in a trembling orange oval, and inside that oval the socket gleamed like a pupil. I got up, eventually, and crossed to it, and put my palm flat against the stone beside it. Cold. Colder than stone had any business being in that humid country — cold like the underside of a glacier, cold like the deep places where sunlight has never filed a complaint.

And beneath the cold, the pulse. Stronger here. I counted it without meaning to — ranger's habit, counting things — and made it one beat every seven of my own heartbeats. Slow. Patient. Older than patience.

"Rosalind." Kess, awake, sitting up. "Come away from it."

"I'm just looking."

"People say that right before they touch things."

"I wasn't going to touch it." I was, a little. I took my hand off the wall to prove I wasn't. "Kess. When we seat it tomorrow — how do we know? How do we know which way is restoring and which way is opening? There's no instruction carved anywhere. No warning label. For a civilization that built all this—" I gestured at the terraces, the temples we'd slept in, the whole patient architecture of the gorge, "—they were remarkably bad at leaving notes."

"They left notes," Kess said. "They left them in people. That's why the clans remember. That's why the Lowlands blame outsiders for a theft their great-grandparents committed — because the memory got bent somewhere and nobody alive knows which way is true anymore." He fed a stick to the fire. "Maybe that's the real lock. Maybe the compass only works right in the hands of someone telling the truth."

"That's very poetic for a man who lied to me for eleven months."

"One lie."

"One *crucial* lie. You told me you bought it from a dying stranger. You neglected to mention the dying stranger was my brother, and that he hired you."

"And you told me you were chasing me for justice," he said, not unkindly. "You neglected to mention you'd have chased me into the sea itself, oath or no oath, because it was his."

I sat down across the fire from him. The pulse beat in the wall behind us, seven to one, seven to one.

"No," I said finally. "I wouldn't have stopped at the sea. I'd have chased you into whatever came after the sea." I turned the oath-knife over in my hands — the knife I'd used to cut us free of the weir, the knife that had been a symbol once and was now just a tool that had done one unforgivable and necessary thing. "I keep thinking about what Ilo said. That the river judges. Everyone keeps saying it like it's a threat. But maybe it's simpler than that. Maybe the river doesn't punish anyone. Maybe it just — refuses to flow through lies. Maybe that's all judgment is."

"Then tomorrow," Kess said, "we'd better be the two most honest people in the Coral Triangle."

* * *

Morning came up green through the moss-light, and the river below us had shrunk again in the night, pooling sullenly in its basins, and I understood with a lurch that the dry season was not coming. It was here. The source was already closing — the river rationing itself, holding its breath behind the wall, waiting for someone to decide whether it would be allowed to breathe freely again or be piped and valved and sold.

Forty days had become nineteen somewhere along the way, and I had stopped counting aloud because the numbers had started sounding like a debt collector.

We ate. We packed. Neither of us spoke much, and the silence wasn't awkward anymore; it was the silence of two people tightening the same knot.

Then Kess stood in front of the socket with the compass in both hands, and I saw his arms shaking, and I went and stood beside him.

"Together," I said.

"Together." He laughed, shaky. "Eleven months ago you'd have shot me for standing this close to it."

"Eleven months ago I'd have shot you for standing this close to *me*. Things change."

He raised the compass. The morning light caught the eight-pointed ring, and the old brass — no, not brass, I could see that now, source-stone dressed in metal, forged here, home here — seemed to drink the light and give it back warmer. Behind the wall, the pulse quickened. One beat in five. One in four. The river knew its heart was in the room.

He fitted it to the socket.

It fit. Of course it fit — the way a name fits a grave, the way a truth fits the space a lie has kept warm for years. The compass sank in with a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard, a deep harmonic hum, and the black glass around the socket began to glow from within, veins of blue light spreading outward like frost blooming across a window—

And down the gorge, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of boots on stone. Many boots. Ordered boots.

Kess and I looked at each other.

"Well," I said, drawing the oath-knife that was no longer an oath-knife, "you called it."

Behind us, the wall shuddered, and the river held its breath, and Vesh came around the bend smiling.
