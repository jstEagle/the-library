## 18. The Truth as Payment

The Gatekeepers did not hang her. That was the first surprise of the morning.

Rosalind had spent the night in a cell that smelled of river-moss and old rope, listening to water count the hours against stone, and she had prepared herself for hanging, or drowning, or at minimum something with a great deal of shouting. Instead, at first light, two gatekeepers in grey-green robes had unlocked the door, handed her a cup of something hot and bitter — "cress-tea," one said, as if this were an explanation rather than an insult — and told her to wash her face.

"You'll want to look presentable," the taller one added. "Truth-telling is a formal occasion."

"Is it?" Rosalind said.

"Oh, yes." He beamed at her, the way a man beams when he has found his life's calling and it happens to involve other people's discomfort. "Rivergate doesn't take payment in coin. Coin can be stolen. Truth can't. Well — truth can be *lied about*, but then the river finds out, and honestly you don't want to see what happens then."

"I've seen what your river does," Rosalind said. "I was in it."

"Then you know." He nodded approvingly, as though she'd passed some small preliminary exam.

They walked her up through the city while the gorge still held its blue morning shadow. Rivergate woke around them in layers: ferry bells on the lower tiers, smoke from bread-ovens on the middle ones, and above it all the long silver thread of the river itself, running fast and high toward the source. Word had traveled ahead of them — word always traveled ahead of them; Ilo had probably been paid in gossip by three separate households before dawn — and people came out onto their walkways and balconies to look at her. Not hostile, exactly. Curious. The way you'd look at weather that might ruin your harvest.

Kess was brought alongside her at the second tier, wrists bound, flanked by two Highland Clan escorts who watched him the way cats watch a bird that has already been caught but might yet do something interesting. He looked terrible. He looked like a man who had nearly drowned holding a mechanism steady against a river's temper, which was because he was one. His eyes found hers over the heads of their escorts.

*You don't have to,* he mouthed.

She looked away. Because he was wrong, and because if she let herself agree with him even for a second, she would never say what needed saying.

* * *

The Speaking Stair stood where the gorge narrowed, a wide amphitheater of steps cut into the living rock, half of them dry and half of them under the river's edge, so that the audience could put their feet in the current while they listened. Rosalind thought this was either beautiful or menacing, depending on how much you trusted rivers, and after the last nineteen days she trusted them exactly as far as she could throw one.

Vesh was already there.

He stood at the top of the stair in his grey-green robes, hands folded, wearing the expression of a man who has already won and is merely waiting for the paperwork. Behind him, the socket-stone glistened wetly where the compass had been seated — where *she* and Kess had seated it, hours ago, before Vesh's gatekeepers swarmed the grotto and took them both. The source ran open now, natural and free, and the machinery Vesh had built along the weir sat drowned and useless, its brass arms waving feebly at the current like a drowned beetle's legs.

But Vesh didn't look beaten. That was the thing about him. He looked like a man recalculating.

The crowd settled. Ilo was there, third row, hair still stiff with salt from the deep water, sitting between a Lowland Clan matriarch and a woman Rosalind didn't recognize who was almost certainly somebody important, because everybody kept leaning over to talk to her. Ilo caught Rosalind's eye and gave her a nod that managed to convey, simultaneously, *you can do this*, *I believe in you*, and *if you die I am keeping your boots*. It was the most comforting thing anyone had said to her in weeks.

The tall gatekeeper from the cell — his name, she'd learned, was Ondrel, and he treated everything, including armed standoffs, as a pleasant administrative duty — stepped forward and struck a small bronze bell.

"The river has judged," he announced. "The source runs true. The machine of Vesh is drowned. Two strangers stand before us, and the law of Rivergate is the oldest law we have: no stranger passes without paying, and the only coin we accept is truth." He turned to Rosalind. "Rosalind Okafor, of the desert rangers. You are accused of entering Rivergate in pursuit of theft. You are accused of breaking your ranger's oath to cut a prisoner free. You are accused of aiding the return of a relic whose history is disputed by three clans and one very persistent man standing next to me." A pause. "How do you answer?"

"I'd like to pay," Rosalind said.

A murmur went through the crowd. Somewhere in the third row, Ilo whispered something that made the matriarch snort.

Ondrel raised a hand for quiet. "Payment is made publicly, fully, and without shelter. Whatever you say will be weighed by the river and by everyone here. If you lie, the river will know, and—" he paused delicately, "—we've discussed the river."

"We have," Rosalind said. Her mouth was dry. The cress-tea had been a trap; it tasted like punishment in liquid form. She looked out at the faces on the stair — hundreds of them, clan-marked and unmarked, old and young, all of them waiting — and then, before she could stop herself, at Kess.

He shook his head, very slightly. *Don't.*

She thought about her brother.

She thought about Daniel — no. Not Daniel. Not anymore. She thought about *Dain*, the name he'd used here, the name Kess had spoken three nights ago on the run, and how the world had tilted sideways when she heard it, how she had sat down hard on a wet rock and laughed until it turned into something else entirely. All those years. All those evenings on the porch of the station house, him teaching her stars and knots and how to read a liar's hands, and not once — not *once* — had he said *by the way, little sister, I was born in a city inside a river gorge, and I stole the most dangerous object in it.*

He hadn't lied to hurt her. That was the worst part. He had lied the way you build a levee: carefully, lovingly, to keep the flood off the people downstream. And the flood had come anyway, and now she was standing in it.

"Speak," said Ondrel gently.

"My brother's name was Tomas Okafor," Rosalind said.

Her voice came out steadier than she expected. Good. Steady was a currency too.

"He was a desert ranger for eleven years. He taught me everything I know — how to track, how to navigate, how to tell when a man is lying by watching his thumbs. He died four years ago in a sandstorm on the flats east of the Saltreach, and when we found him, he was holding a compass in a dead grip, and his last written instruction to me was *find it, keep it safe.*" She paused. "For four years I believed my brother had been robbed. I believed the compass was his, and that a thief named Kess had taken it from his memory, and I crossed a desert and half an ocean to take it back."

The crowd was very still. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"That story was comfortable," Rosalind said. "It made my brother a victim and me an avenger, and it required nothing of either of us. Here is the story that is true." She drew a breath that hurt on the way in. "My brother was born in Rivergate. His birth-name was Dain, of the ferry-line, and he was exiled twenty-two years ago for a theft he committed against this city — the theft of the compass itself."

The sound that went through the stair was not a gasp. It was deeper than that, a collective intake, three hundred people breathing at once. In the front row, the Highland Clan escort gripping Kess's arm went white.

"He stole it," Rosalind continued, "because he learned what Vesh intended to do with it. He learned that the compass was forged generations ago not to find directions but to *lock* the source — to keep the river's rhythm in the river's own hands, and out of the hands of any man clever enough to build a machine and greedy enough to use it. My brother took the lock away from the locksmith. He carried it into the desert and he lived the rest of his life as someone else, in a place where no machine could ever reach the water, and he died holding it, and his last thought was apparently *keep it safe* — which, knowing him, meant *keep it away from everyone, including you, especially you, I'm sorry.*"

Someone in the crowd laughed — a short, startled bark — and then caught themselves. Rosalind found she didn't mind. Daniel would have appreciated it. Dain would have too. They were the same person; that was the whole unbearable point.

"And Kess?" Ondrel asked quietly. "The man you hunted?"

"Never stole anything in his life," Rosalind said. "He bought the compass from a dying stranger on the Saltreach road — a stranger who gave him a message to carry and a destination he couldn't refuse. The stranger was my brother. Kess has spent four years being dragged back here by a dead man's errand, chased across a desert by the dead man's sister, captured by two clans, tried by a weir, and nearly drowned twice, and at no point in any of it did he deserve a single day of it." She turned and looked at him directly, wrists bound, bruised, alive. "I came here to punish a thief. There is no thief. There is only a messenger, and a guardian, and a woman who owes him more than she knows how to count."

* * *

They let her rest after that — an hour, no more, on a bench in the shade while the clans argued among themselves in low, urgent voices. Rosalind sat with her elbows on her knees and discovered that her hands were shaking.

It wasn't relief. Relief would come later, or wouldn't. What shook her was simpler and worse: it was done. There was no version of her brother left intact. She had stood in front of three hundred strangers and dismantled him plank by plank — the hero, the mentor, the martyr of the sandstorm — and laid out underneath the truer, stranger man, the exile, the thief, the son of a ferry-line who loved a river he could never go home to. She had done it to save Kess. She had done it to stop Vesh. She had done it because Rivergate accepted no other coin.

And she had done it, if she was honest, because a memory built on a lie isn't a memory at all. It's just a story you tell yourself in the dark. She'd had enough of the dark.

"You look awful," said Ilo, dropping onto the bench beside her. Up close, the salt had dried in Ilo's hair into little white crescents, like the river had tried to give them earrings. "In a heroic way. Very gaunt. Very *truth-teller*. The women in row four were taking notes."

"They were not."

"One of them was sketching you. Flattering, too. Gave you better shoulders." Ilo nudged her. "You did the hard thing."

"I did the expensive thing."

"Same thing, here." Ilo was quiet a moment. Below them, the river ran loud and free through the gorge, and somewhere upstream the drowned machinery clanked once, pathetically, and gave up. "Your brother," Ilo said. "Dain. I don't remember him — I was small — but my mother ferried the exile boats that year. She said there was one young man who stood at the stern the whole way down the gorge and didn't look back once, and when they asked him why, he said *because if I look, I'll swim.*" Ilo shrugged. "Might not have been him. But it sounds like him."

"It's him," Rosalind said. Her throat was tight. "That's exactly him."

* * *

They recalled her to the stair at midday.

Ondrel stood at the top with the other senior gatekeepers, and beside them — uninvited, unescorted, radiating the calm of a chess player three moves from checkmate — stood Vesh.

"The payment has been weighed," Ondrel said. "The river accepts it. The debt of entry owed by Rosalind Okafor is discharged in full." A ripple of sound through the crowd — approval, mostly, with a hard knot of Highland silence near the front. "There remains the matter of judgment. The relic has been returned to its socket by the hands of the strangers themselves, and the source runs free. Against this: an oath broken, a pursuit undertaken in error, and a confession that names a Rivergate son as a thief." He paused. "The assessment will convene tomorrow at first light. Until then—"

"There is another matter," Vesh said.

Every head turned. Ondrel's pleasant administrative smile thinned by a full millimeter, which Rosalind suspected was the equivalent of another man drawing steel.

"The payment is made," Ondrel said. "The truth stands."

"Truths have consequences," Vesh said smoothly, "and consequences have owners. This woman has confessed that her brother stole from Rivergate. Theft against Rivergate carries restitution — a debt upon the bloodline, is that not the law? The Okafor line owes this city. And I—" he spread his hands, magnanimous, terrible, "—am prepared to settle that debt on her behalf. All of it. Every obligation, every penalty, every year of labor owed by a thief's kin. I ask only one thing in return." His eyes found Rosalind's, and there it was, the recalculation finished, the new board laid out: *the compass is drowned in the socket, but the socket answers to whoever holds the law. Give me the legal claim. Name me warden of the source, and your brother's shame dies with me.*

The crowd murmured. Somewhere behind her, Kess started forward and was held back.

Rosalind looked at Vesh for a long moment. Then she looked at the river — high, bright, running the way it had run for ten thousand years before men built machines and would run ten thousand years after the last of them rusted — and she started, very quietly, to laugh.

"No," she said.

"Consider—"

"I said no." She turned to face the whole stair, and her voice carried the way her brother's had carried across a desert flat at midnight, the way it had carried to her across four years and half an ocean. "My brother didn't steal from Rivergate. He stole *for* it — from a man who wanted to own the water. And if this city wants to charge his bloodline a debt, then fine: I'm his bloodline, and I'll pay it. In labor, in years, in whatever your assessment decides tomorrow. But I won't sell his name to buy my way out, and I won't hand the source to the man he died keeping it from." She looked back at Vesh. "You want to be warden of the river? Ask *it*."

For a heartbeat, nobody moved.

Then, from the third row, Ilo began — softly, tunelessly, the way ferrymen sing when the current is kind — to hum the old boat-song of the gorge. The matriarch joined. Then the woman with the notes. Then a dozen voices, then fifty, then the whole stair, the song rising up the rock like water finding its level, and Vesh stood alone at the top of it, unmoved and unmoving, a man doing arithmetic in a language everyone else had stopped speaking.

Ondrel struck the bell.

"The assessment convenes at first light," he said. "All parties will attend. Including—" and here his pleasant gaze slid, with what Rosalind could have sworn was genuine enjoyment, "—the party who is not a party to anything, but keeps talking."

Vesh smiled at the crowd, bowed slightly, and walked down the far side of the stair without looking back.

Rosalind let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the sandstorm, four years ago.

Tomorrow, they would weigh her broken oath against her service and her truth, and decide what she owed and who she belonged to. Tomorrow, Vesh would make his next move, because men like Vesh always had one, and the drowned machinery along the weir was not the only machinery he'd ever built.

But tonight — tonight the river ran free, and Ilo was pulling her toward a bread-oven, and for the first time since she'd followed a thief into a glowing cave, Rosalind Okafor was hungry.

"Come on," Ilo said. "Truth-telling burns calories. Everyone knows that."

And below them, the source sang in its grotto, open and unowned, keeping its own time.
