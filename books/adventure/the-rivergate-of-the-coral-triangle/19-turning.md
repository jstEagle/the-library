## 19. Turning

The water in the grotto was the color of nothing — not blue, not green, just a depth that had never once been asked to explain itself. Kess stood at its edge with his boots half-submerged and his heart doing something he refused to dignify with the word *praying*.

"Tell me again why I'm the one holding it," Rosalind said.

"Because if you hold it and we die, I die knowing I was right about something."

"That's your eulogy? For yourself?"

"It's a good one. Concise." He turned the compass over in his hands. It was warm. It had been warm since they'd entered the grotto, warm like a sleeping animal, and every time he looked down at it he had the distinct feeling of being *looked back at*. "Besides, my brother-in-carrying chose me for this. Dying man's orders. You don't argue with a dying man. It's rude."

"He wasn't your brother."

"No," Kess admitted. "But he asked me like one."

Rosalind didn't answer. She'd gone quiet in the way she went quiet when she was cataloguing — he'd watched her do it on the salt flats, on the ferry, on the weir, always touching her pockets as if counting what she owned: water, rope, knife, debt. Right now she was probably counting something else. A brother who'd lied to her for thirty years. A name she'd grown up saying — *Tomas, Tomas, mind the goats, Tomas* — that turned out to be an exile's mask worn so long it had grown into the face beneath.

Kess knew something about masks worn too long. His own had fit so comfortably he'd almost forgotten there was no face under it at all.

"The socket," she said finally. "Show me."

He waded out onto the stone shelf where the river was born. That was the only honest way to describe it: the water did not flow *into* the grotto or *out* of it. It rose from the rock itself, up through seams and fissures, gathering in a basin carved by hands that had been dead longer than Rivergate had been alive. And set into the basin's rim, half-drowned, was a ring of source-stone with a hollow at its center — a hollow shaped, exactly and unmistakably, like the back of a compass.

Vesh's machinery clung to the grotto walls around them like barnacles on a shipwreck: iron pipes driven into living stone, brass valves, a great wheel-and-chain assembly bolted above the basin, all of it built to throttle the source when the dry season came and meter the river out drop by drop to whoever paid Vesh best. The gatekeepers who'd followed him down stood along the far wall with their pikes, uncertain. Even armed men look uncertain standing inside a cathedral they've just realized is a cathedral.

Kess knelt at the rim. The hollow waited.

"You could still walk away," Rosalind said behind him. "Give it to Vesh. He offered you passage too, didn't he?"

"He offered me passage *out*." Kess laughed, and it came out shakier than he wanted. "There's a difference between leaving a place and being thrown out of it. I've been thrown out of better places than this."

"You've been thrown out of *every* place."

"Yes, but I have standards."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her, which had been his goal, because he'd learned something in nineteen days of running beside Rosalind Okafor: she fought better when her mouth wanted to smile and wouldn't let it. And whatever happened in the next hundred heartbeats, he wanted her fighting.

He set the compass into the hollow.

It fit the way a key fits — not dropped, not forced, but *received*, the stone seeming to close around it like a hand around something long lost. For a moment, nothing. The grotto held its breath. Somewhere above them, Vesh's voice echoed down through the access shaft, shouting orders nobody was moving to follow.

Then the compass began to turn.

Not the needle — the whole instrument, slow and grave, grinding against stone that had waited generations for it, and from the basin below came a sound Kess felt in his teeth before he heard it with his ears. A low note. Then another, harmonizing. The water rising through the rock began to pulse, in and out, in and out, like breath.

"It's opening," Rosalind said. "Kess — it's opening *early*. The dry season isn't for—"

"It doesn't care about the calendar." He gripped the compass with both hands. It was trying to rotate further, seating itself deeper, and the mechanism beneath it — some ancient counterweight system buried in the rock, older than any clan, older than any lie — was waking up stiff after a very long sleep. "Nothing down here has cared about a calendar since your brother's grandfather's grandfather. This is the river remembering what it is."

"And Vesh's machinery?"

Kess looked up at the wheel-and-chain assembly bolted above the basin. Its chains were beginning to groan. Its valves were beginning to rattle. Its iron pipes, driven into seams that were now widening by the heartbeat, were starting to make a sound iron should never make.

"I think," he said, "the river is remembering that too."

* * *

The first pipe let go with a crack like a glacier calving.

Water hit the far wall hard enough to knock two gatekeepers off their feet, and then everything happened at once, the way everything always does, in an order you can only reconstruct afterward while lying somewhere wet and grateful to be alive.

The basin surged. The counterweight system — Kess could feel it now through the compass, a vast slow turning deep in the rock, gears the size of millstones engaging after decades of rust — began to drive the source open, and the source, given permission, came through like a guest who'd been kept waiting too long to be polite. Vesh's valves slammed shut on their own, then burst. The great wheel spun free of its chain, which whipped across the grotto and sheared a support beam clean through. The machinery that had been built to strangle the river discovered, all at once and catastrophically, what the river thought of being strangled.

"Hold it!" Rosalind shouted. She was waist-deep already, hauling herself toward him along the shelf. "Whatever it's doing — don't let go!"

"I wasn't planning to!"

"You let go of everything! That's your whole history!"

"Not today!" He braced his boots against the rim and leaned back, and the compass pulled against him like a live thing, like a foal on a lead, wanting to complete its turning and finding the last quarter-inch jammed with silt and centuries. If it didn't seat fully, the flow would stay half-open — enough to flood the grotto, not enough to establish the rhythm, and everyone downstream would get either a drowning or a drought with nothing sane in between.

The water took him to the chest.

It was cold in a way that wasn't temperature — cold like the inside of the mountain, cold like the bottom of time. It shoved him against the rim and he felt something in his ribs go, a crunch and a white flare, and he made a sound he was not proud of and held on anyway, because Rosalind Okafor had cut herself loose from her own sacred oath with her own sacred knife to save his worthless life on a weir three days ago, and if he let go of a compass now he would never forgive himself, and worse, *she* would never forgive him, and she'd say something dry and devastating about it at his funeral, and he refused to give her the material.

"KESS!" Her hand closed on his collar. She'd reached him. Of course she'd reached him; the woman tracked thieves across salt flats for sport. "Talk to me — is it supposed to shake like this?"

"How would I know? I bought it off a dying man in a tavern!"

"You said it was a quiet transaction over tea!"

"I lie! We've established this! It's practically my defining trait!" The water hit his chin. He spat it out. "Rosalind — if this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it does — tell Ilo the ferry line idea was his. The whole design. I just drew it badly."

"You're going to tell him yourself."

"Promise me you'll—"

"*No.*" Her grip tightened on his collar until he could barely breathe, which under the circumstances felt less like a threat and more like a policy statement. "You don't get a deathbed scene. You're not dying in a tavern and you're not dying here. You're going to sit through the Gatekeepers' assessment with me, and you're going to be insufferable about it, and then you're going to spend a year hauling rope next to me, and *then* you can consider dying, and I'll still talk you out of it."

Something in Kess's chest cracked that had nothing to do with ribs.

He laughed — a real one, torn out of him by the cold and the fear and the sheer ridiculous stubbornness of her — and in laughing he shifted his grip, and the shift was exactly what the compass needed. The last quarter-inch gave way. The instrument seated home with a sound like a door closing gently in a house where someone had finally come back, and the whole grotto *changed*.

The surge stopped fighting. The water didn't calm — it organized. In and out, in and out, the great breathing rhythm establishing itself through the basin and down through the rock, and Kess felt it pass through the compass into his hands and through his hands into the rest of him, a pulse vaster than any heart, patient and enormous and utterly indifferent to Vesh, to clans, to debts, to him. The river judged. That was what the old carvings had meant, the ones Ilo had seen in the deep. Not wrath. Not mercy. Just judgment, the way a current judges a swimmer: it finds what holds and what lets go, and it carries accordingly.

What remained of Vesh's machinery did not hold.

The wheel tore free of its mounts and went downstream like a coin down a drain. The pipes peeled out of the widening seams one by one, each withdrawal sounding obscenely organic, like teeth coming loose. The chain sank. The brass valves tumbled past Kess's shoulder, spinning, and he watched one bounce off a drowned carving of a robed figure — a figure with broad shoulders and a familiar build, holding a small round object aloft — and thought, deliriously, *Tomas, you absolute menace, you could have mentioned any of this while you were alive.*

The water began to recede. Slowly, grudgingly, the way a flood recedes when it has made its point.

Kess became aware, in order: that he was still gripping the compass; that his ribs were screaming; that Rosalind had both fists in his collar and had not let go once; that his feet had left the shelf entirely and he was floating, supported, held in the basin's palm like something the river had decided, provisionally, to keep.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's — hm."

"Don't move."

"I wasn't going to. Moving seems ambitious right now."

She hauled him in by degrees, arm over her shoulders, both of them staggering up the shelf as the water settled into its new rhythm — strong, steady, alive, the sound of it filling the grotto the way an organ fills a nave. On the far wall, the gatekeepers stood with their pikes lowered and their faces lit blue by the source, and not one of them moved to stop anything, because there was suddenly nothing left to stop. Vesh's claim had been washed off the walls of the mountain. All that remained of his grand design was a scattering of iron on the cavern floor, already wearing a skin of pale mineral, already becoming part of the place it had tried to own.

Through the access shaft, faintly, came the sound of Vesh shouting. It had changed key. It had started as command and passed through outrage and arrived, now, at something smaller and more human — the sound of a man watching his life's work become gravel.

Kess found he couldn't muster much pity. He tried. He gave it an honest effort, the way he gave honest effort to most things for roughly four seconds at a time.

"Is it done?" Rosalind asked. "Is it — did we—"

The compass answered before he could. It gave one slow, final rotation beneath his fingers — needle swinging, settling, pointing — and stopped. Kess looked down at it, expecting north, expecting the sea, expecting anything.

The needle pointed upstream. Into the mountain. Home.

"It's done," he said, and his voice came out strange. "It's pointing home."

* * *

They sat on the high shelf afterward, side by side, dripping, while the grotto breathed around them. Rosalind had bound his ribs with a strip of her shirt and her own rope — "you owe me a coil" — and every breath hurt in a way Kess had decided to interpret as proof of life.

Below them, the gatekeepers were picking through the wreckage in silence. One of them fished Vesh's brass valve out of a pool, examined it, and set it carefully aside, the way you'd handle something that used to matter.

"Do you think they'll arrest us?" Kess asked.

"They'll assess us. That's worse."

"Ah. Yes. The famous assessment. Truth as payment." He tipped his head back against the stone. "I've got quite a lot of truth saved up. Years of it. It's going to make a terrible mess."

"Start practicing brevity."

"Brevity. Right. 'My name is Kess, I never stole anything, I carried a dead man's promise up a river and lied about it the entire way because I was afraid.' How's that?"

"Terrible. You left out the part where you nearly drowned holding a compass and complained the whole time."

"I did *not* complain. I delivered commentary. There's a difference."

"There really isn't." But she was smiling — actually smiling, unguarded, the salt-flat grimness gone soft at the edges — and Kess looked at her and felt the strangest thing settle over him, lighter than the water had been and harder to shake off. Belonging somewhere was a dangerous habit. He'd avoided it his whole life the way other men avoided debt. And here he was, sitting in the belly of a hidden civilization with broken ribs and a soaked shirt, thinking: *I could get used to this. That's how it gets you.*

Footsteps on the shelf. A gatekeeper climbed toward them — an older woman, silver-haired, with the unhurried authority of someone who had assessed a thousand strangers and been surprised by perhaps three. She stopped before them and looked at Kess for a long moment, then at the compass seated in its socket, glowing faintly, keeping its rhythm.

"The Gatekeepers will want your account," she said. "Both of you. All of it. Beginning with how a dead exile's compass came to be in a thief's pocket."

Kess opened his mouth.

"And before you begin," she added, with the ghost of something that might, in a gentler organization, have been called humor, "I should tell you that the river already told us most of it. So whatever you say — make it true. It will know."

Rosalind glanced sideways at him. Kess exhaled, winced at his ribs, and began, for possibly the first time in his life, to prepare an honest answer.
