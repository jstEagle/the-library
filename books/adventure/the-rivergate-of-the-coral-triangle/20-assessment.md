## 20. Assessment

They gave her three days to prepare for the Assessment, which Rosalind found both generous and absurd. What was there to prepare? You either told the truth or you didn't. She had spent the last nineteen days doing very little else.

So she spent the three days the way she'd spent most of her life: walking. She walked the ferry landings where Ilo's repaired boat now sat high on its new keel, painted a blue so bright it looked like it had been stolen from the sky. She walked the weir where the river still ran in its old rhythm, swollen and easy, the flood-machinery Vesh had built already being pried apart by Highland and Lowland hands working — grudgingly, with much muttering — side by side. She walked up to the terraced gardens above the gorge, where an old woman pressed a boiled sweet into her palm and said, "You're the one who told the truth at the waterline," as if Rosalind were a landmark rather than a person.

"I'm the one who broke an oath," Rosalind corrected her.

The woman waved this off like a fly. "Everyone breaks oaths. Almost nobody tells the truth after."

Rosalind carried that sentence around for the rest of the day like a warm stone in her pocket.

On the third evening, Kess found her sitting on the edge of the lower landing, feet dangling over the black water, watching the lantern-ferry traffic thread the gorge. He sat down beside her without asking, which was something he'd learned to do somewhere between the drowned temple and the source grotto, and which she had decided not to object to.

"Nervous?" he asked.

"No."

"Liar."

She huffed. "Fine. A little. It's strange. I've stood before review boards before. Desert rangers get assessed every season — conduct, equipment, judgment. I always passed. I always *knew* I'd pass." She turned the oath-knife over in her hands. The blade was still nicked from the weir, from the rope she'd cut, from the vow she'd broken with it. "This one I can't study for."

"You could lie," Kess offered mildly. "Tell them the current cut you loose. Rivers do that. Nobody would check."

"And then what? Walk out of here with their forgiveness built on a story I invented?" She shook her head. "I did enough inventing on my brother's behalf. I'm out of stock."

Kess was quiet a moment. In the lantern light his face looked older than it had on the salt flats, and younger too, somehow — the fear worn off him like weathered paint, showing plainer wood beneath.

"They'll offer you the year," he said. "Vesh's faction will push for exile instead, but they're finished, and everyone knows it. The Gatekeepers respect payment. And you paid more than anyone who ever came through that gate." He paused. "Ilo says you should take the year. Says Rivergate grows on people. Like moss, but friendlier."

"Does Ilo say anything else?"

"He says the ferry line needs a second pilot who knows how to read stars, because Ilo reads rivers and refuses to read anything else on principle." Kess smiled faintly. "And he says — and I quote — 'the desert woman argues less than the desert woman thinks she does.'"

Rosalind looked out at the water. Somewhere upstream, past the rapids and the drowned temples and the grotto where the river was born, the source was breathing in its ancient rhythm, open now, free now, hers to have seen and never to own.

"I haven't decided," she said.

"Yes, you have," said Kess. "You just want someone to hear you say it first."

* * *

The Hall of Assessment was not what she expected. She had imagined stone and severity, gatekeepers in ranks, the whole machinery of judgment arranged to make a stranger feel small. Instead it was a long room above the eastern gate, open on one side entirely to the gorge, so that the river itself seemed to sit in on the proceedings — running green and gold in the late light, making its opinions known in the language of water against rock, which was the only opinion it had ever needed.

Three Gatekeepers sat at a table of driftwood planks. Not thrones. Planks. One of them was knitting.

Rosalind decided immediately that she liked this civilization better than she'd admitted.

Vesh was there, of course. Stripped of office, awaiting his own assessment, standing at the back with two guards and the particular dignity of a man who had bet everything on controlling a river and lost to the river. He met her eyes once. She had braced for hatred and found something worse and better: a tired, rueful acknowledgment, the look of one gambler to another across a table where neither had won. She nodded, slightly. He nodded back. That was all either of them had left to trade.

"You are Rosalind Okafor," said the middle Gatekeeper, a broad woman with river-silt gray hair braided with copper rings. "Stranger. Oath-breaker. Boat-wrecker." A pause. "Truth-teller."

"That last one is recent," Rosalind said. "I'm still getting used to it."

A ripple went through the small audience — Ilo front and center, arms crossed, radiating loyalty; Kess beside her, having insisted on attending despite having no official standing; a scattering of clan folk, Highland and Lowland, who had come to see the outsider judged. The knitting Gatekeeper looked up, amused.

"Humor," she said. "That's noted. It counts for less than you'd hope and more than you'd think."

"We assess three things," the middle Gatekeeper continued. "The broken oath. The service rendered. The testimony given. We weigh them together, because in Rivergate nothing stands alone — not debts, not deeds, not people." She folded her hands. "Begin with the oath. In your own words. We have reports, but reports are the river seen from the bank. We would hear it from the water."

Rosalind stood. Her legs felt steady, which surprised her; inside, she felt like the ferry in the rapids, everything loose rattling.

"My oath was this: that I would hold the law of the desert ranges above any person, any place, any plea. That I would pursue what was stolen until it was returned, and let no bond outrank the charge." She lifted the knife. "At the weir, the Highland Clan bound me and Kess to be judged by the current — a judgment I knew was wrong, because the thing they wanted judged wasn't ours to answer for. So I cut the ropes. With this knife. On my own oath-word." She turned the blade so the nick caught the light. "I knew what it cost when I did it. I'd do it again before the echo finished. That's my testimony on the oath: broken, knowingly, gladly, and I'll carry the weight of it wherever I go."

Silence, except the river.

"The service," said the third Gatekeeper, an old man whose voice sounded like gravel being kind.

"I guided no caravans and healed no sick. What I did was smaller and stranger. I carried a dead man's secret upstream when every part of me wanted to bury it downstream. I stood at the source and chose the river over my own blood's good name." Her voice caught, just slightly, on *blood*, and she let it catch. Hiding it would have been another kind of lie. "I helped turn the compass. I nearly drowned doing it, which I understand is traditional."

Someone in the audience laughed — Ilo, loudly, unapologetically. The tension in the room loosened by a visible degree.

"And the testimony," the middle Gatekeeper said, gentler now. "We heard it at the waterline. All of it. We heard you name your brother a thief — a thief who stole to protect, but a thief, by your own word, your own family's shame laid open before strangers." She leaned forward. "Understand what we must weigh, Rosalind Okafor. Most people, given your choice, would have protected the memory. Let the Lowland Clan carry the blame — they were prepared to, generations deep. Your brother's honor would have sailed home intact, and no one here would ever have known the difference. Why didn't you?"

It was the question she'd been turning over for three days, and she found the answer came easily, the way truth does when you've finally stopped polishing it.

"Because a lie doesn't stay bought," she said. "I know. My family bought one and paid for it for twenty years — my brother paid for it with his life, alone, in the desert, holding a compass he couldn't explain to the only person who loved him. If I'd let the Lowlands take the blame, I'd have just moved the debt. Debts don't die. They wait." She looked at the Lowland elders in the audience, at their guarded faces. "Your clan deserved the truth more than my grief deserved the shelter. That's all. That's the whole arithmetic."

The knitting Gatekeeper set down her needles.

The three conferred quietly, heads together, while the river ran and the audience held its breath and Rosalind discovered that waiting for judgment felt exactly like waiting out a storm in shallow cover: nothing to do but keep breathing and count what couldn't be taken from you. She counted. Water, rope — gone, spent, well spent. An oath — broken, honestly. A brother — lost twice now, once to the desert and once to the truth, and somehow the second loss hurt cleaner. A friend at the front of the crowd pretending not to cry and failing at it magnificently. A thief-turned-carrier beside her who had waited for her on a salt flat because a compass wanted to go home.

Not so little, actually. Her inventory had improved.

"It is the finding of this Assessment," the middle Gatekeeper said at last, rising, "that the oath was broken in service of a deeper keeping — for oaths are vessels, stranger, and yours held the wrong cargo. That the service was true, though it cost what it cost, and we do not forget Ilo of the ferry line, whom the river kept and returned. And that the testimony was worth more than any payment ever levied at our gate." She drew breath. "For entry without payment, for labor owed under assessment, for the wrecking of one ferry vessel — the standard price is exile or five years' servitude."

Rosalind's stomach dropped. Five years. She'd braced for a year; she hadn't —

"But," the Gatekeeper continued, and here something almost like a smile touched her mouth, "the testimony of the waterline outweighs the rest, as truth has outweighed everything in this hall since before the gates were built. The sentence is one year's labor, served where Rivergate has need. Beginning whenever you choose." She spread her hands. "Or refuse, and walk out at spring tide owing nothing, praised and free. Both doors are open. That is the finding."

The hall exhaled. Ilo made a sound like a kettle achieving joy.

And Rosalind stood there with both doors open, and understood — with a clarity that arrived not like lightning but like sunrise, slow and total — that she had decided this weeks ago. Maybe on the salt flats. Maybe in the drowned temple, reading her brother's silhouette carved in stone. Maybe the day she cut the rope.

She had spent her whole life belonging to things that had claimed her: the ranges, the oath, the memory of a brother who turned out to be a stranger wearing a familiar face. Every belonging she'd ever had had been assigned to her, inherited, owed. This — this silt-gray-haired woman with her copper rings, this laughing ferryman, this reformed thief, this loud green river — this was the first door anyone had ever simply *left open* for her.

"I accept," she said. "The year. Starting tomorrow."

"Not today?" asked the knitting Gatekeeper, eyebrows up.

"Today," Rosalind said, "I'm going to sit on the lower landing and watch the boats, and I'm going to eat whatever Ilo is about to insist on feeding me, and I'm going to do absolutely no work whatsoever. Tomorrow I start."

The hall laughed — really laughed, gatekeepers included — and the Assessment was struck from the record with a tap of the gravel-voiced elder's staff, and it was done.

Afterward, in the corridor, Kess fell into step beside her. He was quiet for a long moment, which for Kess was practically a speech.

"A year," he said finally.

"A year."

"I'll be rebuilding the ferry line. Upstream section, mostly. The rapids took out half the moorings." He scratched his jaw, eyes fixed firmly on the middle distance. "Could use someone who knows knots. Desert rangers supposedly know knots."

"Supposedly," Rosalind agreed solemnly.

"And Ilo says —"

"Ilo says a great many things."

"Ilo says the upper mooring crew eats dinner at sundown, all together, every night, whether you come or not." He glanced at her then, quick and sideways, the way he'd looked at her across the whirlpool, across the temple carvings, across the socket at the source. "Whether you come or not."

Rosalind looked out through the corridor's arch at the gorge, at the lamps coming alive along the terraces one by one like the river lighting its own way home, and felt the strangest sensation — the absence of anywhere she needed to be chasing.

"Then I suppose," she said, "someone ought to tell them I'm coming."

Down at the landings, Ilo was already shouting her name.
