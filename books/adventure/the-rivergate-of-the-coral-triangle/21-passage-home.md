## 21. Passage Home

The year ended the way most good things in Rivergate did: with water.

Rosalind felt it before she heard it — a change in the air of the gorge, a green sweetness riding up from the source, the way the whole city seemed to inhale. Spring tide. She had counted the days the way she'd once counted miles across salt flats: patiently, stubbornly, marking them on the limestone wall of her room in Ilo's house with a knife point that had once been an oath and was now just a tool. Three hundred and sixty-five marks. She'd made the last one that morning, then sat on her bed and looked at it for a long time without feeling anything in particular, which surprised her, because she had expected to feel like a prisoner walking free.

Instead she felt like a woman about to leave home.

"Stop that," Ilo said from the doorway. "You'll make me cry, and I have a reputation."

"You have a reputation for crying at ferry launches."

"I have a reputation for *commemorating* ferry launches. There's a difference, and it's dignified." Ilo leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, her pilot's coat already buttoned to the throat despite the warmth. She looked older than when Rosalind had first met her — or maybe Rosalind just knew her better now, knew the laugh lines were earned and the scowl was decorative. "Boat's ready. Tide turns in two hours. The Gatekeepers want you at the water stairs by mid-morning, which means Vesh wants to make a speech, which means we should leave early enough that you can escape before he finishes the second paragraph."

"He's changed," Rosalind said. It wasn't quite a defense. It also wasn't quite true, and they both knew it.

"Vesh has been *humbled*," Ilo said. "Humbled is not changed. Humbled is what proud men are between humiliations." She pushed off the doorframe. "Come on. If we're late, the river won't wait, and neither will your tide."

Rosalind took one last look around the small stone room. A year ago it had been a cell; then it had been a loan; then, somewhere around the two-hundredth day, without ceremony, it had simply become hers. Her ranger's coat hung on its peg, mended at the shoulder where the weir rocks had torn it, patched now with Rivergate cloth — a blue so deep it was nearly black, woven with a pattern of interlocking currents that Ilo's aunt swore meant *safe passage* and Ilo's uncle swore meant *good riddance*. Her pack sat by the door, lighter than it had ever been. Water skin. Rope. A spare knife. The debt she'd carried out of the desert had been paid here, in labor and truth, and what remained was something she didn't have a word for yet.

She shouldered the pack and followed Ilo down into the sound of the river.

* * *

The whole city came to the water stairs, because of course it did. Rosalind had learned that about Rivergate in her first month: nothing happened quietly here, not births, not judgments, not departures. Grief and joy were both public utilities, maintained like the ferries and the weirs.

They lined the stairway and the terraces above it — the Lowland Clan in their reed-woven sashes, the Highland Clan down from the upper gorges in wool the color of wet slate, the Gatekeepers in their grey, the ferrymen and -women in everything else. Children perched on the railings where their mothers pretended not to notice. Someone had strung lanterns along the mooring posts even though it was full morning, which Ilo explained was traditional: *"Lights for the leaving, so the road remembers you."*

Vesh stood at the head of the stairs in his formal grey, hands folded, wearing the expression of a man who had rehearsed dignity until it almost fit. A year ago he had offered her safe passage home in exchange for the compass, and she had refused him to his face in front of witnesses, and the river had done the rest. He had lost his machinery, his claim, and most of his standing in a single afternoon of floodwater. He had spent the year since rebuilding — not his machines, but himself, slowly and visibly, the way you rebuild a washed-out road: badly at first, then better.

"Rosalind Okafor," he said, and his voice carried the way trained voices do. "A year ago you arrived in Rivergate as a stranger with a debt. Today you leave as—" he paused, and Rosalind braced herself for *friend*, which would have been too much, or *asset*, which would have been worse. "—as a witness. You paid us in truth when truth was expensive. Rivergate does not forget such payments."

He extended his hand. She shook it. His grip was dry and careful, and behind his eyes she saw the thing she'd seen there since the flood: a man still doing arithmetic, but gentler sums now.

"The assessment stands," he added, quieter, for her alone. "If you ever wish to return — formally, as one of ours — the gate will know your name."

"I'll keep that in mind," she said, and found, to her mild surprise, that she meant it.

Then the crowd parted, and Kess came down the stairs.

He looked better than he had any right to. A year of honest work had filled him out, burned the fugitive's stoop out of his shoulders, taught his hands calluses that matched hers. He wore ferryman's clothes now — Ilo's old coat, actually, cut down and resewn, which Rosalind suspected was Ilo's idea of a blessing and Kess accepted as such without knowing it. His hair was tied back. He walked like a man going somewhere on purpose instead of a man fleeing something indefinitely.

He stopped three steps above her, which put them nearly eye to eye, which she suspected was deliberate.

"So," he said.

"So," she said.

The crowd, being Rivergate, waited with the patience of people who considered other people's farewells excellent entertainment. Somewhere in the back a child whispered, loudly, *"That's the thief,"* and an older voice shushed it and then added, *"the famous one,"* in a tone of genuine admiration, and Rosalind bit down on a smile.

"A year," Kess said. "You could stay, you know. There's work. The ferry line needs—"

"You're rebuilding the ferry line."

"I'm rebuilding the ferry line. Which is different from needing help with it. Although I do need help with it. The upstream pilings are a nightmare and Ilo refuses to hold anything steady because she says her job is 'aesthetic supervision.'"

"It *is* aesthetic supervision," Ilo called from the boat. "Someone has to make sure the line looks good from the water."

Kess ignored her with the ease of long practice. "I'm just saying. The gate knows your name now. You wouldn't be a stranger. You'd be—"

"Kess." She said it gently, and he stopped. "I spent a year learning who my brother was. I'm not going to spend another one avoiding finding out who I am. Not yet. Maybe not ever, but I have to go find out first."

He nodded slowly. He'd known. Of course he'd known; he'd watched her mark the days on the wall. But he'd hoped anyway, and she loved him a little for hoping, in the way you love someone for a flaw that is really just courage wearing the wrong clothes.

"You could write," he said. "Through the cave. At spring tides. Ilo runs cargo to the coast sometimes—"

"Ilo runs contraband gossip to the coast and calls it cargo."

"Gossip *is* cargo," Ilo said. "It keeps better than fish."

"I'll write," Rosalind said. "Every spring tide. Whether there's news or not. Especially when there isn't."

Kess exhaled, nodded again, and then did the thing she hadn't expected: he held out his hand, palm up, formal as a Gatekeeper's summons. On his palm lay a small coil of cord — ferryman's knot-work, blue and grey, tight-woven, the kind they used to splice the guide-ropes.

"For your pack," he said. "So if you ever tie off to something and wonder whether it'll hold — it will. That's Rivergate splicing. Best in the world. Second best is whatever you desert people manage, which is to say, charming but doomed."

She took it. Their fingers touched over the cord, briefly, and neither of them made it strange, and both of them noticed.

"No promises," she said.

"No promises," he agreed. "Just peace."

"That's more than I chased you onto those salt flats expecting."

He grinned — the old grin, the thieving grin, except it wasn't thieving anymore, it was just glad. "For the record, you never caught me. I let myself be cornered. Historians should know."

"Historians will know exactly what I tell them," she said, stepping past him down toward the boat, "which is that the notorious Kess surrendered to a superior ranger after a heroic pursuit."

"Superior," he repeated, delighted. "I'm putting that on the ferry line's founding plaque."

"*Do not put that on a plaque.*"

"Too late. I've commissioned it."

And then Ilo was hauling her aboard by the wrist, and the crowd was singing — low, many-voiced, the departure song she'd heard a dozen times for strangers and had somehow never expected to hear for herself — and the ropes came off the mooring posts, and the river took them.

* * *

The gorge unspooled around them the way it always did, walls rising sheer and dripping, drowned temples surfacing and sinking with the light. Ilo handled the boat the way she handled everything: competently, talkatively, and with total disregard for anyone's attempt to have a solemn moment.

"Left side, look — new carving. They finished the record wall last month. Your trial's on it. You're very flattering in stone. They gave you cheekbones you don't have."

"I have those cheekbones."

"You have *adjacent* cheekbones. Stone is generous. Also they carved Kess holding the mechanism at the source, and honestly, the likeness is cruel. They gave him a chin."

"He has a chin."

"He has a *suggestion* of a chin. Stone invented the rest. Artistic license." She steered them through a bend with a flick of her wrist, missing a half-submerged piling by a hand's width, entirely on purpose, because Ilo believed passengers should feel alive. "Anyway. He'll manage. He's got the clans eating out of his hand now. Did he tell you? The Highland elders asked him to sit the relic council. Him! A year ago they wanted to feed him to the weir."

"People can change."

"People can be *useful*," Ilo corrected. "Which is better. Change is temporary. Useful is forever." She glanced sideways, and her voice dropped into the register she saved for actual things. "You'll come back, you know. Next spring tide, or the one after. Everyone comes back. The river doesn't let go of people, it just lets them breathe."

Rosalind watched the water slide past, green-gold, patient. "Maybe."

"'Maybe' means yes. In ferryman, 'maybe' means yes with extra steps." Ilo grinned and shoved the tiller over. "Hold on. Rapids."

They ran the shallows where the boat had wrecked a year and a lifetime ago. Rosalind kept her eyes on the water this time, deliberately, letting it look back at her. The river had taken Ilo here and given her back changed — quieter in some rooms, louder in others, carrying carvings in her memory that had rewritten the history of two clans and one dead man. Rosalind had learned to stop apologizing for it around day ninety, when Ilo had finally snapped: *"Stop saying sorry. I chose to pilot that boat. I'd choose it again. Grief that comes from a choice you'd make twice isn't regret, it's just grief. Let it sit."*

Let it sit. She was getting better at that. Desert rangers weren't built for sitting things; they were built for chasing them. But a year among river people taught you that some things you don't pursue. Some things you carry.

The gorge widened. The air changed — salt threading through the green, the first breath of the sea. Ahead, the cave mouth opened in the cliff face like a held note, and beyond it, impossibly far away, a strip of white light: the coast, the tide-flats, the desert, the world she'd come from.

Ilo brought the boat alongside the stone landing inside the cavern and killed the engine, and for a moment they just sat in the rocking quiet, listening to the tide breathe in and out of the rock.

"Well," Ilo said.

"Well," Rosalind said.

"Don't make it weird."

"You're making it weird."

"I'm *commemorating*," Ilo said, and then ruined her own dignity completely by grabbing Rosalind and hugging her hard enough to crack a rib, or possibly two, while muttering into her shoulder, *"every spring tide, you absolute desert lizard, every single one, or I will find you, I have a boat."*

"Every spring tide," Rosalind managed. "I have a cord that holds."

"Damn right you do."

They separated. Ilo wiped her eyes briskly and blamed the spray. Rosalind climbed onto the landing with her pack, turned, and raised one hand — not waving, exactly. Saluting. Ranger's habit, repurposed.

Ilo saluted back, laughed, and turned the boat downstream, and the sound of her engine faded into the sound of the sea, and Rosalind Okafor stood alone in the cave mouth with a year of Rivergate in her pack and the tide coming in to carry her out.

She walked toward the light.

* * *

The salt flats at dusk were exactly as she'd left them: vast, pale, indifferent, beautiful in the way that only places that don't care about you can be. The tide was drawing back from the cave mouth below the bluff, draining off the rocks in silver sheets, and Rosalind stood at the edge of the waterline with her boots in the wet sand and let the wind take her hair apart.

Behind her, the cave breathed its last of the spring tide. Ahead, the desert went on forever, and somewhere out there was a ranger post that had marked her absent, a ledger with her name in it, a life she'd have to pick back up like a dropped rope.

She found she didn't mind. That was the strangest part. She'd walked out of that cave a year ago as a pursuer — a woman defined by what had been taken from her. She walked back into the world as someone defined by what she'd chosen: the oath broken to save a life, the truth told to save a memory, the year given freely and the peace that came after. Her brother's compass was seated in the source where it belonged, keeping the river honest. His secret was out, and it had cost her, and it had cost him his legend, and the river ran cleaner for it. That was the trade. She'd made it with her eyes open.

She was adjusting her pack straps, turning to go, when she saw it.

At the cave mouth, laid on a shelf of rock just above the tideline — placed, not washed; placed, deliberate, in the way of Rivergate gifts — sat a compass.

Not her brother's. Smaller. Cut from a stone she recognized instantly, because she'd spent a season of her life staring at it: source-stone, river-green shot through with veins of silver, warm to the touch even in the sea wind. No needle spun inside it. Instead a single line of light lay fixed beneath the crystal face, steady as a plumb line, pointing —

Not north.

Upstream. Back toward the gorge, toward the source, toward the river and everyone in it.

Tied to it was a scrap of ferry-cord in blue and grey, and knotted into the cord, in the ferryman's shorthand Ilo had taught her one slow evening a hundred years ago, a message four knots long:

*The road remembers you.*

Rosalind stood in the last light with the little compass in her palm, laughing at the sky, blinking hard, blaming the spray.

Then she closed her hand around it, pointed her boots at the horizon, and started walking — out across the flats, toward the desert, toward home, wherever that turned out to be — while behind her the tide crept back in over the cave mouth, and the river, far inland and all the way downstream, kept her name.
