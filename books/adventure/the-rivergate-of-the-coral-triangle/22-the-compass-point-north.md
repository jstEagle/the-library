## 22. The Compass Point North

The salt flats at dawn were the color of old bone, and Rosalind Okafor had forgotten how loud silence could be.

Not true silence — she'd learned in Rivergate that there was no such thing, that even a still gorge hummed with water talking to stone — but the desert kind of quiet, the wide and windless kind that made her own footsteps sound like an intrusion. A year of living inside a river's voice had recalibrated her ears. Out here, the world seemed to be holding its breath.

She stopped at the top of the rise and looked back once.

The cave mouth was exactly as she remembered it: a black seam in the cliff face, half-drowned, unremarkable unless you knew what to look for. The spring tide was just beginning its retreat, water peeling away from the rocks in long silver sheets, and for a moment — only a moment — she saw it again, that faint blue glow deep in the throat of the cave, like a lamp left burning behind the world.

Then the light guttered out, or hid itself, and the cave was just a cave.

"Goodbye," she said aloud, because Ilo had taught her that Rivergate people said things out loud to rivers and doors and dead men, on the theory that everything was listening. "And thank you."

The tide, withdrawing, did not answer. But it left something behind.

* * *

She almost walked past it.

That was the thing about gifts from the sea — they never looked like gifts. They looked like debris, like the ocean's ordinary litter: driftwood, kelp, a tangle of fishing line gone gray with salt. The object sitting on the wet rock shelf below the cave mouth might have been any of those things if not for the shape of it. Round. Deliberate. Sitting too neatly, the way nothing washed ashore ever sits.

Rosalind scrambled down the rocks, boots slipping on weed-slick stone, and crouched over it.

It was a compass.

Not her brother's compass — that one belonged to the river now, seated forever in the socket at the source, turning slowly with the pulse of the water the way it always should have. This was a new one, smaller, cut from a single piece of pale stone shot through with veins of blue-green that caught the morning light and held it, glowing faintly, the way the cave glowed. Source-stone. She knew it on sight now; she'd spent enough nights in the drowned temples running her fingers over carvings made of the same stuff.

Someone had carved it by hand. The casing bore tool marks, fine and patient, and around the rim someone had etched a line of Rivergate script — the old script, the kind carved into temple walls, not the quick trade-tongue scratched onto ferry manifests. She could read maybe a third of it now. A year of Ilo's lessons, shouted over engine noise and rapids, had gotten her that far.

*The river remembers its own,* it said. Or possibly *The river keeps what is given freely.* Her translation was imperfect. It didn't matter. Either way, the meaning landed in her chest like a dropped anchor.

She picked it up. It was warm.

That was the part that undid her, a little. Not the craftsmanship, not the script, not even the impossible logistics of how a gift had traveled up through the whirlpool and out the cave mouth and been set down on this exact shelf at this exact hour of this exact tide. It was the warmth. Stone shouldn't be warm. But this stone had come from the source, from the place where the river was born, and it carried that birth-heat in it still, the way a loaf carries the oven.

The needle — if you could call it a needle; it was a sliver of the same blue-veined stone, suspended in clear water sealed under crystal — swung lazily, then settled.

It did not point north.

Rosalind laughed. It came out rough, half a sob, and she let it, because there was no one for three hundred miles of salt flat to hear a ranger laugh at a rock. The needle pointed back at the cave. Upstream. Inland. Toward the gorge, the ferry line, the terraced gardens, the gatekeepers' hall where she'd stood a year ago and sworn a year of labor without knowing why the words felt less like a sentence than a homecoming.

Toward Kess.

She closed her hand around it before the thought could grow teeth. No promises, they'd agreed. They'd parted on the ferry dock four days ago without promises, which was its own kind of promise, the kind you make by refusing to make one. He'd stayed to rebuild the ferry line — his idea, offered with that shrug he did when he was pretending a decision hadn't cost him anything. *Somebody has to know these waters,* he'd said. *Might as well be somebody who owes them.*

"You could stay," she'd told him.

"I could," he'd said, and smiled, and didn't.

And she had found, walking away, that she wasn't angry about it. That was the strangest part. A year ago she'd have called it unfinished business, and unfinished business was the one thing a ranger couldn't abide. Now she understood that some things aren't meant to be finished. They're meant to be kept — warm, alive, pointing upstream — so that you always know which way home lies, even if you never take the road today.

She turned the compass over in her palm one more time. Beneath the base, hidden against the rock, someone had scratched a second line, small and hurried, in plain trade-tongue:

*Ilo says hi. — K.*

Rosalind laughed again, properly this time, and sat down right there on the wet rock with the tide sighing around her boots, and read it five more times.

* * *

She made camp that night at the edge of the flats, where the salt gave way to scrubland and the first stunted acacias leaned into the wind. Firewood was scarce, but a ranger knows where to look, and within an hour she had a small flame going and a pot of water warming for tea — real tea, desert tea, bitter and smoky, nothing like the river-mint brews of the gorge. She'd missed it. She'd missed a hundred small things: dry air, starlight unfiltered by canyon walls, the particular loneliness of a horizon with nothing standing on it.

But here was the other thing she'd discovered, sitting alone at her fire on her first night home: she was not the same woman who had walked into that cave chasing a thief.

A year ago, crossing these flats, she had cataloged her possessions the way she cataloged everything — water, rope, ammunition, the debt she owed her dead brother's memory. The debt had been the heaviest item in the pack. It had ridden between her shoulder blades every mile, that ledger of things unsaid and undone, the promise she'd made to a boy who used to navigate their father's fields with a tin compass and a head full of stories. *Find it, Roz. Bring it home.* She'd thought bringing it home meant bringing it back to her, back to the desert, back to the grave and the grief.

Instead she'd brought it home to a gorge full of strangers, and testified before the whole assembled city that her brother — gentle, funny, brave Tomas, who'd taught her to read stars — had stolen the compass to keep a river from being turned into a weapon, and had died carrying it toward safety, and had lied to everyone he loved for twenty years to do it.

She had shattered his memory in front of three hundred people to save an innocent man and stop a tyrant, and it had been the hardest thing she'd ever done, and the Gatekeepers had weighed it — her broken oath, her service, her truth — and pronounced it payment in full, plus interest.

*Tomas,* she said to the fire, in the old language of their childhood, the one nobody else on the flats spoke. *I hope wherever you are, you're laughing. You always did say I'd end up somewhere strange.*

The stars came out. She navigated by them out of habit, naming them the way he'd taught her, and found that the exercise no longer hurt. That was new. Grief, she'd learned in Rivergate, is like the river itself — it doesn't disappear when you dam it. It finds another channel, or it floods everything you love. Let it run. Let it carve. What it leaves behind is a valley, and valleys are where things grow.

She took out the compass and set it on a flat stone beside the fire.

In the dark, the source-stone glowed softly, a little hearth-light of its own, and the needle held steady on upstream. She watched it for a long time. Then, because the habit of a lifetime dies hard, she checked it against the stars — north, south, east, west — and confirmed what she already knew: the thing pointed nowhere useful to a ranger. It ignored magnetic north entirely. It pointed at a gorge two hundred miles inland and a few fathoms underwater, at a socket in a grotto where a river began, and it would point there, she suspected, until the stone wore away or the world ended.

Which meant, she realized, grinning at her own slow wit, that she owned the only compass in the desert that could never get her lost.

Because you can't be lost if you always know the way back.

* * *

She reached the ranger station at Redgate Well on the ninth day.

It was worse than she'd left it, which was saying something — sand had drifted against the north wall in a dune that would take a week to shovel, the rain gauge had cracked, and some enterprising lizard had taken up residence in her boot rack. But the solar array still worked, and the water tank still held, and when she keyed the long-range radio and heard the static crackle and then Deputy Amara Okonjo's voice — *Redgate, is that you? We had you listed missing fourteen months, you absolute legend, where in the world—* — Rosalind found herself laughing again, leaning on the desk, telling the first third of a story that would take all night and most of a bottle to tell properly.

She left out the parts that weren't hers to tell. The clans' secrets, the source's location, the mechanics of the gate. Some truths are public property; others are held in trust. She'd learned the difference from a ferryman's daughter who smuggled gossip for a living and never once smuggled harm.

But she told them about the whirlpool. She told them about the trial by current, and cutting the ropes with her oath-knife, and the moment the river rose up to judge them and, having judged, let them go. She told them about Vesh, because downstream communities deserved to know what a man with a reservoir dream looks like. And when Amara asked, gently, about the thief — *the one you went in after* — Rosalind looked at the compass sitting on the desk between them, glowing faintly, needle steady on a point two hundred miles southeast, and said:

"He wasn't a thief. He was a courier. And he delivered."

Amara, being Amara, asked no further questions, which was why Rosalind had put in for a transfer to Redgate specifically, years ago. Desert rangers understand that some cargo isn't meant to be opened.

* * *

Spring came to the flats the way it always did — all at once, overnight, as if the desert had merely been pretending to be dead. The acacias leafed out. The ephemeral pools filled and rang with frogs. And on the first morning of the spring tides, Rosalind saddled up and rode east to the coast, because she had promised herself she would, and because a ranger keeps her appointments even the ones nobody asked her to make.

The cave mouth was open. Of course it was. Spring tide.

She didn't go in. She'd considered it — seriously, on the ride over, turning it over like a stone in her pocket. The whirlpool ran both ways; Ilo had explained the timing of it with charts drawn in charcoal on dried fish skin. She could be in the gorge by tomorrow evening. She could stand on the rebuilt ferry dock, in the smell of engine oil and river mint, and watch Kess pretend not to be glad to see her, and eat Ilo's terrible cooking, and sleep to the sound of water talking to stone.

She sat on the rocks above the cave for a long time and let herself want it, fully, without shame.

Then she took out the compass.

The needle pointed upstream, as it always did, as it always would. But Rosalind Okafor had spent a year learning what the river tried to teach her in forty days: that a compass is not a command. It's a courtesy. It tells you where a thing is. It does not tell you when to go.

"Not yet," she told it. "But soon. I've got a season's work here first — the well needs re-digging, and Amara will drown in paperwork without me, and somebody has to teach the new recruits which stars are liars." She paused. "Tell him — no. Don't tell him anything. He'll know. He always knew."

The tide breathed in and out beneath her. Somewhere beyond the cave mouth, past the whirlpool and the gate and the drowned temples, a river ran free to the sea, unweaponized, unowned, keeping its own counsel. Somewhere along its banks a man with clever hands was rebuilding a ferry line, and a girl with a pilot's license and a smuggler's heart was probably charging him double for gossip.

Rosalind stood, brushed the salt from her trousers, and settled her pack on her shoulders — lighter now, though she carried more than she had a year ago. Water, rope, a ranger's oath newly written in her own hand on her own terms, and a compass that pointed at the one place in the world she'd chosen instead of inherited.

She turned west, toward the desert, toward the long gold afternoon and the horizon with nothing standing on it.

The needle at her hip swung gently, faithfully, upstream.

Rosalind smiled, and walked — not as a pursuer, not as a debtor, not as anyone's daughter or sister or ghost, but as a woman belonging, at last and entirely, to herself — toward the open country, where the light was already changing, and where, on the far edge of the flats, a rider was coming toward her at a gallop, raising dust, waving something that flashed blue-green in the sun.
