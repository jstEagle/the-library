## 1. Debt of Clear Skies

The storm came up out of the southwest like a rumor nobody had bothered to confirm.

Wren Quintana saw it first as a smudge on the horizon at 2:14 in the afternoon, a bruise-colored thumbprint above the flats where the land ran flat and salt-white for forty miles in every direction. By 2:30 she had the anemometer up and humming. By 2:45 she was doing arithmetic in her head, which was the most dangerous kind of arithmetic there was, because it always ended the same way.

"Come on, sweetheart," she murmured to the sky. "Be worth something."

She stood on the tailgate of her chase rig — a twenty-year-old flatbed she'd named *Delilah*, because Delilah was beautiful, expensive to maintain, and would eventually ruin her — and watched the supercell organize itself with the slow, terrible patience of a thing that had all day and no conscience. The tower was going vertical now, punching through the cap like a fist through wet paper. The anvil spread overhead, flattening against the tropopause, throwing shade across half the county.

Most people looked at a cloud like that and saw weather. Wren saw a ledger.

Every rotation, every flare of green in the hail core, every twitch of the inflow tail translated directly into Syndicate bounty credits. A confirmed mesocyclone logged by an independent chaser with certified instrumentation paid four hundred. A tornado on the ground, verified and time-stamped, paid two thousand. A long-track violent one — those were rare, those were lottery tickets — could clear ten thousand in a single afternoon, if you lived, and if your instruments survived, and if the Syndicate's adjudicators didn't decide your data was "anomalous," which was their favorite word for *we don't want to pay you*.

She'd been chasing for the Vane Syndicate for six years. Long enough to know that the bounties were real money and also never enough money, the way a river is real water and still won't fill a well.

The numbers were simple, which was what made them cruel. She owed the Syndicate 61,400 credits. Principal, interest, penalties, and the special fee they'd tacked on for "administrative hardship," which Wren suspected was Latin for *because we can*. A good season — a genuinely great season, top-decile, the kind of season chasers told stories about in bars — might gross eighteen thousand.

Even if she caught every storm. Even if the sky cooperated like a trained animal. Even if Delilah didn't die, and the hail didn't find her windshield, and the adjudicators paid full rate without quibbling.

Three seasons of perfection to break even. And the interest compounded while she slept.

"You're doing the math again," said the radio, in the voice of Bo Tran, her spotter, who was currently ninety miles east in a diner pretending this was a professional arrangement rather than two broke people keeping each other company over long-distance static.

"I'm not doing the math."

"Your voice does a thing when you do the math. Goes flat. Like a tire."

"My voice is fine."

"It's doing the thing right now."

Wren sighed and keyed the mic. "Cell's organizing northeast of the Salt Flats marker. Rotation's tight already. If it holds together past the dryline we might get something worth naming."

"Named like a hurricane, or named like a horse?"

"Named like a debt collector." She paused. "Actually, yes. Exactly like a debt collector."

Bo laughed, and the laugh crackled and faded, and Wren put the radio down and went back to watching the sky do the only honest work in her life.

That was the thing nobody understood about chasing — the thing she couldn't explain to the loan officers or the repo men or her landlord, who had recently begun leaving notes on her door written in increasingly large fonts. It wasn't the bounty. The bounty was survival. But the *reading* — that was hers. Nobody could repossess it. When she looked at a storm, she saw structure the way a musician hears chord changes: the tilt of the updraft telling her whether the cell would split, the color of the debris-free base telling her whether the air below was hungry enough to feed it, the little shiver in the wall cloud that meant *soon*. Other chasers needed radar overlays and algorithmic nowcasts. Wren needed her eyes and eleven years of standing in fields getting rained on.

It was, she reflected, the single marketable skill she possessed, and the market for it was owned by the people she owed money to.

There was a joke in there somewhere. She didn't have the energy to find it.

* * *

By four o'clock the storm had earned its keep.

It dropped its first funnel at 4:07 over nothing in particular — a white rope dancing across empty salt, touching down for ninety seconds, lifting again like it had just been checking. Wren got the instruments on it, got the timestamp, got the coordinates locked. Four hundred credits, maybe, if the adjudicators were feeling generous, which historically they were not.

But the cell wasn't done. It cycled, reorganized, and by five it had gone mean — the kind of mean that made even veteran chasers back off a mile and reconsider their life choices. The wall cloud lowered and began to rotate with visible intent, a slow grinding wheel of gray, and the inflow winds at Wren's position picked up hard enough to rock Delilah on her springs, hot air streaming into the storm from the south like the whole desert being drunk through a straw.

"There it is," Wren whispered.

She drove the intercept line with the windows down and dust coming off the flats in ropes, one hand on the wheel, one hand logging readings, talking aloud to herself because there was no one else in the cab and because saying it out loud made it true faster. "Base's tightening. Inflow's strong, backed from the southeast — good. RFD hasn't wrapped yet. She's going to drop one inside twenty minutes, and it's going to be a big one."

Bo's voice on the radio: "Say again, Quintana? You're breaking up."

"She's going to be a big one!" Wren shouted, uselessly, joyfully, into wind that was now singing through the door frame in a minor key.

At 5:31 the sky obliged her.

The funnel came down fat and slow, a stovepipe wider than a barn, and it stayed down. It crossed the flats in a long unhurried diagonal, chewing a black line through the salt, and Wren paced it at a respectful distance for eleven minutes — eleven full minutes of ground contact, instruments screaming, cameras rolling, her heart doing something between terror and gratitude — before it roped out and dissolved into the evening like a bad idea being quietly abandoned.

A long-track EF3, maybe EF4 once the survey crews got out there. Verified, timestamped, documented six ways from Sunday.

Ten thousand credits. Maybe twelve.

For about four minutes, driving back off the flats with the storm dying purple behind her, Wren let herself feel rich. She did the fantasy version of the math, the fun version: twelve thousand today, another cell tomorrow, a wet spring, an active summer — she could see the ledger shrinking, could almost taste the specific flavor of owing nothing, which she imagined tasted like cold water and sleeping past dawn.

Then she remembered the interest. Then she remembered the penalty schedule. Then she remembered that the Syndicate's adjudicators had flagged two of her last three confirmations as anomalous and paid sixty percent.

The fantasy died the way it always died: not dramatically, but with a small administrative sound, like a stamp coming down on a form.

"Bo," she said on the drive back, "what are you doing this winter?"

"Same as every winter. Not freezing to death, hopefully. Why?"

"No reason."

"That's your lying voice."

"I don't have a lying voice."

"You have several voices and I've catalogued them all. You've got your math voice, your lying voice, your 'the storm is fine' voice which is actually your 'the storm is not fine' voice—"

"Go home, Bo."

"Roger that. Nice catch today, Wren. Genuinely. That was textbook."

She smiled despite everything. "Thanks, Bo."

Textbook. She'd take it. It wasn't much — it was four hundred words of praise instead of four hundred credits — but it was the only currency the sky paid in, and some days it kept her fed.

* * *

The light was going amber-to-rose when she topped the last rise and saw her landing strip.

It wasn't much of a landing strip — a graded stretch of hardpan where she kept Delilah parked under a lean-to and where the mail drone occasionally deigned to descend. Her entire operation fit in a space smaller than a parking lot: the truck, the instrument cases, a fuel bladder, a cot in the shed where she slept during chase season so she wouldn't waste money renting a room in town.

Two vehicles were parked on the strip. Both of them were new. Both of them were the kind of new that is designed to communicate something — matte gray, unmarked, sitting at precise angles like they'd been placed rather than driven.

Between them stood four people in matching coveralls, and beside them sat a flatbed transport with a winch.

Wren pulled up fifty yards out and sat with the engine idling, looking at them, and they looked back at her with the patient neutrality of men who had done this many times and had never once enjoyed it and had stopped expecting to.

Repo crew.

She should have been surprised. She wasn't, really — she'd known the notice was coming; she'd gotten the final warning letter nine days ago and had read it twice and then filed it under the windshield wiper of Delilah, where it flapped at her every morning like a small persistent bird. Sixty-one thousand in arrears triggers asset seizure per section whatever. They'd come for the instruments first — the anemometer array, the lidar unit, the cameras. The certified kit was worth more than the truck. Without it she couldn't log bounties at all, which meant the debt would grow forever, which was, Wren understood with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, exactly the point.

The Syndicate didn't want her debt paid. Paid debts ended relationships. Unpaid debts were engines.

She stepped out of the cab. The evening air came in cool off the salt, smelling of rain that had fallen somewhere else. One of the coveralls — a broad man with a clipboard and the weary face of a foreman — walked toward her, stopping at a polite distance.

"Ms. Quintana."

"That's me."

"Warrant for asset recovery, Vane Syndicate credit division." He held up the clipboard as if she might want to admire it. "We're authorized to take the instrument package and the vehicle. I'll need you to step back from the truck."

Wren looked past him at Delilah. Twenty years old, dented, held together with baling wire and loyalty. Worth maybe three thousand credits on a good day, which meant seizing her covered roughly four percent of what Wren owed, plus the cost of the crew's afternoon, plus the transport fuel. The arithmetic was so bad it circled back around to insulting.

"You know what's funny?" Wren said.

The foreman did not look like a man who wanted to know what was funny. He looked like a man who had heard what was funny many times.

"I caught an EF3 today," she said. "Eleven minutes on the ground. Clean track, clean data. That's ten, twelve thousand credits to your employers, logged under my certification, which is attached to that truck and those instruments." She gestured at the sky, still faintly green-tinged in the west where the storm's corpse was draining away. "And you're here to take the thing that earns it, over a debt that's mostly fees. So next season there's no data, no bounty, and no payments. Explain to me how that's good business."

The foreman considered this. To his credit, he actually considered it, briefly, the way a man considers a weather report for a region he will never visit.

"Not my department," he said.

"Yeah," Wren said. "I figured."

She stepped back from the truck. There was nothing else to do, and dignity, she had learned, was mostly a matter of choosing which losses to stand near. They worked efficiently — the winch, the ramps, the padded crates for the instruments. She watched the lidar unit go into a crate and felt something in her chest go quiet and cold, the way it did when a storm died too fast, before it was finished saying what it had to say.

The tall one, loading the anemometer mast, glanced at her. "Bad luck," he said, not unkindly.

"It's not luck," Wren said. "Luck would at least be random."

He didn't have an answer for that. Neither did she, really. She sat down on the shed step in the last of the light and watched them chain Delilah onto the transport, and she thought about standing still — about what it would mean to just stop, to sit here on this step until the salt took her, no more invoices, no more sky, no more reading clouds like scripture for a church that charged admission. The exhaustion of it lived in her bones somewhere below the tiredness, older than the debt: the sense that her whole life had been built on a moving platform, and that the platform's owners had discovered they could charge rent on motion itself.

Standing still, she thought, was the one luxury the century didn't sell.

The transport pulled out at dusk, taillights shrinking down the hardpan, taking with it eleven years of storms. The other two vehicles followed. The strip stood empty. Somewhere a coyote started up, rehearsing its one song.

Wren sat on the step a while longer, doing nothing, which cost nothing, which was the only free activity remaining. Then headlights appeared on the access road — a single vehicle, unhurried, expensive, coming toward her strip through the blue dark with its high beams off, navigating by instruments alone.

It stopped at the edge of the light. A man got out. He wore a coat too good for the desert and carried himself like someone whose paperwork was always in order, and he smiled at her the way people smile when they are about to offer you a rope and call it a rescue.

"Ms. Quintana," he said. "My name is Ovid Hale. I think I may have a solution to your cash-flow problem."

Wren, who had catalogued her own voices and knew exactly which one was about to come out of her mouth, said nothing at all.
