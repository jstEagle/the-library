## 2. The Contract Nobody Signed

The repo crew was still there in the morning, which told Wren two things: first, that the Vane Syndicate considered her worth the cost of a second day's patience, and second, that they hadn't yet figured out how to get past the fence she'd strung across her landing strip out of chain-link and spite.

She watched them from the window of her trailer over a cup of coffee that had gone bitter as an audit. There were three of them — two men and a woman who looked like she did crosswords in pen — leaning against a gray utility skiff with the Syndicate's stylized V stenciled on its flank. They weren't doing anything menacing. That was the menace of it. They were just *there*, patient as weather, drinking vending-machine coffee from the fuel depot down the road and occasionally glancing at her strip the way you'd glance at a pie cooling on a sill.

Wren had spent eleven years learning to read skies. She could tell you what a cloud intended before it knew itself. She could feel a pressure drop in her back teeth and name the hour the hail would start. But people were harder. People lied with their whole faces.

The repo crew wasn't lying about anything. They were simply waiting for her money to run out or her nerve to, whichever came first, and they were comfortable either way.

Her total debt, as of the last statement — printed on paper heavy enough to stop a bullet, which she suspected was not an accident — stood at 214,000 credits. A perfect storm season, chasing bounties for the Syndicate's insurance arm, might gross her thirty thousand if the sky cooperated and the hail didn't take out her sensor mast again. Do the arithmetic and you got seven seasons of perfect luck, assuming she ate nothing, fixed nothing, and never once paid interest, which was a word the Syndicate used the way a river uses a waterfall.

She rinsed the cup, pulled on her jacket, and went out to meet the morning like it owed her something.

* * *

Ovid Hale arrived at noon, which was the first interesting thing about him, because nobody came to the flats at noon. The light at noon was a punishment. It flattened the world into glare and made even the distant mesas look embarrassed to exist. Anyone with sense waited for evening, when the land turned gold and forgiving.

Hale climbed out of a white groundcar that had been washed recently enough to be insulting, given the dust, and stood for a moment getting his bearings with the expression of a man reading a menu he already found disappointing. He was sixtyish, lean, silver at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned, and he wore a linen jacket in the desert because he was clearly the kind of man who expected the desert to apologize.

"You're Quintana," he said. Not a question. He said it the way you'd confirm a delivery address.

"Depends who's asking and what it costs."

"Ovid Hale." He offered a hand. His grip was dry and brief, a handshake designed to leave no evidence. "I'm told you're the best storm reader in the basin."

"I'm told a lot of things by people who owe me money."

Something flickered across his face that might have been amusement, filed under a more professional expression. "May I?" He gestured at her trailer, and when she didn't move to invite him, he took it as the invitation it wasn't and sat down at her little table anyway, arranging himself carefully so as not to touch anything. She noticed his eyes cataloging the room — the charts, the spare parts, the stack of unopened debt statements she'd been using as a trivet. Cataloguing, not judging. A man collecting inventory.

She stayed standing by the door. Old habit. You kept the exit in view around men like this, though she couldn't have said yet why he was one. Something about the way he'd said *I'm told*, as though the telling had been arranged.

"Coffee?" she asked, mostly to watch him consider whether her coffee constituted a hazard.

"Thank you, no." He produced a folder from inside his jacket — actual paper, heavy stock, the kind of thing that cost real money in a world gone to screens — and laid it on the table between them like a card player setting down an ace he wanted her to see but not touch. "I represent a client with a problem. The problem is missing, along with four government employees and roughly two hundred thousand credits' worth of survey instrumentation."

Wren leaned against the counter and crossed her arms. "Government employees go missing all the time. Usually into a bar."

"Not these." Hale opened the folder and turned it so she could read it upside down, which was either courtesy or a test. "A hydrographic survey team. Four specialists, contracted through the Interior Water Office, dispatched six weeks ago to chart seasonal flows in the high desert east of here. They checked in on schedule for nine days. On the tenth, nothing. No distress call, no beacon, no wreckage. Their last camp was located by air — tents still standing, vehicles intact, food cached. They appear to have walked away from it."

"People do that," Wren said. "Desert does that too. Takes them."

"The desert doesn't take four trained surveyors without leaving so much as a boot." He said it mildly, but his eyes stayed on hers, weighing. "My client wants the team found, or failing that, their instruments recovered. The data logs especially. Whatever happened out there, my client would prefer it stay a private matter. Hence discretion. Hence no questions."

"Hence a fixer instead of a search-and-rescue outfit."

A pause. Then, with the ghost of a smile: "You've met fixers before."

"I've met paperwork before. You're paperwork that walks."

He let that stand, which she noted he was good at — letting silence do work while he studied her. Outside, the repo crew had shifted position slightly, and she caught the woman looking toward the trailer with professional disinterest. Hale followed her glance, understood instantly, and pretended not to, which was its own kind of fluency.

"Here are the terms," he said, and slid a single sheet from the folder.

Wren read it twice, because the first time she assumed she'd misread it.

Two hundred thousand credits. Completion bonus of forty more. Expenses covered. Debt settlement negotiated directly with the Vane Syndicate upon delivery of the instruments and logs — *negotiated directly*, meaning whoever her client was, they had pull deep enough into the Syndicate to reach into her ledger and burn it.

"That clears my debt," she said slowly, "and then buys me a life."

"It clears your debt twice over," Hale agreed. "And I'm given to understand you're a woman who values being owed nothing by anyone."

That landed harder than she wanted it to. She thought of the repo crew outside, patient as vultures with a corporate benefits package. She thought of seven seasons of perfect luck she didn't have. She thought of the specific flavor of exhaustion that comes from a life where standing still is the most expensive thing you can do — where every hour you're not earning is an hour you're sinking, so you chase storms until your hands shake and call it freedom because at least the moving is yours.

"What's the catch?" she asked. "There's always a catch. In my experience the catch is usually wearing a nice jacket."

"No catch. Terms of engagement only." He tapped the sheet. "Recover the instruments and the data logs. Deliver them to my client, unexamined by third parties. If you find survivors, you may render aid — my client is not a monster — but any records they carry come to us first. And you sign a nondisclosure broad enough to cover whatever you see out there, including, if necessary, nothing."

"And if I find bodies?"

"Then you'll be the first. Mark the location, note the condition, come home. Grief is not my department."

It should have sounded cold. It came out sounding tired, which was somehow worse, and Wren found herself briefly curious what Ovid Hale was tired of. Then she reminded herself that curiosity was how fish ended up in boats.

She picked up the sheet again and read the operational details this time, properly, the way she read a forecast — not for what it said but for what it was trying not to say. Staging point: the old rail depot at Kettle Gap. Search grid: forty kilometers of canyon country along an unnamed drainage. Duration: open-ended, weather permitting.

And then the coordinates. The survey's last known position, printed in tidy figures with a datum notation she recognized from a lifetime of maps.

She read them a third time. Her thumb moved to the margin, checking the grid references against the map burned into the back of her mind, the one she'd drawn herself over eleven years of flying those flats.

The numbers put the camp inside the Corvane Military Reserve. Closed since before she was born. Fenced, patrolled, officially empty, and — according to every legal map ever printed — containing exactly nothing worth a hydrographic survey, because officially there was no water out there to survey. The reserve sat in the middle of the driest country in the Empty Quarter. You didn't send a river team to a place with no river.

Unless there was a river. Unless somebody knew about one that wasn't on the maps.

She looked up. Hale was watching her with the particular stillness of a man who has asked a question without asking it and is waiting to see if she's smart enough to hear it.

"Your survey team was charting water," she said, "inside a closed reserve that's supposed to be a rock pile."

"My client's interests are not mine to characterize."

"Whose client? Who signs the checks?"

"A private party." He said it smoothly, but she'd spent eleven years watching fronts build, and she saw the front build behind his eyes — a half-second of recalculation, a decision about how much truth the transaction required. "The party prefers anonymity. What I can tell you is that they have the authority to settle your obligations with the Syndicate in full, today, upon completion. That kind of authority doesn't grow on trees, Ms. Quintana. It grows in very few gardens indeed."

She set the contract down. Walked to the window. The repo crew was still out there, three small silhouettes and a gray skiff, and beyond them the flats ran gold and endless toward the closed reserve, toward whatever was out there drinking four surveyors and keeping quiet about it.

Temptation, she thought, always dressed itself as rescue. That was the trick of it. Nobody ever got ruined by a bad offer that looked like a bad offer. You got ruined by the one that arrived exactly when you needed it, wearing your own wishes like a coat.

Two hundred and forty thousand credits. Her father's face drifted up out of the past, unbidden — the way it always did around money and maps — a man hunched over charts at their kitchen table, tracing drainages with a blunt pencil, telling her, *water goes where it wants, kiddo, everything else is negotiation.* He'd vanished when she was twelve, and the debts had started when she was nineteen, and somewhere in between she'd learned that the difference between a rescue and a purchase was usually just the paperwork.

"If I take this," she said, turning back, "I want the debt settlement in writing. Signed by someone at the Syndicate with a title longer than 'associate.' I don't burn my bridges on a handshake from a man in a clean jacket."

"Reasonable." Hale produced a pen — an actual fountain pen, because of course — and made a note. "I'll have the amended instrument to you by tomorrow evening."

"And I want the right to walk. If I get out there and decide the job's rotten, I come home, no penalty, no follow-up crews." She glanced pointedly out the window at the gray skiff. "No leashes."

For the first time, Hale's composure genuinely flickered — not much, a hairline crack in good porcelain — and Wren understood that she had just asked for the one thing her anonymous client cared about, though she couldn't have said how she knew it.

"Completion bonus forfeited, naturally," he said. "But yes. You may walk."

"Then I'll sleep on it."

"Of course." He rose, buttoned his jacket, and paused at the door with the folder under his arm, studying her with something that in a better man might have been regret. "One thing, before I go. A professional observation."

"Free of charge?"

"Everything's free of charge. That's how you know the price comes later." He smiled thinly. "The survey team's leader was a hydrologist named Emeric Voss. Meticulous man. Before they deployed, he filed a request to expand the survey's scope — off the books, unofficially. He wrote that the drainage they'd been assigned showed flow characteristics, quote, *inconsistent with any mapped hydrology in the region*. He wanted more time. More instruments. He wrote, quote, *the river is not behaving like a river*."

The trailer went very quiet. Somewhere outside, heat ticked in the metal roof.

"He was denied," Hale continued, "and sent anyway, with a reduced team and a shortened schedule. Six weeks ago." He opened the door onto the glare. "Sleep well, Ms. Quintana. I'll return tomorrow at dusk for your answer."

She watched the white car shrink down the track toward the highway, raising a long feather of dust that the wind began patiently taking apart. Then she looked at the contract lying on her table, at the coordinates sitting in their neat little row like a confession waiting to happen, and she did what she always did with a sky she didn't trust.

She started reading it for what it wasn't saying.

The reserve boundary on her own hand-drawn map — the one no government had ever seen — ran right through the coordinates. And she remembered, suddenly, with the strange clarity of a detail surfacing after years underwater, that her father had owned a map once, an old survey folio he'd kept locked in a case, and that the last time she'd seen it, the pages covering exactly that quadrant had been torn out.

Not lost. Torn. Carefully, along the spine.

Wren stood in the doorway of her own life, with a repo crew at her fence and a fortune on her table and a river that wasn't behaving like a river waiting out there in the closed country, and she felt the temptation settle over her shoulders like a harness, warm and well-fitted and buckled by someone else's hands.

Tomorrow at dusk, she'd say yes. She already knew it the way she knew hail was coming — not from the evidence, but from the weight of the air.

She just didn't yet know what she was saying yes *to*.

* * *

That night she dug the old case out from under her bunk, blew off the dust, and found the folio exactly where memory said it would be, swollen with damp decades, smelling of cedar and time.

She turned to the quadrant. The tear was clean. Deliberate. And in the gutter of the binding, pressed flat where the blade had missed it, a fragment remained — a corner of contour lines and, in her father's cramped drafting hand, a single word written in pencil along what must once have been a blue line:

*Rivergate.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, almost a prayer:

*Don't let them count it.*

Wren sat for a long time in the lamplight with the torn map in her lap, listening to the wind work at her fence, and outside, three silhouettes kept their patient watch over a woman who was beginning to suspect that the debt she really owed had nothing to do with money at all.
