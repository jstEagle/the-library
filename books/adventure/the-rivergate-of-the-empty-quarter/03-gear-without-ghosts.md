## 3. Gear Without Ghosts

The river told her where to look before the coordinates did.

Wren had learned a long time ago that water was the most honest thing in the Empty Quarter. It didn't file reports. It didn't take bribes — well, not small ones, anyway. It just went where it went, and if you were patient and quiet and willing to be wrong for hours at a stretch, it would tell you things no map ever would.

So when the wash that every legal chart called *dry* began to murmur under the sandstone like a man humming to himself in another room, she stopped the rig, got out, and listened.

"Okay," she said to the river. "You're not supposed to exist either. Welcome to the club."

She'd been talking to herself more lately. She'd decided this was a feature of solitude rather than a symptom of anything, and she intended to keep deciding that right up until the moment it stopped being true.

The last coordinates from the survey team's beacon put them four kilometers up-canyon from where she stood, in a slot of red rock that narrowed like a throat. The beacon had gone silent eleven days ago. Ovid Hale's contract said *recover instruments, assess site, no questions* — which, Wren had come to understand, was what people said when they meant *questions will be extremely expensive for you.*

She drove as far as the canyon allowed, which wasn't far, then hiked the rest with her pack riding heavy on her shoulders and the river running beside her, clear and cold and completely unbothered by its own illegality.

It took her two hours to reach the camp. She knew it before she saw it, because the birds stopped.

That was the first wrong thing. Out here, ravens owned everything; they perched on your antenna and critiqued your driving. But as she rounded the last bend, the canyon went quiet in a way that felt less like silence and more like held breath. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

Then she saw the tents.

Four of them, dome-style, expedition grade, pitched in a neat row on the gravel bar with the geometric precision of people who expected to be graded. Guy lines taut. Rain flies staked. One tent's door flap stood open, curled back like a hand offering something.

Nobody was home.

"Hello!" Wren called, because procedure said call out, and because shouting into an empty canyon was still better than the alternative of not knowing. "Government survey! Anyone here? I'm friendly, mostly solvent, and I have coffee!"

The canyon considered this and declined to answer.

She stood at the edge of the camp for a full minute, doing the thing she did with storms — reading the whole system before touching any part of it. Supercells and abandoned camps weren't so different, she'd found. Both had structure. Both had a way of telling you what they intended if you shut up long enough to hear it.

This one was telling her: *nothing here has been disturbed, and that is the disturbing part.*

* * *

She made herself walk the perimeter first. Habit. You read the edges of a storm cell before you drove into the core, and you read the edges of a camp before you walked into the middle of whatever had happened there.

The edges told her a story with a hole in the middle of it.

There was a fire ring, ash cold and old, maybe two weeks. A cache of food crates stacked against a boulder, lashed down against weather with real competence — freeze-dried everything, medical kit sealed, spare batteries in waterproof bags. A solar array angled at the morning sun on a frame somebody had welded themselves, with little care tags on each panel written in different handwriting: *MINE. TOUCH AND DIE — R.* That one made her smile despite everything. Every field crew had an R.

And there were tracks. Plenty of them, pressed into the clayey silt near the riverbank — boot prints, dozens, overlapping, the ordinary chaos of four people living in one spot for weeks. Tracks going to the fire ring. Tracks going to the latrine trench, poor souls. Tracks going down to the water where somebody had set up a sampling station, tripod legs sunk deep, bottles lined up in a rack with dates and depths labeled in grease pencil.

But every track ended at the waterline.

Not faded. Not blown away. *Ended.* The last clear prints — three sets, maybe four, hard to tell with the overlap — walked down the gravel toward the river and simply stopped, as though the ground had gotten tired of recording them. No prints coming back up. No prints leaving camp in any other direction. No trail up the canyon walls, no sign of a scramble over the rocks, nothing heading downstream or up.

Wren crouched at the waterline for a long time, the cold coming off the river and soaking through her knee into the bone.

People didn't vanish from camps. People left camps — on foot, by vehicle, by helicopter, carried out on stretchers or in handcuffs or under their own power while complaining about the food. Leaving traces was what people *did*. It was practically the definition. And four trained government surveyors did not all stroll into a river together without leaving so much as a dropped glove.

Unless they hadn't left at all.

"Okay," she said quietly. "New theory. Bad theory. Let's not be married to it."

She stood up, wiped her hands on her thighs, and went to do the part of the job she was actually being paid for.

* * *

The instruments were the strangest part, and the instruments were the reason she'd come.

They were still logging.

The flow meter mounted in the river ran on its own solar trickle-charge, and its little screen glowed green in the shade of its housing, numbers ticking past with the placid confidence of a machine that had never heard of anything going wrong. Wren pulled the handheld reader from her pack, jacked in, and started downloading.

While it worked, she checked the others. A weather station on a mast above the camp — still transmitting locally, still logging temp, wind, humidity, barometric pressure, dutiful as a monk. A second flow gauge half a kilometer downstream, which she'd pass on her way out. A sediment sampler cycling on schedule, filling its bottles with the patience of a saint.

Eleven days since the beacon died. Eleven days, and every instrument on this gravel bar had kept working like the team had just stepped away to argue about whose turn it was to cook.

That was the hush she'd felt at the treeline — no. There was no treeline, this was high desert, but the feeling was the same. It wasn't abandonment. Abandoned places decayed out loud: flapping tarps, rattling guy lines, the slow percussion of neglect. This place was *maintained*. Taut lines. Lashed crates. Panels clean. Somebody had left this camp the way you leave a house you're coming back to.

Or the way you leave a house you're never coming back to but can't quite admit it.

The download finished. Wren sat on a crate with the reader in her lap and scrolled the flow data, expecting boredom.

She did not get boredom.

The river's discharge record ran back five months, neat daily averages, and for most of it the line wandered the way rivers do — up after rain, down in dry spells, noisy but honest. Then, ninety-one days ago, the line bent. Not gradually. It stepped down, like stairs, week over week, a steady systematic decline that no drought draws and no season explains. Upstream flow dropping while the downstream gauge — she cross-checked from memory of Hale's briefing data — stayed fat and happy.

Something between the two gauges was drinking the river. Something with a straw the size of a canal.

"Huh," Wren said, which was what she said instead of the longer sentence forming underneath it.

She looked up at the canyon. It climbed away northward in red terraces, narrowing, darkening, the river threading up its floor like a vein. Somewhere up there was whatever the survey team had gotten close enough to see. Somewhere up there was also, presumably, an explanation for four missing people that wasn't the one crawling up the back of her neck.

Her reader chimed. An attached log — the team leader's field journal, synced to the station automatically. She hadn't known these units did that. She opened it, scanned the first entry, and felt the hair on her arms stand up in the dry desert air, which should not have been meteorologically possible and yet.

*Day 1. Full crew. Morale good. Quintana would hate it here — no clouds worth the name.*

She blinked. Read the name again. Coincidence. Common enough surname. She scrolled on.

*Day 9. Flow anomaly confirmed. Down 14% at the upper gauge with zero local inputs. Requesting guidance from the district office. Response: continue monitoring.*

*Day 22. Found the diversion intake. Photos attached. Requesting immediate inspection. Response received: STAND DOWN. Repeat: STAND DOWN. Return to base.*

*Day 23. We are not standing down. If we're right, someone is stealing a river, and the district office already knows whose signature is on it. R says we're being naive. D says we're being brave. I say we're being accurate, and accuracy is the only kind of brave that matters.*

Wren sat very still.

The wind moved through the canyon, and the tents breathed, and somewhere upstream the river said the thing it had been trying to say since she'd first heard it humming under the sandstone.

She thought about Ovid Hale's smooth voice. *Recover the instruments. No questions.* She thought about the pay figure, generous enough to clear her debt twice over, and how generosity from people like the Vane Syndicate was never a gift — it was a price tag worn backward.

She looked down at the reader in her lap, at the journal still open, at the words *whose signature is on it*, and understood with a cold, settling clarity that she was not here to rescue anyone.

She was here to tidy up.

* * *

She gave herself until sundown to be scared, and then she gave herself a job, because fear without a task list was just expensive weather.

The task list looked like this: download everything. Photograph the camp, the tracks, the cache, the sample rack — document it like the crime scene it probably was. Cache a copy of the data where a person who wasn't her could find it, because paper burns and drives drown and Wren Quintana had a documented history of ending up in ditches. Then decide, with the benefit of daylight and caffeine, whether to go up that canyon or run for the border of this whole bad idea.

She worked through the afternoon, and the work steadied her the way it always did. There was a comfort in procedure, in the small dignities of doing a thing properly even when nobody was watching. Especially then. She taped a copy of the data inside the food cache, under the crate marked with R's handwriting, figuring anyone possessive enough to label their solar panels would forgive her.

At the sample rack she paused. The last bottle was dated eleven days ago. Filled. Capped. Logged.

Somebody had taken a water sample the same day the team disappeared, and then walked down to the river, and then —

She made herself stop finishing that sentence.

Instead she filled her own bottle from the rack's spares, ate half a ration bar she did not technically steal because the owners were in no position to invoice her, and watched the light go amber on the canyon walls. The hush settled back in around her, that held-breath quiet, and she realized she'd stopped finding it eerie and started finding it sad. This camp wasn't haunted. Haunted meant angry. This place was *waiting* — pitched tents and taut lines and instruments logging faithfully to an audience of nobody, keeping the team's seat warm.

A place that had decided not to be found, and was doing a terrible job of it, because it wanted to be found too badly.

"Yeah," Wren told it softly. "I know the type."

Full dark came down. She rigged her bivy behind the boulder, out of the wind, and lay looking up at a spill of stars so thick it looked like the sky had been overdrawn. Her debt sat in the back of her mind where it always sat, a passenger who never slept and never paid for gas. Twice over, Hale had said. Enough to make it all go away.

And all she had to do was carry four instruments and a stack of data back down to a man who'd sent four people up this canyon after the same data, from whom exactly none of them had returned.

The river talked to itself in the dark, patient, unhurried, going where it went.

Wren closed her eyes. Above her, on the mast, the weather station ticked through its rounds, measuring a sky with nothing in it — and at 3:17 in the morning, it woke her with a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat.

The low, rising moan of wind in the upper canyon. Pressure falling fast. She was outside the bivy in thirty seconds, staring north into blackness, where far up the gorge a smudge of cloud was building over the heights like smoke from a fire that hadn't started yet.

A storm was coming down off the source. And storms, Wren knew better than anyone alive, always blew in from the direction of the thing that made them.

She started packing.
