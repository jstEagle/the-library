## 4. What the Instruments Recorded

The dead men had left the kettle on.

Not literally — the propane was long cold, the kettle furred with dust inside like something that had died of old age in its sleep — but it sat on the burner as if someone had stepped out to check a gauge and meant to come back for tea. That was the thing about the camp that kept snagging in Wren's chest. Not the absence of bodies. The presence of habits. Boots by the tent flaps, toes out. A deck of cards mid-game on an upturned crate, someone holding a hand they'd never get to play. A mug with *WORLD'S OKAYEST HYDROLOGIST* printed on it in cheerful block letters, half full of rainwater and mosquito larvae.

She'd spent two days telling herself she was here for the instruments. That was the contract: recover the equipment, log its condition, no questions asked. Ovid Hale's money, Hale's rules. So she told herself she wasn't cataloguing the mugs or reading the margin notes in the paperback western splayed open on a cot, or noticing that somebody had drawn a smiley face in the dust on the solar panel before the dust stopped being disturbed.

She told herself a lot of things. It was practically her only hobby besides debt.

The instruments themselves were the reason she'd come up the wash in the first place, and they were still working. That was the eerie part. Four data stations, daisy-chained on a relay, all of them humming along on their little solar diets, logging faithfully to a team that no longer existed. She found the field laptop in the lead instrument case, charged at sixty percent because somebody had rigged a spare panel to trickle-feed it, and settled onto the dead man's cot with the western for a pillow.

"Okay, Dr. Vasquez," she said aloud, because the name was stenciled on the case and because talking to empty camps was better than letting them talk to you first. "Let's see what got you killed."

*If* it got you killed, whispered the sensible voice in her head, the one that sounded suspiciously like her loan officer. People vanish in the Empty Quarter all the time. Heat, flash flood, bad luck, worse decisions. Nobody needs a conspiracy when dehydration will do the work for free.

But nobody leaves boots behind. And nobody walks into a river without leaving tracks coming out.

She opened the hydrology suite.

* * *

**SURVEY LOG — R. VASQUEZ, LEAD HYDROLOGIST**
**DAY 1 — STATION ALPHA ESTABLISHED**

*Full crew present: myself, Okonkwo, Lindqvist, Delacroix. Agency says this reach of the Quintana Wash is ephemeral — flows maybe three weeks a year off snowmelt, then bone dry. I have been telling the agency for six years that the wash is not ephemeral, it is shy. There is a difference. An ephemeral stream has no water. This one has plenty; it just doesn't want us to know where it goes.*

*Delacroix says I anthropomorphize rivers. Delacroix also talks to his drill cores, so we'll call it even.*

*Flow at Alpha: 4.2 cubic meters per second. Steady. Cold — 9 degrees Celsius, which is glacier water, which means somewhere upstream there is ice the maps don't admit to. Good. Ice doesn't lie. It just melts.*

Wren huffed a laugh through her nose. *It just doesn't want us to know where it goes.* She knew the type. Every discipline had them — the surveyors who fell in love with their subject until the subject started seeming to have opinions. Her father had been one. She remembered him crouched over kitchen-table maps with a red pen, muttering at aquifers like they were teenagers sneaking out windows.

She hadn't thought about the red pen in years. She made herself keep reading.

* * *

The data came first, because data always does — patient, indifferent, waiting for a human to catch up. She pulled the flow records from Station Alpha and graphed them, and for eleven days the line did what lines are supposed to do: wandered gently around 4.2, breathing with the sun, boring as arithmetic.

Then Day 12.

The line dropped. Not gradually, the way a river fades in drought. It dropped like a stair step. 4.2 to 3.1 overnight. Held three days. Then 3.1 to 2.0 in a single afternoon.

She checked the sensor diagnostics, expecting drift, expecting fouling, expecting the mundane explanations that make up ninety percent of field anomalies. The sensor was clean. The barometric compensation was clean. She pulled the temperature record from the same hour and felt the back of her neck prickle: the water was getting *colder* as the flow dropped. Colder and faster-moving, which meant whatever was arriving at Station Alpha was coming from higher, deeper, closer to the source.

Something upstream was eating the river, and what it left behind ran colder than the river itself.

"Okay," she said quietly, to the mug with the larvae in it. "Okay."

* * *

**SURVEY LOG — DAY 14**

*Station Bravo is in and already arguing with me. Flow at Bravo should be Alpha plus tributary inflow. Instead it's Alpha minus forty percent, and the deficit grows daily. I've run the numbers four ways. There is no geology in the literature that accounts for this. Karst could swallow water, but karst gives it back downstream, and downstream is getting nothing.*

*Lindqvist thinks it's a survey error. Lindqvist has thought every anomaly is a survey error since Ubehebe, and I remind him that Ubehebe was one error and this is forty consecutive ones.*

*Delacroix, who hears everything and repeats most of it, suggested today that we're being watched. I told him to save the paranoia for the report, where it can be properly footnoted. But I'll confess to the log what I won't put in the report: last night I walked down to the wash at 0300 to check a seismometer, and the river sounded wrong. Rivers gurgle. This one gulped. Like something drinking.*

*I am a scientist. I do not believe rivers drink.*

*And yet.*

Wren sat with that one for a while. Outside, the wind moved through the camp, rattling the guy-lines, turning the cards on the crate face-down one by one like a dealer clearing the table. She got up, stretched until her spine crackled, and ate half a ration bar she found in a cache tin labeled, in different handwriting, *OKONKWO'S — TOUCH AND DIE.* She touched it anyway. She figured Okonkwo would understand. Dead men don't need calories, and living women chasing other people's ghosts burn through them fast.

The next log entry was dated nine days later. Whatever happened in between, Vasquez hadn't written it down — or had written it somewhere else, somewhere she intended to find.

* * *

**SURVEY LOG — DAY 23**

*We found the gate.*

*I'm calling it a gate because "structure" sounds bureaucratic and "dam" sounds insane, and both would be true. Six kilometers above Bravo, in a slot canyon the satellite imagery classifies as a dry wash, there is a concrete diversion works the size of a cathedral, fed by a road that appears on no map, powered by a generator array that hums loud enough to feel in your teeth. Headgates. Pumps. A canal heading east toward the Vane holdings, carrying more water than the entire legal allocation of the basin district.*

*Someone is stealing a river. Has been stealing it, by the calcification on the intake screens, for close to a decade.*

*Okonkwo wants to turn around and report. Lindqvist wants to turn around and pretend he never saw it. Delacroix wants to photograph everything, including each other standing in front of it like tourists, which I confess I allowed, because if we die out here I want our last photograph to be ridiculous.*

*I voted to go upstream. Above the diversion there must be a source, a spring complex, something that explains the volume. If we can document the true flow, we can document the theft, and the theft is the story. Whoever built this didn't file permits. Whoever built this buried a government reserve designation under a military closure order so thick that even our own agency couldn't follow us in.*

*Which raises the question I keep circling: how did a closed military reserve come to contain private infrastructure?*

*I have a hypothesis about that too. I don't like it. The land grant on file for this parcel — the original one, pre-closure — carries two signatures. One belongs to a Vane Syndicate director.*

*The other belongs to a hydrologist named E. Quintana.*

Wren stopped reading.

The wind kept moving through the camp. The cards stayed face-down. Somewhere upslope, a rock warmed loose by the sun ticked against another rock, small and indifferent, and Wren sat very still on a dead man's cot with her father's name glowing off a screen she'd been paid to carry out of the desert and destroy.

*E. Quintana.* Emilio Quintana. Who taught her to read clouds before she could read chapter books. Who vanished when she was twelve — walked out one morning with a field bag and a promise to be back for her birthday, and became instead a hole shaped like a man that everyone around her learned to stop looking at. The state said lost. The insurance said presumed. The Vane Syndicate, which held his consulting contracts, said nothing at all, and sent flowers with a card signed in typeset.

She had spent twenty years building a life designed never to stand still long enough for that hole to catch up with her. Storms were good for that. You couldn't grieve at eighty knots; there wasn't time. Debt was good for it too. Debt gave grief a practical shape, a number, a monthly payment, something you could attack with labor instead of feeling.

And now here was the number again, except it wasn't dollars. It was a signature. His hand, on the founding paper of the machine that was drinking a river dry — and, she realized with a slow cold certainty, very possibly the same machine that had drunk *him*, because men who steal rivers do not tolerate the one hydrologist alive who knows exactly how much they're taking.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them the way you notice weather — distantly, professionally, as data.

"Okay, Dad," she said. Her voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal and a relief at once. "Let's see how far you got."

* * *

**SURVEY LOG — DAY 24**

*Day 24, and I am writing this by headlamp with my hands not entirely steady, so forgive the prose.*

*A vehicle came up the unmapped road tonight. No lights. Two men. They did not approach the camp. They parked at the diversion works, walked the length of the canal, and stood looking at our station lights for a long time. Then they got back in the vehicle and drove down-canyon.*

*They were counting us. I am sure of it the way I am sure of the flow deficit: four lights, four people, logged.*

*Tomorrow we split. Okonkwo and Lindqvist take the data drive and the duplicate logs down-canyon, fast, to the agency. Delacroix and I go upstream to the source to finish the documentation. If anything happens to us, the evidence survives. If anything happens to them — well. Two targets are harder to hit than four, and a moving target harder still.*

*Delacroix asked me tonight why I don't just sell what we know. He wasn't joking. He said the Syndicate would pay more than the agency ever would, and he's right, and I told him the truth, which is that some things aren't for sale because they aren't ours. The river isn't ours. The truth about the river isn't ours. We're just the ones currently holding it, and holding a thing isn't the same as owning it.*

*He looked at me like I'd grown a second head. Then he laughed and said, "You sound like somebody's daughter."*

*I never told him my father worked these basins. I never told anyone. Funny what the desert knows anyway.*

Wren read that line three times. *You sound like somebody's daughter.*

The kettle sat on the cold burner. The smiley face faded on the solar panel. And Wren Quintana, who had crossed the flats at night running ahead of a wall cloud because it paid better than fear, sat in a murdered man's camp and understood, finally and completely, why there were no tracks leaving it.

There were no tracks leaving because the team had split. Because two of them had run for help down-canyon and been caught between here and anywhere. Because the other two had gone *up* — up toward the source, up toward the answer, up into country where the only way out was through a canyon the mountain was going to close.

They hadn't vanished. They'd been *intercepted*. And the fourth one — Delacroix, the one who talked to drill cores and photographed everything like a tourist —

Might still be alive.

She sat forward so fast the laptop nearly went off her knees. The final entries. She scrolled, heart hammering, past Day 25, Day 26 — short entries now, terse, the prose of a man conserving battery and breath —

* * *

**SURVEY LOG — DAY 27 — FINAL ENTRY**

*Delacroix has the photographs memorized. All of them. I tested him tonight — called out frame numbers at random and he recited content, timestamps, GPS coordinates, without a single error. Paper burns. Hard drives drown. But a mind like his, walking out of here on its own two feet, is a record no fire can touch.*

*If you are reading this, stranger — and someone will read it, because instruments outlive their owners, it's the one decent thing about them — the evidence is threefold. The flow data on this machine. The diversion works at the coordinates below. And Delacroix, upstream, in the high gorge, doing what he does best: hiding, and remembering.*

*Don't sell it. Whoever sent you will offer. They offered us, near the end, and the offer was generous, and generosity is how they buy the last copy of a thing before they burn the rest.*

*The river owes nobody. But somebody owes the river. Go collect.*

*R.V.*

Below the signature, in smaller text, the coordinates. High. Very high. Up in the granite throat of the source canyon, where the maps gave up and the glacier began.

Wren closed the laptop with hands that had stopped shaking, which was its own kind of frightening. Dread was familiar; she could ride dread for years, had built a whole career on driving straight at it. But this was something else, something with a direction and a speed, dread annealed into purpose like sand into sandstone.

The team hadn't been lost. They'd been getting close to an answer, and the answer had teeth, and one man might still be up there holding it all in his head, alone, for however many months, waiting for a rescue that had been contracted out to the very people who'd ordered the problem solved.

Hale hadn't hired her to recover instruments. Hale had hired her to be the eraser.

Well. She'd been hired to do a lot of things in her life. Attendance at any of them remained optional.

She was repacking the laptop when she heard it — or rather felt it, a tremor in the ground before any sound, the particular bass note of an engine being driven carefully by someone who didn't want to be heard until it didn't matter. Down-canyon. Coming up the wash. One vehicle, moving slow.

Wren killed her headlamp, slid the laptop into her pack, and looked around the camp one last time — the boots, the cards, the kettle waiting for tea that was never coming.

"Hold on, Delacroix," she murmured. "I'm coming. And I'm bringing company's worst day with me."

The engine note grew louder.
