## 5. The Vane Syndicate Sends Regards

The engine sound woke her the way a hand on the shoulder wakes you from a dream you were enjoying — not violently, but with the immediate certainty that something was about to be taken away.

Wren had been asleep in the survey team's largest tent, curled in a sleeping bag that smelled of strangers and cedar, and in the last warm margins of that sleep she'd been dreaming about her mother's kitchen, which was absurd, because her mother's kitchen had been a galley no wider than a coffin and always smelled of diesel and burnt coffee. But dreams are generous editors. In the dream there had been bread.

Then: engines. Not one. Two, maybe three, running low and slow in the way engines run when their pilots don't want to be heard until it doesn't matter anymore.

She came out of the bag without unzipping it, which is a skill storm chasers develop and later regret when they try to demonstrate it at parties. Outside, the desert morning lay flat and gold as a coin, and the river ran its cold green thread past the camp, indifferent to everything. The instruments were still logging. Nobody had come to turn them off.

Three skiffs were coming up the dry wash from the south, riding low, hulls scarred white with alkali dust. Syndicate markings — the stylized V that was supposed to look like a bird in flight but had always reminded Wren of a checkmark, as if the whole organization were grading the world and finding it, so far, acceptable.

Her first thought, shamefully, was about the contract. *Recovery of instruments and records, condition upon delivery immaterial.* She'd read that clause three times on Hale's screen and signed anyway, because twice her debt cleared is a number that makes a person agree to almost anything, and she'd told herself that "immaterial" meant flexible. Now, watching those skiffs, she understood that "immaterial" meant exactly what it said. It meant the condition didn't matter. It meant nobody expected the instruments to arrive intact.

Her second thought was practical: the data drive in her jacket pocket, the one she'd pulled from the flow logger at dawn, weighed less than a sandwich and was currently the most dangerous object in the Empty Quarter.

She did what she always did when the world turned hostile, which was make coffee. There was a camp stove, and the survey team had cached a tin of grounds that had gone stale but not yet gone bitter, and there is a specific dignity in pouring hot water while armed men approach your position. Her mother had done the same thing, once, when repo men came for the boat. Stood on the deck stirring a cup like the whole affair was beneath comment. Wren had been twelve. She remembered thinking her mother was the bravest person alive. She remembered, two weeks later, learning otherwise.

*Don't think about him,* she told herself, and poured the water anyway, and thought about him.

The skiffs beached in a hiss of gravel. Four people got out of the lead one, but only one walked toward the tents. A woman, compact, sun-darkened, wearing the kind of plain utility gear that costs more than Wren's entire rig. She moved like someone who had learned to walk on boats before she learned to walk on land — a slight roll to her gait, weight always ready to shift.

"Quintana," the woman said. Not a question.

"You have me at a disadvantage," Wren said. "I don't know your name, and I've only got the one cup."

"Marisol Vega." The woman stopped six feet out, which Wren noted was exactly far enough to be conversational and exactly close enough to grab her if she ran. Professionals did that math automatically. "You're drinking the dead men's coffee."

"I was hoping they wouldn't mind."

"They mind." Marisol looked past her at the camp — the pitched tents, the neat cache, the instruments still ticking over in their weather housings like patient clocks. Something crossed her face that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite guilt, but lived in the same neighborhood. "They were good people. Okafor kept a garden in a hydroponic tray. On a survey skiff. Tomatoes. Insane little tomatoes."

Wren set down the cup. "You knew them."

"Everyone in this business knows everyone." Marisol's voice went flat, administrative. "That's not why I'm here."

"No," Wren said. "I'm starting to suspect why you're here."

They sat on the gravel by the river, because Marisol insisted on it — away from the tents, away from the other skiff crews, who had spread out through the camp with the unhurried efficiency of people doing a job they'd done many times. Wren watched one of them open an instrument case, photograph the serial number, and then, without any ceremony at all, begin unscrewing the housing.

"You're stripping the site," Wren said.

"We're remediating the site." Marisol pronounced the word carefully, the way you handle something fragile that you also despise. "There's a difference. Legally, there isn't, but morally there is, and I try to keep my morals in a separate account so the lawyers can't touch them."

"That's very tidy."

"I'm a tidy person." She pulled a strip of dried meat from her pocket, offered one to Wren, and when Wren declined, ate hers slowly, looking at the river. "How much do you owe them?"

The question landed like a dropped wrench. "That's not—"

"Two hundred thousand? Two-fifty? I ask because it matters to the arithmetic, and the arithmetic matters to whether you live through this week." Marisol said it without drama, the way you'd report the wind speed. "Hale told you the salvage fee clears your debt. Did he tell you how he arrived at that number?"

"He told me it was generous."

"It was precise." Marisol turned to look at her then, and her eyes were brown and level and very tired. "The Syndicate doesn't pay generous numbers, Quintana. They pay exact ones. Your debt plus operating costs plus a margin calibrated to keep you hungry. They priced you the way you'd price fuel. And here's the part I'd want somebody to tell me, if our positions were reversed: the fee was never going to clear anything. Because the deliverable doesn't exist."

"The instruments exist. Right there. Your crew is currently eating one."

"The deliverable," Marisol repeated, "is the survey team's records, delivered intact, with the team available to authenticate them. That's what makes the data worth anything. Data without witnesses is just noise. And the team—" she gestured, a small economical motion that took in the tents, the cache, the river, the four graves' worth of absence, "—isn't available."

The coffee turned to iron in Wren's stomach. "So the contract was—"

"A leash." Marisol stood, brushed off her trousers, and looked down at her with something that in a better world might have been called kindness. "Not a lifeline. You were hired to walk into a closed reserve, find what's left of a murdered government survey team, and then be standing next to it when we arrived. Do you understand? You're not a salvager. You're a witness. And the Syndicate has never once paid a witness what they promised, because witnesses who talk aren't witnesses anymore. They're problems."

The wind came down the wash and pushed at them, smelling of dust and cold water. Somewhere behind them, a drill whined as the crew pulled the last logger from its mount.

Wren found, to her surprise, that she could still speak, and that what came out was almost steady. "And you're telling me this because?"

"Because I flew the supply runs for that survey team for eight months." Marisol's voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture across the administrative flatness, and then sealed itself again. "Because I carried Okafor's tomato seeds. Because last month I got orders to find whoever Hale sent upriver and make sure they didn't come back down it, and I've decided I'd rather lose my license than my sleep. And because—" she paused, and here she almost smiled, a small crooked thing, "—because you made coffee. Repo crews hit my mother's place when I was fourteen. She made coffee too. I've never forgotten which people do that."

"What happened to your mother?"

"She talked her way out of it. Some people can." Marisol shrugged. "I couldn't, so I joined the thing that scared me. It seemed safer than being afraid of it forever. That was a mistake, for the record. Write that down somewhere."

*

They walked the camp together after that, and Marisol narrated the remediation with the weary fluency of a woman giving a tour of her own disappointment.

"The loggers go back to Vane. The paper logs get burned — there's a burn protocol, laminated, I swear to God, they laminated it. The tents stay; too much trouble to pack out, and nobody comes here. Officially this wash has been dry for sixty years." She nudged a food crate with her boot. "Okafor argued with the quartermaster about these rations. Said the protein bars tasted like drywall. He was right. They taste like drywall."

"Why does the Syndicate care?" Wren asked. "It's a survey. Surveys die all the time out here. Budgets get cut, teams quit—"

"This wasn't a budget cut." Marisol stopped walking. Ahead of them, the river slid past, quick and cold and utterly committed to its own direction. "Do you know where this water goes, Quintana? Really goes? You've been reading the instruments. Tell me what they say."

Wren hesitated. Telling Marisol what she knew felt like handing over the last card in her hand. But Marisol had just told her she was scheduled to die, and there is a certain equality established by that kind of disclosure.

"The flow drops upstream," Wren said. "Not gradually. Stepped. Like gates closing. Something between here and the source is taking hundreds of cubic feet per second out of this river, and doing it in increments, on a schedule. Natural systems don't run on schedules."

"No," Marisol agreed. "Infrastructure does." She crouched, touched the water, let it run through her fingers. "Ten years ago the Syndicate built a headworks in the source canyon. Dams, pumps, canals — enough steel to dam a river twice this size, hauled in piece by piece under a military exemption nobody ever audited. Every drop they take gets sold downstream. To the towns along the legal aqueduct. Towns whose wells have been failing for a decade because the river that used to feed them is being drunk by the people selling them bottled relief." She wiped her hand on her thigh. "The survey team found the intake structures. Okafor radioed it in — I took the call myself. Forty-eight hours later the order came down to pull their support. Just leave them out here. And then the remediation order, and then—" she glanced at Wren, "—you."

"Me."

"Somebody had to confirm the records existed before we burned them. Hale doesn't trust satellite imagery. And somebody deniable, somebody with debts, somebody who'd sign a contract with 'immaterial' in it—" Marisol spread her hands. "You fit. I'm sorry. You fit perfectly. That's not a compliment."

Wren sat down on the gravel. Not dramatically; her legs simply filed the request and were granted. The river made its small constant argument against silence. Somewhere in her chest, a feeling assembled itself that she recognized from bad landings and worse news — that sick, clarifying drop, the one that arrives when you realize the turbulence wasn't weather. It was design.

"I signed a contract," she said, mostly to herself. "I flew nine hundred miles. I slept in a dead man's tent."

"You did," Marisol said. "And now you know what the contract was for. So the only question left is the one I can't answer for you." She sat down too, cross-legged, abruptly informal, as if the formal part of the meeting had concluded and what remained was just two tired women by a stolen river. "The drive you pulled from the logger this morning. Yes, I saw you do it, I was watching through binoculars for an hour, you held your elbow like my grandmother holds her sewing. That drive is the only copy of the flow data that will exist by tonight. Hale thinks you'll trade it for the debt forgiveness. The Syndicate thinks you'll run, and that we'll catch you, and that solves both problems. I think—" she tilted her head, "—I think you're your mother's daughter, and I don't know what that means yet."

"My mother sold her boat to pay a debt," Wren said. "And then the debt grew anyway. Debts do that. They're the only living things the Syndicate raises well."

Marisol laughed — a short, surprised bark, quickly strangled. "God. Okay. You're funny. That's going to make this harder, actually. I was prepared for desperate. Desperate is easy to manage. Funny is a problem."

"What happens now? Honestly."

"Honestly?" Marisol looked upriver, toward the canyon country, where the maps gave up and the washes lied. "Now my crew finishes stripping this camp, and I file a report saying the contractor was never here, and you have approximately"—she checked the sun's angle with a practiced squint—"four hours before the report gets read and somebody senior decides to verify it personally. Four hours to decide what you are. A loose end, or a loose cannon. The Syndicate has procedures for both, but only one of them involves running fast up a river that officially doesn't exist."

"And if I give you the drive right now? Walk away clean?"

"Then you're a loose end with good manners, and I'd still have to report the drive, and the drive would burn with everything else, and four dead people would have died for nothing, and the towns downstream would keep buying back their own water by the liter until the money or the towns ran out." Marisol stood and offered her a hand up. Her grip was dry and strong. "I know what I'd do. But I've already lost my sleep. You haven't lost yours yet. Take the four hours."

Wren took the hand. Stood. Felt the drive in her jacket pocket, light as a sandwich, heavy as a verdict.

"One more thing," Marisol said, turning back toward the skiffs. "The survey team's route logs. Before the comms went dark, Okafor mentioned a settlement upriver. Off every ledger. People who've been watching that canyon mouth for generations." She paused, and the crooked smile returned, briefer this time. "If I were a loose cannon, that's where I'd start. Rivers run downhill, Quintana. But the truth, in my experience, tends to run the other way."

She walked back to her skiff without looking again, and Wren stood alone on the gravel with four hours, a stolen river, and the growing, unwelcome, exhilarating understanding that for the first time in years, she owed nothing to anyone who deserved it.

Upstream, the canyon waited, dark and cool, like a door someone had left open on purpose.

She went to get her boots.
