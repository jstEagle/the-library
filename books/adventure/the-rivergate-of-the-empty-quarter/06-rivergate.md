## 6. Rivergate

The map said dry wash. The map had been saying dry wash for eleven miles, in the confident gray stipple that cartographers use for places they've decided not to think about, and the ground beneath Wren Quintana's boots was saying something else entirely.

It was saying *water*.

She heard it before she saw it — a low, muscular sound, like a crowd murmuring behind a wall — and then the terrain dropped away between two shoulders of red sandstone and there it was: a river running full and cold through a canyon mouth that every legal document in three counties insisted was a dead arroyo. It came out of the dark of the gorge the way a rumor comes out of a locked room, all at once and undeniable.

Wren stood on the lip of the wash with her hands on her knees and let the cold air coming off the water climb up her sleeves. After four days of walking the high desert — after the salt flats and the burned juniper country and the long white silence where her radio could pick up nothing but its own static — the sound of moving water went through her like a struck bell.

"Well," she said to no one. "Aren't you a lie."

The maps called it the Serrano Wash. Dry since the last Pleistocene, according to the water rights filings she'd memorized off Hale's contract tablet. And yet here it ran, deep enough to turn a paddle wheel, cold enough to ache, flowing *upstream* as far as she could see into the canyon's throat — which was impossible, and which she was beginning to understand was the whole point of this place. Everything about the Empty Quarter ran backward if you looked at it long enough. Money flowed uphill. Water flowed off the books. People walked into rivers and left no tracks.

She crouched at the edge and dipped two fingers in. The cold bit down to the bone in seconds. Snowmelt, then, or something close to it — which meant a source high enough and wet enough to feed a river this size, in a desert that officially had neither. Her father would have loved this. That thought arrived uninvited, the way thoughts about her father always did, and she stood up quickly and shook her hand dry like she was shaking off the man himself.

Behind her, far back across the flats, a glint caught the sun.

Marisol.

Wren had been aware of the skiff the way you're aware of a tooth that might go bad — a low background pressure, easy to ignore until you bit down wrong. The Syndicate pilot had found her at the survey camp two days ago, landed on the flat rock like she owned the sky, and delivered the worst news of the trip with the flat calm of a woman reading a grocery list: the salvage contract was a leash, not a lifeline. Recover the instruments, yes. But the records were supposed to burn, and so, eventually, was she. Wren had been hired to be the loose end, and loose ends get trimmed.

And then Marisol had done the second-worst thing possible. She'd let Wren walk away.

"Run upstream," she'd said, leaning against her skiff's rail with her arms crossed, watching Wren with those unreadable brown eyes. "See what you see. I'll be along."

Which meant either Marisol was playing a game Wren couldn't name yet, or she was herding her — driving her up the rivergate toward whatever the Syndicate wanted found, or buried, or both. Wren had spent the whole walk deciding it didn't matter. Either way, the answer was upstream. Either way, she was going.

She shrugged her pack higher and started down toward the water.

* * *

The canyon took her in like a throat.

The walls rose sheer on either side, banded red and ochre, streaked with a mineral weeping that looked like old candle wax. The river ran down the middle of it, fast and glassy over stone, and the only path was a shelf of rock above the north bank, wide enough for one person, worn smooth by — she stopped, crouched, touched it — by feet. Many feet, over many years. A trail that appeared on no map, kept by no agency, but kept nonetheless.

Someone used this road. Regularly. Recently.

That should have frightened her. Instead it steadied her. Uncanny places made her skin crawl, but evidence of people — of ordinary traffic, of somebody's grandmother having walked this exact stone with a basket on her hip — that was different. That was a world with rules. Somebody lived upriver. Somebody knew about the river that wasn't supposed to exist, and had known for a long time, and had told exactly nobody who mattered.

Smart somebody, Wren thought. Look what happened to the last team that wrote things down.

She walked. The canyon narrowed, widened, narrowed again, each turn revealing another impossible mile of green — cottonwoods in the cracks, wild rye in the seeps, a flash of blue-winged something bursting off the water ahead of her and scolding her for existing. The air smelled wet and mineral and alive. Four days of dust fell off her in layers she hadn't known she was wearing.

By late afternoon the light had gone amber and long, and the skiff's engine finally reached her — a thin mechanical whine threading up the gorge from behind, bouncing off the walls until it seemed to come from everywhere. Wren didn't even break stride. She just moved to the outside of the trail, put a wall of stone at her back, and waited, one hand resting casually on the pry bar strapped across her pack. Not a weapon, technically. A tool. Everything was a tool if your paperwork was creative enough.

The skiff rounded the bend below her, riding the current, and Marisol brought it alongside the shelf with a boatman's ease that Wren noted and filed away. The pilot looked up at her. Neither of them spoke for a moment. Somewhere upstream, water fell over rocks with a sound like quiet applause.

"You're making good time," Marisol said.

"You're making better." Wren nodded at the skiff. "Motorized."

"I'm also older than I look and I've been eating river fish since before you were born." Marisol cut the engine. The sudden silence was enormous. "There's a camp two miles up. Ferry landing. You'll want to stop there before dark."

"A ferry landing. On a dry wash."

"On the rivergate, yes." Something flickered at the corner of Marisol's mouth — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. "You keep saying the words on the map like they're going to argue with the water. They won't. The water wins. It always wins. That's actually the entire problem I flew out here to have with you."

"Then have it."

Marisol studied her for a long moment. Up close, in the slanting light, Wren could see the tiredness under the pilot's composure — real tiredness, the kind that lives in the bones, the kind Wren recognized because she wore it herself most mornings. A person doesn't get that tired flying errands. A person gets that tired carrying something heavy for a very long way.

"The Syndicate thinks you're collecting instruments," Marisol said quietly. "They think in about six days you'll come walking back out of this canyon with a data slate full of numbers, and Hale will take it from you, and it will end up in an incinerator in Vane City, and your debt will be marked paid, and everyone will live unhappily ever after. That's the story they're telling themselves. It's a comfortable story. Stories about incinerators usually are."

"And what story are you telling yourself?"

Marisol didn't answer right away. She turned and looked up the gorge instead, at the darkening water, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into something almost private. "I'm telling myself the one where a stubborn storm chaser with bad credit finds something in these hills that can't be incinerated. Because paper burns fine, Ms. Quintana. Paper is the easiest thing in the world to burn. It's what's *underneath* the paper that keeps standing back up."

"That's very poetic for a repo pilot."

"I contain multitudes. Most of them are tired." Marisol restarted the engine, and the skiff swung out into the current. "Two miles. Callow's Ferry. Ask for Ida. And Wren—" She paused, the skiff drifting, the current already pulling it downstream the way currents do, patient and indifferent. "Whatever you find up there, decide fast whose side of the ledger you want to be on. The mountain's going to make that decision for you soon enough. Mountains are terrible accountants. They close the books whenever they feel like it."

Then she was gone around the bend, a wake and a whine and a fading smell of two-stroke exhaust, and Wren stood alone on the shelf with the river talking to itself below her and the first stars coming out over the canyon rim.

Callow's Ferry. She turned the name over. It sounded like a place from a story her mother might have told — except her mother had never told stories, only invoices. Everything Wren had ever been given came with a payment schedule attached.

She started walking. Two miles. In the dark. Toward a town that wasn't on any ledger, guided by a trail worn smooth by strangers' feet, followed — distantly, patiently, like a debt — by the sound of a motor she couldn't decide whether to trust.

* * *

She smelled woodsmoke first.

It came down the canyon on the evening breeze, mixed with frying onions and wet rope and the particular green smell of irrigated ground, and Wren's stomach announced its opinion loudly enough to startle a night bird off the water. When the canyon opened out — a sudden amphitheater where the walls stepped back and the stars poured in — she stopped dead on the trail.

Lamps. Dozens of them, strung along a waterfront that shouldn't exist: a curved shingle beach, moored flat-bottomed boats riding at anchor, a cluster of buildings built into the canyon walls themselves, half-carved, half-carpentered, chimneys glowing. A town. A whole living town, tucked inside a canyon that every map in the world called empty, lit up like a held breath.

And on the water, silhouetted against the lamps, figures were moving — loading boats, checking lines, working with the quick economy of people who had somewhere to be before morning.

Wren stood in the shadows at the canyon mouth for a long moment, doing the thing she was actually good at. Not chasing storms, exactly. Reading them. Feeling the pressure drop, feeling the charge build, knowing which way the cell would shear before the radar confirmed it. She read this the same way: the loaded boats, the lamplit urgency, the town holding its breath. Something was coming. Something with a deadline.

Or, said a colder voice in her head, something was leaving. Something that needed a gullible outsider walking in the front door right about now.

She thought about Marisol's face. About the tiredness in it. About the words *what's underneath the paper keeps standing back up.*

Her pack was heavy on her shoulders. Inside it, wrapped in her spare shirt, was the survey team's data slate, still logging, still recording the impossible arithmetic of a river being drunk by something upstream. Four days ago that slate had been worth exactly her debt and twice over. Right now, standing in the dark looking at a town full of strangers, it felt heavier than money. It felt like a question she hadn't finished asking.

She was still standing there, weighing it, when the fuel problem solved itself.

It happened the way these things happen — not dramatically, but with a small, stupid, irreversible click. She'd been carrying a reserve canister of stove fuel strapped to the outside of her pack, enough to boil water and melt snow on the walk back out. Enough, in other words, to leave. To turn around tomorrow, follow the dry wash-that-wasn't back down eleven miles to the flats, and let the whole canyon stay a rumor.

While she stood hesitating, the strap's buckle — corroded, salt-flaked, tired of its job the way everything in the Empty Quarter eventually got tired — gave way. The canister dropped, bounced once off the rock shelf, rolled, and went into the river with a splash that sounded, in the enormous quiet, like a door closing.

Wren watched the current take it. It bobbed once, twice, spun in an eddy, and then the river folded it under and carried it away downstream, back toward the world of ledgers and incinerators, and it did not come back up.

"Huh," she said.

She stood there a moment longer, listening to her own heartbeat and the water and the distant lamplit industry of Callow's Ferry. She could dive for it. The water was maybe eight feet deep in the pool below, black and freezing. She could probably get it back. Probably.

She adjusted her pack straps instead, and found — to her genuine surprise — that her shoulders sat easier without it. Lighter. Committed. There was a particular clarity that came from burning your return fuel, whether you lit the match yourself or the universe knocked it out of your hand, and Wren had chased enough storms to know that clarity was the rarest weather of all. You couldn't forecast it. You could only recognize it when it arrived, and then you had to move, because it never stayed.

"Okay," she told the river. "Forward, then. But I want it on the record that I was going to choose that anyway."

The river, being a river, declined to comment.

She came down off the shelf onto the shingle beach, boots crunching, hands visible, walking the way you walk into a strange camp at night — open, slow, loud enough to be heard. The nearest figure, a broad-shouldered woman coiling rope by a moored boat, straightened up and looked at her without alarm, which somehow alarmed Wren more than a rifle would have.

"Evening," Wren called. "I'm looking for Ida."

The woman looked her over — the dust, the pack, the pry bar, the four days of desert written on her face — and then jerked her chin toward the largest of the lamplit buildings, where more figures were gathering, and where the smell of onions had concentrated into something that made Wren's knees briefly unreliable.

"She's expecting you," the woman said. "Been expecting you since the ice started talking."

"The ice started—"

"Two nights back." The woman shouldered her coil of rope and started up the beach, apparently assuming Wren would follow, which, having no return fuel and no better ideas, she did. "Whole canyon heard it. Old-timers say when the glacier starts calving early, it means the source canyon's fixing to open. Nine days, maybe ten, and then she shuts for a year." The woman glanced back, and in the lamplight her expression was hard to read — part curiosity, part pity, part something older. "Ida says you came up the rivergate asking questions the last survey team asked. So I guess we'll find out together whether you're luckier than they were."

Ahead, the doors of the big building stood open, spilling warmth and light onto the sand, and somewhere inside it a voice — low, dry, unhurried, a voice accustomed to being obeyed without ever raising itself — was saying:

"Sit her down and feed her first. Questions keep. Ice doesn't."

Wren Quintana stood at the threshold of the town that wasn't on any ledger, with a stolen river at her back, a shadowing pilot somewhere behind her, nine days on the mountain's clock, and nothing left to burn but the truth.

She went in.
