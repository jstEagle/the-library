## 7. The Town That Isn't on the Ledger

The river told me where to go, and I had learned by then to trust it more than my instruments.

Two days past the rivergate, the gorge opened its hand. The walls fell back from the water, and the canyon floor widened into a bench of pale gravel and greasewood, and the current — which had been running cold and quick as a secret — slowed and spread out like something exhaling. My skiff rode easier. Even the light changed, going soft and gold through a haze of cottonwood pollen that drifted on the water like the river was shedding its own small weather.

I nearly missed the town because the town did not want to be seen.

That's not quite true either. It wanted to be seen by exactly one kind of person, and it had gotten very good at sorting. What I saw first was a fish weir — a lattice of willow and stone set across a side channel, angled so the current did half the work. Then a drying rack with chub strung up like silver coins. Then a smokehouse built into the canyon wall itself, so old it looked less constructed than grown, the way a barnacle grows.

Then, around a bend of tamarisk, a woman standing hip-deep in the shallows with a net pole across her shoulders, watching me come.

She didn't wave. She didn't run. She just stood there, gray braid down her back, sun hat pushed off her forehead, taking my measure the way you'd read a cloud front — not alarmed, exactly, but not welcoming either. Behind her, half-hidden in the cottonwoods, I counted rooftops. Eleven. Twelve. A footbridge. Laundry on a line. A child's red jacket hung over a rail to dry beside what looked suspiciously like a still.

Every legal map in the Empty Quarter said this stretch was dry wash. Every census said nobody lived here. And here were twelve rooftops and a smokehouse older than the state water code, sitting fat and green on a river that officially did not exist.

"Looking for someone?" the woman called.

"Looking for the survey team," I called back. "Government crew. Four people. They went missing upstream."

The net pole came off her shoulders slowly. "And who sent you?"

"Their employer," I said, which was technically true, and she looked at me the way you look at a fish that's swallowed your hook but hasn't decided to fight yet.

"Employer," she repeated. "That's a word for it."

* * *

They took my skiff at the landing — a gravel bar below the footbridge where three other boats sat hull-up on blocks, patched and repatched, their paint gone the color of driftwood. An old man named Ferro inspected my engine with the tenderness of a priest handling relics, pronounced it "Syndicate stock, third-rate, but honest about being third-rate," and began pulling parts before I could object. Nobody asked my permission. Nobody seemed to think they needed it. In a place that isn't on any ledger, apparently neither are your possessions until you've earned them back.

The woman with the net pole was Ida Callow, and Callow's Ferry was hers the way a river is a river's — not owned, exactly, but inseparable. She fed me first. That's the thing I keep coming back to when I try to explain this place to people who've never been outside the reach of a bill: they fed me before they questioned me. Beans and smoked chub and flatbread cooked on a drum lid, served at a long table under the cottonwoods where maybe twenty people ate together, and every single one of them looked at me while pretending not to.

"You're storm-chaser," said a boy of maybe ten, sliding onto the bench across from me with the confidence of a customs inspector. He set down a battered radio handset. "Your beacon's been squawking for two days. We heard you coming."

"It's a locator transponder," I said. "Standard salvage kit."

"We know what it is." He leaned in, pleased to be delivering bad news. "It's also how they find you."

Ida, at the head of the table, said nothing. But she looked at the handset a moment too long, and later that evening it was gone from my pack, and my pack was otherwise untouched, and nobody would admit to anything. I decided I wasn't angry about it. There's a particular relief in having your leash confiscated by strangers, even if you didn't know you wanted it gone.

The town worked the way water works — no meetings, no announcements, just movement along the path of least resistance toward whatever needed doing. By morning Ferro had my engine apart; by noon two teenagers had re-caulked my hull without asking; by evening somebody had left a jar of honey on my cot, which I took to be either hospitality or a bill. With these people it was genuinely hard to tell.

I tried to pay for supper. Ida looked at the credit chip like I'd offered her a dead rat.

"What do you take?" I asked.

"Work," she said. "You'll gut fish tomorrow. Your hands look like they've never done an honest day's— " she caught herself, glanced at the calluses on my palms, the rope burns, the burn scar from a hail strike on my forearm. "Hm. All right. You'll gut fish anyway."

* * *

It was on the second night that I showed her the names.

I'd been circling it for a day, the way you circle a storm cell, waiting for the right approach vector. The town had been generous, but generosity here had edges, and I could feel them: questions answered with other questions, conversations that went quiet when I walked up, the boy with the radio — his name was Teo — following me around with the cheerful menace of a very small sheriff.

So after supper, when the table had thinned to Ida, Ferro, and a heavyset woman everyone called Aunt Ru who ran the still and possibly the town's entire foreign policy, I laid the survey manifest on the boards between the plates.

"Four names," I said. "Okafor. Lindqvist. Reyes-Marchand. Delacroix. Government hydrographic survey, contracted eight months ago. They logged their last position forty miles upstream of here and then stopped existing."

Aunt Ru went still with her cup halfway to her mouth. Ferro put down the net he'd been mending.

Ida picked up the paper. She read it the way she'd read the sky — slow, thorough, twice. Then she set it face-down on the table, which is a thing you do when a document has become dangerous.

"I knew Okafor's grandmother," she said. "Emmanuela Okafor. Ran water out of the lower basin during the Allocation Wars, same as my mother. Carried it in milk cans on a mule named after a senator she hated." Her thumb moved along the edge of the page, back and forth. "Sam Okafor used to summer here as a boy. Caught chub off that weir. Terrible at it. Wonderful at everything else."

"You knew him."

"I knew him." She looked up, and her eyes were doing something complicated — grief and arithmetic at once. "He came through last spring. Bought rope, salt, batteries. Told me he was mapping the river for the government." A pause. "Told me that with a straight face, which should have been my first warning. Governments don't map rivers, girl. They claim them."

"And the others?"

"Lindqvist I never met. Reyes-Marchand — there's a family of that name two bends downstream, unregistered like us, good people. Delacroix—" Ferro started to say something and she stopped him with two fingers raised, not looking away from me. "Delacroix I know of. Young. Talked too much, according to Sam's letters."

"Letters? They wrote to you?"

"They wrote to us. We're the only post office on this river that doesn't open mail or sell it." She folded the manifest once, precisely, and slid it back across the table to me. "Last letter came in the spring. Sam said they'd found something upstream that the maps had been lying about for thirty years. He said it cheerfully. He said it like a man who thinks he's found a mistake and mistakes get corrected." Her jaw tightened. "Mistakes don't get corrected, Wren Quintana. Mistakes get buried. Especially the ones with pipe attached."

The table was quiet. Down by the water, somebody was singing — low, wordless, the kind of song you sing to a task, not an audience. The river kept its own time underneath it.

"How many people live here?" I asked. "Really. Off the books."

"On the books?" Ida almost smiled. "None. In truth? Sixty-one. Sixty-two if you count the baby Ru delivered Tuesday, and we do count her, because nobody else will." She gathered plates, stacking them with the economy of a woman who'd been clearing tables since before I was born. "We're not hiding, if that's what you're wondering. Hiding takes energy. We're simply declining. Every form, every registry, every permit — we decline. You can't tax a town that isn't there. You can't fine a ferry that doesn't exist. We survive the way the river survives — by not appearing where they're looking."

"And when they look here anyway?"

Ferro snorted. "Then they find dry wash."

* * *

I slept that night in a guest room that was really a converted smoke rack, on a mattress stuffed with something that smelled gloriously of juniper, and I woke at dawn to the sound of the whole town already moving — buckets, oars, the slap of fish on willow, a language of small necessary noises that no city makes. I lay there a while and let it happen to me.

Here's what I couldn't stop thinking: I had spent six years in a life that punished standing still. Debt doesn't sleep; the Syndicate's interest compounded hourly; every clear sky cost me money and every storm paid only part of it back. I had come to believe that motion was the only safety, that the moment you stopped, the repo crews caught up. And here was sixty-two people who had stopped — who had planted orchards, for God's sake, apricots and mulberries in terraces cut into the canyon wall, trees that take years, that assume a future — and survived. Not survived despite refusing to be counted. Survived *because* of it.

There was warmth in it, real warmth, and it embarrassed me a little, like walking into a lit house from a cold road. Teo appointed himself my guide and showed me the schoolroom (a cave, chalkboard propped against flowstone), the clinic (one room, better stocked than any hospital I'd seen in the flats, all of it bartered), the graveyard (small, tended, oldest stone worn smooth: *CALLOW, 1st of her name, kept the crossing*). He narrated everything with the pride of a boy who does not know he's poor, because he isn't, not in any way that matters.

At midday Ida found me at the weir, gutting fish badly, and sat down on the bank beside me without ceremony.

"Marisol's skiff," she said.

My hands stopped. "You saw it?"

"Teo saw it yesterday, holding station at the mouth of the gorge. Too far to identify, close enough to matter." She watched the water move through the willow lattice. "Pilot named Marisol Vane-Ruiz. Syndicate flight wing. She's been shadowing this river for years, patient as a heron."

"She's not—" I started, and didn't have an ending for the sentence. She's not with me. She's hunting me. She saved my life once and I don't know why. Any of those might have been true.

"I don't care what she is," Ida said. "I care what she's for. And nothing Syndicate flies is ever for anything good on this river." She turned and looked at me directly, and I understood that the sorting had finished, and I had passed, and that this was both a gift and a weight. "You want to know what happened to your survey team. I want to know what Sam Okafor found that got four careful people erased. Those wants point the same direction, girl. Upstream."

"There's a glacier up there," I said. "The source canyon opens when it calves. Nine days, maybe less. After that, nothing gets in or out for a year."

"Then we'd best not waste them." She stood, brushed off her skirt, and paused. "One more thing. Since we're trading truths. That land grant you've been carrying around in your head — the diversion scheme, the water rights, the whole rotten architecture of it. You've seen the founding documents?"

"Some of them. Copies."

"No." She looked at me steadily. "You haven't. Because the originals aren't in any archive. They're here. Under my floor. Where Emmanuela Okafor's mule train put them eighty years ago, and where Sam Okafor asked me to keep them safe last spring, in case his cheerful mistake needed correcting." She held out her hand and pulled me to my feet, firm as a winch. "Come inside, Wren Quintana. There's a signature on those papers you need to see. I'd have shown you sooner, but I wanted to know whose daughter I was showing it to."

The river sang under the floorboards, cold and patient, and somewhere above the source, ice groaned like a door deciding whether to open.
