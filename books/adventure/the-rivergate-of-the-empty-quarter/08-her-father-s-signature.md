## 8. Her Father's Signature

Ida Callow did not keep her important papers in a safe. She kept them in a biscuit tin.

Wren learned this the morning after her arrival at Callow's Ferry, when she followed the old woman down a ladder into a root cellar that smelled of cold earth, lamp oil, and forty years of canned peaches. Ida moved along the shelves with the unhurried certainty of someone who had built every shelf herself, which, Wren suspected, she had.

"You'll want to mind your head," Ida said, without turning around. "The beam there has opinions."

Wren ducked. The beam did indeed have opinions; it clipped her anyway, gently, like a grandmother correcting posture.

At the back of the cellar, behind a crate of jars labeled PLUMS in fading pencil — though Wren would later learn they had not contained plums since before she was born — Ida lifted down a dented tin with roses painted on the lid, half worn away by thumbs. She set it on an upturned barrel and looked at Wren for a long moment.

"Before I open this," Ida said, "I want you to tell me what you think is in it."

"The survey team's records," Wren said. "The originals."

"No."

"The Syndicate's ledgers."

"No." Ida's hands rested on the tin the way other people's hands rested on the shoulders of children. "It's a land grant. The original. Signed and sealed twenty-two years ago, granting water rights to the upper river to a company called Meridian Consolidated Water & Power, which was a name invented by lawyers to sound like something that could be trusted with a river." Her eyes came up. "You've heard of it?"

"No," Wren said honestly.

"Mm. It changed its name twice since. It's called the Vane Syndicate now. Names are cheap. Rivers are not." Ida turned the tin over in her hands. "There were two signatures on the grant. One belongs to a man named Aldous Vane, who is dead and was buried under a monument. The other—" she paused, and Wren felt the cellar get colder, or imagined she did— "the other belongs to a hydrologist named Tomás Quintana."

The name went through Wren like a current through wet ground.

She had spent thirty-one years learning how to keep her face still. Storm chasers learn early that panic reads on radar of its own kind — the repo men, the debt collectors, the Syndicate's bounty assessors all watched for it. So she kept her face still. But her body betrayed her in small ways: her hand found the edge of the barrel; her breath went shallow and stayed there.

"You knew," Wren said finally. "Last night, when you read the names off the survey roster. You already knew whose daughter was standing in your kitchen."

"I suspected," Ida said. "You have his chin. And his habit of looking at moving water the way other people look at a fire." She lifted the lid off the tin. "Sit down, girl. This takes sitting down for."

* * *

The document was folded in oilcloth inside the tin, and beneath it lay a photograph, a fountain pen with a cracked nib, and a child's drawing of a river done in crayon, signed TOMÁS AGE 6 in letters that leaned like trees in wind.

"He kept everything," Ida said. "That was his trouble and his gift."

Wren unfolded the grant with hands that were steadier than she expected them to be. The paper had gone the color of weak tea. The seal of the territorial water authority sat in the lower corner, embossed so deeply it still held shadow after two decades. And there, above the line marked *CONSULTING HYDROLOGIST, SURVEY AND ASSESSMENT*, in ink gone brown but unmistakable:

*Tomás R. Quintana.*

His signature. She knew it better than her own. He had signed her permission slips for school field trips, signed the birthday cards he mailed from survey camps three hundred miles away, signed the last letter he ever sent her, which she still kept in a boot box under her bed in a house the bank owned now, technically, along with everything else she'd ever stood still long enough to own.

*Pumpkin — the river here sings at night. I'll show you someday. — Dad*

She had been twelve. There had been no someday.

"He didn't sign it because he believed in it," Ida said quietly. She lowered herself onto a crate with a grunt, the sound of a woman who had been kneeling in root cellars since before Wren's father was born. "He signed it because signing it was the only way they let him close enough to measure what they were really doing. That's my belief, anyway. I've had twenty-two years to form beliefs, and I've formed several."

"What do you mean, what they were really doing?"

"I mean the grant on its face grants diversion rights for irrigation — four thousand acre-feet a season, agricultural use, the usual language. Four thousand acre-feet is a lot of water. It is not enough water to explain why the towns downstream of the gate have been dying of thirst for a decade while somebody sells water rights on the open market at prices that would make a diamond blush." Ida tapped the document with one blunt finger. "Your father wrote the assessment appendix. Page nine. Read page nine."

Wren turned the brittle pages. The engineering language was dense but legible to her — she'd grown up reading her father's field notes the way other children grew up reading comics. Flow gauging data. Seasonal yield projections. And then, in the margin of page nine, in handwriting she would know anywhere, small and sharp as a bird's track:

*Figures don't reconcile. Intake capacity exceeds permitted allocation by factor of 6+. Requesting audit. T.Q.*

Below it, stamped diagonally in red: *APPENDIX WITHHELD FROM PUBLICATION — AUTHORITY REVIEW PENDING.*

"It was never pending," Ida said. "It was buried. Your father filed his audit request in the spring. By autumn he was gone, and the survey team that came upriver to check his figures vanished too, and everyone downstream learned to stop asking questions out loud." She looked at Wren over the lamplight. "Except us. We never learned. Nobody taught us."

* * *

They climbed back up into the kitchen, where the morning had gotten on without them — Marisol's skiff was visible through the window, anchored midstream where it had been for two days, patient as a heron. Delacroix sat at the table eating eggs with the focused intensity of a man relearning that food was a recurring offer, and he glanced up when Wren came in, then away, then back, the way he did with everything, cataloguing threats and exits even now.

Ida put the kettle on. In Callow's Ferry, apparently, every catastrophe was administered with tea.

"Say it," Ida said, setting two cups down. "Whatever it is you're chewing on. Say it out loud. It digests better in company."

Wren wrapped her hands around the cup. The ceramic was hot and real and helped.

"My whole life," she said, "I thought he abandoned us. That's the story I built. He loved rivers more than he loved us, he got tired of the small life, he walked. My mother never said it, but she stopped defending him, and silence is a verdict." She stared into the tea. "And the whole time, he was — what? Trying to blow a whistle on a theft the size of a sea? Getting erased for it?"

"That's one reading."

"What's yours?"

Ida sat down across from her, and for a moment the matriarch of Callow's Ferry looked simply old, and tired, and like a woman who had lost friends to this same arithmetic.

"My reading is that he made a bargain with himself," she said. "Sign the paper, get inside, gather proof, come back for it. And the mountain of it crushed him. These things always look simple from outside. From inside they're a maze with a wolf in it." She sipped her tea. "But here's what I know that isn't reading. When your father came through here — and he came through twice, once before the grant and once after — he sat at that table where Delacroix is sitting, and he told my mother, and later me, that the water belonged to the towns it ran past. Not to paper. To towns. He said a river is a promise the mountains make to the valley, and no signature can unmake it." She set the cup down. "Then he went upstream to try to keep that promise, and we've been keeping it for him ever since, badly, in the dark, without instruments."

Wren laughed — a short, cracked sound that surprised her.

"What?" Ida asked.

"He used to say that. About promises. 'A river is a promise.' I thought it was just a thing dads say. Like checking the weather twice." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, annoyed at them. "I built a whole personality out of being angry at him, and it turns out I inherited his hobby."

Delacroix, from the table, spoke without looking up from his eggs. "The figures don't reconcile," he recited, softly, in a voice like a man reading scripture he'd memorized under duress. "Intake capacity exceeds permitted allocation by factor of six plus. Requesting audit. T.Q." He looked up then, and his eyes were clear and terrible. "Page nine. I can give you every page. Every figure. Every date. They burned our papers, but they couldn't burn what I'd already read eleven times each, because I read things eleven times when I'm frightened, and I was very frightened for a very long time."

"We know," Wren said gently. "You told us last night."

"I'm telling you again," Delacroix said, "because you looked like someone deciding something, and decisions go better with corroboration."

* * *

Afternoon found Wren on the ferry landing, the grant in a waterproof sleeve against her chest, watching the river run cold and green through the canyon mouth. Marisol's skiff still sat midstream. At some point Marisol herself appeared at the rail, arms crossed, watching Wren watch the water, and neither of them waved, because some conversations hadn't happened yet and both of them knew it.

Ida came and stood beside her, hands clasped behind her back like a general reviewing troops, if generals wore cardigans.

"So," Ida said. "Now you know the shape of it. The debt you owe the Syndicate is paper. Paper burns. But this—" she nodded at the sleeve over Wren's heart— "this is the older debt. The one your father left unpaid. Interest accruing for twenty-two years, compounded nightly, payable to every town on this river."

"You make it sound like a bill."

"Everything is a bill, girl. The question is only who you pay, and whether you pay it standing up." Ida squinted upstream, toward the source canyon, where the glacier lived and kept its own calendar. "There's a complication coming, though. There's always a complication. The ice above the source starts calving soon — nine days, maybe ten, of open passage, and then the canyon seals itself until next summer. If your father's proof is going to travel, it travels in that window, and it travels by boat, and boats need water depth." She turned to look at Wren fully. "Which means the gates at the diversion works need to open. Which means somebody has to go up there and open them, past whatever armed staff the Syndicate keeps between here and the headworks."

Wren looked upstream. The canyon walls rose sheer and gold in the afternoon light, and somewhere above them, hidden, was a dam wearing her father's drawings like a disguise, and somewhere below her feet was a river being robbed in slow motion, and somewhere in her chest was a twelve-year-old girl holding a letter that promised *someday*.

Nine days.

"You're going regardless," Ida observed. It wasn't a question.

"I owe the money," Wren said. "But it turns out I owed this first."

Ida nodded slowly, satisfied, and patted Wren's shoulder once, hard, the way she probably hammered fence posts.

"Well then," she said. "Come inside. If you're going to rob a dam, you'll want supper first. And I'll introduce you to my boats."

From midstream, unnoticed by either of them, Marisol slipped over the side of her skiff and began, quietly, to row for shore.
