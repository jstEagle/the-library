## 9. Marisol's Bargain

The order came in at 0400, which was the Syndicate's way of saying they didn't want her thinking about it.

Marisol Vey had been a Syndicate pilot for eleven years. She knew the rhythm of their communications the way she knew the rhythm of weather: routine traffic came at noon, operational updates came at dusk, and anything that arrived before dawn wearing a priority flag meant somebody upstairs had stopped sleeping. She read it twice on the skiff's cracked display while the river muttered past the hull and the stars did their slow wheeling overhead.

*Subject Quintana to be handled. Recover or destroy all survey materials. No witnesses.*

*No witnesses* was doing a lot of work in that sentence, because Wren Quintana was currently asleep forty meters away in a camp that Marisol could see from here — one small fire banked to embers, one battered storm-chasing rig parked like a tired dog, one woman who had, over six days of shadowing her upriver, done nothing more alarming than fix a carburetor, curse at a topographic map, and share half a tin of coffee with a stranger who claimed to be a freelance salvage pilot.

That last part had been Marisol's idea. It was also, she was beginning to suspect, the worst professional decision of her life.

She put the display down and looked at the water.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about working enforcement for a company that owns the water: you spend a lot of time alone with your own arithmetic.

Marisol had gotten good at not doing the math. That was practically the job description. You flew the routes, you checked the gauges, you escorted the canal barges, and you did not ask why a river that legally did not exist was flowing through three closed military reserves into holding ponds the size of small seas. You did not ask why the towns along the dry washes — towns whose names appeared on no Syndicate ledger, which meant, officially, they were not towns — kept shrinking on the satellite imagery year over year. You did not ask what happened to government survey teams, because asking that question was how you ended up flying night runs alone, and then not flying at all.

But arithmetic has a way of finding you anyway. It had found her last spring, in the person of an eight-year-old girl at a fuel stop outside Perdition Flats, who had watched Marisol hose dust off the skiff and asked, with the terrible directness of children, "Is your company the reason our well died?"

Marisol had said, "No, sweetheart," and given her a candy bar, and flown two hundred kilometers, and pulled over on a salt flat to be sick behind a rock.

That had been ten months ago. The candy bar was still in her jacket pocket. She'd never been able to figure out why she kept it. Now, watching the ember-glow of Wren Quintana's fire and reading *no witnesses* for the third time, she thought maybe she was starting to understand.

* * *

The thing about Wren Quintana — and Marisol had now spent six days close enough to form opinions — was that she was exactly as advertised and completely wrong anyway.

The dossier said: debtor, drifter, competent pilot, no family ties worth exploiting, no loyalties worth leveraging. A woman held together by debt and stubbornness, the kind of asset you hire for dirty work precisely because she has nowhere else to go. The dossier was accurate the way a skeleton is accurate. It had all the right parts and none of the person.

The person fixed things. That was the first thing Marisol had noticed, back when she was still pretending this was surveillance instead of something worse. Wren's camp wasn't a camp so much as a rolling argument between entropy and a woman with a multitool, and entropy was losing on points. The second thing was that Wren talked to herself when she worked, a low running commentary of profanity and encouragement, as if she were both the mechanic and the crew chief. The third thing — and this was the one that had started the crack in Marisol's conscience, though she hadn't known it yet — was that when Wren found the survey team's abandoned instruments and read their logs by headlamp, she didn't celebrate. She sat very still for a long time, and then she said, quietly, to nobody: "Okay. Okay. So where did all of you go?"

Like she already cared about the answer. Like the dead were people to her, and not line items.

Marisol had killed the engine that night and drifted, and told herself the tightness in her chest was altitude.

* * *

At 0530 the fire stirred and Wren emerged from her bedroll looking like a woman who had lost a fight with her own sleeping bag. She shuffled to the water's edge, splashed her face, made a sound like a dying accordion, and only then noticed Marisol sitting on the skiff's gunwale twenty meters off.

"You're up early," Wren said. Her voice was gravel and suspicion in equal measure.

"Couldn't sleep."

"River too loud?"

"Something like that."

Wren squinted at her. Six days of acquaintance had taught Marisol that squint well; it was the look of a woman recalculating. Wren Quintana recalculated constantly — fuel margins, water margins, trust margins. Marisol had watched her do it with maps, with money, with the canned peaches Marisol had traded her on day three. Watching it aimed at herself was less comfortable.

"You got a message last night," Wren said. Not a question.

"Traffic check."

"At four in the morning."

"Weather window."

"In the Empty Quarter." Wren crouched at the fire and fed it a stick. "Where the weather hasn't changed in nine days."

Marisol said nothing, which was itself an answer, and Wren nodded slowly, the way people nod when a long-suspected diagnosis comes back confirmed.

"So let's do this," Wren said. "Day six. You've been shadowing me since the gate. Your 'freelance salvage pilot' story fell apart around day two, when I found the Syndicate registry number under your false transponder code — yes, I looked, I'm in debt to these people, I know their paperwork better than my own handwriting. You've had every opportunity to put a bullet in me and haven't. And now you've received orders that have made you sit out here staring at the water like it owes you an apology." She poked the fire. "So either you're the worst assassin in the history of the Vane Syndicate, or you're about to tell me something."

"I'm not an assassin," Marisol said. "I'm enforcement."

"What's the difference?"

"Assassins get paid more."

It came out flat and automatic, and to Marisol's horror, Wren laughed. Actually laughed, a short rusty bark, like a door that hadn't been opened in years.

"Okay," Wren said. "Enforcement. Sit down, then. Coffee's terrible but there's plenty of it."

* * *

Marisol did not sit down. She stayed on the gunwale, boots in the skiff, because sitting down felt like surrendering and she wanted, just for a few more minutes, to pretend she still had a side.

"I got orders," she said. "Four this morning."

"To destroy the survey records."

"And to handle you. No witnesses."

Wren took this in without flinching, which Marisol respected and also found slightly infuriating. Most people flinched.

"Hale set me up," Wren said. "I figured. Salvage contract with coordinates inside a closed reserve, pay that clears my debt twice over? Nobody pays that for instruments. They pay that for a loose end that walks itself into the desert." She turned the coffee pot with her boot. "I just wanted to see if you'd say it out loud."

"Why?"

"Because if you say it out loud, you're not going to do it."

"That's not—" Marisol stopped. Started again. "You don't know that."

"No," Wren agreed. "But I've watched you for six days. You gave me peaches. You warned me about the sandbar at the narrows when you thought I couldn't hear you. And last night you sat out on that boat for two hours after your orders came in, and assassins don't do that. Assassins sleep fine." She poured two cups of terrible coffee and held one out. "Soldiers don't, though. Soldiers do math."

Marisol took the cup. Her hand was steadier than she expected it to be.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic, then. She laid it out loud for the first time in eleven years, standing on a gunwale in the cold, talking to a woman she'd been sent to kill.

"One aquifer," Marisol said. "One river, actually — the real one, the one that isn't on any legal map. It rises in the source canyon, runs the length of the Empty Quarter underground and in that gorge you're following, and historically it fed every settlement from Callow's Ferry down to the flats. Eleven years ago the Syndicate built diversion works at the source. Headworks, pumps, a dam you can't see from the air because it's built inside the canyon walls. They've been siphoning the upper river ever since."

"Selling it."

"Selling it. Municipal contracts, agricultural leases, industrial supply. Water shipped two hundred kilometers to towns that used to get it free out of the ground, from a river those towns' grandparents settled." Marisol drank the terrible coffee. It helped. Terrible coffee was honest. "The revenue is somewhere north of what I'm cleared to know. Enough that the Syndicate holds paper on half the territory. Enough that when a federal survey team requested access to the reserve last year, somebody upstairs decided that was an existential threat."

"The survey team found the proof."

"They found the flow data. Upstream gauge readings against downstream. There's no natural explanation for a river losing sixty percent of its volume between two points in solid rock. Their instruments recorded it for three weeks. Then they reported in, and their report never arrived, and neither did they." Marisol heard her own voice go careful and flat, the enforcement voice, and hated it, and kept going. "Hale hired you because you're good at finding things and expendable enough to lose. If you find the records, you bring them to him, he destroys them, and then you become part of what gets destroyed. If you don't find them, same ending, different scenery. You were never a salvage contractor, Quintana. You were the mop."

Wren was quiet for a moment. The fire popped. Somewhere upstream, the gorge made a sound like a throat clearing.

"My father," Wren said finally, "was a hydrologist. He vanished when I was twelve. His name is on the original land grant for the diversion scheme — Ida Callow showed me. I've been chewing on that for two days." She looked up. "You knew him?"

"I know the name. Every enforcement pilot knows the name. Quintan's Draw, the old monitoring station — it's named for him. He mapped the upper river before the Syndicate bought the surveys. He tried to blow the whistle on the first diversion phase." Marisol hesitated. "There's a training module about him. Internal. 'The Cost of Disloyalty.' They show it to new hires."

Wren stared at her. Something moved behind her face that Marisol chose not to name, because naming it would have required acknowledging what she was about to do next, and she wanted a few more seconds of being a person who hadn't done it yet.

"So," Wren said, very evenly. "Why are you telling me this?"

* * *

Because of the candy bar. Because of the eight-year-old at Perdition Flats. Because of eleven years of not doing the math, and the specific quality of silence in a canyon where four people had been erased, and because — this was the part that surprised her most — because Wren had asked where the survey team went like she cared about the answer, and Marisol had realized, sitting out on the black water at 0400, that she could not remember the last time anyone in the Syndicate had asked a question because they cared about the answer.

"Because I did the math," Marisol said. "Last spring, mostly. Finished it last night."

"And?"

"And it doesn't balance. It has never balanced. We take the water from towns that aren't on the ledger, sell it back to towns that are, and call the difference profit. But the towns that aren't on the ledger are still towns. People are still living there. I've flown over them. Kids. Wells going dry. And the survey team — four federal employees with families, presumably, with names — erased because they wrote down numbers that were true." She set the coffee cup down on the gunwale with exaggerated care, because her hand had developed an opinion. "I've spent eleven years being loyal to an organization that treats truth as a spillage problem. And I keep waiting for the loyalty to pay out something besides more loyalty. It doesn't. It's a river that only flows uphill, and I'm at the bottom, and I am so tired, Quintana."

Wren said nothing. She was good at that, Marisol was learning. She let silences do work.

"So here's my bargain," Marisol said. "Not Hale's. Mine. I will not handle you. I will fly you wherever you need to go, carry whatever you find, and when this is over — whatever 'over' looks like, and I make no promises that it looks like anything good — I will testify. Tribunal, federal water board, a barn full of angry grandmothers, whoever will write it down. In exchange you don't leave me behind, and you don't shoot me in the back when I sleep, and you understand that I'm not doing this because I'm noble. I'm doing it because the arithmetic finally caught up with me and I can't outrun it anymore."

"That's a terrible bargain," Wren said.

"It's the only one I have."

"No, I mean—" Wren rubbed her face. "I mean it's terrible that it's a bargain at all. You should just — people should just do the right thing without invoicing each other." She paused. "Deal. But I get the peaches back."

"You ate the peaches."

"I ate *some* of the peaches. There's a moral remainder."

Despite everything — the orders burning a hole in her display, the canyon, the graves waiting upstream, the eleven years — Marisol laughed. It came out of her like something uncorked, surprising them both.

* * *

They broke camp fast after that, the way Wren did everything, with the economy of a woman who had never once owned more than she could carry. Marisol topped off the skiff's tanks from her reserve and pretended not to notice Wren clocking exactly how much fuel that was and what it meant about escape options. Smart woman. Marisol would have done the same.

As they pushed off into the current, Wren stood in the bow with the survey leader's logbook open against her chest, reading by the gray first light.

"There's a fourth member," she said suddenly. "Delacroix. The others — the logs stop being logs near the end. They start being letters. To family. You don't write letters unless you think you're done." She turned a page. "But Delacroix's name keeps appearing after the letters stop. Little entries. Position notes. 'Still moving. Still counting.' Like he went to ground and kept recording."

"Then he might be alive," Marisol said.

"He might be alive," Wren agreed, "or he might be a ghost with good penmanship. Either way, he's upstream. Past the narrows, past the old ferry crossing, into the high gorge." She looked back at Marisol, and the morning light caught her face, and Marisol saw that underneath the exhaustion and the debt and the recalculation, Wren Quintana had the look of a woman who had stopped choosing between bad options and started choosing a direction.

"How far to Callow's Ferry?" Marisol asked.

"Half a day, if the river cooperates." Wren snapped the logbook shut. "And Marisol — the glacier above the source. How long does it hold the canyon open?"

Marisol felt the question land in her stomach like ballast.

"Calving season starts soon," she said. "When it goes, the icefall seals the source canyon until next spring. Nine days, maybe ten, if we're lucky." She throttled up. "After that, the door closes for a year."

Wren nodded once, and turned to face upstream, and the skiff ran on into the narrowing gold of the gorge, toward the town that wasn't on any ledger, and the father whose name was on everything, and the mountain that had already begun, far above them, quietly deciding how much time they had left.
