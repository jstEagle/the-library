## 10. The Survey Team's Last Camp

The gorge narrowed the way a fist does — slowly at first, and then all at once.

Wren had been climbing since before dawn, following the river up through country that didn't believe in switchbacks. The walls leaned in overhead until the sky was a blue ribbon no wider than her arm, and the water below ran so cold and so fast it made a sound like applause that never quite finished. Her legs had stopped negotiating somewhere around noon. Now they simply did what she told them, which was more than she could say for most of her life.

Marisol climbed behind her without complaint, which Wren had learned to distrust as a personality trait. Delacroix's trail led upward: scraps of cloth on thornbushes, a tin cup set deliberately on a stone shelf where anyone passing would see it, and once — this was the detail that made Wren's chest go tight — a small cairn of river stones arranged with unmistakable care, topped by a flat piece of slate scratched with a single word in a surveyor's tidy hand:

*UP.*

Not *help*. Not *gone*. Just *up*, like a man leaving directions to his own mind.

"He wants to be found," Marisol said quietly.

"Or he wants someone to know he tried," Wren said, and kept climbing.

* * *

She smelled the camp before she saw it — woodsmoke gone stale, and under it something sweeter and worse, the smell of a place where people had lived hard for a long time. Then the gorge opened into a hanging valley no wider than a threshing floor, walled in granite, floored with gravel and a stubborn meadow of sedge, and there it was.

Three graves.

They sat in a row above the high-water line, each marked with a stone slab and an inscription cut deep enough to outlast weather. Wren stood over them with her hat in her hand because some things you did even when nobody was watching, especially then.

*E. VASQUEZ, HYDROLOGIST.* *T. OKONKWO, INSTRUMENTATION.* *R. MBEKI, LOGISTICS.*

Below each name, the same line, repeated three times, carved by the same hand:

*HE KNEW WHAT THE WATER WAS FOR.*

Wren read it four times. It wasn't grief exactly, or not only. It was a sentence with teeth in it. Whoever had cut those letters had wanted whoever found them to understand that these were not deaths. They were payments.

"The fourth one," Marisol said behind her. "Look."

The fourth campsite was nothing like the others. Where the graves were orderly, almost ceremonial, the last site was chaos folded into discipline — a shallow overhang in the canyon wall screened by stacked stones, a sleeping hollow lined with sedge, a fire ring built so small and so sheltered that its smoke would have been invisible from twenty feet away. And everywhere, everywhere, the evidence of a mind that had refused to stop working: tally marks scratched into the rock in columns of ten; diagrams drawn in charcoal on flattened slabs; and along one whole wall of the overhang, a long table of numbers copied out again and again, each repetition slightly more confident than the last, like a man practicing a speech he intended to give for the rest of his life.

Which, Wren thought, looking at the columns, was possibly exactly what he'd been doing.

"Delacroix," she called softly. "I'm not Syndicate. I'm not government. My name is Wren Quintana, and I came up the rivergate, and I burned my own fuel to do it. You can check my math on that if you want."

Silence. Long enough that Marisol shifted her weight.

Then, from somewhere above them — from a crack in the canyon wall that Wren would have sworn held nothing but shadow — a voice said:

"Quintana. Like the storm woman. Like the one who reads clouds."

"That's me."

"You're late." A pause. "Eleven months, six days. But I suppose you had your reasons. Everyone has their reasons. Reasons are cheap. Water is expensive."

A figure unfolded itself from the crack, and Wren had to work very hard not to let her face show what her face wanted to show.

* * *

He had been a big man once. You could still see the architecture of him under the ruin — broad shoulders, hands like shovels — but the gorge had spent eleven months sanding him down. His beard was a gray-brown thicket going every direction at once. He wore layers of salvaged clothing in no particular order, and around his neck, on a cord, hung a waterproof case the size of a paperback book, worn smooth as a river stone from handling. He kept one hand on it the way other men keep a hand on a weapon.

His eyes were the worst part. They were clear. Completely, terribly clear.

"You're the salvage contract," he said. "Vane sent you. I've been expecting Vane to send someone for a year. I'm surprised they took this long. Their logistics are usually better." He looked at Marisol. "You're theirs too. Skiff pilot. Marisol Reyes. You fly the lower channel runs. I watched you twice from the rim, before I stopped coming down to the rim."

Marisol said nothing, which was its own confession.

"So," Delacroix said, and smiled, and the smile was the first thing about him that looked tired instead of sharp. "Are you here to kill me or to hire me? I should say upfront that I'm bad at both. I've been practicing hiding for a year and I've gotten quite good at it, which is unfortunate, because it means I'll be difficult to murder and impossible to employ."

"We're here to take you home," Wren said.

"Home." He tasted the word like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. "I had a home. It had a cat. The cat's name was General Relativity, because my daughter named it and she was going through a phase. Do you know what happened to my home?"

"No," Wren said gently.

"Neither do I. That's the thing about being erased. They don't just kill you. They kill your address." He turned and gestured at the graves with a hand that trembled only slightly, and Wren understood the trembling wasn't fear. It was the effort of holding still for a year. "Emmanuel Vasquez. Tomi Okonkwo. Ruth Mbeki. We came up here because the instruments told us the river was being stolen, and we came quietly because we were government, and government is quiet by design. And on the ninth day, men came down from the headworks with rifles, and Emmanuel said run, and we ran, and running worked for two of us for four days, and then it worked for one of us indefinitely."

He touched the case at his throat.

"They shot Ruth first. She was the fastest. That sounds like a contradiction but it isn't — she was the fastest, so she was in front, so she was what they saw. Tomi went back for her. Emmanuel and I split the records between us, paper copies, and we agreed on the rule that has kept me alive: *paper can be burned.* So we burned our copies. Both of us. All of them. Every page, in the river, and we shook hands over it like idiots, like men finishing a card game, and Emmanuel laughed — he actually laughed — and said, 'Now they can't take it from us.' And then they took Emmanuel."

Wren's throat was dry. "And you kept—"

"I kept everything," Delacroix said, "in here." He tapped his temple with one finger, and the tap had a rhythm to it, deliberate, metronomic. "Flow rates. Gate schedules. Pump serials. Canal capacities. Eleven years of diversion volumes cross-referenced against Syndicate water sales in four counties. Names. Dates. Signatures." His voice dropped, and for a moment the madman's music went out of it entirely, and what was left was a surveyor reading instruments. "I recite it every morning and every night. If I miss a day, parts start to blur, so I don't miss days. I haven't missed a day in three hundred and forty-one days. I am, at present, the most complete record of the largest water theft in the history of this territory, and I live in a hole in a rock, and I talk to stones, and sometimes I talk to the river, and the river is a better conversationalist than you'd think."

* * *

They made tea on his tiny fire, because he insisted, because ritual was load-bearing. He produced a dented pot, a hoard of tea leaves wrapped in waxed cloth, and three cups — two intact, one carefully riveted back together with wire, which he gave to himself without comment.

"Ask me anything," he said, cradling his cup. "Go on. Test me. Everyone should test me. Verification is the soul of science."

Wren thought of the numbers she'd seen on the survey instruments downstream, the impossible drop in flow. "October eleventh, two years ago. Flow at the gate station."

"Four hundred and twelve cubic feet per second reported. Two thousand nine hundred actual, before the headworks took their share. The discrepancy is sixty-one million gallons a day, annualized, sold downstream through the Vane canal system at municipal rates to towns whose own aquifers the Syndicate had already drained." He recited it flatly, without pride, the way a man reads his own pulse. "I can give you the next nine years if you'd like. I wouldn't recommend it all at once. It goes down easier in monthly installments."

Marisol had gone very pale. "I hauled water for them," she said, mostly to herself. "Two seasons. I flew pump parts up the channel. I thought—" She stopped.

"You thought you were moving freight," Delacroix said kindly. "Everyone thinks they're moving freight. That's the genius of it. Nobody smuggles anymore; they just move freight, and the freight happens to be a river." He sipped his tea. "Your father would have understood that."

Wren's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "What did you say?"

"Your father." Delacroix looked at her, and his clear eyes were suddenly, unbearably gentle. "Emmanuel talked about him. Quintana, the hydrologist. Emmanuel trained under him, did you know that? Before the grant, before the disappearance. Emmanuel used to say, 'If Aldo Quintana signed that grant, then either he was dead already or they made him sign it dead.' He never believed your father was a willing party. Neither do I. I've read the engineering drawings, Ms. Quintana. They contain mistakes. Deliberate ones. Little sabotage knots tied into the specifications, the kind only the designer would know were there. Your father left a signature inside his signature." He smiled sadly. "Hydrologists. We're sentimental about water and devious about everything else."

Wren set her cup down because her hands had opinions about holding things. Somewhere upstream, faint as memory, the glacier groaned — a long tectonic complaint rolling down the gorge like a door testing its hinges.

"How much do you know about the headworks?" she asked, when she trusted her voice.

"Everything. Staffing rotations, armament, the generator schedule, the fact that the floodgates' manual overrides were never installed because doing it properly would have required permits." He grinned, and for one second he looked like a man who owned a cat named General Relativity and a daughter with phases. "Thieves are lazy, Ms. Quintana. It's their best quality and their fatal flaw."

"And the glacier?" Marisol asked. "The calving. Ida Callow says the source canyon opens maybe nine days a year."

Something crossed Delacroix's face then — not fear, exactly. Arithmetic.

"Nine days," he said. "Yes. I've been counting those too. I've been counting everything. That's the cost of knowing, you understand. Knowing isn't free. Knowing is a debt you pay daily, in attention, forever." He looked up at the ribbon of sky, where the light was going long and gold, and the mountain above them muttered again, closer now, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. "But here's the thing nobody tells you about paying a debt that long."

"What?" Wren said.

"When the chance finally comes to spend it — you spend it *all*." He drained his tea and stood, unfolding joint by joint, a man assembling himself out of storage. "So. You say you're taking me home. Fine. But home is downstream, and downstream is a town full of boats, and boats need a river with some authority behind it, and right now the river is being held hostage at the top of this gorge by forty armed men and a pump station your father designed to be blown up." He picked up his walking staff, tested it against the gravel, and pointed it upstream, toward the groaning dark of the source canyon.

"It opens tomorrow morning," he said. "We have nine days. And I intend to use every one of them making the loudest withdrawal in the history of stolen water."

Above them, the glacier answered — a crack like a rifle shot, then another, then a long white rumble of ice letting go somewhere out of sight, falling into the gorge ahead of them, falling into the place they had to go.

Delacroix listened to it with his head cocked, like a man checking a clock.

"Right on time," he said. "The mountain keeps better books than the Syndicate ever did."
