## 11. Calving Season

The mountain spoke first.

Wren was on the ferry landing at Callow's Ferry, mending a net she had no idea how to mend — Ida had handed it to her that morning with the serene confidence of a woman who believed all hands were the same hands — when the sound came down the gorge. Not a crack, exactly. A groan, deep and grinding, like a cathedral settling onto its foundations, or like something enormous turning over in its sleep and deciding not to wake yet.

Every head on the landing lifted at once. The mending stopped. Even the dogs went quiet.

"That'll be the Serac," said a boy of maybe ten, without looking up from the fish he was gutting, because in Callow's Ferry apparently even children announced glaciers the way other children announced dinner.

"The what?" Wren asked.

"Serac Head." He pointed upstream, past the last rooftops, to where the gorge narrowed into a slot of gray stone and, above that, a wall of ice so high it made her neck ache to follow it to its crown. "She calves this time of year. Opens the canyon for a spell, then shuts it."

"Shuts it how?"

The boy looked at her then, with the mild pity of someone explaining rain to a fish. "Ice comes down. Canyon goes away. Come back next year."

* * *

Ida Callow received the full explanation on her porch that evening, in the company of Marisol — who had spent three days being fed by the town while pretending she wasn't grateful — and Delacroix, who sat wrapped in two blankets by the stove and watched the fire with the alertness of a man who had learned, over four years alone in a gorge, that warmth was an event worth attending closely.

"Nine days," Ida said. She said it the way other people said *the mortgage is due*. "Give or take. The Serac opens the upper canyon when the melt cuts her feet out from under her, and for about nine days there's a passage through to the source country. Then she sheds enough ice to fill the gorge wall to wall, and that's that until next calving season. My father timed it once with a calendar and a stopwatch and a great deal of whiskey. Nine days, four hours. He wrote it on the doorframe. It's still there."

Wren thought about that doorframe. About a town that measured its year not in months but in the breathing of a glacier.

"So we have nine days to get the evidence out," she said. "Delacroix's testimony, the logs, the survey records, whatever's left at the last camp. Downriver, past the diversion works, to somewhere the Syndicate can't burn it."

"You'd think so," Ida said. "But you're thinking like a woman with a road. We haven't got a road. We've got a river — and right now our river is a lie."

Marisol shifted in her chair. She'd been doing that all evening, Wren noticed. Sitting like a woman whose body kept trying to leave before the rest of her agreed to.

"The pumps," Marisol said quietly.

"The pumps," Ida agreed, and turned to Wren. "Your Syndicate friends have been drinking this river for ten years. Every drop they pull through those gates is a drop my boats don't float on. Below the headworks, the channel runs shin-deep in August. My runners can't carry cargo on shin-deep water. They never could. That's half the reason nobody ever found us — the river below the gates looks dead, and dead rivers don't invite visitors."

"And if the gates open?"

"If the gates open, the river comes back. All of it, all at once. Enough water to float loaded boats from here to the lowlands in two days instead of nine. Enough to outrun anything the Syndicate sends after us." Ida folded her hands. She had a way of folding her hands that made you feel you were the one being asked a favor. "So your nine days just became two problems, girl. One: get up the source canyon before the mountain closes it. Two: open those gates, because without them, even if we get the evidence down here, it dies on the beach with everything else."

The stove ticked. Outside, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, the glacier groaned again.

Delacroix spoke without lifting his eyes from the fire. "I know where the gates are."

Everyone turned. It was the longest sentence he'd offered in three days that wasn't a recitation of flow rates.

"I carried the survey instruments past them twice," he said. "Before. There's a catwalk on the north wall. And there are men there now — there were men there then, too, but fewer. They wear gray and they don't wave." His hands moved in his lap, small precise motions, like a man playing an invisible piano. "The pumps run on a schedule. Everything runs on a schedule. Schedules can be broken."

"He's been saying things like that since he got here," Ida said, gentler than Wren had ever heard her. "Half of it's gold and half of it's ash. Trouble is knowing which half until it's too late."

"It's all gold," Delacroix said, with sudden, startling firmness. "I memorized it. All of it. You don't survive four years on ash."

Nobody argued. There was something in the way he said it — not pride, exactly. Arithmetic. A man who had paid four years of solitude for a set of numbers was entitled to believe they were worth it.

* * *

They planned by lamplight, spread across Ida's kitchen table like surgeons over a patient.

The geography, once Wren got it drawn, was brutally simple. From Callow's Ferry, the gorge climbed twenty miles to the source canyon, where the river came down off the icefield in a single roaring throat. Somewhere in that throat — Delacroix could point to the bend, though his pointing hand shook — sat the diversion works: dam, gates, pumping station, the whole hidden machinery of thirst. Above the works, the canyon narrowed again, and above *that*, the Serac hung like a sword with a nine-day temper.

"Here's the order of operations," Wren said, pencil tapping the map. "Small party goes up fast — me, Marisol, Delacroix. We reach the works, open the gates. The river rises behind us. Ida's runners load the evidence and ride the flood down to the lowlands. Meanwhile we come back down the canyon ahead of the calving, or—" she paused— "or we don't."

"'Or we don't' is not a plan," Marisol said.

"'Or we don't' is the part of every plan I've ever run," Wren said. "You get used to it. Like weather. You don't argue with the sky; you just make sure you know which way it's leaning."

"And if the Syndicate garrisons the works? You said yourself there are armed men."

"There are," Wren admitted. "Six, by Delacroix's count, plus whoever Hale brings when he figures out where I've gone. But here's the thing about men guarding a secret: they expect trouble to come from downstream, from the world they're hiding from. Nobody expects it to come from above. Nobody in the history of theft has ever looked up at a glacier and thought, *that's my attack vector.*"

Marisol almost smiled. It was a small thing, quickly put away, but Wren caught it, and caught herself being pleased about catching it, and decided not to examine why.

"I should tell you," Marisol said, after a silence, "that when I report in — and I have to keep reporting in, or they'll know something's wrong — I'm going to have to say something about you. Something that buys time. I haven't decided what yet."

"Say I'm dead," Wren suggested. "It's traditional."

"They'll want a body."

"Then say I'm lost. Say the gorge took me. Honestly, Marisol, after everything, I'd think you'd enjoy lying to them a little."

Marisol looked at her for a long moment. "I used to be good at it," she said finally. "That's the problem. I was very good at it, and then one day I did the arithmetic on what all my good lies had bought, and the sum came out wrong." She stood, straightening a jacket that didn't need straightening. "I'll take the first watch."

* * *

The next two days were preparation, and preparation in Callow's Ferry turned out to be a town-wide event, the way a funeral or a wedding is a town-wide event in places that still believe in occasions.

Boats came out of winter storage and were rolled down to the landing on greased logs, forty of them, fifty, hulls patched with pitch and canvas and, in one memorable case, the roof of a school bus. Nets were mended — Wren's net among them, returned to her with three new holes and a compliment. Children were counted and recounted and assigned to grandparents. Ida moved through it all like a conductor who never raised her voice, and somehow every section came in on time.

Wren packed light. Rope, pitons, the survey logs sealed in oilcloth against her back, a week of food, and — after a long internal argument — her father's land grant, the old paper with his signature slanting across it like a man writing while walking away. She told herself she brought it because it was evidence. She knew that was only half true. The other half rode close to her heart the whole climb, heavier than the rope.

On the second evening, she found Delacroix at the landing, watching the water.

"Do you remember the schedule?" she asked him. "The pump schedule?"

"Midnight shift change," he said instantly. "Four-minute gap between relief crews. Gates tested Tuesdays and Fridays, both gates together, ninety seconds of free flow. Generator maintenance the first Monday of each month — backup power only, six hours." He recited it flatly, without inflection, the way a man recites a psalm he no longer believes in but cannot stop believing.

"Ninety seconds of free flow," Wren repeated. "Both gates together. Every Tuesday and Friday."

"Yes."

She looked up the gorge, toward the distant white shoulder of the Serac, catching the last red light of the day like a coal refusing to go out. "Delacroix. What day is tomorrow?"

He considered. This took a while. Days had stopped meaning much to him, she suspected, during the four years when every day was the same day. "Friday," he said at last. "I think. The fish were Friday fish."

"Friday fish," Wren agreed solemnly. "Then tomorrow the gates test themselves. Ninety seconds of free flow, both gates, while the guards stand around watching gauges. And if somebody happened to be standing at the gate controls when the test began..."

"...the test might not end," Delacroix finished. Something moved through his face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, surfacing after a long dive. "Schedules can be broken."

"Schedules can be broken," Wren said.

* * *

They left at dawn on the third morning, before the sun cleared the eastern rim, in the blue hour when the river smoke rose straight and the whole town stood silent on the banks to watch them go.

Ida embraced each of them in turn — briskly, fiercely, the way she did everything — and saved her longest look for Wren. "Nine days," she said. "I'll have boats loaded and waiting by the sixth. If the river rises, we ride. If it doesn't..." She didn't finish. She didn't need to. They both knew what a town did with evidence it couldn't move: it buried it, and buried the people who carried it, and waited a generation for another chance.

"We'll raise your river," Wren said.

"You'll raise more than that," Ida said. "Go on, now. The mountain won't wait, and neither will I."

They climbed. The gorge swallowed them in switchbacks, the town falling away below until it was a scatter of tin roofs and woodsmoke, then a rumor, then nothing but the river's voice echoing off stone. By midday they could see the Serac properly for the first time — not a wall but a country of ice, blue-green in its depths, fractured into towers and blades, hanging over the upper canyon with the patience of geology.

As the sun crossed noon, the mountain groaned again, longer this time, and a plume of snow puffed from a ridge two miles away where some serac the size of a church had shrugged loose and fallen inward, harmlessly, a warning shot.

Marisol stopped and watched the plume settle. "How do you do it?" she asked. "Walk toward things like that?"

Wren considered the question honestly, because Marisol had earned honesty. "I read storms for a living," she said. "A supercell is just a mountain made of weather — same physics, same patience, same indifference to whether you live. You learn two things. One: it doesn't hate you. Two: it doesn't care. Both of those are weirdly comforting, if you let them be." She adjusted her pack straps and started climbing again. "Also, I owe a truly obscene amount of money, which focuses the mind wonderfully."

Behind her, she heard Marisol laugh — actually laugh, short and rusty, like a hinge remembering its purpose.

They made camp that night in the lee of a granite spur, and Wren lay awake listening to the glacier breathe. Groan. Silence. Groan. A rhythm, slow and vast, like the lungs of something sleeping under the world. Somewhere above them, in the dark, the ice was counting. Nine days. Eight, now. Seven and a half, if you listened carefully, and Wren had always listened carefully.

*Two problems,* Ida had said. Get up the canyon. Open the gates.

Tomorrow was Friday. Tomorrow there would be ninety seconds of free flow, and three people with a stolen schedule and nothing left to lose.

The glacier groaned, long and low, and Wren fell asleep inside the sound, and dreamed of water rising.

* * *

They reached the mouth of the source canyon at midmorning on the fourth day, and stopped as one, because the mountain had changed the terms.

The passage Delacroix remembered — a gravel bench along the south wall, wide enough for a cautious man with a pack — was gone. In its place lay a field of fresh rubble, blocks of ice the size of houses, already slumping and sweating in the sun, marking where the Serac had shed her first argument of the season. Beyond the rubble, the canyon ran on, narrow and glittering, the route visibly thinner than it had been any year before. You could see, in the fresh scars on the walls, how much ice had come down already. You could count, if you were the sort of person who counted, how little room remained.

"Three days less," Delacroix said quietly. "Maybe four. She's calving early. Warm spring." He looked at Wren, and his eyes were clear and terrible. "I told you. Schedules break."

Wren stood in the shadow of the ice and felt the clock in her chest wind tighter — not a number anymore but a weight, a physical pressure, the mountain's hand resting gently on her shoulder to remind her whose timetable this really was. Behind her, eight miles back, Ida was loading boats that would never float unless the gates opened. Ahead, past the narrows, the diversion works waited with its gray-clad guards and its humming pumps and its Friday test.

And somewhere downriver — she felt it the way she felt pressure drops before a storm, in her teeth, in the old animal place — Ovid Hale was moving too.

"Then we go today," Wren said. "Not tomorrow. Today. While the route's still here and before anyone expects us." She tightened her pack, checked her rope, and looked up at the shining, groaning, indifferent bulk of the Serac Head. "Ninety seconds," she said, to the mountain, to the river, to whoever was keeping the books. "That's all I need."

The glacier groaned back, and dropped a boulder the size of a wagon into the canyon a quarter mile ahead, and Wren took that, in the spirit it was clearly intended, as a yes.
