## 13. What Water Owes

Ida Callow had buried two husbands, one church, and a great many illusions, and she had learned from all of them the same lesson: when a thing is about to break, it makes a sound first. The ferry bell made it that morning — a cracked, complaining note, half bronze and half rust — and she stood on the landing with her hands in her apron pockets and listened to it the way other women listened to weather.

The bell hadn't rung in nine years. She'd hung it herself, back when the river still had a name on paper, and the last time it rang was the day the water dropped so low the boats went to their knees in the mud. It rang now because a girl from downstream had climbed the landing ladder and pulled the rope without asking, the way young people did things, and the sound went out over Callow's Ferry like a rumor with legs.

Doors opened. That was the thing about the bell. It didn't call anybody in particular; it just told the town that something had arrived, and the town could decide for itself what.

Ida watched them come. Old Ferro with his bad hip and his good opinions. The Marchetti twins, who were sixty and still finished each other's grudges. Sam Otieno from the drying racks, salt to his elbows. And behind them all, at a distance that was trying very hard to be casual, the Quintana girl — Wren — standing with her arms crossed like a woman guarding a door only she could see.

Ida had known her for four days now, which by Callow's Ferry arithmetic made her practically family and by any other arithmetic made her a stranger with a story, and Ida had learned to be careful about which kind you fed first.

"Well," she said to the assembled town, "nobody's dead, so you can all stop looking at me like that. Go on home. Ferro, you can stay, you never listen anyway."

The crowd thinned with the particular slowness of people hoping to overhear something worth repeating. Ida let them. A town that wasn't on any ledger ran on two currencies — water and talk — and she was rich in both.

* * *

The girl came to her house that evening, because of course she did. They always did. Ida had kept a pot of beans going for thirty years on the theory that sooner or later everyone in the Empty Quarter would come to her kitchen with their hands out and their secrets in their pockets, and she wanted to be ready.

Wren sat at the table with her hands wrapped around a cup she wasn't drinking, and said, "Hale offered me a deal."

"I know."

"You know?"

"Girl, I know what the man ate for breakfast before he ever crossed my river. I know he sleeps on his left side and lies on his right. I know he offered you your debt and your father's name in a clean little envelope, and I know he's sitting down at the landing right now in that gray coat of his, looking patient, because patience is the only weapon he owns that works."

Wren's jaw tightened. "He said the name could be cleared. Quietly. No tribunals, no lawyers, no — " she gestured at the room, at the town, at the whole unmapped sprawl of it — "no *this*. Just paper. Clean paper."

"Mm." Ida ladled beans into a bowl and set it in front of her, because a hungry person's conscience is a rumor and a fed person's conscience is a fact. "And what did the paper cost?"

"Delacroix's silence. The logs. Everything." Wren looked at the bowl like it had asked her a question. "It cost the river towns their water for another ten years, is what it cost. I did the arithmetic."

"And?"

"And I told him I'd think about it." The girl's chin came up, defiant, waiting for the blow. "I told him I'd *think about it*, Ida. I sat there and I let him believe it. Because if I said no, he stops offering and starts doing, and I wanted him offering a little longer. I wanted him *comfortable*."

Ida laughed — a short, surprised bark that startled even her. "Well. Hell." She sat down across from her. "You let him believe it."

"I'm a chaser," Wren said, as if that explained everything, and maybe it did. "I read what a storm wants and I let it believe it's winning right up until the moment it isn't. It's the only skill I have."

"It's a good one." Ida tore bread and pushed half across the table. "Better than mine. My skill is outlasting people, and it's slow work, and I'm running out of years to do it in."

* * *

Later, when the beans were gone and the lamp was low, Ida got out the tin box.

It wasn't a secret box. Half the town had seen it; it lived on top of the icebox where anyone could reach it, which was the point. It held the things Callow's Ferry kept instead of money — her mother's ferry license from a state that had since redrawn itself twice, the first survey team's group photograph with its corners soft as cloth, and the land grant, the one with the signature that had made Wren Quintana go white as chalk dust four days ago.

She laid it on the table between them now, and watched the girl look at it.

"My grandfather took this off a lawyer," Ida said. "Lawyer came upriver in 1919 with a satchel full of these, buying water rights from people who couldn't read what they were signing. My grandfather could read. He made the lawyer read it out loud, every clause, in front of the whole landing, and then he kept the paper and sent the man home in his own boat with his own oars and a week of food, because my grandfather was a hard man but not a cruel one." She tapped the grant. "This is the original of the same scheme. Different century, same thirst. Your father's name is on it because they needed a hydrologist who could make the theft look like arithmetic, and he was the best arithmetic in the territory. And then he read his own numbers closely enough to see what they added up to, and he tried to say so, and they erased him for it."

Wren's hand hovered over the signature without touching it. "You knew. When I told you my name."

"I knew the name Quintana from the grant. I didn't know it was yours until you flinched." Ida folded her hands. "Here's what I've been deciding for four days, girl, and I'll say it plain because plain is all I've got left. Hale has offered you a trade. Your father's good name for the river's bad decade. And I want you to understand something before you answer him, whatever you answer him."

"Which is?"

"Your father is dead. The river isn't." Ida let that sit. "I loved my two husbands more than I loved the sunrise, and they're both under the cottonwood, and I will tell you honestly that there were years I'd have traded the whole town's future for one more hour of either of them. Grief will sign anything. That's how men like Hale feed. They don't buy your loyalty. They buy your grief, because grief always thinks it's getting a bargain."

Wren's eyes were wet and furious and she didn't wipe them, which Ida approved of. "So I should let his name stay dirt."

"I should let *nothing* stay what it is. That's the whole point." Ida leaned forward. "You're thinking of this like a debt, because you've spent your whole adult life in debt and it's the only grammar you know. But listen to me. What you owe isn't to the dead man's name. Names are for gravestones and lawyers. What you owe is to the living. To Sam's drying racks. To the Marchetti girls' grandchildren. To every kid in this town who drinks water that some man in a gray coat is selling back to them at a markup." She tapped the table once, hard. "Your father's inheritance isn't his signature. It's the fact that he *noticed*. That's what he left you. The noticing. And Hale can't buy that off you, and he knows it, and that's why he's still sitting at my landing looking patient."

* * *

The next morning Ida walked the landing with the bell rope in her hand and rang it again, properly this time, and the town came, and this time she let them stay.

"Hale's still down at the south anchorage," she told them. "He's got two men, a skiff, and a briefcase. He's offering our guest a trade, and the trade is our water. Now, I'm not going to tell anybody what to think — " Ferro snorted; she ignored him with the ease of long practice — "because this town's never been told what to think and it's the only reason it's still here. But I'm going to tell you what we *are*."

She looked down the line of them. Sam, salt-elbowed. The twins. Ferro. The young ones at the back pretending they weren't listening, listening with their whole spines.

"We are the town that isn't on the ledger. Every one of us or our people came here because somewhere downstream there was a paper that said they didn't exist, or owed too much, or belonged to somebody. And this river took us in. Not because it's kind — it drowned my uncle and it'll drown somebody else before the season's out — but because it doesn't keep accounts. It just runs." She held up the land grant. "That man down there wants to start keeping accounts. He wants to buy a girl's grief and call it a transaction, and then he wants to sell us our own river by the barrel for ten more years, and then he wants the glacier to close the door behind him so nobody ever has to hear about it again."

"So what do we do?" That was one of the young ones — Pia Marchetti's boy, sixteen, all elbows.

Ida smiled. "We do what we've always done. We take in strays and we don't sell them." She turned to Wren, standing at the edge of the crowd with her storm-chaser's stillness, the look of a woman who had been ready to leave for so long that staying felt like a foreign country. "The boats are yours. All nine, and the runners to pole them, and the high-water route up the gorge that isn't written down anywhere and won't be. Delacroix's testimony rides in the lead boat. The logs ride in the second. And you — " she pointed the rolled grant at Wren like a schoolmarm's ruler — "you ride wherever you're most useful, and not one foot downstream until the gates are open and the water's moving."

Wren stared at her. "The glacier's calving. The canyon window is — "

"Nine days. Yes, I can count, and so can the mountain." Ida handed her the grant. "So here's the arithmetic your father was too honest to fudge and Hale is too greedy to do. Nine days, nine boats, one river that wants to run free. That's not a debt, girl. That's a current. And a current only moves one direction."

The town was already moving — Sam to the racks to salt fish for the passage, the twins to the boat sheds, Ferro to complain about everything, which was how Ferro blessed a thing. The bell was still humming in its cracked bronze throat.

And down at the south anchorage, small and gray and patient, Ovid Hale stepped out of his skiff and began, unhurried, to walk toward the landing — and Ida Callow, who had buried two husbands and outlasted everything else, rolled up her sleeves and went down to meet him.
