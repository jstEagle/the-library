## 14. The Ascent of the Source Canyon

The mountain woke at dawn, the way old men do — with a groan, a crack of joints, and no apology to anyone sleeping nearby.

Wren had been awake for an hour when it happened. She'd learned in her first night above Callow's Ferry that the glacier kept its own schedule, and that if you wanted to climb through its gorge you had better learn it too. The first calving of the day came down somewhere behind them, a sound like God dropping a filing cabinet into a bathtub, and the echo rolled up the canyon and rattled the pebbles at her feet.

"Right on time," she said.

Delacroix was already up, sitting cross-legged on his bedroll with his hands folded, watching the light come down the gorge wall like a man waiting for a bus he didn't fully believe in. He'd been like that all morning: calm, tidy, unnervingly present. Four years alone in the high country had either broken him or polished him, and Wren still hadn't decided which.

"You count them," Delacroix said. It wasn't a question. He'd been listening to her count for two days.

"I count them."

"The big ones or the little ones?"

"All of them. Little ones tell you the mountain's talking. Big ones tell you what it's saying." She cinched her pack. "You were a surveyor. You should know this."

"I surveyed rivers," Delacroix said mildly. "Rivers don't throw rocks at you. Well." A pause. "Not on purpose."

Marisol came around the bend of the talus slope with the last of the water bottles slung over her shoulder, moving with the economical grace of someone who had spent her whole life being paid to arrive places slightly before anyone expected her. She looked up at the gorge — a slot of gray ice and black stone climbing toward a white line of glacier far overhead — and made a face Wren had last seen on people reading their own medical charts.

"That's our road," Marisol said. Not a question either. Everyone in this party had stopped asking questions sometime around yesterday afternoon; they'd all just started stating facts at each other until the facts arranged themselves into a plan.

"That's our road," Wren agreed.

"It gets narrower."

"It does."

"And there is a great deal of ice above it."

"There is."

Marisol set the water down. "I want it noted, for the record, that I once had a perfectly good skiff, and a salary, and a pension I was three years from vesting."

"Noted," said Delacroix. "Also noted: your pension was being paid by people who steal rivers."

Marisol considered this. "The record can stand as is," she said, and picked the water back up.

* * *

They climbed.

There was no other way to describe it that did it justice, so Wren tried not to describe it at all while she was doing it. Description required attention, and attention required spare capacity, and she had none. Every ounce of her went into her boots and her hands and the small animal arithmetic of *where does my foot go next*.

The gorge floor was a staircase of boulders the size of trucks, tumbled by centuries of calving, and between the boulders ran the river itself — shallow here, quick, glassy with snowmelt, singing over stone in a voice that never quite let you forget it was cold enough to stop your heart in four minutes. They climbed beside it, over it, occasionally through it, and always, always under it — because the true river, the deep one, ran overhead in the glacier, blue-white and groaning, a hundred meters of compressed winter pressing down on the canyon like a hand deciding whether to close.

The mountain breathed. That was the thing nobody told you about glaciers. They weren't silent. They creaked and ticked and settled in the sun, and every few hours — longer in the cold of night, shorter as the day warmed — something gave way somewhere above and came down.

So Wren counted. She'd been counting since the second hour. Crack, rumble, fall, echo. Then silence, and in the silence, the ticking of the ice adjusting, and then the long held breath of the mountain, and then — if you listened — a rhythm. Not a schedule exactly. Glaciers didn't keep schedules. But a temperament. A mood. And the mood this morning was talkative.

"Big one coming," she said, without breaking stride.

"How do you know?" Marisol asked from behind.

"Because the little ones stopped."

Delacroix, ahead of her, had already flattened himself against the canyon wall, in the lee of a boulder furred with frost. Wren wedged in beside him. Marisol took the third spot with the practiced speed of someone who had decided, somewhere back down the trail, that arguing with Wren Quintana about weather and ice was a fight she could not win and no longer wished to.

Ten seconds later the mountain cleared its throat.

It was not the biggest calving Wren had heard. But it was close — close enough that the air came down the gorge ahead of the ice, a cold slap of displaced wind that smelled of rock flour and ancient nothing — and then the fall itself, a white avalanche of house-sized blocks pouring off the glacier's snout somewhere below their route, hitting the valley floor with a boom she felt in her teeth, in her sternum, in the fillings she could no longer afford to maintain.

Dust boiled up the canyon. Pebbles rained. Somewhere a boulder the size of a chapel rolled twice and settled.

Then silence, ringing and total.

"Huh," said Delacroix, after a moment.

"What?" Wren asked.

"I used to think," he said, brushing dust off his sleeves with fastidious little motions, "that after four years alone I would have forgotten how to be afraid. It turns out I simply hadn't been introduced to anything worth the trouble." He peered out at the settling dust. "I would like to formally introduce myself."

"Delacroix," said Marisol, "meet the glacier."

"Pleased," said Delacroix, and stepped back onto the route.

* * *

They made the narrows by midday.

Wren had known they were coming — Ida had described them, and Ida's runners had drawn them on the map in pencil, with the particular economy of people who drew maps for a living and hated every unnecessary line. But knowing a thing and standing in front of it were different animals entirely.

The gorge pinched to perhaps six meters wide. Above, the walls leaned together, and the glacier rode the gap like a keel — a hundred meters of ice directly overhead, sagging into the slot, veined with meltwater channels that caught the sun and glowed a blue so pure it looked manufactured. Water dripped off it in a steady percussion. And across the floor of the narrows, blocking half the passage, lay the fresh debris of the morning's calving: a fan of ice blocks, some of them taller than Wren, steaming faintly in the sun, melting even as they sat there, ticking and cracking as they released four hundred years of stored pressure one bubble at a time.

"We go through that," Marisol said flatly.

"We go through that."

"Under the glacier. Through the ice it just dropped on us. While it continues to drop more ice."

"When you say it like that it sounds bad."

"How else would I say it?"

"Fast," said Wren, and started picking her way into the debris field.

Here was the thing she had figured out, somewhere in the second hour of the climb, and which she now carried with her like a coin in her pocket: fear didn't actually go away. She'd spent her whole life assuming courage meant the fear leaving — that brave people simply had less of it, the way some trucks had bigger engines. But climbing under a calving glacier taught you otherwise. The fear stayed. It stayed the whole time, a hot wire down the center of her chest. What changed was what she did with it. She fed it into the counting. Crack, rumble, fall, echo. Silence. Ticking. Breath. Move during the breath. Freeze during the voice. The terror became a metronome, and the metronome became a rhythm, and the rhythm became, absurdly, almost a comfort — because a thing with a rhythm was a thing you could dance with, and a thing you could dance with was a thing that hadn't killed you yet.

She called the breaks. She called the moves. And to her mild astonishment, the other two fell into it — Marisol with the crisp obedience of a woman who had spent twenty years taking orders and had finally found some worth taking, and Delacroix with the serene precision of a man who had memorized the evidence of a decade's theft and saw no reason why a glacier should interrupt his composure now.

"Left foot on the gray one," Wren said. "Not the white one. The white one rolls."

"The white one looks stable."

"The white one is a liar. Gray one."

Marisol put her boot on the gray one. The gray one held. Behind them, the white one shifted six inches with a sound like a sighing whale, and Marisol looked back at it with an expression of profound personal betrayal.

"You knew that," she said.

"I spend my life reading things nobody else reads. Clouds. Rivers. Rocks." Wren hauled herself up onto the next block. "It's the only marketable skill I have, and mostly the market doesn't want it."

"Your market," said Delacroix from above, "is about to change."

* * *

They ate at the far side of the narrows, in a hollow beneath an overhang where the glacier's shadow reached down and touched the river — the only cold shade in a canyon otherwise baking in the high-altitude sun. Meltwater in the bottles, jerky from Ida's stores, a handful of the Ferry's dense dark bread that tasted of woodsmoke and kept forever.

Delacroix ate slowly, facing upstream, and after a while Wren realized he was crying. Quietly, without drama, the way a man cries who has been alone so long that tears no longer require an audience or an excuse.

"The headworks?" she asked gently.

He shook his head. "The bread." He laughed, wet and cracked. "Ida Callow's bread. My grandmother baked like this. Before." He turned the heel of the loaf in his fingers. "I used to think, up there, hiding, that if I ever got out I'd want — I don't know. Justice. Headlines. To see Hale's face." He shook his head. "Turns out what I want is bread. Somebody's bread. Made by somebody whose name I know."

Wren looked away, up the gorge, because there are moments you honor best by pretending to check the weather.

Which was when she checked the weather.

The glacier's snout was visible from here — a white wall two kilometers upstream where the ice poured over the canyon's shoulder and terminated in a cliff of rotten seracs, leaning towers of ice stacked like dirty dishes. Even as she watched, one of the towers shrugged, shed a slab the size of a barn, and sent a plume of powder drifting down the gorge.

And below the snout, where the route climbed toward the diversion works, she could see the shelf — the narrow bench of rock and old moraine that Ida's runners said threaded along the gorge wall, the only way up to the dam. And she could see, even at this distance, that parts of the bench were already gone. Fresh scars. Ice-scar. This morning's work, and possibly yesterday's.

"Marisol," she said.

"I see it."

"The bench is shorter than the map says."

"Maps," said Marisol, "are opinions." She screwed the cap back on her bottle. "How much shorter?"

Wren estimated. Hated the estimate. Said it anyway. "Maybe a kilometer of it's been chewed off. We'll see when we get there."

"And if it's gone?"

"Then we climb the wall."

Marisol followed her gaze up the gorge wall — sheer black granite, four hundred meters of it, streaked with ice — and then back to Wren, and Wren watched her do the same arithmetic everyone did, the arithmetic of *this woman is insane but she keeps being right*, and watched her lose the argument with herself in record time.

"The wall," Marisol said. "Fine. The wall. But I want it noted—"

"—noted for the record, yes," said Delacroix. "Your pension. Your skiff. Your three years to vesting."

"My dignity," Marisol said. "I want my dignity noted. I had one, once. It's in a skiff somewhere downstream, and I intend to get both back."

* * *

The afternoon went long and golden and terrible.

They climbed out of the narrows and onto the open bench, and the bench was worse than the narrows in every way except width, because on the bench there was nowhere to hide. When the mountain spoke — and it spoke more often now, the sun working on the ice like a hand working a loose tooth — there was nothing to do but press flat against the rock and wait and count, and hope the falling ice chose the river and not the path.

Twice it nearly didn't.

The first time, a block came off the wall above them — not glacier ice but old avalanche debris, cemented to the cliff and finally letting go — and Wren heard it before anyone, that particular dry click of stone parting from stone, and screamed *down* and they all dropped, and the block went over their heads close enough that the wind of it moved Delacroix's hair, and smashed the bench thirty meters ahead, exactly where they would have been walking.

Nobody said anything for a moment. The river sang. The glacier ticked.

"Well," said Delacroix at last, getting up and dusting himself off. "That was the mountain voting."

"On what?"

"On whether we turn back." He looked at the shattered bench, then at Wren, with eyes that had gone very clear and very calm. "It voted no. So did I. Carry on."

The second time was worse, because it wasn't one block, it was the whole mood changing. Mid-afternoon, with the sun full on the glacier's face, the little ticks stopped — all of them, everywhere, the entire canyon going quiet in a way that raised every hair Wren owned — and she stood stock-still on the bench with her hand up, and Marisol froze mid-step, and Delacroix went still as a heron, and for eleven seconds the largest object any of them had ever stood beneath held its breath.

Then it exhaled.

The calving came off the snout two kilometers upstream, but it was enormous — a whole serac wall folding, a white cataract pouring down the icefall, and the report of it came rolling up the gorge like thunder learning to walk, and the wind of it hit them a full minute later, hot and cold at once, smelling of crushed stone, and the river below them jumped and browned with the shockwave of whatever had landed in it far above.

When the echo died, Wren realized she was shaking. Not from cold. From the wire in her chest, the hot one, the one that never left — it had gone taut as a mooring line in a gale.

But her feet, she noticed, had already resumed the rhythm. Step. Step. Breathe. Because that was the secret she'd carry out of this canyon if she carried anything: the body learns faster than the fear. The body hears the mountain breathing and matches it, and somewhere below thought, below terror, below even hope, the rhythm takes over and simply refuses to stop.

Step. Step. Breathe.

"Talk to me," Marisol said quietly, falling in beside her. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm timing us to the mountain."

"Is that a real thing or a Wren thing?"

"Those are increasingly the same category."

Marisol huffed — almost a laugh, the first one in two days — and adjusted her pack straps. "My instructor at the Syndicate academy used to say, *the mountain doesn't care about your plans.*"

"He was right," Wren said. "But he left out the good news."

"Which is?"

"The mountain doesn't care about its own plans either. It improvises. Which means—" she nodded up the gorge, toward the white line of the icefall, toward the hidden dam and the stolen river and everything her father had drawn and everything the Syndicate had built on top of his drawings, "—there's always a gap in the pattern. You just have to be small enough and stubborn enough to fit through it."

Ahead of them, Delacroix had stopped. He was standing at the point where the bench ended — really ended, sheared off into fresh rubble and empty air, exactly as far as Wren's morning estimate and no farther — and beyond it the gorge wall rose sheer, streaked with ice, four hundred meters of granite between them and the diversion works.

He turned around. In the gold late light, with the glacier looming and groaning above and the river roaring below, he looked, for the first time since the graves, like a surveyor again: a man with an instrument, taking a fix.

"Gentlemen," he said. "Ladies. The good news is, I remember the way in. Every door, every ladder, every gallery — it's all up here." He tapped his temple. "The bad news is, the way in starts with that wall."

Above them, right on cue, the mountain drew a long slow breath.

Wren looked up at the ice-streaked granite, and down at the river, and at the two people she had, through no sensible process whatsoever, come to trust with her life.

"Breathe in," she said. "We climb on the exhale."
