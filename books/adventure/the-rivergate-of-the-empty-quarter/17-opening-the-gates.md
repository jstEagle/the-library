## 17. Opening the Gates

The mountain had been talking all morning, and by the time they reached the last switchback above the diversion works, it had stopped talking and started shouting.

Wren had spent her whole life listening to weather. She knew the vocabulary of thunder, the grammar of wind, the way a supercell cleared its throat before it spoke. But she had never heard anything like the source canyon in calving season. The glacier above them groaned and cracked and let go in long, splintering exhalations, and each time it did, the sound came down the gorge like a freight train running on rails of stone. Between the booms there was a hiss — the meltwater, the river itself, restless under its roof of ice.

"Nine days," Marisol said beside her, flat as a ledger line. "Ida's runner said nine. I'm starting to think the runner was an optimist."

"Runners usually are," said Delacroix.

It was the first thing he'd said in an hour. He had climbed the whole gorge with his arms wrapped around himself, murmuring, and Wren had assumed he was praying, or counting, or doing whatever a man did who had spent a year memorizing the contents of his own mind because paper burns. Now he stopped on the trail and looked up at the glacier with an expression Wren could only call professional.

"Calving is rhythmic," he said. "Listen. Big release, then a rest, then two small ones, then the rest again. It's a pattern. It's been the same pattern since yesterday." He turned to her, and his eyes, which had been swimming since the last camp, were suddenly, terribly clear. "You can climb between the sentences."

Wren looked at Marisol. Marisol looked at Wren.

"The surveyor's gone mad," Marisol said.

"The surveyor's gone *useful*," Wren said. "There's a difference. Come on."

* * *

The diversion works sat in a bowl of granite at the head of the gorge, and from the switchbacks above it looked almost innocent — a low concrete spine across the river's throat, a cluster of buildings with solar arrays glinting on their roofs, a canal gate yawning to the east where the water went to be sold. The Syndicate had built it the way they built everything: to look, from a distance, like nothing at all.

Up close, it looked like a wound. The river came down from the glacier fat and white and cold, hit the dam, and vanished. What continued below was a trickle, an apology, a river in name only. Wren stood on the rim of the bowl and thought of every dry well she'd passed in the flats, every cracked cistern in every town that wasn't on the ledger, and felt something in her chest go very still and very hot.

"That's my father's dam," she said. She hadn't meant to say it out loud.

Marisol glanced at her. "It's the Syndicate's dam."

"It's his engineering. Ida showed me the drawings. He signed them when he still thought he was building a reservoir for drought years." Wren watched a pump station shudder and cycle, drinking. "They just never stopped drinking."

There were eight of them down there. They'd counted from the ridge: eight staff, armed, bored, moving between the buildings on a schedule so fixed you could have set a watch by it. Bored was good. Bored meant they had never once, in all the years of the theft, expected the river to answer back.

"Plan?" Marisol asked. She said it the way she said everything, without inflection, but she had already checked her rifle's action twice, and Wren had learned to read her the way she read weather — in the small movements, the pressure changes.

"Delacroix's rhythm," Wren said. "We go down during the rests. You take the pump house — you're the one who knows Syndicate hardware. I take the gate controls. We open the gates, kill the pumps, and let the river do the rest."

"And the eight?"

"We don't fight them. We flood them out. Nobody has to die today." She paused. "Except maybe us."

"Cheerful."

"I'm a storm chaser. Cheerful is a weather condition, not a personality."

Delacroix made a sound that might have been a laugh. It was rusty, like a gate itself, but it was a laugh.

* * *

They went down between the sentences.

It was the strangest climb of Wren's life — a descent timed to a glacier's breathing, the three of them moving in the long silences, freezing against the rock when the mountain inhaled to speak. Once, halfway down, a calving came early, a house-sized slab of blue ice shearing off the face and detonating on the rocks a hundred meters to their left, and the spray of it hit Wren like a thrown bucket, cold enough to hurt, and she pressed herself flat and thought, with a strange detached clarity: *this is what it costs, this is what the water costs, and I have been paying in the wrong currency my whole life.*

Then the rest came, and they moved.

They reached the dam spine without a shot fired. Marisol peeled off toward the pump house, moving with the economical grace of a woman who had spent her career being underestimated, and Wren went along the concrete crest toward the gate controls, Delacroix at her heels carrying the survey's recovered logs in a waterproof case strapped to his back like a hump, like a burden he had chosen and would not put down.

The gate control room was a shed of a building with a single operator inside — a young man, younger than Wren, eating noodles out of a tin, who looked up when the door opened and said, "You're not on the rotation."

"No," Wren agreed. "I'm the river."

She hated how it sounded the moment she said it. But the kid just blinked, and then Delacroix stepped past her, and something in the surveyor's face — the year of it, the graves of it, the memorized dead of it — made the young man decide, with the speed of the genuinely sensible, that his noodles were elsewhere. He went out the back door and did not run, exactly. He walked very fast toward the barracks, which was the same thing with a delay on it.

"Two minutes," Marisol's voice said in Wren's ear, over the short-band. "The pumps are beautiful, by the way. Genuinely. He over-engineered the whole intake array. It's obscene."

"He always did over-engineer," Wren said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it.

The gate controls were analog — deliberately so, she realized, because analog couldn't be audited remotely, couldn't be subpoenaed, couldn't testify. A bank of levers, a board of brass gauges, a hand-painted placard: *GATE 1 — GATE 4 — EMERGENCY SPILL — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY.* Below the placard, in smaller letters, someone had added in marker, years ago: *and what did you do to become authorized?*

Her father's handwriting. She knew it the way she knew her own pulse.

Wren put her hand on the emergency spill lever and found it was shaking, and made it stop shaking, and pulled.

* * *

The river came home.

There was no other way to describe it. The gates ground up and the water that had been held at the dam's throat for a decade — backed up, banked, bottled — went through the spill channel and down the gorge in a brown-and-white wall, and the sound it made was not a sound, it was a *voice*, it was the voice of a thing that had been made to whisper and remembered it could shout.

On the short-band, Marisol was laughing. Actually laughing. "The pumps are down! I killed the intakes — Wren, the whole canal's going dry, they're going to find a mud puddle in the aqueduct and think God did it —"

"Ida's boats," Wren said. "How long until it reaches the lower gorge?"

"Fast water, narrow channel — an hour. Less." A pause. "Wren. The staff. They're evacuating toward the east road. All but one."

"Who?"

"Hale."

* * *

She found him in the records annex, which was the last building on the compound and the only one he hadn't left.

The annex was a long concrete room lined with shelving, and the shelving was full — ledgers, thousands of them, a decade of the river's theft recorded in careful columns: volumes delivered, towns shorted, prices per acre-foot, the whole arithmetic of thirst in double-entry. Water rising through the doorway. Ankle-deep already, and rising fast, because the spill channel she'd opened ran right past the annex's foundation, and the building was old, and the building was giving up.

Hale stood in the middle of it in his good coat, hauling ledgers off the lower shelves and stacking them on a table, and he looked up at Wren with an expression she had never seen on him before. Not the fixer's smoothness. Not the salesman's warmth. Something raw underneath, like bedrock after a flood.

"They're the only proof," he said. "The originals. Everything else — the copies, the tribunal filings, the testimony — it can be disputed. These can't. These are the Syndicate's own hand. If they go under, the case goes under. The towns get nothing. You understand? I'm not saving the crime. I'm saving the *evidence* of it."

Wren stood in the doorway, water at her boots, and looked at him.

She thought about the arithmetic. That was the thing she'd learned from him, in a way — everything was arithmetic, every choice a ledger, every mercy a transaction with a hidden fee. And the arithmetic said: let him drown, or at least let the ledgers drown, because the ledgers had been built to weigh people, and she had spent her whole adult life being weighed by them.

Then she thought about Delacroix, who had carried the truth in his head for a year because paper burns. About Ida, who kept a town alive by refusing to count it. About her father, whose handwriting was on a placard in a gate shed, asking what anyone had done to become authorized.

"You're right," she said. "They're evidence. So they don't belong to you, and they don't belong to the Syndicate. They belong to the tribunal." She grabbed a stack off the table. "Grab an armful. The rest can testify."

For a moment — one moment — Hale's face did something complicated, something almost like gratitude, and Wren understood, with a lurch, that he had believed in something once. That the Syndicate's founders had believed in something once. That belief was a river too, and it could be dammed and diverted until it drowned the very people it was meant to save.

Then the shelf behind him let go.

It went all at once — the water undermining the footing, a decade of paper weight shifting — and Hale went with it, reaching, always reaching, for the ledgers, and the brown water took him the way the river takes anything that stands in the wrong place at the wrong time: completely, and without malice, and forever.

Wren got one armful of ledgers out. She got them up the slope and into Delacroix's waterproof case, and she stood there gasping, watching the annex roof go under, and she felt — she searched herself for triumph, for vengeance satisfied, for the hot clean burn she had been carrying since Ida's kitchen table, since her father's signature — and she found none of it.

What she found was grief. For Hale, for the believer under the fixer. For her father, whose dream this had been before it became a theft. For the river, which had not wanted any of this and had carried it all anyway.

The water wasn't revenge. She understood that now, standing in the spray with her arms full of someone else's crimes. The water was just the water, going home. Everything else — the fury, the debt, the dead — that was what people did with the water. And she was done doing that.

* * *

They climbed out of the bowl during a rest in the calving, the three of them, moving between the mountain's sentences, and behind them the source canyon ran loud and white and free.

From the ridge they could see down the gorge, miles of it, and far below — a speck at first, then a shape, then unmistakably — boats. Ida's runners, riding the returning river down the narrow channel, poles and paddles flashing, and even at this distance Wren could hear the cheering, or imagined she could, which amounted to the same thing.

"They made it," Marisol said. She was looking at the boats with an expression Wren couldn't read, and didn't try to. "The evidence rides with them. The logs, the ledgers, Delacroix's memory. By tomorrow it's in every river town from here to the tribunal's jurisdiction."

"And the Syndicate's lawyers," Wren said, "will be right behind it."

"Right behind it," Marisol agreed. "Which is why I'm going to be ahead of it." She turned, and for the first time since the skiff on the flats, since the warning at the camp, since the whole long leash of this job, she looked Wren in the eye and held it. "There's a federal water tribunal sitting in Vane in three weeks. I'm going to walk in and give them everything. Names. Dates. My own service record. All of it." A breath. "They'll want me to trade. Everyone trades. I'm not going to trade."

"Marisol—"

"Someone has to be the arithmetic," she said. "Might as well be the one who did the counting."

She started down the trail toward the boats, then stopped, and without turning around said: "Your father's drawings are still hanging in the control room. I couldn't take them down. Didn't seem right." And she was gone, down between the mountain's sentences, steady as a metronome.

Wren stood on the ridge with Delacroix beside her, the glacier groaning its long exhalations, the river running loud below, and she looked up at the ice — at the vast blue face of it, already leaning, already letting go, slab by slab by slab, closing the canyon behind them the way a book closes when the story is finally told.

"Nine days," she said.

"Fewer," said Delacroix, with the calm of a man who had already survived the worst arithmetic there was. "The mountain's in a hurry. I think it wants to keep the secret."

"Let it," Wren said. "We're taking the truth out the front way."

The next calving came early — a crack like the sky splitting, a bloom of white at the glacier's foot — and they ran for the boats, and the mountain, breathing its great breaths behind them, began to close the door.
