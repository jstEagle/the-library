## 18. The Mountain Closes the Door

The mountain announced itself the way an old man announces his opinions: slowly at first, then all at once, and with no interest whatsoever in your rebuttal.

Wren heard it before she saw it — a sound like the sky clearing its throat, deep and wet and enormous, rolling down the gorge and bouncing off walls that had been listening to that voice for ten thousand years. She stopped on the scree shelf, one hand on a boulder still cold from some earlier century, and turned to look back the way they'd come.

The source canyon was breathing its last.

Above the headworks — above what was left of them, which was mostly wreckage and steam and a spreading stain of released water — the glacier's snout had begun to let go. Seracs the size of grain silos leaned out over the gorge like drunks deciding whether to sit down. And then one of them did. The crack was a pistol shot followed by a cathedral falling over, and the ice went down into the canyon mouth in a white avalanche that swallowed the light.

The spray reached her thirty seconds later, a cold mist that beaded on her eyelashes and tasted of stone and old winters. Beside her, Delacroix sat down where he stood, cross-legged on the rock, and watched it with the calm of a man who had already survived the worst thing that would ever happen to him and found everything since to be a kind of bonus material.

"It doesn't hurt," he said, conversationally. "Being buried. The ice is very polite about it."

"Delacroix." Wren crouched next to him. "Look at me. It's not burying you."

"Not today." He smiled. His beard was full of frost and grit and, somehow, a small yellow flower he must have picked somewhere in the last mile without her noticing. "Today it's burying everything else. Which is better, if you think about it. A grave for the evidence. A grave for the ledgers Hale was so fond of. A grave for—" he gestured vaguely upstream, at the drowned machinery, at the whole architecture of theft now dissolving under meltwater and rubble— "all of it. Very tidy. The mountain does housekeeping."

Marisol came up the trail behind them at a jog, skiff keys still in her fist as though there were anywhere left to drive a skiff. She stopped beside Wren, chest heaving, and stared at the calving face with an expression Wren was beginning to recognize — the look of a soldier watching a strategy die in real time and discovering she didn't mind.

"That's it," Marisol said. "That's the whole case, going under the ice."

"No," Wren said. "That's the *paperwork* going under the ice."

She said it more firmly than she felt. Because here was the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the gates blew: every original document — the survey logs, the diversion schematics, her father's drawings, the land grant with his signature sitting on it like a scar — every physical proof of what the Vane Syndicate had done for ten years was now either underwater, under rubble, or under several hundred thousand tons of very determined glacier. The tribunal lawyers downstream would want originals. Tribunals always wanted originals. Originals were how you proved a thing to men who made their living not believing things.

And the mountain had just eaten theirs.

Another serac went. This one farther east, calving off the shoulder of the snout, and the boom rolled through the gorge like a door slamming in a very large house. Dust boiled up, hung, and began to settle over the new icefall — a chaos of blue-white blocks jammed against each other, already refreezing at their fractured edges, welding themselves into a wall no boat, no climber, no Syndicate salvage crew was getting through until next summer. Maybe not even then. Glaciers were bad correspondents. They did not answer letters asking when they planned to reopen.

"The route's closed," Marisol said quietly. "For the season. For maybe longer."

"For good, by the look of the fracture lines." Wren shaded her eyes. "Ida will want to know the exact hour. She'll want it written down."

"She writes everything down," Delacroix agreed. "That woman has been keeping the river's diary for forty years. You think the Syndicate was the only one with records?" He tapped his own temple. "But mine are in here, and hers are in ink, and ink," he added, with sudden fierce satisfaction, "does not calve."

Wren laughed. It surprised her — a short, cracked sound, half sob, that she hadn't budgeted for. But it came anyway, and once it started it wouldn't stop right away, and she found she didn't want it to. She laughed at the absurdity of it: she had spent two years calculating interest payments to the decimal point, and now she was standing in freezing spray watching a fortune in evidence turn to scenery, and the only thing she could produce was laughter.

Because underneath the fear, underneath the grief, something in her had gone quiet. Genuinely quiet, for the first time in longer than she could remember.

It took her a moment to name it. Then she did, and the naming made it stranger and truer both:

Peace.

Not happiness. Not relief, exactly. Peace — the specific peace of a door closing. Of knowing that a version of events was now permanently unavailable to her. She would never stand in a hearing room and slide a water-stained logbook across a table and watch a lawyer's face fall. She would never hand her father's drawings to a federal clerk and receive, in exchange, a clean and simple absolution. That future had been buried along with the survey team's camp, along with the graves she'd stood over yesterday morning, along with the headworks where she'd found her father's engineering hanging framed on the control room wall like icons in a church built to a false god.

All of that was sealed now. Sealed as thoroughly as the canyon.

What remained was messier. What remained was people.

"You're doing it again," Marisol said.

"Doing what?"

"That face. The one where you're doing math nobody asked you to do." Marisol lowered herself onto the scree beside them, wincing as her knees registered their complaints. "I've watched you do it for three hundred miles. You total things up. Debts, distances, odds. So go ahead. Total this up. What do we actually have?"

Wren looked at her hands. They were cut and burned from the pump sabotage, shaking slightly, steady enough.

"We have testimony," she said. "We have Delacroix, who memorized four years of survey data because he understood that paper burns and memory needs a match. We have Ida Callow and forty years of flow records kept by a town that isn't supposed to exist. We have the runners' copies of the logs already moving downstream in waterproof satchels ahead of the Syndicate's lawyers. We have Marisol Vane-Rojas, who is about to walk into a federal tribunal and trade her own freedom for a list of names." She looked up. "And we have whatever my father left that I haven't found yet. Because men like him always leave something. It's practically a professional obligation."

"And none of it is an original document," Marisol said.

"No."

"So it might not hold."

"It might not." Wren turned to face her fully. "But here's what I learned from a mountain today. You can't keep a river by damming it. You can only slow it down. Truth works the same way. The Syndicate spent ten years building infrastructure around this one, and it held for exactly as long as it took three people and a town of unregistered boat-runners to decide otherwise." She nodded at the icefall, still settling, still groaning as it found its final shape. "Let them have the paper. Paper was always their religion. We've got witnesses."

Delacroix raised one finger, as if called on in class. "Also we have the flower," he said, holding up the small yellow bloom from his beard. "Which I intend to present to Ida as a diplomatic gift. She strikes me as a woman who respects a man who brings flowers to a war council."

"She'll make you plant it," Wren said.

"I know," he said happily. "That's why I chose it."

* * *

They descended through the afternoon, down out of the ice zone into the grey-green country of talus and stunted pine where the gorge widened and the river — the real river, the freed one — ran loud and brown and joyful with all the water it had been denied for a decade. Wren kept glancing back. She couldn't help it. Each time, the icefall looked smaller, then different, then simply like part of the landscape, as though it had always been there, as though the canyon had never been open at all.

By the time they reached the lower narrows where Ida's boats were waiting, the sun had dropped behind the western rim and the water was running gold. The boats were loaded, the runners were restless, and Ida Callow stood at the lead boat's stern like a figurehead who had opinions about the ship's heading.

She read their faces before they reached the bank. All of them. She was very good at that; Wren suspected it was the primary skill required for keeping a town alive off the books for forty years.

"The canyon's closed," Ida said. Not a question.

"Sealed by nightfall," Wren said. "Everything upstream of the narrows is under ice. The site, the graves, the headworks. All of it."

Ida was quiet a moment. Downriver, a runner laughed at something, the bright careless laugh of the young, and the sound carried across the water like a promise being kept by accident.

"My grandmother used to say," Ida said finally, "that the Empty Quarter keeps its own archives. Every spring the flood takes a little more of the old banks, and every fall the wind buries a little more of the old roads. People used to fight it. Fenced their claims, filed their papers, put up markers." She spat, ceremonially, into the gold water. "The river took the fences, the county lost the papers, and the wind ate the markers. And the ones who lasted — the ones who built Callow's Ferry — were the ones who learned to carry what mattered *in* something instead of leaving it *on* something."

"In people," Wren said.

"In people." Ida looked at her then, straight on, with those eyes the color of the river in March. "You gave up a clean case today. Don't think I don't know it. There was a version of this where you walked into a courthouse with a box of documents and walked out with a verdict and everybody clapped. That version's under the ice now. What you've got instead is harder and slower and it might fail." She paused. "You look better than you have the whole time I've known you."

"I was just thinking the same thing," Wren admitted, "and feeling guilty about it."

"Guilt's for debts," Ida said. "And you don't owe anybody anything anymore. Get in the boat."

* * *

They ran the river through the dusk, the freed current carrying them fast, and Wren sat in the stern of Ida's lead boat with her father's compass in her pocket — the one thing of his she'd carried since she was twelve, the needle swinging lazily toward a north that had never once lied to her.

Behind them the mountains went dark. Somewhere up there, under new ice, lay the camp where the survey team had pitched their tents and logged their instruments and walked, in the end, into the water. Lay the graves she'd promised herself she'd come back and mark properly, and now couldn't, and understood — with a peace that still startled her — that the marking had never really been the point. The mountain would keep them better than any stone she could carve. It kept everything. It just wasn't taking visitors this year.

Ahead of them, downstream, the first lights of the river towns were coming on. Towns that had been drinking dust for a decade while their water ran through pipes marked with her father's signature. Towns full of people who had never signed anything, agreed to anything, or been consulted about anything — and who were about to hear, from a half-mad survivor and a defected pilot and a debt-ridden storm chaser, exactly what had been done to them.

No originals. No clean case. Just testimony, moving downstream faster than money for once.

Wren put her hand in her pocket and closed it around the compass.

"Hold together," she murmured to it — to him — "one more week."

The needle swung, settled, pointed home downriver, toward the tribunal, toward the reckoning — and toward the one person waiting at the end of it whom Wren had not yet told the truth to: the father who, according to the manifest Marisol had pulled from the Syndicate's own files, was not dead after all.
