## 20. The Reckoning at Vane

Marisol Vega had spent eleven years learning how to enter a room without being noticed. She could cross a Syndicate floor like a draft under a door, sign for cargo without the clerk looking up, stand at the back of a briefing until her name was called and no one could quite remember when she'd arrived.

The federal water tribunal in Vane City made that impossible.

There was a corridor — marble, or something pretending to be marble — and then a set of double doors with brass handles polished by ten thousand nervous hands, and beyond them a chamber shaped like an amphitheater had married a courtroom and neither was happy about it. Three commissioners sat on a raised dais beneath a seal of the Interior Water Authority: a river rendered as three wavy lines, which Marisol thought was generous. The real river had never once been allowed to look that simple.

She stood at the witness table with her hands flat on the wood because if she didn't give them a job they'd go to her sidearm out of habit, and bringing a sidearm into a tribunal chamber was the kind of thing that got a cooperating defendant's cooperation reclassified.

"State your name and former position," said the middle commissioner, a woman named Ostrander who had the eyes of someone who had read every page of everything and remembered where you'd lied.

"Marisol Vega. Formerly flight operations and asset retrieval, Vane Syndicate, High Desert Division."

"Formerly," Ostrander repeated, tasting the word.

"I resigned this morning," Marisol said. "Effective as of the moment I walked through your doors."

"There's no such thing as resigning from the Syndicate, Ms. Vega."

"There is now," Marisol said. "I'm doing it in front of witnesses."

Someone in the gallery laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed. Marisol didn't look to see who it was. She'd learned long ago not to spend attention on things she couldn't use.

* * *

They had given her a room the night before. Not a cell — a room, with a bed and a window that opened six inches and a carafe of water that tasted like it had come through municipal pipes, chlorinated and honest and nothing like river water, and Marisol had lain awake listening to the building settle and thinking about arithmetic.

That was what it always came down to, in the end. Not loyalty. Loyalty was the word the Syndicate used for arithmetic so lopsided you'd stopped checking it.

Eleven years. She'd joined at twenty-three because the Syndicate paid flight school debts the way other employers paid signing bonuses, and because her mother's dialysis clinic sat on a Syndicate water allotment and there were ways to make sure the pressure never dropped. She'd told herself it was family. It was family. It was also that the work was clean in its motions even when it was filthy in its purpose — fly the route, log the hours, retrieve the asset, don't ask what happens after retrieval. A soldier's life is mostly the discipline of not finishing sentences.

Then Wren Quintana had stood on a gravel bar in the Empty Quarter with river mud to her knees and said, *You knew. You've known for years, and you flew the routes anyway,* and Marisol had started finishing sentences.

Not all at once. That was the thing nobody understood about conscience. It wasn't lightning. It was seepage. It got into the mortar of you over years and then one day a wall came down and everyone acted surprised, including you.

* * *

"You've submitted a substantial evidentiary package," said the third commissioner — Calloway-Briggs, young, careful, the one who kept glancing at Marisol like she was a weather system he hadn't learned to read yet. "Survey logs. Photographic documentation of the diversion headworks. Engineering drawings bearing the signature of Elena Quintana. Testimony from a surviving member of the Meridian Survey. And ledgers."

"Ledgers," Marisol confirmed.

"Recovered from the headworks control room."

"Recovered from Hale's personal effects," Marisol said. "He was carrying them out. He prioritized them over, I'm told, his own footing on a wet catwalk." She let that sit. "I recovered them from the river. He did not resurface."

Ostrander wrote something. "You understand these ledgers document a decade of siphon operations. Volumes diverted by season. Purchasers. Shell allotments. Payments to officials in four counties."

"I can read," Marisol said, and heard the gallery shift — that dry rustle of people deciding whether they liked her. She didn't need them to like her. She needed them to believe her, and those were different trades with different prices.

"The Syndicate will argue you were a low-level operative acting under duress," Ostrander went on. "That your testimony reflects coercion. That your immunity request is self-serving."

"It is self-serving," Marisol said. "I'd like that on the record too. I'm not here because I'm good. I'm here because I did the math and the math finally came out against me. Eleven years of flying stolen water past towns that were buying it back from the people who stole it — I did that math every quarter and I balanced it. What changed wasn't my ethics. What changed was that I stopped being able to balance it." She paused. "But the fact that my reasons are ugly doesn't make the numbers wrong. Check the numbers."

Calloway-Briggs leaned toward his microphone. "Ms. Vega, the immunity framework before this tribunal offers protection from prosecution in exchange for full disclosure of principals. Is that your understanding?"

"That's why I'm here."

"And you're prepared to name every principal you have knowledge of."

Marisol looked at him for a moment. Then she reached into her jacket — slowly, telegraphing it, the way you move around jumpy people — and set a single folded paper on the table.

"This is a list of fourteen names," she said. "It's incomplete. It's what I know firsthand, verified, no rumor, no payback entries. I could have given you forty names and half of them would be garbage I wanted you to chase. I didn't, because garbage names are how guilty people buy time, and I've bought enough time." She slid it forward. "The complete list is in the ledgers' margin codes, which I've decoded and provided separately. Cross-reference me. If I missed anyone, charge me for the omission. That's the deal I want. Not absolution. Audit."

Ostrander's pen had stopped moving. "You're asking us to verify you rather than trust you."

"I'm asking you to do your job instead of mine," Marisol said. "Mine was following orders. Look how that turned out."

* * *

They broke for recess, which meant they put her in a side room with a window and a pitcher of water and a guard who pretended to read a newspaper. Marisol drank two glasses and stood at the window watching Vane City do its morning — delivery trucks, a tram, a man arguing cheerfully with a fruit vendor — and tried to feel like a person who had done something instead of a person who was about to find out what it cost.

Her comm buzzed. Unregistered relay, which meant one of maybe four people in the world.

**IDA CALLOW:** *Tribunal feed says you're holding. Delacroix wants to know if tribunal food is any good. He has opinions.*

Marisol almost smiled. **Tell him the coffee tastes like punishment. He'll feel vindicated.**

**IDA:** *Wren says: don't let them offer you a deal before lunch. They're gentler before lunch and you'll take worse terms.*

**MARISOL:** *Since when does Wren Quintana negotiate?*

**IDA:** *Since she grew up on the wrong end of contracts, girl. Listen to her.*

Marisol put the comm away. Outside, the fruit vendor won the argument. Small mercies.

* * *

Afternoon session. The Syndicate sent lawyers, which surprised no one, but they sent *good* lawyers — a silver-maned man named Aldous Frey who spoke like money changing hands and who had, Marisol knew, drafted three of the contracts she'd signed in her first year. He requested the floor.

"Tribunal members," Frey began, "the Vane Syndicate acknowledges the gravity of these allegations—"

"There it is," Marisol murmured, too low for the microphones. *Acknowledges.* Not denies. Acknowledges. The first word of every settlement.

"—and wishes to note that certain operational records may reflect unauthorized actions by mid-level personnel operating outside standard oversight protocols. The Syndicate has commissioned its own internal review—"

"With respect," Ostrander cut in, "your internal review would be conducted by individuals named in the evidence."

"The Syndicate disputes the provenance of that evidence."

"The evidence includes your own letterhead, Mr. Frey."

Frey adjusted his cuffs. Somewhere behind him, Marisol knew, a room full of principals was recalculating. She could picture it: the long table, the bottled water imported from two hundred miles away while the county aquifer dropped, men and women who genuinely believed — that was the part she'd finally understood, talking to Hale on the catwalk with the river roaring back into its own channel below them — who *genuinely believed* they were the only competent adults in a desert full of people who'd waste the water. Idealists, Hale had said, founders' idealists, people who thought control was care. The corruption hadn't been a fall from belief. The belief itself had rotted from the inside, quietly, the way a dam does, and everyone downstream had been assured right up until the morning of the flood.

"The tribunal recognizes Ms. Vega's cooperation," Frey said smoothly, "but questions the reliability of a witness negotiating immunity. We request her testimony be weighted accordingly."

"Weighted," Marisol said, before the commissioners could answer. "Mr. Frey, weight it however you like. Then explain to me why your client's diversion records show the town of Callow's Ferry buying back its own river at a markup, four years running, from a shell company whose registered agent is your client's general counsel. Weight that."

Silence. The good kind — the kind with weight of its own.

Frey looked at her, and for one unguarded second the lawyer fell away and Marisol saw the actual man underneath, tired, doing his own arithmetic, and she felt something almost like pity. Almost. She filed it under *later*, where she kept most of her softer feelings these days.

* * *

It took three days. Nobody warned her tribunals moved like glaciers — appropriate, she supposed, given the subject matter. They deposed her twice. They brought in hydrologists who argued about cubic feet per second with the passion other men reserved for scripture. They brought in the ledgers and made her walk them through the margin codes line by line, and on the second afternoon Ostrander looked up from a page and said, very quietly, "This entry references a survey team. Meridian. Marked *resolved*."

"Resolved means killed," Marisol said. "It's a logistics term. Asset resolution. I didn't write the vocabulary. I just had to learn it."

Ostrander held her gaze for a long moment. "And you flew support for resolution operations."

"Twice," Marisol said. "I told myself I was flying supply runs. The supplies were for the crews doing the resolving." Her hands were flat on the table again; she noticed she'd put them there. "I'm not asking this tribunal to weigh my guilt less. I'm asking it to weigh everyone's equally. Including mine. Put me on the list."

Ostrander blinked. "Ms. Vega, your name isn't—"

"Put me on the list," Marisol repeated. "Appendix. Footnote. Whatever keeps it on paper. I flew the routes. If the principals answer for the orders, I answer for the flights. Otherwise this whole proceeding is just the big fish eating the little ones in front of an audience, and I've seen enough of that to last a lifetime."

The chamber was quiet. Calloway-Briggs was writing fast. And Marisol understood, standing there, that this was the sentence she'd actually chosen — not the immunity, not the testimony, but this: the refusal to be handed a clean exit by the same machinery that had handed her dirty work for eleven years. Absolution was a product. The Syndicate sold it. So did tribunals, sometimes, in softer packaging. Accountability you couldn't buy. You could only pay for it, in installments, forever.

"Note the witness's request," Ostrander said at last, to the clerk. "For the record."

"For the record," the clerk echoed, and Marisol exhaled.

* * *

That evening they let her out onto the tribunal steps, pending tomorrow's final session, and the air outside tasted like dust and tram exhaust and freedom, more or less. Wren was waiting at the bottom, leaning against a railing like she owned it, wearing the one jacket she owned and an expression Marisol had learned to read as cautiously hopeful.

"Well?" Wren said.

"They took the list. They took the codes. Tomorrow they vote on the immunity framework." Marisol came down the steps. "Also I demanded they prosecute me."

Wren stared at her. "You *what*?"

"Appendix. Footnote. Whatever."

"You had immunity *in reach*—"

"I had a leash in reach," Marisol said. "Different animal, same collar. Wren. Eleven years I did what I was told and slept fine. You think walking free off that step fixes anything? Every route I flew, some town's well dropped another foot. I want my name somewhere it can be found, so that when somebody's kid asks whatever happened to the water-runner who helped them steal a river, there's an answer that isn't *she got a deal*."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then: "Ida's going to cry about this. She'll say you're family and family doesn't footnote itself."

"Ida cries at weddings and funerals and strong coffee. It's a whole system." Marisol looked out at the city lights coming on, the desert going violet past the edge of town. "Delacroix testify today?"

"Three hours. Didn't repeat himself once. Half the gallery was crying by the end and the other half was taking notes." Wren's mouth twitched. "He described the headworks pumps as 'a heart transplant performed by burglars.' One of the commissioners wrote it down verbatim."

"He'd be pleased. He'll want to know if it was *attributed*."

"Oh, it was attributed. With title. He's decided his title is 'Keeper of the Numbers.'"

Marisol laughed — really laughed, the first time in longer than she could date — and it echoed oddly off the marble, and she found she didn't mind being heard.

"So tomorrow," Wren said. "The vote."

"Tomorrow the vote." Marisol watched a tram slide past, lit up like a lantern, ordinary people going home to ordinary water they'd pay fair price for now, or close to it. "And then whatever they decide — prison, probation, some committee job counting acre-feet until I retire — I'll take it. Because here's the thing I finally figured out, chasing your father's ghost up a canyon: the Syndicate taught me that every debt can be settled. Cash, favor, silence. Everything's negotiable. But some things aren't debts at all. They're just *owed*. And you don't get to stop owing them. You just keep paying."

Wren nodded slowly. "Ida calls that inheritance."

"Ida would." Marisol pushed off the railing. "Come on. Delacroix found a diner that serves pie. He's already ordered for everyone, which should terrify you."

They walked down the steps together, and behind them the tribunal doors closed on the sound of clerks still typing, still entering names, the list growing longer by the hour — and somewhere across the city, in a tower of glass and imported water, a telephone was ringing in an office where a man named Frey was about to learn that fourteen names had become public record, and that the fifteenth call of his evening would be from a principal asking him, in a voice gone very quiet, exactly how much a lawyer was worth now.
