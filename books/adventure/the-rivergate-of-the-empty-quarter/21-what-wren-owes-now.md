## 21. What Wren Owes Now

The tribunal met in the old county courthouse in Dry Fork, a building that had been scheduled for demolition twice and reprieved twice, mostly because nobody could agree on who owned it. It had the air of a place that had survived by being nobody's responsibility — which, Wren thought as she climbed the steps with Marisol beside her, made it the perfect venue for what they were about to do.

"You're fidgeting," Marisol said.

"I'm adjusting my cuff."

"Your jacket doesn't have cuffs."

"Then I'm adjusting my sleeve."

Marisol snorted. She was out of her Syndicate grays, wearing civilian clothes that fit her like a borrowed opinion — correct but not hers. Her immunity deal had cost her everything except the one thing she'd wanted to keep: the names. Forty-one principals of the Vane Syndicate, read aloud into the record over three days while lawyers aged visibly in their seats. She'd taken a suspended sentence and a monitoring ankle band she referred to, cheerfully, as "the anklet of accountability."

Inside, the hearing room smelled of dust and floor wax and the particular staleness of institutional coffee. The presiding arbitrator was a narrow woman named Okonkwo who had the eyes of someone who had read every document twice and found the third reading unnecessary. Wren had testified the day before — four hours, most of it spent watching Okonkwo's pen move — and now they were back for the part that mattered to her personally, which was embarrassing, because the part that mattered to everyone else was so much larger.

The river towns had sent representatives. Ida Callow sat in the front row like weather itself, hands folded on a cane she didn't need for walking but did for emphasis. Behind her, two dozen faces Wren had come to know over the past year — the ferry families, the water-runners, a boy named Teo who had appointed himself Wren's shadow and asked questions with the relentless precision of a drill bit.

"—and accordingly," the Syndicate's lead counsel was saying, in the tone of a man reading his own eulogy, "the respondent does not contest the characterization of the Quintana debt instrument as proceeds of an unlawful enterprise."

Wren blinked. *Does not contest.* She'd expected a fight. She'd prepared for a fight. She had, in fact, rehearsed arguments in the shower for three weeks, and here was the Syndicate folding like a tent in wind she hadn't even felt yet.

Okonkwo looked up. "For the record, counsel, why does your client not contest?"

"Because, Madam Arbitrator, our forensic review confirmed the findings of this tribunal. The debt was structured to bind Ms. Quintana to services rendered in furtherance of the concealment of criminal activity. Enforcing it would constitute enforcement of an illegal contract." He paused, and something almost human crossed his face. "Also, our client has been dissolved."

"Dissolved," Okonkwo repeated.

"The Vane Syndicate no longer exists as a legal entity. Its assets are in receivership. Its remaining principals are—" he consulted a page he clearly wished he wasn't holding, "—elsewhere."

"Incarcerated," Marisol supplied helpfully from her seat. "Mostly. Two fled to the coast. There's a warrant out. I have copies if anyone wants them."

"No one wants your copies, Ms. Reyes," Okonkwo said, without heat. "You gave us forty-one already."

"It's called thoroughness."

Wren felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was clenched. Eleven years. Eleven years since the storm season when the bounty hadn't come through and the loan had, since the arithmetic that never once worked in her favor no matter how many supercells she chased, how many perfect readings she made, how many times she flew into weather that grounded everyone else and came back with data worth real money — none of which ever quite reached the principal. The debt had been designed, she now understood, the way a river is designed by an engineer: to flow in one direction only, and always toward Vane.

"The tribunal finds," Okonkwo said, and her voice carried the flat finality of a gavel before the gavel, "that the debt instrument held against Wren Quintana is void ab initio as proceeds of an illegal enterprise. All records of said debt are ordered expunged. Ms. Quintana owes nothing."

*Owes nothing.*

She'd imagined this moment for eleven years, usually at three in the morning, usually with the taste of cheap coffee and cheaper fuel. In the imagining it had always felt like winning. Now it just felt like standing very still in a large room while a stranger read numbers off a page, and somewhere behind her Teo whispered, loudly, "That's my friend," and Ida shushed him without any conviction whatsoever.

"One further matter." Okonkwo removed her glasses. "This tribunal has also reviewed the historical land grant filed under the name Callow, amended 2147, co-signed by one Elias Quintana, hydrologist. Evidence presented — including testimony of Mr. Delacroix, recovered survey logs, and engineering documents authenticated by this tribunal — establishes that Dr. Quintana discovered the diversion scheme during its construction, reported it through proper channels, and was subsequently subjected to coordinated defamation, financial ruin, and disappearance. This tribunal formally clears the name of Elias Quintana. His signature on that grant was procured under duress and misrepresentation, and his report — which we have entered into the record — was, in the arbitrator's assessment, the finest piece of hydrological analysis submitted to any body in this territory in forty years."

Wren's father. Cleared. Posthumously, in a courthouse scheduled for demolition, by a woman who had read everything twice.

She had planned to be dignified about this part. She had planned it the way you plan a landing in crosswinds — knowing the theory, trusting the practice. What actually happened was that her vision went blurry and Marisol's hand arrived on her shoulder with the gentle firmness of someone who had once been paid to do much worse things and had decided, permanently, to do this instead.

"He kept a copy of the report," Wren heard herself say. "In the house. Under the floorboards. I used to hide there when I was small. I sat on the truth for twenty years and didn't know it."

"Most people do," Okonkwo said, and for the first time her voice warmed by perhaps half a degree. "The difference is what they do when they find out. Adjourned."

* * *

Outside, the light was doing that high-desert thing it did in late afternoon, coming in low and gold and making even the courthouse parking lot look like a place worth photographing. The river towns' people spilled down the steps, talking all at once. Someone had brought fry bread. Someone else had brought a guitar, and was being firmly discouraged.

Ida found Wren at the bottom of the steps, away from the noise.

"Well," Ida said. "You're a free woman."

"That's what the paper says."

"Papers." Ida tapped her cane on the ground, twice, like punctuation. "I've got papers says I own a ferry crossing that hasn't existed since the army moved the road. Papers are how other people tell you what you are. The trick is deciding for yourself first, so theirs arrives late and doesn't matter."

Wren laughed despite herself. "Is that wisdom or just contrariness?"

"Both. They grow on the same plant out here." Ida studied her a moment, the way she studied the river before letting anyone cross. "Callow's Ferry's got a spare room. Has had one for years. We keep it for strays. You'd have work, if you want it — teaching the young ones to read sky and water both. Teo asks about you when you're gone. Asks the way children ask, which is to say relentlessly and with follow-up questions."

Wren opened her mouth to give the answer she'd been giving all year — *I'll think about it, I've got the truck, I've got the gear, I've got a season to chase* — and found that the words weren't there. She went looking for them and found something else instead: the memory of the last time she'd stood still anywhere longer than a week. She couldn't locate it. There was the landing strip, the repo crew waiting, the seasons blurring into a single long sentence that began with *owing* and had never reached a period.

"I don't know how to stop," she said finally. It came out more honest than she'd intended.

"Didn't ask you to stop." Ida's eyes crinkled. "Asked you to arrive. Stopping's different. Stopping's what you do when you're done. Arriving's what you do when you've found where you belong and intend to keep it." She leaned on the cane. "Your father knew the difference. That's why he fought for the river. He wasn't a wandering man, whatever the record says. He was trying to get home to you the whole time. The record just buried that part."

Wren looked out past the parking lot, to where the flats began, shimmering. Somewhere out there was the river, running free for the first time in a decade, lifting boats it hadn't lifted since she was a child. Somewhere upstream, ice had sealed the source canyon behind them, and Delacroix was planting a garden outside Ida's kitchen with the methodical intensity of a man rebuilding a mind one row at a time. Somewhere, two fugitive principals were discovering that the coast was smaller than they'd hoped.

"I'll want to chase storms still," she said. "From Callow's Ferry. If that's — I mean, the town should know I bring weather with me. Professionally."

"Wren." Ida patted her arm with a hand like cured leather. "We live on a river in the Empty Quarter. Weather is our whole personality."

And Wren laughed — really laughed, the kind that hurt a little, the kind she hadn't managed in eleven years — and understood, standing in a demolished-scheduled parking lot with a voided debt in her pocket and her father's name clean in the record, that the debt had never been the chain. The chain was believing she had to keep moving to stay ahead of it. The door had been open the whole time. It had just been behind her.

"Show me the room," she said.

Ida smiled and turned toward the truck. "Fair warning — the mattress sags, the window sticks, and Teo will be underfoot by seven."

"Ma'am," Wren said, following, "after the last year, that sounds like luxury."

Behind them, the courthouse stood in the gold light, unscheduled for anything at last — and far downstream, faint on the wind, came the sound of water running where the maps said there was none, carrying news toward towns that had already begun, quietly, to put themselves back on the map.
