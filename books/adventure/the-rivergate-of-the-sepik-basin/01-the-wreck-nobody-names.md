## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The river gave up its dead slowly, and it never gave them back whole.

August Dunmore learned that on his third dive, the one where he'd found a boot with a foot still in it, and he'd thought about that boot every morning since — eleven years of mornings now, each one beginning the same way: coffee boiled in river water, the taste of silt and diesel, and the long grey expanse of the Sepik sliding past his barge like something asleep that was not asleep at all.

He stood at the rail before dawn and watched the mist burn off the water. The lower reaches spread wide here, a mile across in places, braided with channels that moved every flood season like a man turning over in bed. Somewhere under that brown silk lay the wreck. Nobody named it. That was the arrangement.

The navy had sealed her in '89 — charges set along the hull, hatches welded or blown shut, the whole business written into a file that officially did not exist. A patrol boat, they said. Others said a supply vessel carrying things the government preferred drowned. August didn't care what she'd been. He cared about the brass fittings, the copper piping, the propeller shaft still bright under twenty years of mud, all of it worth money to a broker in Angoram who paid cash and asked nothing, which was the only kind of honesty August trusted anymore.

Kessler. Even thinking the name left a residue, like oil on water.

* * *

He went down at first light because the light didn't matter down there anyway. Visibility in the Sepik was a rumor — eighteen inches on a good day, less after rain in the highlands, which there always was, somewhere. Diving the Sepik meant diving by touch, by memory, by the map you built in your skull one fingertip at a time.

He checked his gear the way other men said their prayers. Regulator serviced twice monthly. Backup knife on the left calf, main on the belt. Air line from the compressor on deck — old diesel beast, Tobe's predecessor had called it the Dragon, and it coughed blue smoke all day like something dying slowly and enjoying it. Thirty metres of hose, inspected for chafe every dive, because the river forgave nothing but exploited everything.

"Compressor's good," he told himself, because there was no one else to tell. His voice came out flat and strange in the dawn quiet, swallowed immediately by the width of the water.

The descent line was a length of chain he'd hooked to the wreck's forward mast two seasons ago. He went down it hand over hand, and the world closed around him — green going to brown going to black, the pressure taking his ears one at a time, the cold arriving at ten metres like a hand laid flat against his chest.

At fourteen metres his gloves found the hull.

She lay on her port side, half-buried, stern toward the channel current. He knew her the way you know a house in the dark: the torn gash aft where the demolition charge had opened her, the superstructure collapsed into a tangle he could navigate blind, the cargo hold where the good metal lived. His fingers read the hull plating like braille. Rivet lines. A seam of rust soft as rotten fruit. Then the gash, and through it, the dark inside.

He clicked on his torch. The beam died three feet out, a yellow smear in the broth, but enough. Enough, if you'd done this a thousand times, and he had, or close to it.

Inside the hold, the silt lay deep and fine as flour. He worked by feel mostly, torch tucked under his arm, prying copper pipe loose with the short crowbar clipped to his harness. Each fitting went into the mesh bag at his hip. Copper sang even underwater — a faint bell-note when it knocked against itself — and he filled the bag until it rang softly with every movement, a little music no one would ever hear.

He was working a valve wheel free of its housing when the river changed.

It wasn't sound. Sound down there was just your own breathing, amplified, intimate, the loudest thing in the world. It was pressure — a shift in the current pushing through the gash behind him, a slow deep pulse, like the wreck had a heart and it had turned over in its sleep.

Silt clouded. His torch beam shrank to nothing. He stopped moving, hung in absolute blackness, and felt the old fear come up his spine on cold feet.

Flood pulse. Rain in the headwaters, hundreds of kilometres away, arriving without warning as a surge along the bottom channels. He'd been caught in one before. Every diver on the Sepik had a story about one; some of them were alive to tell it.

The current strengthened. Not fast — the river never hurried — but relentless, a giant's hand closing around him, drawing him toward the gash, toward the open water beyond, or worse, dragging him deeper into the wreck's collapsed throat where the hull plates overlapped like broken ribs.

August did the arithmetic calmly, because panic was a luxury billed later at compound interest. Air: forty minutes left on the line. Current: building. Exit: the gash, aft, thirty seconds' swim in still water. In this, unknown.

He clipped the bag shut and swam for it.

The blackness fought him honestly, no tricks, just mass and motion. He swam with the current rather than against it, angling for the gash by counting — five kicks, ten, fifteen — and his trailing hand found the torn plate's edge, razor-sharp even through the glove, and then he was through, out, into the channel where the flood pulse ran strongest.

It took him.

Not violently. The Sepik never did anything violently. It simply picked him up the way water picks up a leaf, and carried him downstream, and the air line came taut behind him with a snap he felt in his shoulders like a dislocation.

Thirty metres of hose. He'd measured it himself. He was at twenty-two metres of distance from the compressor and moving away at the speed of the current, and the hose paid out, and paid out, and he clawed at the bottom — silt, no purchase, nothing to hold, the riverbed offering him its usual indifference — and the line went tight, and held.

For a moment he hung there, horizontal in the brown dark, the current streaming him like a flag. The hose hummed with tension. Above and far behind, the Dragon coughed and hammered, oblivious. If the coupling let go, he'd be a body found next week in a fishing net near Tambanum, and Kessler would write off the debt, and nobody would name that either.

August pulled himself along the line, hand over hand, the way he'd descended the chain, except now the chain was trying to kill him. One metre. Two. The current tore at his legs. Three. His lungs began their small insistent argument. Four — and his glove closed on the anchor point, the mast chain, and he wrapped it twice and held on while the river tried to decide about him.

It decided nothing. That was the thing about the Sepik. It didn't decide. It just continued, vast and unbothered, and whatever lived or died inside it was beneath its notice.

When the pulse passed — ten minutes, maybe twelve, an eternity measured in breaths — August climbed the line to the light, broke the surface beside his barge, and spat brown water and laughed once, a single dry bark with no humor in it, at the sky.

Nobody had seen. Nobody was ever going to see. That was the deal he'd made with his life, apparently, though he couldn't remember signing it.

* * *

The copper weighed forty kilos wet. He loaded it into the tinny and ran the sixty kilometres downriver to Angoram with the Dragon's spare outboard hammering, arriving mid-afternoon in the stink and noise of the town — trade stores, betel spit like old blood on the concrete, the mission bell tolling flatly over everything.

Kessler's office sat above the trade store, behind a door with frosted glass and no name on it. Inside: a desk, a ledger, a wall calendar from a fuel company, and a ceiling fan turning with the weary patience of a condemned man. Kessler himself was small and neat, a German-Sepik creole whose shirts were always pressed and whose hands were always clean, which in August's experience meant someone else did the dirty work.

"You're late," Kessler said, without looking up from the ledger.

"Current."

"There's always current." He looked up then. His eyes went over August the way a buyer goes over stock — checking for damage. "You look tired."

"I look like I nearly drowned this morning."

Something flickered in Kessler's face — not concern. Calculation. "And the salvage?"

"Forty kilos. Good copper. Some brass." August dropped the mesh bag on the desk. It rang dully, that same dead bell from the deep.

Kessler weighed it on the spring scale, made a note, made another note, and pushed across the desk a fold of notes so worn they felt like cloth. August counted it. Short, as always. He'd stopped arguing about the shortfall a year ago; the shortfall was interest, Kessler said, on the advance that had bought the compressor, and the compressor was worth more than August's life, according to the ledger, and the ledger was the closest thing to scripture in that room.

"The wreck's giving less," Kessler said. "Each month, less."

"She's giving plenty. You're paying less."

Kessler smiled thinly. "The market, August. Brass is down. Everyone knows brass is down." He closed the ledger. "I have something else, if you want real money. There's a dredge job up past Ambunti. Deep channel work. Weeks of it."

"I don't do contract work for people who aren't you. You know why."

"Because you owe me, and you think loyalty is cheaper than freedom." Kessler said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "Fine. Stay with the wreck. But August—" He paused, and the fan turned, and somewhere below a truck unloaded beer crates with a sound like distant artillery. "There are men asking questions about the lower reaches. Navy men. Retired ones. Asking about sealed sites."

August kept his face still. "Lots of wrecks on the river."

"Not lots of sealed ones." Kessler reopened the ledger, wrote something, closed it again. "If anyone asks you what you take from that hull, you take scrap. Scrap metal, sold for scrap prices, to me. Say it exactly like that."

"That's what I say."

"Mm." Kessler's eyes rested on him a moment too long. "Go home, August. Sleep. Dive tomorrow."

* * *

He ran back upriver in the last light, and the question rode with him the whole way, sitting in the tinny like a passenger: how did Kessler know what questions the navy men were asking? How did a scrap broker above a trade store hear about retired officers poking through files?

Unless the questions weren't coming to Kessler. Unless they were coming *from* him, or through him, and August was the hand reaching into the dark so the clean hands stayed clean.

He pushed the thought down where he pushed everything — debts, loneliness, the boot with the foot in it — down into the hold of himself, and nailed the hatch shut.

Night came down on the lower reaches. He ate rice and tinned fish on the barge deck, watching the water go from grey to black, crocodile eyes stitching the far channel like sequins on a dress. The Dragon ticked as it cooled. Somewhere across the river a village drummed, faint and rhythmic, a heartbeat belonging to other people, people with families and fires and names for their children.

August washed his plate in the river and thought, not for the first time, that he should leave. Sell the gear, settle what he could of the debt, get out while the getting was possible. Go south, go anywhere, be a man who walked on land for a living.

But the debt was a hook, and Kessler held the line, and the river held Kessler, and besides — where would he go? The Sepik had taken everything else from him years ago. What remained was his, in the way a scar belongs to you.

He rolled into his bunk. The barge rocked gently, rocked him like something almost kind.

Tomorrow he'd dive again. Deeper along the hull, toward the stern section he hadn't yet mapped, where the sonar — Kessler's sonar, loaned grudgingly, logged jealously — had shown a hard return the size of a crate, sitting in the shadow of the engine room, too regular to be rock.

Scrap, probably. Scrap metal, sold for scrap prices.

He fell asleep telling himself that, and the river went on outside, wide and dark and patient, keeping what it kept, waiting for what was coming.
