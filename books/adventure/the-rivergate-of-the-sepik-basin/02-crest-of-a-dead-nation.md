## 2. Crest of a Dead Nation

The chest came up on the third day of dredging, when the river had already decided it wanted August Dunmore dead twice.

The first time had been Tuesday, a snag of cable in the old wreck's superstructure that caught his air line and held him forty seconds while he worked the knife against it in water so thick with silt he might as well have been blind. The second time was Wednesday morning, when Kessler's payment for the last load came up short and August understood that the arithmetic of his life had shifted again — that whatever he pulled from the Sepik this month would go to interest before it ever touched principal. He was thinking about that, about the shape of a debt that never shrank, when the dredge bucket hit something that rang.

Not the dull knock of mud-caked steel. Not the hollow clatter of beer bottles or the soft give of rotted timber. This was a sound with weight behind it, a bell-note that traveled up the cable and into the deck plates and through the soles of his boots into his bones. He killed the winch and stood listening to the river.

The *Marianne B* — his barge, his home, his whole visible estate — sat anchored off a bend eight kilometers downriver from Angoram, over a depression in the channel floor where an old government barge had gone down sometime in the years when nobody was keeping records worth trusting. The navy had sealed the site once; the buoy markers had long since rotted to stubs, and the seal itself existed now only in a file somewhere in Port Moresby that no one had opened since the decade it was written. Wrecks like this were the reason August ate. Men had died on the Sepik in every decade since the war, and the river kept their property the way a miser keeps coins — jealously, silently, and without any intention of spending.

He swung the bucket out over the deck and let it drain. Silt ran off in brown sheets, and then the water thinned, and then there it was, sitting in the bucket's throat like a heart in a ribcage: a box, maybe sixty centimeters long, sheathed in lead.

August had pulled enough lead-lined things out of rivers to know what the lining meant. Lead was what you wrapped around documents when you intended them to survive. It was what you lined ammunition boxes with, and strongrooms, and the coffins of men you expected to be dug up someday by people who hated them. Nobody went to the expense of lead for cargo they expected to see again next season.

He crouched over the bucket for a long time before he touched it.

The morning was doing what Sepik mornings did — building heat in layers, the mist burning off the water in slow rags, the far bank dissolving into its green blur of sago and karuka. A pair of white herons worked the shallows downstream, indifferent. Somewhere across the channel a village was waking; he could smell woodsmoke, faint and domestic, and hear the first outboard of the day coughing itself alive. The ordinary world, going on. He looked at the box and felt the strange, cold certainty that the ordinary world was about to end for him, though he could not have said why.

He lifted it out with both hands. It was heavier than it looked — lead does that, adds its own gravity to whatever it holds — and the seams were soldered shut, run over with a torch by someone who'd known what they were doing. The surface was furred with river growth, a grey pelt of algae and mineral bloom, but under that, where his thumb scraped, the metal came up bright as a coin.

And stamped into the lid, deep enough to have survived fifty years of burial, was a crest.

He knew it at once, the way you know a face from an old photograph even if you never met the man. A bird of paradise rising over crossed spear and shield, ringed by a circle of text too corroded to read entire. But he didn't need the text. Every kid who'd grown up on this river knew the story the way they knew ghost stories — told in bits, half-laughing, half-afraid. The Republic of New Guinea. Declared in the last days of 1974 by a handful of men on the middle river who looked at the coming independence and decided they wanted none of it. A nation with a flag, a president, a radio transmitter, and a lifespan of some months before the world declined to notice it existed. Dead before it was born, recognized by nobody, mourned by almost no one.

Except somebody had stamped its crest into lead, soldered it shut, and given it to the river to keep.

* * *

He took it below, into the cabin where the kerosene lamp hung on its gimbals, and he did not open it right away. That mattered to him later, though he couldn't have explained it at the time. He made coffee first. He ate half a tin of biscuits. He listened to the river slap the hull and told himself, in the flat voice he used for self-negotiation, that it was probably nothing. Deeds to a copra plantation. Church records. Somebody's family papers, drowned with a bureaucrat's briefcase, worth maybe a day's fuel to a collector in Wewak.

Then he got out the hacksaw and the soldering iron and went to work along the seam, and the work took an hour because whoever had closed it had meant it to stay closed, and by the time the lid came free the light had gone gold and slantwise through the cabin windows and his shirt was soaked through and his hands were shaking in a way that had nothing to do with effort.

Inside, on top, lay oilcloth packets tied with tarred twine. Under them, nested like eggs in straw, were three objects: a leather map case gone stiff as shell, a bundle of letters bound in rubber that had melted and re-hardened into one black mass, and a document in a folder of heavy paper, cream-colored, stamped with the same crest as the lid.

He started with the maps because maps were honest. They were survey sheets, hand-annotated, of the middle Sepik — reaches he knew the way he knew the backs of his hands, except these showed things that weren't on any chart he'd ever bought or stolen. Old channels. A mission station marked with a cross and a name in German: *Vunapope*. And beside the mission, in ink that had faded to the color of weak tea, a set of coordinates written and rewritten, corrected, initialed by two different hands.

He unrolled the folder next.

It was a concession deed. He knew enough paper to recognize the species — he'd watched lawyers fight over lesser documents in Kessler's office, watched men's fortunes turn on stamps and signatures. This one granted exclusive rights to extract and dispose of mineral wealth from lands along the Karawari, executed in the name of the Republic of New Guinea, signed by its president and countersigned by a district officer whose name meant nothing to him. The date was March 1975. The republic had been dead within the year, unrecognized, its every act voided — which meant, on its face, the deed was worthless. A curiosity. A museum piece.

Except that near the bottom, in clauses drafted with a lawyer's patient venom, was language he had to read four times to believe: provisions for survival of the grant notwithstanding dissolution of the granting authority, assignment to successors and assigns, recognition obligations binding on any successor government. Someone had built this document to outlive the country that issued it. Someone had believed, in 1975, that paper could hold its breath longer than nations.

And beneath the deed, folded small, was a letter that named the cache.

He read it standing up, though his legs wanted a chair. The handwriting was careful, educated, the hand of a man writing what he expected to be his last full accounting. It spoke of bars — not a number he could make sense of at first reading, the figure too large to fit inside his head alongside the price of diesel and the cost of a diver's medical exam and the eleven hundred kina he owed Kessler by Friday. It spoke of a barge, a night crossing, men who had sworn oaths in three languages. It spoke of Vunapope, and of the altar, and of the decision made rather than surrender it — *we gave it to the river rather than give it to a republic that will never live, and we gave it to God, who has kept better custody than either.*

The letter ended with names. Seven of them. Men who had done the sinking, listed so that their descendants would know what was owed and to whom.

August set it down on the chart table very carefully, the way you set down something sleeping.

* * *

For a long time he just stood there while the lamp hissed and the river talked against the hull.

He tried to feel what he supposed he was supposed to feel. Wonder, maybe. Or greed — clean, simple, animal greed, the kind that makes men sell their boats and mortgage their futures on a coordinate written in fading tea-colored ink. He reached for it deliberately, the way you reach for a tool. He made himself do the arithmetic. Gold at world price. The tonnage implied. His debts erased ten thousand times over. A new boat, a real boat, a boat with an engine that didn't need Tobe-style miracles every second week. Land somewhere high above flood line. A life in which no one could look at him the way Kessler looked at him, the way creditors looked at him, the way the river looked at him — as a thing to be spent.

The numbers were so large they stopped being numbers. They became weather. They became a climate he might live inside for the rest of his days.

And that was exactly when the fear arrived — not climbing, not gradual, but all at once, the way the river drops away under a boat that has found the edge of a submerged snag. Because he understood, standing in his one-room cabin with the lamp hissing, that the treasure was not the danger. The treasure was inert. Metal doesn't want anything. Metal has never killed anyone who left it alone.

The paper was the danger. The paper was alive.

He thought about who knew the river. Not casually, not the way tourists knew it from lodge verandas, but the way men know a thing they intend to own. Kessler knew it. Kessler, who paid in silence and kept ledgers in his head and had once, mildly, without raising his voice, explained to August what happened to divers who found things and failed to report them. Kessler, who had brokers in Wewak and friends in Moresby and a memory like a lead lining — everything sealed in, nothing getting out.

He thought about the government. There were ministries whose entire business was land and resources, and ministers whose careers were built on knowing which papers mattered before anyone else did. If a deed like this surfaced, it wouldn't surface quietly. It would surface the way blood surfaces in water — and everything with teeth would come from every direction.

He thought about the seven names at the bottom of the letter. Men who had sworn oaths in three languages. Men who had sons, and sons of sons, living now in villages along the Karawari, who had inherited the story the way you inherit a scar — without being asked, without being able to put it down. What did those families believe about the gold? That it was theirs? That it was cursed? That it was waiting? Any of those beliefs, held for fifty years, becomes a kind of law. And laws enforced by families are enforced differently than laws enforced by courts.

Three factions, minimum. Consortium men, government men, river men. And himself, alone on a barge with a lead box, holding the only key.

He looked at the deed again, at the survival clause with its patient venom, and he understood the real horror of it. Whoever held this paper could argue — in court, in a ministry, at gunpoint — that the claim had never died. That the republic's death changed nothing. That the gold beneath the drowned mission still belonged to whoever carried the right piece of paper, and that everyone standing between them and it was an obstacle with a legal name.

Paper like that didn't make men rich. Paper like that made men dead. It made them dead in proportion to how seriously other men took it, and every faction on this river would take it seriously, because every faction had more to lose from someone else holding it than from dying in the attempt.

Outside, the light was going. The herons had gone wherever herons go at dusk. The outboard sounds had faded and the woodsmoke smell had thickened, and somewhere downriver a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything.

August did the only thing he could think to do, which was to put everything back exactly as he'd found it — the letters, the map case, the deed folded small — and close the lid. Then he sat with the chest between his boots and thought about the seam he'd cut, and realized that a cut seam was a confession. Anyone who saw it would know the box had spoken.

So he re-soldered it. Badly, on purpose — good enough to pass a glance, wrong enough that a closer look would find a repair, because he was already learning the grammar of this new life and its first rule was that every lie should be a small one, deniable, the kind a man could swear to with a straight face. A box recovered sealed. A box he hadn't opened. A box he was bringing in to Kessler, because not bringing it in would be the loudest confession of all.

That was the plan, anyway, as he sat in the dark with the lamp turned low and the river running past in the black. Bring it in. Let Kessler see the crest, watch his face, learn what the old man knew. Keep the truth back like a card held against the hull of the boat.

He slept badly, and dreamed of an altar under green water, and woke before dawn with the chest's weight still in his hands though his hands were empty.

By first light he had hidden the deed and the letter inside the double wall of his spare tank, wrapped in oilcloth, and the barge was moving downriver toward Angoram, toward Kessler's office with its shaded windows and its smell of carbon paper and old money, carrying a dead nation's crest on its deck like a flag of truce that both sides would shoot at.

He told himself he was in control of the route.

The river said nothing. The river had heard that before.
