## 3. Kessler's Arithmetic

The broker's office sat on stilts above the water at the edge of Angoram, the way everything in the lower Sepik did — perched between the mud and the sky, belonging fully to neither. August had been coming here for six years, long enough to know that the walkway planks rotted in a predictable order, that Kessler replaced them only when a client actually fell through, and that the smell inside never changed: diesel, mildew, and the sweet-rot sweetness of paper money kept too long in river humidity.

Kessler himself was behind his desk when August came up the ladder, and he was already smiling.

That was the first wrong thing. Kessler smiled the way crocodiles basked — with the mouth open because it cost nothing, not because anything had entered it. But this morning the smile was waiting for him, arranged before August's boots had cleared the last rung, and beneath it Kessler's eyes were doing something they almost never did. They were bright.

"August Dunmore." He spread his hands, palms up, as if presenting a gift. "The man who comes back from the dead. Sit. Sit down. You look like something the river chewed and spat back out of politeness."

"I need an advance." August stayed standing. The office was dim, shuttered against the heat, lit by a single bulb that buzzed like an insect trapped in glass. On the desk between them lay a ledger, closed, its corners soft with handling. "The dredge took damage on the last run. Impeller's scored. I can't work until it's fixed, and I can't fix it without—"

"You found something."

The words dropped into the room and lay there.

August felt his pulse change gear, slow and deliberate, the way it did underwater when he needed his body quiet. He let three seconds pass. He made his face into the face he wore for brokers, for police, for anyone who wanted something from him: blank, tired, faintly stupid. It was a good face. He'd had years to practice it.

"Found scrap," he said. "Brass fittings off a hull. Some copper. The usual."

"Mm." Kessler opened the ledger without looking down at it, as though his hands knew the gesture the way a priest knows the mass. "Copper's down eight percent since June. Brass worse. And yet." He turned the ledger around and slid it across the desk with one finger. "And yet my buyer in Wewak tells me a dredge registered to you was seen Tuesday, forty kilometers upriver of your usual grounds, in water nobody works because there's nothing there worth working. Forty kilometers, August. Fuel is expensive. Men don't burn fuel for copper."

August looked at the ledger. It was open to a page of figures in Kessler's cramped hand, and none of them meant anything to him except the date at the top, which was yesterday.

"Maybe I heard there was a wreck," he said.

"Maybe you did." Kessler leaned back. His chair creaked, a sound like a small animal being stepped on. Behind him, through the slats of the shutters, the river ran past in bars of brown light, patient as ever, carrying whatever it carried. "Here is what I know, and then we can stop wasting each other's morning, which I know you value and I certainly do. I know that on Tuesday you brought up a chest. Lead-lined. Sealed. Approximately this size—" he measured the air with his hands, and the measurement was exact, and August's stomach went cold and stayed cold "—and heavy enough that your winch complained about it, because I pay a man at the fuel depot who hears your winch complain. I know that you did not bring it to me. I know that you have sold me every gram of metal you've pulled from that river for six years, and that this time you didn't, and I have asked myself why, all night, over several drinks, and I have arrived at an answer I find very interesting."

He let it hang. The bulb buzzed. Somewhere below the floorboards, water slapped the pilings in a rhythm like a slow heart.

"So," Kessler said pleasantly. "Show me what my river gave you."

* * *

There were perhaps nine ways out of the room, and August had counted them in the first ten seconds, the way he always counted exits in rooms where men smiled at him. Two windows shuttered but unlatched. The ladder behind him. The gap under the floorboards where the tide came through — no, too narrow, that was for eels and smaller men's secrets. None of the exits mattered, really, because Kessler wasn't going to touch him here, in his own office, in front of his own clerks. Kessler's threats were never physical in the room. Kessler's threats were arithmetic, and they followed you home.

"The chest was empty," August said. "Sealed, but empty. Lead lining, nothing inside but silt and a smell like old batteries. I dumped it back."

"You dumped it back." Kessler's voice was warm with disbelief, the warmth of a man enjoying himself enormously. "A lead-lined sealed chest, and you — August Dunmore, who once spent four hours free-diving a snagged propeller for eleven dollars of bronze — threw it back into the river."

"Lead's not worth the lifting."

"No," Kessler agreed. "Lead isn't." He stood, unhurried, and crossed to the window, and stood with his back half-turned, looking out at the water through the slats. When he spoke again his voice had changed — flattened, businesslike, the performance set aside like a coat. "I'm going to tell you a story, Dunmore, and you're going to listen, because stories are the only currency on this river that appreciates. In 1974, before independence, before the republic that lasted as long as a monsoon, there was a mining concession registered out of Wewak by a consortium whose name I will not insult your intelligence by pretending you haven't already guessed. Gold. Alluvial first, then hard rock. The concession papers were signed by a district officer acting for a government that ceased to exist before the ink was dry. Do you follow?"

"I follow."

"The war came sideways through here. The concession holders fled or died. The gold that had been pulled out — and there was gold, August, there was always gold — went onto a barge, and the barge went upriver, and the barge never came back. Everyone said pirates. Everyone said the flood took it. Everyone said whatever everyone says so they can sleep." Kessler turned around. In the striped dark his face was unreadable, half shadow, half slat-light. "But paper doesn't drown, Dunmore. Paper floats. Paper waits. A concession that was never formally extinguished is still, legally, alive — and whoever holds the original deed holds a claim that certain people in Port Moresby would trade a great deal to make disappear, and other people would trade more to make real. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I think you're telling me a story."

"I'm telling you," Kessler said softly, "that I am offering to buy a story from you. An empty chest story. That's all it is — a story. You tell me where you dumped the chest, I send two boys with a grappling hook, and you get five thousand kina and my continued goodwill, which as you know has a market value considerably higher than five thousand kina. Your debts stand at—" he consulted the ledger without touching it "—four thousand two hundred and sixty. So you'd clear your account, walk out of here square, and go back to scraping hulls like an honest poor man. That is the offer. It expires when I lose patience, and I want you to appreciate how generous that is, because if I were a crueler broker I'd simply report to certain parties that a certain diver recovered a certain document, and then you'd learn what it means when people with helicopters start asking after you. I'm not reporting anything. Yet. Because I like you, August. You're the only man on this river who pays attention."

The bulb buzzed. Downriver a drum sounded somewhere, far off, three beats and a silence, three beats and a silence, like something counting.

"And if I say the chest is gone?" August said. "Silted over. Unfindable."

Kessler sighed, genuinely, the sigh of a teacher with a promising student who insists on failing. He came back around the desk but did not sit. He put both hands flat on the ledger instead, and leaned, and for a moment the smile was entirely gone and what was underneath was older and much less friendly.

"Then I write in this book that August Dunmore's debts are called," he said. "Today. Not next month, today. And you know what calling means. Your dredge is collateral. Your gear is collateral. The roof over your head is collateral, such as it is. And when I take the dredge, you are a man on a riverbank with empty hands, and empty-handed men on this river end up working for people much worse than me, or floating in it. I don't want that. I genuinely don't. I want us both to be richer and comfortable and lying to each other comfortably for years." He straightened. The smile returned, reassembled, flawless. "Five thousand kina for a location. Think of it as selling me a fishing spot. Men sell fishing spots all the time. There's no crime in it. There's no future in it either, but there's no crime."

August looked at him for a long moment. Then he scratched his jaw, and let his shoulders drop a centimeter, and produced the laugh of a tired man conceding to a smarter one.

"You're right," he said. "Fuel's expensive. I'll show you where I sank it. Half now."

"Half now," Kessler agreed warmly, and opened a drawer, and counted notes onto the desk with the tenderness of a man feeding a bird. "You see? Arithmetic. Everything on this river is arithmetic, if you're honest about the numbers."

August took the money. His hands were steady. He gave Kessler coordinates — real ones, ones he'd already chosen on the run downriver, a bend full of snags and sunken logs two hours south, deep water, bad visibility, a place where two boys with a grappling hook could search until the wet season ended and find nothing but the river's own junk. He watched Kessler write them down and felt nothing at all, which was its own kind of warning.

At the ladder he paused.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the chest smelled like batteries. Whatever was in it, lead keeps things out as well as in."

"Go home, Dunmore," Kessler said, smiling, writing. "Fix your impeller."

* * *

He didn't go home. He went the long way, by canoe, through the side channels where the sawgrass grew tall enough to swallow a boat, and he checked his wake twice, and no one followed, or no one he could see, which on this river amounted to the same thing.

The deed was where he'd left it, wrapped in oilcloth and a plastic bag and a fish-scaled shirt, wedged in the tarpon-slimed fuel drum lashed under the stern canopy of his own punt — not the dredge, never the dredge, the dredge was collateral and collateral got searched. The drum stank of old petrol and fish rot and something mineral underneath, and when he lifted the bundle out, the oilcloth came away slick and silvered with slime, and he held the thing that half the powerful men of two nations would soon be hunting, and it weighed almost nothing. That was the obscenity of it. Maps. Letters. A concession deed signed in 1974 by a dying government, naming a cache sunk at a mission upriver, unextinguished, alive on paper. Enough weight in gold to buy the whole lower Sepik. And it weighed less than the fish he'd sold that morning.

He unwrapped just enough to check, though he'd checked twice already, and the signatures swam up at him through the lamplight of his memory — names, dates, a district officer's careful hand, and beneath them, in different ink, older, a line of Karawari names he didn't recognize yet but would. Men who had been there. Men who had done the sinking. Men whose grandsons were probably alive, and fishing, and unaware that their inheritance was a target.

He wrapped it again. He thought about burning it, right there, holding the oilcloth over his stove until the paper curled and the claim died and every faction on the river lost its reason to come for him. He even struck the match. He watched the flame stand in the dead-calm air of the cabin and understood, with the clarity that only came to him underwater, that burning it wouldn't save him — Kessler already knew the chest existed; Okuk's ministry already knew; some consortium office in Wewak already knew. The paper wasn't the secret anymore. He was. As long as August Dunmore was alive and silent, the hunt would continue; the moment the deed vanished, he became not a holder but a loose end, and loose ends got trimmed. The trap wasn't the debt. The trap was knowledge itself, and the only way through it was forward, upriver, toward the thing itself, ahead of everyone.

He dropped the bundle back into the drum, pressed the lid down into its slime, and lashed it tight.

Outside, night had come down over the channels, and the frogs had started, and far off across the black water a launch engine turned over — once, twice, idling, stopping. Listening, maybe. Or just a fisherman. On the Sepik you never got to know which.

Two days later, a surveyor named Bram Houtman would step off the morning boat at Angoram with a leather case and a contract, and August would understand that Kessler's arithmetic had never been about the five thousand kina at all. It had been about confirming the route. But that evening, sitting alone in the drum-stink and the frog-noise with a stranger's fortune under his feet and a broker's money in his pocket, all August knew was this: he had lied to the most dangerous man on the lower river, taken his money, and sent him digging in the wrong bend — and the river, which forgave nothing, had begun quietly keeping score.
