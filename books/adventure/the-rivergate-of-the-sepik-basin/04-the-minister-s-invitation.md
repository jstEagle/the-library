## 4. The Minister's Invitation

The memo came to her by accident, which was to say it came to her the way all important things came in Port Moresby: through someone else's carelessness and her own willingness to read what was not addressed to her.

Ilana Verrec had been in the Ministry of Lands conference room since seven that morning, waiting for a meeting with a deputy secretary who would not arrive until nine and who would cancel at half past eight without telling anyone to tell her. She had learned long ago that waiting rooms were where governments kept their secrets — not in safes, not behind passwords, but on unattended desks and in printer trays, because no one imagined a woman from the provinces sitting alone for two hours with nothing to do but look.

The printer in the corner coughed out its pages at 8:14. She heard it the way she heard everything now: with the particular attention of a person who had stopped expecting help. The secretary who should have collected them was at the coffee urn down the hall. Ilana crossed the room on the pretext of opening a window that did not open, and took the top page off the stack, and read it standing up, and did not put it back.

*RESTRICTED — CIRCULATION: MINISTER'S OFFICE ONLY.*

*RE: Salvage recovery, Sepik Basin. Object bearing seal, Republic of New Guinea (1975). Recovered by licensed diver, name withheld pending verification. Concession documentation enclosed in original chest. Consortium interest confirmed (Houtman, Bram — Meridian Pacific Surveys). Minister Okuk directs: locate and secure claimant lineage prior to consortium contact. Priority absolute. Budget line: discretionary.*

She read it twice. The second time her hands were not steady, and she noticed this with a kind of clinical detachment, as though observing a stranger's symptom. *Republic of New Guinea.* A country that had existed for eleven days in 1975 — proclaimed by a handful of men on the lower Sepik, recognized by no government on earth, dissolved back into Papua New Guinea so completely that most history books gave it a sentence, if that. Her grandfather had been one of the men who signed its declaration. He had never spoken of it. Her mother said he drank more in the years after, and that when he died there was a tin box under his bed they'd never managed to open.

And now a diver had pulled a chest with that dead nation's crest out of the river, and the Minister of Lands wanted the heirs found before a mining consortium got to them, and the words *priority absolute* sat on the page like a loaded weapon left on a kitchen table.

She photographed both sides of the memo with her phone. Then she set it back on the tray, exactly as it had lain, one degree off square, and went back to her chair, and opened her briefcase, and took out the file she had carried for six years — the Karawari land-claims file, thick as a Bible, annotated in three colors of ink, rejected four times by four different departments for four different procedural reasons that all meant the same thing: nobody with power cared about a few hundred people up a black-water tributary whose ancestors had signed nothing anyone in Port Moresby recognized.

Except, apparently, they had signed something. And whatever it was, it was worth a minister's discretionary budget.

At 8:31 the deputy secretary's assistant came in to tell her the meeting was cancelled. Ilana thanked her warmly, packed her briefcase, and walked out into the wet heat of Waigani with the taste of iron in her mouth, which she recognized, after a moment, as ambition.

---

Her office was a converted storage room above a print shop in Boroko, one window facing a wall, furnished with a desk that had come from a defunct bank and a ceiling fan that turned with the moral seriousness of a man walking to his own execution. It cost her almost nothing, which was fortunate, because it earned her almost nothing. Land-claims work in this country was charity dressed as law. She kept it going on the occasional commercial contract — a resort here, a logging boundary dispute there — and on money she did not examine too closely, sent by an aunt in Cairns who believed Ilana was saving for a house.

She spread the file across the desk and began, for the sixth year running, to build the case. But this time the case had a shape it had never had before. This time there was a document in existence — sealed in a lead-lined chest, in the possession of some diver on the middle Sepik — that might prove what she had spent six years arguing could only be inferred: that the Karawari signatories of 1975 had held title, real title, recognized even if only by a republic that died unborn, to lands and concessions along the middle river. Title that had never been extinguished, because you cannot extinguish a claim against a nation that was never absorbed, only forgotten.

If the deed survived, the descendants were not squatters petitioning the state. They were creditors.

She worked until the light went copper behind the wall outside her window, and then she made herself stop, because she had learned that about herself too: that she could want something so hard it bent her judgment, and the memo was exactly the kind of thing that bent. She ate rice and tinned fish standing at the counter. She slept badly. In the morning she drafted a letter to the Minister's office requesting a meeting regarding "ancestral claims documentation relevant to current ministry priorities," which was true, and vague, and guaranteed to be ignored — unless the minister's office was already looking for her, in which case it was bait.

It was answered within four hours. Not by letter. By phone.

"Miss Verrec." The voice was dry, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it. "This is Okuk. You wrote to my office."

"Minister. Thank you for—"

"You photographed a memorandum yesterday that was not addressed to you."

The fan turned. Somewhere below, the print shop's roller thudded like a slow heart. Ilana stood very still at her desk and made herself breathe normally, and understood, with a cold clarity that felt almost like relief, that she had been seen taking the page — or been reported by the secretary, or caught on some camera she'd never noticed — and that the summons had already been written before she ever sent her letter. She had not set the trap. She had walked into one that was older than her interest in it.

"Yes," she said. "I did."

A pause, long enough to be felt. Then, faintly, she thought she heard him smile. "Tomorrow. Ten o'clock. Come alone."

He hung up. She looked at the phone for a while. Outside, the traffic on the Gordon's Road ground past in its eternal argument, and the wall outside her window held the last light, and Ilana Verrec discovered that her hands were shaking again, and that beneath the fear, patient and warm as a coal, was the same iron taste.

They wanted her. For six years she had begged this government to acknowledge that her clients existed, and now a minister knew her name, and knew it because she was useful. She was not naive enough to mistake being useful for being respected. But she was honest enough with herself to know she would take it either way.

---

The Minister of Lands received her in an office on the ninth floor where the air conditioning fought a losing war against the humidity and the curtains were drawn against a view of the whole city, as though Port Moresby itself were something a man in his position preferred not to be reminded of. Okuk was smaller than he sounded on the phone, a compact grey man in a pale suit, reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, hands folded on a desk bare except for a single folder. Everything about him suggested a man who had learned that authority was quieter than volume and far more durable.

"Miss Verrec." He did not stand. He indicated the chair opposite with one finger. "You represent the Karawari claimants. How many families?"

"Two hundred and eleven households across nine villages on the middle tributaries. I hold signed mandates from the senior claimants of each."

"Mandates." He said the word as though tasting it. "Signed by people who cannot read the documents they're signing."

"Some can. I read every mandate aloud in the presence of two witnesses before signature. If you have concerns about my conduct, Minister, you can address them to the Law Society."

"I have no concerns about your conduct." He opened the folder — her own letter, she saw, and beneath it a photograph, grainy, of a lead-lined chest crusted with river silt, a crest stamped into the lid. Even blurred, she knew the device: the bird of paradise over crossed paddles, the eleven stars of the short-lived republic. Her grandfather's tin box had held a medallion with the same face. "I have concerns about your interests. Sit down, Miss Verrec. Let us not perform at each other. We are both too good at it, and neither of us has the time."

She sat.

"The chest exists," Okuk said. "It was recovered eleven days ago by a licensed salvage operator working the lower Sepik. His name is Dunmore. August Dunmore. He is currently — we believe — in Angoram, or soon will be. A consortium called Meridian Pacific has also become aware of the recovery, through channels I will not describe, and their surveyor is already moving to engage him. The chest contains concession documentation dating from 1974. If that documentation says what I believe it says, it establishes a gold concession over mission lands near Vunapope that was granted, witnessed, and never revoked."

"And never honored," Ilana said. "Because the granting authority collapsed in eleven days."

"A technicality." He folded his hands again. "You are a lawyer. You know better than most that the law does not care whether a nation lived long enough to matter. It cares whether paper exists. Paper exists."

"What do you want from me?"

"I want you to find the heirs." He said it simply, the way a man asks for a glass of water. "Before the consortium does. Before their lawyers manufacture claimants of their own — and they will, Miss Verrec; Meridian has manufactured heirs before, in Bougainville, and it worked. I want legitimate descendants, properly mandated, represented by counsel of record, filing a claim that the courts must take seriously. Your claimants are legitimate. You are competent. Together we are credible."

"Together," she repeated.

"I am offering you legitimacy, Miss Verrec. Ministry endorsement of the Karawari claim. Expedited hearing. Protection for your clients from the sort of attention that finds isolated villages on remote rivers when valuable things are nearby." He let that sit between them, and she felt it the way one feels a change in water pressure diving: the depth of the threat beneath the courtesy. "In return, when the concession is validated, the ministry requires a negotiated role in its administration. Royalties flow through proper channels. Order is maintained. Everyone is paid. That is all governance is, in the end — making sure everyone is paid in the correct order."

"And my clients? Do they get paid in the correct order too?"

"They get paid first," Okuk said, and smiled, and it was the smile of a man giving away something that had already cost him nothing. "That is the beauty of your position. Their priority is genuine. I merely wish to stand behind it."

She looked at the photograph of the chest for a long time. She thought of her grandfather's tin box, and of Enoch Warapai's grandfather, whose name was in her file as a signatory of the 1975 declaration — old men now, or dead, who had watched soldiers come up the river and had made choices she was only beginning to understand. She thought of two hundred and eleven households who had waited six years for a hearing and would wait six more if she refused this man, and what the flood season did to the stilt villages in the meantime, and how many children had gone without the clinic boat because the claim was always next year's business.

And underneath all of it, quiet and unsentimental, she counted: legitimacy, access, a seat at the table she had been pounding on from the outside for six years. The river had never been home to her. She had left it at nineteen and had no illusions about returning; the Sepik was not a place you loved, it was a place you survived, and survival was a craft like any other. What the river was, was leverage. It always had been. The difference was that today, for the first time, she was holding the lever instead of being the weight on the other end.

"If I find the heirs first," she said carefully, "and file the claim — what happens to the diver?"

Okuk's expression did not change at all, which was itself an answer. "Dunmore is a salvage operator with debts. Men like that are instruments, Miss Verrec. Instruments are useful, and then they are set down. I would prefer he be set down gently. Whether that happens depends largely on how quickly you work."

"And Kessler?" She watched him as she said the name — the broker's name that had surfaced twice in her research on lower-river salvage, the name that appeared in the memo's margins in pencil, half-erased.

For the first time, something moved behind Okuk's eyes. It was small, and it was fast, and it told her more than the entire conversation had. "Kessler is a private citizen," he said, "with no official standing in this matter. You will not mention that name again."

"No, Minister," Ilana said, and did not mean it, and was fairly sure he knew she didn't, and that this too was part of the arrangement — two professionals agreeing to lie to each other in a well-lit room.

He stood, and offered his hand, and his grip was dry and precise. "Forty days," he said. "The consortium's permits lapse in forty days, and everything accelerates toward that date. Find your heirs. File your claim. Be ready to move upriver when I call." At the door he added, without turning: "Your grandfather signed the declaration, didn't he. I checked. You may find that makes you sentimental about this. Don't be. Sentiment is how the other side will get to you."

She walked out past the secretary, past the flags, into the stairwell because she didn't trust herself to wait for the lift, and stood in the concrete coolness with her hand pressed flat against the wall, breathing.

Then she took out her phone and opened the photograph of the memo, and looked at the pencilled margin, and began — methodically, in the way that had gotten her through law school on scholarship and six years of losing cases without losing herself — to make a list of everything Okuk had just told her without meaning to.

The first item was Kessler. The second was that Okuk feared her finding something before he did. The third, written in her mind in her grandfather's handwriting, was the oldest rule of the river, the one every family on the Sepik taught its children:

*What floats downstream belongs to whoever grabs it first.*

She went back to Boroko and started packing a bag.

* * *

Three nights later, a fax came to the print shop downstairs, misdirected — or not — addressed to a ministry office number two digits off hers. She read it standing among the reams of paper and the smell of hot toner, under the single fluorescent tube, while the fan turned overhead.

It was a charter manifest. A survey vessel, a consortium logo, a list of names. And there, third from the top, typed in a clerk's indifferent font, the name she had been sent to find before anyone else could reach him:

*DUNMORE, A. — guide/salvage consultant — contracted.*

Beneath it, a departure date. Angoram, ten days hence.

Ilana read it twice, then folded it into her bag beside the mandate file, and stood for a moment in the empty shop listening to the rain begin on the tin roof, thinking about a man she had never met who was already contracted to the wrong side, and about forty days, and about how far upriver she would have to go to get to him first.

She booked passage to Angoram in the morning.
