## 5. Concession Permits Expire in Forty Days

The rain came in off the grasslands that morning like something with intent, driving the river flat and grey and turning the whole world the color of gunmetal. August Dunmore sat under the tarpaulin awning of his stilt house at Angoram, mending a net he had no intention of using, and watched a government survey launch come upriver against the current with the arrogant steadiness of a thing that had never once worried about fuel.

He'd known it was coming for three days. Kessler's radio man had gone quiet on the band — that particular silence that meant someone important was listening — and the broker himself had stopped answering August's calls altogether. In the Sepik delta, silence was not absence. Silence was an animal holding its breath.

The launch was white-hulled, twin-screwed, flying no flag but wearing the discreet decal of the Meridian Basin Consortium on its transom: a stylized river forked through a gold ingot. It came alongside his jetty with the practiced ease of a vessel whose skipper had done this in a hundred rivers, and the wash rocked August's own boat, the *Nambawan*, against her tires with a sound like slow applause.

Two men came up the ladder. The first was local security, broad-shouldered, bored, carrying nothing visible and everything implied. The second was a tall Dutchman in a shirt too clean for the river, khaki pressed into knife creases, sunglasses pushed up into hair going silver at the temples. He carried a leather document case the way a priest carries a missal.

"August Dunmore." Not a question. "Bram Houtman. Consortium survey division." He extended a hand that was dry and cool despite the humidity, as if he kept it refrigerated somewhere between handshakes. "You're a difficult man to find."

"No," August said. "I'm an easy man to find who doesn't answer."

Houtman smiled without warmth, the smile of a man cataloguing the response for later use. He looked past August at the stilt house, the rusting dredge, the *Nambawan* with her patched hull and her engine held together by Tobe's stolen genius. He looked the way Kessler looked — like a man reading a balance sheet written in flesh and rivets.

"May I?" He didn't wait for permission. He stepped onto the deck, chose the one dry stool, and opened the document case across his knees. The security man remained at the top of the ladder, facing the river, watching the rain.

August stayed standing. It was a small thing, but he had learned that standing while other men sat was sometimes the only ground a poor man owned.

"You recovered something last month," Houtman said. "From the navy wreck below Tambunam. A chest. Lead-lined."

"I recover scrap," August said. "Copper, brass, propeller bronze. I sold it all to my broker, same as always."

"Of course you did." Houtman drew out a folder, squared its edges against his knee with a small compulsive tap-tap-tap. "Mr. Dunmore, I'm going to speak plainly, because I've read your file and I know you don't have time for anything else. You owe Kessler eleven thousand kina against equipment advances, and he holds paper on your dredge. Your diving certification lapsed in March. And three weeks ago, a lead-lined chest bearing the seal of the Republic of New Guinea passed through this stretch of river, and every intelligence service from Port Moresby to Singapore has been asking quiet questions about it ever since." He looked up, and behind the lenses his eyes were pale and patient as a heron's. "I don't care whether you found it. I care that you know what it means. Because I do, and I'm offering you a way to survive knowing it."

The rain thickened. Somewhere down the bank a child was shouting at a dog, and the sound seemed to belong to another country.

"The Republic of New Guinea existed for eleven months," Houtman continued, when August said nothing. "Declared by a handful of councilmen and a German mining syndicate in '74, recognized by no one, dead before the ink dried on its own constitution. But during those eleven months, it issued concessions. Mining concessions. One of them covered a gold strike in the middle Sepik basin — a strike that officially never happened, because the republic died and took its paperwork with it." He tapped the folder again. "Except paperwork doesn't die, Mr. Dunmore. It just waits. And a concession that was never extinguished is still, legally, a live claim. Our lawyers say so. Their lawyers say so. Everyone's lawyers are very excited."

"And what does any of that have to do with me?"

"Forty days." Houtman said it simply, and the number landed in August's chest like a dropped anchor. "That's how long remains on the provisional exploration permit the consortium filed when fragments of the deed surfaced last year. Forty days from tomorrow, the permit lapses, and if we haven't conducted a documented site survey at the concession coordinates, the claim reverts to open tender — where Minister Okuk's people will take it, or worse men will. We need to reach those coordinates, verify, and file. By boat. Through water no consortium vessel has charted since the war." He finally removed the sunglasses, and August saw that his eyes were the color of weak tea, bloodshot at the rims, a man who slept badly. "We need someone who knows the river. Someone deniable. Someone expendable, frankly, though I'd advise against dwelling on that word."

"And if I say no?"

Houtman reached back into the case and produced a single sheet, laid it face-down on the deck boards between them, and turned it over with two fingers. It was a photocopy of Kessler's ledger page. August's name. The figure. The collateral line: *dredge, Nambawan hull, stilt lease, Angoram.* And beneath it, in Kessler's cramped hand, a note August had never seen: *Buyer pending. Asset liquidation authorized on default.*

"He's already sold your debt," Houtman said. "To us. As of Tuesday. Which means the choice you think you're making — the one about loyalty, about keeping your head down — doesn't exist anymore. If you refuse, we call the paper due. You lose the boat, the house, the gear. You'll be diving other men's wrecks on shares until your lungs give out, and every broker on this river will know you're a man who can be squeezed." He folded his hands. "Or you guide us upriver. Forty days. You control the route, the pace, the crew. When it's done, the debt is extinguished — genuinely extinguished, not transferred — and there's a completion fee that would let you stop scraping and start choosing."

August looked at the ledger page for a long time. The rain drummed the tarpaulin overhead, and beneath him, through the gaps in the decking, the river breathed its slow brown breath against the pilings. Eleven thousand kina. Four years of salvage, of cold mornings and black water and the taste of silt, compressed into a number another man had written down.

"What exactly am I guiding them to?"

"A site survey. Coordinates from the deed. Nothing more."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I'm authorized to give." Houtman rose, smoothed his trousers, and set the contract on the stool where he'd been sitting. Three pages, dense with clauses. At the bottom, a signature block waited with August's name printed above it in tidy type, as though the outcome had been decided before the conversation began. Perhaps it had. "Take tonight. The launch returns at first light. But Mr. Dunmore—" he paused at the ladder, and for one moment the surveyor's polish slipped and August saw the tired operator underneath, a man also carrying paper he hadn't chosen, "—there are others coming for this. Men who won't offer contracts. Whatever you decide, decide it understanding that the river has more than one crocodile in it this season."

Then he was down the ladder, into the launch, and the white hull swung away upstream toward Angoram's government wharf, leaving a wake that slapped the pilings like slow hands.

* * *

August didn't sleep. He sat on the edge of the deck with the contract under the pressure lamp and read it twice through, then a third time, mouthing the clauses like a man tasting for poison. Most of it was what Houtman had said. Some of it wasn't. Clause fourteen gave the consortium "operational authority over route selection in matters of safety." Clause nineteen indemnified the consortium against "losses arising from third-party interference" — which meant if anyone shot at them, it was August's problem. Clause twenty-two, buried near the end, granted the consortium right of first refusal on "any materials of historical significance recovered en route."

Any materials. He thought of the deed hidden in the tarpon-slimed fuel drum beneath his floorboards, wrapped in plastic, and felt the old fear shift its weight inside him like a fish turning.

Because here was the truth he could not put in any clause: he had the coordinates already. Had memorized them the night he opened the chest, and burned nothing, told no one, but carried them in his skull like a splinter working toward the heart. If he signed, he would be leading these men to a place he already knew — and the only power he would have in the whole arrangement was the shape of the route, the pace of the days, the choice of channels. The illusion of control. He turned that illusion over in his hands and found it warm, found that he wanted it, found that wanting it shamed him less than he expected.

He thought about refusing. He constructed the refusal carefully, even rehearsed it — *I dive scrap, I don't ferry surveys* — and then he walked it down to the waterline of reality and watched it sink. Refusal meant Kessler's creditors. It meant the *Nambawan* impounded at the mooring, the dredge auctioned, the stilt lease voided. It meant becoming a ghost on his own river. And underneath all of it, quieter and colder: refusal meant Houtman would simply find another pilot, some younger man with fewer debts, and whatever happened at those coordinates would happen without August anywhere near it. At least this way he'd see it coming. At least this way, if there was a moment to bend events, he'd be standing at the tiller when it came.

That was the story he told himself, anyway, sitting alone with the lamp hissing and the rain easing off toward midnight. That signing was a kind of steering. Men drowned telling themselves stories like that; the river was full of them.

Near dawn he went below, pried up the floorboard, and lifted the fuel drum's lid just enough to confirm the plastic bundle was still there, still dry, still smelling of tarpon slime and diesel. The deed's signatory stared up at him from the top letter — *E. Kaip, for the Council of the Republic* — and beneath the name, the coordinates he knew by heart. He closed the drum. He did not burn the contract. He signed it instead, at first light, on the deck of the white launch, with Houtman watching and the security man witnessing, and the pen scratched across the page with a sound like a small animal struggling.

"Forty days," Houtman said, taking the pages back. "I'll transmit the crew requirements today. Pilot, engineer, general labor. Keep it small and keep it quiet — no one with government ties, no one who talks." He paused, buttoning his jacket. "And Mr. Dunmore — the crew is yours to choose, but their names go in the manifest, and the manifest goes to Port Moresby. Choose people you can account for."

"I always do," August said, and hated how easily the lie came.

The launch pulled away eastward into the morning glare, and August stood on his jetty watching the wake unroll, feeling the contract's weight settle into him like swallowed ballast. Forty days. He had forty days to assemble a crew of strangers he could trust with his life and betray with his silence; forty days to steer a consortium expedition along a route only he knew, toward a secret only he possessed, without letting either side discover the other's full hand.

He went inside and began, on the back of an old tide table, writing names. A pilot. An engineer. Hands for the winch. And at the bottom of the list, after a long hesitation, a fourth name — old Enoch of the Karawari side, who had once, years ago and half-drunk on bush rum, looked at August across a fire and said, *You dive for dead men's things, boy. One day the dead things will dive for you.*

August didn't know yet why the name belonged on the list. He only knew that leaving it off felt like rowing with one oar.

By noon the list was finished, and by evening the first answer had come back over the radio — a woman's voice, low and unhurried, identifying herself as Meja, late of the mission hospital at Wewak, asking what the cargo was and whether anyone aboard intended to tell her the truth about it.

August looked at the radio for a long moment before keying the mic.

"Come to Angoram," he said. "We'll see."
