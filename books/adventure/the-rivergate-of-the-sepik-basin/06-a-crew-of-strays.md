## 6. A Crew of Strays

I spent three days in Angoram before I understood that the hardest part of the expedition would not be the river. It would be the people.

Houtman had left me a list — his surveyor's handwriting, all angles and underlines — of men the consortium had vetted and found acceptable. Every name on it was a man who owed Kessler money, which meant every name on it was a man who reported to Kessler whether he knew it or not. I burned the list in the drum behind the trade store and started over with my own arithmetic: not who could be bought, but who was already ruined enough to have nothing left to sell.

The river keeps its strays the way driftwood collects at a bend. You only have to know where the bends are.

* * *

Meja Rangat I found where I expected to find her, which was nowhere near the boats.

She'd been the best pilot on the middle Sepik once — everyone said so, even the people who wouldn't say her name anymore. Seven years running cargo between Angoram and Pagwi, threading the flood channels at night when nobody else would move, until the year the mission clinic at Timbunke lost its only nurse and she went ashore for six months to fill the gap. What happened there came out later in fragments, the way these things always do: a child who died with a needle mark in his arm, an inquiry that couldn't decide whether it was negligence or malaria, and Meja walking away from both without defending herself. The board struck her off. The river, which forgives nothing, forgave her faster than the town did. But forgiveness on the Sepik is a private thing. It doesn't come with invitations back aboard.

I found her at the fish smokehouse below the market, mending nets for the Chinese buyer, her hands moving through the work with the blind speed of someone who hated every second of it. She was maybe forty-five, built low and dense like a mooring post, with a face that had stopped bothering to be friendly somewhere around the time of the inquiry.

"Dunmore," she said, when I sat down on the upturned dinghy beside her. "The diver. Kessler's diver."

"Not anymore."

"Everyone's Kessler's diver eventually." She didn't look up from the net. A mosquito coil smoked between us, doing its useless ceremonial work. "You want something repaired."

"I want a pilot. Forty days, upriver past Ambunti. Consortium money."

That got her eyes. They were the color of the river in dry season, brown going on amber, and they looked at me the way a crocodile looks at a dog that has wandered too close to the water's edge — not hostile yet, but measuring.

"The consortium." She tied off a knot with unnecessary force. "Bram Houtman's outfit. The ones buying permits to go look at old ground." Word traveled fast on the Sepik; word about paper traveled fastest of all. "Why me? There are pilots who haven't been struck off anything."

"Because the ones who haven't been struck off anything are still trying to keep their names clean, and clean names do what they're told. I need somebody who'll tell Houtman no when the channel says no." I watched her hands pause over the netting. "And because you don't owe Kessler. I checked."

"You checked." Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. "The diver checks. And what happens when this expedition goes wrong, Dunmore? Because expeditions with consortium money go wrong. It's practically their business model."

"Then you'll be the one who saw it coming. That's worth more than a clean name."

She put down the net then, finally, and looked at me straight on for a long moment. Behind her the smokehouse dripped and ticked, and somewhere across the water a diesel generator ran its flat evening note.

"I'll take double what you're paying," she said. "Half up front, into my sister's account in Wewak, so if I drown you can't cheat my family out of it. And if anyone on that boat asks me about Timbunke, I stop the boat wherever we are and they can walk."

"No one will ask. I'm not interested in what people did before I met them. Only in what they do after."

She studied me like I was a channel she hadn't charted. "That's either honesty or a very good lie."

"On this river it's usually both."

She spat in her palm and held it out, and I took it, and that was how the first stray signed on.

* * *

Tobe I didn't recruit. Tobe recruited me.

I came back to my mooring at dusk to find the engine hatch of my workboat open and a pair of legs sticking out of it like something the river had deposited there. For one bad moment I thought of Kessler's creditors, of wrenches and sabotage, and I crossed the deck quietly and took hold of an ankle.

The legs kicked. The rest of the boy followed them out — sixteen at most, all elbows and collarbone, hair cropped close against lice, wearing a shirt that had been white during some previous geological era. He landed on the deck in a crouch with a spanner raised, and when he saw me his face went through fear, calculation, and a kind of furious dignity, all in the space of a breath.

"It's flooded carb," he said, before I could speak. "Number two cylinder's running rich. Been running rich since Wewak, whoever serviced it last didn't gap the plugs right. I fixed it."

I looked into the hatch. He had, or he'd done something to it; the idle, when I thumbed the starter, came back even and quiet as held breath. The boy watched my face while the engine ran, and I watched him watch me, and I understood that I was being auditioned by someone with far more practice at it than I had.

"Tobe," he said. "Like the fish. Everyone says it."

"Who taught you engines?"

"Nobody teaches you engines. Engines teach you, if you listen." He wiped his hands on his ruin of a shirt. "I listened to the mission generator at Marienberg for two years. Then the brothers caught me taking parts off the ambulance boat and decided listening wasn't the point."

"Stealing?"

"Borrowing." His chin came up. "The ambulance boat was broken anyway. I made it run. Nobody ever thanks you for making a thing run. They just remember the part you took to do it."

I thought about the chest hidden in a fuel drum fifty meters from where we stood, and about the number of people on this river who would sell each other for less than what was in it, and about the fact that a boy who borrowed parts to make broken things run was probably the most honest person I'd met since I'd opened it.

"I'm putting together a crew," I said. "Forty days, upriver. Engine hand. Pay's real, food's real, and when it's done you'll have worked a consortium contract, which means the next ten offers won't ask where you learned your trade."

He narrowed his eyes with a shrewdness that belonged on a man three times his age. "Consortium. Houtman's people?" When I nodded, he spat over the side with great ceremony. "They surveyed our ground last year. Paid nothing, promised everything, left a chain-link fence around a wellhead that never produced. My uncle tore it down for scrap." He considered me, head tilted. "You're not consortium."

"No."

"But you're working for them."

"For now."

"That's what my uncle said about the fence. 'For now.'" He grinned suddenly, and the grin rearranged his whole face into something young and quick and bright. "Okay. But I sleep aboard, I eat first, and if anybody touches my toolbox I quit mid-river and take the toolbox with me."

"Deal," I said, and shook his hand, and felt — for the first time since the chest came up dripping out of the dark — something that was almost lightness.

* * *

Enoch was the one I went to find, and the one I most feared finding.

The deed named its signatory: Yawan Karu, headman of the Karawari branch, witness to a concession granted in the last mad months of 1974, when the Republic of New Guinea existed on paper and in the ambitions of perhaps four hundred men and nowhere else on earth. The name meant nothing to me when I first read it. Names on old paper are dead letters. But Houtman's survey notes mentioned that the Karawari line still held customary claims along the upper channels, and if any living person remembered what those signatures had cost, it would be an elder, and elders do not forgive trespassers politely.

I went upriver to the Karawari side in Meja's old punt, because she'd insisted on seeing who else I was hiring before she committed to drowning with them, and because I was beginning to understand that I wanted her there for reasons that had nothing to do with piloting.

The village stood on stilts above blackwater shallows, houses the color of weathered bone, sago palms standing behind them like a congregation. Children raced the punt along the bank, shrieking, until an old man's voice came down through the trees and the shrieking turned to laughter and retreat. Enoch Karu was waiting on the lowest platform when we tied off, as though he'd known for days. Perhaps he had. Elders on the Sepik are informed by channels deeper than radio.

He was small and dry as a stick of driftwood, with clouded eyes that didn't seem to miss anything, and he looked at me for a long time before offering his hand.

"You're the diver," he said. His Pidgin was slow and precise, each word set down like a stone in a crossing. "The one who pulled the box out of the navy wreck."

The bottom dropped out of my stomach. "How—"

"Nothing stays secret on a river. The river talks. Fish talk. Men talk worse." He lowered himself onto the platform's edge with the care of a man whose joints had opinions. "Sit. Both of you. You came a long way to ask me something, and you're afraid to ask it. So I will save you the trouble." He turned those clouded eyes on me. "The signature on your paper. Yawan Karu. Was my father."

Meja made a small sound beside me. I said nothing. There was nothing to say that the silence wasn't already saying better.

"He signed it because the soldiers put the pen in his hand," Enoch went on, "and because in those days we believed paper might protect us. The Germans taught us that. Paper, signatures, stamps — the missionaries carried their God in books, so we thought power lived in books too." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen on a living face. "Then the republic died before it was born, and the paper protected no one. Except the men who kept it."

"Mr. Karu," I began. "Enoch. I need to know—"

"What I remember." He nodded slowly. "Yes. You do. And I will tell you, diver, because you came yourself instead of sending soldiers or lawyers or men with money, and because your boat-pilot woman looks at you like she hasn't decided yet whether to trust you, which means you haven't lied to her yet. That is rare. On this river that is very rare." He rose, unhurried, and gestured toward the longhouse. "Come inside. There is a story from 1974 that you must hear before you go another kilometer upriver. And there is a question I must ask you first, and your answer will decide whether I come with you or whether you leave here alone."

We followed him up the notched ladder into the dimness, where smoke from the cooking fire hung in layers like sediment, and the whole history of the house was written in the objects around its walls — masks gone soft with age, a German hymnal swollen fat with damp, a rifle from a war nobody local had started.

Enoch settled by the fire and looked at me across it.

"My father helped sink that gold," he said. "Him and five others. They sank it so no republic could spend it and no company could steal it, and they died keeping the secret — all but him, and him only long enough to tell me, the way you tell a child something heavy, hoping he grows strong enough to carry it." The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, a woman laughed, ordinary and human, and the sound made the longhouse feel older and quieter. "So here is my question, August Dunmore. The men who sent you — the ones with the permits and the ledgers — when they find what my father drowned, what will they leave alive behind them?"

I thought of Kessler's veiled arithmetic. Of Houtman's contract with its clauses I hadn't read closely enough. Of the gunmetal color of men who treat rivers as routes and people as freight.

"Honestly?" I said. "Probably nothing."

Enoch studied me for a long time, the firelight swimming in his clouded eyes. Then he laughed — a short, dry bark, entirely without humor and somehow entirely without despair.

"Good," he said. "An honest answer. My father waited fifty years for an honest man to come asking. He would have settled for an honest coward." He pushed himself upright. "I am coming with you. Not for the consortium. Not for the gold. I am coming because whatever is down there should meet the grandson of the man who put it there before it meets anyone else." He paused at the ladder, silhouetted against the daylight. "And diver — bring your drum of diesel. The one with the false bottom. You think nobody notices a man who hides paper in fuel. Old men notice everything. We have nothing else to do."

* * *

We loaded at dawn.

There is a particular hour on the Sepik just before first light when the river goes absolutely still, mist lying on the water like a held breath, and every sound carries a kilometer — paddles, roosters, the cough of an engine being woken. We worked in that hush, and something happened among the four of us that none of us planned and none of us spoke about.

Meja directed the loading with a pilot's tyranny, redistributing weight by eye, cursing Tobe's stowage of the fuel drums in a voice that would have stripped varnish. Tobe took the cursing grinning, and re-stowed them properly, and then — thinking no one watched — went back and lashed them a second time, better, with knots no one had taught him. Enoch sat on the rail wrapped in a blanket against a chill he'd deny having, watching the mist burn off the water, and when Tobe passed him a mug of tea without being asked, the old man took it and drank and said nothing, and the nothing was warmer than most things people say.

By full light we were a crew. Not a good one yet — a crew is only good after the river has tried to kill it once — but a real one, four strays lashed to one deck, each of us carrying something hidden: my deed, Enoch's grief, Meja's Timbunke, Tobe's toolbox of borrowed history. Shared distrust of the paymasters was the only ballast we had in common, and it held.

Houtman arrived at eight sharp with the contract countersigned and a satellite phone he handled like a relic, and stood on the wharf counting us with the flat appraisal of a man inventorying equipment.

"Four," he said. "I approved three."

"You approved names on a list," I said. "These are people. They come as a set."

His jaw worked. Upstream, the mist was fully burned away now, and the river lay open and brown and patient, forty days wide and waiting.

"Cast off, then," Houtman said, and made his first call on the satellite phone before we were clear of the mooring — and I heard, just once, just a fragment carried back across the water on the morning air, the words *she's aboard* before the wind took the rest.
