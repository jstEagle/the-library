## 7. Ilana Boards at Angoram

The woman came down the Angoram ramp at half past seven in the morning, when the market smoke was still rising straight and the river lay flat as poured tin. August watched her from the deck of the *Marianne B*, a clipboard under one arm and a government-issue satchel riding her hip like it had been issued with her birth certificate. She wore trousers that had been good once, boots that had never been good at all, and the expression of someone who had already read every document in the folder she was pretending not to clutch.

"August Dunmore," she said. Not a question.

"Depends who's asking."

"Ilana Verrec. Ministerial oversight. Okuk's office." She held up a letter with a stamp on it, red wax gone soft in the heat so it looked like something wounded. "You're required to carry me."

August took the letter and read it slowly, which gave him time to think and also time to notice that the signature was real but the phrasing was not the Minister's — too clean, too legal, drafted by someone who understood permits the way a shark understands blood. The letter said oversight. What it meant was: I know there is something on this boat worth overseeing.

"There's no cabin," he said. "There's a tarp between the fuel drums and the galley, and Meja snores."

"I've slept in worse places for better pay." She stepped aboard without waiting for permission, and the *Marianne B* rocked under her weight — not much, but enough that Enoch, sitting cross-legged by the winch with his hands loose in his lap, opened his eyes. He looked at her the way he looked at the water before a storm: not alarmed, only taking its measure.

"The Karawari man," Ilana said, nodding at Enoch. "You'll be our guide's guide."

Enoch said nothing. His patience had a texture to it, August thought — like rope, old rope, the kind that holds because every fiber has already been tested.

"We leave within the hour," August said, folding the letter. "Rules are simple. You don't touch the sonar, you don't touch the dive gear, and if Houtman gives you an order, you bring it to me first. He's the consortium's man, not yours, whatever his paper says."

"And what are you, Mr. Dunmore?"

He almost smiled. "The man who knows where the sandbars sleep."

She held his gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed, then went forward to stake out her corner of the tarp, and August stood at the rail watching the market smoke bend now in the first breath of wind, and thought: Kessler sold my route to the Minister, and the Minister has sent me a lawyer instead of a gun. That meant the Minister wanted the thing found intact, documented, deniable. It meant everyone on this river wanted him to be their hands.

Behind him, Enoch spoke without turning. "That one reads people the way you read water."

"Is that bad?"

"It means she'll find out what we're hiding," Enoch said, "unless we stop hiding it from each other first." Then he closed his eyes again, and would say nothing more.

* * *

Ilana's account of the same morning began earlier, in the Minister's anteroom in Wewak, with the ceiling fan turning slow enough to count its revolutions and Okuk behind his desk like a man posing for currency.

"You'll board at Angoram," he'd said. "Dunmore's expedition. You'll represent my office. You'll observe."

"Observe what?"

Okuk had smiled then, and Ilana had catalogued the smile the way she catalogued everything — filed, dated, cross-referenced. "Whatever rises out of that river," he said. "My grandfather traded on this water. Everything that comes up belongs to somebody. My job is making sure it belongs to the right somebody. Yours is making sure nobody else writes the story first."

She had taken the assignment because refusing it would have ended her usefulness, and her usefulness was the only currency she had ever been able to hold onto. But standing now at the stern rail as Angoram fell away behind them — the sawgrass, the stilt houses, the church with its rusted roof like a wound in the green — she made herself look honestly at the river for the first time. It was not leverage. It was not a file. It was brown and enormous and utterly indifferent to the Republic of New Guinea, to the consortium, to the Minister, to her. Somewhere beneath that water was a chest of paper that three factions were prepared to spend lives on, and the river did not care, and there was something in that indifference that loosened something in her chest she hadn't known was clenched.

Then she caught August Dunmore watching her from the wheelhouse, and the clench returned.

He was harder to read than Okuk. Okuk performed power; Dunmore performed nothing at all, which was either honesty or the most expensive performance on the river. He moved like a man conserving himself, spoke like a man rationing words, and when she'd boarded he had checked her satchel with his eyes — quickly, professionally — the way she herself checked other people's briefcases. Which meant he had things to hide too, and knew she might be hunting them.

At midday she found her opening. Tobe — the boy, sixteen maybe, all elbows and engine grease — was elbow-deep in the auxiliary motor while Meja conned the boat through a braided channel, and August was below checking the dive compressor. Ilana climbed to the wheelhouse and stood beside Meja, saying nothing, watching the channel markers that weren't there.

"You're the lawyer," Meja said finally. Not friendly.

"I'm the oversight."

"Same thing out here. Nobody likes either." Meja shifted the throttle a hair. "You want to ask me about the chest."

Ilana kept her face still, which took work. "What chest?"

Meja laughed, short and unamused. "Everyone on this boat is lying about the same thing. It's almost restful." She turned back to the channel, conversation over.

Below, through the deck plates, Ilana heard the compressor cough and catch, and August's voice low, and she understood the shape of the game now: they all knew, none of them would say, and the first one to speak honestly would set the terms for everyone else. She had won negotiations on that principle twice. But those negotiations had happened in rooms with air conditioning and witnesses. Out here the river pressed against the hull like a thumb, and honesty felt less like a weapon and more like a hole in the boat.

* * *

They anchored at dusk in a cut above Korogu Point, where the channel narrowed and the current ran quiet and the mosquitoes rose off the banks in a sound like distant rain. Houtman complained about losing an hour; August ignored him with a fluency that told Ilana this was an established art. Supper was rice and tinned fish and a chili sauce Tobe guarded like state property. Enoch ate last, alone at the bow, facing upstream.

Ilana washed her plate and carried her satchel to the tarp, and that was when August appeared beside her, unhurried, holding two mugs of the terrible sweet coffee Meja brewed.

"Ministerial oversight," he said, offering one.

"Thank you." She took it. Their fingers didn't touch; both of them arranged it that way.

"You filed a land-claims case in 2019," August said. "Karawari clan boundaries, upper reaches. It went nowhere."

It was not a question, and that was the point of it. She drank the coffee rather than answer immediately. "You researched me."

"Houtman researches everyone. I read what he leaves lying around." August looked out at the water. "A lawyer who spent three years fighting for river clans doesn't take a Minister's coin for a salvage run unless she thinks there's something in it for the clans. Or unless she thinks there's something in it for her and needs the clans as cover. I haven't decided which."

"And you?" she said. "A diver who pulls scrap for a broker who pays in silence takes a consortium contract upriver to coordinates nobody will explain. I haven't decided whether you're desperate or brilliant, and I've noticed those look identical from the outside."

For a moment — a real moment, she saw it happen — something moved behind his eyes, some door opening a crack onto a room with debts in it. Then it shut.

"First watch is mine," he said. "Sleep."

She lay under the tarp listening to the boat tick and settle, the river sliding past the hull with a sound like breathing, and she did not sleep. She thought about the memo she had intercepted — *salvage recovery, Republic-era materials, priority handling* — and about the way August had flinched, barely, at the word *chest*, except he hadn't flinched, he had gone still, and stillness was worse. Stillness was a man deciding how much you already knew.

Near midnight she gave up and came out to the stern. August sat on the rail with his feet hooked in the lifeline, a torch off, just his shape against the stars.

"Can't sleep either?" he said, without turning.

"Tell me what's in the chest."

The silence lasted long enough for her to hear the water change direction around the anchor chain. When he finally spoke, his voice was level and careful, a man walking on a submerged log.

"Paperwork. Old paperwork. If it matters, Houtman's permits matter, and if it doesn't, we've all wasted forty days." He turned then, and in the dark his face was mostly absence. "You tell me what Okuk promised you, and I'll tell you what I think is down there. Trade even."

She considered it. She genuinely considered it — the offer sat in her chest like a warm coal, the first honest exchange anyone had offered her since Wewak. And that was exactly why she couldn't take it. Whatever August Dunmore thought was down there, it was worth more than her career, and she could not spend her career on a stranger's candor she might need later as ammunition.

"Not tonight," she said.

"No," he agreed quietly. "Not tonight."

She went back under the tarp. Through the thin canvas she heard him move along the rail, heard him stop, heard — faintly, unmistakably — the scrape of a fuel drum lid being checked, and the small metallic whisper of something being confirmed inside it.

* * *

It was Enoch who woke them, or rather it was the quality of Enoch's silence, which carried across a boat the way a stone carries across still water. Ilana came out into the blue-black hour before dawn and found the whole crew awake without anyone having called them: Meja at the rail, Tobe wrapped in a blanket with his eyes huge, Houtman buttoned and unhappy, August standing beside the old man at the bow.

Enoch pointed with his chin at the water.

Eyes. Dozens of them, riding the black surface around the anchored boat, catching the starlight flat and yellow-green, motionless as nails driven into the night. Crocodiles, drawn perhaps by the fish that had gathered in the lee of the hull, perhaps by nothing at all — the river's own congregation, come to look at what had come among them. They did not approach. They did not leave. They floated at the exact distance where the eye could not measure it, and the boat sat inside that ring like a secret being considered.

Nobody spoke. There was nothing to say that the eyes wouldn't hear.

"They do this sometimes," Meja whispered finally. "Whole river full of them some nights. Like a meeting."

"What are they deciding?" Tobe asked.

"That we're not food yet," Enoch said, and there was no humor in it at all.

Ilana stood at the rail with her arms crossed against the damp cold and watched the eyes, and understood suddenly that she had been looking at the wrong metaphor all day. She had thought the boat was a negotiation table. It wasn't. It was this — a small lit thing surrounded by patient attention, every party measuring every other, nobody moving first, the water black and deep beneath all of them holding whatever it held. Okuk's eyes. Kessler's eyes. Houtman's ledger. Her own ambition, which looked, from out here, uncomfortably similar to everything else circling in the dark.

Across the deck, August was watching her watch the crocodiles, and when their gazes met neither of them looked away, and neither of them smiled, and the understanding passed between them anyway, wordless and provisional: *we are both predators pretending to be passengers, and we both know it.*

Toward dawn the eyes sank away one by one, without ceremony, the way lights go out in a distant town. Enoch nodded once, satisfied, and went to wake the fire for breakfast.

They raised anchor as the mist came off the water in ropes. Two hours upriver, Meja spotted it first — a smudge of gray against the far bank, running without lights, keeping pace with them through the sawgrass.

"Whose is that?" Ilana asked.

August lowered his binoculars very slowly. "Nobody's," he said. "Yet."

But his knuckles had gone white around the glass, and Ilana, watching him, began composing in her head the first line of the report she would someday write about all of them — and wondering, for the first time in her life, whether she would be writing it as a witness or a survivor.
