## 8. The Middle Sepik Turns Against Us

The river told you things before it did them, if you knew how to listen. My mother taught me that on the Blackwater, poling a dugout through the mist before dawn — *listen to the water, Meja, it gossips worse than the market women.* The Sepik had been talking for two days now, and nobody aboard the *Kurakura* was listening.

It started with the color. The middle Sepik ran brown-gold in the dry season, tea with milk, but by the third morning out of Angoram it had gone the color of old blood, thick and muscular, carrying whole islands of torn water hyacinth down from the highlands. It carried other things too: a dead pig, bloated and turning; half a sago-leaf roof, still dripping; a child's rubber thong. The river was shedding its skin somewhere upstream, and everything it shed came past us like refugees.

I said nothing at first. I was hired to drive the boat, not to forecast the weather, and a disgraced nurse learns quickly which opinions are welcome. But when I brought August his coffee on the foredeck and saw him watching a floating fence post go by — a whole fence post, wire still attached, torn out of the ground somewhere above Ambunti — I decided some opinions were worth losing my place over.

"Water's coming up," I said.

He didn't look away from the post. "How do you know?"

"The hyacinth mats are fresh. Broken this morning, not last week. And the smell." I nodded at the river. "You can smell the clay banks underwater. That's new mud being ripped up. Somewhere up there the floodgates of heaven opened, and all of it is coming down this throat."

August took the coffee. He drank it the way he did everything, without hurry, as though the world could wait for him even if he couldn't afford to wait for it. "Houtman wants to make twenty klicks today."

"Houtman wants a lot of things."

That earned me half a smile, there and gone, like a fish rising. "Keep her in the channel, Meja. We'll watch the gauge."

The gauge rose eleven centimeters by noon. By mid-afternoon it had risen thirty more, and the channel itself began to lie to us. That's the thing about floodwater on the Sepik — it doesn't just rise, it rearranges. Sandbars walk. Channels that held a meter and a half last week hold forty centimeters today, or hold nothing at all because the river has decided to move its furniture. My depth sounder started singing its little death song every few minutes, and I found myself driving by eye and memory and the color of the boils on the surface, the way my mother would have done it, the way I swore I'd left behind when I put on a nurse's uniform and then lost it again.

Tobe hung around the wheelhouse most of the day, pretending not to be useful and being extremely useful. The boy was sixteen going on forty, all elbows and engine grease, with a face that looked like it had been assembled from spare parts and a talent for machinery that bordered on the criminal — indeed *had* been criminal, twice, before August fished him out of Angoram's police cells and gave his talents a legal outlet. He'd re-tensioned the winch cable that morning without being asked, and now he crouched beside me at the console, reading the sounder upside down.

"It's lying," he said.

"I know."

"No, I mean it's *lying*. Look — it says four meters, but there's a boil right there." He pointed at a patch of water off the port bow where the current stood up in ugly wrinkles. "Boils mean shallow. Water going over something."

"You've got good eyes, Tobe."

"I've got thief's eyes. You learn to read a room fast when you're deciding whether to run." He grinned, gap-toothed, unashamed. Being Tobe meant never having to apologize for your history; it was all currency.

I swung wide of the boil. Under us, invisible, the sandbar we'd have hit slid past like a sleeping animal. The boy went back to the engine hatch whistling, and I thought, not for the first time, that August Dunmore collected strays the way other men collected debts — compulsively, and against his own interest.

* * *

The village refused us at four o'clock.

We'd been fighting the current for hours, the *Kurakura*'s old diesel hammering, making maybe six kilometers for every ten we burned fuel, when the channel narrowed between two crumbling bluffs and a village appeared on the left bank — stilt houses, a church with a rusted roof, canoes pulled up on the mud like beached animals. Houtman wanted fuel and intelligence. There was a government station upriver, he said, and he wanted word of road conditions, water levels, anything. August wanted permission to tie up for the night, because anchoring midstream in rising floodwater is how boats die.

Enoch went ashore first, alone, as was proper. The old man moved slowly down the gangplank with his walking stick and his white hair and his terrible dignity, and the village received him the way villages receive their own — quietly, with recognition. He was Karawari, and these were Sawos people, but the river binds its nations together older than any border. We watched from the rail while he talked with a circle of elders under a house, and we watched the talk change weather. It started warm. It got cold. One of the elders stood up. Another spat into the mud.

When Enoch came back down the plank his face was closed like a fist.

"They say no," he told August. "No one may land, no one may buy fuel, no one may pass their frontage after dark."

"Why?" Houtman demanded. He came down the deck with his ledger under his arm like a weapon. "What's the price? Everything has a price on this river."

"Not this." Enoch lowered himself onto a crate with the care of a man whose joints had opinions. "Three days ago the water took a canoe at Biwat. A father and two sons. They found one son alive in a tree. The village is in mourning, and mourning villages close their doors. You don't argue with grief, surveyor. Grief was here before permits."

Houtman looked at August. August looked at the sky, which had gone the color of wet tin, and then at the river, which was climbing the bank in slow gulps.

"We anchor midstream," August said. "Double anchor, bow and stern. We'll be fine."

"We will lose a day," Houtman said.

"We'll lose less than a boat."

What Houtman said next he said quietly, which was worse than shouting. "Mr. Dunmore, the permit clock does not stop for funerals. Forty days was generous. We are nine days in. Every hour you spend being sentimental is an hour Kessler's competitors spend ahead of us." He tapped the ledger. "Remember what happens to men who cost me time."

I watched August take that. I watched him decide, visibly, the way a man decides to swallow glass, to say nothing. And I remember thinking: *this man is not free either.* None of us aboard were free. That was the one thing we all had in common, the debt-collectors and the drowned reputations and the stolen engines, and maybe that's why the crew stopped being a crew and became something else later that night. Misery recognizes its own.

We anchored midstream in the failing light, bow and stern, and the river rose around us like a tide of dark glass.

* * *

The winch snapped at dusk.

Not the anchor winch — the cargo winch on the foredeck, the one we used for lifting salvage. Tobe had re-tensioned it that morning, but tension is a promise steel makes and breaks under load, and when a floating log the size of a church pew came down the current and caught the slack cable, the winch drum took the shock and the old mounting bolts took the drum, and the whole assembly let go with a crack like a rifle. The cable whipped. The drum tore loose, bounced once off the deck, and went over the side, taking the cable with it, and Tobe — who had been greasing the fairlead not three meters away — went over too.

I heard the splash. Then I heard the absence of splash, which is the worst sound on a river: a boy in the water who isn't swimming.

Floodwater doesn't behave like water. It behaves like a crowd — shoving, panicking, full of debris moving faster than you can think. Tobe surfaced once, ten meters downstream, arms windmilling, mouth open in a shout that the noise of the current ate whole. Then the log's wake rolled him under.

I was moving before I'd decided to move. So was August. We met at the rail and he already had the life ring in his hand, and I already had the rope, and behind us Houtman's voice cracked across the deck like the winch bolt had:

"Don't you dare! Nobody goes in that water! Dunmore! The current will take you both to the coast!"

August tied the rope around his own waist in four seconds flat. His hands never shook. "Meja — take the turn. If I yell twice, haul."

"Dunmore—" Houtman started.

"If you touch that rope before I yell," August said, without looking at him, "I will put you over this rail myself."

And he went in.

I have seen men dive into the Sepik in flood since, and I have never seen it done the way August Dunmore did it that evening — no dive, no drama, just a flat committed entry like a stone thrown by God, angled downstream toward the spot where Tobe had gone under. I took the rope around the bollard and paid it out and prayed, which I hadn't done since the hospital, since the night that ended my nursing, since the inquiry that decided one dead patient needed someone to blame and chose the brown woman from the coast rather than the doctor with the family name. Prayer came back to me easily that dusk. It had only been waiting.

Thirty seconds. The rope hissed out. Forty seconds. On the bank, the village had come alive — torches appearing in the stilt houses, drums starting, low and urgent, the rhythm they beat for a drowning. Even shut-out, even refusing us passage, they were beating for our boy. I understood then that the door had never been closed against *us*, only against the noise and hunger of what we carried. Rivers teach you these distinctions if you survive them.

Then the rope went taut and jerked twice, hard, and I hauled.

They came up together, August on his back with Tobe locked across his chest in a carry I recognized from training manuals — cross-chest tow, chin high, keeping the airway clear even half-drowned — and the crew pulled them hand over hand up the hull and over the rail into a heap of shaking limbs and river water and vomit and, I confess it, my own tears, which I turned my back to hide.

Tobe coughed half the Sepik onto the deck and lived.

* * *

Here is what I remember about the hours after, in fragments, the way you remember anything that matters:

Tobe wrapped in every blanket aboard, propped against the engine housing because the warmth of it comforted him, swearing weakly that he was fine, absolutely fine, boss, and his teeth chattering so hard the words shattered. Me kneeling over him with my hands remembering what they were made for — pulse, breathing, pupils, the quiet inventory of a body — and realizing halfway through that I wasn't the disgraced nurse anymore. For those ten minutes I was just a nurse, the only kind I'd ever wanted to be, and the disgrace sat somewhere on the far bank, small and irrelevant.

August sitting down heavily beside the boy, soaked to the soul, saying nothing, doing nothing, just *being there*, his shoulder against Tobe's shoulder, the way you sit with a frightened animal. The rope burn on his waist where the line had bitten. When I treated it he didn't flinch, and when I asked why he'd gone in — a man who knew better than anyone what floodwater does, a diver who respected the river like a religion — he said, "Because he's ours," and seemed surprised to have said it aloud.

Houtman standing apart at the stern rail with his ledger, writing. Writing, during all of it. I saw him look up once when August said *he's ours*, and I saw something cross the surveyor's face that might have been envy, or might have been arithmetic — the calculation of a man who has never been anybody's, tallying what such a thing costs.

Enoch coming down the deck in the torchlight from the bank, where the village drums still spoke, and laying his hand on Tobe's wet head the way you'd lay it on a grandchild. "The river tried you tonight," the old man said. "It tries everyone it intends to keep."

And the village itself. After midnight, when Tobe was sleeping and the flood had climbed another handspan up the banks, a dugout came out to us through the dark — two men, one of them the elder who had spat at Enoch. They said nothing about passage. They simply handed across a bundle: roasted sago, smoked fish, a coconut, and a small carved figure of a crocodile with its jaws bound shut by a strip of bark. "For the boy," the elder said. "So the water knows he is claimed. Nothing takes what is claimed."

Then they paddled back into the dark, and I stood at the rail holding a bound crocodile and understood that we had crossed some line that had nothing to do with coordinates or permits. This boatload of liars and thieves and broken professionals had become, in the eyes of the river at least, a *people*. Claimed. And I looked down the deck at August sitting his lonely watch with the crocodile figure in his hands, turning it over, his face unreadable in the dark, and I wondered what it cost a man to be claimed when he was carrying secrets heavy enough to drown us all.

Because I'd seen them too, you understand. I drive the boat; I see everything. I'd seen August and Ilana Verrec circling each other since Angoram like two dogs sharing a bone neither will admit to, each waiting for the other to name what's buried. I'd seen Houtman write in his ledger by lamplight, pages he shielded with his arm. I'd seen the gunmetal glint far back in the sawgrass two dawns ago and said nothing, because saying it would make it real.

The river rose all night. Toward dawn I stood my own watch and listened to it gossip — the deep muscular hiss of it against the hull, the groan of the anchors, the far-off thunder of banks collapsing upstream — and it sounded to me less like water than like a warning being repeated in every language the basin had ever spoken.

Something was coming down this river toward us. The water knew it. I think August knew it too, because when I relieved him at first light he was standing at the bow facing upstream, into the current, into whatever the flood was bringing, and he did not turn around for a long time.
