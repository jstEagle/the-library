## 9. What Enoch Remembers

The fire was small, the way Enoch liked his fires. A big fire told the whole river where you slept. A small fire told only the smoke, and the smoke kept secrets.

He sat on an upturned fuel drum with his back against the hull of the *Marianne K*, and he watched the young ones argue about tomorrow's channel. Meja had her chart spread across a crate, weighted at the corners with four stones she had chosen carefully, and Tobe was leaning over it pointing at a bend that Enoch knew would be gone by morning, eaten or moved, because the Sepik did not keep its promises longer than a night. August stood apart, as he always stood apart, one shoulder against the wheelhouse door, listening the way a man listens when he is carrying something heavy in his chest and does not want anyone to hear it shift.

Houtman had gone to his tent early. That was good. The Dutchman's presence made the fire smaller in another way, made the talk careful and thin, like fish caught in a drying net.

Enoch waited until the argument burned itself out, the way arguments do when they are only tiredness wearing another man's face. Then he said, quietly, into the space between the crackle of the fire and the long breathing of the river:

"You want to know what is down there."

It was not a question. He had watched them all day watching him watch the water. He had seen August's eyes go, again and again, to the folded paper in the oilskin packet — the deed — and then away, quick, like a man touching a stove he has already been burned by.

Meja looked up first. "Down where?"

"Where the paper says." Enoch let the silence sit. An old man could afford silences. They were the last thing a man accumulated that no one could take from him. "You have carried that paper for eleven days now, boy." This to August. "You carry it like a man carries a snake he has not decided is dead."

August's jaw worked. The firelight found the salt-scars on his forearms, the diver's marks, the rope burns. "You know what it is," he said.

"I know whose name is signed at the bottom of it."

The river said nothing. The river never said anything; that was its oldest trick, making men believe their own voices were its voice.

"Sit," Enoch said. "All of you. I am old and my legs are bad and I will not tell this standing up like a man selling something at the market. I will tell it sitting down, like a man burying something."

They came. Even August came, though he came last and sat farthest, at the edge of the firelight where the dark could still claim part of him. Tobe dropped cross-legged close to the coals, hungry for story the way he was hungry for everything, engine parts and food and belonging. Meja folded her chart away with the care of a woman putting away a weapon she hoped not to need.

Enoch looked into the fire for a while. Not because he needed to remember. Because he needed to decide how much of himself to give away tonight, and looking into fire made the deciding easier, the way counting money makes spending easier.

"My grandfather's name was Yawan," he began. "You will not find it in any book. The books from that time wrote our names wrong or wrote them not at all. But the soldiers wrote it down once, properly, in ink, on a piece of paper, and that paper is the reason I am sitting on this drum tonight instead of sleeping in my village being an ordinary old man."

* * *

"1974," Enoch said. "I was nine years old. Old enough to carry things, too young to be asked questions. That was the best age to be, in that year. Everyone wanted carriers and no one wanted witnesses.

"The war had been finished for nearly thirty years, but the river did not know it. The river keeps what falls into it. All through those years men had been pulling things out — helmets, rifles, a landing barge near Tambanum that three villages fought over until the government took it away. And there were stories. There are always stories. Gold left behind by the Japanese, gold moved by the Australians, gold that the missionaries hid from everyone. Most of it was smoke. Smoke is cheap and every man can afford to breathe it.

"But this smoke had a smell to it.

"There was a missionary. German, from before the war, a man named Brandt who had come up the river in 1938 and stayed through everything — the Japanese occupation, the bombing, all of it — because he said God had posted him here and he did not accept transfers. The people laughed at him but they loved him too, in the way you love a man who is stubborn on your behalf. He ran the mission at Vunapope, on the stretch of water your paper calls coordinates, though we called it by its name, because a place with a church and a garden and graves deserves its name more than its numbers.

"Brandt had a barge. Not a war barge — a mission barge, flat-bottomed, for carrying copra and timber and sick people down to Angoram. In '74 that barge was old and leaking and worth nothing, which is exactly why it mattered.

"You understand, the world was ending that year. Not the real world — the paper world. Port Moresby was counting down to independence, flags were being sewn, anthems were being written by men who had never sung anything but hymns. And in the middle of it, some clever men in Wewak decided to make their own country first. The Republic of New Guinea. Did you learn this in school?" He looked at Tobe, who shook his head. "No one teaches it. A country that lived for less time than a crocodile egg takes to hatch. They printed seals. They printed deeds. They printed promises. Paper is the cheapest thing a man can make and the most expensive thing a man can die for.

"The soldiers came upriver in October. Not many — eleven, twelve men, half of them drunk on their own importance. They had heard the same smoke everyone had heard, but they had heard it from someone who had smelled real gold, and so they came with rifles and a requisition order with the republic's seal on it, ordering that all property of strategic value on the middle river be surrendered to the provisional government.

"Strategic value." Enoch spat, deliberately, into the fire, and watched the spit die. "That is what they called it. My grandfather was one of the men they came to. Yawan had been a carrier in the war — for the Japanese, then for the Australians, whichever side was nearest and least cruel that month — and he knew what soldiers' greed looked like. He had seen men kill each other over tinned meat. So when the officer put the requisition paper in front of him and said the gold hidden along this stretch belonged to the republic now, my grandfather looked at the seal on the paper and understood something the officer did not.

"He understood the country on the paper was already dead.

"It had not died yet in the calendars. But it was dead the way a man is dead after the spear goes in and before he falls. The Australians were coming back with their administration, the new government in Moresby would swallow everything, and these eleven drunk men with their printed seal were holding a claim to something that would belong to no one soon — which meant it would belong to whoever held it hardest.

"So my grandfather did the most dangerous thing a man can do. He said yes. He bowed. He helped them load.

"They went downriver to Vunapope. Brandt met them at the mission with his white beard and his Bible and his terrible German patience, and he read their paper very slowly, twice, and then he said: *Gentlemen, I will help you load your gold.* And the officer, who had expected a fight and prepared for a fight and was disappointed without knowing it, relaxed.

"That was the mistake. You must never relax around a patient man.

"For three days they loaded the barge. Gold from the wartime cache — and I will not tell you tonight where it came from, because that is another story and my voice is not strong enough for two stories — crates of it, bars wrapped in sacking and grease, sealed in drums, and on top of it all Brandt insisted on loading his mission records, his books, his vestments, the altar silver, everything, saying a mission's soul must travel with its body. The soldiers laughed at the old man fussing over his papers. They let him arrange the loading. They even let him choose where the drums sat, because weight is a mystery to drunk men and Brandt had carried cargo on that river for thirty-five years.

"On the third night, the barge lay at anchor off the mission. The soldiers celebrated, because men who have finished stealing always celebrate. Brandt sent down to the village for palm wine and my grandfather and six other men — carriers, boatmen, men whose names are written nowhere — came aboard to help secure the deck cargo for the morning run down to Wewak.

"And in the night, in the black water, with the soldiers singing on deck, the men of the river opened the river's own door.

"They had cut the bilge strakes weeks before. Do you understand? Weeks. My grandfather had helped build the repairs to that barge the year before, and he had left his work weak on purpose, the way a man leaves a fence gate unlatched for a pig he intends to catch later. Brandt knew. Brandt had watched him do it and said nothing, because Brandt had already decided whose side the gold belonged to. It belonged to the ground it came out of and the people who bled beside it. Not to a flag. Not to a seal. Not to eleven drunk men and a country that would never draw breath.

"The barge went down in ninety seconds, in eight meters of water, fifty meters off the bell tower. The soldiers drowned — most of them, the ones below decks, drunk and sleeping among the very drums they had come to steal. Some say Brandt went down with it on purpose. I say he was old and tired and he had finished his work. The ones on deck swam for it. The officer swam for it, and reached the shore, and my grandfather was waiting for him in the reeds with a paddle, and I will not tell you what happened in the reeds, except that the officer's requisition paper floated back out into the current the next morning, and my grandfather took it from the water, dried it, and kept it his whole life.

"Why?" Enoch opened his hands. "Why does a man keep the paper of the men he killed? Because paper is a debt. Because he knew that someday other men would come with other seals and other claims, and the paper would prove that the sinking was not theft. It was burial. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.

"The republic died in '75 before anyone recognized it. The claim on that gold died with it — should have died with it. The men who sank the barge agreed: no one speaks of it, no one dives for it, the river keeps it and we keep the river. My grandfather kept the secret forty years. He taught me to fish, to read water, to hold my breath until my ears sang. He never once taught me where the bell tower stood. He thought he had buried it deeper than memory.

"But a dying man's hands shake, and a shaking man writes things down, and paper outlives every promise made against it."

* * *

The fire had burned down to a red heart. Somewhere across the channel a night bird called, three notes, and got no answer.

Enoch reached inside his shirt and drew out the thing he had carried since Angoram, since before Angoram, since a lawyer woman with cold clever eyes had shown him a photocopied page and watched his face the way a hunter watches a wallow.

He held it out to August.

August took it like a man taking a live coal. The deed — the original, the true one, the one whose copy had set the whole river boiling — and beneath it, folded twice, brittle as a cicada shell, another paper. The requisition. The republic's seal, blurred but unmistakable. And across the bottom, in faded iron-gall ink, a signature: *Y. Karawari.*

"Your grandfather," August said. His voice had gone rough.

"My grandfather signed the surrender of gold he had already decided to drown." Enoch's voice did not waver; he had spent sixty years teaching it not to. "And now a Dutchman with a permit and a minister with a helicopter both hold copies of that signature, and both of them believe it makes the gold theirs. And every day that paper exists, men with guns come further up my river. So you tell me, salvage man — you who pull dead things out of dead boats for a broker who pays in silence — what does a man do with a debt that is drowning everyone who touches it?"

August looked at the papers for a long time. The firelight moved on his face and could not settle anywhere.

"You've known since Angoram," he said finally. "Since Verrec showed you the copy. You've known what the coordinates point at, and you boarded anyway."

"I boarded," Enoch said, "because if the gold must come up, I would rather it come up in front of my eyes than behind my back. And because—" and here the old man stopped, and the stopping cost him more than all the telling had, and they saw it, even Tobe saw it, thirteen years old and suddenly still. "Because my grandfather drowned that barge to keep it from men like Houtman. And if I sit ashore and let men like Houtman raise it, then everything he was dies a second time, and this time I will have helped kill it."

Meja spoke softly, into the embers. "Then why show us? Why not turn us back?"

"Because the river turns no one back. It only asks what you are carrying." Enoch rose, joints cracking like green wood, and stood over the fire with the light climbing his lined face. "My grandfather believed the gold was cursed. Cursed by the drowning, cursed by the dead men in it. I am an old man and I have buried many people and I will tell you the truth he never learned: there is no curse in gold. There is only what men do to get it, and what they do to keep it, and the curse is not in the metal — it is in us, and it travels wherever we carry it, and it does not stay behind in eight meters of water no matter how hard we wish."

He looked at August then, long and level, the look of a man passing a burden hand to hand.

"Nine days from now, maybe less, the flood comes and closes the channels, and whatever happens at that bell tower happens between now and then. So sleep. All of you. Tomorrow we go where the paper says, and I will show you the way in past the sandbars that move, because I promised my grandfather's ghost I would be there when it ended — one way or the other."

He turned toward his sleeping mat, then paused, his back to them, an old man shaped like a question mark against the dark.

"One thing more. The Dutchman's tent is dark, but dark tents have ears, and this river has carried strangers' radios before. Whatever you decide about that paper — decide it quietly."

* * *

Enoch did not sleep. Old men rarely do after giving something away; the body mistakes the lightness for falling.

He lay on his mat and listened to the camp breathe. Meja turning over twice, a woman used to watchfulness. Tobe's deep, shameless boy-sleep. August awake — he could hear it in the quality of the silence around the younger man, the way wakefulness has a sound the way water has a taste.

And beyond them, downstream, faint under the river's long murmur, the sound he had been hearing for two nights and telling himself was a log rolling on a bar.

An engine. Idling low, far off, patient as a crocodile.

Enoch closed his eyes and did the arithmetic of his grandfather's last years, the sums a man does when he understands he has handed his children a debt instead of a gift. The barge down there in the cold green dark. The bones arranged on the crypt floor — he had seen the sonar image August tried to hide, and he had known instantly what the arrangement meant, because his grandfather had described it once, drunk on grief, to a boy who pretended to sleep: *we laid them out straight, all of them, so the river would know it was burial and not murder.*

Burial, not murder. That distinction had held for fifty years.

He listened to the distant engine and understood, with the certainty of a man who has watched enough dry seasons end, that somewhere out in the sawgrass someone else had done the same arithmetic — and that the days of burial were finished, and the days of reckoning, which his grandfather had postponed with a paddle and a cut strake, had finally come due.

Tomorrow they would run the moving sands past the bell tower.

Tonight, something was already running the river toward them.
