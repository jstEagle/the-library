## 10. Kessler's Second Boat

The sawgrass began where the river forgot its manners.

For three days the Sepik had been widening into a sprawl of oxbows and dead channels, the water braiding itself around islands that were less land than suggestion — mud and cane and the roots of sago palms standing hip-deep in their own reflection. The chart Meja kept rolled in a plastic sleeve above the wheel showed this stretch as a smear of blue with a single dotted line through it, and even that line carried the annotation *surveyed 1968, unreliable*. Beyond it, the paper simply gave up.

August stood at the bow of the *Marianne K* with a mug of tea gone cold in his hand and watched the grass move against the wind.

That was the thing. The wind was coming out of the northeast, steady as it had been all morning, and every stand of sawgrass along the southern bank leaned with it — every stand except one, two hundred meters back from the channel, where a patch of cane bent the wrong way. Not violently. Just enough. The way grass leans when something heavy has passed through it recently and hasn't finished recovering.

He didn't say anything. He set the mug down on the deck plate, walked aft past the winch housing where Tobe was supposed to be greasing the cable and was instead reading a comic book inside an oil drum for shade, and climbed to the wheelhouse.

Meja had the wheel. She always took the morning watch, said her hands were steadiest before the heat came up, and August had learned not to argue with a pilot about her own watch. She was a broad-shouldered woman in her forties with a nurse's economy of movement and a face that gave away nothing she didn't choose to give away, which after Angoram was a quality he'd come to value more than any certificate.

"Grass on the south bank," he said quietly. "Two hundred meters back. Moving wrong."

She didn't look up from the channel. "How long?"

"Since before I noticed."

"That's usually how it works." She adjusted the wheel a degree, unhurried, and let her eyes flick sideways once — a single sweep of the bank, cataloguing, dismissing, filing. "Could be pigs. Could be a croc dragging off. Could be nothing."

"And?"

"And it could be a boat running its engine at idle behind the cane, holding station on us." She said it the way she'd have read out a blood pressure. "There's a slough back there. I can see the cut in the canopy from here if I know what I'm looking at. Somebody who knew this water could sit in that slough all day and watch us go by without showing so much as a windshield."

"Somebody like who?"

Meja's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "You're the one carrying the paperwork, Dunmore. You tell me who'd want to know where you're going."

He looked back at the bank. The grass had settled now, or stopped mattering, or been told to stop mattering by whoever was minding it. Houtman sat under the awning amidships with his ledger open on his knees, making his small pencil marks, a man pretending with tremendous discipline that he was on a survey and not a treasure hunt. Enoch was forward, smoking, watching the water the way old men watch water — as if it owed him an explanation.

"I'm going to run a test," August said.

"What kind?"

"The honest kind." He turned for the ladder. "Kill the radio."

* * *

They'd been transmitting position reports twice daily since Angoram — short, coded bursts to Houtman's consortium office in Wewak, per the contract, plus Meja's own weather calls to any station listening. It was routine. It was also, August understood now, a trail of breadcrumbs laid on the water for anyone with a receiver and a reason.

At noon he had Tobe pull the main set's fuses and wrap the antenna feed in a rag soaked in bilge water, a sabotage crude enough to be believable and complete enough to be final. Then they ran silent for six hours, no transmissions, while the *Marianne K* ground upriver through the heat at her usual four knots.

At 18:40, with the light going copper and the birds starting their evening argument in the rookery trees, the launch came out of the sawgrass.

It came out two kilometers astern and slightly east, sliding from a side channel with the casual confidence of something that had never once worried about being seen. Gunmetal grey, low-slung, maybe fifteen meters, with a cabin block and a radar arch and engines that made a sound like a dentist's drill amplified through a tin roof. No name on the transom that August could make out. No flag. It held their course, held their speed, and settled into their wake exactly the way a remora settles onto a shark — close enough to be fed, far enough not to be touched.

Tobe spotted it first and came up the wheelhouse ladder two rungs at a time, forgetting to be cool, forgetting everything except the fact of it. "Boat," he said. "Behind us. Been there — I don't know how long, I just saw it—"

"I know," August said. "I've got it."

Houtman appeared in the doorway with his ledger still in hand, and for one unguarded second his face did something interesting: it went pale, then it went careful, then it went blank, in that order, like a man flipping through responses until he found the one that fit. "Probably a fisheries patrol," he said.

"At dusk? In a dead channel?" Meja didn't turn from the wheel. "Fisheries patrols don't run triple outboards, Mr. Houtman. And they don't shadow cargo boats through grass nobody's surveyed since before independence."

"It could be Okuk's people," Ilana offered from the rail. She'd come up quietly, notebook closed, and August noted that she'd positioned herself where she could see both the launch and Houtman. She was getting better at that. Or worse. "The minister has resources."

"Okuk's people would announce themselves," Houtman said. "He wants oversight. Oversight likes paperwork."

"Then who?" Ilana asked, sweetly, and let the question sit there like a hook in the water.

Houtman went below. That answered more than any words would have.

August stayed at the stern rail until full dark, watching the launch's running lights burn red and green in the black water, patient as coals. Whoever they were, they weren't hurrying. They didn't need to hurry. A river only goes one way, and they knew which way he was going, because somewhere between Wewak and the sawgrass, someone had sold them the route.

And August knew exactly who had something worth selling.

Kessler. Of course it was Kessler. The broker who paid in silence had finally found a buyer loud enough to pay in something better. Okuk's rivals — or Okuk himself wearing a rival's shirt — or some third party entirely; it hardly mattered. What mattered was the arithmetic underneath: August was no longer guiding an expedition. He was leading a procession, and everyone in it had paid admission except him.

* * *

They anchored that night in the lee of a nameless island, generators down, running lights doused, the crew moving on deck by feel and moonlight. The launch anchored too, two kilometers back, its lights a faint smudge on the water like a cigarette left burning on a table.

August called a meeting in the wheelhouse that he did not call a meeting, because meetings implied plans and he had none. Meja came because she was always there. Tobe came because Tobe went where Meja went. Enoch came because Enoch had been waiting for them to catch up to what he already knew. Ilana came because she made it her business to be where information changed hands. Houtman did not come, which told August he'd guessed correctly about whose meeting it was.

"They've been on us since the sawgrass," August said. "Maybe longer. Maybe since Angoram."

"The radio," Meja said. "We've been broadcasting our position twice a day like good little children. Anyone with a set and a chart could've followed us blindfolded."

"So we go dark," Tobe said. "We already pulled the fuses—"

"We go dark, they come closer, because dark means we're hiding and hiding means we're worth catching up to." Meja shook her head. "No. We do something stupider than hiding. We get lost."

Enoch laughed. It was a dry sound, like wind through cane, and it surprised everyone including, possibly, Enoch.

"You mean it," Ilana said slowly. "You're talking about leaving the charted channel."

"I'm talking about the channels that aren't on anybody's chart," Meja said. "My grandmother fished these backwaters before the war. There's a network of sloughs and cutoffs through the middle basin that connect when the water's high enough, and right now—" she tapped the window, where the river gleamed wide and swollen under the moon, "—the water is high enough. If we take them, no launch with a meter of draft follows us. And no radio signal matters, because nobody knows where we are. Including us, half the time."

"And the risk?" Ilana asked.

"We ground out on a sandbar that moves overnight. We take a channel that dead-ends in a wall of grass and spend two days backing out. We meet a logjam with the whole river's flood behind it." Meja counted them off on her fingers, a nurse listing symptoms. "Or we lose the permit window sitting in a cul-de-sac while Houtman has a stroke on the foredeck. Forty days, remember. We've spent eleven."

Silence. Somewhere out in the dark a crocodile slapped the water, once, like a door closing.

"Enoch," August said. "Your people fished these waters."

The old man took his time. He always took his time; you learned that the pauses were where he kept the important parts. "My grandfather's canoe trails are older than your charts," he said. "Some of them the river has closed. Some of them the river keeps open because the river forgets to close them." He looked at August with eyes that had gone pale as driftwood. "But understand what you're asking. Out there, the map is memory. My memory, and I am old, and my memory has holes in it like a net."

"Better holes than a tail," Meja said.

"There's another thing," Enoch said, and paused, and August felt the pause like a change in current. "A boat that follows a known channel is a boat that expects to arrive. A boat that goes where the charts end—" he shrugged one shoulder, "—that boat can be met. Ambushed at a narrows. Waited for at a junction. The men who follow us know the big water. They do not know the small water. But small water has its own teeth."

"Everything on this river has teeth," Tobe said. "That's why I like it."

August looked around the wheelhouse — the pilot with her ruined reputation, the kid with his stolen engines, the elder with his inherited grief, the lawyer with her copied secrets, and himself with a deed hidden in a fuel drum and a lie growing in his chest like a tumor. None of them should have trusted each other. All of them were about to bet their lives on each other anyway, because the alternative was a gunmetal launch and whatever waited aboard it.

"First light," he said. "We take the cutoffs. Meja picks the water, Enoch picks the memory, and nobody tells Houtman until the anchor's up."

* * *

Houtman took it exactly as badly as August had hoped, which was to say loudly and then quietly, and the quiet part was worse.

"You're deviating from the agreed route," he said on the foredeck, dawn coming up grey behind him, the ledger clutched to his chest like a breviary. "The contract specifies—"

"The contract specifies arrival at coordinates within forty days," August said. "It doesn't specify the road. Your permits don't care which water we float on. And unless you'd like to explain to those gentlemen astern why we led them straight to your gold, I suggest you help me cast off."

Houtman looked back at the launch. In the early light it was visible now in detail: the grey hull, the figures on deck — four, maybe five, too distant to count weapons but close enough to count silhouettes, and every silhouette was standing very still, watching, the way herons watch the shallows. Something moved behind Houtman's eyes, some private recalculation, and August thought: *he recognizes them.* Thought: *he knows exactly whose boat that is, and it isn't the minister's.*

But he said nothing, and neither did Houtman, and the silence between them filled up with the sound of the river.

They slipped the anchor at 06:15 and took the cutoff at 06:20.

It opened off the main channel like a secret — a gap in the sawgrass barely wider than the *Marianne K*'s beam, invisible from ten meters, marked only by the way the current leaned into it and a heron standing sentinel on a fallen palm as if posted there on purpose. Meja brought them in with the throttle at idle and her whole body leaning into the wheel like a woman dancing with a heavy partner, and the banks closed over them, and the main river, and the launch, and the world of charts and permits and witnesses, all of it dropped away behind a wall of green.

For an hour it was almost beautiful. The slough ran narrow and brown and glass-still, the canopy meeting overhead in places, orchids hanging from branches that leaned out over water the color of strong tea. Parrots argued in the high limbs. A monitor lizard the length of a canoe slid off a log ahead of them and vanished without a ripple. Tobe hung over the bow rail grinning like a boy at a carnival, and even Ilana put her notebook away and just looked.

Then the channel forked, and forked again, and the beauty acquired edges.

Meja navigated by things August couldn't see: the color of the water, the lean of the current, a notch in a tree that might have been natural or might have been a century-old canoe marker. Enoch stood beside her and supplied the memory — *left here, my grandfather said, when the fig tree still stood; the fig tree is gone, so trust the sandbar instead* — and between the two of them they wove the boat through a labyrinth that existed nowhere on paper.

At midday they entered a reach so narrow the hull brushed grass on both sides, and August heard it: a sound from deep in the green, upstream and downstream both, faint but unmistakable. Engines. Idling. More than one.

Meja heard it too. She killed their own engine and let them drift, and in the silence the sound resolved — two boats, maybe three, positioned at the junctions ahead and behind, sweeping, searching, the way dogs work a field. Someone had guessed. Someone had always known these waters too, or knew them well enough, and had moved to cut the maze before the mouse got through.

Enoch's face, when August looked at him, had gone very still and very old.

"There is a third way," the old man said quietly. "My grandfather spoke of it only once, and he crossed himself when he said it. The drowned channel. Where the river used to run before it turned, under the roots, through the place the mission people called—" he searched for the word, found it, and gave it like a stone, "—the Throat. It connects to the Vunapope approach. It is narrow. It is dark. Things live in it that I will not name in daylight."

Ahead, through the screen of cane, sunlight flashed off a grey hull crossing the junction.

"How narrow?" August asked.

"Narrow enough," Enoch said, "that we will hear the river breathing on both sides of us. Narrow enough that if we meet anything coming the other way, one of us does not continue."

Meja was already turning the wheel. "Then we'd better pray we don't meet anything," she said, "coming either way."

The *Marianne K* slid toward the gap in the roots, into the green dark, and behind them the engines of the hunting boats grew louder, patient, certain — certain, August thought, of a maze that had just grown a door no one had told them about.
