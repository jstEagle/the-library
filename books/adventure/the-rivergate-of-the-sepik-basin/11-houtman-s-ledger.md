## 11. Houtman's Ledger

The rain came at dusk the way it always did on the middle Sepik — not falling so much as arriving, a warm grey curtain that turned the river and the sky into one continuous thing. Ilana Verrec sat under the expedition boat's canvas awning with a case file on her knees and listened to the water hammer the deck, and she thought, not for the first time, that she had spent eleven years learning to read documents the way other people read weather. A clause was a cloud bank. A signature was a change in the wind. And somewhere in the papers stacked in Bram Houtman's cabin, she was almost certain, a storm was waiting with her name on it.

She had come aboard with a minister's commission and a lawyer's caution, and she had kept both folded close, the way you keep matches dry in the tropics. Okuk wanted heirs found before the consortium found them. The consortium wanted a guide who knew the river. And she wanted — she had told herself she wanted — the Karawari claim filed clean, legitimate, untouchable. That was the story. She had told it so many times on the flight from Port Moresby that it had begun to feel load-bearing.

The truth was less comfortable. The truth was that she looked at the Sepik and saw jurisdiction.

* * *

Houtman kept his cabin locked, which was itself an answer to a question nobody had asked. The other men aboard — the two surveyors, the quiet one called De Vries who cleaned his rifle more often than the humidity required — slept in the hold among the crates marked GEOLOGICAL EQUIPMENT in stencilled letters that had been painted over an older stencil. Ilana had noticed the older letters on the second day. She had a lawyer's habit of noticing what people tried to hide under paint, because paint was the oldest legal instrument there was.

On the ninth night upriver, the rain gave her her chance.

Houtman went forward to argue with the pilot about anchorage — the man had a genius for manufacturing arguments, Ilana had learned, arguments that kept everyone's eyes on him while his hands did something else. The deck was a river of running water. The generator coughed. And the padlock on Houtman's cabin door, she discovered when she tried it with two fingers, was the cheap brass kind sold in every trade store from Angoram to the coast, the kind that rusted shut and rusted open and could be persuaded with a flat screwdriver and patience.

She had a flat screwdriver. She had learned patience in courtrooms, waiting for witnesses to lie themselves into corners.

The lock gave on the fourth try with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and she was inside, and her heart was going hard enough that she could feel it in her teeth.

The cabin smelled of diesel and insect repellent and, faintly, of the leather-and-paper smell of a man who still kept actual ledgers in an age of satellites. Houtman was old consortium, Dutch on one side and colonial on the other, a man whose family had been extracting things from this country for three generations — gold, copper, timber, labour — and who spoke of sustainability the way his grandfather had spoken of civilization. He kept his papers in a steel document case under the bunk. Of course he did. Men like Houtman did not trust the cloud; the cloud could be subpoenaed.

The case was unlocked. That surprised her, and then it didn't. He had a padlock on the door and nothing on the papers because the padlock was theatre, and the real security was the assumption that no one aboard would dare. Ilana had built a career on the assumption that everyone dared, given sufficient reason.

She lifted the ledger out and set it on the bunk and opened it to the light of her phone's torch, cupped in her palm like something stolen, which it was.

* * *

The first thirty pages were what she had expected: survey grids, core sample logs, assay figures for alluvial deposits along a stretch of the Sepik that the consortium had been quietly mapping for two years. Boring, legal, deniable. She turned the pages faster. Then, at page thirty-one, the handwriting changed. The neat surveyor's capitals gave way to a tighter, harder script, and the entries stopped being about geology.

*Concession ref. RNG 1974/9. Status: never extinguished. Republic collapsed before ratification. Deed held: D. (recovered Angoram, Aug.)*

Ilana went very still. Rain filled the silence around her, drumming the deck above, running in cold threads down the back of her neck where the canvas didn't reach. *D. recovered Angoram, Aug.* Dunmore. August. The diver with the dredge and the drowned look, the man who had signed Houtman's contract with the reluctance of a man signing his own indictment.

She read on, and the river seemed to lean in around her.

*Expedition objective (formal): locate and verify cache per deed coordinates. Expedition objective (actual): recovery of bullion, est. 400+ bars, wartime provenance, title clouded. Legal exposure: HIGH. Mitigation: see annex C.*

Annex C was a single folded sheet, typed, unsigned. She unfolded it and read it twice because the first time she had assumed she was misreading.

*Upon successful recovery, all persons with knowledge of the concession's existence or contents are to be managed. "Managed" is defined per standard contingency. Priority list follows. Note: the lawyer (V.) represents potential rival claimants. If V. cannot be detached from the Karawari matter by completion of the expedition, she is to be included in the general mitigation. The minister's cooperation has been secured through K. and is contingent on his deniability, which ends at the recovery. After recovery, the minister is also a liability.*

*The guide (D.) knows the contents of the chest. He signed under duress of debt. Debt is sufficient control for the river phase. It is not sufficient control afterward. Plan for the afterward.*

*The elder (E.) — see attached genealogy. Grandson of a signatory. If the cache is recovered, the Karawari claim dies with him. He is old. The river is dangerous. No action required; the river is patient.*

Ilana sat down on Houtman's bunk because her legs had made the decision without consulting her. She was aware, in a distant professional way, of how her mind worked under shock: it went to language. *Standard contingency. General mitigation. No action required.* The grammar of murder, dressed in the passive voice so that no one ever had to be the one who did it. She had read this grammar before — in mining company filings, in government memoranda, in the quiet annexes of agreements that transferred mountains from one set of hands to another. She had always told herself that reading it was a form of fighting it.

Tonight, reading it, she understood that she was on the list. That her name — *the lawyer (V.)* — sat in a ledger between a debt-enslaved diver and an old man whose death had been pre-authorized by a clause about the river's patience.

And beneath that understanding, quieter and more shameful, a second thought rose like silt in disturbed water:

*You are still safer on this boat than off it.*

Because Houtman had written *if V. cannot be detached* — which meant there was a version of events, a compliant version, in which Ilana Verrec walked away rich and unbothered, her firm's retainer doubled, her name cleared of the Karawari entanglement, her career advancing on the wreckage of people who had trusted the river to protect them. Okuk had offered her legitimacy. Houtman, it appeared, was offering her the same thing with better margins.

She sat in the rain-noise and the diesel smell and let both offers sit on her chest, weighing them honestly, because she had promised herself years ago that she would never lie to herself in the privacy of her own skull. Lies told to oneself were the only lies that never came up in cross-examination, and they were the ones that convicted you in the end.

* * *

She thought about her father.

He had been a lands officer in the old administration, a minor man with a major stamp, and he had signed away — with a rubber stamp and a cup of tea — a stretch of coast that three villages had fished for a thousand years. He had not thought of it as theft. He had thought of it as procedure. He had died comfortable, pensioned, respected, and Ilana had built her entire career on the refusal to become him. That was the story she told at conferences. That was the story she believed, most days.

But her father had also taught her, without meaning to, the other lesson: that the stamp always lands on someone, and the person holding it is never the one it falls on. Every document she had ever filed, every claim she had ever advanced, had moved weight from one set of shoulders to another. She had chosen whose shoulders, and told herself the choosing was justice. Okuk had chosen her the way you choose a tool. Houtman had filed her under *liability, conditional.* And August Dunmore — she had watched him for days now, watched him run his boat and his crew with the careful, exhausted competence of a man holding a family together with baling wire — August had never chosen anything except survival, and the river had chosen the rest.

*Plan for the afterward.* The words sat in her mind like a hook set in flesh.

If she warned August tonight, what happened? He was not a fool; he would believe her, or he would pretend to, and either way the expedition would change shape. Houtman would notice the change of shape. Houtman's quiet man De Vries would begin cleaning his rifle in her sightline instead of at the edge of it. And the Karawari claim — Enoch's claim, the old man's patient, dignified, decades-long claim to the mission lands and the memory of what his grandfather had done — would die in a channel upriver, unfiled, unmourned, another thing the Sepik swallowed.

If she said nothing — if she kept the ledger's secret in her own locked cabin of the heart and played her position to the end — she survived. She might even win. There were versions of the afterward where Ilana Verrec stood in a courtroom in Port Moresby with the concession dead and the consortium discredited and her own hands clean enough for the record, having said nothing, done nothing, risked nothing, while the people on this boat were *managed* one by one by a patient river.

She looked down at the ledger in her lap and understood that she was already deciding, had been deciding since she opened it, and that the decision was the compromise itself: not to warn, not to betray, but to *copy.* To take the evidence and hold it, and tell herself that holding it was the same as using it. That was what her father would have done. That was what everyone did. Information was power, and power deferred was power preserved, and the people it was preserved against could drown in the meantime.

The rain eased. Somewhere forward, Houtman's voice rose and fell in its manufactured argument, and she heard August's deeper voice answer it, flat and unhurried, a man who had learned that the river always won arguments eventually.

* * *

She worked fast after that, and she worked well, because fear and shame are both fuels and she had a full tank of each.

The ledger's pages photographed poorly in torchlight — shadow-banded, the tight script dissolving at the edges — so she copied the critical entries by hand into the back of her own notebook, in her own compressed legal shorthand, the writing she had developed in law school for lectures that came too fast. *RNG 1974/9, never extinguished. Recovery then mitigation. V. conditional. E. — river patient. Minister deniable, ends at recovery.* She copied the annex entire, every euphemism, every passive construction, preserving the grammar of it exactly, because in court the grammar would hang them. *Managed is defined per standard contingency.* Let three judges read that sentence aloud and watch their faces.

She put the ledger back in the steel case, squared it to the dust-shadow lines on the case's floor the way she'd noted them, closed the lid, and let herself out. The padlock she re-hung with the screwdriver's help, and it caught on the third try, and she stood a moment in the rain with her forehead nearly touching the cabin door, listening to her own breathing.

Then she walked the length of the deck, past the hold with its double-stencilled crates, past the galley where Tobe — the boy, the engine thief, all elbows and watchfulness — sat mending a fuel line by lamplight. He looked up at her as she passed, and his eyes went to her hands, to the notebook under her arm, and something moved in his face that was not quite suspicion and not quite anything she could name. Children on the river learned to read adults the way she read documents. She smiled at him, and the smile cost her more than the copying had.

In her own bunk, she lay with the notebook beneath her and the rain returning, harder now, a real storm building over the middle Sepik, lightning walking the horizon in long silent strokes. She rehearsed the future the way she'd rehearse a closing argument. *I copied the ledger to protect the claim. I intended to act. I was gathering strength.* All true. None of it sufficient. She knew the difference between evidence held and justice done, and she knew which side of that line she had just chosen to sleep on.

And yet.

And yet there was the other arithmetic, the one she could not put down. Houtman's plan required the expedition to succeed first — required the gold up, the coordinates confirmed, the afterward to begin. Every day the cache stayed buried was a day the mitigation stayed theoretical. If the recovery stalled — if the permit clock ran down, if the river did what the river did — then the list in annex C stayed a list instead of a schedule. She was not saving anyone by her silence. She was buying time. She told herself that buying time was a thing lawyers did, that timing was everything in litigation, that the difference between a leak and a flood was a matter of when you opened the gate.

The lightning came closer. Under it, she heard the anchor chain shift, the hull breathe against the current, and beneath all of it the deep river sound, that vast slow swallowing the Sepik made day and night, the sound Enoch sat with in the evenings, facing it, patient as his own name.

She thought of the old man's hands, scarred and certain, turning the deed's paper as if it were the skin of someone he had known. *He is old. The river is dangerous. No action required.*

She pressed her palm flat against the notebook through the canvas of her bunk and made herself one promise, small and legal and already hedged: *if it starts, if the afterward begins while we are still on this river, I will not be quiet.* She did not specify to whom she made the promise, or what penalty attached to its breach. She was, after all, a lawyer. She knew how to draft an obligation she could later argue was unenforceable.

The storm passed over them around midnight, and the river rose a hand's width by dawn, brown and fast and full of the mountains it had eaten. When Ilana came out on deck, August was already at the bow reading the water, and Houtman was checking his instruments, and the expedition turned its face upriver toward Vunapope and whatever the drowned mission held.

She caught August's eye once, across the deck, and held it a half-second longer than accident required. He frowned slightly — a man used to being watched by creditors, not colleagues — and looked back at the current.

She had the ledger in her head now, every line of it. She had her own name in it, and his, and Enoch's, and the minister's, all filed under *afterward.*

She only hoped the afterward would wait for her to be brave.
