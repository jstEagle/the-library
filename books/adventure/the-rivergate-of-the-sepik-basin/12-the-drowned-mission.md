## 12. The Drowned Mission

The channel changed its mind sometime in the night.

I knew it before I opened my eyes — the sound of the water against the hull had gone from the broad, patient slap of the main river to something tighter, quicker, a hiss like breath drawn through teeth. Meja was already at the wheel when I came up into the grey light, one hand on the throttle, her face lit green by the depth sounder's dying glow.

"It moved," she said, without turning around.

"Rivers do that."

"Not like this. Not a whole channel." She tapped the sounder with one fingernail, twice, as if scolding it. "Houtman's chart says we should be running down the middle of forty metres of brown. We're standing over nine."

Nine metres. I looked over the side and saw nothing but the Sepik doing what the Sepik had always done — eating its own banks and spitting them out somewhere else, rearranging the world while men slept. Somewhere under that tea-dark water was ground that had been dry within living memory. Ground that had held buildings. Ground that had held people.

Ground that had held a church.

* * *

We anchored where Enoch told us to, off a point of rotting sago palms that leaned over the water like old men listening for their names to be called. Houtman wanted to push straight in on the coordinates; I told him the channel had shifted and he looked at me the way men look at instruments they don't trust — which is to say, the way they look at other men.

"The deed gives coordinates," he said. He had the paper out again, the third time that morning, holding it flat against his knee so the wind couldn't take it. Forty days had become twenty-six, and every hour he aged a little more visibly, the skin around his eyes tightening like shrink-wrap.

"The deed was written by men standing on land," I said. "There is no land there now. You want the gold or you want the argument?"

He wanted the gold. Men like Houtman always wanted the gold; the argument was just what they used to justify drowning for it.

Enoch stood at the bow rail with his hands folded on the rail's worn wood, watching the point of palms come closer. He hadn't spoken much since the fire two nights back, since he'd told us about 1974 — the soldiers, the German missionary's barge, the men who sank the wealth rather than let a republic that never drew breath claim it. His grandfather had been one of those men. Enoch carried that the way you carry a stone in your chest: not painfully, exactly, but always, always aware of the weight.

"There," he said quietly, and pointed.

At first I saw only water. Then the current showed me what it had been hiding — a seam in the brown surface, a long ridge of disturbed flow running maybe sixty metres out from the bank, the way water breaks over anything that refuses to move. And rising through the middle of that seam, black and streaming, a shape.

A tower.

It broke the current like the rib of some drowned leviathan, brick and coral-stone construction gone soft-edged with fifty years of river, but unmistakable — arched belfry openings, three of them, two still open to the sky and one choked with a growth of water-fern that hung down like hair. A cross had stood at the peak once. Only the socket remained, and a length of rusted iron stubble where the arms had been torn away or rotted free.

Nobody spoke. Even Tobe, who could not keep quiet through a funeral or a fuel stop, said nothing. Meja cut the engine and we drifted the last hundred metres in silence, the way you approach a grave.

Vunapope. The mission that the maps still marked and the river no longer honored. Sometime in the decades after the war the channel had swung wide, taken the mission grounds, swallowed the church whole, and moved on — leaving behind this: a bell tower standing in nine metres of moving water, the rest of God's house somewhere beneath us in the dark.

"That's why they sank it here," Enoch said. His voice was very low, almost lost under the lap of water on the hull. "Not in deep water. Not far away. Here. Under the church. Where it would be watched."

"Watched by who?" Tobe asked.

"By everyone who ever loved this place," Enoch said. "And by no one, forever."

* * *

Houtman wanted divers in the water inside the hour. I made him wait until mid-morning, when the sun stood high enough to throw some light past the first metre of silt-brown, because I have dived enough drowned things to know that the difference between a careful dive and a careless one is usually the difference between coming up and not.

I went down first. It was my expedition to run, whatever the permits said, and besides — I did not entirely trust what we would find, and if something down there was going to frighten a man into bolting for the surface, better it frighten me alone.

The water took me the way the Sepik always does: completely, without ceremony. Warm at the surface, cooler at two metres, then a darkness that thickened rather than deepened, brown giving way to a green-black murk where my torch beam didn't cut so much as suggest. I followed the shot line down hand over hand, equalizing, watching the tower grow out of the gloom above me — no, below me, all wrong, inverted, a building hanging upside down from the surface like a reflection that had decided to stay.

At six metres the roof resolved. Terracotta tiles, most of them still locked together after half a century underwater, furred with silt and a fine black weed that waved in the current like a field of wheat seen from beneath. At seven metres I passed through the broken lantern of the nave roof, through a gap where a bomb or a storm or simple gravity had opened the church to the sky it now lived under, and dropped into the body of the drowned mission.

My torch found pews first.

They were still ranked in their rows, oak gone black as bogwood, marching away from me toward an altar I could not yet see, buried to their seat-backs in accumulated silt. Fish — small silver darts of them, hundreds — poured through the aisle windows and scattered around my light like sparks. A freshwater crocodile slid out of the shadows near the transept, unhurried, utterly unimpressed by me, and poured itself through a window gap into the open channel. Everything down here behaved as though the church were simply a part of the river now, another feature of its country, and I was the intruder.

I swam down the central aisle. My fins stirred the silt and the murk bloomed, and for a moment I hung in a cloud of my own making, blind, breathing hard through the regulator, hearing only my own heart and the small electric crackle of the river. Then the cloud settled and the altar appeared.

It was stone. It had been brought from somewhere else — I could see the pale grain of it, coral limestone, nothing local — and it had been set on a stepped platform that the silt had half claimed. Carved into its face, still legible under a skin of algae, was a single word in Latin, and beneath the Latin, smaller, in German: a date. 1938. The missionaries had built their altar to outlast everything, and everything had indeed outlasted it except the water.

The deed's coordinates put the cache directly beneath the altar. Of course they did. Beneath the altar, in the crypt, in the dark under God's own table — the safest place the men of 1974 could imagine, and the most blasphemous, and perhaps those were the same thing to men who had decided that no nation, living or dead, deserved what they carried.

The crypt entrance was behind the altar, where such entrances always are: a stair going down, now flooded, its arch still intact. I hung at the top of that stair for a long moment, breathing, letting my light run ahead of me down the steps into a darkness even the torch seemed reluctant to enter.

Then I went down.

* * *

The crypt was small — a barrel-vaulted chamber, maybe eight metres by five, built to hold the mission's dead. The air in my tank gauge read fine. The water in there was colder, stiller, almost clear compared to the nave, as though the vault had spent fifty years refusing the river's sediment, keeping its own counsel.

My light swept the floor and stopped.

Bones.

Not scattered. Not heaped the way floodwater leaves things, tangled and random. Arranged.

They lay in rows along the walls — skulls facing inward, long bones laid parallel between them, ribcages collapsed into lace — dozens of them, scores, ordered with a care that made the back of my neck prickle inside the hood. This was not a grave. Graves are dug in haste or in grief, and even the most loving burial has a looseness to it, a human untidiness. This was geometry. This was intention. Someone had come down into this chamber, in the dark, in the last days of a republic that never existed, and had laid out the dead in ranks like soldiers on parade, or like words in a sentence.

And in the centre of the floor, formed of bones themselves — femurs and humeri laid end to end, a skull at each compass point — was a shape.

A cross.

No. I moved closer, my light trembling slightly, and corrected myself. Not a cross. A cross with a bar beneath it — the double-barred patriarchal cross, the kind carved over Orthodox graves, the kind stamped on old Russian trade goods that came up the river a century ago. Or perhaps not that either. Perhaps it was only what my eye needed it to be, because the alternative was that it meant nothing, and nothing frightened me more.

Around the bone-cross, pressed into the silt in a great circle, were objects. I lifted one carefully: a clay pipe bowl, intact. Another: a brass cartridge casing, wartime, corroded green. A child's shoe, leather gone to wax. A broken rosary, its beads scattered in an arc like a comet's tail. A rusted entrenching tool. A hymnal fused into a single block of pulp. Each object placed deliberately, each one ordinary, each one a life.

It was a message. I understood that with a certainty that arrived in my stomach before my head. Whoever had arranged these bones had not been burying the dead. They had been leaving a warning, or a testimony, or a curse — written in the only language that would still be legible when the paper rotted and the maps dissolved and the men who remembered were themselves bones. *Here lie the ones who paid.* Or: *Turn back.* Or perhaps simply: *We were here, and it cost everything, and it will cost you too.*

I hung there in the cold still water of the crypt, in the middle of a sentence written in skeletons, and felt awe and violation arrive together, inseparable, like twins. Awe, because I had never seen anything made by human hands so patient, so certain of being found. Violation, because I had come with a salvage rig and a contract and a man on the surface checking his watch, and everything about me — my tanks, my torch, my purpose — was a desecration. These dead had not been waiting for me. They had been waiting for whoever came next, and they had left instructions, and I already knew that Houtman's instructions and theirs were not the same.

Something touched my shoulder and I nearly tore my regulator out.

It was the shot line, swinging in a slow current eddy, brushing me like a finger. I got hold of it, steadied myself, and made myself look one last time at the bone-cross in the centre of the floor. At the skull at its head, facing the stair, facing up, facing me.

Its jaw was open. Fifty years underwater and the jaw was open, and I know — I know — that jaws fall open, that it means nothing, that the ligaments slacken and gravity does its work. But in that moment, in that cold vault under the altar of a drowned church, it looked like the skull was speaking. It looked like it had been speaking for fifty years, patiently, to the dark, waiting for someone to finally come down the stairs and listen.

I went up.

* * *

They were all on deck when I surfaced — Houtman pacing, Meja at the rail, Tobe leaning out so far I thought he'd go in after me, Enoch standing apart with his hat in his hands, bareheaded, which I had never seen.

"Well?" Houtman said. "Is it there?"

I hung on the ladder, mask pushed up, breathing river air that tasted like mud and diesel and life, and I understood that I stood at a hinge. Whatever I said in the next ten seconds would decide what happened in this place. I could tell him about the crypt. About the bones in their ranks, the cross of femurs, the circle of ordinary things. I could tell him it was a grave and watch him order it blasted open anyway, because men like Houtman do not read messages written in bone; they read ledgers.

Or I could lie.

I looked past him at Enoch. Enoch was looking at me with an expression I had never seen on him before — not patience, not grief, but something older than both. Recognition, maybe. As if he'd known, all along, exactly what waited under the altar, and had been waiting to see whether I would come up from it as the same man who went down.

"The crypt's flooded," I said. "Structurally compromised. The vault's cracked — probably in the quake back in '89. There's silt migration through the whole floor. Nothing can be raised without shoring work and a proper survey."

Houtman's eyes narrowed. "The coordinates—"

"Point beneath the altar. Which is sitting on a cracked vault over a flooded crypt full of—" I paused, and chose the truth shaped like a lie. "Full of remains. Mission burials. Dozens of them. If you blast that floor, Minister Okuk's escorts will have photographs of a mining consortium dynamiting a mass grave inside a week, and no permit on earth will save you."

Silence. On the water, the bell tower streamed and dripped, and somewhere inside it — I swear this, though no one else heard it — the bell, sunk in its belfry fifty years, shifted on its rotten headstock and gave a single note. Low. Almost subsonic. Felt in the deck plates more than heard.

Tobe's head came up. "You hear that?"

"Heard what?" said Houtman.

But Meja had heard it too. And Enoch — Enoch had closed his eyes, and his lips were moving, and whether he was praying or answering I could not tell.

That night I wrote the false sonar readings in the log with a steady hand, and told myself I was buying time. Buying time to think. Buying time to choose between the fortune that would erase me and the message in the bones that had waited fifty years for someone to read it.

What I did not tell any of them — what I kept behind my teeth like a stone — was that I had seen, wedged in the wall niche beside the crypt stair, half-hidden behind a fallen beam, a sealed lead tube of the same make as the chest that started all of this.

And that the seal on it bore the crest of a republic that never lived —

— and that it had been opened. Recently. The wax was broken clean, and the silt around the niche was disturbed, and there were fresh scrape marks on the beam.

Someone had beaten us to Vunapope. Someone had been here within days.

And unless I was very much mistaken, the gunmetal launch that had shadowed us through the sawgrass was not finished following us yet.
