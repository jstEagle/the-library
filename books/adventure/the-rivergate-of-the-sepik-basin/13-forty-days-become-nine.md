## 13. Forty Days Become Nine

The river told us before the radio did.

I'd been running boats on the Sepik since I was nineteen, first as deckhand for a mission barge out of Angoram, then for myself, then for nobody at all after the incident at Pagwi that ended my nursing and nearly ended everything else. You learn a river the way you learn a sick patient — by the things it stops doing. And for three mornings running, the Sepik had stopped breathing properly.

The water was coming up brown and thick, carrying whole trees with their roots still clutching island soil, carrying fences, carrying a dead pig so bloated it looked like a brown moon rolling past us at dawn. The current had gone from a walk to a run. The eddies behind the sawgrass islands had filled and vanished. And the smell — that was the worst of it. The river in flood smells like a cellar that has drowned, like everything buried in the world coming up for air at once.

I stood at the rail of the *Karawari Star* with my coffee going cold and watched the gauge stake August had driven into a mudbank two days earlier. It had been showing a hand's width of wood. Now it showed nothing at all.

"Meja." August came up beside me, quiet the way he always was in the mornings, like a man entering a church he wasn't sure he believed in. He was drying his hands on a rag, though they hadn't been wet. He did that when he was thinking. "You see it."

"I see it." I nodded at the drowned stake. "Nine days. Maybe less if the rain in the hills is heavy. After that, the middle channels close. The Ambunti cutoff goes first, then everything east of it turns into one wide brown sea with no banks and no channel marks. We could still get out — barely — but not with a barge loaded heavy, and not slow."

"And Houtman knows?"

"Houtman knows what his permits say. Forty days." I looked at him. "The river doesn't read permits, August."

He almost smiled. Almost. There was a time — I'd known him barely three weeks, but it felt longer, the way time stretches when people are afraid together — when I think he would have smiled for real. Not now. Now he had that look he'd been carrying since Vunapope, since the dive under the bell tower, since he'd come up out of the flooded crypt with his face gray under the brown water and said, in a voice like a man reading someone else's mail: *There's a chamber. It's real.*

He hadn't said what was in it. He hadn't had to. Houtman had been walking around the deck ever since like a rooster who'd found the grain bin open, and the rest of us had been living inside the change in him, the way you live inside a change in the weather.

"I'll talk to him," August said.

"You'll talk to him." I set down my cup. "August. Whatever you're going to say to him — say it straight. That man counts everything. Lies cost more with him than truths."

He looked at me then, and I saw something flicker behind his eyes, something I didn't have a name for yet. I would have a name for it by nightfall. But that morning it was just a shadow, and I let it go, the way you let go of a patient's small symptom because the big ones are taking all your attention.

"Nine days," he said, to the river more than to me.

"Nine days," I said, and went to wake Tobe, because the engine doesn't care about floods or gold or the secrets of men. The engine only cares that it's loved.

* * *

Houtman received the news the way a ledger receives an error — with cold, total displeasure.

He'd set up his field desk under the awning amidships, and it was very like him: a folding table squared to the boat's centerline, papers weighted with stones, his theodolite case locked beside it, his radio handset coiled like a snake he trusted more than people. He was a big man going soft at the edges, with forearms like hawsers and a face that had been sunburned so many times it had stopped being skin and become leather with opinions.

"Nine days." He said it as if I'd made it up personally to inconvenience him. "Your estimate."

"The river's estimate. I'm just the one who reads it." I kept my voice flat. With Houtman, flatness was a currency. "Flood season. The whole middle Sepik goes up two, three meters. The channels we came up through will be under the sawgrass. The marks will be gone. We go down heavy, we go down blind, and this river doesn't forgive blind."

"Then we go down light." He tapped the papers in front of him — the salvage schedule, the hoist specifications, the dive rotations he'd drawn up in his neat surveyor's hand. "We raise it now. Today. The hoist is rigged. August has the chamber mapped. Two days to bring it up, one day to secure the cargo, five days of running water. It closes. It closes exactly."

Nothing closes exactly on the Sepik. I didn't say it. Enoch had taught me — in his slow, patient way, the way he taught everything — that the river is not a machine that breaks, it is a creature that changes its mind. But Enoch was in the bow that morning with his fishing line out, not fishing, watching us the way he watched everything, and I thought: *he* knows the river won't close exactly. And he isn't saying anything either.

"Two days to raise it," I said, "if nothing goes wrong."

"Nothing will go wrong. I have planned for—"

"You have planned for a river that doesn't exist." I heard my own voice sharpen and let it. Some patients need shocking. "Houtman. I have pulled bodies out of this water. Children, old men, a whole wedding party off a barge that overloaded in flood because the owner had a schedule. I know what floodwater does to a drowned chamber. The current down there won't be a current, it'll be a fist. Your divers won't be able to hold position on the line. The silt will come up so thick a torch is a rumor. And if the entrance shifts — and it will shift, that channel has been eating that church for fifty years — your gold stays down there and so does whoever is under it when it goes."

For a moment the only sound was the water slapping the hull and Tobe's voice below decks, singing to the engine the way he did, some garbled pop song half-remembered, off-key with total devotion.

Houtman folded his hands on the schedule. "Then we accept the risk," he said. "The permits lapse. The consortium does not pay for permits that lapse. And you—" his eyes moved past me, finding August where he stood at the winch, pretending to check the brake band, "—you are contractually obligated to make every reasonable effort to complete recovery. I have read you the clause."

"You wrote the clause," August said.

"I had it written. The distinction is immaterial." Houtman stood, and the awning shadow made his face briefly unreadable. "Tomorrow at first light. Full dive rotation. Mr. Dunmore, you will dive the chamber yourself, as agreed. I want the first bars on deck within thirty-six hours."

He gathered his papers with the crisp finality of a man who believes paper beats water, and went forward to check his theodolite, which needed no checking.

August stood at the winch a long time. I went and stood beside him, and for a while we watched Enoch's line drift in the brown water, catching nothing, wanting nothing.

"He's going to kill someone," I said quietly. "Push it like this, in this water, and it's not a question of if. It's who."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I've seen that look before, August. On men with schedules. It's the look that lets them sleep after."

He was quiet so long I thought he hadn't heard. Then: "I'll handle it, Meja."

And there it was again — that shadow behind his eyes. I want to be honest, since honesty is what this whole story came to be about: I believed him. That was my failure that morning. I believed him because I wanted to, because he'd gone into the flood current for Tobe without a second's thought, because he was August, and I had already, without deciding to, made him the sort of man you believe.

* * *

He handled it, all right. He handled it with a grease pencil and a sonar unit and a lie.

I found out the way I found out most things on that boat — sideways, at night, from someone smaller than the trouble. Tobe came to me after midnight, when I had the anchor watch, and he didn't say anything at first. He just stood at the rail beside me, fourteen years old and trying to be twenty, his hands jammed in his pockets, watching the water.

"You can't sleep either?" I said.

"I sleep fine." Which meant he hadn't slept at all. Then, after a while: "Meja. If a man changed the sonar readings. Made the channel look shallower than it is. Made the chamber look like it's collapsing. So Houtman wouldn't make us dive." He said it all in one breath, staring straight ahead. "Is that a lie?"

My stomach went cold and slow, the way it does before bad news. "Why do you ask?"

"Because I helped him do it." His voice cracked on *helped*, and he hated that it cracked; I watched him hate it. "He said it was just buying time. He said Houtman would get someone killed and the readings would buy us days, and when the water dropped we'd tell the truth and it'd be fine. He showed me how the transducer works. He let me hold the grease pencil." Tobe's jaw was working. "He let me hold the pencil, Meja. That's how he got me. You make a kid hold the tool and he's in it, he's yours."

I looked at the boy — at this boy I had already, without deciding to, started to love, the way you love a stray that follows you home — and I felt the thing I'd felt in hospital corridors a hundred times: the specific cold of watching someone you're responsible for walk into something you can't treat.

"What did you change?" I asked.

"Depth readings. Silt density. I drew in a fault line that isn't there — August drew it, but I did the shading, I made it look old, like it was on the original survey." He finally turned and looked at me, and his eyes were wet and furious, mostly at himself. "And then tonight Houtman showed me the printouts, and he was *happy*, Meja. He said the chamber was degrading faster than expected so we had to raise everything immediately, tomorrow, before the structure fails — he used August's lie as a reason to go *faster*."

The river slapped the hull. Somewhere out in the dark a crocodile slid off a bank with a sound like a door closing.

"Where's August now?"

"Asleep. Or pretending. He's been pretending a lot."

I left Tobe on watch and went aft. August's cabin door was open to the heat, and in the light of my torch I saw him lying on his bunk, boots on, charts under his hand, awake — I knew he was awake because sleeping men don't hold charts like they're drowning.

"Tobe told me," I said.

He didn't move. "I was going to tell you."

"When? After the water dropped? After we were safe?" I crouched by the bunk so he had to look at me. "August. I stood on this deck and told you to be straight with Houtman because lies cost more. And you went and built one anyway. And you built it with a *child's hands*."

"I built it so no one dies." He sat up, and in the torchlight his face looked ten years older than it had at Angoram. "You told me yourself what that dive rotation means. The current down there is a fist, you said. Houtman wants thirty-six hours of diving in it. Someone goes under that altar and doesn't come up, and it's not gold he's after, it's just — it's just a body we have to explain to a permit office." He rubbed his face. "The readings bought us time. The water drops in nine days, we run, and the readings never matter to anyone."

"That's not how lies work on a boat." I heard my voice go low, the nurse's voice, the one that comes out when the situation is past arguing. "I spent six years on a ward, August. I've seen a hundred small lies told for kind reasons. The kind reason doesn't matter. What matters is that once the crew finds out the readings are false, they never trust a reading again. Not the depth gauge. Not the engine temperature. Not *you*. You don't get to spend trust to buy time. Trust isn't money. When you spend it, it doesn't come back with interest. It just doesn't come back."

He was quiet. Outside, the rain started — fat, warm drops at first, then the real rain, the hills-rain, the kind that decides how many days we actually have.

"I know," he said finally. "I know. But Meja — if I'd told Houtman the truth, that the chamber is sound and the gold is reachable and we could do it safely if we waited for the water — he'd have us diving tomorrow anyway. The lie was the only lever I had." He looked at me, and there it was, that shadow, and this time I could nearly name it. It was a man discovering that every tool he owns is a lie. "I'm not sorry I did it. I'm sorry Tobe was holding the pencil."

"That's the thing," I said, standing. "You don't get to choose who holds the pencil. That's what secrets do. They hand themselves to whoever's nearest."

I went back up on deck. The rain was coming down hard now, drumming the awning, and the river had gone the color of strong tea, rising, patient, keeping its own count of the days. Tobe was still at the rail. I stood beside him and didn't say anything for a long time, because there wasn't anything true enough to say.

"He lied to Houtman," Tobe said at last. "But it felt like he lied to us. Is that — is that how it is? On boats? On crews?"

I thought about the ward. About the doctors I'd loved who'd lied to families to spare them, and what it did to the families when it came out, and what it did to the doctors. I thought about Pagwi, and the thing I'd done, and the silence I'd kept, and how the silence had cost me more than the deed ever had.

"No," I said. "It's not how it's supposed to be. But it is how it is right now. And Tobe — listen to me. What you do with it matters more than what he did. You could take it to Houtman tomorrow. Trade it. Buy yourself a friend."

His head came up, shocked. "I wouldn't—"

"I know you wouldn't. That's why I'm telling you what you already are." I put my hand on the back of his neck, the way I used to with the young ones on the ward. "But hear the rest. August's lie is going to come out. Lies on a small boat always come out, because a small boat is too small to hide in. And when it does, you're going to be angry, and you're going to be right to be angry. And then you're going to have to decide whether the man who lied is the man who dove into floodwater after you. Both of those are true. You'll have to hold both."

He was quiet a long time. The rain eased to a steady whisper. Down in the black water, the drowned church waited with its cargo and its arranged bones and its bell tower holding up the current, and somewhere below, I supposed, the gold sat in the dark doing nothing, being innocent, the way gold always is while it ruins everyone above it.

"Nine days," Tobe said.

"Maybe eight," I said. "It's raining in the hills."

* * *

Morning brought Houtman onto the deck with the printouts in his fist and triumph in his leather face, and I watched August receive the consequences of his own lie like a man accepting a sentence he'd written himself.

"The chamber is failing," Houtman announced to the whole boat, waving the pages August had falsified. "Structural degradation, accelerating. Which confirms the schedule. We dive today. Not tomorrow. Today. Every hour the structure degrades, we lose recovery tonnage." He thrust the printouts at August. "Your own survey says so, Dunmore."

And August took the pages, and I watched him look at his own lie being used against him, and I watched him understand — I saw it land, actually land, in real time — that a lie is a weapon with no handle. You cannot pick it up without cutting yourself.

"The readings need verification," August said. His voice was steady. Too steady. "I want a second pass with the transducer before anyone goes down. If the chamber's failing, I need to know how fast, and I'm not trusting a single survey for a dive rotation that size."

"Verification costs hours."

"Death costs more." August handed the pages back. "You want the gold out. I want the crew alive. The second pass takes three hours. Take it or find another diver, because I don't dive on bad data."

The boat went very quiet. Tobe stood by the engine hatch with a rag in his hands, not wiping anything. Enoch had stopped pretending to fish. Even the river seemed to lean in.

Houtman looked at August for a long, cold moment — and I saw him decide, the way men like him decide, that the data served him and the delay was tolerable, that August's caution could be filed under greed rather than defiance. "Three hours," he said. "Then we dive on whatever the second pass says. And Dunmore — if your readings were wrong, if this survey was *wrong*, there will be consequences that outlast your contract."

"Three hours," August said.

Houtman went aft to his radio. And August stood there holding nothing, in the middle of his own trap, and I watched Tobe watch him — watched the boy's face do the arithmetic of it, the lie feeding the schedule feeding the danger feeding the lie — and I saw the exact moment the boy decided not to say anything. Not yet. He looked at me, just once, across the deck, and what was in his face wasn't forgiveness. It was a debt being written down in a ledger of his own.

Three hours. The transducer hummed. The rain in the hills kept falling. And downriver, though none of us knew it yet, a helicopter was lifting off from a minister's compound with a man aboard who had never needed a sonar to know exactly where the gold was — because he'd known all along, and so had the man who'd sold us the route.

We had eight days. Maybe seven. And every one of them was already spoken for.
