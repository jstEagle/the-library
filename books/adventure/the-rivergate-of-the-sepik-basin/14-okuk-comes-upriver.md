## 14. Okuk Comes Upriver

The helicopter came out of the south like a dragonfly with a grudge, and the whole river seemed to flinch beneath it.

Ilana heard it first as a tremor in the water — a shiver running through the moored hulls of the expedition boats, the canvas awnings snapping, the birds going up from the flooded kampongs in ragged black sheets. Then the sound arrived, that flat mechanical hammering that had no business this far up the Middle Sepik, where the only engines were outboards and the occasional chainsaw, and even those sounded apologetic against the vast green throat of the country.

She was on the *Karawari Star*'s aft deck with Houtman's ledger open on her knees, her copy of it folded into a plastic sleeve against her chest, when the machine swung into view above the sawgrass. It was government white, or had been once; now it wore the mud-colored patina of every aircraft that worked this coast, and along its flank, in letters gone gray with sun, the crest of the Ministry of Lands and Natural Resources.

Okuk's crest. Okuk's machine.

She closed the ledger slowly, the way you close a door on a room where something is sleeping.

*They told me he'd come by road,* she thought. *They told me three days, maybe four.* The memo she'd intercepted in Wewak had said nothing about helicopters, because memos never did. Memos were written by men who believed the river could be scheduled.

The helicopter banked, found the wide water below the camp where the channel braided around a drowned island, and began its descent. Its rotor wash flattened the flood grass in a perfect circle and drove a standing wave across the anchorage; Meja's voice carried from the wheelhouse, swearing with real artistry as the *Star* yawed against her anchor. August stood at the rail of the lead boat, motionless, watching the machine come down the way a man watches a storm front cross the water toward him — not afraid yet, but already counting what it would take away.

Ilana slid the ledger under the chart table's false bottom, pressed her palm flat over the wood for one breath, and went to meet the minister.

* * *

He stepped out of the rotor wash like a man stepping off a ferry in Port Moresby — unhurried, spotless, absurd. White shirt, creased trousers, polished loafers that sank instantly to the ankles in river mud, and he didn't so much as glance down. Two escorts came after him: young men in mismatched fatigues with new-looking rifles slung muzzle-down, their eyes moving over the boats, the crew, the tree line, with the flat professional hunger of men paid to look and licensed to act.

"Miss Verrec." Okuk extended his hand. His grip was dry and cool, a handshake conducted entirely in an office somewhere else. "You found us. I confess I underestimated you."

"You sent me," Ilana said. "Finding you wasn't difficult."

"I sent you to find heirs." He smiled. It was a good smile, practiced on many audiences, and it reached his eyes exactly as far as it needed to and no farther. "You've been finding other things instead. But then, that's why I chose a lawyer who reads everything twice."

Behind him, Houtman came down the bank from the survey tent, wiping his hands on his trousers, wearing the expression of a man greeting a creditor. That was when Ilana understood — fully, in the stomach, the way you understand a current has taken you — that the surveyor had known this visit was coming. Had perhaps requested it. She thought of the ledger's last pages, the initials side by side in the margin of the disbursement records: **B.H.** and **M.O.**, and beside them a third set she hadn't been able to place, a company stamp she'd assumed belonged to some Port Moresby holding firm.

**K.S.L.** Kessler Salvage Limited.

Not a client. A partner. Three names on one line, sharing one claim, and she had spent eleven days on this river believing she was playing them against each other.

"The camp is honored," Houtman was saying. "We weren't sure the weather—"

"The weather is mine to be unsure about." Okuk turned, taking in the anchorage with the proprietary sweep of a man appraising cattle. The rain had eased overnight and the light lay silver on the flooded forest; somewhere across the channel a village drum was talking, slow and unimpressed. "Remarkable country. My grandfather traded here. Sago and shell money, before the war made everything simpler." He looked back at Ilana. "Walk with me. I didn't come all this way to conduct business on a mudbank."

* * *

They walked the levee above the camp while the escorts hung back and the crew pretended, badly, not to watch. Okuk walked well for a man in loafers, picking the high ground without appearing to look for it, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into a register meant for two people only.

"Do you know what I admire about you, Miss Verrec? You came up this river to represent descendants who may not exist, on a claim you know is legally rotten, and you have done it superbly. Better than I hoped. Better than Kessler expected." He stopped at the levee's edge, where the water stood brown and patient against the grass. "Which tells me you've read the deed closely enough to know it's fraud, and stayed anyway. That's not idealism. That's ambition. I respect ambition. It's the only honest thing left in this profession."

"You're mistaken about what I want."

"I'm rarely mistaken about what people want. It's the cheapest commodity on this river — cheaper than sago, easier to harvest." He watched the water. "You want what your mother wanted, when they moved her off the Karawari block in '68. Standing. A name that can't be revoked. And you want it in a currency this country actually honors, which is not justice. Justice is a mission-school word. I'm offering the real thing."

Ilana kept her face still. Inside, coldly, methodically, she was doing what she always did: filing. *He knows about my mother. He's had me investigated since Wewak, probably since law school. Which means everything I've done upriver is already in a file somewhere, and the file has a conclusion written in it.*

"And August Dunmore?" she said. "What does he want?"

"Ah." Something moved behind Okuk's eyes — not warmth, but the memory of warmth, the way a lamp remembers electricity. "Dunmore wants absolution, though he'd call it something else. A man who dives wrecks for Kessler's scrap money is not a man choosing poverty. He's a man paying a debt that isn't financial. Do you know what he did before the river?"

"He doesn't talk about it."

"No one with anything worth hiding talks about it. That's how you find them." Okuk turned from the water at last, and the smile returned, gentler now, which made it worse. "I can give him what no one else can. A clean record. Citizenship papers — real ones, backdated, countersigned. A bank account in a country whose banks don't ask questions. Enough that he never has to put his head underwater again unless he wants to. All I require is the deed, the letters, and his silence. Yours too, naturally, but yours comes free. You'll get your land claim — the mission lands, properly titled, Karawari names on the register. A legacy, Miss Verrec. The kind lawyers build monuments out of."

"And the gold."

"The gold," Okuk said, "is a national asset recovering to its rightful custodians. That sentence will appear in the gazette. Whether any of it survives the recovery process is a matter of logistics." He shrugged, a small elegant movement. "Governments are not museums."

*And neither are ministers,* Ilana thought. She looked past him, down the levee, to where August stood at the *Star*'s rail with Enoch beside him — the old man small and straight-backed, his carved walking staff planted in the deck like a stake driven against the sky. They had both heard the helicopter. They had both come to see. And August was looking at her now across forty meters of brown water with an expression she couldn't file anywhere: not accusation, not trust. Waiting. As if he already knew what she was being offered and wanted to see whether she'd take it.

She realized, with a lurch, that she didn't know either. That was the most frightening thing the minister had given her all day.

"I'll need to speak with Mr. Dunmore," she said.

"Of course." Okuk checked a heavy watch, an anachronism of gold and leather. "I'll make the same offer to him directly, this evening. I find it saves time when everyone knows the price of everything." He began walking back toward the camp, then paused. "One more thing. The old man — Enoch. His grandfather signed the concession as witness. Did you know that?"

"Yes."

"So does he. And he hasn't told Dunmore what the signature means, or he'd have burned the papers weeks ago." Okuk resumed walking, loafers printing neat crescents in the mud. "Families keep secrets, Miss Verrec. Crews keep secrets. This entire expedition is a conspiracy of people lying to each other for their own reasons. All I've done is arrive before the lies finish killing someone. Consider that a public service."

* * *

The offer was made that evening in the survey tent, and Ilana was not invited, and heard every word anyway, because canvas walls are paper and the night air carries voices across water like a confessional.

She sat outside her own tent with a cold cup of tea she had no intention of drinking, and listened to Okuk lay it out for August the way a jeweler lays out stones — unhurried, generous, each item named and priced. Citizenship. Erasure. Money with a number attached that made Tobe, eavesdropping shamelessly from the engine deck, go very quiet in a way she'd never heard from him before.

August's answers came slowly, spaced far apart, and Ilana learned more from the silences than the words.

"Why me?" August asked at last. "You've got Houtman. You've got Kessler's launch somewhere behind us. You could take the coordinates off the deed yourself — you've obviously got copies. Why buy anything from a salvage diver?"

"Because documents are evidence, Mr. Dunmore, and evidence has a way of surfacing. Because you opened that chest alone, which means whatever you took from it exists nowhere else. And because—" the pause here was surgical, "—because a man who has already lost everything once is the easiest man in the world to make disappear, and the hardest to threaten. I prefer to buy men like you. Threatening them only teaches them they have nothing left to lose."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you say no. I'm a minister, not a pirate." A soft laugh, shared with no one. "Though I will note that my escorts' contract runs another nine days, that Kessler's associates consider this route an investment, and that the flood season does not care who holds which permit. The river will do most of my work for me, Mr. Dunmore. It usually does. I'm merely offering you the chance to be paid for what happens next regardless."

Silence, long enough that Ilana set down the untouched tea.

Then August's voice, and it was different — stripped, level, the voice of a man reading his own autopsy. "You knew about the chest before I pulled it up. The navy seal, the coordinates, all of it. You've known for years. Kessler's been paying me scrap rates on a wreck he already knew held a republic's paperwork, waiting for me to be stupid enough to bring it up." A breath. "I was never a diver to you. I was a dredge."

"You were an instrument," Okuk agreed pleasantly. "A very good one. Instruments that perform well are rewarded. Instruments that don't are replaced. There is no third category, Mr. Dunmore, whatever the novels say. Now — the deed. We can conclude our business tonight, and tomorrow you can dive for pleasure, which I'm told is the only honest kind."

More silence. Ilana found she was gripping the cup hard enough to hurt her hand.

When August finally answered, his voice was almost gentle, and somehow that was the worst thing she'd heard all day. "You just told me exactly what I am to you, in front of witnesses, thinking it would make me sign faster." A pause. "It did make things clear. Just not the way you wanted. No deal, Minister. Take your helicopter home."

Canvas rustled. Boots on planking. And then Okuk's voice, one last time, pitched low and carrying perfectly across the still water:

"A pity. Well. Nine days, Mr. Dunmore. The river keeps better books than either of us."

* * *

He left at first light, the helicopter lifting off through its own spray, and the river closed over the sound of it like water closing over a stone.

But Ilana had seen his face in the last moment before the canopy swallowed him — seen it turn, once, toward the levee where she stood, and the smile was gone. What remained was something older and much more honest: the flat appraisal of a man confirming the location of an asset he intended to recover later, by other means.

She stood a long time after the sound faded, the copied ledger heavy against her ribs inside its plastic sleeve, and made herself say the truth of it silently, in full, the way she'd make herself read a bad clause twice before signing.

*Kessler and Okuk were partners before any of us boarded these boats. Houtman reports to both. The permits, the deadline, the forty days — none of it was ever pressure applied to August. It was theater staged for him, and I helped write the script, and Enoch's people will pay for the production if this goes wrong.*

Below her, on the *Star*'s deck, August was coiling line with the mechanical calm of a man who had taken a blow and filed it somewhere it could be used later. Tobe crouched beside him, saying nothing, handing him coils. Meja stood in the wheelhouse doorway with her arms crossed, watching the empty southern sky.

And Enoch — Enoch had not come out of his tent at all. When Ilana crossed to check on him, she found the old man sitting cross-legged among his few belongings, his grandfather's letter open on his knees, and he looked up at her with eyes that held no surprise whatsoever.

"He showed you the ledger," Enoch said. Not a question.

Ilana went still. "How—"

"The minister's grandfather and mine drank together on this bank in 1974, the week before the barge went down. Whatever those two families started, ours finished." He folded the letter with great care, along its original creases. "Now the sons have come to collect. So we should decide, lawyer, before tonight — what we tell the boy with the dredge. Because he just refused a fortune for us, and he still doesn't know the half of what we're carrying."

Outside, far down the southern channel, so faint it might have been imagination, an engine note that was not one of theirs slipped through the sawgrass and was gone.
