## 15. Night Assault at the Crypt

The river went quiet an hour before they came.

I had learned to read the Sepik's silences the way other men read faces, and this one was wrong. Not the hush of heat, not the held breath before rain — this was the silence of animals leaving. The flying foxes had gone upriver in a ragged black ribbon just after dusk. The frogs along the mission's drowned colonnade had stopped mid-chorus, as if a hand had closed over the whole swamp. Even the water itself seemed to flatten, spreading glassy and black around the bell tower of Vunapope, which stood out of the current like a broken tooth.

"They're coming," Enoch said.

He said it the way he said everything now, softly, from his chair by the rail where Meja had rigged a mosquito net that he refused to use. His shoulder wound from the flood week had been dressed with what little we had, and beneath the bandages I could feel the heat coming off him like a stone left near a fire. He should have been lying down. He had refused.

"How many?" I asked.

"Two boats." He tilted his head, listening to something below the sound of water. "One big engine, lazy. One small, angry."

Kessler's gunmetal launch, then — the lazy engine — and Okuk's escorts in their aluminum runabout, the angry one. Converging on us from opposite banks of the night, the way Houtman's ledger had promised they would. Ilana had copied those orders three days ago and shown them to me by flashlight in the hold: *recover the cache, then ensure no witnesses hold claims.* I had read it twice and felt something in my chest go very still and cold, the way the water did forty feet down.

"Tobe," I called quietly.

He came up from the engine well wiping his hands on a rag, sixteen years old and trying hard to look like a man who'd been expecting this all his life. He wasn't. His eyes were too bright.

"Kill the running lights. Kill everything. If we're lucky, they'll think we anchored blind."

"And if we're not lucky?"

"Then you take Meja's tender and you go east into the sawgrass and you don't stop for anything. Not for me. Not for anyone. You hear me?"

He heard me. I watched him not believe me, which is its own kind of hearing.

Meja surfaced from below decks with the shotgun we'd taken off one of Houtman's survey men back at the middle-river village, and Ilana came last, holding the ledger against her chest like a child she meant to throw into the river rather than surrender. We arranged ourselves along the rails of the *Karawari Star* — four people and a dying old man, guarding a hole in the world full of gold nobody living had earned.

Below us, under nine meters of brown floodwater, under the collapsed roof of the church nave, past the pews standing in rows like a congregation that had waited eighty years for a sermon, the crypt entrance gaped where Kessler's divers had blasted it open that afternoon. We had heard the charge go off while we were still arguing about whether to run. A flat cough through the water, and then a column of bubbles rising through the bell tower's flooded arches, silver in the last light, beautiful and obscene.

They had opened it. And then they had withdrawn — Houtman's launch pulling away downstream at speed, radios silent — because Houtman did not intend to fight us for it. Houtman intended to let Kessler and Okuk kill each other and us, and come back at dawn for whatever floated.

That was the arithmetic I'd been doing since sundown. That was why we hadn't run. Running meant being caught in open water between two boats that could outrun us, and it meant leaving the crypt — the only place on this river with walls thick enough to hide behind — undefended.

So we stayed. Four outcasts and an elder, moored to the bell tower with two lines, above a drowned church full of dead men's gold.

The first boat showed itself at half past eleven.

Not by lights. By smell — diesel exhaust riding the land breeze across the water — and then by the low shape of the launch sliding out of the dark upstream, running without lights herself, her silhouette blotted out of the stars. She came down the channel toward Vunapope with the confidence of a vessel whose pilot knew every snag, and I understood that Kessler had been buying charts of this river longer than I had been diving it.

Behind her, minutes later, the aluminum runabout appeared from the south, and I heard Okuk's escorts talking before I could see them clearly — young men, government-issue rifles, nervous voices carrying flat across the water. They were not soldiers. They were policemen doing a minister's dirty work, and they were afraid, and fear makes men loud.

The two boats saw each other at the same moment we saw them both.

For a long moment nothing happened. Three vessels in a drowned cathedral's channel, all dark, all armed, all pretending not to be there. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered over the Central Range, and lightning lit the clouds from inside like a lamp behind skin.

Then Kessler's launch put a spotlight on the runabout.

It was a brutal white light, and it caught the escorts frozen mid-motion, rifles half-raised, faces blank with shock. A voice came across the water through a megaphone, smooth and unhurried, a voice I knew from a broker's office in Angoram where I had signed away pieces of my life one page at a time.

"Gentlemen. You are trespassing on a private salvage concession. I have the permit here in my hand. Minister Okuk may have told you otherwise. Consider whose signature will still be worth anything in a month."

Silence. Then a younger voice, cracking: "We have written authority—"

"You have a photocopy." The megaphone clicked off. The spotlight swung.

And found us.

I felt the beam hit the *Star* like a physical blow, pinning us to the tower, and in that instant of blindness I knew exactly what Kessler wanted: he wanted the escorts to see us, see the gold's guardians, and fire first. He wanted us to be the reason the police started shooting.

What he got instead was Tobe.

I don't know where the boy found the nerve. Later Meja swore she saw him load the flare gun himself, and I believe it, because the first thing out of our darkness was not a bullet but a red star shell that screamed up over the channel and burst directly above Kessler's launch, drenching her deck in stuttering crimson light — and showing every man on that boat, and every man on the runabout, the shapes of the others' weapons.

The night tore open.

The first shots came from the launch — I'm nearly sure of it, though no one ever proved it afterward — rifle fire hammering across the water, and the escorts answered in panic, muzzle flashes strobing from the runabout, and the air over Vunapope filled with the crack-and-whine of rounds going everywhere and nowhere. I pulled Ilana flat behind the wheelhouse coaming. Meja fired the shotgun once, twice, not aiming at anything, aiming at the idea of us being easy targets. The bell tower rang — actually rang, a single deep note struck by a stray round, rolling out across the floodwater like the church itself objecting to all of this.

"August!" Enoch's voice, calm as Sunday. "The lines. Cut the stern line. Let her swing."

I didn't understand until I did. With the stern line cut, the current took our bow and swung the *Star* broadside behind the tower, putting thirty feet of drowned masonry between us and both boats. I slashed the line and the river did the rest, swinging us around like a partner in a dance, and the next burst of rifle fire chewed the water where our stern had been.

But the fight wasn't about us anymore. It was about the crypt.

I saw it happen in fragments, lit by muzzle flash and the dying red afterglow of Tobe's flare. Kessler's launch ran straight at the bell tower — straight at the coordinates, at the blasted entrance below — and men went over her side in diving gear, four of them, splashing into water already stitched with gunfire. On the runabout, an escort officer screamed at his men to hold fire, hold fire, and one of them, terrified, seeing divers in the water and enemies everywhere, threw a grenade.

I had forgotten, until that moment, that the escorts carried grenades.

It went off in the shallows near the tower's base. The concussion came up through the hull of the *Star* and into my teeth, and the water around the tower lifted in a white dome and fell back, and when it fell, the screaming started — not from the boats, but from the water. One of Kessler's divers had been close. Very close.

"August." Enoch's hand closed on my arm. His grip was still strong. It was the strongest thing about him. "Tobe. The tender. Now."

"No—"

"Now, boy. Before the shooting finds the fuel drum."

He was right, God help him, he was right — the tender was lashed on the seaward side, away from the firing, and it was Tobe's way out, and every second I hesitated was a second the crossfire drifted closer to thirty gallons of petrol. I hauled Tobe over the rail by his collar while he fought me, actually fought me, shouting that he wasn't leaving, that he was crew, that I'd told him once that nobody gets left —

"I lied," I said. "Get in the boat."

And then Enoch moved.

I will see it for the rest of my life, however many nights that turns out to be. The old man rose out of his chair like something being pulled upright by strings, took the shotgun out of Meja's hands without asking, and stepped to the rail in full view of both boats — a thin silhouette, an elder of the Karawari standing above a drowned church his grandfather had helped fill with gold and grief — and he fired the shotgun again and again into the water ahead of Kessler's launch, not at men, at the bow wave, forcing the helmsman to sheer off, forcing every eye on that black water to look at *him.*

"Why?" I screamed at him. "Enoch, why?"

"Because they cannot shoot an old man first!" he called back, and there was almost laughter in it. "It shames them! It buys seconds! Go!"

The launch's return fire came anyway — shame is a fragile thing at midnight — but it was high, wild, thrown. Bullets slapped the tower. One punched through the *Star*'s cabin wall an inch above Ilana's head, and she didn't even flinch; she was shouting into the expedition radio, broadcasting the ledger to every station on the band, her voice steady as a metronome, and later I would understand that what she began that night finished this whole rotten business, but that night I only heard the words *fraud* and *witnesses* and *Minister Okuk* going out over the floodwater to a hundred listening sets.

Tobe was in the tender. The engine caught on the second pull. I shoved him off with a boot against the gunwale and he grabbed my wrist and for one suspended second we looked at each other across the rail — the boy I'd fished out of the middle Sepik, the boy who'd nearly drowned and whom I'd gone back in for, the boy who was family in every way that counted and none that were legal.

"East," I said. "Don't stop."

He went. The tender's wake unspooled into the sawgrass and vanished.

And that was when the bullet found Enoch.

It didn't knock him down. That was the terrible part. He simply stopped firing, and leaned against the rail, and sat down slowly, the way an old man sits down when he's tired, and Meja reached him before I did and I saw her hands come away from his side black in the flare light. The shotgun clattered to the deck. His eyes found mine and they were clear — clearer than they'd been in days, as if the fever had burned off all at once and left only the man.

"The letter," he said. "Inside pocket. You take it now, August. While I can watch you take it."

My hands were shaking so badly I nearly couldn't find the oilskin packet. I took it. He watched me take it, and nodded once, satisfied, like a man checking off the last item on a long list.

"The gold was never cursed," he said. "Only the men. Remember which."

The fighting was moving away from us now — toward the crypt, toward the tower's base, where Kessler's surviving divers had reached the blasted entrance and were rigging lights below, and where the escorts, having heard Ilana's broadcast, were arguing loudly among themselves about mutiny. Through the flooded arches of the bell tower, down through nine meters of silt-browned water, I could see it beginning to glow: a work light descending into the crypt, and then another, and then — reflected up through the water, wavering, golden, impossible — the shine of stacked metal.

Bars of it. Rows of it. Rusted at the edges, fused in places into solid masses by eighty years of river, but real, real as sin, real as debt, enough gold down there to buy every life on this river twice over and pay for the funerals besides.

Enoch saw me looking. He smiled, and it cost him everything, and he paid it anyway.

"Don't touch it," he whispered. "Whatever happens next. Don't you touch it."

I knelt in his blood on the deck of the *Karawari Star*, above a drowned church, in a war over a nation that died before it was born, and I gave the old man my word.

Below us, the lights in the crypt went out — all at once, together — and the water over Vunapope went black as a closed eye, and from somewhere inside the church came the sound of more charges being set.

* * *

The second blast lifted the *Star* clean off her mooring lines.

I felt it through the deck, through my knees, through the bones of my hands where they pressed Enoch's wound — a deep, subterranean boom, the river itself flinching — and then the bell tower groaned, a sound like a giant clearing its throat after eighty years of silence, and the whole ruined structure listed two degrees to the east and settled with a rush of displaced water that slopped over our rails.

Meja looked up from Enoch's side, her face grey. "They're bringing the roof down. Sealing themselves in with it."

Or sealing everyone else out. Or sealing the witnesses in. With Houtman's ledger in my memory and Okuk's name in Ilana's broadcast, I no longer believed any of these men planned to surface with what they found. Gold was heavy. Dead men float better without pockets.

The runabout's engine turned over — the escorts leaving, actually leaving, Ilana's words having done what bullets couldn't — and Kessler's launch hung in the channel with her dive lights off and her deck crew shouting numbers to each other, hoist rigging clanking, and I understood with a clarity that felt like cold water in my lungs that the window was closing. Whatever happened in the next hour, it would happen down there, among the pews, in the dark, and it would decide who walked away from Vunapope and who became part of the church.

I tied off the tourniquet Meja had improvised from the mooring line's chafe gear. Enoch's breathing was shallow but steady, and his hand lay over mine on the oilskin packet in my pocket, and his lips moved without sound — *don't touch it, don't touch it* — and I pressed his hand once and stood up.

"Watch him," I said to Meja. "Both of you. Whatever comes up that ladder, it isn't me unless I say my mother's maiden name."

"Which is?" Ilana asked.

"Nothing," I said. "She never told me. That's how you'll know."

I went over the side with a tank I hadn't checked and a torch I didn't trust, into water the color of strong tea, down past the bell — huge, green-slimed, hanging silent in its flooded frame — down past the arches where the fish hung motionless, disturbed, listening, down into the nave where the pews waited in their rows and the work lights of dead men still glowed faintly on the floor of the crypt, and the gold lay stacked in the dark like a congregation of its own, patient, rusted, real, waiting to see what kind of man the river had finally sent it.

Behind me, faintly, through the water, I heard the launch's winch begin to turn.

And above me, at the mouth of the crypt, a diver's silhouette slid into view — headlamp blazing straight down at me, rifle slung across his chest, aimed at the only ladder out.
