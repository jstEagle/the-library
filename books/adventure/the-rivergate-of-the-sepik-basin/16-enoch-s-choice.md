## 16. Enoch's Choice

The bullet had gone in below my collarbone and come out beneath my arm, and I knew before the bleeding told me that it had found the lung. I knew because my grandfather had taken a spear there in 1943, fighting the Japanese at Ambunti, and he had described to me once, when I was a boy, the way the breath went strange afterward — how each inhale came with a wet sound, like water being poured from one shell cup into another. His wound had healed. Mine would not. A man learns to recognize his own death the way he recognizes his own name called across the water.

They carried me up from the crypt on a stretcher of pews torn loose from the drowned church, Tobe and Meja hauling while August held pressure against the hole in my side with both hands, and Ilana ran ahead to clear the debris of the night's fighting — Houtman's men scattered into the sawgrass, Okuk's escorts standing confused in the torchlight like men who had forgotten whose war they were fighting. The gold still sat below us in its flooded crypt, stacked and rusted and real, lit by the last of Meja's flares sinking slowly through the black water, and I remember thinking that it looked exactly as my grandfather had described it: patient. Gold is always patient. It is only the men who hurry.

They laid me in the wheelhouse of our boat, on the bench beneath the window where I could see the river. This mattered to me. I did not say so, but Ilana understood — she was the one who arranged the blankets, who wedged the stretcher so that my head faced the open water rather than the cabin wall. She was a woman who thought in documents and claims and boundaries, but she understood this one thing without being told: a Karawari man should not die looking at wood.

* * *

Dawn came gray over Vunapope channel. The bell tower of the drowned mission stood out of the current like the rib of some animal the river had eaten long ago and not finished digesting. Birds were working the shallows. Somewhere downstream, Kessler's launch sat grounded or sunk — we could hear nothing of it now, only the ordinary sounds of the river resuming itself after a night of men shooting at each other over metal that belonged to none of them.

August came to me first, before anyone else. He had not slept. There was blood on him that was not all mine, and his hands shook as he knelt beside the stretcher, and I watched him try to find words and fail, and I took pity on him because dying gives you a kind of authority that living men must respect.

"Sit," I said. "You are making the floor nervous."

He almost laughed. It came out broken. He sat.

"I am going to tell you things," I said, "and then I am going to give you something, and then you are going to do what I ask, and you will do it because by the time I finish speaking you will understand that there has never been any other choice worth making. Not for you. Not since the chest came up out of the mud with your name written on it in a language you didn't know you could read."

"Enoch—"

"No." I stopped him with two fingers, weak as they were. "Let an old man have the whole of his breath. I have been saving it."

* * *

I told him about 1974.

Not the story I told around the fire — that story was for the crew, shaped and smoothed like a canoe paddle, true but not whole. This telling was for a dying man to give and another man to carry. I told him about the barge, the German missionary's old steel barge that the soldiers brought upriver in the wet season of '74, flying no flag because the flag it should have flown belonged to a country that existed only on paper — the Republic of New Guinea, proclaimed in a hall in Wewak by eleven men and a typewriter, recognized by nobody, dead within a year, and buried so thoroughly that even the mud forgot it until August pulled its seal up out of the dark.

I told him about my grandfather, Lukas Enoch, who signed the concession deed because the missionaries told him signing was the same as understanding, and who spent the rest of his life learning the difference. I told him about the night the soldiers came to Karawari wanting the barge moved downriver to the coast, and about the council of elders that met in the men's house and argued until the fire burned to white ash, and about the decision that came out of that argument like a stone out of a sling: the gold would go into the ground under the mission church, where the God of the Germans could sit on top of it, and the papers saying who owned it would be scattered among five families, and no single man would ever hold both halves again.

"They sank it," I said, "not to keep it. That is what your consortium man will never understand, and what your minister will never understand, and maybe what you have not yet understood either. They sank it to end it. To make it a story instead of a fortune. Because they had already seen what it did — to the soldiers, to the traders, to the young men who went down to Wewak and came back talking about banks. My grandfather said the gold was like a crocodile egg someone had carried into the village. It isn't evil. It's just that if you let it hatch, everything small around it gets eaten."

August was quiet a long time. The river made its sounds against the hull. Down in the crypt, forty meters of black water away, the flares had gone out and the gold sat in the dark doing what gold does.

"And now it's hatched," he said finally.

"Now it has hatched," I agreed. "And look what has come to eat. A minister. A broker. A surveyor with a ledger full of other men's names. Men died last night on the bottom of a church for it, and more will die today, and more tomorrow, until somebody does what my grandfather's generation did not quite dare to do."

"Which is?"

"End it. Not hide it. Not move it. Not share it out and hope the sharing holds. End it. Burn the paper. Break the seal. Let the claim die the death the republic never got to have — quickly, completely, with witnesses." I turned my head, and the effort of it cost me something, and I paid it, because the next part needed my eyes on his face. "You have the deed. You have known since Angoram what the chest truly held, and you have carried that secret like a stone in your belly through every mile of this river, and I have watched you carry it, and I am not angry. A man hides what he is afraid of losing. But the hiding is finished now, August Dunmore. The river has flushed it all into the light — Houtman's ledger, Okuk's partnership with Kessler, the fraud in the concession itself. There is nothing left to protect by silence. Only something left to lose."

"The gold," he said. "It's real. It's right there. Forty days of permits, a hoist already rigged—"

"I know what is down there. My grandfather helped put it there. Do you know what he wrote in his last letter? He was an old man by then, blind in one eye, and he wrote it out slow with a pencil a missionary gave him, and he gave it to me folded inside a bamboo tube and told me: *when the gold comes back up, give this to whoever pulls it.*" I closed my hand around the tube that had hung against my chest since the fire at the middle Sepik, since before the flood, since before any of them knew what I carried. "I thought it would be a consortium man, or a soldier, or a thief. I did not think it would be a diver who goes back into black water for a boy who is not his kin. But the letter does not say who. It says *whoever*. And the river chose."

I pressed the bamboo tube into his hands. His fingers closed around it and I felt him trembling, and I made my voice as steady as the remaining air in me allowed.

"Read it later. Not here. When you are alone with the water, read it. But I will tell you what it says, because I have had fifty years to learn it by heart. It says: *the gold was cursed only because men let it be cursed. Curses are debts, and debts can be refused. Refuse this one. Sink the claim, not the metal. Let the metal sleep. No more Karawari blood for a republic that never lived.*"

* * *

Meja came in quietly while we talked, and stood by the door with her arms crossed, pretending to check the fuel gauge, which is a thing nurses do when they are giving a room its privacy and cannot bear to leave it. Tobe hovered behind her, and the boy's face was wrecked — he kept looking at my shoulder, at the bloody padding, at the place where his own escape had been bought, and I beckoned him over and made him take my hand.

"You dove for me," he said. "In the flood. And last night you—" He couldn't finish it.

"I did what old men are for," I said. "We are heavy. We sink slowly. We make good anchors." His mouth twitched, almost a smile, tears running freely down his face anyway, and I gripped his fingers as hard as my body would let me. "Listen to me, boy. You are quick and you are brave and you steal engines like a spirit steals fire. Stay with these people. They are not your blood, but blood is only one way of being family, and often the weaker one. Family is who stands between you and the crocodile. Remember who stood."

Ilana came to the doorway then, and behind her the others, and I understood that the wheelhouse had become the men's house, and that whatever was decided here would be decided the old way — with everyone present, and the dying man speaking last.

"You heard," I said to them all. "Good. Then I don't spend breath twice. The claim ends. The deed burns. The gold stays where my grandfathers put it, under the altar, in the dark, with the bones that were arranged to warn you — and they were a warning, August, not a grave; my people do not bury strangers under their altars. And when the permit dies and the consortium scatters and the minister's helicopter takes him back to his committees, you will dive down one last time, not for the bars but for the bell. The bell comes home to Karawari. It hung over that church when my mother was baptized in it. It belongs to the living, not the ledger."

Nobody spoke. The river spoke for them, slapping the hull, patient as gold.

"It will cost us everything," August said at last. "Houtman's men are still out there. Okuk won't walk away from a fortune because a dying man asks nicely. If we burn the deed, every one of them has lost, and men who have lost everything get dangerous."

"Yes," I said. "That is why it must be done fast, and loud, and in front of witnesses. Secrecy is what killed the men of '74. They hid the truth in five families and the families suffered fifty years for it. Do not hide. Tell the whole river. Tell the radio band. Tell the police at Angoram before they can be bought. Truth moves faster than gunboats, if you start it early enough." I looked at Ilana. "You are the one with the law in your throat. You will know how to shout it so it sticks."

She nodded slowly, and I saw the cold ambition in her face do something it had perhaps never done before — bend toward something other than herself.

"And you," I said to August, and my voice was going now, thinning out like the last thread off a spindle. "You wanted a life the gold would buy. Debts erased. A dry bed. I know. I have watched you count it in your sleep. But hear an old man who has counted everything twice: the river forgives nothing, but it spares those who respect it, and respect means knowing what to leave down there. Leave it. Come home poor. Poor is survivable. Owned is not."

His jaw worked. His eyes were wet. He held the bamboo tube against his chest the way I had held it for fifty years, and I saw the moment the weight transferred — not the paper, not the gold, but the burden, the watching, the keeping — pass from my line into his hands. Found family is made like this. Not with weddings. With funerals. With a dying man handing a stranger the thing he was born carrying, and the stranger taking it.

"Swear it," I whispered. "Not to me. To the river. Out loud. All of you. So the water hears, and the water keeps accounts better than any ledger Houtman ever kept."

And they did. August first, hoarse, then Ilana, then Meja with her hand on Tobe's shoulder, then the boy himself, voice cracking, swearing to the brown sliding water outside the wheelhouse window that the claim would die and the bell would rise and no more blood would pay for a nation that never breathed.

I listened to them with my eyes on the channel, on the gray morning light coming up the river, on the bell tower standing its lonely watch over the drowned church, and I thought: *grandfather, it is finished, the egg goes back in the nest, the crocodile sleeps.*

The wet sound in my breathing grew louder, each breath a shell cup pouring into another, slower, slower.

The last thing I saw was August folding the bamboo tube into his shirt, over his heart, and turning toward the radio.

* * *

Darkness, but not the bad kind. The warm kind, the river kind, the kind my grandmother promised. Sounds came to me from far away through it — Ilana's voice on the radio band, clear and furious and magnificent, reading Houtman's ledger aloud to every station from Ambunti to the coast; Meja's engine turning over; Tobe laughing, actually laughing, at something; and underneath it all the deep patient voice of the Sepik, carrying me the way it carries everything, down through the delta gates, out toward the sea.

I was not afraid. I had done my work. The letter was delivered. The burden had found shoulders broad enough.

The river took me the way it takes everything it loves — without ceremony, without cruelty, holding me a while longer than it needed to, and then letting go.
