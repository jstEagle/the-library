## 17. The Weight of Gold

The crypt held its breath with me.

I stood on the altar step of a church that had been dead for fifty years, water to my thighs, black and warm as bathwater gone wrong, and in each hand I held a gold bar the size of a house brick. The hoist Houtman's men had rigged hung above me — a chain hoist on a salvaged A-frame, its cable running up through the collapsed roof beams to the skiff waiting in the channel where the bell tower broke the current. Ready. Everything was ready. All I had to do was clip the bars to the sling and send them up, one by one, and become a man who owned things.

The bars were heavier than they had any right to be. Not physically — maybe twelve kilos each, nothing my shoulders hadn't carried worse — but there was a weight in them that had nothing to do with gravity. I'd felt it the moment my gloves closed on the first one, down in the dark at the bottom of the crypt where the bones had been arranged in a ring like a warning written in a language only the dead could read. The metal was cold even through the neoprene, and it stayed cold, and I understood that it would always stay cold, no matter how long you held it, no matter how much of it you held.

I had done the arithmetic before. I did it again now, because that was what I did — I was a man who survived on numbers, on the price of scrap brass and the interest Kessler charged on diesel and the exact depth at which a man's lungs decide they'd rather be filled with river than air. So I stood in the flooded house of God and I counted.

One bar, in Angoram, through a broker who asked nothing — enough to clear Kessler. Not the debt itself; the debt's *shadow*, the way it leaned on everything I touched. Two bars and the dredge was mine outright, and the *Marianne* too, and I could work my own wrecks and answer to no one's silence. Five bars and I was a man with a bank account in a country that had banks. Twenty bars and I was a man with a past that could be paved over, a name that could be sanded and repainted, a life with a door that locked from the inside.

The crypt held, by my rough count from what the floodlamp showed before its battery died, somewhere north of three hundred.

Three hundred bars. I turned the numbers over and they turned into pictures. A house on stilts above clean water, not the brown sludge of the lower Sepik but something clearer, somewhere the crocodiles were shy. A boat built new instead of resurrected. Tobe in a school that didn't smell of diesel and shame. Meja with her nursing license restored, if she wanted it — she'd never said she wanted it, but I'd watched her splint Tobe's arm in the dark of the middle river with hands so steady and so certain that I'd understood I was looking at a woman who had been amputated from herself and was walking around anyway.

That was the arithmetic. That was what the gold bought. And the price was written on the walls around me in bone.

* * *

I set the bars on the altar step — they made a sound like knuckles cracking, a small obscene click in all that drowned silence — and I made myself look at the ring of skeletons again.

The floodlamp was dying. Its beam had gone from white to the color of weak tea, and in that failing light the bones seemed to shift, the way things do at the edge of visibility. Fifty years in the water had made them the color of the river itself, silt-brown, and the men who had arranged them had been careful. Not piled. *Arranged.* Facing inward. Hands, where the small bones still held together, folded or extended toward the center of the circle, toward the place where the gold had been stacked beneath a false floor until Houtman's charges tore it open three nights ago.

Enoch's letter was in the waterproof pouch against my chest, and I didn't need to take it out to read it. I had it by heart now, every line, the way you memorize a thing that's been driven into you. *The gold was never cursed. My grandfather wrote that the night they sank it. It is only metal. It became a curse because every man who touched it decided the men around him were worth less than what he held.* And then the last line, the one Enoch had made me swear on with his hand clamped around my wrist hard enough to leave bruises, his blood thinning in the water of the crypt while Tobe screamed and the shooting came through the pews: *End it, boy. No more Karawari under the river.*

Enoch was on the skiff above me, or what was left of him, wrapped in the sailcloth Meja had been keeping for the mainsail, breathing in that wet rattling way that meant hours, not days. He had made me swear, and I had sworn, and now I stood in the crypt with three hundred bars of the only currency that could buy everything I had ever been too poor to have, and I was doing the arithmetic anyway.

I want to be honest, since honesty is the only thing I have left to give anyone: I did not want to burn it. Not at first. Not in the clean way I told it later, at Angoram, under the fan turning in the district office while the recorder hissed. In the crypt, in the dark, with the water at my thighs and my whole ruined life laid out in gold like a hand of winning cards, I stood there and I *wanted*. I wanted it the way a drowning man wants air — not greed, not even desire, just the animal certainty that the thing in my hands was the thing that would keep me alive. Every year of scrap diving, every dive where the silt closed over my head and I thought *this is how it ends, in the dark, for a few kina of brass*, every time Kessler had smiled his small smile and named a price and I had taken it because the alternative was nothing — all of it was in my hands, and all I had to do was clip the sling.

I even got as far as the clip. My fingers were on the shackle. The first bar was in the sling, and the cable hung slack above me, and all it would take was one pull on the hoist, and then another, and I could stop being August Dunmore, salvage man, debtor, orphan of nothing, and become a man with a future.

That's when I heard the engines.

* * *

Two of them. Coming down the channel from the north, against the current, which meant they weren't drifting — they were driving, and driving hard. I knew the sound the way you know a voice in a dark house. Houtman's workboats, the twin Mercurys with the slightly out-of-sync idle he never bothered to have tuned because men he planned to bury didn't need smooth engines.

But it was wrong. That was the thing that turned my blood to river water. It was wrong because Houtman's boats had left at dawn — Houtman, two of his men, and the last of Okuk's escorts who hadn't mutinied yet — running downriver to the fuel cache at the junction, a six-hour round trip. I had hours. I had planned on hours. I had stood in this crypt with three hundred bars and done my arithmetic precisely *because* I had hours, because a man can tell himself any lie if he gives it enough time to set.

The engines were too close. They were coming around the point below the drowned bell tower, and I could hear the pitch change as they throttled down, and under the engines, faint, carried flat across the water the way sound travels over floodwater at night, a voice. Houtman's voice. Giving an order in that clipped Dutch-English of his, and the order had a number in it, and the number was *two*.

Two charges. I'd helped set the first ones — shaped charges against the crypt wall, meant for salvage, meant to widen the breach so the hoist could work. Houtman had kept the rest of the explosives in the workboat's hold, and I had told myself that was inventory, that was caution, that was the habit of a careful man. I heard the number *two* come across the water and I understood, with the clean terrible clarity that only ever arrives too late to be useful, that the charges were never inventory. They were always the plan. Recover the gold. Silence the witnesses. Sink the crypt with everything in it — the bones, the evidence, the men — and let the river close over the whole rotten story one more time, the way it had closed over it in 1974. The Sepik was good at keeping secrets. Houtman had read the same deed I had. He knew the river's habits.

And above me, tied to the skiff, were Enoch, dying. Meja, with a rifle she barely knew how to use. Tobe, sixteen years old, who had followed me into this because I'd looked at him once the way nobody had ever looked at him, like he was worth something.

The price of survival, made literal. That's what the whole river had been teaching me since the day the chest came up on my dredge, and I had been a slow student, because I kept trying to pay in anything but the truth.

I let the sling go. The bar inside it dropped back onto the altar step with that knuckle-crack sound, and the sound seemed very loud, and I stood still in the failing light and listened to the engines come on, and I made the last calculation of the man I had been.

Three hundred bars. One hoist. Two charges. Four people in a skiff, one of them dying, one of them a child. And me, alone, in the one place in the world where I could choose what the river would keep.

* * *

The charges were where Houtman's man Vos had stacked them, in the flooded vestry off the north transept, wrapped in plastic against the water — Houtman was a careful man, a meticulous man, and I had counted on his meticulousness all expedition, because a meticulous man labels his explosives and stores them dry and tells his crew exactly how many he has. Four charges. Two had opened the crypt. Two waited in their plastic like sleeping things, and beside them the detonators in their cardboard tubes, and the spool of detonation cord, and the firing wire coiled neat as fishing line.

I had helped set the first pair. I knew the geometry of this place the way I knew the geometry of every wreck I'd ever dived — where the walls were thickest, where the roof beams had rotted through, where the water would go when you gave it a reason. The crypt was a stone box under a stone floor under a church that was already half-collapse, and the channel had been eating it for fifty years, and the bell tower stood because the tower's foundations were the only part of the building the river hadn't decided to take.

I'm not an explosives man. I want that on the record too. I'm a salvage diver; I blow things *open*, I don't blow them *down*. But I had spent three nights watching Vos place the first charges, and Vos liked to talk, and I had listened, because listening is the other thing I do instead of living. The crypt walls would take a shaped charge and breach. The *tower*, though — the tower was old mission masonry, coral limestone and river stone, and it had been leaning for fifty years over the channel, held up by habit and by the fact that nobody had given it a reason to fall.

The charges weren't for the crypt. I could see it now, the whole design of it, the way you see a wreck's shape all at once when the silt clears. Houtman hadn't brought two charges to finish the gold. He'd brought them to drop the bell tower across the channel mouth — to seal the crypt's only entrance under fifty tons of masonry, with the witnesses inside or beside it, and the river would do the rest. The Sepik was good at keeping secrets. He'd only ever needed to ask it nicely.

So I did the arithmetic one more time, the real arithmetic, the one that mattered. And then I picked up both charges, and the detonators, and as much of the cord as I could carry, and I waded out of the crypt through the breach Houtman's first charges had torn, into the flooded nave where the pews stood in their drowned rows like a congregation that had stayed too long, and I went to work.

* * *

I won't pretend it was clean. My hands shook. The water made everything slow and clumsy, and the floodlamp died for good while I was running the cord, and I finished the last of it by feel, in the black, with the engines idling now just beyond the tower, close enough that I could hear Houtman's voice giving Vos the geometry — *the base, man, at the base, the tower comes down across the mouth* — and every word was a confirmation, and every confirmation was a nail in the coffin he'd built for us.

I set the charges at the tower's foundations, on the channel side, where the masonry was already weeping and cracked. Both of them. I wired them back to the crypt, because the crypt was stone and stone was cover, and I ran the firing wire up through the breach to the altar step, and there I stopped, one last time, with the spool in my hand and the gold at my feet and the whole drowned church holding its breath around me.

The bars sat on the altar step in the last gray seep of light from the breach above. Three hundred of them, or near enough. Everything I had ever been too poor to have. A house on clean water. Tobe in school. Meja made whole. My name sanded down and repainted, my debts dissolved, my past paved over. All of it one pull of a hoist away, and the hoist was rigged, and the skiff was above me, and the engines were idling beyond the tower, and the men who owned those engines had already written the number *two* into their evening.

Enoch's voice, in my head, in that dry rusted-out whisper the bullet had left him: *It is only metal, boy. It became a curse because every man who touched it decided the men around him were worth less than what he held.*

I thought about the bones in their ring. Fifty years they'd waited, facing inward, hands open toward the empty center, and I finally understood what they were saying. Not *cursed*. Not *keep out*. They were saying: *we were worth less than this, to the men who put us here. Don't let it be said of you.*

I clipped the last of the detonators to the cord. I took the firing spool in my hand. And I left the gold where it lay.

The wire ran back into the black of the crypt, and I followed it, and I knelt on the altar step among the bones of men who had chosen this same thing in 1974 and paid for the choosing with their lives, and I put my thumb on the firing plunger, and above me, through the breach, I heard the workboats' engines come up out of idle — Houtman moving, committing, coming to drop the sky on us — and I heard, thin and high across the water, Meja's voice shouting Tobe's name.

I pressed.

* * *

The world became a fist.

I've been near explosions before — a dredge pipe once, a fuel drum on the *Marianne* — and nothing, nothing prepares you for the way a detonation underwater reaches through the water into your chest and squeezes. The sound arrived through my bones before it arrived through my ears. The crypt walls flexed. Fifty years of river silt came off the ceiling in a black snowfall, and the pressure wave went through the flooded nave like the hand of God clearing His throat, and somewhere above and behind me the bell tower — leaning for fifty years, waiting for fifty years, held up by nothing but habit — finally, gratefully, fell.

The noise was enormous and then it was gone, and in its place came the deeper sound, the one the river makes when it takes something: a long grinding roar of masonry going into the channel, water displacing, the whole drowned church shuddering on its foundations. The tower came down across the channel mouth exactly as Houtman had planned — except that Houtman had planned to be on the far side of it when it fell, and instead his workboats were inside the mouth when fifty tons of coral limestone came down, and I heard, over the grinding, the sound I will hear for the rest of my life: engines screaming up to full throttle in a channel that was no longer there, and then the crack of hull on stone, and then Houtman's voice, no longer giving orders, just *shouting*, one word, over and over, a word I couldn't make out and didn't need to.

I came up through the breach into the night, into the rain of disturbed bats and the smoke-smell of cordite on floodwater, and I hauled myself onto the altar platform with my lungs full of broken stone and my ears ringing like the bell that had come down with its tower, and I looked back once through the breach, down into the crypt.

The water was settling. The silt was falling. The bars were where I'd left them, on the altar step, in their ring of bones, and already the river was reaching for them, drawing its brown veil across the gold the way it had drawn it across everything else — the mission, the republic, the men of 1974, every promise anyone had ever made on this water. By morning the silt would be a meter deep. By next flood season, no one would find it without the deed, and the deed was going into the fire with everything else. The gold would keep. The gold was very good at keeping. That was the whole horror of it.

The skiff was thirty meters out through the breach, and Meja had the engine started, and Tobe was pulling Enoch upright, and all three of them were staring at the falling tower, at the wall of water it had thrown, at me, standing on the altar of a drowned church with a detonator spool still in my fist.

I raised the spool so they could see it. I don't know what my face was doing. I couldn't hear anything but the ringing and the river.

Then, out of the smoke and the settling spray at the channel mouth, from the wreck of the nearer workboat, a flashlight beam swung and found me — and held — and a voice came across the water, hoarse with stone-dust and fury, and it was not Houtman's.

It was Vos. And he was alive, and he was in the water, and he was swimming toward the skiff where Enoch lay dying, and he had a pistol in his raised hand.
