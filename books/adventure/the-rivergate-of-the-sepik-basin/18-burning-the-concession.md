## 18. Burning the Concession

The radio room of the *Karawari Star* had been a broom closet once, before Tobe stripped it and rebuilt it into something between a shrine and a cockpit. Wires ran along the ceiling like vines in a greenhouse. The set itself was a hybrid of three dead radios and one living one, its dial glowing a soft amber that reminded Ilana of the lamp her grandmother kept burning in the front room in Angoram, the one you left lit so the river could find you if it ever needed to come home.

She sat with Houtman's ledger open on her knees.

She had copied it two nights ago in the blue hour before dawn, when the camp at Vunapope slept badly and even the sentries Okuk had posted were nodding against their rifles. She had copied every line in her own hand — the neat, lawyer's script she'd learned drafting land claims for people who couldn't read them — because a copy made by machine could be confiscated, but a copy living inside her skull and on paper hidden in the false bottom of her document case was something else entirely. Something that could not be blasted out of a crypt or bought off with citizenship papers for a man who had never legally existed.

Page after page. *Recovery costs, projected.* *Concession value, 1974 valuation, adjusted.* And then the columns that had made her hands go cold despite the river heat: *Personnel disposition post-recovery.* Her own name was there. Not spelled out — initials, *I.V., oversight, retain until documentation phase complete, then release to local authorities (cooperative).* August's name was there too, under a heading so blunt it might have been written by a butcher: *Guide: expendable once route knowledge no longer required.*

And beneath all of it, the entry that mattered more than gold, more than permits, more than forty days ticking down to nine: *Legal basis of concession: defective. Original deed executed under authority of Republic of New Guinea (unrecognized). No successor state ratified transfer. Title void ab initio. Recommend suppression of this finding until physical recovery accomplished; thereafter claim may be re-founded under consortium charter with ministerial endorsement.*

Void from the beginning. The whole edifice — the permits, the expedition, the men with rifles sleeping around her, Enoch's blood still dark on the crypt stones — all of it stood on paper that had never been legal for a single day. A republic that died before anyone recognized it had signed a document, and fifty years later men were willing to kill for the ghost of an ink stamp.

Ilana had spent eleven years learning to think like the law thought. The law, she had discovered, was not a temple. It was a river. It looked solid from the bank, but underneath it moved everything, carried everything, drowned whatever could not swim. Men like Okuk believed they owned the river because they held concessions to dredge it. Men like Kessler believed they owned it because they held the debts of everyone who worked it. But the law itself — the real current beneath both of them — answered to only one thing: what could be proven, publicly, in daylight, to everyone listening at once.

She looked at the radio.

Outside, the night was doing what flood-season nights did: raining without conviction, a thin grey drizzle that softened the edges of the world. The channel had risen another half-meter since dusk. She could hear it against the hull, patient as arithmetic. Somewhere across the water, in the drowned mission's bell tower, Okuk's escorts had string lights hung and cards going, their laughter carrying flat across the black surface. They were waiting for morning and the salvage hoist. They believed they were guarding a fortune.

They were guarding a fraud, and when it was done, most of them would be guarding nothing, because dead men guard poorly.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected fear — had rehearsed fear, actually, lying awake imagining the moment — but what came instead was a strange, clean lightness, like the feeling of setting down a case file she'd been carrying too long. For eleven years she had played the game the way it was dealt to her: leverage, access, information traded quietly among people who never said what they meant. She had told herself it was strategy. Sitting here with the ledger on her knees, she admitted what it had really been: she had wanted to be the kind of person the river's powerful men needed, instead of the kind of person the river's powerless people needed.

Enoch had died for a letter. For paper. For the truth that his grandfather and six other men had sunk a barge-load of gold rather than let a nation that never lived buy the deaths of everyone downstream. He had died covering a sixteen-year-old engine thief, shot by men whose claim to anything was a signature from a government that had lasted nine months and was recognized by no one.

You want to know what I'm worth? she thought, addressing the ledger, addressing Okuk, addressing the whole grey machinery of men who valued things. Here is my price. Everything.

She keyed the transmitter.

* * *

The set woke with a hum she felt in her back teeth. Tobe had labeled the frequency bands in grease pencil — *coastal, police, mission net, anyone* — and the last one, the open band, the one every station from Angoram to the coast monitored because gossip was the Sepik's second-most-valuable export, glowed under her fingertip.

She pressed transmit and spoke clearly, the way she would speak to a court.

"This is Ilana Verrec, counsel of record for the Karawari land claims, transmitting from position upriver near the former Catholic mission known as Vunapope. All stations, all stations, please record the following."

She opened the ledger.

"For the attention of the Minister for Lands, Mr. Okuk; the consortium represented by Bram Houtman, surveyor; and any party holding or purporting to hold concession rights over the salvage site designated by coordinates contained in a deed dated 1974." She read the coordinates aloud, slowly, twice. Let every boat on the river know exactly where these men stood. "I hold in my possession a certified copy of internal documents belonging to the above parties. These documents state, in the surveyor's own hand, that the legal basis of the concession is defective. That the original deed was executed under the authority of the Republic of New Guinea, a state which was never recognized by any government, and that title is therefore void from inception. Void. From. Inception."

Her voice did not shake. Eleven years of courtrooms had built her voice like a seawall.

"I will now read selected entries verbatim."

And she read them. The personnel dispositions. The recommendation to suppress the defect until recovery was complete. The line about witnesses — *release to local authorities (cooperative)* — and the line about August Dunmore, *expendable*. She read the dates, the initials, the arithmetic of a plan that treated human beings as line items to be resolved. She read until her throat was raw and the rain had thickened against the cabin roof and somewhere outside a man's laughter had stopped.

When she finished, she paused, and then gave them the thing none of them expected, the thing she had decided on at three in the morning with the pen shaking in her hand:

"I make no claim on the gold. I state for the record that the Karawari descendants I represent claim the mission lands, the graves, and the memory of the seven men who sank that cargo rather than let it be used. The gold itself belongs to no one. It has killed enough. Any party attempting recovery after this transmission does so knowing the title is void, the permit is void, and every death from this hour forward is simple murder committed for nothing."

She released the key.

Silence filled the cabin, the particular silence of a radio room after transmission, when you can feel the whole river listening back. Then, faintly, through the static, voices began to answer. A mission station down near Ambunti, asking her to repeat the coordinates. A police post at Pagwi, dry-voiced, requesting verification. Someone — she thought it might have been old Father Anselm's successor at Timbunke — simply said, "Received. God keep you," and signed off, and that small blessing undid her more than all the threats she'd braced against.

She read the whole ledger again, top to bottom, for the second audience. Repetition, she had learned long ago, was how truth became fact. Once was an allegation. Twice was a record.

On the third pass, the shooting started.

* * *

It began not as gunfire but as argument — raised voices from the escort camp across the water, words torn apart by distance, and then a single rifle shot that cracked flat over the channel like a branch breaking. Ilana was out of the radio room and onto the deck before she had decided to move.

Across the black water, the string lights swung. Men were standing among them, and the card game was over. One of the escorts — she recognized him vaguely, a heavyset sergeant from the coast who had shown Tobe, absurdly, how to fold a paper boat two nights ago — had his rifle slung and his hands raised, palms out, facing another man whose weapon was still shouldered.

"Read it yourself!" the sergeant was shouting, and thrust a crumpled sheet of paper — someone had transcribed her broadcast, or written it down as she spoke — into the lantern light. "It's in their own writing! *Void!* There's no pay in a void! There's no pension for dying in a hole for nothing!"

"You don't know that's genuine—"

"It's *their own writing*, man! Their own numbers! You saw what happened to Enoch! You want your mother to hear you died for a stamp nobody ever stamped?"

A second rifle appeared, then a third — but pointing, Ilana realized with a vertigo that was almost giddy, in the wrong direction. Not at the sergeant. At the officer. The escort was not attacking anyone. The escort was coming apart, dissolving like sugar in river water, and the solvent was a piece of paper read aloud in the dark.

Okuk emerged from the command tent in a raincoat too fine for the place, and Ilana watched him take in the geometry of his own collapse in real time. He was, whatever else he was, intelligent. She saw him understand instantly that the situation had passed beyond money, beyond threats, into the one country where he had no passport: the country where men refuse. He raised his hands — a mirror of the sergeant's gesture — and began to speak in the low, reasonable register she had heard him use in his office, the voice of a man offering deals.

No one lowered a rifle. But no one fired either. The mutiny stayed exactly where she had hoped it would stay, in the territory of refusal, and Okuk stood in the middle of his own escort's silence like a man stranded by a change in the current.

Then the launch engines started.

Not Okuk's helicopter — that was grounded by weather on the far bank. The gunmetal launch. Kessler's boat, the one that had shadowed them through the sawgrass, riding low at the mission's improvised dock with its engines idling all night, its crew pretending to sleep. Now it was pulling away from the dock, running lights off, bow swinging toward the main channel and the flooded narrows below.

And on its stern deck, visible for one second in the wash of the escort's lanterns, stood Bram Houtman, soaked, coatless, clutching a leather case to his chest like a child's toy. His face turned toward the *Star* as the launch gathered way. Even at that distance Ilana saw the expression, and it was not rage. It was the look of an accountant watching a column refuse to balance — bewilderment that the universe had failed to follow his arithmetic.

He had the case. She understood at once what was in it: the original ledger, perhaps, or the deed itself, the physical documents he believed were the treasure now that the gold was contested. He thought he could run downstream past the flood, past the police posts already waking to her broadcast, past everything, and re-found his claim somewhere the river's memory didn't reach.

He had been on this river three weeks. He still didn't know it didn't have a somewhere else.

"Let him go," Ilana said quietly, to no one, to everyone. The sergeant, nearest the dock, glanced across at her — she saw the question in his face, the escort's training warring with its new freedom — and she shook her head. "The flood will do the accounting."

The launch's engines rose to a whine and it drove out into the channel, into the rain, and the darkness took it the way the river takes everything, without malice and without hurry.

* * *

Okuk came to her an hour later.

She had expected shouting, bargaining, the full repertoire. Instead he stood at the base of the *Star*'s gangplank in the rain, raincoat finally abandoned, a diminished man in a wet shirt, and asked, in a voice scraped nearly clean of authority, "Is any of it recoverable? Legally. If I resign the portfolio. If I cooperate with the inquiry."

It was such a lawyer's question that she almost laughed. Almost.

"You're asking me," she said, "whether surrendering saves you."

"I'm asking what the law says."

"The law says what I broadcast tonight. You can quote me chapter and verse whenever you like — I'll be saying it again at Angoram, under oath, in daylight, to whoever holds the inquiry." She came down two steps so they stood level, and the rain fell on both of them equally, which felt like the first honest thing the night had done. "But I'll tell you what the law doesn't say, Minister. The law doesn't say why. And when they ask me why I burned it all on an open band, I'm going to tell them about an old man named Enoch who died on crypt steps protecting a boy, for a piece of paper proving his grandfather chose right. That's the part you'll carry. Not the charges. The reason."

He looked at her for a long moment. Rain ran off his chin. Whatever he had been — and he had been formidable, she granted him that; it took a certain greatness of appetite to build a betrayal this large — he was looking now at the shape of his own ending, and finding it ordinary.

"I offered Dunmore a new life," he said, almost to himself.

"He has one. He's standing on it."

She went back up the gangplank and did not watch him walk away, because some departures deserve privacy, and because behind her, in the radio room, the amber dial was still glowing, and the band was still alive, and there was one more thing to say.

She keyed the transmitter a final time, near midnight, when the rain had thinned to mist and the channel breathed slow and high against the pilings.

"All stations. Final transmission from Verrec. The site at Vunapope is closed to salvage. The Karawari claim filed this evening covers the mission lands and graves. To the family of Enoch Karawari: your father and grandfather died protecting his people, and the whole river heard it tonight. That is all."

She signed off with her call sign and sat in the amber glow, exhausted, hollowed, lighter than she had been in eleven years. Through the bulkhead she could hear the boat's small sounds — Meja checking lines, Tobe's radio-bay tools clinking, August's footsteps crossing the deck above, unhurried, unhidden, a man who no longer had anything to conceal walking openly across his own ship.

Downstream, far off, thunder muttered over the narrows where a gunmetal launch was running dark into floodwater, chasing a leather case full of paper toward a sandbar that hadn't been there yesterday.

Ilana closed the ledger, and for the first time since she'd copied it, she locked it away not as ammunition, but as evidence — proof, for whoever asked, that the truth, spoken loudly enough to the whole river at once, was heavier than gold.
