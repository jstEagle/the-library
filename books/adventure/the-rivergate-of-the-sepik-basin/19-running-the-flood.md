## 19. Running the Flood

The river told us before the radio did.

I felt it in the wheel first — a tremor through the spokes like a pulse too fast for a dying man — and then in the water itself, which had gone from brown to the color of strong tea with milk stirred in, thick with silt torn from hillsides two hundred kilometers away. The Sepik was rising. Not the polite seasonal rise that comes and goes like a guest, but the flood, the one the old men on the middle river speak of in lowered voices, the one that erases channels overnight and moves whole islands and does not apologize to anyone for what it takes.

Nine days. That was what August said when he spread the charts on the deck plates that morning, his finger tracing our route downstream through country that would not exist by the time we reached it. Nine days before the flood crest closed the narrows at Biwat and turned our return into a drowning. Houtman was gone by then — fled upriver into the wall of brown water after Ilana's broadcast gutted his permit and his men refused to die for a dead claim — but the damage he'd left behind lived in our engine room, where his people had sabotaged the fuel lines on their way out, and in our trust, which Tobe was still rationing out to August one small spoonful at a time.

"Nine days," August said again, as if repetition could make the river obey arithmetic.

"The river doesn't read your charts," Enoch had told us once, weeks ago, around a fire that seems now like it burned in another life. He was gone — buried in the shallows above Vunapope, under a cairn of river stones his people would come back for when the water allowed — but his voice was still aboard. It lived in the way Tobe checked the bilge twice instead of once. It lived in the way I steered now, reading the current not from instruments but from the color of boils on the surface, the way driftwood stood on end, the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

The river forgives nothing. But I had begun to understand, in the way you understand things only after they've cost you blood, that it spares those who respect it. Not because it loves them. Because it recognizes them.

* * *

The engine died on the second day, forty minutes below the Ambunti cutoff, in a stretch of channel so narrow the buttress roots of the bank trees combed our wake.

I heard it happen before I understood it: a cough, a stumble, the deep note of the engine dropping half an octave like a singer losing heart mid-phrase. Then the temperature gauge began its climb, and black smoke came up through the deck boards smelling of burnt sugar and something worse beneath it.

"Tobe!" August's voice, already moving.

Tobe was down the hatch before August finished the word, and I brought us toward the bank with the current pushing us faster than I liked, the boat slewing sideways in water that had gained maybe a meter of depth since dawn and gained speed with every centimeter. The banks were going soft. You could see it — the mud slumping into the river in silent grey slides, whole sections of shoreline quietly deciding to become river themselves.

"Injector line's sheared," Tobe shouted up through the hatch, his voice bouncing off engine metal. "Not wear — cut. Someone cut it clean." His head appeared in the companionway, face smeared black, eyes furious. "Houtman's people did this before they ran."

"They wanted us stranded for the flood," August said quietly. "Or slowed enough that Kessler's launch could take us at their leisure."

"Well, they got the first part." Tobe wiped his hands on a rag that only moved the grease around. "Second injector pump's cracked. I can't fix cracked. I can maybe — *maybe* — bypass off the auxiliary line, if I cannibalize the washdown pump hose and the spare fittings aren't rusted through, which they will be, because nothing on this boat is ever where it should be—"

"How long?" I asked.

He looked at me, and for a moment I saw him clearly: sixteen years old, grease to the elbows, standing in a flooding river valley on a crippled boat with armed men somewhere behind us, and not one flicker of fear on him. Only calculation. Whatever else this river had taken from Tobe, it had given him this — the certainty of his own hands.

"Six hours if the fittings hold. Eight if I have to fabricate. Twelve if the universe hates me personally, which, statistically—"

"Six hours," August said. "Anchor in the eddy behind that point. Meja, keep us bow-on to the current. If we swing broadside in this flow, we'll wrap ourselves around a tree."

We anchored in the lee of a clay point where the flood had carved a slow green eye of slack water, and Tobe disappeared into the engine room, and the river went on rising around us like a tide with no moon to answer to.

I took the noon watch alone while August slept — truly slept, for the first time since Vunapope, the sleep of a man whose body had simply overruled him. Ilana sat on the foredeck with the copied ledger open on her knees, though she wasn't reading it. She was watching the water go by, all that brown power, carrying trees whole, carrying roofs, carrying — I was sure of it, though I never said so — carrying things we didn't want to think about from the drowned mission downstream toward the sea.

"Do you regret it?" she asked me suddenly. "The broadcast. What it cost."

I thought about Enoch's cairn upstream. About the gold we'd left sealed in the dark, and the men who'd died reaching for it, and the fact that I couldn't remember any of their faces anymore except Enoch's, and that Enoch's face was the only one I'd ever needed.

"No," I said. "Do you?"

Ilana closed the ledger. "I spent ten years learning to use paper as a weapon," she said. "It turns out the same paper can be a shield. Depends who's holding it." She looked downstream, toward the narrows we still had to run. "Kessler won't stop, Meja. Men like him don't accept a loss. They convert it."

She was right. We all knew she was right. The radio traffic that afternoon confirmed it — fragments of chatter on the working bands, a voice with Kessler's flat coastal accent asking questions about our position in words too careful to be innocent. Somewhere behind us, closing at whatever speed a gunmetal launch could make against the building flood, was the last debt we owed, coming to collect.

* * *

On the fourth day we ran the Biwat narrows with the engine held together by prayer and a washdown hose, and I learned what the flood does to moving water.

It doesn't just raise it. It *quickens* it. The channel that had been a slow brown lane on our way upriver was now a race, five knots where there had been two, and the standing waves at the constriction stood taller than our gunwale, folding and unfolding like something breathing. Whirlpools opened and shut along the eddy lines with a sound like great mouths smacking. A floating island of stripped pandanus came down the throat of the narrows ahead of us, spinning slowly, and vanished into a boil and did not come up.

"Meja." August stood beside me at the wheel, one hand on the rail, watching my hands rather than the water. "Your river."

My river. My grandmother had fished these waters; my father had run log barges through them until the river took his barge and then, a year later, decided it wanted him too. I had grown up on the understanding that the Sepik is not a road. It is an animal, old and patient and utterly without malice, and you do not fight an animal like that. You ask it. You watch what it wants, and you give it exactly that, no more.

"Throttle a quarter," I said. "Follow the slick water on the left — see how it's smooth where everything else is broken? That's the main vein. And whatever happens, don't let him steer us straight at the standing wave. The wave isn't the danger. The hole behind it is."

August nodded and gave the helm to me entirely, and I took us down.

For eleven minutes I did not breathe properly. The boat surfed the flood like a leaf on a spillway, the banks blurring past, Tobe's rebuilt engine screaming a note I didn't like but couldn't refuse, and twice the current grabbed our stern and tried to swing us broadside and twice I caught it with rudder and throttle together, feeling the hull answer me the way a horse answers a rider it has decided to trust. Then the channel widened, the standing waves lay down behind us, and the roar of the narrows faded into the general mutter of the flood, and August exhaled beside me like a man surfacing.

"Grandfather ran barges here," I said. I don't know why I said it. Maybe because Enoch would have wanted someone to say it aloud, in that place.

"He'd have been proud," August said. And then, quieter, almost to himself: "All of them would have."

Behind us, far back at the edge of sight, a grey shape entered the mouth of the narrows and stopped. Even at that distance I could read the hesitation in it — the launch hanging at the constriction, refusing the vein, fighting the water instead of asking it. Then it turned aside, into the eastern channel, taking the long way.

"The river knows," Tobe said at my shoulder. He'd come up silently, grease-black, watching the shape recede. "It always knows. That's why the old people talk to it."

* * *

The seventh night, anchored in drowned forest with our decks lit by a single pressure lamp and the water lapping at branches that should have been three meters over our heads, Kessler's launch found us anyway.

I heard the diesel before I saw anything — a different note than ours, higher, harder, a sound like a blade being drawn — and then the running lights came around the drowned treeline and there it was, gunmetal grey, riding low with the weight of whatever it carried, and standing at its rail with a spotlight in his fist was a man I recognized from Angoram: Kessler's chief enforcer, the one they called Salau, whose reputation preceded him the way a wake precedes a boat.

"August Dunmore!" The voice carried flat across the water. "You have property of Mr. Kessler aboard. Documents. You hand them across tonight, and we part friends. You don't—" the spotlight swept our deck, finding each of us in turn, Ilana with her chin up, Tobe frozen halfway out of the hatch, August standing very still by the wheel, "—and the flood does our work for us. Your choice. You have until I finish this cigarette."

The red dot of the cigarette glowed in the darkness between the boats. The flood muttered through the drowned forest. Somewhere close, a crocodile slid off a log without a sound, and I thought: even the crocodiles are listening.

August didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was calm in a way I had learned to recognize — the calm of a man who has already done his arithmetic and found, to his own surprise, that the answer costs less than he feared.

"You want documents, Salau? There are no documents. There's a burned deed and a dead claim and a minister in custody, and the only thing aboard worth stealing is a bell we're bringing home to Enoch's village. Take it up with the river if you disagree."

A pause. The cigarette glowed. Then Salau laughed — a short, ugly sound — and the launch's engines came up out of idle, and I understood with a cold drop in my stomach that negotiation had never been the plan. The spotlight swung and fixed on our wheelhouse, blinding me, and the grey shape began to move, cutting the corner across the flooded timber toward us, straight through water no pilot alive knew the depth of.

"Tobe," August said. "Now."

What happened next happened fast, and I have replayed it so many times since that it has worn smooth, like a stone in a current.

Tobe had spent two days, in the hours between watches, doing something to the forward winch cable and the anchor and a length of chain he'd salvaged from the wreck at Kanaini — jury-rigged, illegal in four jurisdictions, brilliant. At August's word he threw the switch, and our anchor shot out on its cable across Salau's approach line, dragging the chain with it, and the chain sank into the flooded timber exactly where the submerged fence-line of a long-drowned garden ran — a line of old iron fence posts, chest-high once, now invisible under two meters of brown water, lying in wait for anyone who crossed that corner blind.

Salau's pilot couldn't have known. Nobody could have known; Tobe had found the posts himself, snagging his dive line on them the day before, and marked them in his head the way he marked everything, that magpie memory of his.

The launch hit the fence line at seven knots.

The sound was enormous — tearing metal, snapping timber, a human scream — and then the grey shape lurched, slewed, and began, horribly, quietly, to settle by the bow. Not dramatically. The river doesn't do drama. It simply opened a place in itself and accepted the boat, the way it accepts everything, without comment.

"Throw them the lifering!" August was already moving. "Both rings! Now!"

And here is the thing I will carry longer than any scar: we saved them. Three of the five, anyway — Salau among them, hauled gasping over our transom in his soaked uniform, coughing brown water, his spotlight lost, his cigarette long gone. Two of his men the river kept. We searched with lights until August called it, because searching floodwater at night is how rescuers become victims, and Salau sat on our deck plates shivering and staring at nothing, and finally he said, to no one, in a voice like a child's:

"There was no sandbar there yesterday."

No. There hadn't been. The flood had built one overnight, a bar of silt laid down beneath the surface exactly where a fleeing boat would cross it, and the drowned fence posts had done the rest. The river had set its trap and sprung it, indifferent as gravity, and spared us — not because we were good, I think, but because we had asked. Because Tobe had listened to the water and August had trusted him and I had steered the vein instead of fighting the current, and because Enoch's people had taught all of us, in one way or another, that you do not take from the Sepik without giving something back.

"We'll put you ashore at Pagwi," August told Salau. "You'll walk to Angoram and tell Kessler it's finished. Tell him the deed is ash and the claim is dead and the men who ended it are coming home. Tell him the river settled his account."

Salau looked up at him, water still dripping from his hair, and something in his face had emptied out — not defeat exactly, but the particular hollowness of a man who has seen the instrument of his employer's will fail against something older and larger than any employer.

"And if he sends me back?" he asked.

August crouched to his level. "Then next time," he said gently, "we won't throw the rings."

* * *

We came down the last hundred kilometers with the flood cresting around us, the river wider than a lake now, the banks gone entirely, the whole world one moving brown sheet dotted with the crowns of drowned trees. Tobe's engine held. Ilana stood watch with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against the spray. And on the ninth morning, exactly as August had promised, we rounded the last bend above Angoram and saw the town — roofs, masts, the mission bell tower, smoke rising straight in the windless air — and the whole crew came out on deck without being called, and nobody spoke, because there was nothing to say that the silence didn't say better.

Except Tobe. Tobe leaned on the rail beside me and watched the town grow, and after a while he said, in the matter-of-fact tone he used for engine reports:

"They'll want statements. Police, the district officer, everyone. August is going to tell them all of it — he said so, didn't he? Everything. The chest, the lies, all of it."

"Yes," I said.

"And us? What are we going to say?"

I thought about it. About nine days of floodwater and a fence line and a sandbar that hadn't existed yesterday. About Enoch's cairn waiting upstream for his people to come. About the difference between the story we could have told and the one we were going to.

"The truth," I said. "All of it. It's the only thing we've got left that the river hasn't already claimed — and it's the one thing Kessler can't buy back."

Ahead of us, at the Angoram wharf, I could make out figures waiting — police shirts, a district officer's white shirt, and one figure apart from the others, tall, motionless, watching us come in with the stillness of someone who has been standing there a long time.

Ilana saw him too. Her hand tightened on the rail.

"That's the magistrate from Wewak," she said quietly. "He came early. He came to hear it firsthand."

The bell tower loomed closer. August rang the ship's bell once, slow, for Enoch, and the sound went out across the floodwater, and the waiting figures on the wharf grew no smaller at all.
