## 20. Testimony at Angoram

The police post at Angoram had once been a trade store. You could still see it in the bones of the place — the wide verandah where bêche-de-mer and croc skins had been weighed a lifetime ago, the iron roof gone orange with rust, the counter where a constable now sat behind a ledger instead of scales. The river ran brown and swollen past the stilt legs, carrying the flood season's debris: whole trees, half a canoe, a dead pig bloated and turning slowly in the current like something considering whether to come back.

I sat on that verandah for three hours before anyone came to take my statement, and I was grateful for every minute of it. Grateful because I knew what I was going to do, and I wanted to feel the weight of it settle into me properly, the way silt settles after a flood — not all at once but layer by layer, until you can't lift it anymore and you stop trying.

Meja brought me tea in a tin mug that tasted of kerosene. She sat down across from me without being asked, which was her way, and looked out at the water.

"You don't have to tell them everything," she said.

"Yes, I do."

"August." She turned the mug in her hands. "Tobe told us what he saw. The sonar readings. We're not going anywhere."

"I know." I watched the dead pig swing lazily around a snag. "That's not why."

She waited. Meja was good at waiting; nursing teaches you that the silence does half the work.

"Because I've been silent my whole life," I said. "Silent about Kessler. Silent about what I pulled off that wreck. Silent about the deed in the fuel drum, the coordinates, the lies I fed Houtman, the sonar readings I faked while Enoch was bleeding out on the deck of my own boat. Every bad thing that happened upriver happened inside my silence, Meja. It grew there. Like rot in a hull — you don't see it from outside, but it's eating through the planks the whole time." I looked at her. "I'm done keeping things below the waterline."

She studied me for a long moment, and then she did something I didn't expect. She reached over and put her hand flat on mine, brief and hard, the way you'd press a dressing onto a wound.

"Then tell it all," she said. "And when they ask you why you burned it, don't dress it up. Tell them the true reason, even if it makes you look small."

"What's the true reason?"

"That you wanted it," she said. "For one night in that crypt, with your hands on the gold, you wanted it more than you've ever wanted anything. And you gave it back anyway. That's the part worth telling."

* * *

They took my statement in the old store room, where the shelves still stood along the walls with their price tags faded to ghosts. A sergeant named Waim did the writing, a thin man with careful hands and reading glasses that kept sliding down his nose, and a district officer called Mrs. Anis who asked the questions. She was Sepik-born, I could hear it in her voice, and she had the particular patience of someone who has heard a thousand men lie and can afford to wait out the truth.

"Start at the beginning," she said. "Not with the chest. Before the chest."

So I started with Kessler. I started with the broker who paid in silence, whose arithmetic never appeared on paper but always came out in his favor, who owned my debts the way a man owns a dog — not cruelly, most days, but completely. I told them about the navy-sealed wreck nobody names, the scrap runs, the near-drowning when my air line fouled on a spar and I hung forty feet down in green blindness listening to my own heart decide whether to keep going. I told them how I'd lived, which is to say survived, which is not the same thing.

Then the chest. The lead lining, the crest stamped in wax and metal — the sun rising over the ranges, the words Republic of New Guinea beneath it, a nation declared by a handful of men in 1974 and dead before any government on earth shook its hand. I described the documents exactly as I'd found them: the concession deed, the survey maps drawn in a German missionary's careful hand, the letters naming a cache moved inland ahead of the Japanese advance and sunk rather than surrendered — sunk by Karawari men, among them Enoch's grandfather, who chose the river over the republic because the river at least belonged to someone.

Mrs. Anis let me talk for two hours without interrupting. When I finally stopped, she took off her glasses and cleaned them on her blouse, unhurried.

"You hid the deed from Kessler," she said. "You signed Houtman's contract knowing the permits were a fiction built on a fraud. You falsified sonar readings to stall an expedition you were guiding. You concealed the contents of the chest from every person on that boat, including a man who died protecting your secret." She said it without heat, laying each item on the counter like scrap to be weighed. "Why should this office believe anything you say now?"

"Because everything I just told you makes me guilty," I said. "If I were lying, I'd have made myself braver. I'd have told you I planned to burn it from the start. I didn't. I stood in that crypt with a bar in each hand and I calculated. I know exactly what my life was worth per kilo. I'm not asking you to believe I'm good. I'm asking you to believe I'm finished."

Waim's pen had stopped moving. Mrs. Anis put her glasses back on.

"The charges against you will be substantial," she said. "Concealment. False declaration. There may be questions about the deaths — Okuk's escorts, the men from Kessler's launch. The Crown Prosecutor will want to know why you detonated charges in a flooded structure with people nearby."

"Because Houtman's men were coming back early to bury witnesses, and flooding the crypt was the only way to close it before they arrived. I'll swear to that under oath. And I'll swear to the other thing too — that Enoch Karawari was shot covering a boy's escape, and that his blood is on every hand that came up that river chasing paper. Mine included."

She wrote for a while. Outside, the rain started again, soft on the iron roof, and the river rose another finger's width against the stilts.

"You understand," she said at last, not looking up, "that a statement like this doesn't end with you. It goes up. Port Moresby. Maybe further. Men with more power than Minister Okuk had will read it, and some of them will be angry, and some of them will be afraid, and both kinds will look for ways to make you regret having spoken."

"I've been regretting things my whole life," I said. "At least this one would be worth it."

* * *

Ilana found me on the verandah at dusk, when the rain had thinned to mist and the river had gone the color of hammered pewter. She was carrying her document case the way she always carried it — clamped under her arm like a shield — but there was something different in her face, something loosened.

"They released the transcript to me this afternoon," she said, sitting beside me on the bench. "As counsel of record for the Karawari claim. I've been reading it for three hours."

"And?"

"And it's the first honest document I've seen since this whole thing began." She laughed, short and without much humor. "Do you know what I did when I intercepted that memo? I went to Okuk. I offered to find him his heirs, thinking I could steer the claim toward my clients and cut myself a piece of legitimacy on the way. I saw the river as leverage. I told myself it was for the Karawari, but it was for Ilana Verrec first, and the Karawari got whatever was left over." She looked out at the water. "Enoch saw it in me from the first day. That patience of his wasn't kindness. It was assessment."

"He liked you anyway."

"He liked what I might become." She opened the case and took out a folder, thinner than the ones she usually carried. "Which brings me to what I filed this morning. While you were in there giving your statement, I was at the lands office doing the same thing in my own way."

"The mission lands."

She nodded. "Vunapope. Not the gold — the ground. The claim I filed names the Karawari descendants as customary owners of the drowned mission site and the channel lands around it, based on Enoch's testimony, the 1974 letters, and your statement. No mention of bullion. No mention of a concession. As far as the registry is concerned, the Republic of New Guinea never held title to anything, because no one ever recognized it, and a deed issued by a nation that never existed conveys nothing. It's not even fraud, legally speaking. It's a nullity." She smiled faintly. "Houtman's permits authorize salvage of property that cannot, under law, exist. I've already sent copies to the mining registrar."

"You killed it with paperwork."

"I killed it the way I know how. You used fire. I used forms." She closed the case. "Between the two of us, I think we've buried it deeper than the river ever did."

We sat in the mist and listened to the water. Somewhere downstream a boat engine coughed and caught, coughed again, steadied.

"There's something else," Ilana said. "Kessler."

"What about him?"

"He's ashore. Has been since yesterday — came into Angoram on the mail boat, quiet, alone, no launch, no muscle. Mrs. Anis says he booked a room at the rest house and hasn't left it." She turned to look at me. "Your statement went up the line fast, August. Faster than statements go. And somebody up the line remembered that a broker with no registered vessels, no licenses, and no visible income has been financing salvage operations on protected wrecks for eleven years. Two constables walked to the rest house an hour ago."

I felt something move in my chest that I couldn't name at first, and then I could. It was the absence of fear. For eleven years, the thought of Kessler learning my name had lived in me like a splinter worked so deep the flesh grew around it. Now the flesh was closing over empty space.

"Is he resisting?"

"He's sitting on the verandah of the rest house drinking coffee," Ilana said, "waiting for them. Mrs. Anis says he thanked the constables when they arrived. Said he'd been expecting them for years and was surprised it took this long." She paused. "He asked them to pass a message to you."

The rain had stopped. The mist was lifting off the river in long rags, and the far bank emerged tree by tree, dark and dripping.

"What message?"

"He said—" Ilana hesitated, and I realized she was choosing whether to soften it, and decided not to, because she understood by now that I was done with softening. "He said: 'Tell the diver the river keeps better books than I do.'"

I laughed. It came out of me unexpectedly, rough and real, the first laugh since Enoch died, and it hurt somewhere under my ribs like a stitch tearing loose.

"Yeah," I said. "That sounds like his arithmetic."

* * *

They came for my formal interview the next morning, and I gave it in full, on the record, under caution. Waim wrote until his hand cramped and another constable relieved him. I told them about the night assault at the crypt, the gunfire among the submerged pews, the lantern light moving underwater like something alive. I told them about Enoch dying with his grandfather's letter pressed into my palm, and what the letter said — that the gold was cursed only because men let it be, and that the curse could end with a choice. I told them about standing alone in the crypt with a bar in each hand, hearing boots on the deck above, and setting the charges meant for salvage to flood instead. I told them I had wanted the fortune. I told them I had given it back. I told them the truth the way you take out a splinter — quickly, all at once, and then the strange clean ache of the wound beginning to close.

When it was done, Mrs. Anis removed her glasses and regarded me across the old trade-store counter for a long moment.

"Do you have anything to add?" she asked.

"One thing." I leaned forward. "There are four people on my boat who trusted me less than they should have and stayed anyway. Meja Raka, Tobe Waim — no relation, I think — Enoch Karawari, who is dead, and Ilana Verrec. Whatever happens to me, their conduct on that river was better than mine. I'd like that in the record."

"It's in the record," she said. Then, quieter, almost off the page: "My father fished the middle Sepik. He used to say the river forgives nothing, but it remembers everything — and that the two are not the same thing." She squared the papers. "Go home, Dunmore. We'll send for you when we send for you."

* * *

I walked down to the water in the late afternoon. The flood had peaked overnight and begun its slow retreat; the stilt lines showed a wet band above the current, marking where the river had been, the way a scar marks where a wound was. My boat lay at the mooring with Meja aboard, running the engine at idle, flushing the silt out of the cooling system. Tobe was up the mast — our mast, our rusted, patched, resurrected mast — re-lashing the antenna bracket with wire and a confidence that would get him killed someday or make him rich, and possibly both.

Ilana stood on the bank with her shoes off, ankle-deep in the shallows, reading a telegram she'd collected from the post office. When she saw me she folded it away, but not before I caught the shape of her expression — not joy, exactly. Something more careful than joy.

"Good news?" I asked.

"The Karawari claim has been docketed for hearing. Customary title, Vunapope site and channel lands. First listing after the flood season." She looked at the water. "Enoch's village will own the ground where his grandfather sank the gold. They'll own the bell, when we raise it. They'll own the story." She glanced at me sideways. "It's not much, compared to what we threw away."

"It's everything, compared to what we almost became."

Downstream, near the rest house, I could see a small knot of people on the bank. Two constables, a district officer, and a heavy man in a white shirt standing with his hands at his sides, unresisting, watching the river as though it owed him money. Even at this distance there was something in his posture I recognized — the stillness of a man doing final arithmetic. They walked him toward the police post, and he went without hurry, and just before the trees swallowed the group he stopped and turned and looked directly downriver, directly at my boat, though I don't see how he could have known which one it was.

Then he raised one hand — not a wave, not quite. An acknowledgment. A bookkeeper closing a ledger.

Meja cut the idle and the engine dropped to a tick. Tobe slid down the mast and landed on the deck light as a bird. Ilana waded back up the bank and put her shoes on, and none of us said anything, because there was nothing left that needed saying out loud.

But as I stood at the tiller, readying to take my people home, a boy came running down the bank from the post office, waving a radio flimsy over his head, shouting my name against the current — a message come up the band from somewhere downriver, urgent, unsigned, and addressed to the diver by a name I hadn't heard spoken in eleven years.
