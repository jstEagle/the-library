## 21. What the River Keeps

The bell came up on the ninth day of the new season, and it came up singing.

I was on the winch when it broke the surface — Meja's rule was that whoever ran the line got to see it first — and for a moment all I could do was stand there with my hands still on the crank and let the water pour off it in silver sheets. The bronze caught the morning sun the way a fish flashes when it turns broadside, and the whole channel seemed to hold its breath around us. Then the *Wanderer* rolled a little on her mooring lines, the bell swung against its sling, and it rang. One note. Low and round and old, like a voice that had been underwater so long it had forgotten how to be anything but patient.

"Steady," Meja said beside me, though her voice wasn't steady at all. It had a crack down the middle of it.

August came out of the wheelhouse without being called. He'd been below since dawn, going over the charts of the Vunapope channel that we weren't supposed to have anymore — the permit was dead, the coordinates were burned, everything official about this stretch of river belonged to nobody now, which meant it belonged to the river. He stood at the rail and looked at the bell swinging there, dripping, ringing its one soft note every time the current nudged the hull, and he took off his hat.

Enoch's village was two days downstream. We were bringing it home.

* * *

You have to understand what Vunapope was by then. It was nothing. That was the whole point.

Weeks had passed since the night at the crypt — weeks of statements and hearings and Ilana filing papers in Angoram while Kessler sat in a cell ashore learning what it felt like to be the thing in the drum instead of the man who owned it. Weeks of Houtman nowhere, which everyone pretended was the same as him being dead, because no one wanted to say out loud that a man like that doesn't drown just because you'd like him to. The flood had come and mostly gone, leaving the banks scoured and rearranged, sandbars moved like furniture in a house where nobody was watching. The Sepik had redrawn itself the way it always did, indifferent to gold and permits and men with guns.

And August had taken the *Wanderer* back to work. Ordinary work. Scrap work. The kind of diving that pays for diesel and rice and Tobe's school fees, if I ever went back to school, which was a conversation Meja kept having and I kept surviving.

We'd been running a line of small wrecks downstream of Ambunti — a collapsed trading post barge, an outboard engine graveyard, a ferry pontoon that had been sitting in the mud since before I was born. Honest salvage. The kind where what comes up is worth exactly what it weighs, and nobody follows you, and nobody dies. For the first week I kept catching myself listening for engines behind us. Gunmetal launches. Radios crackling with other people's plans. But the river behind us stayed empty, and slowly my shoulders came down from around my ears, and I learned what August already knew: that silence isn't always a trap. Sometimes it's just the river, breathing.

Meja taught me to dive properly in those weeks. Not the way I'd taught myself — the breath-holding, the grabbing, the boy who goes down and comes up fast with whatever his hands found. She taught me the slow way. The way where you check your gear twice and your partner once and yourself three times, and you go down the line hand over hand, and you equalize early and often, and when the panic comes — because it comes, she said, it comes for everyone, it came for her the first year after they struck her name off the register — you don't fight it, you count it. Ten counts. Then ten more. The water doesn't care how fast you are. The water only cares whether you're honest with it.

"You dive like someone who used to steal things," she told me, third week, after I'd come up too fast off the pontoon wreck with a brass fitting jammed in my mesh bag. "Quick hands, quick exits. That's a good thief. It's a dead diver."

"I'm not a thief anymore."

"No," she said, and something in her face went soft and far away, looking at some register only she could see. "Neither am I. That's why we're both still here."

So I slowed down. I learned to hang in the green half-light with my fins barely moving and watch the catfish patrol the ribs of a drowned boat like old men walking a fence line. I learned that the river keeps everything, but it keeps it its own way — softening edges, taking names, turning iron to lace and wood to shadow. Everything down there becomes part of the same slow story. Even me, when I was under. Especially me.

* * *

It was my idea to go back to Vunapope. Or maybe it wasn't mine. Maybe it was Enoch's, working through me the way the flood works through the channels, finding the low places.

He'd died in the crypt with his grandfather's letter folded into August's hand, and we'd buried him at his village with the whole clan standing in the shallows, and I had stood there sixteen years old and dry-eyed until the old women started their keening and then I hadn't been able to stop, and Meja had put her arm across my shoulders and held me up like a strut holds a deck. Afterward, on the boat, none of us talked much. But I kept thinking about the mission. About the church under the water with its pews fanned open like a rib cage, and the bell tower standing above the current with its bell still hanging, still whole, still waiting.

Because here's the thing I couldn't stop thinking: the gold was gone. August had flooded it, buried it, ended it, and the river had come in behind him and closed the account. Nobody would ever dig there again; Ilana had made sure of it with filings and claims and a Karawari land title that covered the mission grounds like a lid. The concession was ash. The chest was evidence in a court file. All of it finished, sealed, kept.

But the bell wasn't gold. The bell was never anybody's treasure. The bell was just the thing that had called people to church, back when there was a church and people and mornings, before the channel shifted and the river swallowed the whole idea of Vunapope. And Enoch's grandfather had helped sink that barge in 1974, and Enoch had carried that weight his whole life, and now Enoch was gone, and his village sat on the bank two days downstream with no bell and no mission and a hole in it shaped exactly like an old man.

"Some things should stay down," August said, the night I finally worked up the nerve to say it out loud. We were at anchor off a nameless bend, and the mosquitoes were doing their evening work, and he was cleaning the dive masks with toothpaste because Meja said it kept them from fogging and August believed everything Meja said about diving, which was fair.

"The gold," I said. "Yeah. The gold should stay down."

"But?"

"But the bell isn't gold." I picked at the deck planking. "His village sang for him. I heard them. They've got no church anymore, but they sang anyway, and it sounded like — like something missing its other half." I looked up at him. "You said the river forgives nothing but spares the ones who respect it. Maybe respecting it means knowing what to take and what to leave. Everybody knows what to leave now. Nobody's asked what to take."

August was quiet a long time. Out in the dark a crocodile slid off the bank with a sound like a door closing.

"Meja," he called, without raising his voice, because she was already in the doorway of the cabin, because she was always already wherever she'd decided to be five minutes earlier.

"I heard the boy," she said. "He's right. And I want to see it." She came out and sat on the rail, feet dangling over black water like she didn't know what lived in it, which she knew better than any of us. "One dive. Clean survey first, then rigging. If the tower's unsound, we walk away and nobody argues."

"And if it's sound?" I asked.

She smiled, and in the lamplight I saw the nurse she'd been before the disgrace, the one who'd signed on to our crew of strays because nobody else would have her, and who had become the spine of the whole operation somewhere along the way without anyone announcing it.

"If it's sound," she said, "we raise it proper. Hand over hand. Slow. The way you bring anything home that matters."

* * *

The Vunapope channel had changed. Of course it had. The flood had taken the eastern bank out entirely and pushed the main current hard against the tower, so that what had been a side channel was now a throat of brown water funneling past the brickwork with a sound like continuous applause. We anchored upstream and ran a safety line down to the tower base, and Meja made me do the survey myself, because — her words — "you found it, you look at it, that's how ownership works underwater."

Down there the world was green murk and drifting silt, and the tower rose out of it like something between a chimney and a tree. The bricks were furred with weed. A school of tiny silver fish moved around the belfry arches in a single thought. And hanging in the arch, tilted maybe fifteen degrees, furred and patinated and utterly intact, the bell.

I hung on the line and looked at it for a long time, counting my breathing the way Meja taught me, and I swear the fish circled it like they were keeping it company. There was a cross cast into its shoulder, worn nearly smooth. There was a date, 1938, in raised numerals the silt hadn't quite reached. And there was a rope — no. Not a rope. A vine, grown down through the arch from the surface world, trailing from the bell's crown into the dark of the nave below, as if the river itself had been ringing it, slowly, all these years, for services nobody attended.

I went up and reported: sound, tilted, reachable, heavy — four hundred kilos if it was a gram, plus fifty years of concretion. Meja nodded like I'd read a chart correctly, which apparently I had.

Raising it took three days. We rigged a lifting frame off the *Wanderer*'s mast and a chain bridle under the bell's crown, and August dove with me to set the shackles, and I will tell you what nobody tells you about salvage: most of it is not glory. Most of it is fouled lines and dropped spanners and Meja swearing fluently in three languages at a shackle pin that wouldn't seat. But on the third morning the frame took the load, the bell lifted free of the arch with a shrug of silt that turned the whole channel the color of milk tea, and the *Wanderer* groaned upright under the weight, and we brought it home.

Hand over hand. Slow. The way you bring anything home that matters.

* * *

The village came down to the water before we even rounded the last bend. They must have heard the engine — or maybe word travels faster than boats on the Sepik, which it does, everyone knows it does — because there were forty of them on the mudbank, and when the bell swung into view above our rail, hanging in its sling like a great bronze heart, the sound they made was not cheering. It was lower than that. It was the sound people make when something they'd stopped hoping for walks out of the bush.

We grounded the bow gently and worked the sling ashore with half the village hauling on lines, and the old women directed everything, because old women direct everything, that is a law older than the Republic of New Guinea ever was. They walked the bell up above the flood line and set it on a frame of cut hardwood facing the river, and then an old man — Enoch's cousin, someone said, though at his age everyone was somebody's cousin — stepped forward and struck it once with the flat of his hand.

The note rolled out across the water and came back off the far bank, and I watched it travel through the crowd like wind through cane. People closed their eyes. Some of the old ones wept without any noise at all, just letting it run, the way the river lets things run. And I understood then what I'd only guessed at on the night I pitched the idea to August: the bell wasn't a relic. It was a voice. It was the missing half of the song. Fifty years the village had sung with only their own voices, and now the other singer had come home out of the deep, and the harmony could start again.

They fed us until we could barely walk. Fish and sago and greens from gardens I'll never find again on any map. And after dark, when the fires were down to coals, Enoch's cousin came and sat beside me on the log where I was mending the mesh bags, because that was my job now, my real apprenticeship alongside Meja's lessons — the one where you learn to keep things together instead of taking them apart.

"You are the one who found it," he said. Not a question.

"I found where it was. August brought it up. Meja rigged it. It was — everybody."

"Yes." He nodded slowly, the way old men nod when a young person has accidentally said something true. "That is how it should be. My grandfather's generation sank things alone, in secret, in the dark, and look what it cost." He looked at the bell, black against the stars, humming faintly whenever the breeze touched it. "Your generation raises things together, in daylight. Maybe that is the whole difference between a curse and a gift. Not the object. The number of hands."

I didn't say anything. I just kept mending the net, and he sat with me, and after a while he went to his fire, and I sat there a long time listening to the bell hum and the river run, and I thought about Enoch, and I didn't cry this time. It was more like the feeling after a good dive, when you're sitting on the gunwale with the sun on your back and your lungs full of air that belongs to you, and the water below you is just water again, holding what it holds, keeping what it keeps.

Some things should stay down. The gold. The lies. The men who came for both. Let the river have them; it's better at keeping secrets than any of us ever were.

But some things should come home. And now, whenever we pass that bend on charter runs — because yes, we take charters now, honest ones, tourists and surveyors and once a government hydrologist who paid cash and apologized for Okuk without being asked — whenever we pass, if the wind is right, we can hear it. Faint. One note, carried two kilometers over the water.

Calling people in. Calling them home. Doing the only job it ever knew.

* * *

We were three days out of Angoram, running light on the return leg, when the radio woke up.

Tobe was at the helm — my watch, my wheel, Meja's grudging certification — and I almost didn't answer it, because the band had been quiet for weeks except for market prices and a preacher in Pagwi who broadcast to no one. But the voice that came through was crisp, clipped, city-accented, and asking for the *Wanderer* by name.

"*Wanderer*, this is Verrec. Ilana Verrec. Do you copy? August — if you're aboard, you need to hear this. There's a man in Angoram asking questions about your crew. He says his name is Houtman."

The river ran on, brown and patient, carrying everything toward the sea.

"He's alive," Ilana's voice said, static chewing at the edges of it. "And he knows where you keep your charts."
