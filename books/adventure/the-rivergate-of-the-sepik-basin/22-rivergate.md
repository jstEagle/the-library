## 22. Rivergate

The morning the river ended, it did not end dramatically. There was no wall where fresh water met salt, no line you could point to and say *here the Sepik dies*. It simply grew wide, then wider, until the banks dissolved into mangrove haze and the water turned the color of pewter, and then of pearl, and then there was no more river at all — only the sea, breathing under a sky so pale it seemed embarrassed to be watching.

August Dunmore stood at the wheel of the *Karawari Star* and let her run.

She was not much to look at anymore. Nine days of floodwater had stripped her paint down to gray wood, torn away half her canopy, and left a scar along her port rail where Kessler's launch had come at them in the narrows like something surfacing from a bad dream. Her engine — Tobe's engine now, in every way that mattered — ticked beneath the deck plates with the contented irregularity of a heart that had learned to live with its murmurs. Meja said it sounded like an old man climbing stairs. Tobe said it sounded like money, eventually. August thought it sounded like the only honest voice he'd ever trusted.

Behind him, the others slept in whatever shade the broken canopy still offered. Ilana sat propped against the fuel drum — the same tarpon-slimed drum where he'd once hidden a dead nation's deed, though the drum held nothing now but diesel and the smell of old secrets — with a legal pad on her knees, writing in a hand too small and too steady for a woman who hadn't properly slept in two weeks. Meja lay flat on the deck with her cap over her face, one hand resting on the throttle housing as if even unconscious she didn't trust anyone else to touch it. And Tobe was at the bow, sixteen years old and sunburnt through, dangling a hand in the wake and pretending not to watch the horizon the way a man watches for landfall when landfall means going home.

August watched them all in the cracked mirror he'd wired above the wheel, because watching people directly had never been a skill he'd managed to learn, and because the mirror told him something the naked eye would have argued with: they looked like a crew. No. That wasn't right. Crews were hired. They looked like something crews became, if they were lucky and if the river permitted it, which it almost never did.

He looked back at the water ahead.

The mouth of the Sepik opened before them like a gate indeed — the word Enoch had used, that last night in the crypt's failing lamplight, his grandfather's letter open on his ruined chest. *The rivergate,* the old man had whispered, and August had thought it was delirium talking. It wasn't. It was geography. Every river has a place where it stops being itself, and the Karawari had always known this one: the spot where the brown water gave up its silt and its dead and its gold and its griefs, and handed everything over to the sea, which kept nothing and forgave nothing and forgot everything.

Enoch had asked to go home to his village, and they had taken him there, and the village had buried him on the ridge above the oxbow where he'd been born, facing the water, per the old custom. But the letter — his grandfather's letter, the one Enoch had carried folded against his skin for sixty years without telling anyone what it was — that letter had a different instruction. Enoch had made August swear to it with the last breath he had strength to shape into words.

*Take it to the rivergate,* the letter said, in a missionary-school hand gone brown with age. *What we sank, we sank for love of our own. What my grandson carries, let him give back to the water that holds us all. Nothing written should outlive the men who wrote it.*

So August had brought it here.

He cut the engine when the bottom went from ten fathoms to thirty, and the silence that followed was enormous — the particular silence of deep water, which is not empty but full, full of everything the river had ever swallowed and declined to mention. The *Star* rocked on a long sea-swell that had traveled, Meja once told him, all the way from some storm off New Ireland, and would keep traveling after they were gone.

* * *

They gathered at the stern without being asked. That was the thing about these three, August thought — the thing he still hadn't gotten used to, six weeks after Angoram, after the testimony, after the arrests and the hearings and the long slow business of becoming men and women with names again instead of instruments. You didn't have to ask them. They just came.

Ilana stood closest to him, close enough that he could see the ink stains on her fingers. She'd filed the Karawari claim on the mission lands three days after the flood dropped them at Angoram — the lands, not the gold; she'd been very clear about that, in front of the magistrate, in front of Okuk's lawyers, in front of the whole sour room. *The descendants claim the ground their grandfathers bled on,* she'd said. *The gold belongs to whoever can dive for it, and good luck to them.* The newspapers had loved her for it. The minister's office had not. Okuk himself was in custody now, awaiting trial alongside Kessler, whose launch had foundered on a sandbar that hadn't existed the day before — the river's last joke, delivered without expression — and whose ledgers, copied in Ilana's small steady hand, were currently dismantling half the consortium's board.

"You don't have to read it," August said. "You've read it."

"I want to hear it in your voice." Ilana looked out at the water. "Everything true sounds different out loud."

So he read it. He unfolded the brown paper carefully, the way you handle anything older than your own guilt, and he read Enoch's grandfather's words to the sea: about 1974, about soldiers and a German missionary's barge, about men who chose to sink a fortune rather than feed a republic that died before it drew its first breath. About how the curse everyone whispered of was never in the metal at all. *It is only this,* the letter finished, *that men let paper stand between them and each other. Burn nothing. Drown it. Let the water do what fire cannot — make it nothing gently.*

When he finished, nobody spoke. The swell lifted the boat and set her down, lifted and set down, patient as breathing.

Tobe cleared his throat. "Can I —" He stopped. Started again. At sixteen, he'd learned more about dying this season than most men learned in a lifetime, and it had aged his voice without aging anything else about him. "Can I say something? To him? To Enoch?"

"Say it," August said.

"I stole engines." Tobe addressed the water directly, chin up, the way he did everything since the crypt. "That's how you met me. I stole your spare propeller off the *Star* and I would've sold it in Angoram for forty kina and I'm not saying I wouldn't steal again, exactly, I'm saying —" His jaw worked. "I'm saying he taught me to dive. Nobody teaches anybody anything for free on this river. Everybody wants something. He wanted nothing. He just — taught me. Like I was worth teaching." He swiped his face with his wrist, fast, furious at his own eyes. "I was worth teaching."

"Yes," Meja said quietly. "You were."

Meja took the letter next. She held it a moment, reading it again silently, then folded it with a nurse's precision — corners aligned, crease sharp — and handed it back. "I ran from Wewak with a dead man's morphine in my bag," she said, to no one and everyone. "I figured the river was the one place big enough to hide in. Turns out it's the one place you can't hide. It finds out everything about you and then it puts you on a boat with three strangers who find out the rest." She almost smiled. "I used to think that was the worst thing that could happen to a person."

"And now?" Ilana asked.

"Now I think drowning alone is the worst thing," Meja said. "And we didn't."

* * *

August knelt at the transom with the letter in both hands.

The paper was light. That was the thing that undid him, kneeling there — not the weight of history or grief or any of the large abstractions he'd braced himself for, but the sheer weightlessness of it. Sixty years of carrying, a grandfather's whole testament, a dying man's last request, and it weighed less than a cigarette packet. All winter, in the dark months after the chest came up off the wreck nobody named, he had imagined that paper pressing on him like a headstone. It weighed nothing. It had never weighed anything. The heaviness had been his own, all along, and he had been pressing it against himself and calling it fate.

He thought about the crypt at Vunapope. He thought about standing alone in that flooded dark with a gold bar in each hand, hearing Houtman's men coming back through the pews to bury the witnesses, doing the arithmetic every man does — *this much buys the boat free, this much buys the debts gone, this much buys a new name in a new country* — and then setting the bars down anyway, because somewhere below the arithmetic a quieter voice had said: *you will spend the rest of your life listening for footsteps.* He had flooded the charges meant for salvage and swum out through black water while Vunapope's bell tower shuddered above him, and he had surfaced poorer than he'd ever been, and he had never once, in six weeks, missed it.

Still poor. Still marked — the magistrate's caution hung on him like weather, and there were men in Wewak who would cross the street rather than shake his hand, and men in Port Moresby who would buy him a beer purely to ask about the gold, and he would lie to neither kind and satisfy neither kind. That was the price. He paid it gladly. But it was real, and he had stopped pretending otherwise, because pretending otherwise was the old disease, the Kessler disease, the Okuk disease — the belief that a man could trade his way back to innocence.

He let the letter go.

The current took it instantly, the way the river takes everything — without malice, without ceremony. The brown paper rode the seam between river and sea for a moment, turning, turning, showing its lines of handwriting to the sky one last time, and then the swell came through and it was gone, and the water closed over the place where it had been as smoothly as if it had never been there at all.

Nothing written should outlive the men who wrote it.

"Done," August said. His voice came out rough. He stood, and his knees complained, and he was absurdly grateful for the complaint — for the ordinary animal fact of his body, alive and aching and his own.

Ilana put her hand briefly on his shoulder. She was not a woman who wasted gestures; he'd learned that about her somewhere around day four of circling each other with mutual concealment, back when they'd both been lying by omission across a chart table. Now there was nothing left to omit. It was astonishing how light that made her hand feel.

"The claim hearing is Thursday," she said. "In Angoram. The elders want you there. Not as a witness — as family. Enoch named you, before the end. There are people in that village who consider you kin now, and kin show up."

"I'll be there."

"They'll feed you until you can't move. Fair warning."

"He'll survive it," Meja said. "Barely."

Tobe was already forward, coiling line that didn't need coiling, the restless energy of a boy who'd learned that hands that aren't busy start remembering things. August watched him a moment and felt the strange doubled grief of it — grief for Enoch, and underneath it, unfamiliar and harder to name, something like gratitude with nowhere to put itself.

* * *

They raised the anchor around noon and turned the *Star*'s nose back toward the river.

The charter had come in that morning, by radio, crackling through the static: a fisheries survey out of Angoram, two weeks' work, modest pay, honest work — counting fish, essentially, instead of raising ghosts. August had accepted before the transmission finished. Two weeks of soundings and sample nets and Meja swearing at the engine and Tobe asking questions faster than she could answer them and Ilana annotating tide tables in her small steady hand. It wasn't treasure. It was better than treasure. It was Tuesday.

The river took them back the way it takes back its own — slowly, in its own time, without comment. By midafternoon the water had thickened from pearl to pewter to the familiar tea-brown of the lower Sepik, and the mangroves closed in green and dripping, and the first crocodile slid off a log ahead of them with barely a ripple, unimpressed. Somewhere upstream were wrecks nobody named, and a drowned mission with its bell raised and gone home to Enoch's village, where it hung now in a frame of bush timber and rang on Sundays, and children who had never seen the sea climbed the ridge to hear it.

August steered. Behind him, Tobe was explaining sonar to Ilana with the supreme confidence of the recently converted; Meja was asleep again, cap over her face, hand near the throttle; the engine ticked its uneven honest tick. In the mirror, the four of them looked like nothing much — a broke boat, a busted crew, a lawyer with ink-stained fingers, a kid, a disgraced nurse, a diver with a caution on his record and salt drying white on his forearms.

Poorer than he'd ever been. Marked for good. Alone in exactly none of the ways that counted.

Ahead, the channel bent north toward Angoram, and the afternoon light came down the river in long gold bars — ordinary light, worthless light, light no one could sink or sell or kill for — and August Dunmore turned the wheel and followed it home.
