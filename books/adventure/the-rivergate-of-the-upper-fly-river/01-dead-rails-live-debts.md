## 1. Dead Rails, Live Debts

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, and Yara Vasquez stood in the mud of the washout with her boots sinking to the ankle, listening to the river argue with itself below the embankment. The Fly was up again—half a meter overnight, brown and muscular, carrying whole trees sideways past the collapsed culvert like it was clearing its throat before saying something worse.

"Boss." Tomas's voice came from the far end of the spur, where the track simply ended, rails bent into a shape like a question mark. "You want to see this before we pull it."

"I've seen it," she said. "It's the same washout as the one at D'Albertis Junction, except this time we're getting paid for it."

"We're not getting paid yet."

"We're getting paid late. There's a difference, and the difference is interest."

Tomas grinned through the rain sheeting off his hard hat. He was forty-two, built like the ballast he hauled, and he had been with her six years—the longest anyone had stayed, including her. He knew her rhythms the way the crew knew the sound of a rail about to let go: a high singing note under the hammer, a warning you learned or you lost fingers.

Yara crouched at the edge of the washout and read the damage the way other people read a page. Twelve meters of embankment gone, slurried down into the gorge. Two rail lengths twisted past salvage. The culvert beneath had failed first—a rusted colonial-era pipe, undersized from the day it was laid—and everything above it had followed. Standard work. Ugly, heavy, standard.

The mining outfit that owned the spur called it an emergency. Everything was an emergency when it cost someone money. In truth the line had been dying for years, and the company had known it, and they'd paid for surveys that said so and filed them in drawers. Now the ore trucks had somewhere to be, and Yara Vasquez and her eleven people were the cheapest way to make the ground obey.

She straightened, wiped her hands on her thighs out of habit rather than effect—everything on her was already mud—and turned to face the crew assembling along the intact track behind her. Eleven faces under eleven hats, backlit by the gray coming up over the ridge. Nia already had her tool roll open, counting sockets by touch. Okonkwo stood apart, as always, watching the water instead of the rail, because water was his business and water was never finished talking.

"All right," Yara said. "Culvert first. We can't rerail onto a throat that's going to swallow us. Nia, I want the old pipe cut and the new sections staged by noon. Tomas, cribbing on the upstream shoulder—we build the embankment back from the bottom, not the top, and if anyone tells you faster is better, send them to me. Bea—" she caught herself, the name half out of her mouth before she remembered Bea wasn't there, hadn't been there for three years, wouldn't be there ever again unless grief performed a miracle it showed no signs of attempting. She covered it without breaking stride. "Okonkwo. Gauge on the creek above. If it comes up another thirty centimeters, everyone off the embankment, no arguments, no heroics. I don't pay enough for heroics."

"You don't pay enough for anything," said Devi, the youngest, from the back, and the crew laughed the tired laugh of people who had made the same joke in four provinces.

"That's why we're still cheap," Yara said. "Move."

* * *

They worked through the morning the way water works through a crack—steadily, without drama, finding every weak point in the problem and widening it until the problem gave. By ten the old culvert was cut free and dragged out of the muck, a corroded iron intestine that made Devi gag when she saw what had been living inside it. By noon the new sections were staged. By two the cribbing was holding weight, and Tomas walked the rebuilt embankment stamping his boots like a man testing ice, which in every way that mattered he was.

Yara did what she always did during the heavy hours: she walked the line. Not supervising exactly—Nia needed no supervision, and Tomas ran his gangs like a man running his own small country—but present, visible, reading. A crew was an instrument and it went out of tune quietly. You heard it in how people set their feet. You heard it in the jokes getting shorter.

Devi's feet were fine. Old Man Sipriano, who drove the tamper, was fine. But Roka, who'd been with her two years, was setting his feet like a man packing a bag, and when Yara came alongside him at the material sled he didn't quite meet her eyes.

"Kessler's recruiters were at the Junction hotel," he said, without preamble, because Roka had never learned to approach anything indirectly, including cliffs. "Two of them. Buying beers. Talking about a big contract coming. Big money, long duration."

"Long duration," Yara repeated. The words sat wrong in her mouth, like a rail gauge measured wrong by millimeters—nothing you could point to, everything you could feel.

"They said whoever takes it will be working for years. One outfit. No more chasing washouts."

"Sounds boring," Yara said, and handed him a spike, and he took it, and neither of them said anything else, but she carried the conversation with her the rest of the afternoon like a stone in her boot.

Because that was the thing about Dario Kessler. He didn't poach crews with money. Money was easy; everyone had money eventually, or thought they did. Kessler poached with *duration*. With the promise of stopping. And Yara had built her entire operation—her reputation, her rules, her life—on the understanding that stopping was the most dangerous thing a person could do.

Her rule was simple and she'd never broken it: never take a contract twice, never stay a season. Finish the job, collect, move. Up the Fly, down the Strickland, across into the high spurs where the old colonial lines went nowhere anymore except into memory. Motion was armor. A crew that kept moving owed nothing to any place, and no place owed anything to them, and nothing had time to catch up—not debts, not inspectors, not the past.

Twelve years ago something had caught up with her father, and it had been waiting for him precisely because he had stopped moving. He had taken a contract twice. He had stayed seasons. He had believed in a place enough to seal a tunnel and sign his name to it, and the place had eaten him, and Yara had signed the report afterward because she was seventeen and her hands were shaking and men in clean shirts told her it was the only way to protect the crew.

So: motion. Always motion. It wasn't wisdom. It was just the only shape of safety she trusted.

* * *

The payment came late, as promised, and short.

The company man arrived at dusk in a white pickup that had no business being within fifty kilometers of a working railhead, spotless, air-conditioned, the kind of vehicle that existed to transport paperwork between offices where rain was a rumor. His name was Hargreaves and he had the smile of a man who practiced apologies in mirrors.

"Ms. Vasquez. Bit of a snag with the transfer, I'm afraid. Head office wants the final sixty percent held against a performance bond until the line passes inspection."

"The line passes inspection tomorrow," Yara said. "You watched us build it. Your own engineer signed off on the culvert at midday."

"And he'll sign off again after the test train. Procedure." Hargreaves spread his hands, palms up, a gesture meant to convey helplessness and conveying, instead, the particular satisfaction of a man enjoying his position. "I know it's inconvenient. Cash flow, and all that. But you understand these days everyone wants security."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. Behind him the camp fires were lighting, orange points in the blue dark, and she could hear Devi laughing at something and the clatter of the mess kit and the ordinary music of her people ending a day alive. Sixty percent of the contract was payroll. Payroll was food, fuel, and the parts that kept the tamper running. Held long enough, a performance bond wasn't a bond—it was a hook.

"Here's what I understand," she said. "I understand your ore doesn't move until my line passes. I understand every day you hold that money costs you more than it costs me, because I've got eleven people eating beans and you've got shareholders. And I understand that if the full amount isn't in the account by the time the test train clears the culvert, my crew and I will be gone by nightfall, and you can explain to head office why their emergency contractor vanished with their emergency unresolved."

Hargreaves's smile flickered, recalibrated. "That would be a breach of—"

"It would be a woman honoring the only clause that matters, which is the one where I decide whose ground I stand on." She stepped closer, mud to his polished boots. "You people think contractors are like rails. Lay us down, run your loads over us. But rails get pulled up, Mr. Hargreaves. Ask anyone downstream what happens to a company that learns that lesson slowly."

He left with the smile reassembled but the eyes doing arithmetic, and Yara stood in the firelight knowing she'd won the week and possibly lost the month, because men like Hargreaves remembered, and companies talked to each other, and the map of people willing to hire her contracted a little every time she chose dignity over hunger.

Tomas appeared at her elbow with two tin mugs of the terrible sweet tea the cook made, and handed her one.

"Held sixty percent," he said. It wasn't a question; he'd been close enough to hear.

"Held sixty percent."

"Roka's right, you know. About the recruiters."

"Tomas."

"I'm not saying take it." He drank, looking out at the dark gorge where the river ran invisible but audible, that constant low argument. "I'm saying the math is getting worse every year. Washout rates are up, monsoons come early, and every outfit with debt is squeezing contractors like Hargreaves squeezed you tonight. We keep moving because moving is what we do. But boss—sometimes I think we're not outrunning anything. Sometimes I think we're just circling."

Yara drank her tea and said nothing, because the true answer was that he was right, and the truer answer was that circling was still preferable to landing, and the truest answer—one she kept in a waterproof tube with her father's maps, in the bottom of her kit, wrapped in oilcloth like a wound—was that there was one place on all these rivers she could never stop circling, because landing there would mean going in.

* * *

She opened the tube that night, which she almost never did, which was itself a kind of symptom.

The camp slept around her. Rain had started again, soft now, a drizzle ticking on the tarp. In the lamplight of her tent she unrolled the survey sheets her father had drawn twenty years ago, in the strong confident hand of a man who believed infrastructure was a moral act. Spur profiles. Culvert schedules. Grade calculations neat as scripture.

And one sheet, older than the others, drawn in different ink—red, faded to the color of dried blood—that traced a line up the gorge of the upper Fly to a place marked with a concrete portal symbol and a single handwritten word:

**sealed.**

No date. No signature. Just the word, pressed hard enough into the paper that she could feel the groove of the pen with her fingertip, the way you feel a scar.

The Rivergate. Her father had spent the last season of his life on that spur, and the last hour of his life inside it, and the official record said structural failure during planned demolition, sole casualty Elias Vasquez, site sealed per report co-signed by E. Vásquez-Ybarra, age seventeen, junior survey assistant.

Co-signed. As if she'd been a partner. As if a seventeen-year-old girl with her father's blood under her fingernails had been anything but a pen in someone else's hand.

She rolled the map away before the feeling could finish arriving. That was the discipline. Grief, like floodwater, was manageable only in channels; let it spread and it took the embankment down with everything on it.

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere upriver, thunder spoke in the register that meant the real monsoon was still weeks out but thinking about it. Yara banked the lamp and lay down and did the accounting she did every night: wages owed, fuel remaining, distance to the next contract, days until they moved again.

Eleven days. They'd finish the inspection, collect—she'd make Hargreaves bleed for the delay—and take the next job wherever it was, as long as it wasn't twice, as long as it wasn't here, as long as it wasn't anywhere near the red line.

She was almost asleep when Tomas's knock came, two taps, the code for *awake but not urgent.*

"Boss. Radio traffic on the company band. Somebody's chartering barges at the Junction—big ones, crane-equipped. Charter manifest says mineral survey." A pause. "Charterer's name is Kessler."

Yara lay in the dark with the rain on the canvas and the word *sealed* still grooved in her fingertips, and understood, with the flat certainty of a rail singing under a hammer, that the circling was over.

Something had found her.
