## 3. An Offer from Kessler

The helicopters came at dusk, which was the first insult. Nobody legitimate arrived at dusk on the Upper Fly. Dusk was when the river took things back—light, warmth, the will to keep working—and anyone who wanted something from you knew better than to interrupt that transaction.

Yara heard them before she saw them, a double-thump of rotors rolling down the gorge like a barrel dropped on stone. She was standing over the rerailing jack with a wrench in her hand and forty minutes of light left, watching Nia's crew lever the last flatcar back onto the spur they'd spent nine days rebuilding for a mining outfit that paid in promises. The sound made her look up before her mind caught up with her body, and for one unguarded second she felt it—the old animal certainty that the sky was about to take something from her.

Then the two machines came around the shoulder of the ridge, white-bodied, corporate-clean, running lights already on against the failing day. They didn't belong here the way a survey chopper belonged, all dents and dust. These had been washed. Someone cared how they looked.

"Tomas," she said, without turning. "Kill the work lights."

Tomas was already moving. That was the thing about Tomas—he read her voice the way other men read weather. By the time the lead helicopter flared over the camp pad they'd cleared last month for a medevac that never came, the camp had gone quiet in the particular way Yara liked: generators idling low, crew drifting toward their tents but not into them, every face turned outward with the polite blankness of people who had learned that strangers with money were more dangerous than strangers with guns.

The helicopters set down in a wash of rotor-wash that flattened the grass and sent a sheet of grit hissing across the rail bed. The doors opened before the skids fully settled, which told her everything about the man inside. Impatient men opened doors early. Careful men waited. Men who were both—that was a different category entirely, and Yara had spent twelve years hoping never to stand in front of one again.

Dario Kessler stepped out into the red light like a man arriving at a party he'd been assured would be worth his trouble. He was taller than she remembered, or maybe he'd just gotten better at using height as punctuation. Silver at the temples now, cut close. A field jacket that had never seen a field, worn open over a shirt pressed sharp enough to draw blood. Behind him came four men who moved like security contractors trying to dress down as engineers, and behind them, carried by one of them, a hard case the size of a coffin lid.

"Vasquez." He said her name the way you'd test a rail with a hammer—one clean strike, listening for the flaw. "You've lost weight since Port Moresby."

"You've gained a helicopter fleet." She kept the wrench in her hand. It wasn't a threat; it was a habit. Her hands were calmer holding tools. "This is a working camp. We don't have a bar."

"I don't need a bar." He smiled, and it was a good smile, the kind that had closed deals on three continents. "I need ten minutes and your attention. I'm told the second one is harder to buy."

Around her, the crew had arranged itself into its evening shape: Nia leaning against the water tank with her arms crossed, Okonkwo half out of his tent with a hydrology report still rolled in his fist, Bea nowhere visible, which meant Bea was watching from somewhere Yara couldn't see. Good. Let her watch. Let everyone watch what happened next, because whatever it was, it was going to cost somebody.

"Ten minutes," Yara said. "Then you lift off and we finish our contract."

"The mining outfit." Kessler glanced down the line at the half-rerailed spur, at the flatcar sitting proud of the rail with its wheels greased and waiting. "Koroba Consolidated. Yes." He said the name gently, almost fondly, and Yara felt the ground shift under her feet the way it did before a washout—not visibly, not yet, but somewhere deep below, something had begun to move. "I know them well. I own a great deal of what they owe."

* * *

He didn't want her tent, or the mess shelter, or any of the places a negotiation might be private. He wanted to walk the line. So they walked it, the two of them with the escort trailing far enough back to pretend it wasn't an escort, while the dusk went purple over the gorge and the first insects rose whining out of the grass.

"Do you know why I chose this hour?" Kessler asked.

"Because you thought I'd be tired."

"Because I wanted to see this." He stopped and gestured with one hand at the whole descending valley—the dead spurs fanning out from the main line like broken fingers, the rusted turntables, the loading ramps dissolving back into jungle. "Twelve years ago this basin moved eleven thousand tons of freight a week. Copper, timber, machinery. And then—" he snapped his fingers, a small dry sound swallowed by the river noise, "—nothing. Every company pulled out. Every rail left to rot. Do you know what that costs, Vasquez? Not in money. In nerve. An entire infrastructure abandoned because one man decided it should be."

"One man sealed a tunnel because it was killing people."

"One man," Kessler agreed pleasantly, "sealed a tunnel. And everyone else ran. Except your father, of course. He was very thorough. Blast patterns, structural plugs, the works. I've studied the demolition reports." He turned to look at her, and the smile was still there but the eyes above it had gone flat and patient, like water standing in a drill hole. "They're elegant. You must have been proud."

"He was proud of them," Yara said. "I was seventeen."

"Yes. You signed the closure report. I've read that too." He resumed walking, hands clasped behind his back, a man strolling his own estate. "Here's what interests me. Everyone else treated the Rivergate as a wound. Close it, forget it, let the river have it. Your father treated it as a problem to be solved permanently. Which means he understood something about what was down there that nobody else did. And understanding like that—" he glanced sideways at her, "—runs in families. Or so I'm prepared to bet."

"There's no job here, Kessler. Whatever you think is in that tunnel, the answer is water. Two hundred meters of flooded gallery behind a plug your own engineers called permanent."

"My engineers are new." He said it lightly, but the case his man carried said otherwise, and Yara found herself cataloguing it despite herself—hardshell, pressure-sealed, the kind of case that held documents worth more than the men carrying them. "The monsoon comes in five weeks. After landfall, the gorge becomes impassable for four months. Which means if anything is going to happen at the Rivergate, it happens now, with the best crews money can hire, under my direction." He stopped walking and faced her fully, and for the first time he dropped the charm entirely, letting her see the machinery underneath. "I want you to reopen it, Yara. I want your father's daughter running my dewatering and demolition. And I am prepared to pay in ways that will make this"—he gestured at the camp, the dead rails, the whole threadbare enterprise of her life—"look like what it is. A holding pattern."

"We're not for sale."

"Everyone is for sale. Some people just price themselves in loyalty instead of cash, which is more expensive for me but more reliable." He reached into his jacket and produced a folded sheet of paper, unhurried, a man who had rehearsed this moment in mirrors. "Your payroll. Fourteen names. I know what each of them is owed. I know Nia Okafor's explosives certification lapsed in March and she can't renew it without a sponsoring employer. I know Okonkwo has been sending money to a hospital in Lagos for a sister whose bills exceed his salary by a factor of three. I know Tomas Reyes has a daughter in Lae he's seen twice in four years because you keep him moving so he never has to go home and admit the moving isn't working." He watched her take the hit, and took his time about it. "And I know Koroba Consolidated owes this crew sixty-one thousand kina for work already performed, and that their receivables portfolio was quietly acquired eleven days ago by a holding company whose beneficial owner is a man standing in front of you."

The wrench handle creaked in Yara's grip. She loosened her fingers deliberately, one at a time, and made herself breathe the way her father had taught her—slow exhale, count of four, the breath you take when the charge is set and there's nothing left to do but walk away.

"So that's the offer," she said. "Pay me, or bury me."

"That's not an offer. That's context." Kessler's voice softened, and somehow that was worse. "The offer is this: reopen the Rivergate before monsoon. Full salvage contract, your terms on crew selection, hazard rates that will clear every debt on that page twice over. When it's done, your people stop being a salvage crew that survives and become a company that chooses. That's what I'm actually selling you, Yara. Not money. Choice. You've been running up and down these dead lines for twelve years because motion feels like control. I'm offering you the chance to stop."

The word *stop* went through her like a spike through a tie. She thought of her rule—never take a contract twice, never stay a season—and heard it suddenly for what it was, a wall she'd built to keep something out, and Kessler standing on the other side of it with his hand on the gate.

"No," she said.

It came out quieter than she intended. She made herself say it again, louder, with the whole camp listening.

"No. Find another crew. There are plenty who'll take your money and never ask what's behind the plug. I'm not one of them. My father died sealing that tunnel, and I signed the paper that says it stays sealed, and that paper is the only honest thing I've ever put my name to. So take your helicopters, take your payroll, and get off my line before dark, because after dark I stop being polite."

For a long moment Kessler simply looked at her, and she watched him recalculate—not defeated, nothing so satisfying as that. Repricing. A man finding the true weight of an object he'd misjudged.

"Well," he said finally. "That was worth the flight."

He turned and walked back toward the helicopters, and the escort fell in around him, and the hardshell case went with them, and Yara stood on her dead rails watching the rotors spin up and told herself it was over. Told herself he'd fly away and find some softer target and she'd never see that pressed shirt or hear that pleasant voice again.

The lead helicopter lifted, banked, and held—hovering over the camp for three full seconds, long enough for Kessler to look down at her through the glass. He raised one hand. Not a wave. Something more like a salute, or a promise.

Then they were gone, and the dusk closed over the gorge, and the crew drifted out of their tents and back toward the work lights because there was still a flatcar to seat and a contract to finish, and none of them asked her what had been said, because they'd all heard enough.

It was nearly midnight, the generator ticking down, when Tomas appeared at the flap of her tent. He didn't come in. He stood in the lamplight with his jaw set in the way it did when the news was bad and he'd already checked it twice.

"Satellite phone," he said. "Message came through the relay service. From Koroba Consolidated." He paused, and Yara watched him decide whether to soften it, and watched him decide not to, because he loved her too much for that. "Our contract's been terminated for cause. Nonpayment of outstanding invoices cited as crew negligence. Effective immediately." He looked past her, out at the dark where the river ran. "We're unpaid, Yara. All fourteen of us. And there's a barge scheduled through tomorrow with Kessler's expedition equipment aboard, heading upriver." His voice dropped. "Three of the crew have already asked me if he's hiring."
