## 4. What Stopping Costs

The radio crackled to life at dawn, and Yara knew before she answered that the news would be bad. Bad news kept banker's hours; it never came at a decent time.

"Vasquez." The voice belonged to Odili Mensah, the accounts manager for the Kikori Mining Consortium, a man who had spent three years perfecting the art of sounding apologetic while doing nothing. "There's been a— an acquisition. A change in ownership structure. I'm afraid your outstanding invoices are now under review."

"Under review." Yara stood outside her tent with the radio pressed to her ear, watching mist lift off the river in long gray ribbons. Behind her, the camp was waking: the clank of the cook pot, Tomas's voice running the morning tool count, the diesel generator coughing through its warm-up. Forty-one days of labor on the Wabag spur, rerailing eleven kilometers of washed-out track through country that wanted to eat the rails alive. "Odili. We finished the job. You signed the completion certificate yourself. I watched you sign it."

"And I stand by the work. It's excellent work. But the new owners have frozen all legacy disbursements pending audit."

"Who are the new owners?"

A pause. Long enough to be an answer in itself.

"Odili. Who?"

"A holding company," he said carefully. "Meridian Basin Holdings. Registered out of Port Moresby. I'm told they have interests in— in several river enterprises."

Yara closed her eyes. She could see it plainly, the way you could sometimes see weather coming before the clouds formed: Kessler sitting in some air-conditioned office above the harbor, signing papers with a fountain pen, buying her crew's wages the way another man might buy a round of drinks.

"When will the audit conclude?"

"That's difficult to say. These things can take months."

Months. By which time the monsoon would have come and gone, the spur would be underwater again, and half her people would have walked off into the bush looking for anyone who'd feed them.

"Tell your new owners," Yara said, "that I keep books too. And my book says they owe us two hundred and fourteen thousand kina."

"I'll pass that along," Odili said, in the tone of a man passing along a message to the sea.

She set the radio down and stood for a moment in the growing light, listening to her camp breathe. Then she went to find Tomas.

* * *

He was at the rail yard, as always, checking the tie-downs on the equipment flatcars with the methodical devotion of a man who believed the world could be held together by sufficient tension on sufficient straps. He was forty-three, built like the ballast he handled, and he had been with her since before the Rivergate, since before everything. He didn't look up when she stopped beside him.

"You heard," she said.

"Heard what?"

"Mensah called. The mining company sold our debt. Meridian Basin Holdings."

Tomas straightened slowly. He wiped his hands on a rag that had been black so long it was more grease than cloth. "Kessler."

"Kessler."

"So he doesn't take no for an answer. He just buys the answer." Tomas spat, a habit he performed less from contempt than from punctuation. "How much?"

"Two hundred fourteen thousand. Plus the retention we were holding for warranty work."

"That's payroll for four months. That's fuel. That's the new winch cable, the compressor overhaul—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "That's everything."

"It's everything," Yara agreed.

They stood together in the yard silence. Around them the evidence of their competence lay neat and orderly: rails stacked in graded lengths, ballast tamped to line and level, the whole eleven-kilometer stretch of resurrected track gleaming faintly in the morning like something worth being proud of. It occurred to Yara, not for the first time, that this was the only inheritance she had ever managed to build — a habit of finishing what she started, even when finishing paid nothing.

"Tomas," she said. "I need you to say what you're thinking."

"What I'm thinking is that Dario Kessler has been planning this since before he landed his helicopter in our camp." He folded the rag with unnecessary precision. "You don't buy a debt portfolio overnight. He filed the paperwork while he was drinking our coffee. Maybe before. This wasn't pressure, Yara. This was a siege laid out in advance, and we've been inside the walls the whole time without noticing."

* * *

The first defection happened on the third day, and it was Rupa.

That was the part that hurt. Not because Rupa was the most senior — he wasn't; that was Tomas — but because Rupa was the one who laughed. Twelve years on the crews, through washouts and wage freezes and one memorable season when they'd eaten sago and tinned fish for six weeks straight, and Rupa had never once stopped finding the joke in it. When the pump failed at Kilometer Seven, Rupa had named the pump. When Bea left after the funeral, Rupa had been the only one who said her name out loud in front of Yara, gently, once, and then never again.

Now he stood at the edge of camp with a duffel bag over his shoulder and wouldn't meet her eyes.

"They're paying a signing bonus," he said. "Three months' advance. Yara, I've got a daughter in boarding school. Term fees are due."

"I know."

"The helicopter comes through tomorrow. There's room if anyone else—"

"I know, Rupa."

He shifted his weight. The duffel strap creaked. "It's not about you," he said, and then, wretchedly: "That's a lie. It's a little about you. But mostly it's about my daughter."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. She thought about telling him to stay, about invoking whatever unspoken contract held a crew together past the point where money explained anything. But she had watched men stay out of loyalty before, and she had watched what staying cost them, and she had promised herself somewhere in the last twelve years that she would never again be the reason someone drowned.

"Term fees," she said. "Get them paid. And Rupa — if it goes bad up there, at the gate, you get word to me. However you can."

His face did something complicated. "It won't go bad. It's a tunnel job. Tunnels are just mines that got moody."

"Sure," Yara said. "Moody."

He clasped her forearm, hard, the way rail men did, and walked down to the landing beach without looking back. She watched the helicopter take him the next afternoon, its rotor wash flattening the grass in a circle, and she counted — deliberately, like taking inventory — how many of her crew stood watching it go with expressions that were not entirely grief.

Six. Six faces wearing the arithmetic of their own departures.

* * *

By the end of the week, Meridian Basin Holdings had made itself known in other ways.

The fuel supplier in Kiunga suddenly required cash on delivery. The parts house that had extended Yara credit for nine years — through floods, through the bad season, through the year the whole basin economy hiccupped — sent a polite letter explaining that their lending arrangements had been "restructured." A man she'd never seen before appeared at the trading post where the crew bought rice and cooking oil, asked friendly questions about Vasquez Salvage, and paid for everyone's beer while he did it.

"They're not trying to starve us out," Tomas observed, studying the letter from the parts house by lamplight. "Starving takes too long. They're making it *easy* to leave. Every door out of here opens smoothly except the one that stays shut, and that's the one leading forward."

"Kessler always did think in logistics," Yara said.

"You know him well, then."

The question sat between them on the table like a third cup neither had poured. Tomas rarely asked about the years before the crew. He had been there for some of them — not the deepest ones, but enough — and he had learned that Yara's past was a tunnel with collapsed sections: you could walk certain galleries, but others you went around, and you did not ask why the supports looked scorched.

"Well enough," she said. "He was on the survey circuit when my father was. Different outfit. We crossed paths at conferences, tender meetings. He used to lose bids to my father and smile about it, and everyone thought the smile meant he was gracious." She turned the lamp wick down. "The smile meant he was keeping receipts."

"And now he's collecting."

"Now he's collecting."

Outside, the river ran dark and constant. Yara listened to it and thought about water tables, though she couldn't have said why. Some instinct older than reasoning told her that everything Kessler was doing — the debt purchase, the soft doors, the patient acquisition of her people — was upstream of something. He was holding water back somewhere, building pressure, and the thing he wanted was at the bottom of a flooded hole her father had died to close.

* * *

The second defection was Marris and Deng together, and they didn't even pretend it was about the bonus.

"We're not traitors," Marris said, standing in Yara's tent with her helmet under her arm like a woman at a funeral deciding whether to view the body. "We just did the math. If the debt's called in, the company's done. If the company's done, we're all out of work anyway. At least with Kessler there's wages."

"There's wages," Yara agreed. "And there's the lower gallery."

Marris blinked. "What about it?"

"Nothing." Yara made herself smile. "Sign whatever they put in front of you. Read it first. Especially the indemnity clauses."

Deng lingered after Marris left. He was the youngest of them, twenty-two, hired two seasons ago off a dredge barge, all elbows and appetite. He stood turning his cap in his hands.

"Boss," he said. "Is it true what Rupa says? About the gate? That your old man sealed it himself?"

The question landed in the quiet of the tent like a dropped tool. Yara heard herself say, evenly: "Rupa talks too much."

"Yeah," Deng said. "But he doesn't make things up." He put his cap on. "Whatever's down there — whatever it is you all go quiet about — Kessler's crew is betting it's worth dying for. I'd rather bet the other way. Just so you know somebody did the math on that side too."

Then he was gone, and Yara sat alone with the lamp and the ledger of her own losses, and did her own math.

Fourteen gone in ten days. Thirty-one remained. Payroll covered through the end of the month, if she spent the emergency reserve, which meant no reserve, which meant the next breakdown — and there was always a next breakdown — would be the one that ended them. The Wabag spur contract was dead; its buyer owned her invoice and had no intention of paying it. No other contracts were coming, because word traveled fast in the basin, and the word now was that Vasquez Salvage was a bad bet, a sinking barge, a name attached to debts.

And underneath all of it, patient as bedrock, the fact she had been refusing to look at directly: Dario Kessler did not actually need her to say yes. He needed her to say yes *voluntarily*, because a willing expedition chief with a father's knowledge of the Rivergate was worth ten times a conscripted one. But if she wouldn't volunteer, he would simply wait until the crew dissolved, hire whoever remained piece by piece, and reopen the gate without her — with Rupa running her pumps and Marris running her rails and strangers blasting through the door her father had built with the last full day of his life.

Refusal, she understood now, had never been an option. Refusal was just surrender with better posture.

The trap hadn't been closing. It had already closed. Everything since the helicopter landing was just the sound of the jaws settling.

* * *

On the eleventh day, Bea arrived.

Not Bea herself — a radiogram, relayed through the Kiunga office, in her cousin's characteristic prose: *HEARD YOU'RE HIRING OR DYING. EITHER WAY I'M ON THE THURSDAY BARGE. HAVE EQUIPMENT. DON'T MAKE IT WEIRD.*

Yara read it three times. Tomas read it once over her shoulder.

"Your cousin," he said, "has the diplomatic instincts of a rockslide."

"She's a diver. Buoyancy control is the whole job. Words are optional." Yara set the paper down, and found that her hand wasn't quite steady, and hated that. "Twelve years, Tomas. She hasn't spoken to me since the funeral. Not a Christmas card. Nothing. And now she's coming, right when Kessler needs divers and I need family, and I can't tell if that's a coincidence or if he recruited her too."

"Does it matter?"

Yara looked at him.

"If she's here because Kessler sent her, she's still your blood, and blood is leverage you can't buy clean anywhere else. If she's here because she wants to be —" Tomas shrugged his enormous shrug. "Either way, she's on the Thursday barge, and either way, you'll be glad. That's usually how family works. Terrible logistics. Reliable delivery."

Yara almost smiled. Then the radio behind her hissed, and a voice she recognized with a lurch of pure cold came through the static, smooth and unhurried, a man who had never in his life needed to raise it.

"Vasquez. Dario Kessler. I hear you've had some staffing changes. My sympathies — turnover is the tax on principle." A pause, letting her hear that he was smiling. "I'd like to renew my offer. Same terms, plus one amendment: I clear your company's debts today, every kina, and I carry your remaining crew's contracts across intact. Seniority, rates, everything. Nobody loses a day's pay for following you."

"And in exchange," Yara said, "I hand you the Rivergate."

"In exchange, nobody else has to choose between you and their families. That's the whole offer, Yara. I'm not asking you to betray anything. I'm asking you to stop making the people who trust you pay interest on your grudges." The static breathed. "You have until the water rises. Which, given the season, is a shorter deadline than either of us would like."

The channel clicked dead.

Yara stood in the lamplight with the radio humming and Tomas watching her, and she thought of her father's map rolled in its oilcloth tube beneath her cot, the red line through the Rivergate spur, the single word in his careful surveyor's hand: *sealed.*

He had sealed it with explosives and his life. Kessler meant to open it with her signature and her crew.

And the worst truth, the one she finally let herself hold: she was going to say yes. Not because the trap had closed — traps were for animals that didn't think. She was going to say yes because it was the only way to be inside when the jaws sprang, the only way to stand between Kessler and whatever her father had died believing no one should ever touch.

"Pack the survey kit," she told Tomas. "All of it. And get me Nia Okoro on the radio — the demolitions woman out of Lae. If we're going back to the gate, we're going armed with more than opinions."

Tomas studied her face for a long moment, reading there whatever he read there, the thing twelve years of partnership had taught him to see.

"And the lower gallery?" he asked quietly. "When Kessler's men reach the door your father built — what then?"

Yara looked at the lamp flame, small and stubborn in the draft from the river.

"Then," she said, "we'll find out what kind of seal he really left."
