## 5. Terms of Surrender

The rain started an hour before dawn, a thin greasy drizzle that turned the camp's red clay into something like wet leather, and Yara stood under the tarp of the mess tent watching Kessler's men unload his helicopter with the efficiency of people who had never once been told to hurry. Crates. Cable spools. A generator the size of a coffin. They moved like money moved—smoothly, without apology—and she hated how much her eyes kept cataloguing their technique, because good logistics was still good logistics no matter whose flag it flew.

Kessler found her there at first light. He came alone, which was itself a message: he didn't need muscle for this conversation. He carried two coffees in paper cups from the barge galley and set one on the folding table between them without asking if she wanted it. The cup said *Kessler Holdings* on the side in small gold letters. Even his coffee was branded.

"You slept on it," he said.

"I watched your men sleep on it. In my camp."

"Your camp sits on land I now hold a lease to." He sat down across from her, unhurried, folding his long frame into the cheap chair as if it were boardroom furniture. "But let's not start with threats. Threats are for people who've run out of arguments, and you're not there yet. You're the best rail-and-salvage crew in the basin, Yara. Possibly the only one. I want that expertise pointed at the Rivergate before the monsoon closes the window."

"The answer was no yesterday."

"It was no yesterday at yesterday's price." He slid a tablet across the table. On its screen: a contract, already drafted, already formatted, her name sitting in a signature field like bait in a trap. "This morning's number has three more zeros and a completion bonus tied to schedule, not discovery. You get paid to open a tunnel, not to find anything in it. That should suit you."

She looked at the number. She made herself look away from it slowly, contemptuously, the way you'd look away from anything that had gotten too close to something soft.

"And when we hit water?" she said. "Because we'll hit water. That tunnel's been drowning for twelve years."

"Then you pump it. That's what hydrologists are for. I'm told you know where to find a good one."

The drizzle ticked against the tarp. Somewhere behind the mess tent, Tomas was running the morning check on the rerailing job they'd been cheated out of, and she could hear his voice going through the counts—*plate, plate, spike, spike*—the litany that meant the crew was still hers, still whole, still moving. For now.

"Here's what I know about the Rivergate," Kessler said, leaning forward, voice dropping into the register men use when they think honesty is a currency. "My backers want whatever's in that tunnel assessed, recovered, and documented before the wet season makes the site inaccessible for another year. That's it. That's the whole job. No treasure hunt, no ghosts. A salvage assessment with a deadline." He spread his hands. "You're the only person alive who knows that spur's geometry. Your father drew the survey. You signed the closure report. I could hire a dozen engineers who'd take three months to learn what you knew at seventeen."

The word *seventeen* landed exactly where he'd aimed it. She kept her face flat.

"My father's dead," she said.

"Which is why I need you and not him." Kessler smiled without warmth. "Dead men can't be liable."

Yara stood up. Not to leave—to pace, because standing still across a table from Dario Kessler felt like standing still in rising water. She walked the length of the mess tent and back, and he watched her do it with the patience of a man fishing with no bait, just a bare hook, waiting to see what would bite out of hunger.

"If I say yes," she said finally, "it's on my terms. All of them. Non-negotiable, written into the contract, and the first time you break one, I walk and you can explain to your backers why their window closed."

"Name them."

"One. I lead. Not co-lead, not consult. Every operational decision on site is mine. Your men follow my orders or they leave the gorge. If your security chief disagrees with me about where to stand, he stands somewhere else."

Kessler nodded once, easy. "Reasonable."

"Two. I pick the crew. Mine stays intact—Tomas, all of them—and I bring in specialists of my choosing. You pay their rates. You don't interview them, you don't vet them, and you don't poach them afterward, which I know is your habit."

"Habit implies enjoyment," Kessler said mildly. "It's just efficiency. Agreed."

"Three." She stopped pacing. This was the one that mattered, and she made herself say it evenly, the way her father had taught her to read blast charges aloud—steady voice, steady hands, because the ground listens to your breathing. "No one enters the lower gallery. Not your men, not mine, not you. We dewater the upper sections, assess the track and the portal structure, recover whatever cargo sits above the bulkhead line. The lower gallery stays sealed. Permanently. It's structurally compromised—it's why my father sealed it—and anyone who goes past that door dies in there, and then I have a body recovery instead of a salvage job, and neither of us wants that paperwork."

For the first time, Kessler paused. Not long—a half-second, the length of a man deciding whether to show a card or keep it—but she caught it, because she'd spent twelve years learning to read pauses the way other people read faces.

"The lower gallery," he repeated. "That's the flooded section."

"That's the section my father collapsed with eleven hundred kilos of emulsion, yes."

"And you want it left alone."

"I want it *sealed*. There's a difference, and the difference is that sealed means nobody tests whether the seal held."

He studied her for a moment, and she watched him decide something. Then he picked up the tablet, thumbed through the contract with two fingers, and spoke aloud as he typed, dictating the clause into the document like a man writing scripture: *"Clause fourteen. Lower gallery access prohibited. Breach of clause voids contract and triggers full payment of remaining term."* He looked up. "There. Sealed, in triplicate, legally speaking. Happy?"

"No," Yara said honestly. "But I'm hired."

They shook hands. His grip was dry and precise, calibrated to end exactly one beat before it became uncomfortable. Over his shoulder, through the tarp's open flap, she saw the helicopter lifting off for the ridge survey, and beyond it the grey wall of cloud building over the western ranges—the monsoon, still days out but no longer theoretical, stacking itself against the sky like freight waiting for a clear line.

Kessler gathered his tablet and stood. At the flap of the tent he paused, half-turned, and said, conversationally, almost kindly:

"For what it's worth—I'd have agreed to four terms. You only asked for three."

Then he was gone into the rain, and Yara stood alone in the mess tent with his untouched coffee cooling beside her name, and tried to work out why the easiest negotiation of her career felt exactly like the click of a detonator finding its circuit.

* * *

Dario Kessler walked back down the camp's central path with the drizzle beading on his shoulders, not hurrying, letting the mud take his boot prints because boot prints were a kind of record and he liked leaving records. Behind him, in the mess tent, Yara Vasquez was doing what he'd predicted she would do: rereading clause fourteen, looking for the teeth in it, finding none, and distrusting it anyway. Good. Distrust kept people sharp, and sharp people signed contracts faster than comfortable ones.

His assistant Meret fell into step beside him under an umbrella, tablet already open. "The lease filings went through at 0400. The mining company's debt instruments transferred at 0430. She doesn't know yet."

"She will by noon, when her payroll clears—or doesn't." Kessler stepped over a cable run, noting idly that her crew had anchored it properly, off the walking line, tagged at both ends. Competent. That was the tragedy of Yara Vasquez: genuinely competent, and competent people always believed competence was a form of safety. "And the specialists?"

"Okonkwo confirmed last night. The explosives woman—Nia Okoye—is negotiating. And there's a diver in Port Moresby with Rivergate experience. Beatriz Vasquez. Family, I believe."

"Cousin. They haven't spoken since the funeral." He said it without looking up, and enjoyed the small silence that followed, the sound of Meret recalibrating her estimate of how thoroughly he'd done his homework. "Book her. Offer double rate. People who aren't speaking to their families will cross any water for double rate—it's cheaper than therapy."

"Noted." Meret typed. Then, carefully: "The lower gallery clause. Do we honor it?"

Kessler stopped walking. Ahead of them, the camp fell away toward the river, and beyond the river the gorge walls climbed into cloud, and somewhere inside those walls, behind eleven hundred kilos of a dead man's demolition work, sat the thing his backers had sent him eight thousand kilometers to retrieve. Twelve years of silence around the Rivergate. Twelve years of the Vasquez name functioning as a lock. And the daughter of the man who built the lock had just handed him the key by insisting the door stay shut—because nothing on earth marked a target more clearly than a prohibition.

"Do you know what she did in there just now?" he said. "When I said 'lower gallery,' watch her again next time. She flinched. Half a centimeter, shoulders first, then the jaw. She's been flinching at that word since she was seventeen years old, which means the lower gallery isn't structural to her. It's personal. And people guard what's personal far less intelligently than they guard what's valuable, because grief makes terrible engineers."

"So that's a no on honoring it?"

"That's a *yes* on honoring it—" he resumed walking, boots printing the mud, "—right up until the upper gallery gives us what we need to justify going further. Contracts are weather, Meret. They change when the pressure changes. Clause fourteen buys her cooperation today, and today is the only day that exists. By the time we're inside, she'll have a crew to protect and a monsoon over her head, and a woman like Yara Vasquez will trade any principle on earth for her people's skins. Her father did."

Meret glanced at him. "You knew him."

"I audited him." Kessler reached the edge of camp, where the helicopter squatted on its pad with rotors drooping, and turned once to look back at the mess tent—at the small silhouette in the doorway, watching him with the fixed attention of something deciding whether it was prey. He raised a hand, courteous, and saw her not return it.

"Her father found something down there he wasn't supposed to keep," he said. "The report says accident. The report was signed by a seventeen-year-old girl who believed what adults told her, which is the most reliable ink in the world." He ducked under the rotor. "Get me the manifest archives from the old railway consortium. Everything pre-closure. And Meret—if anyone asks what we're recovering from the Rivergate, the answer is rail infrastructure. Rolling stock. Corporate assets."

"And if it's not?"

Kessler settled into his seat and pulled the harness across his chest, and through the perspex he watched the rain thicken over the gorge, the river below beginning to swell with the first brown tongue of the monsoon, rising toward a concrete face in the rock that had been holding its breath for twelve years.

"Then it's still corporate assets," he said, "because I'll own them by Friday."

The rotors bit the air, and the camp dropped away beneath him—tarp tents, dead rails, and one woman standing very still in the rain, already breaking her own rule, taking a contract twice.
