## 6. The Crew Assembles

Yara found Nia Okafor where she always found Nia Okafor: elbow-deep in the guts of something that used to be a machine, in a workshop that smelled of solvent and burnt copper, on the wrong side of the rail yards in Daru where the salvage brokers didn't bother to come.

Nia didn't look up when Yara ducked under the hanging chain curtain. She never did. She finished whatever she was doing—something with a detonator housing and a jeweler's screwdriver—and only then wiped her hands on a rag that had once been white and turned to face her.

"Vasquez." No warmth, no hostility. Just the name, like a gauge reading.

"Okafor."

"You're standing in my light."

Yara stepped aside. "I need a blaster."

"You always need a blaster. Last time it was a culvert on the Strickland spur, and your spec sheet said 'light demolition' and it turned out to be a bridge abutment with rebar like a whale's ribcage." Nia set the screwdriver down with exaggerated care. "I billed you for heavy. You paid late."

"I paid."

"I said late, not never." Nia leaned back against the workbench and crossed her arms. She was a small woman, compact as a charge itself, with a burn scar running from her left wrist to her elbow that she'd never explained and Yara had never asked about. "So. Who's paying this time? Because if it's the mining outfit again—"

"It's not."

"Then who?"

Yara had rehearsed this in the barge cabin for two nights, and the rehearsal had produced exactly one usable sentence, which was: "Kessler."

The word landed in the workshop the way a dropped wrench lands in a fuel drum. Nia went very still. Outside, a crane groaned through its arc, and somewhere a radio played a station that was mostly static and occasionally, miraculously, music.

"Kessler," Nia repeated. "Dario Kessler. The man whose helicopters cost more than my workshop. That Kessler."

"He's reopening the Rivergate spur. Before monsoon."

Nia's eyes did the thing they always did when she was calculating—not narrowing, exactly, but focusing, the way a lens focuses. "The Rivergate." She said it slowly, tasting the shape of the name. "The line your father sealed."

"You've heard of it."

"Everyone's heard of it. It's a ghost story with a survey number." Nia pushed off the workbench and walked to the window, where the rail yards spread out in their gray tangle of dead track and rusting rolling stock. "Twelve years ago a survey expedition went in and came out short one chief engineer, and the government sealed the whole complex with a demolition charge so clean that half the blasting community considers it a masterpiece. You're telling me Kessler wants to un-seal it. In monsoon season."

"I'm telling you he's paying for it. Triple rates. Hazard multiplier."

"Money." Nia turned around. "You've never taken a job for money alone, Yara. You take jobs to keep moving. That's your whole religion. So either Kessler is paying you enough to buy the religion, or he's got something on you, and I don't like either option."

Yara held her gaze. "I need someone who can read a demolition pattern from the outside. Someone who can tell me who set a charge and why, just by the fracture lines. And I need someone who won't ask me questions I can't answer yet."

"That's three someones."

"I'm only hiring one."

Nia was quiet for a long moment. Then she laughed—short, dry, without much humor in it—and went to the corner of the workshop where a steel footlocker sat under a tarp. She pulled the tarp off and opened the locker, and Yara saw the foam-cut shapes inside: detonators, shaped charges, spools of det cord, all of it clean and oiled and ready, as though Nia had been waiting years for someone to ask.

"I'll come," Nia said. "But I want it in the contract. In writing. All blasting decisions are mine. If I say a gallery is too unstable to charge, we don't charge it. If I say we walk out, we walk out. Not Kessler. Not you. Me."

"Done."

"And Yara?" Nia closed the footlocker. "Whatever's in that tunnel that you're not telling me about—I'll find out. I always find out. It's better for both of us if you tell me first."

Yara looked at the footlocker, at the neat foam cradles, at the woman who had once, in another life, taught her the difference between a charge that breaks rock and a charge that breaks people.

"I know," she said.

* * *

Okonkwo kept an office above a fish market in Kiunga, which was either a joke or a statement, depending on how much hydrology you knew. Yara climbed the outside stairs through the smell of brine and diesel and found him at a desk buried under contour maps, a ceiling fan turning with the weary patience of a machine that had outlived its warranty.

He was older than she remembered—everyone was—but the same, too: a big man gone soft at the edges, with reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair and ink stains on his fingers that no amount of washing ever fully removed. He looked up, recognized her in about a second and a half, and did not smile.

"Yara Vasquez." He took the glasses down. "The last time I saw you, you were seventeen years old and lying to a government investigator."

"Hello to you too."

"It was a very good lie, for a seventeen-year-old. Structurally sound. Emotionally consistent. I only knew it was a lie because I'd seen the water." He gestured at the chair across from him. "Sit. You want the Rivergate."

It wasn't a question, and Yara didn't pretend it was. She sat. "Kessler's reopening it. I'm leading the crew."

"Mm." Okonkwo pulled a contour map off the pile—she recognized the gorge immediately, the tight hairpin bends of the upper Fly, the concrete rectangle of the portal drawn in someone's careful hand—and laid it flat. "Then you'll want to know what I know, which is that the water in that complex has never behaved like water in a tunnel."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning that when your father flooded it—assuming he flooded it, which is what everyone assumes—the levels never equalized. A flooded gallery connected to a living river reaches equilibrium. It finds its level. This one didn't. Twelve years of survey echoes and sonar work by people with more curiosity than sense, and the water in the lower sections sits four meters above where it should, like it's being held. Like something upstream is standing on the plug." He tapped the map. "Tunnels don't hold grudges, Yara. But water tables remember."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a 'keep listening.'" Okonkwo leaned back, and the fan turned, and somewhere below them a fishmonger shouted a price. "Kessler came to me six weeks ago. Offered me the same job. I said no."

Yara felt the floor tilt a degree. "Six weeks."

"He wanted a dewatering schedule that would let him put men in the upper gallery by the end of the month. I told him the hydrology didn't support it. He told me he hadn't asked for hydrology, he'd asked for a schedule. So I declined, and he found someone less stubborn, and that man's model is wrong, and when the monsoon comes early—and it will come early, the pressure systems are stacking wrong over the gulf—his schedule is going to kill someone." Okonkwo took off his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose. "So I'm going to say yes to you, and here's why. Not the money. Because if I don't go, the person reading that water table will be someone who tells Kessler what he wants to hear, and I'd rather it be me telling him what's true, loudly, in front of witnesses."

"That's the most pessimistic yes I've ever received."

"I'm a hydrologist. Pessimism is a professional credential." He began stacking his maps with quick, practiced motions. "My terms. I report the water as I find it, and my reports go to the whole crew, not just to you, not just to Kessler. And if I call an evacuation, it happens. No debate. You saw the Strickland flood in '09?"

"From the ridge."

"Then you know what water does when it decides. I don't argue with it and I don't let anyone else argue with me about it."

Yara thought of her father's rule—never take a contract twice, never stay a season—and wondered what he would make of a crew assembled from people who each carried a veto. Probably he'd have approved. He'd built his crews the same way: not with loyalty, but with competence, and the kind of stubbornness that keeps people alive.

"Done," she said.

Okonkwo paused, maps in hand, and looked at her over them with an expression she couldn't read. "One more thing. The lower gallery. Kessler's contract says the crew works upper sections only?"

"It does."

"Your father's charge was set from inside the lower gallery. Whatever's holding that water is down there too." He slung his kit bag over his shoulder. "You can fence off the lower gallery on paper, Yara. Water doesn't read."

* * *

Bea was diving the wreck of the *Wewak Star*, a coastal freighter that had gone down off the delta forty years ago and had been picked clean of anything valuable decades since. She dove it anyway, every few months, alone, which told Yara everything about why she was there and nothing about whether she'd come.

Yara chartered a skiff from a man who asked no questions, and sat at the anchor line for two hours watching the surface for the telltale silver bubble-train of a diver ascending. The sun climbed. The water went from gray to hard green to a blue so deep it looked structural. Twice Yara almost told the skiff man to turn back. Both times she thought of Kessler's contract, of the sump channels marked in red on her father's survey, of the word *sealed* in his handwriting, and she stayed.

Bea surfaced at noon.

Yara saw her before Bea saw the skiff—a dark shape rising through the blue, unhurried, perfectly controlled, and then the mask breaking the surface and her cousin's face appearing above it, saltwater streaming, eyes finding the boat with the flat instant assessment of someone who had learned, somewhere, to check for threats before checking for anything else.

Bea saw her. Yara watched the recognition move through her cousin's face like weather: surprise, then nothing, then a careful blankness that was worse than anger.

Bea swam to the ladder, climbed aboard, and stripped her mask without a word. She was thirty now, lean as a cable, with their grandmother's jaw and a white scar through her eyebrow that Yara didn't know the story of and had no right to ask about. Twelve years of silence sat between them on the deck of the skiff, and it took up more room than the engine.

"You look like hell," Bea said finally.

"You look like you've been diving alone on a dead wreck for fun."

"I have been diving alone on a dead wreck for fun. It's peaceful down there. Nothing talks." Bea wrung out her hair and sat on the gunwale, deliberately out of reach. "Kessler's people came to me three days ago. Helicopter, contract, the whole theater. I told them I'd think about it. I told them that because I wanted to see how long it would take you to come find me yourself."

The words landed harder than Yara expected. "You knew I'd come."

"I hoped. Hoping and knowing are different." Bea looked out at the water. "Twelve years, Yara. You didn't come to the reading of the will. You didn't answer the letters. I sent letters for three years. Did you even get them?"

"I got them."

"And?"

"And I didn't have anything to say that wouldn't have been a lie."

Bea laughed, and there was nothing kind in it. "That's the family disease, isn't it. The Vasquez honesty. Uncle Emilio had it too. Told the truth about everything except the one thing that mattered, and it killed him, and then you—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "You signed the closure report. Seventeen years old, and you signed it, and then you disappeared, and I had to bury him with the whole river basin believing he'd made some stupid surveyor's mistake."

"I know what I signed."

"Do you?" Bea turned and looked at her directly, and Yara saw that under the anger there was something rawer, something that had been waiting twelve years for this conversation and had rehearsed it in the dark. "Because I've read that report a hundred times. It's clean. Too clean. It reads like it was written by a grown man with a government salary, and your name's at the bottom like an afterthought. So either you wrote it, or someone handed it to you and told you to sign, and either way you've had twelve years to tell me which, and you've told me nothing. So don't sit on my boat—"

"Kessler's boat."

"—and tell me you know what you signed. Tell me what's in the tunnel, Yara. Tell me why a man like Kessler wants it open. Tell me why you're really taking this job, because I've watched you work for twelve years from a distance, and you never once took a contract that led back to that gorge. Not once. You routed around it like it was a minefield. And now you're sailing straight into it, and I'm supposed to believe it's about money?"

The skiff rocked. Somewhere below them, the *Wewak Star* sat in her green silence, picked clean, holding nothing but the shape of what she'd been.

"I can't tell you yet," Yara said. "Not because I don't want to. Because I don't know all of it yet, and if I tell you the part I know and it's wrong, I'll have spent the one truth I have."

Bea stared at her for a long time. Then she did something Yara hadn't seen her do since they were children: she exhaled, all at once, like a diver venting, and some of the rigidness went out of her shoulders.

"Triple rates," Bea said. "Hazard multiplier. And my terms are non-negotiable. I dive my own lines. I set my own decompression. Nobody—nobody—overrides me on a dive profile, including you, including Kessler, including God."

"Okonkwo has the same clause."

"Good. He's the only person on that river I trust to read water." Bea reached for her kit bag, and then paused, one hand on the strap, not looking at Yara. "The sump channels in the lower gallery. Your father's survey marks three of them. I've read the survey."

Yara went cold. "That survey isn't public."

"No," Bea agreed. "It isn't." She shouldered the bag and finally, finally looked at her cousin, and her voice, when it came, was quieter. "I've had twelve years to be angry at you, Yara. I've used most of them. But I kept the survey, and I kept diving, and when Kessler's helicopter landed I said I'd think about it because some part of me knew you'd need a diver who already knew the water. So here's the deal. You tell me the truth when you have it—all of it, the day you have it—and I'll get you through whatever's down there. Family's a liability, cousin. Always has been. But it's the only lifeline that doesn't fray."

Yara reached out and Bea let her take her hand—briefly, hard, diver to diver, the grip that says *I have you* and *don't let go* in the same motion.

The skiff man started the engine. Behind them the *Wewak Star* settled back into her silence, and ahead the delta opened toward the river, toward the gorge, toward the concrete face in the rock that Yara had spent twelve years routing around.

They made Kiunga by dark. By the time the barge was loaded, the crew stood at seven: Tomas, Nia, Okonkwo, Bea, and three of Yara's old hands who'd asked no questions and been paid in advance.

It was Tomas, checking the manifest by lamplight, who found the anomaly. He brought the paper to Yara without a word, and she read it twice, and the river sounds of the evening seemed to recede.

Kessler's supply order included, under *specialized equipment*, a line item for a submersible pump rated for four meters of head.

Four meters. Exactly the height that Okonkwo said the water sat above its natural level.

Someone had already measured what was down there.

Yara folded the manifest and put it in her pocket, and told no one, and watched the northern sky where the pressure systems were stacking wrong over the gulf.
