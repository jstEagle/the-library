## 7. Up the Fly

The barge was named *Patience*, which Tomas said was the first lie of the expedition, because no one who owned her had ever waited for anything in their lives.

She was a hundred and forty feet of riveted iron, older than Yara and older than the river's last good decade, with a deck stacked to the rail with pumps, compressors, generators in wooden crates stenciled with Kessler Consolidated's eagle, and enough diesel in drums to run a small war. Kessler's men had loaded her in a single night with the crisp efficiency of people who had never had to count their money. Yara's own gear went aboard last, and she watched every case of it herself, because the last thing she trusted on this river was other people's hands.

They cast off from Wabag Landing at dawn, under a sky the color of wet slate, and the Fly took them the way it took everything—slowly, without malice, and without any intention of giving them back.

Yara stood at the bow rail with her father's survey roll under her arm and the current pulling at the hull below her like a hand testing a knot. Behind her, the expedition organized itself into the shape Kessler had paid for: twelve of his men in matching rain shells, two of his engineers, a surveyor named Pell who smiled too easily, and the six of hers—Tomas at the stern winches, Nia smoking where the wind would carry it away from the fuel drums, Okonkwo already down in the hold with his water instruments, Bea nowhere in particular, which was Bea's way of being everywhere.

The river communities began an hour upstream.

Yara knew them the way you know the rooms of a house you grew up in but haven't entered for years. Stilt villages strung along the oxbows, houses on poles above the brown water, canoes cutting across the current at angles that looked reckless until you understood they were the only angles that worked. Smoke from cooking fires standing straight up in the windless morning. Children on the platforms, shouting at the barge, the way children had shouted at barges on this river since before the railway, since before the mines, since before anyone decided the Fly needed improving.

But this time the shouting stopped.

It happened at the first village—a scatter of houses on the north bank where the channel narrowed and the current leaned. Yara saw the children first, waving, and then she saw a woman come out of the nearest house and say something, and the children stopped waving. Not all at once. In a ripple, like a wind going through cane. By the time the barge drew level, the platforms were lined with people who were not waving and not hiding either. They were watching. The particular watching of people counting something.

"They know the name on the crates," Tomas said quietly, coming to stand beside her. He nodded at the stenciled eagles stacked behind them. "Kessler's been buying land options up here for two years. Quiet ones, through a company out of Port Moresby. The river people aren't stupid. They know what an option is."

"It's not the name on the crates," Yara said.

Tomas looked at her.

"It's the name on the manifest." She tapped the survey roll under her arm without quite meaning to. "Twelve years ago, barges went up this river with my father's company name on the paperwork. Same channel. Same villages. They remember which names came and what was different afterward."

The village fell behind. The watching didn't.

* * *

They made their first stop at midday, at a fuel cache Kessler's advance team had established on a gravel bar below a place the charts called only Bend 44, because the surveyors who named things on the Fly had never bothered learning what the people along it called them. The crew took on diesel, and Yara took the opportunity to walk the bar and put distance between herself and the sound of Kessler's men laughing over their rations.

The elder was waiting for her at the edge of the trees.

She didn't see him arrive. That was the thing about the river people that outsiders never understood and that Yara, who had grown up on this water, still found herself forgetting—you could watch a bank for an hour and see nothing, and then turn around and someone was standing there, as if the forest had simply decided to hand you a person. He was old the way the railway bridges were old, upright but weathered, in a shirt gone soft with washing and a string bag slung over one shoulder. His eyes went to the barge, to the crates, to Yara, in that order, and stayed on her.

"You are Vasquez's daughter," he said.

Not a question. Yara's hand tightened on the survey roll.

"I'm Yara Vasquez."

"Yes." He said it as if confirming something he'd already paid for. "I am Anis. I knew your father. He stopped at our village, the last time he came up. He ate at my fire." Something moved in his face that was not quite grief and not quite anger, and Yara recognized it because she'd seen it in mirrors for twelve years: the look of a person carrying a debt that had no one left to be paid to. "He was a man who looked at the river like it was a person. Not like the others. The others looked at it like a road."

"He was," Yara said, and her voice came out flatter than she wanted. "I'm sorry, but we're on a schedule. The monsoon—"

"I know about the monsoon." Anis said it without heat, the way you'd tell a child the sun would set. "Everyone on this river knows about the monsoon. We have known about this one since the birds moved in March." He reached into the string bag. "I have something for you."

He held it out on his open palm.

It was a carving, no bigger than her hand, of a dense black river hardwood she couldn't name—hard enough to ring when tapped, worn smooth at the edges from long handling. It was a serpent, or a channel, or both: a coiled form with an open mouth at one end, and through the coil ran a hole, bored clean, so the whole thing could be threaded on a cord. Someone had carved it a long time ago. Someone had carried it a long time since.

"I can't take this," Yara said. "It's—this isn't mine."

"It was never ours either." Anis pressed it into her hand and closed her fingers over it with surprising strength, his skin dry and warm as bark. "It came to us to hold. That is different from owning. Your father gave it to us for keeping, and he said—" and here the old man's voice changed, dropped into the cadence of something recited many times, to many people, in the hope that one of them would finally listen, "—*when my daughter comes up this river, give her this, and tell her: I promised the river would be returned.*"

The gravel bar seemed to tilt.

Yara heard the generators behind her, and Kessler's men laughing, and the current working at the hull of the *Patience*, and underneath all of it her own pulse, once, hard, in her ears. *Returned.* Not saved. Not drained. Not opened, not salvaged, not recovered—every word Kessler had used in the helicopter, in the contract, in the long persuasive monologue of money. *Returned.* A word that meant the river had been taken from.

"What else did he tell you?" she asked. "About the gate. About what he found."

Anis looked at her for a long moment, and in that look Yara understood that the answer was no, and that the no was not cruelty but measurement—the old man deciding how much weight this particular daughter could carry, today, on a gravel bar, with the eagle crates watching.

"He told us the river keeps what it keeps," Anis said. "And he told us that the men who paid for the railway were not the men who built it. And he told us that one day the water would do what the law would not." He stepped back, gathering the string bag. "The water is patient. We are patient. You should ask yourself, girl, whose patience runs out first."

He turned and went into the trees, and the forest closed behind him the way water closes over a stone, and Yara stood on the bar with a carved serpent in her fist and the taste of her father's voice in her mouth from twelve years and a grave away.

*Flood it. Tell no one.*

She hadn't found that message yet. She wouldn't find it for weeks. But standing there, she felt it waiting for her downstream like a lamp lit in a dark house, and her skin crawled with the certainty that everyone on this river knew exactly where the house was.

* * *

They tied up that night at a mission landing gone to ruin—corrugated chapel, collapsed dormitory, a bell on a frame with no rope—and Yara posted watches and then couldn't sleep and walked the deck instead.

The river at night was a different country. The water made sounds it didn't make in daylight: long sucking exhalations against the pilings, the knock of drifting logs finding each other in the dark, and somewhere out in the channel the low boom of a fish or a spirit or a hippo's ghost, depending on which stories you'd been raised on. Yara had been raised on all of them. Her father had never once told her which were true, on the theory, he said, that a river you couldn't frighten was a river you'd stop respecting.

She found Bea at the stern, sitting on a coil of hose with her dive mask pushed up on her forehead like a second pair of eyes, looking at the black water.

"Can't sleep either?" Yara said.

"I sleep fine." Bea didn't look around. "I just don't sleep near you."

Yara took it. Twelve years of taking it had built the muscle. She leaned on the rail a few feet away, not closer, and let the silence do what silence did on the Fly, which was fill up with everything neither of them would say.

"The old man today," Bea said finally. "Anis."

"What about him."

"He didn't come to the bar to give you a carving." Bea turned the mask in her hands. "I checked the bank after he left. There was a canoe pulled up in the reeds a hundred meters downstream, and someone had cut a fresh paddle rest into a sapling. That's not a man who wandered out to meet you. That's a man who was sent, and who planned his exit." She looked up at Yara then, and in the dark her eyes were the flat gray of the water. "You knew him. Or you knew of him. I watched your face. You weren't surprised."

"I was surprised."

"You were *hit*." Bea stood, coiling the mask strap around her fingers. "There's a difference, and I've watched you take hits for twelve years, so I know what it looks like. Whatever your father promised these people, whatever he gave them to hold—you already knew some of it. You knew enough to be afraid of the rest." She stepped past Yara, then stopped, close enough that Yara could smell the river on her. "We're going into a hole in the ground that our family sealed, working for the man whose money is all over those crates, and the people on this bank are sending you messages in hardwood. When you're ready to tell me which part of that scares you most, cousin, I'll be the one holding the line when you dive."

She walked forward along the deck and was gone into the dark between the crates, and Yara stood at the stern rail with the serpent carving in her pocket, its bored hole pressing against her hip like a small cold eye.

* * *

The second day, the villages stopped being quiet and started being absent.

It was subtle at first. A platform where there should have been canoes. A fish trap pulled up and drying, its owner gone. Gardens on the banks with no one working them. By afternoon, Okonkwo came up from the hold with his water charts under his arm and a look on his face that Yara had learned, over three contracts together, to read like weather.

"We have a problem with the river," he said.

"We have several. Pick one."

"The villages." He spread his chart on the deck plate and weighted the corners with washers. "I've been plotting our position against the settlement markers from the old survey sheets. There should be eleven inhabited villages between Bend 44 and the gorge. We've passed seven sites. One had people. Six were empty." He tapped the paper. "River people don't abandon fishing water in the dry season. They move for sickness, for feud, or because someone told them to move."

"Kessler's advance team."

"Would be my first answer. But." Okonkwo's finger moved to a notation in his own handwriting, small and precise. "This morning, at the empty site above the second bend, I went ashore to check a spring line. Someone had been there. Recently. They'd built a signal frame—three sticks, lashed, pointing upstream. And tied to it—" he reached into his shirt pocket and set a small object on the chart, "—this."

A strip of hardwood bark, curled, with a single notch cut into it.

"Navigational," Nia said, coming up the ladder with her hands black with grease. "River people use them for current warnings. Notch count tells you what's ahead." She looked at the bark, then at Yara, and her voice went careful. "One notch. That's not a warning, though. One notch means *message*. It means: someone upstream wants to talk, and knows you're coming."

The deck was quiet except for the engines, turning over below them like something patient.

"So the villages didn't flee the expedition," Tomas said slowly. "They *concentrated*. Everyone's moved upstream, ahead of us, toward the gorge." He looked at Yara. "Toward the gate."

"Or toward each other," Bea said from the rail. "You don't gather a river's worth of people in one place for a message. You gather them for a decision."

Yara picked up the bark strip. It was light as a moth wing and cut clean, one notch, deliberate. Her father's handwriting had looked like that—one stroke where other men used five. She put it in her breast pocket next to nothing, because the carving was in her trouser pocket and she did not want the two of them touching, which was irrational, and which she did anyway.

"Pell," she called across the deck, and Kessler's smiling surveyor looked up from his instruments. "When we make the gorge tomorrow—whose ground do we tie up on first? Ours or theirs?"

Pell's smile didn't move. "Mr. Kessler's instructions are to go straight to the portal. He was very clear. Time is the enemy, Ms. Vasquez. The monsoon—"

"I know what the enemy is." She rolled Okonkwo's chart closed. "But we make one stop. The signal frame said someone wants to talk, and on this river, you talk before you dig. My father's rule. It kept every crew he ran alive, and it's the only inheritance I'm spending on this job."

Pell wrote something in a notebook. Yara watched him do it and let him, because there was no point pretending the notebook didn't exist, and because she wanted Kessler to read, in whatever summary reached him tonight, that his salvage chief was obeying a dead man's rules on a living man's river.

That night she stood her own watch at the bow, past midnight, past the turn of the moon. The channel ahead was invisible, but she could feel the country changing through the soles of her boots—the water quickening, the banks closing in, the first far-off sound of the gorge where the river went from wide and slow to narrow and furious, the sound her father had described in the survey roll under her arm in a line she'd read a hundred times and never once understood until now:

*The Fly does not end at the gate. It waits there.*

And on the black water ahead, so far upstream it might have been a star fallen into the current and refusing to drown, a single light was burning where no village should be, and it did not move, and it did not go out, and it was waiting for them too.
