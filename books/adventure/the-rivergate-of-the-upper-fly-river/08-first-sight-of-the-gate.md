## 8. First Sight of the Gate

The gorge announced itself before they saw it.

For two days the barge had crawled up the narrowing river, past banks that folded closer and closer like a throat, and then the water changed—went from brown to a hard, glassy green, and the sound of the current deepened into something that came up through the deck plates and into the soles of Yara's boots. Okonkwo stood at the rail with his instruments and said nothing for a long time, which from Okonkwo was a speech.

"Velocity's doubled in the last kilometer," he said finally. "The gorge is throttling the river. Whatever your father was holding back, it's still holding."

"He wasn't holding back water," Yara said. "He was holding back everything that comes after water."

She said it flatly, the way you say things you've said to yourself a thousand times, and she watched Okonkwo decide not to ask. He was good at that. It was one of the reasons she'd hired him, back when hiring him had been a choice and not a surrender.

They came around the last bend at midmorning, and the Rivergate was there.

Yara had prepared herself for it the way you prepare for a punch: by pretending you wouldn't feel it. She had seen photographs. She had a survey map in her cabin with the whole complex drawn in her father's precise, patient hand, every gallery and adit and drainage culvert rendered in ink so fine it looked like it had been applied with a needle. She had stood on the ridge above this gorge at seventeen, coughing on concrete dust, watching the river go white with debris.

None of it helped.

The portal was a concrete face poured directly into the gorge wall, forty meters wide and thirty high, and the river had taken the lower half of it. Twelve years of flood seasons had gnawed at the base until the structure seemed to grow out of the water itself, a gray cliff with a black slot at its center where the tunnel mouth had been sealed. Above the waterline the concrete was stained in long vertical tears, rust bleeding down from rebar, algae painting it in bands of green and black like the growth rings of something very old. The gorge walls rose on either side, sheer and dripping, and the portal sat between them with the terrible composure of a thing built to outlast the people who made it.

"Madre de Dios," Bea said quietly at the rail beside her.

Yara didn't look at her cousin. They had managed eleven days of civil silence, communicating through manifests and depth soundings, and Yara wasn't going to break it over a concrete wall, no matter what the wall did to her chest.

"Mooring's on the left bank," she called forward to Tomas. "Fifty meters downstream of the portal. Nobody ties off upstream. I want the barge below the outfall, not above it."

"You think it's still discharging?" Okonkwo asked.

"I think it's been discharging for twelve years," Yara said. "Slowly. Through cracks nobody's mapped. Which is why we're going to map them before a single pump gets set up."

The barge swung wide, and Yara made herself keep watching the portal as they passed it, because looking away felt like a kind of lying. The morning light came down the gorge at a steep angle and lit the upper face of the structure, and that was where she saw it. That was where she stopped being able to breathe evenly.

The blast damage.

Twelve years of weathering had softened it, but the pattern was still there, written across the concrete in the language her father had taught her: a line of boreholes drilled in a staggered arc across the tunnel mouth, the charges sequenced to fold the rock inward on itself, a controlled collapse rather than a shattering. He hadn't blown the portal open or apart. He had made the mountain eat it. The crater above the sealed mouth was a smooth concavity, like a bite taken by something patient, and the fracture lines radiating from it followed the natural joints in the rock because he had read those joints first, spent days reading them, and then asked the stone to close along its own seams.

She knew the pattern the way she knew her own handwriting. She had learned it at his knee, on contract demolitions all through her teens, hauling drill steel while he explained why you never fought the rock, why you asked it, why the difference between a demolition and an earthquake was the difference between a sentence and a scream.

*He did this,* she thought. *My father did this. And I signed the paper that said he did it wrong.*

"Yara." Bea's voice, low. "You're bleeding."

Yara looked down. Her hands were on the rail and her knuckles were white and her nails had cut four half-moons into her palms. She hadn't noticed. She opened her hands, flexed them, watched the blood bead.

"Rigging," she said. "Get your dive gear staged. I want you in the water by fourteen hundred, before the light goes."

* * *

The left bank mooring took an hour, the current fighting them the whole way, and by the time the barge was secured to deadmen anchors Tomas had hammered into the gravel, the crew had gone quiet in the particular way crews go quiet when a job stops being a job and starts being a place.

Yara had seen it before. Every salvage crew she'd ever run had a threshold, a moment when a contract stopped being numbers on a manifest and became a location with weather and weight and history, and the men and women on the deck would stop joking and start watching. It happened at shipwrecks. It happened at collapsed mines. It was happening here, and the portal hadn't even opened its mouth to them yet.

Nia felt it fastest. She stood at the rail with her kit bag over her shoulder and looked at the blast scar on the portal face for a long moment, and then she looked at Yara, and Yara saw the question arrive behind her eyes—the question every blaster asks when they look at someone else's work.

"Whoever closed this," Nia said, "knew what they were doing."

"Yes."

"Better than good. That's a master's pattern. The sequencing on that crown—" She gestured at the smooth concavity. "You don't learn that from a manual. You learn it from somebody."

"Drop it," Yara said.

Nia held her gaze for a second longer than a subordinate should, then shouldered her bag and went down the gangway. Yara stood on the deck and watched her go and understood that she had just told Nia everything, because that was how it worked: the things you refused to say were the loudest.

The camp went up on the gravel bench fifty meters downstream, tents and the pump skids and the generator, all of it placed with the river at their backs and the portal in their sightline because Yara wanted it in their sightline. She wanted her crew looking at that concrete face every time they lifted their heads. She wanted them to feel what she felt, which was not awe exactly, and not dread exactly, but the specific pressure of being watched by something her father had built.

By midafternoon the survey line was running. Okonkwo had his gauges in the water, reading the outfall flow through the portal's weep cracks, and his face got longer with every reading.

"You were right," he said, crouching beside Yara at the waterline. "It's discharging. Maybe two hundred liters a second through the fracture network. That's not seepage, Yara. That's a managed leak."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning the water inside is under pressure, and the pressure is being released at a controlled rate. Either the tunnel complex was designed to weep—" He shook his head. "No. It wasn't. The geology's wrong. This rock wants to flood. Something is metering the outflow."

Yara looked up at the portal, at the black slot above the waterline, and thought about her father's map, the fine needle-line ink, the single word in red across the lower gallery.

*Sealed.*

Not *collapsed.* Not *flooded.* *Sealed.* A word you used for a thing that could be unsealed.

"Tomas," she called. "Get the survey crew on the face. I want every crack, every weep point, every fracture mapped and photographed before dark. Nobody goes within ten meters of the tunnel mouth. Nobody."

"You got it, jefa."

* * *

She climbed to the face herself in the last hour of light, alone, which was against her own standing orders and which she did anyway, because there are things a leader has to do without witnesses.

The rock shelf above the waterline was slick with spray and twelve years of moss, and she went up it slowly, hands finding the old drill scars, and at the top she stood on the ledge her father's crew had stood on, and she put her palm flat against the concrete.

It was cold. That was the thing nobody warned you about, with concrete. It held the cold of the water and the cold of the mountain and the cold of the years, and it gave none of it back.

She traced the borehole scars with her fingers. Forty-one holes, she counted without meaning to, forty-one, staggered in the arc pattern, and the spacing was his—slightly wider than regulation, because he always trusted the rock a little more than the manual did, and he was always right, and the one time it mattered most he had been right again, and the collapse had held for twelve years and would have held for a hundred.

He hadn't died because his work failed. She had known that for twelve years and had never once said it aloud, because saying it meant saying what actually happened, and what actually happened lived in a report with her signature at the bottom.

Below her, the camp lights were coming on, small yellow points against the gathering dark of the gorge. Bea's dive light strobed under the water near the portal's base, a green ghost pulsing in the black slot's reflection. Somewhere upstream, thunder muttered—the monsoon clearing its throat, still days away, but audible now, a presence.

"Twelve years," she said to the concrete. Her voice came out smaller than she intended, swallowed by the gorge. "You could have told me. You could have left me one sentence. One."

The concrete said nothing. It was very good at that. It had been built by a man who was also very good at that.

She stayed until her hands were numb and the light was gone, and then she climbed down, and at the bottom Tomas was waiting with a thermos and no questions, which was its own kind of sentence.

"Okonkwo wants you," he said. "And Bea's back. She found something at the portal base."

"Found what?"

"A door," Tomas said. "Under the waterline. Steel, bolted, newer than the concrete. And jefa—" he hesitated, and in the lantern light his face looked older than his years, "—it's got fresh scratches on the bolts. Someone's been working on it. Recently."

Yara took the thermos and drank, and the tea was too sweet the way Tomas always made it, and she looked up at the black slot in the concrete face, at the mountain that had closed its mouth over her father's secret and held it for twelve years.

They were not the first to come back.

She had known it since the map, maybe. Since Kessler's helicopter. But knowing it in the abstract and seeing fresh tool marks on a door her father had drowned were different animals, and the second one had teeth.

"Get Nia," she said. "And wake Okonkwo. I want that door looked at tonight."
