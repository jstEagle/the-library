## 9. The Sworn-Off Job

Twelve years is a long time to carry a sound. Most people would have let it go by now, worn it smooth like river stone, until it was just another shape in the pocket of memory. Yara Vasquez had not. She carried the sound the way her father used to carry his brass survey tags—strung on a cord around her neck, tapping against her sternum with every step she took.

The sound was a door closing. Not slamming. Closing—the deliberate, careful click of a steel blast door seated into its frame, followed by the muffled thunder of charges going off in sequence behind it, and then the long silence of men who understood that they had just buried something alive.

She had been seventeen years old. She had signed the report.

* * *

The expedition had gone upriver in the last dry week before the monsoon, eleven people and forty tons of equipment aboard a barge called the *Meridian Star*, which belonged to a company whose name Yara had since forgotten but whose logo she never would: a stylized flywheel inside a laurel wreath, gold on green, stamped on every crate and every pay envelope. Her father, Emilio Vasquez, was chief surveyor. She was there as his assistant, unpaid, officially a "trainee," which meant she carried the instrument cases and logged the readings he called out and tried very hard not to look seventeen in front of the engineers.

The contract was simple, or simple the way contracts are when you're young enough to believe them. The railway consortium wanted the old Rivergate spur surveyed for reopening—a tunnel complex cut through the gorge in the last century, abandoned when the river shifted and drowned the lower levels. The consortium's money smelled different from mining money. Cleaner paper, older families, men who wore linen suits even at altitude and spoke about "heritage corridors" and "cultural infrastructure" as if those were things you could lay track through. Yara remembered thinking, at sixteen when the offer first came to their house, that she had never seen her father so quiet about a job. He read the contract twice. He drank two glasses of rum without saying anything. Then he said yes, because the pay was triple the salvage rate and Yara's mother's medical bills had eaten everything they had, and a man does not get to be principled when his daughter needs insulin and the roof leaks.

So they went up the Fly, past the villages where the people watched them pass without waving, and moored below the gorge where the portal stood half-drowned in its collar of concrete, and began the work.

For six weeks it was ordinary. Hard, wet, ordinary work. They pumped the upper gallery clear, shored the roof, laid temporary rail, and ran their surveys by lamplight while the river muttered somewhere below them like a thing dreaming. Yara learned to read her father's silences during those weeks. He had three kinds. There was the silence of concentration, which hummed. There was the silence of fatigue, which dragged. And there was the silence he went into whenever the survey line approached the lower gallery—the sealed level, the one the old plans marked with a hatch pattern and no explanation—and that silence had teeth in it. He checked the lower gallery door every morning. Not glanced at it. Checked it, with a key he kept on a chain under his shirt, though everyone said the lock hadn't worked in fifty years.

On the morning of the discovery, the silence broke.

Yara heard him shouting before she reached the upper gallery, and she came around the curve of the tunnel at a run, certain someone was hurt, and found him standing in the middle of the fresh-surveyed stretch with a crate open at his feet and his hands shaking.

"Papa?"

He didn't look up. "Get Okonkwo's predecessor—the hydrologist, what's his name, Ferro. Get him down here. And nobody else. Nobody else comes past the second marker."

The crate held carved panels. Wood so dense it had survived sixty years of floodwater, worked with figures—herons, serpents, a woman with her arms full of river. The craftsmanship made Yara's chest hurt just looking at it. It was museum-grade. It was also, unmistakably, packed for shipping: straw, oilcloth, stenciled numbers, a shipping firm's mark burned into the slats. A firm she'd later learn was owned by the same families whose crests appeared on the consortium's letterhead.

They found more over the next three days. Crates stacked in side chambers, sealed against the water, an entire warehouse of the stuff sitting in the dark above the flood line. And beneath the crates, in a strongbox welded to the rock, her father found the ledger.

Yara saw it only once, briefly, by lamplight. A fat book bound in oiled canvas, pages of dense handwriting and columns of figures, receipts and names. Her father sat with it for four hours without moving, and when he finally looked up his face had aged ten years and settled into something she had no word for yet. She has the word now. It's *condemned*. He looked like a man reading his own death warrant and finding it accurate.

"Papa, what is it?"

"It's proof," he said. "Of what this railway was built for. Of who paid for it and what they stole to do it." He closed the ledger. "And proof doesn't stay buried, Yaya. That's the problem with proof. Somebody always comes back for it."

* * *

The argument happened on the ninth night after the discovery, in the survey tent, and half the camp heard it through the canvas.

Her father wanted to seal the gallery. All of it—crates, ledger, everything—behind a proper demolition closure, and report the spur structurally unsound, unrecoverable, dead. Let the river take it back. The consortium's men wanted the opposite: extraction, cataloguing, "rescue of heritage assets from looter damage," a phrase one of the linen suits repeated so many times it stopped meaning anything. Between those two positions stood the crew, eleven people with salaries and families, and the crew's foreman, a broad, tired man named Rivas, kept trying to find a third position that let everyone live.

"You can't sit on this, Emilio." That was the consortium's site director, a man named Aldous Wren, whose voice never rose and therefore never had to. "Those objects are national patrimony. Leaving them to rot in a flooded hole isn't protection, it's negligence."

"They're not rotting. They've lasted sixty years down there."

"And they'll last sixty more under glass, where they belong."

"Belong to whom?" Her father's voice cracked across the tent. "You want to count them, Wren? Count them into whose inventory? I've read your shipping manifests. I know which firm packed those crates. I know who owns that firm. You think I haven't done the arithmetic?"

A pause. When Wren spoke again, his voice was softer, which was worse. "I think a man with your debts should be very careful about the arithmetic he does out loud."

Yara, bringing coffee in from the mess tent, stood frozen at the flap with the pot burning her hand through the rag, and did not go in, and did not leave. She listened to her father breathe. She listened to Rivas trying to smooth it over—*we're all tired, we'll reconvene tomorrow, nobody decides anything at midnight*—and she listened to the specific quality of silence that follows a threat delivered politely among witnesses.

That night her father woke her. He crouched by her cot with the lamp turned low and pressed something into her hands: a folded copy of the survey map, the master, with the lower gallery marked in red ink and a single word written across it in his cramped hand.

*Sealed.*

"If anything happens to me," he said, "you sign nothing. You hear me? Nothing. Not a form, not a report, not a receipt. You put your name on paper only when you know exactly what it buys and exactly what it costs. Promise me."

She promised. Seventeen-year-olds promise easily; that's what makes them useful.

Three nights later, the lower gallery exploded.

* * *

Afterward, the official account ran to four pages and explained everything except the truth. Seismic instability. Deteriorated charge storage in the old construction magazine. An accidental sympathetic detonation during routine stabilization work. Two fatalities: Emilio Vasquez, chief surveyor, and a pump operator named Deshi Marun, whose family received a settlement check and a form letter. The lower gallery collapsed; the upper gallery was compromised; the spur was recommended for permanent closure.

Yara signed the closure report on the eleventh day after the explosion, sitting at a folding table under a tarp, with Wren standing beside her and Rivas standing behind her, and both of them silent in ways that told her everything.

Here is what actually happened, as far as she has ever been able to assemble it, piece by piece, over twelve years of not talking about it:

There was a second set of charges. Her father had ordered demolition materials through the consortium's own supply chain—officially for "stabilization work"—and had spent two days placing them himself, alone, in the access drifts around the lower gallery. His plan was to close the gallery before the consortium could move the crates, present the destruction as an accident or an act of the river, and take the loss. Proof, he told her once, in the last conversation she ever had with him, is only safe when it's underwater. He intended to come back for the ledger someday, when the families' attention had moved on. He built the closure himself, with his own patterns, his own spacing, his own signature in the way he drilled the holes—because a man who has planted charges for thirty years cannot help signing his work.

But the consortium had its own men on site. Wren's security detail, four of them, who had been conspicuously idle for weeks. On the last night, her father went down to arm the final sequence. He took the ledger with him—he would not leave it in the tent, not anymore. He did not come back up.

What detonated, detonated early and wrong. Too deep, too concentrated, in a pattern none of the crew recognized—which Yara knows now, because she has spent twelve years learning demolition from Nia's kind of people, and because she walked the blast damage at the portal last week and recognized her father's hand everywhere except in the one place it mattered. Someone used his materials to kill him and collapse the gallery on top of him, burying the evidence room, the ledger, and the man, in a single stroke. Then they handed a seventeen-year-old girl a pen.

Why did she sign?

This is the question she has asked herself on every bad night for twelve years, and the answer has never changed and has never gotten easier. She signed because Rivas stood behind her and said, quietly, "Sign it, Yaya. Your mother needs the settlement. Sign it and live." She signed because Wren's men had already started packing the surviving crates onto the barge, and because she understood, with the terrible clarity available only to the young and the powerless, that if she refused, the report would be signed anyway—by Rivas, or forged outright—and her mother would get nothing, and she herself might join her father in the gorge. She signed because she was seventeen and alone and everyone who might have protected her was either dead or bought.

She signed. The pen scratched. Somewhere below them, under two hundred tons of collapsed rock and rising water, the ledger slept on, and the river began the slow work of keeping what it keeps.

* * *

They left the gorge three days later. The barge went downstream heavy with crates and light with men; two of the crew quit at the first landing and walked off into the bush rather than ride another hour in that silence. Wren shook Yara's hand at the gangplank. His palm was cool and dry.

"Your father was a fine surveyor," he said. "It's a shame about the ground conditions."

She said nothing. She was holding the folded map against her ribs, inside her jacket, where she had kept it since the night he gave it to her, and she could feel the red ink through the paper like a brand.

Her mother lived four more years. The settlement paid for the insulin and the doctors and the funeral, and Yara paid for everything after that with salvage money, running crews up and down the dead spurs, always moving, never staying, never taking a contract twice. People assumed it was about the work. Tomas once asked her why she never stayed anywhere a full season, and she told him it was about margins and reputation, and he nodded, because Tomas is loyal and polite, and because some lies are load-bearing.

But the truth is simpler and uglier. She never stayed because staying means stopping, and stopping means the sound comes back—the click of the blast door, the muffled thunder, the silence after. Motion drowns it out. Twelve years of motion, and it still only mostly works.

And now Kessler has hired her to reopen the very gate her father died sealing, with helicopters and contracts and the patient confidence of a man who has already read her file, and somewhere upstream the water table is behaving strangely, and somewhere in the dark below the portal there may be a strongbox with a canvas-bound book inside it, waiting.

She knew taking the job meant facing all of it. She told herself she could control it—that she would lead, pick the crew, keep everyone out of the lower gallery, and walk away clean before the monsoon.

Seventeen-year-olds promise easily. Thirty-one—no. Twenty-nine. She is twenty-nine years old now, and she has learned better than to make promises she can't keep.

But she has also learned that the river gives back what it keeps, eventually, whether you're ready or not.

Tomorrow they break through into the upper gallery.
