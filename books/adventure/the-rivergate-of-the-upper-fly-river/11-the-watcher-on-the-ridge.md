## 11. The Watcher on the Ridge

Tomas woke her with two fingers on her shoulder, the way he'd learned to do it years ago, back when a hand on Yara Vasquez in the dark could cost him his teeth.

"Ridge," he said. "North side. Two of them. Maybe three."

She was already reaching for her boots. The camp lay quiet around them—generators idled to a murmur, the pump shed humming its one long note, Kessler's men asleep in their green tents on the far bank of the creek like a wound that hadn't decided whether to close. Above it all, the ridge stood black against a sky rinsed thin by moonlight, and she saw what Tomas had seen: a shape where no shape should be. Then another, sliding behind a stand of pandanus with the unhurried patience of something that had been there a while.

"How long?"

"Since before my watch started. They don't move much. Professionals, or locals who know the ground better than we do." He handed her the binoculars without being asked. He always knew which she'd want first—her eyes or her boots.

She glassed the ridge. The figures were still as stones. One crouched; the other stood with hands loose at its sides, and even at this distance there was something in the posture that made the hair rise along her arms—not menace, exactly. Attention. The way a heron stands over water.

"Kessler's people?"

"His men are all accounted for. I counted them off after lights-out, like you taught me." A pause. "These aren't his."

Yara lowered the binoculars. Twelve years ago, on another night not so different from this one, someone had watched a camp from a ridge above the Fly, and her father had told her not to worry about it. *Survey crews get looked at,* he'd said. *We're looking too.* Three days later the lower gallery had burned with thunder, and the man who'd been standing watch had never been identified, and the report she signed had said *accidental detonation* in her own careful seventeen-year-old handwriting.

"Get Bea," she said.

"She's already up. She's been dressed for an hour." Tomas's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Your cousin doesn't sleep any better than you do."

* * *

Bea met them at the edge of camp where the floodlights gave out and the dark took over, and for a moment the three of them stood in the seam between light and shadow, saying nothing, listening to the river work at the gorge below. Bea had her dive knife and a coil of thin cord looped over one shoulder, which meant she'd planned to go regardless of what anyone said. She was thirty-one and built like the water she worked in—all lean muscle and economy—and when she turned to face Yara, the old distance between them was there as always, but tonight it felt less like a wall than like a door left ajar.

"I can be up there in twenty minutes," Bea said. "The scree on the north face is loose but it's fast if you know how to fall."

"You're not going alone."

"I wasn't going to ask permission, Yara. I was going to tell you so you didn't shoot me coming back down."

Tomas made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Yara ignored them both. She looked at the ridge, at the place where the shapes had been—and they were gone now, both of them, melted into the dark as if the mountain had swallowed them back.

"They'll move once they know they've been seen," she said. "If they're scouts, they have a route. If they have a route, they'll use it again." She weighed it, hating every option, because every option meant motion and motion meant exposure and exposure was how expeditions died out here—not from floods, not from rockfall, but from the things people did to each other in the dark between watches. "Go. Quiet. If you make contact, you do nothing stupid. You come back and you tell me."

"And if they run?"

"Then run after them," Yara said, "and remember you're a diver, not a soldier."

Bea's teeth flashed white in the dark. "I'm a Vasquez first. We're all soldiers. We just pretend it's logistics."

She went up the scree like water running backward, and Yara lost her in under a minute.

* * *

The wait was the worst part. It was always the worst part.

Yara walked the camp perimeter twice, checking the pump line, the fuel cache, the crate staging area under its tarpaulin—the crates whose manifests existed only in Kessler's head, the ones Nia had already started eyeing with the particular restlessness of an explosives woman who didn't trust other people's packing. Everything was where it should be. Nothing was wrong. And still the wrongness sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, because the camp had been watched, and she had been watched, and there was no version of this where that meant anything good.

Tomas found her at the creek crossing, where the water ran shallow and loud over slate.

"You want to talk about it?" he said.

"No."

"Good. Because I've been thinking instead." He crouched, dipped a hand in the current, watching the water braid around his fingers. "Twelve years ago, your father's expedition got watched too. You told me that once, drunk, in Port Moresby, and then you pretended you hadn't."

"I don't remember that."

"You remember everything. That's your whole problem." He shook the water off his hand. "So here's what I'm thinking. Kessler buys your debts, Kessler hires your crew, Kessler knows your father's name and your father's methods and your father's demolition patterns—which, by the way, is information that exists in maybe three living heads, one of which is yours. Now somebody's sitting on a ridge counting our generators. Either Kessler's playing games with his own operation, which rich men do love to do, or there's a third party in this gorge that nobody's mentioned."

"The river communities," Yara said. "Okonkwo said the villages upstream went quiet when the barge passed. Not hostile. Quiet. Like a house where everyone's listening through the wall."

"Exactly. So the question isn't who's watching us." Tomas stood, and in the starlight his face was older than his forty years, worn smooth by rivers the way stone is worn by water. "The question is what they think we're going to find. Because nobody hikes up a ridge at midnight to watch a dewatering operation, Yara. They're waiting for something. Which means they already know it's there."

Before she could answer, a pebble clicked somewhere upslope, deliberate as a knock on a door, and both of them turned—and Bea came down out of the dark with her hand raised, palm out, empty, and beside her moved a second figure, slighter, wrapped in a woven rain cape that made it nearly invisible until the moon caught the edges.

"They didn't run," Bea said. "They wanted to be found. Well—one of them did. There was a second watcher higher up. He went east toward the old survey trail when I got close." Her eyes found Yara's. "This one came down to talk. To you specifically. She asked for the daughter of Elio Vasquez by name."

* * *

The scout was younger than Yara expected—maybe twenty-five, with a broad, calm face and eyes that held the flat patience of deep water. She would not enter the camp. She stood at the boundary where the floodlight died, and she spoke English with the measured care of someone who had chosen to learn it rather than grown up inside it, and she kept her hands visible the whole time, which told Yara more about the politics of this gorge than any briefing document ever had.

"My name is Serau," she said. "My mother's people keep the upper channel. My father's people keep the caves below the falls. I keep the ridge."

"That's quite a job description," Tomas murmured.

Serau ignored him. She was looking at Yara the way Okonkwo looked at a bad water table—with professional, unblinking assessment. "You are the daughter."

"I am."

"Then you know what your father promised." It wasn't a question. From inside her cape she drew something small and pressed it into Yara's palm—a carved token, hardwood dense as iron, worn smooth by handling, cut into the shape of a fish turning in a spiral of water. Yara's skin recognized it before her mind did. She had seen its twin twelve years ago, hanging from her father's neck on a leather cord, and she had seen it missing from his body when they finally let her look, and she had spent years telling herself it had been lost in the blast.

"Where did you get this?" Her voice came out wrong. Too level. Too controlled. The voice of a woman signing a report.

"He carried it for eleven months," Serau said. "He came to our village before the last survey season. He ate at our fires. He asked us questions about the tunnel that no engineer asks—about what the old people remembered, about what was taken and where it was taken from. And at the end he gave us his word." She paused, and something moved behind the calm of her face, some grief that had been carried so long it had become structural. "He said the river would be returned. He said the things in the gate would go home. He said—" and here she stopped, and looked down at the ground, and when she looked up again her voice had changed, dropped into something rawer, "he said whatever happened, no one else would profit from it. Not the companies. Not the families. No one."

The word *families* landed in Yara's chest like a hook finding flesh.

"What families?"

Serau studied her for a long moment, and Yara understood that she was being weighed—that the whole camp, the pumps, the crates, Kessler's green tents, her own bloodline, all of it was being weighed against something the scout carried that was heavier than any of it.

"You don't know," Serau said at last, and there was no triumph in it, only a tired kind of confirmation. "You really don't know. Or you know and you're good at wearing the same face your father wore." She stepped back, one pace, into the dark beyond the light. "Listen to me, daughter of Elio. The families sent Kessler. The families sent your father. Ask him what he found."

"He's dead," Yara said. "He's been dead twelve years."

"Yes," Serau said. "And the families have been asking him what he found ever since. Why do you think the gate stayed shut? Why do you think no one came? Why do you think a man who sealed a tunnel with his own charges has a daughter who gets hired to open it by the same money that paid for the railway?" She pulled the cape closed around her shoulders. "The water remembers, Yara Vasquez. The water keeps what it keeps. But people forget, and forgetting is how the families stay rich."

She turned and went up into the dark, and the mountain took her the way it had taken the others, without sound, without thanks, without leaving anything behind except the token still burning cold in Yara's fist.

* * *

They stood at the edge of the light for a while after she was gone. The generator hummed. Somewhere downstream a night bird called three times and quit.

"Families," Bea said finally. "Plural. With a capital F, by the sound of it."

"Kessler's backers," Tomas said. "The shipping consortium. The museum trusts. Whoever owns the paper that owns the paper." He rubbed his jaw. "Your father's journal. The one Kessler's holding. I'd bet my pension it says the same thing she just said."

"It says a lot of things," Yara said, and heard her own voice go flat and guarded, and saw Bea notice, and hated herself for it.

Bea stepped closer. In the spill of the floodlight her face was pale and set, and for once the estrangement between them seemed to bend under the weight of something larger. "Yara. What did Dad find down there?"

And Yara stood in the light with the carved fish cutting a spiral into her palm, and thought about a seventeen-year-old girl with a pen, and a report with the word *accident* in it, and a door at the bottom of a flooded gallery that she had sworn on her father's grave no one would ever breach—including, especially, herself.

"When we break into the upper gallery tomorrow," she said, "you'll see."

It wasn't an answer. All three of them knew it wasn't an answer. But Bea held her gaze for a long moment and then nodded slowly, filing it away, and Tomas looked up at the empty ridge with narrowed eyes, and Yara turned the token over in her hand and read, in the moonlight, for the first time, the tiny characters carved along the fish's spine—her father's initials, and beneath them, in a language she couldn't read but suddenly, desperately needed to:

a date. Eleven days before the explosion.
