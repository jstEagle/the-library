## 12. The Upper Gallery Opens

The last meter of rock came out in pieces the size of a man's fist, and Nia stood back with her hands on her hips and let the crew's hammers finish what her charges had started.

"Nobody breathes the first air," she said. "Give it ten minutes."

They gave it eleven. Yara counted them herself, standing at the lip of the breach with her lamp turned down low, watching the dust hang in the beam like something alive that hadn't decided yet whether to move. Behind her, the pump line hummed its constant note, the sound that had become the expedition's heartbeat over nine days of dewatering. Ahead of her, through a ragged hole no wider than a doorway, was darkness that had been dark for twelve years.

Okonkwo ran his air monitor along the crack and nodded once. "Breathable. Cool, though. Cold enough to mean something big behind it."

"Cold means water," Bea said.

"Cold means stone," Okonkwo said. "Water we'd hear."

Yara swung her lamp up to the top of the breach and found what she'd been looking for since Nia set the charge: the steel lintel, half-buried in concrete, stamped with the old railway foundry mark. Above it, someone had painted a number in enamel that had survived the damp better than anything else in the gorge.

*UG-1.*

Upper gallery one. Her father's numbering system. She had known it would be there — she had told Kessler it would be there, when she drew the breach point on his plans — but knowing a thing and seeing it were different animals, and the second one bit.

"Tomas," she said. "You're with me. Nia, hold your people at the charge line until I call it clear. Nobody else crosses."

"And me?" Bea asked.

"You're on the line," Yara said. "If I shout, you pull."

Bea looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw in Yara's face made her clip her tether to the anchor plate without another word. Twelve years of silence between them, and now they spoke fluently in rope and hand signals, as if the language had been waiting all this time for them to need it again.

Yara went through the breach low, lamp high, boots finding the rubble slope by feel. The air inside was cold and mineral and utterly still, the stillness of a place where nothing had moved except water, and even the water had been patient about it. Her light swept left, swept right —

— and stopped.

Track. Intact track, running straight into the gallery's throat, rails bright under the dust where the floodwater had scoured them clean before it receded. Sleepers true. Gauge true. A handcar sat on the rails forty meters in, upright, its handle wrapped in oiled canvas, as if the men who left it had intended to come back after lunch.

"It's whole," Tomas said behind her, and his voice did something she had never heard it do in six years of working together: it cracked. "The whole line is *whole*. Boss, look at the drainage cuts. Look at the walls. This isn't a ruin. It's a station somebody closed the door on."

Yara was already walking. She knew why the gallery was whole; she had known since she was seventeen and read the survey numbers twice to be sure. Her father had sealed the lower gallery and flooded it deliberately, but the upper levels he had only closed — bulkheads, blast doors, a paperwork grave. He had not destroyed the upper gallery because destroying it would have meant explaining why, and explaining why would have meant showing them what was inside.

She had signed the report that let him do it. She had been seventeen, and the report had needed an adult signature, and hers had been the only one available that wasn't his.

Her boot struck metal.

She looked down. A brass tag lay in the ballast between two sleepers, small as a coin, tarnished green-black at the edges but still legible where the floodwater had polished it. Survey tags. Every rail crew in the basin used them — punch a hole, stamp the initials, wire the tag to the benchmark so the next crew knew whose work they were standing on. Cheap brass, machine-stamped, thousands of them scattered across a thousand kilometers of dead line.

This one was stamped **E.V.**

Esteban Vasquez. Her father's initials. His stamp, his hand, his tag, lying exactly where his fingers had dropped it or the water had carried it, twelve years ago, in the last week of his life.

"Boss?" Tomas said. "What have you got?"

"Ballast marker," she said, and her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Standard practice. Keep moving."

She picked it up anyway. She told herself it was procedure — tags got logged, tags got bagged, tags went in the survey record. But her hand closed around it before the thought finished forming, and it went into her chest pocket, against her skin, cold as a river stone, and she did not log it and she did not bag it and she did not tell Tomas, and the gallery swallowed all three facts without comment.

* * *

The upper gallery ran four hundred meters into the mountain before the first bulkhead, and they walked all of it, Yara calling measurements and Tomas writing them down in the log with hands that weren't quite steady. The tunnel was a time capsule of the railway's last confident year: signal boxes with their glass faces intact, tool racks holding tools, a wall calendar frozen on a month neither of them could quite make out through the grime. Water stains climbed the walls in tide lines, marking every flood season the sealed door had held back, a dozen annual rings of brown and black.

At the bulkhead, the story changed.

The door was steel, riveted, industrial — and it had been opened. Recently. The rust around the wheel-lock was broken in a clean ring, the hinge pins freshly greased, and the floor in front of it showed boot prints in dried mud, modern tread patterns, at least two sets.

Yara crouched and put her finger in one of the prints. Dry. Weeks dry, maybe more.

"Somebody's been in here since the sealing," Tomas said.

"Somebody's been in here since *last season*." She stood. "Kessler?"

"His advance team never got past the portal. I checked their logs myself." Tomas frowned at the prints. "These aren't his people's boots either. Wrong sole pattern."

Yara remembered Okonkwo's warning from the pumping stage — *the water table is wrong, something upstream is holding water back* — and Nia's find, the modern blasting caps sitting in a cache near the portal like a gift nobody had claimed. They are not the first to return. She had filed those facts away as data. Standing here, in her father's gallery, looking at someone else's footprints on her father's floor, the data became a presence. Someone else knew. Someone else had walked this line while it slept.

"Log it," she said. "Photograph everything. We treat the gallery as compromised from this point forward — lights, locks, and a watch rotation. And Tomas—"

"I know. Nobody hears about this but us."

"No. That's not what I was going to say." She looked at him, and he looked back, and six years of shared payroll and shared danger stood between them like a third person. "I was going to say: thank you for being the one who spotted the ridge watchers. If there are people watching this place, I want to know who, and I want to know before Kessler does."

Tomas wrote that down too, in the margin, where logs didn't officially go.

* * *

Beyond the bulkhead, the gallery opened into the storage spur, and this was where wonder curdled.

The crates were stacked in ranks, three high, timber-framed and stenciled, running back into the dark farther than their lamps reached. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Each one stenciled with a shipping code, a destination abbreviation, and a handling classification Yara recognized from her salvage days because it meant *fragile, high value, climate-sensitive* — the kind of marking you put on precision instruments, or on things that belonged in glass cases under lights.

No manifests hung from any of them. No customs stamps. No waybills. In a functioning railway, a crate without paper was a crate that didn't exist, and here were hundreds of crates that had never existed at all, riding a ghost line into a mountain, waiting out a war or a scandal or a decade in the dark.

"They're packed like museum freight," Tomas said quietly. He ran his lamp along a stencil: *KV-114 — FRAGILE — THIS SIDE UP.* "Somebody spent real money keeping these things safe."

"Somebody spent real money getting them *out*," Yara said. "Look at the routing codes. These aren't local destinations."

She didn't touch them. That was the discipline she set for herself, standing in the aisle with her father's brass tag burning a cold circle against her chest: look, measure, photograph, walk away. Because she could feel the other impulse rising, the one that lived deeper than curiosity — the urge to pry a lid and know, finally, concretely, what her father had died sealing shut. What he had seen in the lower gallery that made a careful man blow a tunnel and forge a report and use his own daughter's signature as a shield.

Not yet. Not here, not with Kessler's cameras on the portal and his accountants counting crate numbers off the manifest he'd given her. Whatever was in these crates, the moment she opened one, the job stopped being a job and became a choice, and she wasn't ready to make it in front of other people.

"Seal the bulkhead behind us," she told Tomas. "We log the inventory from the doorways. Nobody handles cargo without my say-so. That includes Nia."

"That especially includes Nia," Tomas said.

They were turning back when Yara's lamp caught the far wall, past the last rank of crates, where the gallery narrowed toward the abandoned connection to the lower levels. There was a door there — smaller, older, timber with iron straps — and on it, at eye height, someone had painted a message in white enamel that had run slightly in the damp but remained perfectly readable:

**SEALED BY ORDER OF E. VASQUEZ, CHIEF SURVEYOR. DO NOT OPEN. THE RIVER KEEPS WHAT IT KEEPS.**

The same words. Almost the same words she would find scratched inside the lower lock, though she didn't know that yet; all she knew was that her father had written them twice, in two places, in two hands' worth of urgency, and that the second time he had written them for whoever came next.

For her. He couldn't have known it would be her. But he had written them for whoever came next, and it was her, and she stood in the cold dark reading her father's handwriting for the first time in twelve years and felt the gallery tilt very slightly, the way a boat tilts when a passenger moves wrong.

"Boss." Tomas's voice, careful. "You okay?"

"He knew people would come back," she said. "He built this place to be found. Or to be *not* found. Both. He wanted whoever broke in to read this first."

"What does it mean? 'The river keeps what it keeps'?"

Yara touched the paint. It flaked under her fingertip, a fleck of white no bigger than a snowflake, and she watched it fall into the dark between the sleepers and vanish.

"It means he ran out of time to write a longer sentence," she said.

* * *

They came back up the gallery in single file, and the crew at the breach line read their faces and didn't ask. Nia fell in beside Yara as they climbed out into the grey afternoon light, and Yara watched her glance back at the dark mouth of the tunnel with an expression Yara recognized professionally, having worn it herself most of her adult life: the look of a person doing arithmetic they've been told not to do.

"How many crates?" Nia asked.

"Hundreds. Sealed. Stenciled for long-haul shipment."

"Any open?"

"One row looks disturbed. Why?"

Nia shrugged, too casually. "Just wondering what we're guarding. Kessler's manifest lists cargo as 'railway infrastructure, non-historic.' That's what the insurance riders say, anyway. I read the riders. It's a habit."

"Your habit is going to get you in trouble someday."

"My habits keep me alive." Nia smiled, and it didn't reach her eyes. "Infrastructure, huh. You ever see infrastructure packed in climate-controlled crating with no manifests?"

Yara looked at her. Nia looked back, level, and for a moment the two of them stood on the portal ledge in the wind off the gorge, each waiting to see how much the other would say.

"Not yet," Yara said.

Nia nodded slowly, as if that answer had confirmed something rather than dodged it. "Right," she said. "Yet."

That night, after the generators settled and the watch rotations took the dark, Yara sat alone in her tent with the brass tag in her palm. She turned it under her headlamp. E.V., punched through with a lanyard hole, the brass worn smooth on one edge where a thumb had worried it — his thumb, probably, over years of habit, the way she worried her own tags now without thinking.

He had carried this one. Of the thousands of tags he must have stamped across the basin, this was one he'd kept in his pocket, and it had ended up in the ballast of the gallery he died sealing, and she had stolen it from the survey record within thirty seconds of touching it.

Wonder curdling into recognition. That was the shape of the day. She had walked into her father's masterpiece expecting ruins and found a warehouse instead — a warehouse full of things valuable enough to kill for, guarded by a sentence he'd painted in white, and already visited by boots that belonged to none of them.

She wrapped the tag in cloth and put it in the bottom of her kit, under the false panel where she kept the things that mattered, which until tonight contained only her father's map.

Through the tent wall she heard footsteps stop outside — Tomas, taking up his unasked post, saying nothing, asking nothing. Farther off, toward the portal, a light moved that shouldn't have: someone checking the breach seal hours ahead of schedule.

And below it all, faint through sixty meters of rock, the sound of the pumps changing pitch — Okonkwo's voice cutting across camp, sharp, telling someone to wake up, telling someone the water table had moved again, upstream, overnight, like something on the far side of the mountain shifting its weight.

Yara closed her hand around nothing, where the tag used to be, and listened to the mountain breathe.
