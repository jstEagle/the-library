## 13. Crates Without Manifests

The upper gallery smelled of cold stone and old iron, a smell that had been waiting twelve years for someone to breathe it.

Yara walked the line of track with her lamp held low, the way her father had taught her—light the ground first, then the walls, then let your eyes climb to what the dark was hiding. The rails ran true into the mountain, polished along their crowns where wheels had once passed, rusted everywhere else in orange lacework. Brass survey tags glinted at intervals on the rock wall, each one stamped with initials she knew better than her own: *E.V.* Esteban Vasquez. Her father had tagged this gallery like a man tagging his own veins.

Behind her, the crew worked in pairs under the work lights they'd strung from the generator line. Okonkwo had set up his instruments near the standing water at the far end, taking readings he didn't like and saying so only with his face. Tomas ran the winch crew, moving crates off the flatcars one by one into the staging area Kessler's people had designated—a square of clean tarpaulin laid over the ballast, lit like an operating table.

Yara had given the order that morning, simple and absolute: *Nothing gets opened. Nothing gets moved off the staging area. We inventory, we log, we wait for Kessler's manifest team.*

It wasn't sentiment. It was procedure. Unmanifested cargo in a sealed tunnel was either evidence or liability, and both of those were words that got salvage crews sued, jailed, or worse. She'd told Nia specifically, because Nia was the one who couldn't leave a locked box alone the way other people couldn't leave a hanging thread alone.

"You want me to just look at them?" Nia had said, offended.

"I want you to look at the *seals*," Yara said. "Count them. Photograph them. If a seal is broken when we found it, that goes in the log. If a seal is broken because you got curious, that also goes in the log, and then you go on Kessler's payroll, because I'm not paying for lawyers."

Nia had grinned and said something about Yara having no soul, and Yara had believed, more or less, that the matter was settled.

* * *

It was Bea who came looking for her, three hours later, walking fast enough down the gallery that her boots threw echoes.

"Yara." Her cousin's voice had a strange flatness to it, the tone of someone who has decided not to feel whatever they're feeling until later. "You need to come see this."

"See what?"

"Nia opened one."

Yara closed her eyes for half a second. The lamp light pressed red through her lids. Twelve years of keeping this family's tempers and debts balanced, and here it was again—the same equation, always the same: one person's curiosity, everyone else's consequences.

"Which crate?"

"Number forty-one. The one with the double seals."

"Is she still there?"

"Where do you think?"

Yara handed Tomas the winch log without a word and followed Bea up the line. The gallery narrowed past the third tag, the ceiling coming down to meet the tops of the crates stacked on pallets, and the air grew colder, mineral, almost sweet—stone water and creosote and, underneath it, something else. Something organic and faintly resinous. Wood oil. Lacquer.

She smelled it before she saw the open crate, and her stomach dropped, because she recognized that smell from somewhere she couldn't name yet, and recognition is always worse than mystery.

Nia stood beside crate forty-one with her hands raised slightly from her sides, palms out, like a woman demonstrating she'd touched nothing—which was its own confession, because Nia's gloves were off and folded on the crate lid.

"Before you say anything," Nia began.

"That's not how confessions work."

"Before you say anything," Nia repeated, stubborn, "you need to look. Because I've blown up safes, Yara. I've opened containers that smugglers sealed in three countries. I know packing. And this—" she gestured at the crate's open mouth, and her voice did something Yara had never heard it do, which was go quiet, "—this isn't smuggling. This is *archaeology*. Somebody packed this like it was going to a museum. Somebody packed this like it mattered."

Yara stepped up onto the rail bed and looked inside.

The crate was lined with foam blocks cut to custom shapes, each recess holding its object the way a hand holds an egg. The foam had yellowed at the edges but the cuts were precise, professional, done with a hot wire by someone who'd measured twice. Nested in the center recesses, wrapped in acid-free tissue gone soft as cloth, lay a set of carved panels—six of them, hardwood darkened almost to black, each one taller than her forearm. Even in the bad light she could see the figures cut into them: long-beaked birds, coiled serpents, human forms whose limbs dissolved into water patterns. The carving was deep and confident, done by someone who had grown up with these stories in their hands, and the surfaces carried a patina no forgery bothers to fake because it takes a hundred years of being touched.

Around the panels, smaller recesses held ceremonial objects—a mask with eyes of shell, a staff head worked in some pale stone, bundles wrapped so carefully she could see the individual knots.

And beneath the foam insert, visible where Nia had lifted it out and leaned it against the crate wall, was the bottom layer of packing material: a stiff board with printed text.

Yara crouched and angled her lamp at it.

**MERIDIAN CULTURAL LOGISTICS — FINE ARTS DIVISION**
**CONDITION REPORT / TRANSIT CASE NO. MCL-7734**
**HANDLE AS MUSEUM SPECIMEN. DO NOT EXPOSE TO MOISTURE.**

Meridian Cultural Logistics. She turned the word over. It wasn't a name she knew, but it was a *kind* of name she knew—the kind of company that existed in the space between auction houses and customs offices, the kind that moved things across borders with paperwork so clean the objects themselves became invisible. Companies like that didn't advertise. They had clients, and clients had names, and those names appeared on foundation boards and mining prospectuses and the letterheads of banks in cities she'd never been able to afford to enter.

Companies like that had owners.

"Okonkwo," she called down the gallery, not taking her eyes off the board.

He arrived at his own pace, because Okonkwo never hurried for anyone, and stood over the crate with his hands in his pockets, reading the condition report upside down before Yara shifted to let him see it right way up.

"Hm," he said.

"'Hm' what?"

"This firm." He tapped the logo with one finger, not touching the paper itself—he was careful that way, always aware of what a surface might give up. "Meridian. I've seen the mark before. Port surveys, years ago. They handle repatriation shipments. Official ones. Government-to-museum." He looked at her. "They don't run private contracts. Their charter doesn't allow it."

"Then why is their packing in a tunnel?"

"Because someone used their materials," Okonkwo said, "or someone used their *name*, or—" he paused, and Yara watched him decide whether to finish the thought, "—or someone owns enough of them that the distinction stopped mattering."

Bea made a small sound. Not quite a laugh. "Kessler's backers," she said. "You heard him at the briefing. The consortium. Halvorsen Group, Andrade Holdings, the rest of them. Mining money, shipping money." She looked at the logo. "Museum money."

Nobody said anything for a moment. In the silence Yara could hear the whole gallery breathing: the drip of seepage somewhere back toward the portal, the hum of the generator line, the distant clank of Tomas's winch crew still working, still moving crates, still doing exactly what they'd been paid to do without knowing what the cargo was.

That was the thing about the job's true shape, Yara thought. It never announced itself all at once. It came into focus the way these tunnels had—lamp low, ground first, then the walls, then the thing the dark was hiding. First it had been a reopening contract. Then it had been a race against monsoon. Now it was museum-grade packing, in a tunnel her father had died sealing, shipped by a firm owned by the same families whose names Kessler spoke with the particular reverence men reserve for creditors.

"Seal it," she said.

"Yara—" Nia started.

"No. Listen to me." Yara straightened up and made herself look at each of them in turn—Nia, Bea, Okonkwo—because an order given to a face is worth three given to the air. "Everything goes back exactly as it was. Foam, tissue, wraps, knots. Nia, you're the only one who knows how it came apart, so you're the one who puts it together. Photograph the condition report first, both sides, then reseal. New lead-and-wire seal from our own stock, logged as inspection access, my authorization number. That's the honest version of what happened, and it's the version that goes in the record."

"And the dishonest version?" Bea asked quietly.

"There isn't one yet." Yara climbed down off the rail bed. "But there will be. There always is."

* * *

She didn't sleep that night, or rather she slept the way she'd learned to on bad jobs—in shallow slices, surfacing every hour to check the sounds of camp. The generator. The rain starting on the tarps, early, heavier than the season promised. Somewhere down the slope, the river working at the gorge the way it had worked for ten thousand years, patient as a creditor.

Near midnight she gave up and sat outside her tent with the camp stove making its small orange argument against the dark, and she took out the brass tag she'd pocketed in the gallery two days ago. *E.V.* She turned it in the firelight. Her father's initials, stamped into a tunnel he had helped build and then helped destroy, in a job he had never explained and a death nobody had ever adequately accounted for. *Equipment failure,* the report had said. *Detonation misfire during emergency closure.* She had signed the closure paperwork at seventeen because a man from the railway authority had put it in front of her and said the crew needed the compensation released and signatures release compensation, and she had signed, and the tunnel had become a grave with a concrete door, and she had spent twelve years running rails in the opposite direction.

Now she was sitting thirty meters above that door, being paid by the man who'd bought her debts, cataloguing crates packed by her father's killers—or by whoever her father's killers answered to, which in Yara's experience amounted to the same organism wearing different clothes.

Footsteps on gravel. She didn't startle; she'd known the weight and rhythm before she identified them. Tomas lowered himself onto the supply crate beside her, handed her a mug of the terrible coffee, and sat looking out at the rain.

"The winch crews are asking questions," he said.

"What kind?"

"The good kind. The dangerous kind." He blew on his coffee. "Forty-one crates moved today. Word travels. Someone asked me if we're moving museum pieces. Someone else asked if that's why Kessler wants the lower gallery so badly." He glanced at her. "I told them manifests come Monday. It bought us two days, maybe three."

"You think I should tell them."

"I think," Tomas said, in the tone he saved for truths he'd already decided on, "that a crew will forgive you for hiding danger. They'll sign on for danger, they always have. What a crew won't forgive is finding out you hid the *truth*, because truth is the only thing on a job site that belongs to everybody." He drank. "Your father understood that. From what you've told me."

"From what little I've told you," Yara corrected.

Tomas smiled into his mug and said nothing, which was his way of agreeing while giving her room to pretend otherwise.

Rain drummed on the tarp. Down in the gorge the river rose a little louder, or maybe she only imagined it, the way she imagined the tunnel breathing below them, the way she sometimes imagined her father's voice in the acoustics of any enclosed space. *Light the ground first, mija. The ground tells you what the walls are hiding.*

"Tomas," she said. "When we get the manifest team's schedule tomorrow—I want you to find out who signs their transit documents. Names, companies, everything. Quietly."

"And if the names are the ones we think they'll be?"

Yara looked at the brass tag in her palm, then closed her fist around it, the stamped letters pressing into her skin.

"Then this stopped being a salvage job," she said, "the day we broke the portal. And I need to have a conversation with Dario Kessler before he finds out we've had it without him."

Across the camp, at the edge of the lamplight, a figure detached itself from the communications tent and started toward the portal road—Kessler's night man, the one who never slept and never explained, walking his rounds with the unhurried certainty of someone who already knew how this story ended.

Yara watched him go, and finished her coffee, and began composing in her head the conversation that would decide whether any of them left this gorge alive.
