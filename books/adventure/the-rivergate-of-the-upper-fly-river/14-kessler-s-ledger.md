## 14. Kessler's Ledger

The rain found them at dusk, a thin warm drizzle that turned the camp's red clay to grease and made the tarps sag like tired shoulders. Yara stood under the awning of the survey tent for a long time before she moved, watching the light die over the gorge, turning the Rivergate portal from concrete grey to the color of an old bruise.

She had spent three hours since Nia's confession doing nothing, which was, for her, a kind of violence. Three hours of not walking to Kessler's command barge, of not saying the words that were already loaded in her chest like charges in a borehole. Three hours of inventorying what she knew: carved panels in museum-grade foam, packing crates stenciled with a shipping firm she'd traced on Okonkwo's satellite terminal to a holding company whose directors' names read like a guest list from a Port Moresby charity gala. The same names, if her memory served—and it always served—that appeared on the dedication plaques of half the schools and clinics along the lower river. Generous families. Respectable families. Families with foundations.

Families who had built a railway through country that never asked for one.

Tomas found her there. He didn't ask where she was going. He just handed her his rain jacket, because hers was still drying over the generator exhaust, and said, "Take someone."

"No."

"Then take this." He pressed a short-handled bolt cutter into her hand instead, which was Tomas's way of saying he understood without understanding, and that he would be awake all night regardless.

The command barge sat moored against the old loading quay below the portal, lit up like a riverboat casino, its deck crusted with cable drums, pump manifolds, and the quiet menace of Kessler's security detail—four men now, up from two, rotating watches Yara hadn't authorized because she'd never been asked to authorize anything. That was the thing about Dario Kessler's operations: they grew teeth the way mold grew in the wet, silently and exactly when you weren't looking.

A guard at the gangway lifted a hand. "Ms. Vasquez. Mr. Kessler's expecting you."

Of course he was. She climbed aboard anyway.

* * *

Kessler kept his office in what had once been the barge's cargo hold, and he'd furnished it the way he furnished everything—expensively and without warmth. A folding table covered in green baize. A lamp with a proper brass shade, hauled upriver at obscene cost so he could read contracts under decent light. Two chairs, one of them good. He was pouring coffee from a steel thermos when she came down the steps, and he poured a second cup without asking, sliding it across the baize toward the empty chair.

"You've been to the upper gallery," he said.

It wasn't a question, and it wasn't an accusation either. It was delivered with the mild satisfaction of a man confirming a delivery. Yara didn't sit. She stood with her boots planted on the deck plating, water dripping off Tomas's jacket onto his nice floor, and let the silence do some work first.

"Nia opened a crate," Yara said finally. "Against my orders. Against yours too, I assume."

"She did." Kessler sipped his coffee. "I'll have her disciplined, if you like. Though between us, I'm glad someone's curiosity finally outran their caution. It saves me a conversation I was dreading." He set the cup down and folded his hands, and Yara watched him assemble the expression he wore the way other men wore ties—the one that made boardrooms and government ministries feel they were dealing with a reasonable man. "You've seen what's in them."

"Carved panels. Ceremonial pieces. Some of them older than the railway by four hundred years." She kept her voice level. "Packed like eggs. Foam cut to fit each piece individually. Shipping labels from Meridian Cultural Logistics."

"Meridian," Kessler agreed pleasantly. "Good firm. Climate-controlled containers, full insurance, provenance documentation—" he caught himself, smiled thinly, "—well. Documentation."

"Provenance documentation for stolen heritage," Yara said. "That's a new phrase even for you."

Kessler leaned back in his chair, and something in him shifted—not defensively, she realized, but with relief, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried up a long staircase. When he spoke again, the boardroom polish was gone, and what was underneath was almost worse: candor.

"Yes," he said. "All right. Yes. They're stolen. Or—they were stolen, forty years ago, sixty years ago, by men who are dead now, using a railway your father's generation surveyed and sealed and pretended never existed. Do you know what happens to objects like these when they stay in the ground, Yara? Rot. Termites. Flood. A kid with a machete who finds a crate and sells a ceremonial mask for the price of a outboard motor, and burns the rest for kindling because he doesn't know what it is." He spread his hands. "I'm not the looter here. I'm the recovery operation. There's a difference, and the difference is that everything I take gets catalogued, conserved, and eventually—it will end up in institutions. Proper ones. With climate control and security and scholars who can read what's written on them."

"Institutions owned by the same families who paid to steal them in the first place."

The words came out of her flat and cold, and she saw it land—saw the flicker behind Kessler's eyes, quick as a fish under a boat. But he recovered fast. He always recovered fast.

"You've been reading the crates," he said softly. "Not just opening them. Reading them."

"I've been reading everything."

For a moment neither of them spoke. On the deck above, boots crossed the plating in a slow patrol rhythm. Water ticked against the hull. Somewhere up the gorge, the pumps throbbed in their endless, patient argument with the dark.

Kessler stood. He walked to the bulkhead where a steel document case sat—military green, combination lock, the kind of case that traveled with its own insurance policy—and rested his hand on it without opening it.

"Do you know why I hired you?" he said. "Not your crew. You. Not because you're the best rail salvage outfit in the basin, though you are. Not because you know this spur better than any living person, though you do. I hired you because of your father."

Yara's jaw tightened. "Careful."

"He was the finest survey engineer this river ever produced, and everyone who worked with him knew it, and then one day he blew up the most important tunnel on the line and died doing it, and the official report was signed by his seventeen-year-old daughter." Kessler turned to face her fully. "I have spent eleven years trying to get access to this site through every legal channel that exists. Permits, leases, heritage exemptions, a mining claim I bought purely for the mineral rights underneath the spur. Eleven years, Yara. And do you know what stopped me every single time? Not the government. Not the courts. A closure report. One page, signed in a teenager's handwriting, declaring the gallery structurally unsound and unrecoverable. Your signature has been the most effective lock on that door for twelve years. I couldn't pick it. So I hired it."

"That's a filthy way to put it."

"It's an honest way to put it, which in my experience is rarer and more useful." He came back around the table but didn't sit, didn't offer her the chair again; he seemed to understand that she would stand until this was done. "Here is what I actually believe, since we're being honest. Those artifacts were taken from the communities upriver by men who bribed their way past every law that existed, and the proof of those bribes—if it ever existed—is buried in the lower gallery your father flooded. I can't recover the proof. I don't especially want to. What I want is the objects, saved intact, before the monsoon turns this gorge into a washing machine and takes them downriver in pieces. Call it redemption. Call it laundering. Call it whatever keeps you working. But look me in the eye and tell me the panels rotting in that floodwater are better off than they'd be in a conservation lab."

"They'd be better off home," Yara said. "In the villages they came from. Ask anyone upriver. Ask the elder who gave me—"

"I know about the token." Something hardened in his voice, the first real edge she'd heard from him. "I know about the scouts on the ridge, the visits to your camp, the little economy of favors running between your crew and the river people. I employ hydrologists, Yara. Hydrologists notice who comes and goes by water." He paused, and then, deliberately, softened again, and the softness was the most frightening thing yet. "But none of that matters tonight. Tonight I want to show you something."

He spun the dial on the green case. His fingers moved without hesitation—he knew the combination the way she knew her own crew's radio callsigns—and the lid came open on a layer of oilcloth, and under the oilcloth, a book.

She knew it before he lifted it out. She knew it by the shape, by the water-stained canvas cover, by the way the spine cracked when it opened—she had grown up watching that spine crack across her father's knees at the kitchen table while he annotated survey plots by lamplight. Her father's original field journal. The one that had gone into the ground with the expedition. The one that had been listed as destroyed in the incident report.

Her hand was moving before she decided to move it, and Kessler let her take it—let her hold it, feel the weight of it, smell the mildew and the graphite—and only when she'd turned to the first page, to her father's block capitals writing the date and the coordinates of a world that no longer existed, did Kessler speak again.

"Recovered from the estate of the expedition's logistics officer," he said. "Who sold it to me eight years ago, along with certain other papers, for a sum that kept him comfortable in retirement. I've read every page. So have my lawyers. And my backers." He let that word sit in the air between them. "Your father was a meticulous man. He wrote down everything. Elevations, water tables, blast patterns—and conversations. Who visited the site. What they wanted removed. What they offered him. What he refused." Kessler tapped the cover gently, almost affectionately. "This book is the reason the Rivergate was sealed, Yara. Not structural failure. Your father found something in the lower gallery that certain families could not afford to have found, and he chose the only exit available to an honest man: he drowned it, and himself with it."

"Give it to me." Her voice came out rougher than she intended.

"No." Kessler took the journal back from her hands—gently, courteously, the way a banker takes back a pen—and slid it into the case, and closed the lid, and spun the dial. "Not yet. This is a partnership offer, not a gift. Here are the terms. You finish the recovery on schedule. You keep your crew disciplined and your mouth shut about Meridian and the crates. In exchange, you get ten percent of the recovery's appraised value—which, conservatively, is enough to retire every debt you have twice over—and when the last crate leaves this gorge, the journal becomes yours. All of it. Every page. Unredacted."

"And if I say no?"

Kessler smiled, and for the first time all night it reached his eyes, and what lived in his eyes was patient and cold as groundwater.

"If you say no, I finish the job with the crew I already have—I've been quietly interviewing yours for weeks, and you'd be surprised how many of them have opinions about your payroll delays—and the journal goes into a vault in Singapore, where it will be worth exactly nothing to anyone except me. Or—" he shrugged, a small economical motion, "—it has an accident. Paper burns, Yara. Even meticulous paper."

Above them, the patrol boots crossed the deck again. The pumps throbbed. Yara became aware that her hands had curled into fists at her sides and made herself open them, finger by finger, because Dario Kessler was watching her hands the way he watched everything—cataloguing, appraising, filing.

"You're offering me my father's murder weapon as a signing bonus," she said.

"I'm offering you the truth," Kessler said, "which is more than anyone else in this basin will ever offer you, and I'm offering it at a price you can afford. Think about what your father would have wanted. An honest man doesn't drown the truth forever. He waits for someone strong enough to carry it." He extended his hand across the green baize table. "Ten percent. The journal. Partnership. Say yes, and tomorrow morning we're colleagues."

Yara looked at the hand. She thought about the ledger pages she had photographed in the dark of the lower gallery, hidden in a waterproof bag inside her kit, that Kessler did not know existed. She thought about the elder's carved token in her pocket, worn smooth by a river's worth of waiting. She thought about her father's block capitals, and how a man that meticulous would have hated—hated—to leave his last word in the keeping of a man like this.

She took the hand. It was dry and cool and it closed around hers like a contract.

"Partnership," she said. "On one condition. The journal stays in this case, in this hold, and I get supervised reading access starting tomorrow. If a single page goes missing or burns, our arrangement dies with it—and I promise you, Dario, you do not want to find out what I do when it dies."

Kessler laughed—a real laugh, delighted, awful. "There she is," he said. "The daughter. I wondered when she'd come up for air." He released her hand and poured her coffee again, pressing the cup into her grip like a seal on a deal. "Tomorrow, then. After breakfast. Bring your careful eyes."

* * *

She walked back up the quay alone in the rain, and the drizzle had thickened into something with intent, drumming on the tarps, silvering the floodlights. At the edge of camp, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the fuel bladder—Tomas, still awake, still holding the bolt cutter he'd given her like a rosary.

"Well?" he said.

"He has my father's journal."

Tomas went very still. "The real one?"

"The real one. Every page. Including, if he's telling the truth—and God help me, Tomas, I think he was telling the truth—everything my father wrote down about who came to buy what out of that tunnel, and what he refused, and what it cost him." She looked up at the black shoulder of the gorge, where the portal waited behind its curtain of rain. "He's going to let me read it tomorrow. Supervised. Like a prisoner getting visiting hours."

"And what does he want?"

"Me. Quiet. Compliant. Ten percent of a theft, laundered into a legacy." She wiped the rain off her face with the heel of her hand. "He thinks he just bought me."

"Did he?"

Yara was quiet for a moment. Somewhere downriver, thunder rolled along the gorge like freight cars coupling, one after another after another—the monsoon clearing its throat, nine days early or twenty days late, nobody knew anymore.

"No," she said. "But he showed me exactly where he keeps the one thing in this world I'd burn his whole empire to hold. And that, Tomas, is the most dangerous thing a man like Dario Kessler can ever do—show a Vasquez where the vault is." She handed him back the bolt cutter and turned toward her tent, toward the photographs hidden in her kit, toward the long arithmetic of the night ahead. "Wake me at five. Tomorrow I'm going to read my father's last words—and then we're going to find out what he really knew, and who else it can convict besides us."
