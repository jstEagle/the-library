## 15. The Journal

Kessler left the journal on her cot like a man leaving bait in a trap.

That was the first thing Yara noticed about it when she came back from the portal at dusk—the way it sat there, centered on her folded blanket, squared to the edges, deliberate as a signature. Not dropped. Not forgotten. *Placed.* Around it, the canvas of her tent held the last grey light, and somewhere outside Tomas was running the evening generator check, and Nia was swearing at a jammed detonator box in the magazine tent, and the whole camp went on with its small mechanical noises while Yara stood in the doorway of her own quarters and did not move toward the thing on her bed.

It was bound in oilcloth, the kind survey crews used to waterproof their field books, and it was swollen with old water damage, the pages inside fanned out like gills. Her father's initials were stenciled on the spine in faded black: **E.V.** Emilio Vasquez. The hand that had taught her to read contour lines before she could read sentences.

She had spent twelve years telling herself that everything of his had gone down with the flood, or been burned in the official incineration at Port Moresby, or simply ceased to exist because grief was a kind of erosion and it wore even paper away eventually. She had built her whole life on that assumption the way you build a bridge on a pier you never inspect. And now Kessler had set the pier down on her blanket and walked away whistling.

"He wanted you to have it tonight," Bea said from the doorway. Yara hadn't heard her come up. Her cousin stood with her arms crossed, dive knife on her hip, silhouetted against the work lights. "His aide brought it over an hour ago. Said Mr. Kessler thought you'd want some privacy."

"Did he say anything else?"

"He said—" Bea's mouth twisted, working the words around like gravel. "He said, 'Tell her the truth reads better than the report she signed.'"

Yara closed her eyes. *The report she signed.* Of course he knew about that too. He'd known everything since the helicopter landed; probably since before. Every card she'd ever kept close was already face-up on his table.

"You don't have to read it," Bea said quietly. "Whatever's in there. You can burn it. I'll stand watch and swear I never saw."

"And then what? He keeps the copies. He always keeps the copies." Yara picked up the journal. It weighed almost nothing. That was the obscenity of it—twelve years of dread, and it weighed less than a hammer. "Get some sleep, Bea. Tomorrow we dewater the lower approach."

Bea lingered a moment longer. In the work light her face looked older than thirty-one, carved along lines Yara didn't remember putting there. "You were seventeen," she said. "Whatever he wrote in there, whatever happened—you were seventeen, and everyone else in that tunnel was grown." Then she was gone, boots crunching down the gravel path toward the water tanks, and Yara was alone with the oilcloth and the dark coming up out of the gorge like something rising to feed.

* * *

She didn't open it right away. That was its own ritual, and she let herself have it. She boiled water for coffee she didn't drink. She checked the pump schedule twice. She cleaned her sidearm, which didn't need cleaning, and racked it by the cot where it lived like a sleeping animal. Outside, the Fly spoke its endless low language against the barge hulls, and the first true stars came out over the ridge where the watchers had stood, and Okonkwo's instruments ticked and hummed in the hydrology tent like insects in a wall.

Then she sat cross-legged on the cot, turned the lamp down to its lowest ring, and untied the oilcloth.

The first third of the book was ordinary. Survey notes, three years old by the dates—her father's cramped, beautiful script, each numeral drawn with the same care he'd given everything, as if a sloppy digit might insult the mountain. Benchmarks. Flow rates. Rail bed elevations along the abandoned spur, annotated in pencil: *ballast rotted, salvageable ties approx. 40%, do not trust the 1948 alignment maps, they lie.* She could hear his voice in the margins, dry and amused, the voice that had narrated every workday of her childhood. *They lie, Yarita. Maps always flatter the men who paid for them.*

There were sketches. The portal face, drawn from across the gorge, with blast damage already penciled in—so the demolition had happened before this journal began, which meant this was the second expedition, the quiet one, the one that officially never existed. There was a drawing of the upper gallery track, and a list of crate numbers, and beside one entry, dated eleven months before the accident, a single underlined word: ***why?***

Yara turned pages slowly. The lamp hissed. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered over the western ranges—the season gathering itself, still weeks out, but gathering.

The middle section changed character. The handwriting got faster. The margins filled with questions instead of measurements.

*Crates 41 through 88 are not railway stores. No manifests. Packing stencils match Meridian Fine Arts Transport, Singapore — why is a fine arts shipper consigned to a colonial-era rail spur in the Star Mountains? Opened Crate 57 (with R. Okafor, who has since requested transfer). Contents: carved house panels, spirit boards, malu boards, ceremonial bowls. Museum-grade crating, climate indicators, acid-free tissue. These are not being stored. They are being hidden.*

Yara's thumb stopped on the page. *R. Okafor.* She knew that name from nowhere and everywhere—from the crew lists she'd memorized as a girl, from the memorial plaque nobody had ever funded. One of the six. One of the four whose bodies had never come out.

Her father had written on:

*R. says the families know. Which families? He won't say on paper. He says men from the company come through every quarter and take inventory, and they are not railway men. They wear good boots. Railway men do not wear good boots in the Star Mountains.*

*I counted today. Two hundred and fourteen crates in the upper gallery alone. If the lower gallery holds even half that—*

The entry stopped mid-sentence. The next page was torn out. Not damaged—torn, cleanly, at the gutter, by someone who wanted a specific record gone. Yara stared at the ragged stub of it and felt the temperature of the tent change. Someone had read this journal before her. Someone had curated it. Kessler had handed her a weapon with the safety still on—or so he believed.

She kept going.

* * *

The entries after the torn page were written at night, she could tell—the ink shifted to a different pen, the letters crowded against the ruled lines like men pressing against a door. Her father had started hiding the journal itself; there were notes about where (*under the floor of the survey hut, third board, the loose one, tell no one*) and notes about who he trusted, which was a short list getting shorter.

*Met with the elder's grandfather today — the one they call Waima now, though the company files call him by his baptismal name. He showed me where the objects came from. Not all from here. Some carried over the ranges decades ago, sold or stolen village by village while the rail was being pushed through. The railway wasn't built for copper. Copper doesn't need a tunnel this gauge. The railway was built to carry the river's memory out of the mountains and sell it. Every bridge, every spur, every cut — financed by men whose names are on hospital wings in Sydney and gallery walls in London.*

*And there is a ledger.*

Yara read the sentence three times. The lamp buzzed. Her hands had gone cold, and she noticed it the way you notice weather—as fact, distant, unimportant.

*A ledger. R. found it in the lower gallery, in a strongroom behind the flood door — the original door, not mine. Payment records. Dates, amounts, names. Bribes to district officers, to customs men, to two ministers whose signatures I would know anywhere, because their names are still spoken on the radio every morning as benefactors of the nation. The artifacts moved out under police escort. The payments moved back in under rail guard. Twelve years of it, itemized, in the company's own hand. They kept records, Yarita. Men who keep records intend to be forgiven later, not caught.*

*Waima's people have known for a generation. They watched their carvings go down the rails and they remembered every crate. What they never had was proof with names attached. Now it exists, in a flooded room behind a door I am supposed to seal.*

*The company has asked me to sign the closure order. Permanent seal. Structural failure, they will write. No recoverable cargo. I have until the end of the month.*

*If I sign, the ledger drowns and the families sleep well forever, and my daughter grows up thinking her father built a wall.*

*If I refuse, they will find another engineer, and they will find a way to make the refusal expensive. R. thinks they have already begun. Someone went through our crates at the staging camp. Someone asked the cook where I sleep.*

*There is a third option. The river. The lower gallery sits below the wet-season table; the old sluice gate on the upstream face still turns — I checked it myself, yesterday, with my own hands. If the sluice opens during high water, the gallery floods in minutes. Irrecoverable. Unsalvageable. The strongest room in the world becomes the safest room in the world, because no one can afford to dig for what the river holds.*

*But the flood will take the access drift with it. Anyone below the bulkhead when the sluice turns will not come out.*

*I am writing this at midnight and I cannot make my hand write the next part, so I will write it fast: they know I know. This morning there were new men at the portal, good boots, and R. did not come back from the lower gallery, and the company says he fell.*

*R. did not fall. R. was the best climber on the crew and he did not fall.*

*So. Decision made, or rather, the decision was made for me the day I opened Crate 57 and understood what I was holding. I will seal the galleries my own way — not their way. I will build the bulkhead to hold, and I will leave the sluice rusted half-open, just enough, so that one day the river finishes the job on its own schedule. And I will put the ledger where only the river can reach it, and I will tell my daughter nothing, because she is seventeen and stubborn and she would want to go in after it.*

*God forgive me. Tell no one. The river keeps what it keeps.*

The last entry was dated nine days before the explosion.

Yara sat very still for a long time.

The lamp burned its small yellow circle. Outside, the generator cycled down for the night and the camp sounds thinned to wind and water and Nia's distant laugh, one bright note in the dark gorge. Inside Yara's chest, twelve years of architecture was coming down—not collapsing, nothing so merciful as collapse, but dismantling, beam by beam, the way her father would have done it, carefully, with the load paths calculated in advance.

The accident had not been an accident. She had known that in her body since she was seventeen—had known it in the way the survivors' stories never quite matched, in the way the company settled fast and sealed faster, in the way her father's final demolition pattern at the portal had been too precise, too *engineered*, for a panic charge. But knowing a thing in your body and seeing it itemized in your father's handwriting were different countries, and she had just crossed the border.

He hadn't died sealing the tunnel to bury shame. He had died sealing it to hide evidence—and the evidence was still down there, behind his bulkhead, in the strongroom, wrapped in oilcloth and acid-free tissue, waiting. *Irrecoverable,* he'd written. *Unsalvageable.* Except that a man named Dario Kessler had arrived in the gorge with helicopters and contracts and a dewatering plant rated for exactly this kind of impossible recovery, and except that Kessler's money came, generation-laundered and foundation-washed, from the very names in her father's ledger.

They weren't reopening the Rivergate despite its history. They were reopening it *because* of it. The families had sent her father to seal the truth, and when he'd sealed it wrong—when he'd sealed it *alive*—they had waited a decade and sent Kessler to finish the job properly. Pump the water. Empty the room. Burn the ledger. Recover the merchandise, even—the crates were assets, after all; waste nothing, not even the evidence of the theft, if the theft could be rebranded as rescue.

And she had signed on to help them. She had negotiated terms. She had shaken the hand.

Yara became aware, gradually, that she was gripping the journal hard enough to crease the cover, and that her breath was doing something ragged, and that her eyes were wet—not grieving, exactly, though grief was in there somewhere beneath everything, vast and patient—but furious, a white-hot engineering-grade fury with load calculations of its own.

*Tell my daughter nothing, because she is seventeen and stubborn and she would want to go in after it.*

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty tent, to the dead man in the pages, to the river talking in the dark outside. "I'm not seventeen anymore. And I'm already in."

* * *

She read the rest of the night.

The final entries were hurried, almost shorthand—bulkhead dimensions, pour schedules, the exact compromise he'd built into the sluice gate (*rusted 40% open; looks like neglect; functions like intent*). On the last written page, wedged into the binding, was a brass survey tag stamped **E.V.**, identical to the ones she'd pocketed in the upper gallery, and beneath it, pressed flat, a strip of photographic negative. She held it up to the lamp.

Columns of figures. A date. A signature she recognized from the radio, from the newspapers, from the dedication plaques of schools—a name that was now, she realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the mountain air, connected to the shipping firm stenciled on the crates in Gallery One, and to the foundation whose logo rode on Kessler's helicopter tail.

One frame. One name. Enough to hang a legend, if anyone could get it out of the gorge alive.

Yara slid the negative back into the binding, wrapped the journal in its oilcloth, and did not hide it under a floorboard. She sewed it into the lining of her survey vest, against her ribs, where it rode like a second heartbeat. Then she stepped out into the blue hour before dawn.

Okonkwo was already awake, standing at the hydrology tent with a mug cooling forgotten in his hand, staring at his instrument readouts the way a man stares at a bad diagnosis.

"Doctor," she said. "You look like the river told you something rude."

"It moved," Okonkwo said, without turning. "The upstream gauge. Fourteen centimeters in six hours, and it's not rain—we've had none. Something above us is holding water back, Yara. Or someone is." He finally looked at her, and his face in the pre-dawn gloom was grave. "Your monsoon models. I need to show you the revised landfall window. We may have less time than anyone thinks."

Behind him, far up the black gorge, thunder rolled again—closer now, and closer, like a door being unlocked at the top of the stairs.
