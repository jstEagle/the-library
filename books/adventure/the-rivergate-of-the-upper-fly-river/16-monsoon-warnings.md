## 16. Monsoon Warnings

The rain came as a rumor before it came as a fact.

Okonkwo brought the rumor to Yara's tent at four in the morning, holding his laptop against his chest like a wounded animal, its screen the only light in the camp. She had been awake anyway—she was always awake at that hour now, lying in the dark listening to the pumps cycle through their rhythm below the portal, counting them like a heartbeat she didn't trust.

"Say it," she said, sitting up.

He set the laptop on her cot and turned it toward her. The model output glowed: a spaghetti tangle of projected tracks, every one of them bending toward the Gulf of Papua, every one of them earlier than the last run.

"The ridge of high pressure over the Coral Sea broke down three days ago," he said. "I've rerun it six times with six different datasets. It doesn't matter which I use. Landfall moves up." He tapped the screen. "Nine days. Maybe ten if we're lucky. The monsoon trough is going to make landfall on the twenty-third, not the second."

Yara looked at the date and felt something cold settle behind her sternum. "We had five weeks."

"We had five weeks yesterday."

She swung her legs out of the cot and found her boots in the dark without looking. Twelve years of camp life taught a body where its boots were. "Show Kessler."

"He's already awake. He's been awake since two." Okonkwo hesitated at the tent flap. "Yara—the margins you negotiated. The pump redundancy, the evacuation window, the stand-down triggers. If he's going to hold schedule, he's going to come for those first."

"I know."

"And Bea's already in his office."

That stopped her with one boot half-laced. "Doing what?"

"Showing him her dive maps. Arguing that the sluice channel can be surveyed faster if they run double shifts from tonight." Okonkwo's voice was carefully flat, the way it went when he'd decided a thing wasn't his to have an opinion about but wanted her to know he had one anyway. "She told him the water would give them nine days. She used your numbers."

*My numbers.* Yara finished lacing the boot. Outside, the camp was already stirring—generators coughing, the galley tent rattling, someone shouting for line handlers down at the barge landing. The Fly River ran black past the camp, swollen already with the season's first false starts, carrying whole islands of torn vegetation downstream like offerings.

She walked to Kessler's operations tent through a drizzle so fine it felt like the air itself had begun to sweat.

* * *

Kessler's command tent was a cathedral of screens and laminated charts, and he stood in the middle of it the way he stood everywhere—perfectly still, hands clasped behind his back, letting the room arrange itself around him. Two of his own engineers flanked the main table. Bea leaned over the flood-channel survey with a grease pencil in her hand, marking the sump line, and she didn't look up when Yara came in.

Okonkwo spread his printouts across the table without being asked. "Landfall moves to the twenty-third. Nine days, plus or minus thirty-six hours. Every scenario puts the storm surge into this reach of the gorge within twelve hours of landfall. The river here will rise six feet minimum. Possibly nine."

One of Kessler's engineers whistled low.

"So we accelerate," Kessler said.

"We *stop*," Yara said. "The safety margins I wrote into the contract exist for exactly this. Pump redundancy drops to one working unit if we push the primaries harder than eighty percent. The lower gallery stays sealed until the upper gallery is fully catalogued. And everyone is off the water by nightfall every day—no exceptions, no dive ops after dark."

Kessler turned to her slowly, and she watched him do the thing he did best: take bad news and find the angle where it became leverage. "Those margins were priced for a five-week window. You sold me five weeks, Yara. The river just took two of them."

"The river doesn't care what I sold you."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But my backers do. And my insurers. And the forty-one people on this payroll who go unpaid if this project slips into the wet season and drowns in its own mud." He put one finger on Okonkwo's printout, on the fat red arrow of the storm track. "Double shifts. Starting tonight. Day crew and night crew, twelve hours each. We finish the upper gallery catalogue in four days instead of eight, and we run the sluice survey concurrently."

"You can't run dive ops concurrently with heavy pumping," Bea said—not objecting, correcting, which was worse. "You can if you stagger them. Six hours of pumping, then a six-hour quiet window for the dive team. I've mapped it." She slid her grease-penciled chart toward the center of the table. "It works."

It worked. That was the terrible thing. Yara stared at her cousin's handwriting—Bea's blocky, impatient capitals—and saw a schedule that was aggressive and tight and, on paper, survivable. On paper. Paper didn't know what the lower gallery held. Paper didn't know what Yara had photographed there, or what Kessler intended to carry out of it.

"It works until the river disagrees," Yara said. "One flash flood in an upstream tributary during a quiet window and your dive team is inside a flooding tunnel."

"Then we watch the gauges," Bea said, finally looking up. Her eyes were red-rimmed; she'd been arguing with someone all night, though Yara couldn't tell whether it had been Kessler or herself. "We watch them the way you taught us. Gauge one at Kiunga bridge, gauge two at the D'Albertis junction, the staff gauge at the portal itself. Three readings an hour. Any rise of more than a foot between readings, we pull the divers. That's your protocol, Yara. I'm proposing we follow your protocol *faster*."

The tent was very quiet. Kessler watched the two of them the way a man watches a fire he's decided not to put out yet.

"Margins," Yara said finally. "The contract margins stay. All of them. Double shifts if you want—but the stand-down triggers stay, the pump redundancy stays, and the lower gallery door stays shut. Those aren't negotiable. They're the reason your crew is still alive."

Kessler smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was how she knew it meant nothing. "Done. Margins intact. Double shifts tonight." He gathered Bea's chart, squared its edges against the table. "Thank you both. Get some sleep. You'll want it."

Yara walked out into the drizzle and heard, behind her, Kessler say quietly to his engineers: "Move the artifact crating forward. Everything in the upper gallery gets packed before the catalogue is finished. I want it ready to load."

* * *

The camp transformed overnight into something with two hearts.

Day crew and night crew passed each other at shift change under floodlights, nodding, not speaking, too tired for talk. The generators never slept now; the diesel smell settled into everything—clothes, hair, the rice in the galley pot. Nia worked eighteen-hour days wiring the upper gallery for the survey lighting and came back to her tent smelling of solder and sweat, falling asleep mid-sentence whenever anyone tried to converse with her.

Tomas ran the day shift like a man holding a door shut against a crowd. Yara watched him from the portal staging area on the second morning, standing at the tunnel mouth with his tally board, checking crates in and out, and she saw him doing arithmetic with his lips moving. Counting hours. Counting crates. Counting, maybe, the things that were wrong.

She joined him at the board. "Talk to me."

"Night crew moved eleven crates to the crating shed last night," he said quietly. "Catalogue says seven were surveyed. Four crates went into that shed that nobody has opened, measured, or photographed." He kept his eyes on the tally board, his pencil moving. "I flagged it. Night foreman says it's a paperwork lag. Paperwork lag, at three in the morning, with no lights on in the shed."

"Kessler said pack everything."

"Kessler said pack everything *after* it's catalogued. That was the order I received." Tomas made a small mark, precise as a surgeon. "This isn't that order."

Yara looked down the track into the dark of the upper gallery, where the work lamps burned in a receding string of yellow stars. Somewhere down there were brass tags stamped with her father's initials, and sealed crates from a shipping firm owned by men whose names appeared—if her father's journal told the truth—in a ledger of bribes older than she was.

"Keep flagging it," she said. "In writing. Every discrepancy, dated, initialed. Build me a paper trail so thick that even Kessler can't walk through it without leaving footprints."

Tomas glanced at her sideways. "And when he notices the trail?"

"Then we'll know he's reading."

* * *

The fight with Bea happened on the fourth night, at the edge of the water, where the barge lamp threw a yellow circle onto the current.

Yara hadn't planned it. She'd gone down to check the mooring lines against the rising river and found Bea sitting on the barge rail in her wetsuit top, feet dangling over the black water, eating cold rice out of a tin. For a moment—just a moment—it was fifteen years ago and they were kids on their grandfather's dock, and Yara felt the old ache of affection so sharply it hurt.

"You argued for his schedule," Yara said.

Bea didn't turn around. "I argued for a schedule that keeps people alive. There's a difference."

"Is there? Because from where I stood, you handed him the justification to cut every rest interval I fought for."

"I handed him *your protocol*, run at speed, because the alternative was him improvising without it." Bea set down the tin and turned, and in the lamplight her face was all sharp planes and exhaustion. "You think I don't know what you're doing? You've been sandbagging this job since the portal. Slow surveys. Extra checks. Waiting for the monsoon to end it for you. I watched you do the same math Okonkwo does, except your model runs on hope instead of data."

"That's not—"

"You want the storm to close this tunnel again," Bea said. "You want it sealed the way your father sealed it, and this time you want the weather to sign the report so your hands stay clean. Well, the storm moved up nine days, Yara. Your weather isn't coming to save you. So somebody has to manage what actually happens, and apparently that somebody is me."

The river sucked at the pilings beneath them. Downstream, a floating log the size of a canoe ground against the barge hull and spun away into the dark.

"You don't know what's down there," Yara said. "In the lower gallery."

"Then tell me."

The words stood in the air between them, and Yara discovered she could not pick them up. Not here. Not with the floodlights of the crating shed burning fifty meters away, with Kessler's night foreman walking the bank with a flashlight, with the ledger pages folded in a waterproof bag sewn into the lining of her jacket like a second heart.

"Not here," Yara said. "Not yet."

Bea stood, picked up her fins, and slung them over her shoulder. "You know what the worst part is?" she said. "Twelve years ago, everybody protected everybody else by telling them nothing, and it got your father killed and our family broken. And you're still doing it. You've just gotten better at calling it leadership." She stepped off the barge onto the gangway. "When you're ready to trust someone, cousin, I'm easy to find. I'm the one diving into your father's flood channel on your schedule."

She walked up the bank toward the dive tent, and Yara stayed on the rail a long time, watching the river carry the light of the floodlamps away in long trembling ribbons.

* * *

On the fifth night, the river gave them its first warning, and nobody but Okonkwo seemed to hear it.

He came to her at midnight with the gauge log printed in his neat hand. "Staff gauge at the portal rose fourteen centimeters between the nineteen-hundred and twenty-two-hundred readings. No local rain. Nothing upstream in the tributaries above threshold." He laid the page on her table. "Fourteen centimeters out of a clear sky."

"The dam theory," Yara said. "Something upstream holding water back."

"Or releasing it. In pulses." He sat down heavily on her gear crate, and for once the hydrologist's composure looked thin. "Yara, I need to say something outside the contract. This mountain is not behaving like geology. It's behaving like a system under management. My models keep needing corrections that only make sense if the water table has structure—gates, channels, something built. Your father didn't just seal a tunnel. I think he built a machine, and I think it's still running."

Yara thought of the scratched message inside the lock door. *Flood it. Tell no one. The river keeps what it keeps.*

"Log it," she said. "Every anomaly, timestamped. And keep it out of the daily report."

Okonkwo studied her face for a long moment. "There's a great deal you're not telling me."

"Yes."

"And the margins Kessler agreed to keep?"

"Are the only thing keeping this crew alive," Yara said. "Which is why tomorrow, when he asks you to relax the stand-down trigger because the schedule slipped again—you're going to refuse. Loudly. In front of witnesses."

He almost smiled. "And if he fires me?"

"He won't. He needs your models. It's the one thing on this river he can't buy." She paused. "Yet."

* * *

The hook came on the sixth evening, in the form of a radio call from the Kiunga bridge gauge, relayed through camp in Kessler's flat, unhurried voice over the PA—a voice chosen, Yara realized, precisely because it made emergency sound like administration.

"All personnel, attention. Upstream gauge shows a sustained rise of eighteen inches over four hours. Projected crest at the portal in approximately nine hours. Night dive operations are cancelled. All other operations continue. Day crew reports at oh-six-hundred as scheduled."

Eighteen inches. A pulse. Something upstream had opened, or been opened, and the river was on its way down the gorge toward them with a message.

Yara found the camp already dividing itself along the fault line she'd been watching widen for days. Half the crew headed for the portal to sandbag the pump intakes—her people, Tomas's people, Nia still in her wiring harness with a shovel in her hand. The other half moved toward the crating shed, where Kessler's foremen were already rolling tarped pallets toward the barge landing under floodlights, loading cargo while the river rose.

At the center of it, halfway between the tunnel and the shed, stood Bea in her drysuit, dive bag over her shoulder, looking from the rising black water to the loading crews to Yara—as if measuring, for the first time, which side of the camp she was actually standing on.

The first fat drops of warm rain hit the dust around them as Yara started toward her.
