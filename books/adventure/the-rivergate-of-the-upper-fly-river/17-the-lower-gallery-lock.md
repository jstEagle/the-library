## 17. The Lower Gallery Lock

The door was the first thing her father had ever built that was meant to outlast him.

Yara had understood that even at seventeen, standing in the pump-smell dark of the upper gallery while he walked her through it: the steel plate cut from a decommissioned boiler car, the frame anchored into living rock with hand-set expansion bolts, the lock mechanism machined on a lathe in Port Moresby because nothing off the shelf would hold against what he called *determined men*. He'd run his palm along the welds the way other men touched their children's heads.

"Water's the only honest guard," he'd said. "But water can be pumped. So we put a door behind the water."

She'd asked him who he was keeping out. He'd looked at her for a long moment — she could still summon the exact quality of that look, lamplight and iron and something like apology — and said, "Everyone I couldn't buy."

Twelve years later, Kessler's men were cutting through it with an angle grinder, and the sound carried up the flooded gallery like a scream being filed down to a whisper.

* * *

Yara heard it from the survey tent at 0400, before she heard anyone moving toward it. Sparks travel in this country — word of them, anyway. The night crew's radio chatter had gone clipped and formal, which on Kessler's payroll meant someone important was awake and unhappy about it. She was out of her cot and across the compound before Tomas caught her arm at the edge of the floodlights.

"You knew," he said. Not a question. His jaw was tight, and there was plaster dust on his sleeve from a shift he'd worked that wasn't his.

"I suspected." That was true. For three days Kessler's engineers had been running core samples along the bulkhead line, ostensibly checking integrity, actually mapping the door's thickness to the centimeter. Nia had shown her the drill logs without being asked, her face carefully blank. "I didn't know it would be tonight."

"Does it matter? You told him no one goes below the lock. He agreed. In writing."

"He agrees to everything," Yara said. "That's how you know the agreement means nothing."

Tomas held on a second longer, then let go. Twelve years of working together had taught him which fights to have now and which to have later, and this one, with the grinder already singing through two hundred meters of tunnel, was going to be lost either way. He fell into step beside her instead, and they walked down into the gallery together, past the dewatering pumps breathing their wet rhythm, past the crates stacked under tarps like coffins waiting for names, down to where the light went from workmanlike yellow to the gray of a place that hadn't been seen by human eyes since the river took it back.

* * *

There were six of Kessler's men at the face of it, plus Voss, the site manager Kessler had installed like a tumor. The grinder operator was a big Solomon Islander named Etu whom Yara had rated highly during hiring and now watched with a bitter recalculation. Sparks sheeted off the steel plate and died in the standing water at their boots. The plate had gone from silver to a hot orange crescent, and through the crescent's edge she could see the first pale thread of daylight on the far side — no, not daylight. Lamplight. Someone was already through a secondary breach, or the door was thinner than her father had wanted it to be, or —

"Stop," Yara said.

Voss turned. He was a tidy man, compact, with the patient expression of someone who had been paid to be patient in many unpleasant places. "Ms. Vasquez. It's late for you to be up."

"It's my name on the safety plan for this gallery. Every man standing in water next to live electrical equipment is in violation of it. So is whoever's cutting a pressure-sealed door without shoring the bulkhead behind it." She kept her voice level, almost bored, the voice she used for arguing freight rates. "If that wall lets go, the surge takes out the pump line, the pump line goes down, and the whole upper gallery refills in forty minutes with your men inside it. I've done the math. Have you?"

Voss glanced at the water lapping at their boots, and she saw the arithmetic land behind his eyes — not fear exactly, but the professional discomfort of a man who had been handed a schedule by someone who didn't have to stand in the flood zone himself. "Mr. Kessler authorized—"

"Kessler authorizes from a helicopter. You're the one who'll be explaining to Etu's family why the river has him." She let that sit. Then, quieter, so only Voss could hear it over the grinder: "You want the door open. Fine. But you do it my way, or you explain to Dario why his timeline just slipped a week while we re-pump the gallery."

The grinder died. In the sudden quiet the tunnel made its own sounds — water moving somewhere it shouldn't, the pumps' arrhythmia, the deep structural groan of rock adjusting to being dry again after twelve years of drowning. Okonkwo had warned her about that groan. *A tunnel doesn't forgive being opened,* he'd said. *It renegotiates.*

Voss consulted a satellite phone he pretended wasn't a leash, got whatever answer leashes give, and nodded once. "Your way. Dawn. Full shoring."

"Dawn," Yara agreed, and hated herself for the relief in it, because dawn meant hours, and hours meant she still had time to decide what she was going to do when the thing her father had died to close finally stood open in front of her.

* * *

They spent those hours the way her crew did everything — precisely, and without talking about why.

Nia set the shoring herself, refusing help, checking each acrow prop with a torque wrench and then checking it again. Bea ran the water levels twice and brought Yara the numbers without comment, though the numbers themselves were a comment: the sump below the lock was dropping slower than the pumps should allow, as if something downstream were holding the water the way a fist holds it. Okonkwo stood at the bulkhead for a long time with his instruments and finally said, to no one in particular, "This isn't seepage. This is architecture. Your father didn't just build a door. He built a valve."

Yara wrote that down and didn't answer.

It was Tomas who found the secondary breach. He came back from scouting the old maintenance adit above the gallery with dust on his knees and an expression she'd learned to read as bad news delivered early. "There's a crawlway. Collapsed at both ends years ago, but someone's reamed it out recently — fresh tool marks, modern anchors. Small man, maybe two. They've already been through to the other side of the door."

"How recently?"

"The anchor epoxy's still tacky."

So Kessler hadn't waited for the grinder at all. The grinding was theater, a legal-looking breach for the record, while his real men went through the body of the mountain like worms through fruit. Yara stood in the lamplight at the top of the adit and felt the trap's shape with perfect clarity: whatever was beyond the lock, Kessler would have hands on it within the hour, and every argument she'd won tonight had only bought her a front-row seat.

Unless she went first.

* * *

The crawlway was exactly wide enough for her and too narrow for her fear, which traveled with her anyway, pressing against her spine in the dark. She went in alone. She told herself it was reconnaissance; she told herself she'd report everything to the crew; underneath both stories, older and truer, ran the single sentence she'd been carrying since the map: *sealed* — red ink, her father's hand — and the question that had lived inside the word for twelve years like a seed inside stone.

What did he find?

The crawlway dropped, kinked left, dropped again. Her headlamp found tool marks in the rock — recent, yes, but beneath them, older ones, faded, oxidized. Her breath stopped in her throat. She knew those marks. She'd grown up watching them made. A chisel struck at a shallow angle, a surveyor's habit, economical. Her father had cut this crawlway himself, or widened it, years ago — before the expedition, maybe, or during it. Before the end.

And near the bottom, where the crawlway opened into the shadow of the great door's back face, the marks changed character. These weren't cuts. They were letters.

He'd written on the steel itself, scratched deep with something harder than hope, in the cramped block capitals of a man who expected his handwriting to be read by strangers:

**FLOOD IT. TELL NO ONE. THE RIVER KEEPS WHAT IT KEEPS.**

Below that, smaller, angled into the corner where only someone lying flat in the crawlway could see it — someone her size, someone seventeen, someone who might one day come looking:

**YARA. NOT YOU.**

She lay there a long time with the grit of the tunnel against her cheek and the lamp shaking slightly in her hand, reading four words until they stopped being words and became the last hour of his life, spoken backward across twelve years of silence. *Not you.* As if he'd known she'd follow him here someday. As if the message had two audiences, the world and his daughter, and the second one mattered more.

Flood it. Tell no one.

Her father had never in his life given an order she hadn't argued with, and this one — this one he'd carved into steel rather than say aloud, because he'd known she'd argue with it too.

Behind the door, faintly, she heard voices. Kessler's men, through the breach, already inside. Already among whatever it was. The lock's tumbler mechanism, visible from this side through the gap the crawlers had left, hung open like a jaw.

All she had to do was go back up the crawlway, gather her crew, and tell Kessler the door was compromised. All she had to do was obey the last order her father ever gave her.

Instead she put her shoulder to the inner hatch — a maintenance panel her father had built, she realized, with the same care as the rest, a panel sized for one person, a panel that had never been sealed shut — and slid through into the dark on the other side of twelve years.

* * *

The lower gallery breathed cold.

Her lamp found rails first — the same gauge as the main line, running straight into blackness, polished bright along the crown by decades of water and untouched by rust, as if the tunnel had been keeping them ready. Then the walls: concrete-faced, ribbed with steel, built to a standard the railway had never used anywhere else on the spur. This wasn't storage. This was a vault that had learned to pretend it was a tunnel.

And then, fifty meters in, stacked in ranks along both walls with German precision, the crates.

They were unlike anything in the upper gallery. No shipping stencils, no manifests, no company markings at all — just small brass plates, hand-engraved with catalog numbers, and a wax seal pressed into each lid. Museum-grade. Archive-grade. The kind of packing that exists to convince the future that whatever's inside deserves to survive it.

One crate stood apart from the others, nearer the door, its lid half lifted, a flashlight lying beside it on the rail bed — dropped, not set down. Kessler's crawler had started here and moved deeper. Beside the crate, wedged upright against the wall where someone had placed it deliberately, was a steel document case, military green, its latch broken.

Yara knelt. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside the case: oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth, a ledger bound in canvas, its pages dense with columns of figures, dates, initials, and — when she turned to the middle sections with the care of someone defusing something — names. Names she knew. Names on hospital wings and mining concessions and the letterhead of the foundation that funded Kessler's expeditions. Beside each name, amounts. Beside each amount, a route number. Route numbers on the Upper Fly spurs. Her spurs. The lines her crews had rerailed and maintained for a decade, carrying, all this time, whatever these families had needed carried.

The railway hadn't been built to serve the mines. The mines had been built to serve the railway.

She photographed three pages with the small camera in her chest pocket, hands finally unsteady now, shutter clicks enormous in the cold air. Then she heard footsteps returning from the deep end of the gallery — unhurried, confident, more than one pair — and she made the choice that would define everything after: she closed the case, replaced it exactly as it lay, lifted the crate lid back to its half-open angle, and stepped into the shadow behind the door mechanism as the lights swung toward her.

Through the gap between crate and wall she watched Kessler's men reach the document case. Watched the tall one open it, flip pages, and laugh — a short, disbelieving sound. Watched him photograph it with a phone and say, clearly enough to carry: "He wasn't lying. The old man kept receipts."

Then the tall one's radio crackled, and a voice Yara knew said, "Bring it up. All of it. And find out if anyone else has been down there."

The lights swept toward the door. Toward the shadow. Toward her.

Yara pressed herself flat against the cold steel of her father's lock, four feet of machined iron between her body and the men coming to collect the truth, and in the darkness she mouthed the words scratched on the other side of it, feeling them like a hand on her shoulder:

*Tell no one.*

Too late, Dad, she thought. And also — not yet.
