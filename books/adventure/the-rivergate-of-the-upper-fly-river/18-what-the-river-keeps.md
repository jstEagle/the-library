## 18. What the River Keeps

The door her father built hung open like a wound.

Yara stood before it for a long time before she went through. Behind her, the upper gallery still smelled of diesel and cut concrete—Kessler's men had breached the lock at dawn with a thermal lance and a hydraulic spreader, and they'd left the corridor lit with work lamps strung on cable, humming their flat electric hum. Ahead of her, past the ruined door, there was no light at all.

She clipped her headlamp to its highest setting and stepped over the threshold.

The air changed immediately. Twelve years of standing water had moved through this space and then been pumped back out of it, and what remained was a smell she knew from her childhood—from her father's gear shed, from the inside of his survey cases: cold stone, old iron, and something mineral and sweet, like rain on hot slate. The floor was slick with a skin of silt that gave under her boots like wet flour. Her lamp beam cut a cone through air so still it felt held, as if the whole gallery were waiting to see what she'd do.

The passage ran straight for thirty meters, then opened.

She had expected the lower gallery to be a tunnel. It was not. Her father's survey maps—the ones she'd studied until the ink wore soft in her memory—showed a chamber here, but the maps had never conveyed scale. The space was a cathedral of poured concrete and living rock, ribbed with steel arches, wide enough that her headlamp couldn't find the far wall on first sweep. Water had reached the crown of the arches once; a dark tide line ran around the entire circumference like a ring on a bathtub, and below it everything was furred with pale silt, everything softened, everything made ghostly.

And it was full.

Crates, yes—she'd known about the crates since Nia pried one open in the upper gallery against orders, since Yara had ordered it resealed and lain awake that night staring at the tent roof. But the crates in the upper gallery had been shipping stock: museum-grade packing, foam cutouts, inventory tags. This was different. This was organized like someone's mind.

Racking ran along both walls, steel shelving bolted into the rock, and every shelf was labeled. Not with shipping codes—with handwriting. She recognized it before she was close enough to read it, the way you recognize a voice in a dark room. Her father's hand. Blocky capitals, drafted with a surveyor's precision: *OBJETS — PANELS — STONE. TEXTILES — DRY ONLY. DOCUMENTS — SEALED. DO NOT OPEN BELOW WATERLINE.*

He had catalogued all of it. He had come down here, in whatever days remained to him, and he had built an archive.

Yara walked the racks slowly, her boots printing the silt. Ceremonial stools wrapped in waxed cloth. Carved house panels stacked upright in padded frames, their faces turned to the wall as if in shame or modesty. Drums. A whole shelf of them, slung in netting, their skins long since gone to leather and rot. Stone figures the size of children, each one cradled in a wooden cradle someone had built by hand. She counted without meaning to. Forty-one. Forty-two. She stopped counting when the numbers started to feel like a body count.

These weren't crates in transit. These were things that had been taken apart from the places that made them mean something, and her father had found them and could not move them and could not leave them, so he had done the only thing left to him. He had kept them. He had sealed the door and drowned the room and become, in the last act of his life, a curator of stolen goods—a jailer of other people's ancestors.

Her throat worked. She stood in front of the drums for a while and let the lamp shake.

* * *

It was the far rack that stopped her heart.

The shelving there was different—not steel but timber, rough-sawn, newer than the rest, and the labels weren't her father's drafting capitals. They were typed. Small cards in plastic sleeves, the kind used for field evidence, and Yara knew that kind of card because she had signed hundreds of them at seventeen, because the survey expedition had run on them, because she had spent one terrible season of her life writing *CHAIN OF CUSTODY* in block letters until the words lost all meaning.

DOCUMENTS — FINANCIAL. BOX 1 OF 6.

DOCUMENTS — CORRESPONDENCE. BOX 2 OF 6.

PHOTOGRAPHS — SITE AND PERSONNEL. BOX 3 OF 6.

Six boxes. Sealed with tape, taped over with more tape, wrapped in plastic sheeting tied with cord, and beneath the plastic she could see the edges of ring binders, of manila folders, of the fat grey government-issue ledger books that the colonial railway administration had used for accounts and that had outlived the administration itself.

Her father hadn't just hidden the artifacts. He had hidden the proof.

She thought of the argument—twelve years ago, the night before the blast, her father's voice low and furious in the survey tent while she pretended to sleep: *You don't understand what I'm holding. You don't understand who will come for it.* She had thought he meant the artifacts. She had thought he meant the objects themselves, the beautiful stolen things, and that his fury was a curator's possessiveness. Now, standing in front of six plastic-wrapped boxes in a drowned room, she understood that he had meant this. Papers. Names. Numbers. The machinery behind the beauty—the bribes, the shipping manifests, the ministers and magnates whose family names now sat on hospital wings and university buildings and the letterhead of the Kessler Recovery Group.

*The families sent Kessler,* the scout on the ridge had said. *The families sent your father. Ask him what he found.*

He had found this. And they had killed him for finding it, and called it an accident, and made his seventeen-year-old daughter sign the report.

Yara's hands were steady. That surprised her. She had expected them to shake; instead they moved with the same weary competence she brought to rerailing washed-out track, untying the cord, peeling back the plastic, lifting the lid of Box 1. Inside, ring binders stood shoulder to shoulder, their spines labeled in faded type. She pulled the first one out and opened it on her knee, crouching in the silt, and the pages fell open to a column of figures in fountain-pen brown:

*Received, R. Kessler Holdings Ltd., via Port Moresby account — payment re: consignment 14/7 — freight & discretion —*

A name. A date. An amount large enough, in that year, to buy the whole spur line twice over. She turned the page. Another name, another account, another consignment. Page after page after page, years of them, a river of money flowing one direction and a river of objects flowing the other, and every crossing point signed by someone whose descendants now funded helicopters and salvage crews and the reopening of flooded tunnels.

She understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, why Kessler wanted the lower gallery. Not for the panels. Not for the drums. Anyone could buy those on the open market from a dozen private collections. He wanted this room because this room could end him—and the families behind him—in a single afternoon of testimony.

And he wanted her father's journal because the journal would tell him whether this room existed, and where, and how well it was protected.

He had agreed too easily to her term. *No one enters the lower gallery.* He had flattered her grief and given her command of the crew and let her set boundaries, because boundaries told him exactly where the treasure was buried. She had drawn a map to her own father's grave and handed it to the man who'd profited from digging it.

Yara closed the binder. Sat back on her heels in the dark. Listened to the silence—the deep, pressurized silence of a room that had been underwater for twelve years and remembered it.

Then she took out her phone.

* * *

She worked methodically, because method was all she had. The gallery was beyond cell range—of course it was—but the camera didn't need a signal. She checked the battery: seventy percent. She checked her spare power bank in her jacket pocket: full. She had come down here planning to photograph nothing, telling herself she only wanted to see, only needed to stand where her father had stood, and she had brought two batteries anyway. Some part of her had decided before she did.

Box 1 first. Every page of the primary ledger, photographed flat on her knee, the lamp angled to kill glare off the plastic sleeves. The pages were damp-warped at the edges but legible, the ink preserved by the cold and the dark, and she shot them in pairs, then singles, checking focus on each frame, her breath fogging faintly in the lamplight. Consignments. Dates. Accounts in three countries. Marginal notes in a second hand—smaller, fussier—that she suspected belonged to a shipping clerk who had kept honest records inside a dishonest enterprise, perhaps the only honest man in the whole operation, recording theft with perfect bookkeeping accuracy.

Box 2: correspondence. Letters on ministry letterhead. Telegrams. One typewritten memo, its carbon copy still sharp, discussing "the relocation of sensitive cultural materials pending the completion of the road program," signed by a name she knew from the news, from a scholarship fund, from a portrait in a bank lobby. She photographed it three times.

Box 3: photographs. Black-and-white glossies in paper sleeves. Men in suits standing beside open crates on a rail platform, smiling, hands in pockets, the carved face of a spirit board visible between them like a hostage. She photographed the photographs. Somewhere in the stack she saw a younger face she half-recognized—a much younger Dario Kessler, smooth-cheeked, standing at the edge of the frame, not smiling, watching the crates with an expression she recognized because she'd seen it in mirrors: a person calculating what a thing is worth and what it will cost.

Her battery hit forty percent. She swapped packs without stopping, the way her crew changed drill bits, no ceremony, no pause.

By the time she finished Boxes 4 and 5—site surveys, personnel rosters, pay records for men listed only by initials—her knees ached and the cold had gotten into her shoulders. Box 6 was the smallest. Inside it, wrapped separately in oilcloth, was a folder she knew on sight, because she had spent twelve years believing it had burned in the explosion.

Her father's original survey journal. The real one—the one Kessler claimed to hold, the one he had dangled in front of her like bait. She lifted it out with both hands. The cover was swollen with water damage, the spine cracked, but the pages inside were intact, protected by the oilcloth, protected by the flood her father had planned down to the waterline.

Kessler's copy was a fake. Or a partial. Or a transcription bought from whoever had killed her father and searched the body. It didn't matter. She was holding the true one, and Kessler didn't know it, and that knowledge sat in her chest like a loaded charge—dangerous, and hers alone to place.

She photographed the journal too. Every page. The maps, the marginalia, the final entries in a hand grown shakier as the weeks went on, and then the last entry, which she already knew by heart from the scratched message inside the lock door, but which hit her differently here, in the room itself:

*Flood it. Tell no one. The river keeps what it keeps.*

She had always read that as despair. Sitting in the archive he had died to build, she read it differently. Not despair. Instruction. The river keeps what it keeps—meaning: some things are safer drowned than owned. Meaning: don't let them have it. Meaning, maybe: *I'm sorry, Yara. I'm leaving you the hard part.*

She repacked the boxes exactly as she'd found them—cord, plastic, tape, lids, the works—measuring herself against her own memory of how they'd sat. She wiped her boot prints from the silt in front of the racks with a flattened piece of cardboard, absurdly, uselessly, knowing the breach team would trample through tomorrow regardless. Old habits. You leave a site the way you found it. Her father's rule. She was beginning to suspect nearly every rule she lived by was his, and that he had never meant for her to carry them this far.

At the threshold she stopped and looked back once. Her lamp found the tide line ringing the walls, the pale shelves, the dark shapes of the drums and the stones and the boxed-up dead paperwork of a hundred guilty men. Then she killed the light and stood in the absolute black for ten seconds, letting the room be a room again instead of a crime scene, and walked out.

* * *

Above, the upper gallery was all noise and motion. Kessler's technicians had strung new cable, and a generator throbbed somewhere, and two of his men were wrestling a dewatering hose toward the shaft, shouting over the racket. Nobody looked at her. Nobody had looked at her going down, either—they assumed she was inspecting the breach, doing her job, the grieving daughter keeping professional distance from the gallery she'd sworn off. The best hiding place in the world was a reputation.

Tomas found her at the portal, squinting against the afternoon light, the gorge blazing gold and green below.

"You were down there a long time," he said. Not accusatory. Tomas never accused; he observed, and his observations landed heavier than accusations.

"Structural assessment." She heard her own voice come out level, bored, and marveled at it. "The racking's failing. If we pump the lower gallery dry, half those shelves come down."

"Kessler wants a full inventory by Friday."

"Kessler can want." She checked her watch, made herself look at the schedule board, made herself say something about hose pressure and shift rotations. Tomas watched her the whole time with those patient river-pilot eyes, and she knew—he knew *something*, the way he always knew something—but he asked nothing, because that was their contract, twelve years old and never written down: he hauled her out of the water when she went under, and he didn't ask what she'd seen down there.

Her phone sat against her ribs in the inner pocket, warm from the work. Two hundred and eleven photographs. Six boxes. One journal. Enough to hang a dozen respected names, enough to burn Kessler's empire to the waterline, enough to finish what her father started—or to get every person in this camp killed, depending entirely on who learned of it first and when.

She had told no one. She would tell no one. Not yet. The secret was hers now, the way the debt was hers, the way the map was hers, the way the red-inked word *sealed* had been hers since she was seventeen. Some inheritances you choose. This one had chosen her, and all she could decide was what to do with it, and how fast, and whether anyone she loved would still be standing when it went off.

Below the portal, far down the gorge, she heard the barge horn—two short blasts, the signal Bea used coming upriver from the sump survey. Rising water, the message meant. Channels changing. Bea would want to talk dive plans tonight, would stand across the campfire with her arms crossed and her jaw set, wanting numbers Yara wasn't sure she could give honestly anymore.

Yara touched the pocket where the phone lay, once, lightly, the way you touch a wound to confirm it's real.

"Come on," she said to Tomas. "Before Kessler invents another deadline."

They started down toward camp, and behind them the lower gallery settled back into its twelve-year silence, keeping what it kept.
