## 19. Bea's Dive

The sump was exactly where Okonkwo's models said it would be, which was the first thing that should have worried her.

Bea Vasquez had been diving flooded workings since she was nineteen—coal adits in the highlands, tin shafts down south, once a drowned cathedral crypt in the old mission district where the water was so cold it felt like being forgiven. She knew the grammar of underground water the way Yara knew rails: where it pooled, where it lied, where it waited. And everything about this sump told her it had no business existing.

"Say it again," she said, crouched at the lip of the flooded gallery, running her light across the black water. It lay flat and patient as poured glass.

"Flow rate's near zero," Okonkwo said behind her. He crouched beside her, tablet balanced on his knee, stylus tapping the cross-section he'd built from the survey data. "That's what I keep saying. A sump at this elevation, with the river table where it is, should be moving. Should be breathing with the river. This one isn't. It's held."

"Held by what?"

"That," he said, "is why you're going in."

Behind them, the pump train chugged its slow rhythm up the spur, hauling water out of the upper gallery in steel tanks. Kessler's operation had doubled shifts after the monsoon forecast moved up nine days, and the whole camp ran now on a clock nobody said aloud: get the crates out before the sky opened. Kessler himself had signed off on Bea's dive plan without reading it—she'd watched his eyes slide down the page like a man skimming a menu—and that, too, should have worried her.

Yara had read every line twice.

Her cousin stood apart from the dive site, arms crossed, wearing the expression she'd worn since the funeral: not anger, exactly. Inventory. As if Bea were a crate whose manifest didn't match its contents.

"Line's rigged," Bea said. "Two hundred meters of guide to the far wall, then I'm through the breach into the flood channel. Map it, tag it, come back. Forty minutes bottom time."

"Thirty-five," Yara said.

"It's my dive."

"And it's my protocol." Yara came closer, boots grinding on the crushed ballast. "Thirty-five minutes. If you're not back on the line at thirty, I send Nia's crew to blow the emergency relief and flood the channel behind you."

"You'd drown me to save me."

"I'd close the door so nothing follows you." Yara said it flatly, without drama, and that was the worst part—the absolute calm of it, the certainty of a woman who had already done this arithmetic once, twelve years ago, with their father's name in the equation. "Thirty-five minutes, Bea."

Bea looked at her for a long moment. Twelve years of silence sat between them, and it had learned to disguise itself as professionalism.

"Thirty-five," she agreed.

* * *

The water took her the way deep water always does—not violently but completely, a hand closing over the whole world.

At three meters the daylight died. At eight, her light found the guide line and she followed it down into the throat of the old flood channel, kicking smooth and slow, conserving air the way her father had taught her before she could spell regulator. *Breathe like you're paying for it,* he used to say, laughing, his big hands checking her straps twice over. *Because you are.*

She tried not to think about him. She almost never managed it down here.

The channel was a rail tunnel once—one of the Rivergate's lower arteries, drowned when her father pulled the charges that sealed the complex. The blast had collapsed sections of roof, and the rubble made a broken staircase of concrete and twisted rail descending into deeper dark. Her light played over it: rebar like exposed ribs, a section of tunnel wall still bearing the faded stencil of a shipping company, silt lying undisturbed in gray drifts.

Undisturbed. That was the thing she kept noticing. No current lines in the silt. No debris fans. Water that hadn't moved in years, maybe decades, sitting in a channel that connected—according to every drawing—to a living river system.

Held. Okonkwo's word followed her down.

At sixty meters in, the channel narrowed where a roof fall had half-blocked it, leaving a gap barely wide enough for a diver with tanks. Bea checked her gauge—still fat, still generous—slipped her reel off her harness, clipped it, and eased through sideways, exhaling to shrink herself, feeling the rock press against her tank like a hand testing her resolve.

On the far side, the channel opened again, and her light found something that stopped her heart for one full cycle.

A gate.

It stood across the tunnel like a portcullis, steel, man-height, crusted with orange rust and pale mineral bloom—but intact. Not blast-damaged. Not collapsed. Deliberately set, bolted into channels cut into the rock, with a wheel mechanism at its center and—her light steadied as her hands refused to—a brass plate bolted to the frame, green with age, stamped with letters she could read even through the murk:

**RIVERGATE FLOOD CONTROL — SLUICE 3 — VASQUEZ SURVEY AUTHORITY**

Their father's name. Their father's authority. A sluice gate, built into the flood channel, hidden below the level anyone had ever surveyed—which meant he hadn't just blown the tunnels closed. He'd built a way to open them again. A way to let the river in, on purpose, whenever someone decided it needed to come in.

Bea hung in the black water, bubbles ticking upward past her mask, and understood two things at once, the way divers understand things—in the body first, the mind after.

The first: her father had planned this. Whatever happened twelve years ago, whatever the explosion was really for, he had left a door in it.

The second: the water wasn't holding itself back. *He* was holding it back. The gate was shut, and the sump above was the river pressing its palm flat against the other side, waiting.

She was still staring at the plate when the channel groaned.

* * *

Later—much later, in the medical tent, wrapped in blankets with her ears ringing—Bea would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. Memory underwater doesn't file itself in order; it files itself in weight.

What she kept, always, was the sound. A low boom transmitted through the rock, felt in her teeth and chest before her ears caught it, somewhere upstream in the gallery complex. Then the silt around her lifted off the floor all at once, a gray blizzard erupting from stillness, and her visibility went from ten meters to nothing in under two seconds.

Then the line went slack.

Not cut—slack. Somewhere behind her, in the blizzard, the guide line had snagged or parted or been buried under shifting rubble, and when she reached for it her glove found only water. She swam a meter along where it should have been. Another. Nothing. The reel on the blockage behind her was a memory in a whiteout.

Okay. Okay okay okay. Training. Father's voice: *When you lose the line, you stop. You don't hunt. You anchor, you breathe, you think.* She found a stub of rebar, clipped herself to it, killed her light to save the battery and because there was nothing to see anyway, and hung in the total dark of a drowned tunnel while the silt snowed down around her like ash.

Her gauge read eleven hundred psi. Enough for forty minutes of calm breathing. She was not calm. Calm was a currency and she could feel herself spending it.

The boom came again—closer, or bigger, hard to tell through stone—and the water moved.

That was the horror of it, the thing she would never fully be able to explain to people who hadn't dived. Water in a tunnel doesn't surge like a wave. It *decides*. A pressure wave rolled through the channel, gentle and enormous, tugging her clip, dragging silt past her like a river remembering its job, and Bea understood with perfect clarity that the gate she'd found—or another like it, or the collapse itself—had shifted, and the held water was beginning, at last, to move.

And it was moving toward the low side. Toward the sump. Toward the way she'd come in, which meant the gap in the roof fall behind her was about to become a drain, and she was anchored fifty meters downstream of it with a finite bottle and no line.

She unclipped.

It was the wrong call by every protocol she'd ever been taught, and she made it anyway, because the alternative was to be pinned here when the flow arrived. She swam blind into the whiteout, following the channel wall by touch, right hand trailing the rough concrete, counting kicks because counting was all she had. Two hundred. Four hundred. Her gauge bled down through nine hundred. The current strengthened against her face—no, wait, that was wrong, if the water drained toward the sump and she was swimming toward the sump the current should be at her *back*—

Unless the flow had found another path. Unless the pressure wave was oscillating, the whole drowned complex breathing in and out like something waking up.

Six hundred psi. The math turned ugly. Her reserve whistle would give her perhaps five minutes of screaming alarm at the surface, if there was a surface, if the sump wasn't already a churning drain full of her own name.

Then her hand left the wall.

The wall simply ended—a junction, a side chamber, the channel branching in the dark—and her momentum carried her into open water, tumbling, disoriented, and when she righted herself she couldn't find the wall again in any direction. She hung in the void with six hundred pounds of pressure behind her teeth and no floor, no ceiling, no line, no light worth the name, and the panic rose in her like floodwater, fast and total, and she heard herself make a sound into her regulator that was not words.

*Breathe like you're paying for it.*

She breathed. It cost everything she had.

* * *

Yara broke her own protocol at minute twenty-eight.

Bea learned this afterward, pieced together from Nia's account and Tomas's and finally, grudgingly, Yara's own version, each telling different in the way that truths are different from each other. What Nia saw: at minute twenty-eight, with no beacon signal and no line movement, Yara ordered the standby diver kitted. At minute thirty-one—the threshold, the number they'd agreed on—Nia's crew stood ready at the relief charge, and Yara looked at the plunger and did not give the order. At minute thirty-three, with Okonkwo shouting about pressure transients in the channel and the pumps registering a surge, Yara stripped off her own outer gear, took a spare bottle and a torch, and went into the sump alone, unqualified for channel diving, unbriefed, breaking every rule she had written for this expedition and most of the ones she'd written for her life.

Tomas tried to stop her. She told him later, in the flat voice she used for facts too large to decorate: "I sealed her in once already. I'm not doing it twice."

What Bea knew, from inside the dark, was simpler.

A light bloomed out of the whiteout—her light, dying, had been a rumor of yellow, and this was white and hard and coming fast—and then hands. Hands on her harness, sure and furious, checking her gauge, finding her mouthpiece seated, doing in four seconds the full buddy check their father had drilled into both of them at ages when other children were learning bicycle safety. Then Yara's face, inches away in the beam, mask fogged, eyes wide and blazing above the regulator, and she pressed her backup octopus into Bea's hands and pointed up and *pulled*, and they swam together through the settling blizzard, two bodies on shared air, following a route Yara could not possibly have known except that she'd spent twelve years keeping her father's maps in her head whether she admitted it or not.

They surfaced in the sump chamber to shouting and lights and Nia's voice cracking on a word that might have been prayer or profanity, and Bea coughed out half the Fly River and heard her cousin, hoarse, saying her name over and over like a man working a pump handle—*Bea, Bea, breathe, Bea*—and thought, distantly, through the ringing and the shivering: *So that's what it takes. Nearly dying. Good to know.*

* * *

They put her in the medical tent with a blanket and a thermos of terrible sweet tea, and the whole camp drifted past the flap pretending to have errands. Okonkwo checked her ears twice. Nia sat with her a while and said nothing, which from Nia was a eulogy-grade honor. Even Kessler sent a man with a bottle of whisky and a message about how valuable she was to the operation, which Bea accepted with a smile she practiced in the reflective surface of her tea.

Yara came last, after midnight, when the generators had thinned to a drone and the rain had started—fat, warm, preliminary drops, the monsoon clearing its throat.

She stood at the tent flap like a woman reporting for discipline.

"You broke protocol," Bea said.

"You were at thirty-one minutes."

"The protocol said thirty."

"The protocol assumed the channel was stable." Yara came in and sat on the supply crate across from her, elbows on knees, and for a moment she looked less like the woman who ran crews and settled debts and more like the girl who'd stood at the funeral with her jaw set so tight Bea had wanted to shake her. "Okonkwo thinks the pressure transient was the gate shifting. The one you found."

"You know about the gate." It wasn't a question. Bea had watched her cousin's face when she'd given her report, half-drowned and shaking, and seen the flicker—there and gone—of a person hearing confirmation of something long suspected. "You've known. About Dad. About what he built down there."

"I knew he was more careful than the official story allowed." Yara rubbed her face with both hands. When she spoke again, her voice was lower. "I signed the closure report, Bea. I was seventeen. They handed it to me at the hospital and told me it was what he would have wanted, and I believed them because believing them hurt less than the alternative." She looked up. "I've never said that out loud. Not to anyone."

The rain thickened on the canvas. Bea held her tea and felt twelve years of silence shift its weight, the way the water in the channel had shifted—one boom, and everything that lay still starts to move.

"Why did you take his side?" Yara asked. "Kessler's. The schedule, the double shifts, all of it. You've been pushing this operation like you're paid by the hour of danger."

And there it was—the question Bea had been carrying since the helicopter pad, since before, since the funeral, and she found that nearly drowning had burned away whatever she'd planned to say instead of the truth.

"Because you don't talk," Bea said. "Twelve years, Yara. You'd take a contract in a war zone before you'd take a phone call about Dad. I sided with Kessler because it was the only way I could think of to make you angry enough to say *anything*. And then today—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, and let it—"today I nearly died in his tunnel, and you came in after me, and you broke your own rules, and I realized I've been punishing the wrong person. You've been standing guard over that report your whole life. Somebody should have relieved you years ago."

Yara stared at her. In the lamplight her eyes were wet, and she didn't wipe them, and she didn't look away, and Bea understood that this—this exact moment, unarmored, unfixed—was the thing her cousin feared more than floods or debts or Dario Kessler.

"He left a gate," Bea said softly. "Sluice Three. His name is on it. He built a way to flood the complex on purpose, Yara. Which means the explosion twelve years ago wasn't an ending. It was an *option*."

Yara went very still. Outside, the rain found its rhythm, steady now, committed, the sound of a season turning.

"There's more," Bea said. "When I was coming out, before the line went—I saw fresh tool marks on the gate's wheel housing. Bright metal. Recent." She met her cousin's eyes. "Somebody else has been down there, Yara. Somebody's been *turning* it."
