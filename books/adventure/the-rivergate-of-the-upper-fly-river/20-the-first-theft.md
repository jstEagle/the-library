## 20. The First Theft

The rain came in from the west that night like something with an appointment to keep.

Yara heard it before she saw it—a long hiss building in the gorge, the sound of the river swallowing the first of it—and she lay in her cot doing the arithmetic she had been doing for three days now: nine days until Okonkwo's revised landfall, maybe eight if the storm was greedy, and Kessler still had two hundred crates in the upper gallery and a barge sitting light at the lower landing like a mouth waiting to be fed.

She had learned to sleep through rain on the spurs, where a storm meant mud and rerailing and a payroll week gone soft. But this rain had a different weight. It sounded like a countdown.

She was still counting when the lamp outside her tent went dark.

Not flickered—went dark, deliberate, the way Tomas killed lamps when he wanted the camp to look asleep. Yara lay still and listened past the rain. Underneath it, faint but unmistakable, came the groan of the portal winch taking load. Someone was running the gallery hoist at half past two in the morning, and they were doing it quiet.

She dressed without a lamp, pulled her boots on wet, and stepped out into a night the color of wet slate. The camp was dark except for the barge landing, where three work lights burned behind canvas screens, angled down so their glow never left the gravel. Somebody had thought about sightlines. Somebody had planned this.

Yara moved along the tree line toward the ridge path, keeping off the open ground, and it was there, in the shadow of the water-tower legs, that a hand closed on her arm and nearly put her heart through her ribs.

"It's me." Nia's voice, low and fast. The demolitions expert materialized out of the dark with her hood up and her face pale as paper. "Don't go down there."

"Then tell me what I'm walking into."

Nia glanced back toward the landing. Down-slope, the winch groaned again, and beneath it now Yara could hear the particular careful shuffle of men moving crates—crates that weighed more than tools, crates packed in museum-grade foam, crates without manifests.

"They started an hour ago," Nia said. "Six of them. Voss and his crew, plus two of Kessler's security men I've never seen before—they came up on the supply run Tuesday and nobody logged them in. They're running the hoist on the auxiliary motor so the main generator stays cold. No log entry. No work order. Nothing signed."

Yara felt the night get colder around her. "You checked the books."

"I check everything," Nia said. "It's how I keep people alive." She hesitated, and in the hesitation Yara heard the thing Nia had actually come to say, the thing bigger than bookkeeping. "Yara. I wired the stabilization charges this afternoon. Perimeter pattern, per the schedule. When I came back through the upper gallery to collect my tools, the crates were already staged at the transfer point. Forty-one of them. Numbered in sequence. Somebody picked them out hours before the loading started—which means somebody made a list, and the list isn't mine, and it isn't yours, and it sure as hell isn't in any manifest I've seen."

"Which crates?"

"That's the part that'll keep you up." Nia's jaw tightened. "The good ones. The panels from bay twelve—the ones we resealed after I opened one against orders. The ceremonial pieces. The stuff a museum would fight over. Whoever made that list knew exactly what they were looking at, and they didn't need to open anything to know it, because—" She stopped herself, but Yara finished it inside her own head, because there was only one place that knowledge could live.

Because Kessler had known the inventory before any of them ever broke ground. Because the survey that brought him here hadn't been about reopening a rail line at all.

Down at the landing, a crate swung out of the dark on the hoist line, slow and pendulous, and settled onto the barge deck with a thud that the river swallowed whole.

* * *

They watched from the ridge path for twenty minutes, crouched behind the deadfall where Tomas kept his observation post, and Yara made herself study the operation the way she'd study a rival crew's work—not as a crime but as a job, because that was the only way she could hold her hands steady.

Voss ran the loading, and Voss was good. He'd staggered the hoist cycles so no more than one crate crossed open air at a time. He'd posted a man at the portal mouth and another at the bend in the spur road, both with radios, neither smoking, neither talking—discipline you couldn't buy overnight. The two strangers worked the barge end, guiding each crate into a cradle lashed down with real seamanship, and between cradles they laid tarpaulins, so that by the time they were done the deck would read, to any passing eye, as a machinery shipment. Pump parts. Generators. The boring cargo of a dewatering operation.

"They've done this before," Nia murmured. "Not here. Somewhere else. More than once."

"Yes."

"How many sites do you think there have been?"

"I think," Yara said, "that my father found one of them, and it got him killed, and Dario Kessler has spent twelve years making sure nobody ever counted the rest."

Nia was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked on the deadfall above them. Then she said, in a voice carefully stripped of anything at all: "I can stop it."

Yara turned. In the dark she could just make out Nia's profile, the set of her mouth, the way her hands had curled into fists inside her sleeves.

"There's a junction box forty meters up the spur road feeds the landing lights and the aux hoist both," Nia went on, low and rapid, the way she talked when she'd already built the thing in her head. "One charge, quarter-stick, shaped, and I take out the box without touching the conduit. Hoist goes dead, lights go dead, and every man down there has to make a choice in the dark about whether he wants to explain to Kessler why his midnight loading got interrupted. They won't choose the explanation. They'll scatter. By morning the crates are back in the gallery and we've bought time—"

"And Kessler knows someone blew his junction box," Yara said. "And the first name he looks at is his explosives expert who wasn't in her tent."

"He can't prove—"

"He doesn't need to prove. He needs to suspect, and then he needs one week, and then Bea's dive records get falsified, or Tomas gets a tax audit, or your certification gets pulled on some technicality, and we lose everything, all at once, on his schedule instead of ours." Yara heard her own voice come out flat and cold and hated it, hated how easily the arithmetic came, hated that she had gotten good at this—the math of surrender, the ledger of what could be sacrificed. "No charges, Nia. Not tonight."

"Then what do we do?" And there it was under the question, plain as a flare: *you're the boss, tell me we're not just going to watch.* "We let them load stolen heritage onto a barge and wave it goodbye? We spent three weeks digging those crates out of a hole. We reseal them, we document them, we play it straight, and tonight the man who owns our paychecks walks off with forty-one of them while we sit on a ridge like—"

"Like what?"

"Like the people who signed the report," Nia said. "Twelve years ago."

The words landed exactly where she'd aimed them, and Yara let them, because she deserved them, and because the pain of them was useful—it kept her honest in a way comfort never would. Below, another crate crossed the air, slow as a body on a rope.

"Nia," she said finally. "How many days since you slept more than four hours?"

"What does that—"

"Answer me."

"Five. Maybe six."

"Go back to your tent. Sleep. Tomorrow you're going to inventory every charge, every cap, every coil of det cord in the magazine, twice, and log it, and keep the log on your person, because in the next ten days somebody is going to ask you to prepare demolition work whose real purpose isn't demolition, and when that day comes I need you clean, and rested, and holding receipts." She held Nia's eyes until the other woman looked away. "That's not nothing. That's the whole game. Do you understand me?"

Nia understood. Yara watched it arrive in her face—the slow, unwilling comprehension that Yara wasn't declining to fight, she was choosing a battlefield that didn't exist yet. It didn't make Nia happy. It didn't make Yara happy either. But Nia squeezed her arm once, hard, and slipped away down the ridge path toward camp, and Yara stayed alone in the rain-dark and did the thing she had come up here to do, which was to watch, and to count, and to hate herself with great precision.

Forty-one crates. At nine minutes a cycle, call it six hours. The barge would be loaded and tarped and gone upriver on the pre-dawn tide, riding high, riding legal-looking, carrying the carved history of the Fly River basin into the holds of men who had never once asked the river's permission.

And she was going to let it happen.

* * *

She told herself it was strategy, and it was, mostly.

Kessler was watching her—that had been true since the portal, probably since the contract, certainly since the night she'd confronted him over the opened crate and he'd smiled with all the warmth of a man showing her a door he could lock. If the loading was interrupted, if so much as a radio call went sideways, he would know the leak was inside her circle, and he would start cutting, and he would cut with paperwork and lawyers and the kind of violence that arrived wearing a suit. She had five people she loved on this mountain. She had a father's journal hidden in a waterproof sleeve under her cot floor. She had photographs of a ledger that could burn down a dozen family fortunes, and none of it was worth anything if Kessler decided tomorrow that Yara Vasquez was a problem instead of an asset.

So she let the winch run. She sat on the ridge in the rain with her back against a trunk that smelled of bark and lightning, and she watched the theft happen crate by crate, and she memorized everything—faces, times, crate numbers where the stencils caught the screened lamplight, the order of the tarping, which stranger tied which knot. Evidence. Ammunition. A debt she intended to collect with interest, later, on ground of her choosing.

That was the strategy. That was the story she told herself.

But underneath the strategy, in the place where she actually lived, something older and colder was at work, and she knew its voice too well to pretend otherwise. *You've done this before,* the voice said. *You were seventeen, and men told you what to sign, and you signed it because signing was safer than fighting, and you have been moving ever since—camp to camp, contract to contract, rail to rail—because motion is how you don't hear the pen scratch.* The rain thickened. The winch groaned. *You are watching a crime happen right now, and you are telling yourself it's tactics, and the truth is that part of you is relieved. Part of you is glad someone else is making the decision, so that all you have to do is fail to stop it.*

"No," she said aloud, to the rain, to the voice, to nobody.

But the crates kept coming.

Toward four o'clock the rhythm changed. The hoist cycles lengthened; voices dropped; and Yara realized they were nearing the end of the list, the last few cradles, the moment when the barge would be sealed and the decision window would close forever. Her hand found her radio. It sat in her palm, heavy as a stone, one press from Tomas, one press from waking the camp, one press from a fight she might lose but would at least have chosen.

She thought about her father's last message scratched inside the lock door. *Flood it. Tell no one. The river keeps what it keeps.*

She thought about the elder's carved token in her kit, and the promise it carried, and the communities downriver who had gone quiet at Kessler's name the way villages go quiet for a flood they can see coming and cannot stop.

She put the radio back in her pocket.

At 4:40 the work lights died in sequence, deliberately, top to bottom, and the darkness below was total except for the barge's single stern lamp. Lines came off. The diesel burbled up from idle to load, and the barge eased off the landing into the current, riding low but not too low, anonymous, correct, gone. Within ten minutes the rain had erased even the sound of it, and the gorge belonged to the river again, and Yara Vasquez sat alone on a ridge above a crime scene and added one more signature to a collection she'd sworn at seventeen she'd never grow.

* * *

She didn't go straight back to camp. She walked the long way, down the spur road past the dead junction box, through the portal's cold breath, into the upper gallery with her headlamp off, feeling her way by the wall the way her father had taught her—palm on rock, small steps, trust the tunnel to be a tunnel.

The transfer point stood empty. Forty-one gaps in the dust patterns along the staging line, crisp-edged, fresh, like teeth pulled from a jaw. She stood among the absences for a long time. Somewhere deeper in, water moved in the dark with the patience of something that had already won every argument it had ever entered.

"You should have woken me."

Tomas's voice, from the gallery mouth. She hadn't heard him follow; she wondered if he'd been awake all night, keeping the kind of watch nobody asked him for. His silhouette filled the portal, headlamp hooded, and even at fifty meters she could feel him reading her—the set of her shoulders, the direction of her stare.

"You'd have stopped it," she said.

"Yes."

"That's why I didn't wake you."

He came down the gallery slowly, boots ringing on the old ballast, and stopped beside her, and looked at the empty staging line the way a man looks at a grave he expected to find dug. For a while neither of them spoke. The tunnel breathed around them.

"Voss will report it as routine equipment transfer," Tomas said at last. "Kessler will deny everything. The crates are beyond reach by breakfast." He paused. "You know the river people will know anyway. They always know. The scout on the ridge sees the barge move at night, and word goes downriver faster than any radio, and when the elder comes—and he will come, Yara, soon, with his people behind him—they are going to stand in front of you and ask what you did while their history floated past your camp, and you are going to have to answer them with your face."

"I know."

"Do you?" He turned to her then, and in the dim spill of his hooded lamp his expression was the old one, the patient one, the one that had survived twelve years of her rules and her motion and her refusal to stay anywhere long enough to be known. "Because there are two ways to carry a secret like this. One way, it's a weapon—you hold it, you aim it, you spend it when the spending wins. The other way, it's a rot. It eats the carrier first." He touched the empty staging line with one finger, lightly, the way you'd touch a coffin lid. "Decide which one you're carrying before the elder asks. Not after."

He left her there. That was Tomas—he delivered the truth and then gave you room to bleed in private.

Yara stood a while longer among the gaps where the crates had been. Then she took out her notebook, and by shielded headlamp she wrote down everything she had memorized on the ridge: times, faces, knots, numbers, the sequence of the lights going out. Forty-one entries in her small square hand. When she finished, she tore the page free, folded it into eighths, and sealed it inside the waterproof sleeve beside her father's journal, where the evidence of what she'd allowed was now filed directly next to the evidence of what it had cost the last person who'd tried to stop these men.

Outside, the rain had found its full voice. Okonkwo's models said eight days. The river said sooner.

She was halfway back to her tent, soaked through, hollowed out, rehearsing the lie she'd give Kessler's morning briefing—*heard the aux motor, assumed maintenance, logged nothing because there was nothing to log*—when she saw the fires.

Downriver, along the far bank where the channel widened before the gorge, torches had appeared in the grey pre-dawn. Not two or three. Dozens. A procession of them, moving along the waterline in the old ceremonial spacing the river communities used, coming upstream toward the camp with the unhurried certainty of a tide.

At their head, small and straight-backed in the growing light, walked the elder.

And in his hand, raised so the whole river could see it, he carried a carving identical to the token sleeping in Yara's kit—her father's promise, made flesh, coming to collect.
