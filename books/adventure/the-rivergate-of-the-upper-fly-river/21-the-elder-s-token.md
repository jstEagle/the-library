## 21. The Elder's Token

They came out of the mist at first light, and Tomas saw them before anyone else.

Yara was in the mess tent, drinking coffee that had gone bitter from sitting too long on the burner, when Tomas ducked through the flap without knocking. He didn't say anything. He just held the flap open behind him, and she read it in his face—the particular stillness he got when something was coming that couldn't be shot or pumped or rerailed.

She stepped outside into the grey wash of dawn. The river ran high and brown below the camp, carrying the monsoon's first argument downstream in branches and silt. And along the old portage trail that climbed from the water to the rail spur, thirty people were walking up through the fog in single file, unhurried, as if they had all the time the river had ever given them.

They weren't armed. That was the first thing Yara noticed, and it frightened her more than rifles would have. Men with guns wanted something you could refuse them. These people carried digging sticks and woven bags and a drum lashed to a frame of bamboo, and they walked like they were arriving somewhere they'd always been expected.

At their head came the elder.

She had seen him once before, weeks ago, from the deck of the barge—a figure on the bank holding up a carved token while the engines drowned whatever he said. Now, close, she could see he was older than she'd guessed: small and wiry, with a white beard cropped short and eyes that had the flat patience of a man who had watched this river for seventy years and expected to watch it for seventy more. Around his neck, on a cord of braided bark, hung a wooden pendant no bigger than her thumb.

A fish. Carved in the round, worn smooth by hands, its tail curled as if mid-turn in current.

Yara's hand went to her pocket before she could stop it. The token's twin sat there against her hip, where it had lived since the day the elder pressed it into her palm on the barge deck—*your father promised the river would be returned*—and she had carried it every day since like a stone she couldn't put down and couldn't swallow.

"They're stopping at the tree line," Tomas murmured. "Not coming in."

"I can see that."

"What do you want to do?"

What she wanted to do was walk down there alone and ask the elder everything—about her father, about the promise, about what the communities knew and how long they'd known it. What she could afford to do was a different arithmetic entirely. Kessler's men were already stirring in their tents. Two of his security detail stood at the edge of the equipment yard, and one of them had a radio in his hand and was speaking into it with the unhurried tone of a man reporting weather.

"Nobody moves," Yara said. "Nobody photographs them. Nobody talks to them but me. Wake Bea—no. Leave Bea sleeping. Wake Okonkwo and have him stand at my shoulder looking official. Hydrology consultations make excellent cover for standing still."

Tomas almost smiled. Almost. Then he went to do it, and Yara stood alone at the edge of camp and watched the river people wait for her under the dripping trees, patient as bedrock.

* * *

She crossed the fifty meters of mud and gravel between the camp boundary and the tree line slowly, deliberately, so Kessler's watchers would see there was nothing furtive in it. Okonkwo fell in beside her halfway, clipboard in hand, playing his role with the grave dignity of a man who had played many roles in many countries and had stopped apologizing for any of them decades ago.

The elder let her come to him. That, too, was a kind of message.

"You remember me," he said. His English was accented but precise, each word set down like a stone in a wall.

"I remember," Yara said. "You gave me a gift I didn't earn."

"You kept it." His eyes went to her pocket—not guessing; knowing—and something in his face eased, though it did not soften. "That is why we are here. A woman who keeps a promise-shaped thing in her pocket is a woman who can still be spoken to."

Behind him, the others had begun to arrange themselves without any visible instruction: elders and young men, two women with children asleep against their backs, a boy of perhaps ten who stared past Yara at the concrete face of the Rivergate portal visible above the camp, half-drowned in the gorge, and made a small sound in his throat that might have been grief.

"We don't want trouble," Yara said. "And I'll tell you plainly—I'm working for the man who owns this project. If you've come to protest—"

"We have not come to protest." The elder lifted a hand, and the file of people behind him settled onto the ground, onto roots and stones, as naturally as water finding its level. "Protest is for people who believe someone will listen. We have come because the water is rising, and because you are the daughter of the man who sealed the gate, and because there are things that must be said before the monsoon takes the choice away from everyone."

Okonkwo's pen had stopped moving. Yara felt the morning tilt around her.

"What things?"

The elder looked at her for a long moment. Then he gestured, and an old woman beside him opened a woven bag and drew out its contents with the care of a surgeon: three objects wrapped in banana leaf. She unwrapped the first.

A brass survey tag, green with age, stamped with initials Yara knew better than her own name. *E.V.* Her father's. The same stamping die that had punched the tags she'd found in the upper gallery, the one she carried now in the same pocket as the carved fish, so that the two pieces of her inheritance knocked together when she walked.

"This was found," the elder said, "in the shallows below the gate, after the explosion. Twelve years ago. My people found four such things. We kept them." He turned the tag over in his dark fingers. "We did not know then what your father had found inside. We know now."

"How?" The question came out of Yara harder than she intended. "How could you possibly know what's inside? Nobody knows what's inside."

"The families know." He said it simply, the way you'd say *the rain falls*. "The families have always known. They built the railway to carry away what was never theirs—our carvings, our drums, the bones of our ancestors in crates marked as machinery. Your father found the proof. The ledger. The names. And the families sent men to make sure the proof stayed underground, and your father sent the river instead." He paused. "He was one of us, you know. Did he ever tell you that? Not born to us. But he surveyed our channels for six years before the railway came. He ate in our houses. He learned the names of our dead. When he promised the river would be returned, he was promising it to family."

The gorge seemed very quiet. Somewhere above, a crane Kessler's crews had rigged groaned against its cable, and the sound passed over them like a bird.

"He never told me any of it," Yara said. "He told me nothing. He died telling me nothing."

"No," the elder agreed. "He told you everything. You simply have not finished reading it." He held out the brass tag. "This belongs to the work he started. We give it back to you, so you understand: we are not asking you to choose between strangers. We are asking you to finish being your father's daughter."

Yara didn't take the tag. Her hands stayed at her sides, and she was distantly proud of that, because every nerve in them wanted to reach out.

"What is it you're asking, exactly? Say it plainly. I deal better in plain."

The old woman unwrapped the second object: a photograph, brittle and foxed with damp, showing a room lined with shelving—crates, document boxes, a filing cabinet—and in the foreground, blurred by long exposure, a man in a surveyor's vest turning toward the camera. Yara's breath stopped. She knew the vest. She knew the turn of the shoulders. The photograph had been taken inside the lower gallery, years before the flood, and it showed her father standing guard over an evidence room that officially had never existed.

"This was given to us," the elder said, "by a man who worked for the families and grew ashamed. Before he died. It is why we know the ledger survived the water. Water keeps what it keeps—but paper drowns slowly, if it is packed well, and your father packed well. He learned packing from us." A ghost of humor moved through his voice and was gone. "So. Plainly, then, as you ask. We want three things. We want the artifacts to come home to the villages—not to museums in the capital, not to glass cases with other people's names on the doors. Home. We want the ledger, so that the families who stole from us can finally be named in courts instead of around fires. And we want the gate closed forever, so that no third expedition ever comes up this river hunting our ancestors' belongings."

"And if I can't give you those things?"

"Then we will take them ourselves, in our own time, in our own way, and some of us will die doing it, and the blood will be on the families and on the river, which has carried enough blood." He said it without heat, without threat—a forecast, nothing more. "But we would rather it went another way. Which is why I am going to ask you a question, daughter of Elias Vasquez, and I ask you to answer it honestly, because the river hears lying even when men don't."

He leaned slightly forward. Behind him, thirty faces waited without moving.

"Have you seen the lower gallery?"

Every instinct Yara owned screamed at her to lie. Kessler's watchers were fifty meters back, close enough that a sharp-eyed man with binoculars could be reading lips. Her crew's safety, Bea's certification, the whole fragile architecture of her cover—it all balanced on the next sentence.

And underneath all of it, quieter and heavier than the rest: the memory of her father's scratched message on the lock door. *Flood it. Tell no one.*

"I haven't been below the lock," she said.

It was true. Every word of it, technically true, and the elder studied her for a long moment with eyes that weighed truth the way his people weighed fish—by hand, by feel, by long practice.

"That," he said at last, "is not the same as *no*. But it is not a lie either, and I will accept it—for now." He straightened. "The monsoon comes early this year. Earlier than your machines predict; the birds left the low islands nine days ago, and the birds are never wrong. When it comes, the river will do what rivers do. What happens after that depends on what you have decided by then."

He extended his hand—not for a shake, but palm up, open, waiting.

Yara understood. Slowly, she took the carved fish from her pocket. It was warm from her body, and lighter than it had any right to be for how much it had cost her to carry. For a moment she almost kept it—almost told him she needed more time, that she wasn't ready to give back the only piece of her father's promise she possessed.

But that was exactly the cowardice dressed as caution that had signed a false report at seventeen.

She laid the token in his palm.

The elder closed his fingers around it and nodded once, and the nod traveled down the line of his people like wind through cane. Then he reached up—he was shorter than her by a head—and pressed the brass survey tag into her hand instead, folding her fingers over it.

"A trade," he said. "You keep his mark. We keep his promise. And when the water rises, daughter of Elias—one thing only. Remember which side of the gate the river is on."

* * *

She watched them go down to the water, single file again, fading into the mist off the channel until there was nothing but the drum's last echo and the brown river sliding past. Okonkwo stood beside her the whole time, saying nothing, his clipboard forgotten against his hip.

"Well," he said finally. "That was a hydrological consultation."

"Don't."

"I'm merely noting it for the record." He glanced at her. "Nine days. He said the birds left nine days ago. My models put landfall at eleven days out, and my models don't account for birds."

"Your models also don't account for a man who reads lies by weighing them in his hand." Yara opened her fingers. The brass tag lay in her palm, green and cold, her father's initials catching the first real light of the morning. "Okonkwo. If I asked you—if I asked any of them—to do something that ended this contract early, something that burned every bridge Kessler owns and probably landed us in court... how many would follow?"

Okonkwo was quiet for a moment. Downriver, the mist was burning off, and the portal of the Rivergate emerged from it inch by inch, gray and wounded, waiting.

"Wrong question," he said. "You're asking how many would follow you. Ask instead what you're going to tell them, and whether it's finally the truth. Crews follow truth longer than they follow leaders." He picked up his clipboard, tucked it under his arm, and started back toward camp. "Also—you should know that Kessler's security chief logged the meeting. Radioed it in word for word. Whatever you decide, you've got maybe an hour before Dario comes asking what the river people wanted."

Yara stood alone at the tree line a moment longer. Then she slipped the brass tag into her pocket, where it clicked against the empty space where the carved fish used to be, and followed him in.

She had barely reached the mess tent when she heard the helicopter.
