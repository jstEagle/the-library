## 22. Sabotage

The pump line died at 3:14 in the morning, and Yara knew it before anyone woke her, because the sound of the camp changed. For nine days the dewatering pumps had run in a steady triple heartbeat—two diesels and the big electric unit on the generator line—and she had learned to sleep inside that rhythm the way a sailor sleeps inside the sound of hull against water. When it broke, some animal part of her sat up in the dark with her hand already reaching for her boots.

She was out of the tent before the first shout came from the pump pad.

The gorge at night was a black throat. Headlamps bloomed along the slope like startled fireflies, and by the time Yara reached the intake gallery the rain had already soaked through her jacket to the skin. The monsoon was still four days out by Okonkwo's revised models, but its outriders had arrived early: a thin, needling drizzle that turned the clay slopes to grease and made every headlamp beam look like it was drowning.

Okonkwo was crouched at the intake sump, his long fingers moving through the wreckage of the suction line with the tenderness of a man examining a wound.

"Talk to me," Yara said.

"Someone cut it." He didn't look up. "Not failed. Cut. Here—" He lifted a section of the flexible intake hose into the lamplight. The slash was clean, one pass, made by a blade wide enough to open the hose in a single stroke. "And they didn't stop there. They pulled the foot valve off the strainer and dropped it into the sump. Then they opened the drain cocks on both diesel units."

Yara looked down into the black water of the intake pool. Somewhere at the bottom, the brass-and-rubber heart of the pump system lay in six feet of cold river backwash, and above them, behind the concrete face of the Rivergate, forty feet of floodwater waited in the galleries for the chance to come home.

"How long to fix?"

"If I dive for the valve tonight, two hours. If I wait for light, four." Okonkwo finally turned, and the lamplight carved his face into planes of shadow. "But Yara. That's not the question you should be asking."

"I know what I should be asking."

"Then ask it."

She stood at the edge of the sump, rain running off her chin, and asked it anyway, because saying it aloud would make it someone else's problem too. "Who walked the pad tonight?"

"Tomas had second watch. Relieved at midnight by one of Kessler's men—I don't know which one, they rotate." Okonkwo rose, wringing water from his sleeve. "The pad is eighty meters from the tents. Anyone could have done this in four minutes without a lamp, if they knew where everything was."

*If they knew where everything was.* The words hung there. Only three people outside Kessler's own crew had walked that pad enough times to work it blind: herself, Tomas, and Nia. And none of them had.

Which meant either a stranger had studied their camp for days without a single person noticing—

—or the stranger had been invited in.

* * *

Kessler held court at dawn under the expedition tarp, and he did not hold it for his own men. He held it for everyone: Yara's crew, his riggers, the two surveyors who'd come upriver with the second barge, all of them arranged in a wet half-circle while he stood at the map table with his hands flat on the plastic covering, a man displaying calm the way other men displayed weapons.

"Last night," he said, "someone sabotaged this operation. Someone who knows this basin, knows our equipment, and wants us gone before we finish the job." He let his gaze travel the circle slowly. "There is only one group of people who fit that description."

"You don't know that," Yara said.

"The scout your diver chased off the ridge two weeks ago. The elders who came to camp asking questions about cargo. The villages that go silent when my name comes up the radio." Kessler's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which was how she knew he'd rehearsed this. "I'm not accusing anyone of anything. I'm observing a pattern."

"A pattern isn't proof."

"No," he agreed. "Proof is what happens next." He straightened. "Effective this morning, my security detail doubles. Rifles on the pad, rifles on the portal, rifles on the barge landing. Curfew inside the wire after dark. Any unidentified person approaching camp gets challenged, then detained."

"With guns," Nia said from the back. It wasn't a question.

"With deterrence," Kessler said. "I didn't come up this river to lose a hundred-million-kina operation to a man with a knife and a canoe."

Yara felt the ground shift under the conversation—the real ground, the moral footing she'd been standing on since she'd signed Kessler's contract. This was what the sabotage had bought him, whether or not he'd ordered it. A frightened crew will surrender anything for safety: their schedule, their judgment, their willingness to ask whose fear is being manufactured. She watched the half-circle of faces and saw the exact moment most of them decided that armed men on the perimeter sounded reasonable.

Only Bea, arms crossed, dripping beside the map table, caught Yara's eye and gave the smallest shake of her head. *Don't fight it here.*

So Yara didn't. She said, "My crew works the pad. Nobody else touches the pump line," and walked away before Kessler could turn agreement into victory.

* * *

They recovered the foot valve by mid-morning, Okonkwo diving the sump in the gray light with Bea spotting him from above, and by noon the triple heartbeat resumed. But the camp had changed shape overnight. Kessler's men now moved in pairs. There were sandbags at the corners of the wire. And there was a new sound underneath the pumps: boots on gravel, unhurried, constant, the percussion of an occupation.

Tomas found Yara at the survey tent at midday. He looked like he hadn't slept, which was true of everyone, but on Tomas exhaustion showed as precision—he moved slower, spoke shorter, checked things twice.

"You think he staged it," Tomas said.

"I think it's convenient."

"Convenient." He almost smiled. "That's a word people use when they can't prove the worse thing."

"Then let me say the worse thing." Yara kept her voice low, kept her eyes on the map spread between them—her father's survey, the red line, the word *sealed*. "Kessler needed a reason to arm his crew before the artifacts start moving. You don't put rifles on a salvage site; you put them on a site that's about to be robbed. He's been waiting for an excuse since the elder came to camp. Now he has one, and it even has a knife mark in it."

"And if he didn't stage it? If it really was the river people?" Tomas tapped the map, over the villages upstream. "Then we're sitting inside a wire full of armed men who've been told to detain the same people who brought you that—" he nodded at her chest pocket, where the carved token lived these days, riding over her heart like a second pulse. "You see the trap either way."

She saw it. That was the thing about Kessler: every door he built had two bad sides.

"There's a third option," Tomas said quietly. "We find out who actually cut that hose. Before anyone else gets hurt deciding for us."

* * *

They found the first trace that evening, and it pointed nowhere they expected.

Bea had spent the afternoon in the water—not diving, just wading the shallows below the pump pad with a mask and a slate, reading the creek bed the way she read current, and she came back to the survey tent with mud to her knees and an expression Yara had learned to dread, because it meant Bea had found something true and unwelcome.

"Whoever cut the line came from downstream," Bea said. "There's a trail in the clay on the far bank—barefoot, or near enough, narrow heel, light weight. Crossed at the riffle below the intake, came up through the cane grass, went back the same way. One person. Small."

"River people," Tomas said.

"River people cross at the riffle, sure. But look at the timing." Bea wiped her slate clean and drew the pad layout from memory: pumps, fuel drums, the wire, the tents. "The cut happened after midnight. Second watch changed at twelve. Whoever took over that watch—Kessler's rotation—logged nothing unusual. But here's the thing." She tapped the pad. "The drain cocks on the diesels are on the *inside* of the units, facing the wire. To open them fast, in the dark, you stand between the pumps and the fence. With a rifle patrol walking rounds, that's the one spot on this camp nobody looks at, because everybody's watching outward."

Silence in the tent. Rain ticked on the canvas.

"So it's one of his," Yara said slowly. "Or someone working with one of his."

"Or," Bea said, "someone small and barefoot who knows our pad better than we do, and wanted us to blame ourselves instead of the villages." She met Yara's eyes. "I'm not saying Kessler staged it. I'm saying whoever did it understood exactly how he'd react. That takes knowing him a long time."

Yara thought of the elder's face at the token exchange—*the families sent Kessler, the families sent your father*—and felt the shape of something old and patient moving underneath the sabotage, something that wanted the expedition at war with the river. Divide the basin against itself, and no one watches the crates.

"We tell no one," she said. "Not yet. If Kessler learns his own man might be involved, he doesn't investigate—he escalates. We keep Bea's trail between us three, and we watch the watch rotations."

* * *

It went wrong within thirty-six hours, because plans built on silence always do.

The second night of the doubled patrols, a figure came down the far bank at dusk—not hiding, walking openly along the waterline with a dugout drawn up behind him—and Kessler's sentries challenged him from the wire with rifles leveled, and the man stopped, raised both hands, and stood very still in the way of someone who has done this before, in front of soldiers, more than once.

Yara was halfway to the wire when she recognized him: the scout. The one Bea had tracked on the ridge, the one who'd delivered the warning about the families. He was younger than she remembered, or the low light made him so, and he held something white in one raised hand—a cloth, a flag of his own invention.

"He's unarmed," Yara called. "Stand down."

"Nobody approaches," said Voss, Kessler's security chief, a broad ex-naval man with a voice like a hatch closing. "Protocol. He waits there until Mr. Kessler clears it."

"He's not detaining anyone on this river," Yara said, and kept walking.

What happened next happened in the space of four seconds, and afterward Yara could never assemble those seconds into a single honest sequence, only fragments. Voss stepping past her to intercept. Her shoulder hitting his arm. The scout turning to run—not toward his canoe, toward the cane grass, because a man who'd faced rifles before knew canoes were where they shot. A sentry swinging his muzzle to track him. Tomas, coming out of the dark from the pad side, getting between the barrel and the treeline with both hands up, shouting the scout's name in his own language—because Tomas knew him, had known him since the barge journey, had been trading quiet words across the water for weeks.

And the rifle, startled by a shape erupting out of the rain-dark, discharging once into the ground between them—or into Tomas; the angle afterward suggested both, and no one agreed—Tomas folding not like a man shot but like a man whose legs had been taken away, and the whole camp arriving at once in a storm of lamps and shouting, and the scout gone into the cane as if the river itself had swallowed him.

The round had torn through the outside of Tomas's left thigh, a hot ugly groove, flesh and not bone, blood and not catastrophe. Nia had a trauma kit open before the shouting stopped. Okonkwo knelt and pressed and talked to Tomas in a low stream of reassurance while Nia packed the wound, and Yara held her cousin's hand—no. Not her cousin's. Tomas's hand, gray-faced in the lamplight, gripping hers hard enough to grind the small bones.

"Stay," he said. Just that. "Stay."

"I'm staying," Yara said. "Nobody's going anywhere."

Across the wire, Voss was already on the radio to Kessler's barge, reporting an attempted breach by hostile locals, shots fired in defense of the perimeter. The story was writing itself in real time, clean and official, and every word of it was wrong.

* * *

Kessler arrived by helicopter at first light, because of course he did; the man had a genius for arriving after disasters he'd helped arrange. He stood over the field dressing on Tomas's leg, listened to Voss's report with his head tilted like a man appraising timber, and then turned to Yara with an expression of genuine, unbearable sympathy.

"One of your people got hurt protecting a trespasser," he said. "I'm sorry. Truly. But this is what I warned about. The river people probed our line, they cut our pumps, and last night they tested our wire. Three incidents, one pattern. From today, my rules are not negotiable, and any contact between your crew and the villages ends. Including—" his eyes flicked, delicately, to her breast pocket, "—tokens."

"They weren't testing anything," Yara said. "He came with his hands up. He came to talk."

"To talk." Kessler sighed, a man burdened by other people's naivety. "Yara. Your hydrologist says the water table's still wrong. Your diver nearly drowned in that channel. Your explosives expert found caps that someone else planted. Every sign says we're not alone in this tunnel, and now the banks are not neutral either. I can't run an operation on goodwill. Goodwill is what got your father killed."

The sentence landed exactly where he aimed it, and he knew it, and for one raw moment neither of them blinked.

"Your father tried to be friends with the river," Kessler said softly. "Look where the rivergate stands."

Yara looked at Tomas, sedated under the tarp, his thigh wrapped white and rust. She looked at the wire, and the sandbags, and the young sentry who couldn't meet anyone's eyes, the rifle still in his hands like something he no longer understood. She looked upstream, toward the villages that had gone quiet, toward the elder's token burning against her ribs, toward the ledger pages hidden in the lining of her gear bag that she could show no one, that were becoming heavier every day she carried them alone.

The expedition had turned on itself. Kessler had his war now, or the shape of one, and wars made men agree to anything to end them.

That night, sitting by Tomas's cot while he slept, Yara felt a folded scrap of paper pressed into her palm—Nia, passing without a word, eyes forward, face blank. Yara waited until the lantern was out, then opened it by feel.

One line, in Nia's square engineer's hand:

*Counted the blasting caps again. Two missing. Ask yourself who signed them out — and who benefits if we're all pointing guns at each other when they go.*
