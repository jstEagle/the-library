## 23. Nia's Choice

Nia Okoro had wired charges in places that would make a demolition engineer weep: under a bridge in Kaduna with the river already eating its footings; inside a decommissioned smelter so hot her primer cord sweated in its coil; once, memorably, in a flooded quarry where she'd had to waterproof every cap by hand because the client refused to wait for the pumps. She was good at her work. She was proud of it. And in eleven years of handling explosives, she had learned one law above all others: *know what you are being paid to destroy.*

So when Kessler's site manager, a broad man named Ferro with a clipboard he treated like scripture, handed her the work order at dawn and said the word "stabilization," Nia read the document twice before she touched it.

"Stabilization," she repeated.

"That's what it says."

"It says that." She tapped the page. "But stabilization means shoring. Mesh, shotcrete, rock bolts, maybe a bulkhead if you're feeling generous. Stabilization is how you keep a tunnel *up*." She looked up at him. "This order specifies charge placement along the lower gallery access corridor. Sixteen points. Shaped charges. That's not how you keep a tunnel up, Ferro. That's how you bring one down."

Ferro's face did something careful. Not quite blank — Kessler didn't hire men who could go fully blank, they made auditors nervous — but arranged, the way a man arranges his face when he has been told what to say and what not to say.

"The engineers assessed the lower gallery as structurally unsound," he said. "Risk of collapse onto the working galleries above. Mr. Kessler wants it brought down in a controlled manner rather than let it fail on its own and take the rest of the complex with it."

"And the artifacts down there?"

"Recovered already. Manifest closed Tuesday."

Nia kept her expression level, which took some doing, because she had spent Tuesday night on the barge deck counting crates going aboard in the dark, and she knew exactly what had gone into that manifest and what hadn't. She knew that Yara Vasquez had stood at the rail beside her and watched the same loading and said nothing, and that the silence between them had been its own kind of agreement, or its own kind of wound — Nia still wasn't sure which.

"Sixteen points," she said again. "Show me the corridor."

* * *

The lower gallery access corridor ran off the main adit like a throat, sloping down toward the drowned sections. Yara's father had built the lock door at its mouth twelve years ago — thick steel, hand-welded reinforcement, the kind of barrier a man builds when he intends it to be the last thing anyone gets through. Kessler's crew had breached it four days ago with a thermal lance and a patience that Nia had found genuinely unsettling. They hadn't rushed it. They'd cut it open the way you'd open a safe.

Now the door hung twisted against the wall, and beyond it the corridor stretched down into air that tasted of iron and old water. Work lights burned every twenty meters, but the light didn't seem to stick to anything down here. It fell into the dark and the dark kept it.

Nia walked the corridor slowly, marking her stations, and let the engineer in her do what the engineer in her always did: read the rock.

The gallery walls were competent. That was the first thing. Competent grey siltstone, well-bedded, standing square after sixty years and a flood and a demolition. There were stress fractures near the old blast damage, yes — the explosion twelve years ago had done real work here — but nothing that threatened the upper workings. Nothing that any honest assessment would flag as an imminent risk to the galleries above. If Kessler's engineers had actually surveyed this corridor, their report would have said *monitor and maintain*, not *demolish*.

Which meant either the engineers hadn't surveyed it, or their survey had been told what to find.

She knelt at station seven, chalked the mark Ferro's plan called for, and sat back on her heels.

Here was the thing about shaped charges placed the way this plan specified: sixteen points, staggered, timed sequence running from the deep end back toward the portal. That sequence didn't drop a ceiling. It dropped a *corridor*. It would bring thousands of tonnes of rock down across the access way and pack it so tight that no excavator on earth would reopen it in less than a year, and no one would try, because there'd be nothing left to find. The lower gallery wouldn't be collapsed. It would be *entombed*. Sealed more thoroughly than her father's steel door ever managed — and this time sealed over whatever evidence remained inside.

Because things did remain inside. Nia had seen them.

Two nights ago, before the manifest closed, she'd been sent down with a lighting rig to help the recovery team finish. The lower gallery opened past the corridor into a chamber the flood had never fully claimed — pumped dry now, dripping, lit for the first time in twelve years. She had expected mud and rot. What she'd seen instead was order. Shelving built from tunnel timber. Crates stacked by date. A steel document cabinet, its drawers swollen shut with corrosion, stamped with a shipping company's name that Nia recognized because she'd seen it on the packing foam in the upper gallery crates — the firm owned by the families whose money sat under Kessler's empire like bedrock.

And papers. Even through the cabinet's rusted seams, papers. Rows of them. A room that someone had filled with records and then flooded on purpose, and the flood that should have destroyed everything had instead preserved it, cold and airless, like a tomb built to keep a secret fresh.

Yara's father hadn't just sealed a tunnel, Nia thought, kneeling in the corridor with chalk dust on her fingers. He'd built a vault. And now Dario Kessler wanted to pour the mountain over it permanently, once the last sellable objects were out, and call it engineering.

She finished walking the corridor. She marked all sixteen stations, because refusing to mark them would only get her removed from the job and replaced with one of Ferro's own people, someone who wouldn't ask questions. Then she climbed back up into the daylight and went looking for Yara.

* * *

She found her at the pump line, of all places, crouched with Okonkwo over a pressure gauge that had been reading wrong since morning. The hydrologist had his sleeves rolled and his forearms in the sump, feeling along a joint, muttering to himself in the tone of a man arguing with water. Yara stood over him holding a spanner she clearly wasn't going to need, watching the treeline the way she'd watched it every day since the scuffle that put Tomas in the medical tent — not afraid, exactly. Counting.

"Nia," Yara said, without turning. "You're supposed to be placing charges."

"I've marked them. I haven't drilled yet." Nia crouched beside them, lowered her voice. "I need to talk to you. Not here."

Yara glanced at her then — a quick, weighing look, the look of a woman who had spent two weeks cataloguing everyone's loyalties and filing them somewhere behind her eyes. "Okonkwo," she said. "How long for that joint?"

"An hour, if the water cooperates. Two if it doesn't. Water rarely cooperates."

"Take your hour." Yara set down the spanner. "Walk with me."

They walked up the tramline toward the portal, boots ringing on dead rails, until the camp noise fell away and the gorge opened out around them — green walls, brown water, the concrete face of the Rivergate half-drowned below, wearing its twelve-year-old scars. Yara stopped where she always stopped, at the point where you could see both the portal and the barge landing. Nia had noticed that. Yara Vasquez liked positions with two sightlines. She positioned herself like a woman who expected trouble from more than one direction.

"Talk," Yara said.

"Ferro gave me a demolition order this morning. Stabilization, he called it." Nia recited it flatly, professionally, the numbers doing the arguing for her: sixteen points, staggered timing, deep-to-shallow sequence, shaped charges. "That sequence buries the access corridor under five hundred tonnes of rock. Permanently. Nobody reopens that. Not in our lifetime."

Yara said nothing. Her jaw tightened, a small motion, quickly controlled.

"I walked the corridor," Nia went on. "The rock is sound. I've blown tunnels on three continents and I'm telling you, that gallery does not need stabilizing. This isn't engineering. This is a grave, and somebody's digging it ahead of schedule." She paused. "There's a document cabinet down there, Yara. Steel, corroded shut, full of paper. Same shipping company as the crate foam upstairs. You know what that is. Everyone on this crew with eyes knows what that is."

"Do they?" Yara's voice was quiet. "Do they know? Or do they suspect, the way you suspect?"

"I don't suspect. I *know*. I helped load the barge on Tuesday night. I know what went aboard and I know what stayed." Nia held her gaze. "I've been telling myself it's salvage. That the stuff's coming out of a hole in the ground either way, and better us than looters, and the courts can sort ownership later. I've been telling myself that for two weeks. But this—" she jerked her chin toward the portal, toward the buried dark below it— "this isn't salvage. This is destroying evidence. Once those charges fire, there's no court anywhere that can prove anything about this railway except that it existed. Kessler doesn't want the artifacts. The artifacts are the *fee*. The ledger is the prize, and the prize is burning the evidence room, and he's paying me to push the button."

The wind came down the gorge and moved the surface of the flooded portal, slow swells lapping at the concrete lip. Somewhere below, water shifted in the drowned galleries with a sound like breathing.

"You've thought about this," Yara said at last.

"Since Tuesday."

"And you came to me instead of Ferro. Why?"

"Because Ferro would smile and tell me the engineers signed off, and then I'd spend the rest of my life knowing I packed a grave for hire." Nia folded her arms. "And because you watched that barge load and said nothing, and I want to know whether I'm talking to the person who's going to stop this or the person who's going to let it happen and hate herself quietly afterward. Because those are different conversations, Yara, and I've had both, and I'd like to know which one I'm having."

For a long moment Yara didn't answer. She looked down at the gate — at the blast-scarred face of it, at the water — and Nia saw something move through her expression that she'd only seen once before, the night at the funeral twelve years ago, when a seventeen-year-old girl had stood at the edge of the mourners with a signed report in her family's files and a face like a locked door.

"My father sealed this place," Yara said finally. "He killed himself doing it, or he died sealing it, depending on which story you believe, and I signed the paper that said accident because I was seventeen and terrified and they told me signing was how I protected the crew." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Twelve years later I'm standing on his grave making deals with the man who bought his killers' employers' grandchildren's trust funds. So when you ask me whether I'm going to stop this or let it happen—" she turned, and her eyes were dry and very tired— "I'll tell you what I can, Nia. I will not sign another paper for these people. Whatever happens next, that much is decided. The rest—" 

She stopped. Below them, at the barge landing, Ferro had appeared with two of Kessler's security men, and even at this distance Nia could see him gesturing — toward the portal, toward the corridor mouth, toward the timetable pinned to the site board. Checking progress. Hurrying the grave-diggers.

"He's moved the schedule," Nia said. "Hasn't he. The monsoon's early, so he's using it. Firing the charges before the storm makes the roads impassable, calling it emergency mitigation."

"Okonkwo's models say landfall in six days. Ferro wants the lower gallery fired in three."

"Three days." Nia exhaled slowly. "Then we're out of time for quiet decisions."

"We were out of time the day Kessler landed his helicopter." Yara picked up the spanner she'd abandoned, turned it over in her hands like she was remembering what tools were for. "Get your charges ready, Nia. Drill your stations. Do everything Ferro's order says, exactly, visibly — I want Kessler's people certain you're a good little contractor right up until the moment it matters. Can you do that?"

Nia studied her. "And when the moment comes?"

"When it comes, I'll tell you what the charges are really for." Yara started back down the tramline, then paused, not looking back. "One thing first. The cabinet downstairs — the paper in it. Did you touch anything?"

"No. I looked. That's all."

"Keep it that way. And Nia—" now she did turn, and something in her face had hardened into decision, or into the shape decision leaves behind— "if anyone asks you what's in the lower gallery, you saw mud and rust and nothing else. Not Bea, not Tomas, not Okonkwo. No one. Not yet."

"Not even Bea?" Nia raised an eyebrow. "She's your cousin. She dove the sluice channel for you."

"Especially not Bea." Something flickered there — pain, quickly filed. "Bea has dive logs Kessler's people have already falsified once. Everything she touches, he can twist. She stays clean of this until it's done."

Nia nodded slowly, and understood, and hated the understanding: the fault lines weren't coming. They were already here. They ran through the crew like the fractures through the gallery rock — invisible from the surface, mapped only by the people willing to go down and feel for them. On one side, Kessler's payroll and Ferro's clipboard and the comfortable lie of stabilization. On the other, whatever Yara Vasquez was building in the dark behind her tired eyes, with a hydrologist's flood maps and an explosives expert's charges and a diver she couldn't afford to trust.

And Nia herself, standing on the line, holding sixteen marked stations and a detonator she hadn't yet wired.

"All right," she said. "I'll drill your holes, boss. Just remember one thing when your moment comes."

"What's that?"

"I don't blow mountains for free, and I don't blow them for liars. Whatever you're planning — when you tell me, it better be the truth. All of it. Because the next thing I set off, I want to know exactly what I'm burying, and exactly what I'm digging out."

Yara held her gaze for a long second. Down in the gorge, the river kept moving, patient and brown and full of everything it carried.

"You'll get the truth," Yara said. "All of it. Tomorrow night. Bring the crew."

She walked on down toward the pumps, and Nia stood alone on the dead rails above the drowned gate, listening to the water breathe in the dark below, and began — quietly, methodically, with the care of a woman who knew exactly what she was being paid to destroy — to plan what she would build instead.
