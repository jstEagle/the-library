## 24. The Confession at the Portal

The rain came in the afternoon, not the real monsoon yet but its herald—a warm, fat-dropped drizzle that turned the gorge's dust to red paste and sent the camp under tarps and into the portal's mouth for shelter. By evening the work had stopped being possible and started being merely dangerous, and Kessler's men grumbled over cards in the mess tent while Nia inventoried her caps by lamplight with the door of her magazine zipped shut against damp.

Yara found Bea at the portal.

Not inside it—outside, on the concrete apron her father had poured in another lifetime, where the river slid past six feet below and the old survey brass still glinted in the floodlights' spill. Bea sat with her legs hanging over the edge, dive mask pushed up on her forehead, running a thumb along the regulator hose she'd stripped down for the third time that week. Maintenance as meditation. Yara knew the ritual. She'd invented a dozen versions of it herself.

She sat down beside her cousin. The concrete was cold through her trousers, cold as something that had been underwater for twelve years and hadn't entirely forgiven the air.

Bea didn't look at her. "Pump two's cavitating again. Okonkwo thinks there's silt in the intake."

"I know."

"Then why are you here?"

Yara watched the water. It moved the way it always did at dusk—black on black, only the sound of it, a long low exhale against the rock. Somewhere upstream, the thing Okonkwo couldn't name was holding back the sky.

"Because I owe you a story," Yara said. "And I've been paying everyone else first because yours is the hardest."

Bea's hands went still on the regulator.

* * *

Twelve years ago, Yara had been seventeen years old and certain that adulthood was a place you arrived at by signing things.

Her father had taught her that much, at least—the paperwork of the bush. Waybills, manifests, incident reports. A signature was a promise that what you wrote was true, and her father had been religious about truth on paper even when he lied fluently in person. *The report is the only thing they can't argue with later,* he used to say, tapping the carbon copies. *Say it plain, sign it clean.*

So she told Bea about the last night of the survey expedition, and she told it plain.

They had come down out of the lower gallery at dusk, four of them—her father, the two company men whose names Yara had spent twelve years refusing to remember and could not forget, and herself, carrying the sample bags. Her father had found something in the flooded chamber below the lock he'd built, something that made him walk differently, like a man carrying a full cup across a moving deck. He wouldn't say what. He said it had to go to the river communities first, before anyone else saw it, before the company's people came back up the line. He said the expedition was over. He said it loudly, in front of the company men, and then he said quietly, to Yara alone: *Whatever happens tonight, you get on the barge tomorrow. You don't ask questions. You just go.*

"What happened tonight?" Bea asked.

"The explosion," Yara said. "You know that part. Everyone knows that part. The official story is that my father misjudged a charge while sealing a unstable section, that the blast brought down the lower gallery access, that he was caught in his own demolition." She heard her own voice go flat, the way it did when she read cargo manifests aloud. "I signed the addendum to that story. Page three. *Witness statement, Yara Vasquez, age seventeen, daughter of the deceased.*"

The drizzle ticked against the tarp overhead. Downriver, someone in Kessler's mess tent laughed at a card.

"There was no misjudged charge," Yara said. "My father didn't touch the detonator that night. The two company men set it themselves, after he told them the expedition was finished and the gallery would stay sealed. They waited until he went back down to retrieve his instruments—they insisted he go back down, Bea, they *sent* him—and then they fired the shot from the upper gallery and let the mountain do the rest." Her throat closed around the next sentence and she made herself push through it. "I was twenty meters away. I heard him call out once. Then the rock came down, and after that nobody called out at all."

Bea had stopped breathing somewhere in the middle of it. Yara could feel it, the way you feel a held breath in your own chest, sympathetic.

"They walked me back to camp," Yara went on. "It took maybe an hour. Long enough for them to explain how it worked. My father had insurance through the company—death benefit, double for line-of-duty. There was a crew of eleven waiting at base camp who'd all be paid off if the closure report went through clean, and none of them paid if the report said sabotage, because sabotage meant investigation, and investigation meant the operation froze, and frozen operations don't disburse." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "I was seventeen. I did the arithmetic they gave me. Eleven paychecks against one word on one page. And they were kind about it, that's the worst part. They sat me down with tea. One of them cried—he was good at crying, I'll give him that—and he said, *Your father would want the crew looked after.* And I believed him. God help me, I wanted to believe him so badly that I signed page three in handwriting so neat it looked typeset."

"You were seventeen," Bea said.

"I was old enough to know better. That's what being seventeen means." Yara finally turned to look at her cousin. In the floodlight's edge Bea's face was half shadow, half wet shine, and unreadable in either. "I told myself for twelve years that I signed to protect the crew. Some nights I even believe it. But here's the true version, the one I've never said out loud: I signed because they scared me. Because a man who cries well told me my father would have wanted it, and I wanted my father's permission for anything, even for burying him wrong. I took the story they handed me because the alternative story had my father screaming behind forty feet of collapsed rock, and I could not carry that one. So I carried theirs instead. I've been carrying it ever since. Every contract I take twice, every season I refuse to stay—it's all the same run, Bea. Running from page three."

The river exhaled against the rock. Bea set the regulator down on the concrete between them, carefully, the way you put down something you might need to pick up fast.

"Uncle Emilio," she said. Her voice came out rough. "At the funeral, you wouldn't stand with us. You stood apart, by the trucks, with your arms crossed like you were guarding something. Tía thought you were angry at the family."

"I was guarding the story," Yara said. "If anyone asked me anything, I was going to recite page three, and I couldn't trust my face while I did it. Distance was safer for everyone."

"Twelve years," Bea said. "Twelve years, Yara. You let me hate you a little. Not big-hate. Small-hate. The kind that lives in the back of the throat. Every Christmas. Every time Tía mentioned your name and changed the subject too fast." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, an impatient gesture, furious at the tears for arriving without clearance. "And the whole time you were—what? Doing penance? Hauling rail iron up dead spurs like some kind of—"

"Yes," Yara said. "Exactly like some kind of that."

Bea made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and entirely exhausted, and then she leaned sideways until her shoulder pressed Yara's shoulder, and stayed there. The contact was brief and hard and worth more than everything Kessler had put on the table in his helicopter-borne generosity.

"I forgive you," Bea said into the dark past the portal's lip. "For page three. For the funeral. For making me learn to dive so I'd have something of his that wasn't yours." She straightened. "That's done. You hear me? Done. Don't drag it out again. Don't make it a thing we revisit at altitude."

"I hear you."

"Good." Bea picked the regulator back up, and Yara watched the forgiveness settle into her cousin's hands—not softness exactly, but a loosening, weight redistributed. Then Bea looked at her, and the floodlight caught her eyes, and what was in them was not softness at all.

"Now the harder question," Bea said.

Yara braced.

"You're going to sign something again." Bea nodded toward the portal, toward the dark beyond it where the pumps throbbed and the crates sat in their rows and the ledger pages lived in the memory card taped inside Yara's boot. "Kessler's already got the closure paperwork drafted—I've seen it on his table, same as you have. *Structural failure, monsoon damage, no recoverable cargo.* Same words, new letterhead. He's going to slide it across to you in the next few days and tell you it protects the crew, and you're going to recognize the feeling, because you've felt it before, at seventeen, with tea." She tapped the concrete twice, a diver checking her own pulse against the deck. "So. What will you sign this time?"

* * *

Yara didn't answer right away, and Bea had the grace not to fill the silence.

What could she say? That she'd already decided? She hadn't—that was the honest truth, and honesty was the whole currency of this conversation, and she refused to debase it now. She had photographs of the ledger in her boot and no plan for them. She had a father's message scratched inside a steel door—*Flood it. Tell no one. The river keeps what it keeps*—and a rival's ultimatum forming somewhere in the mess tent like weather. She had eleven people in this camp, again, always eleven, the number followed her—and every path she could see ended with some of them hurt.

"I don't know yet," she said at last. "But I know this much. Last time, I signed what other people wrote. This time, if I sign anything, I write it myself. Word by word. Even if the words are lies, they'll be *my* lies, chosen, paid for, understood. That's the difference. That's the whole difference, and maybe it isn't enough, but it's mine."

Bea considered this with the flat attention she gave current readings and decompression tables.

"It's a start," she said. "Here's what a diver knows that a rail-runner doesn't. When you're deep and something goes wrong, you don't get to choose whether the situation happens. You only get to choose what you do in the first ten seconds. Everything after that is just the consequences of those ten seconds." She stood, slinging the regulator over her shoulder, and offered Yara a hand up—an offer, not a demand, which from Bea was practically a love letter. "Your ten seconds are coming. Probably sooner than you want. Kessler's counting crates tonight; I watched his foreman doing inventory by flashlight like a man rehearsing. Whatever he's planning, it has a schedule."

Yara took the hand and let herself be pulled upright. Her knees ached from the cold concrete. Twelve years older than the girl who signed page three, and the ache was the proof.

"One more thing," Bea said. She hesitated, and Yara realized her cousin was deciding whether to spend something she'd been saving. "When I dove the sump and the channel grabbed me—you broke protocol to pull me out. Your own rule, the one you've never broken for anyone. Tomas told me afterward you went in without a line." Her jaw worked. "Why?"

"Because it was you."

"That's not an answer. That's a reason. Why break the rule?"

Yara looked at the portal—at the concrete face, the blast scars, the dark throat of the tunnel where her father's work and her father's death and her father's message all waited together in the wet black. "Because rules are how I keep the world at arm's length," she said. "And I'm tired of arm's length. I'm tired of motion as a way of standing still. If the choice is between the rule and the person, I want to be someone who picks the person. I want that to be the thing I sign, even when there's no paper."

Bea studied her for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way she acknowledged a depth reading that matched her tables.

"All right," she said. "Then when Kessler's paper comes, remember what you just said. Because he's going to make it about the crew. He's going to make it about protecting people. That's how they got you at seventeen and it's how they'll try to get you now—the trap doesn't change, Yara, only the bait gets older." She shouldered her gear and turned toward the camp lights, then paused. "And whatever you decide—decide it before the paper comes. Never sign anything you decided under pressure. First rule of the family business."

She walked away down the apron, small against the gorge wall, sure-footed on wet concrete, and Yara stood alone at the portal with the river talking its long dark talk below.

* * *

She didn't sleep.

She tried—went to her tent, lay down, closed her eyes—but the camp sounds arranged themselves into the old expedition's sounds, and page three kept unspooling behind her eyelids in her own seventeen-year-old handwriting. Around midnight she gave up and walked the perimeter, and that was how she came to see the light moving inside the portal at an hour when no work was scheduled.

Not a headlamp. A lantern, steady, low, carried with care.

Yara flattened against the pump housing and watched. Two figures moved among the crate rows in the upper gallery's mouth, and between them they passed objects—small, wrapped, deliberate—from the sealed crates into long waterproof duffels. The duffels went out a side breach she hadn't known was cleared, toward the river side, toward where the barge lay dark at its moorings.

Kessler's men. Loading at night. Moving cargo that had no manifest, no permit, no destination Yara had approved.

Her first instinct was to move—to stride in, lantern or no lantern, and put her body between the crates and the duffels the way she'd put it between Bea and the flood. Her muscles actually gathered for it, twelve years of forward motion compressed into one step.

She made herself stay flat against the pump housing.

Because Bea's question was still ringing in her, and the answer she'd given at the portal—*I write it myself, word by word*—meant nothing if she acted blind. If she confronted them now, she'd burn her cover, lose her access to the lower gallery, lose the ledger's context, lose everything her father had died twice for, and Kessler would simply reschedule. The theft would happen with her or without her. The only question was whether she'd see the whole shape of it first.

*Let it happen,* whispered the cold practical voice that had signed page three, and Yara flinched at the sound of it.

*No,* she answered it silently. *Watch it happen. Learn it. Then end it—my way, on my schedule, with the words written by my own hand.*

The figures finished their work and folded away into the dark toward the river, and the lantern light died, and the portal stood open and silent, breathing its cold mineral breath.

Yara stayed against the pump housing until her legs cramped, memorizing the side breach, the count of the duffels, the hour. Then she turned back toward camp—and stopped.

A figure stood at the edge of the floodlight spill. For one lurching second she thought of the watcher on the ridge, of scouts and surveillance and Kessler's eyes everywhere.

It was Tomas. Arms crossed, watching the portal, watching her, having clearly watched everything.

He didn't ask what she'd seen. He just fell into step beside her as she walked, matching her pace, saying nothing, the way he'd stood watch outside her tent without being asked. But as they reached the tents he spoke once, quietly, to the dark ahead of them rather than to her:

"Storm's turning. Radio says landfall's early. Days early."

Above the gorge, the herald-rain thickened, and far upstream, the thing that held back the water shifted its grip—and the river began, very softly, to rise.
