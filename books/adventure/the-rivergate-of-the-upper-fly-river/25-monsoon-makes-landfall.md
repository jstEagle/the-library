## 25. Monsoon Makes Landfall

The storm did not arrive. It announced itself.

Yara woke to a sound she had heard only twice in her life—once as a child, once at seventeen, the night her father sealed the Rivergate—and both times it had meant that everything downstream of the mountains was about to change. It was not thunder. Thunder came from above and passed. This sound came from upriver, from the high valleys, and it did not pass. It was the noise of an entire watershed deciding, all at once, to let go.

She was out of her tent before her boots were laced.

The sky over the gorge had gone the color of wet slate, and the rain came down not in drops but in sheets, in walls, in a horizontal driving gray that turned the camp's floodlights into smears of yellow. The Fly had been brown for weeks. Now it was the color of iron, and it had climbed. She could see it even from the ridge above camp—the marker post they'd driven at the waterline three days ago, the one Okonkwo had painted red at the six-foot line, was gone. Swallowed whole.

"Okonkwo!" she shouted into the wind, and found him already at the hydrology station, hunched over his instruments like a man praying over them. His screens were alive with data that made no comfortable sense. The stage gauge read 5.8 meters and climbing. The upstream telemetry—what little signal fought its way through the storm—showed tributaries peaking simultaneously, catchments saturating, the whole basin draining into one channel at once.

"Nine days," Yara said. "You said nine days."

"I said nine days based on the models." He didn't look up. His voice was flat, professional, and underneath it something she'd never heard from him before: something close to apology. "The models assumed the monsoon would make landfall where it always does. It didn't. It came ashore two hundred kilometers west and cut straight across the divide. Everything on the wrong side of the mountains is coming down the Fly." He finally turned. Rain ran off the brim of his hood. "This isn't the monsoon, Yara. This is the monsoon arriving angry."

"How high?"

He looked back at the gauge reading, then at her, and did the thing he always did when the news was bad: he gave her the number without decoration. "Another four meters by morning. Maybe five. The gorge narrows below us—it'll back up here first."

Five meters. Nearly seventeen feet. Her mind went where she knew it would go, where she'd been keeping it away from for two days: the barge.

* * *

Kessler's crew had loaded the first shipment onto the barge yesterday evening—twelve crates of "recovered material," plaster and river clay under museum-grade packing, though only six people in the world knew that—and had anchored it mid-channel to wait out the final days of pumping before it ran downstream to Kessler's waiting transport. Standard procedure. Safe anchorage in deep water, well clear of the debris that floods carry along the banks.

Except nobody had planned for a river that rose six feet in a night.

By the time Yara reached the portal, the scene at the landing was chaos trying to organize itself into panic. Kessler's men were hauling equipment upslope, shouting over the rain. The pump lines that fed the dewatering operation had been shut down—not sabotaged this time, just abandoned, the crews pulled off them as the water climbed toward the intake pipes. In twelve hours, everything they'd pumped out of the Rivergate for three weeks would start flowing back in.

And the barge sat in the middle of it all, dead center in the channel, riding higher than it should have because the river beneath it had swollen around its anchor lines and lifted it, straining them taut, dragging the anchors through the gravel bottom with a slow, grinding inevitability that anyone who knew boats could read from a hundred meters away.

It was drifting. Downstream. Toward the narrows.

Tomas reached her first, soaked through, shouting directly into her ear because the wind ate anything less. "The anchors are walking! Both of them! If it comes loose it goes into the rapids and—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Everyone at the landing knew what waited eight kilometers downstream: a granite throat the river squeezed itself through, full of undercut rock and reversal currents, a place the local boatmen didn't enter even in dry season.

"The winch barge?" Yara shouted back.

"Tied up at the lower landing. Its deck crane might reach if we can get close enough."

"Then we get close enough."

"No."

The word cut through the rain like a blade. Dario Kessler stood at the top of the landing ramp, flanked by two of his security men, and he wasn't looking at the barge. He was looking at Yara, and his face held the particular calm of a man who has already done the arithmetic and doesn't intend to watch anyone redo it.

"It's too dangerous," he said. "The current in mid-channel is past rescue speed. I'm not losing people or equipment on a salvage gamble."

"It's your cargo," Yara said.

"And it's insured." He said it without shame, without even a flicker. That was the thing about Dario Kessler that twelve years of rivalry had taught her to recognize: he never pretended. Cruelty dressed as kindness was his habit, but in moments of real pressure he became almost honest. "Twelve crates, Yara. Paper and packing foam and whatever's inside them. I can replace every item on that manifest. I cannot replace a drowned crew. Nobody works the channel tonight."

Behind him, his men shifted. Some of them looked relieved. A few—one or two, faces she'd started to know over these weeks—looked at the barge, and at the small figure standing on its deck, and their relief curdled into something else.

Because there was someone on the barge. Of course there was. Rafferty, one of Kessler's riggers, had ridden out with the load last night to keep the anchor watch, and when the storm hit he'd been alone on a steel deck in the middle of a rising river with no way back. They could see him now through the rain, a small dark shape at the barge's rail, working frantically at the winch controls, trying to motor the barge toward shore against a current that was carrying him backward.

"He's got power," Bea said, appearing at Yara's shoulder, hair plastered flat, eyes bright with the cold clarity that diving gave her. "If he can get the bow into the current he might hold station until the water drops."

"He can't," Okonkwo said quietly. He'd come down from the station, and he stood looking at the river with the expression of a man reading a sentence he wished weren't there. "Look at the eddy line. The main current's pushing him toward the far bank, and the far bank is where the flood debris is running. Whole trees. If one of those hits him broadside at that flow rate—" He shook his head. "He has maybe an hour before the anchors go. Less if the debris finds him first."

"So we take the winch barge out," Yara said. "We come alongside, we take him off, and if the river gives us ten minutes we put a line on the cargo too."

"I said no one works the channel." Kessler's voice hardened. "That's an order, Vasquez. Your contract is with my expedition, and your contract says—"

"My contract says I run safety on this operation." She stepped closer, close enough that the rain between them seemed to thin. "You wrote that clause yourself, Dario. After the pump-line sabotage. You wanted my name on the safety protocols so the liability sat with me. Well. It's sitting with me now."

For a moment neither of them moved. The rain hammered the landing. Somewhere behind them, metal banged against metal as Kessler's men secured equipment that no longer mattered.

"You'd risk six people for one rigger and twelve crates of—" Kessler stopped himself, barely, a half-second too late.

"Of what?" Yara asked softly. "Say it."

His jaw worked. Whatever he'd been about to say—*of fakes*, of plaster and clay, of cargo worth exactly the insurance paper it rode on—he swallowed it, because saying it aloud in front of this crowd would cost him more than the barge ever could. His own men were listening. His own men were watching the rigger on the deck and doing their own arithmetic about what kind of man left a man on a sinking island of steel because the paperwork said the contents were covered.

"Save the cargo first," Kessler said finally. "If you're going out anyway, save the cargo first. Then the man. That's the order. Cargo, then crew. In writing, Vasquez, so everyone knows whose decision it was."

"There it is," Tomas said, very quietly, beside her. Not meant to be heard. Meant to be heard.

Yara looked at the barge again. At Rafferty, tiny against the gray water, still fighting the winch. At the crates lashed under tarps amidships. And she understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that this moment was not about the barge at all. Every person on this landing was watching her, and every one of them was learning, right now, in the rain, what the expedition actually valued—and what it would ask them to value about themselves.

"Cargo, then crew," she repeated. "Fine. That's the order I received."

Kessler nodded, satisfied, and turned to go inside.

"That's not the order I'm giving," Yara finished.

* * *

They took the winch barge out with six: Yara at the helm, Tomas on the deck winch, Bea and Nia handling lines, and two of Kessler's own men—Osei and Brandt—who stepped aboard without being asked, without orders, without looking back at the landing where their employer stood watching. Yara marked their faces. She would remember them later.

The first hundred meters were manageable. The second hundred were not.

The river had changed species. What had been a channel was now a moving hillside of water, and the winch barge climbed swells that hadn't existed an hour ago, wallowed in troughs, took green water over the bow that knocked the deck crew off their feet and ripped one coil of line clean off its cleat. Yara drove by feel and by Okonkwo's voice crackling over the radio—the hydrologist had positioned himself at the gorge rim with a spotlight and a view of the whole channel, calling the debris runs before they arrived.

"Log, log, log—far side, coming down fast, thirty meters, hold your line—"

A whole tree went past them, root ball first, traveling faster than the barge could motor, spinning slowly like something drowning. It missed them by the length of the barge. Nia watched it go with wide eyes and said nothing, which from Nia was a great deal of speech.

Rafferty saw them coming and began, visibly, to lose his composure—waving both arms, pointing first at himself and then, horribly, obediently, at the crates. Even now, even alone on a drowning deck, he'd absorbed the order. Cargo first. He was pointing at the boxes instead of the rescue line.

"To hell with that," Bea said, and was already coiling the throw line.

Getting alongside a drifting barge in flood current is a violence disguised as a maneuver. The winch barge came in on the downstream quarter, using the current itself to press them together, and the impact threw everyone sideways. Bea had the heaving line across the gap before the hulls stopped grinding. Rafferty caught it on the second throw, and then he was coming across hand over hand on the messenger, over black churning water, and the moment his boots hit the winch barge's deck his legs simply quit and Osei caught him under the arms and dragged him clear.

"Anchors," Tomas shouted. "We can still get a line on the—"

The river answered before he finished. There was a sound like a rifle shot, muffled by water and distance, and every head turned. Out in the channel, the barge swung sharply as the starboard anchor parted. It began to pivot, stern-first, into the main current.

"Sixty seconds," Okonkwo's voice said in Yara's ear, very calm. "Maybe less. When it goes broadside it's gone."

Yara looked at the crates. Twelve of them, lashed under sodden tarps, each one heavy enough to need two men and a winch to move. Sixty seconds. She could hear Kessler's voice as clearly as if he stood beside her: *Cargo, then crew. In writing.*

She thought of her father's handwriting instead. *The river keeps what it keeps.*

"Cut it loose," she said. "Everyone off the deck edge. We're letting it go."

"We can save two crates," Tomas argued, already moving to comply. "The two nearest the rail—"

"Two crates won't float a barge's worth of guilt, Tomi. Let it go."

The barge swung broadside. The current took it the way a hand takes a leaf, and it went downstream toward the narrows, riding high and slow and doomed, twelve crates of plaster and river clay rocking gently under their tarps like sleeping things. Nia watched it go and started, very quietly, to laugh—a strange, breathless sound that might have been relief.

Because Nia knew what was in those crates. So did Yara. So did Bea, and the elder's people upstream, and the six other people in the world who mattered. The river was taking nothing but a lie, and delivering it to the rocks.

The real cargo was already three days upriver, wrapped in sago palm mats, moving through villages that sang at night.

* * *

They came back to the landing soaked to the soul and were met by silence—which was worse than cheering and better than Kessler's face. He stood apart from his men, arms crossed, watching them tie off, and when Yara stepped onto the ramp he spoke just loudly enough to carry.

"You disobeyed a direct order."

"I amended it."

"You cost me a shipment."

"The river cost you a shipment. I cost you nothing but the illusion that your men will drown on command." She stripped off her gloves, finger by finger, never breaking eye contact. "Ask them, Dario. Go on. Ask any man who was on that deck tonight whose order they'd follow tomorrow."

He didn't ask. That was the answer, and they both knew it. Behind him, Osei and Brandt stood dripping among her crew, and no one told them to go back to the other side of the camp. No one needed to.

But as Yara walked past him toward the light of the mess tent, Kessler leaned close, and his voice dropped to something private and cold, meant for her alone.

"Enjoy the heroism while it lasts, Vasquez. The water's rising everywhere tonight. Not just in the channel." He smiled, thin as a knife's edge. "I wonder what else it's reaching. Ask your sister what she keeps in her dive locker."

Yara stopped walking.

Bea's certification papers. The falsified dive logs. The leverage Kessler had spent weeks assembling, sitting in a filing cabinet in the operations tent like a loaded weapon—and the operations tent was on the low ground, near the landing, and the river was climbing five meters by morning.

She turned and looked at the camp: the tents sagging under rain, the cables strung with lights, the operations tent at the waterline with its records and manifests and carefully curated evidence. And beyond it, up the slope, the portal of the Rivergate, dark and patient, drinking back everything they'd pumped out of it.

The monsoon had landed. Nothing was going to stay where anyone had put it.

"Okonkwo," she called. "How long do we have before this camp floods?"

He consulted his gauges, and the number he gave her was smaller than the night.
