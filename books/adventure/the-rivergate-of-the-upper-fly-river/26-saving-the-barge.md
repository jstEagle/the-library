## 26. Saving the Barge

The river came for the barge at dusk, the way a creditor comes—patient, inevitable, and without malice.

Yara stood on the portal apron and watched it happen. The *Meridian Star*, Kessler's floating warehouse, sat mid-channel where the flood had caught her with her anchors dragging, six hundred tons of counterfeit crates and one very real crane barge pinned against the current like a moth on glass. Her running lights made small orange smears in the rain. Behind her, upstream, the gorge roared with water that had risen six feet since sundown and was not finished.

"She's holding," Okonkwo said beside her. He had to half-shout over the storm. "The hull's sound. It's the moorings I don't trust. That stern line will part within the hour."

"And then?"

"Then she swings broadside into the current, rolls, and everything on deck goes into the river. Including whoever Kessler left aboard to guard his cargo." Okonkwo's face was a pale oval under his hood. "He ordered them to stay. Told them the cargo was worth more than their lives, more or less. He used politer words."

"How many?"

"Four. Maybe five. The radio traffic is bad. I heard Voss answer, and two others confirm. There might be a deckhand who never keyed up."

Yara watched the barge lurch as a wave took her quarter. The stern line groaned—a sound she felt in her back teeth, a sound she knew from twelve years of dead rails and failing bridges, the specific frequency of steel deciding whether to hold or to quit.

Kessler stood fifty meters down the apron under a tarp shelter, lit by a work lamp, surrounded by men with radios. She could not hear what he said, but she could read the shape of it: arms crossed, jaw set, pointing at the barge, then at the portal, then at the barge again. Organizing. Prioritizing. Deciding, with the calm of a man spending money he hadn't earned, that the cargo would be saved first and the crew second—if there was time.

Tomas appeared at her shoulder, soaked through, breathing hard from the climb up from camp. "They're asking for volunteers," he said. "Kessler's people. To take a boat out. Nobody's moving."

"Because nobody wants to die for plaster crates they think are real."

"Because," Tomas said, "they watched him do the math out loud. I heard him. He said if the barge goes, we lose the shipment, but if we lose two men getting her back, we still have the shipment and we've lost nothing that can't be replaced." He paused. "His words."

The stern line parted with a crack Yara heard even over the storm, a gunshot swallowed by thunder. The *Meridian Star* swung her bow downstream, slow and terrible, like a door opening.

She was moving before she'd decided to move.

* * *

The dead rails ran along the north bank of the gorge, twelve years rusted, half-buried in scree, climbing away from the portal toward the old Rivergate loading yard before dropping again toward the lower channel where the river widened and slowed—or had slowed, once, before the monsoon turned it into something with opinions.

Yara had walked those rails in daylight a hundred times as a girl, when her father was surveying the spur and she was his shadow with a notebook. She knew every culvert, every washout, every place the ballast had slumped into the cut. In the dark, in the flood, with the river eating the bank thirty meters below—it was a different country. But it was a country she had a map for, and that was more than anyone else on this river could say.

"Nia!" She found her at the magazine tent, already pulling waterproof charges cases, because Nia's answer to every crisis began with explosives and worked backward. "Not tonight. Tonight I need rope and lights and your worst temper."

"You have all three. What are we doing?"

"The barge. Kessler's sending a boat from the upper landing, but the channel between here and her is a washing machine, and his pilot has never run this gorge in the dark. I'm going to give him a line to follow."

Okonkwo, arriving with Bea behind him, shook his head immediately. "You want to rig a guide line along the north bank? The bank is going under. By midnight the rails will be knee-deep, by two they'll be gone."

"Then we work fast." She was coiling rope as she talked, muscle memory, hands doing the arithmetic while her mouth did the arguing. "We anchor at the old loading yard stanchions—concrete, poured by my father, rated for locomotives, they'll hold. We run the line down the rail corridor, high enough to clear the flood at the culverts, and we hang strobes every fifty meters. Any boat that follows that line stays off the rocks and out of the eddy below the bend. Without it, they're guessing. With it—" she cinched the knot, looked up, "—they're navigating."

Bea had gone very still. "And you're going to walk that line yourself."

"I know the corridor."

"It'll be dark and the river will be at your knees and you haven't slept in two days."

"Yes," Yara said. "All true. Are you coming?"

Something moved across Bea's face—not quite a smile, but its cousin. "Someone has to keep you honest about the depth readings. Okonkwo, give me your gauge. And someone tell Kessler what we're doing before he shoots at us thinking we're looters."

"I'll tell him," Tomas said grimly. "I'll enjoy it."

* * *

Kessler did not try to stop them. That was the thing Yara kept turning over later, the detail that would not sit flat: he didn't argue, didn't pull rank, didn't remind her whose contract she was working. He stood in the lamplight with rain streaming off his jaw and looked at her for three full seconds, and then he said, "What do you need?"

"Your radios on our frequency. Your landing crew ready to receive. And your men told that if my line is lit, they follow it exactly, no improvising, or they'll end up in the eddy below the bend and the crocodiles can sort out the paperwork."

"They're not my men," Kessler said quietly. "That's what you don't understand, Vasquez. They're employees. There's a difference, and the difference is that I can't order them to die." He glanced upstream at the swinging barge. "I can only ask. Most nights, asking is enough. Tonight it isn't." He turned back to her, and for one unguarded moment he looked less like an empire than like a tired man in the rain. "Why are you doing this? They're guarding cargo you've told me you don't care about."

Yara checked the last carabiner on her harness and did not look up. "Because four years ago a survey crew left me on a ridge in a storm because I was cargo too, and not valuable enough. Somebody came anyway. I've been trying to be that somebody ever since." She clipped the line to her belt. "Get your radios sorted, Dario. We're burning night."

* * *

The rail corridor was a black throat swallowing rain.

They went in a team of five: Yara leading, Bea ten meters back with the gauge and the light, Nia hauling the rope drum, and two of Nia's riggers—Sefa and the quiet one called Miro—who had volunteered before Yara finished asking. The strobes went up as they came: chemical light sticks zip-tied to the catenary stanchions, each one a small green heart beating in the murk, fifty meters apart, a rosary of them strung down the drowned corridor.

The water found them at the first culvert. It came over the rails at mid-shin, then thigh-deep, cold enough to make her gasp, carrying debris—branches, a plastic crate, something soft and unidentifiable that bumped her leg and moved on. The current pushed sideways across the cut, patient and strong, and twice Yara went to one knee and felt the river consider taking her, weighing her, finding her not yet worth the trouble.

"Gauge!" she shouted, and Bea's voice came back through the rain: "One point one! Rising!"

At the bend above the lower channel, the rails left the cut and ran along a shelf of rock above the water, and from there Yara saw the whole catastrophe laid out below her like a diagram. The *Meridian Star* had swung broadside, exactly as Okonkwo predicted, and was grinding her flank against a submerged gravel bar with a sound like a giant clearing his throat. Her deck lights still burned. Figures moved on deck—small, frantic, backlit. The upper landing was two kilometers downstream, and between the barge and safety lay a stretch of channel where the flood had erased the shoreline entirely, where the water ran brown and muscular over trees that had been trees that morning.

Kessler's rescue boat was already in it.

She saw the running lights first, two white points bucking wildly, and understood in one cold flash that they'd launched before her line reached them—that some brave idiot at the landing had taken a boat into that channel on compass and prayer, and the prayer was losing. The boat climbed a swell, hung, and vanished into the trough behind it, and when it rose again it was sideways, shipping water, being carried toward the eddy below the bend where the river folded back on itself and held things.

"Hold the line here!" Yara screamed at Sefa, and then she was running along the shelf, rope paying out behind her, toward the anchor point at the old yard stanchion—the concrete her father had poured, the concrete that would hold—and her plan rearranged itself in her head with the speed of things that happen faster than thought.

The line couldn't reach the boat. But the line could reach the barge. And the barge had a crane.

"Miro! Get to the stanchion, lock the main line off!" She was shouting into the radio now, keying Kessler's frequency, praying someone at the landing was listening. "Barge, barge, this is Vasquez at the portal. If you have a crane operator alive up there, swing your boom downstream and drop your hook line to the north bank shelf—I'm marking the point with strobes. You get a man ashore, he clips our guide line, and we walk your whole crew out along the rails. Do you copy? *Do you copy?*"

Static. Rain. Then a voice, young and terrified and galvanized: "*Copy, Portal. Copy.*"

* * *

It took forty minutes to bring the first man across.

The barge's crane swung its boom through the storm like a wounded animal feeling for the ground, and on the third pass the hook line came close enough to the shelf that Yara could grab it—close enough that it nearly took her off her feet when the barge lurched. A figure came down the cable hand over hand, swinging wide over the flood, and Yara and Miro hauled him onto the rock and clipped him to the guide line before he could even speak.

He was maybe twenty-five. Kessler's coveralls. Eyes enormous. His name, when he could talk, was Anselm, and he was the deckhand who had never keyed up, and he wrapped both arms around the guide rope like it was his mother.

"Others?" Yara shouted.

"Voss won't leave! He says the cargo manifest—he says Mr. Kessler said—" The boy's face did something complicated. "He's staying with the crates."

Yara looked down the guide line, at the green strobes marching away into the dark, at the channel where Kessler's volunteer boat was still fighting its way toward the landing with its rescued crew of none. Then she looked at the barge, grinding itself to death on the bar, defending plaster statues with a dying man's loyalty.

"Anselm." She took his chin and made him look at her. "How many crates fit in your wheelhouse?"

"What?"

"The wheelhouse. Is it dry inside?"

"...Yes?"

"Then here's what you're going to tell Voss." She leaned close so the words wouldn't drown. "Tell him the manifest is already photographed. Tell him every page exists in three copies in three places he'll never find. Tell him the crates are worth exactly nothing except to the man who needs him to believe otherwise, and ask him how much his life weighs on that scale." She let go of his chin. "Can you carry that message?"

The boy looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and went back up the cable into the storm.

* * *

They brought eleven men across the guide line that night.

One at a time, clipped to the rope, hand over hand along the drowned rails while Yara or Bea or Miro stood at the bad spots and called the depths and the footing—*left side, high ballast, don't trust the tie*—in a voice worn down to wire. The strobes burned green through the worst of it. Twice the river surged to waist height and they stopped the whole procession and waited, eleven soaked strangers and five of her own, pressed against the cut wall, feeling the water decide. Both times it decided to let them go.

Voss came across ninth. He was a heavyset man in his fifties with a foreman's gray stubble and the particular silence of someone who has just discovered his employer's arithmetic includes him. He crossed the line without speaking, stood on the shelf above the portal, and looked back at the barge for a long time.

"You told Anselm the manifest was photographed," he said finally. Not a question.

Yara, wringing out her sleeves, didn't bother denying it. "Was I wrong?"

"No." Voss's jaw worked. "I loaded those crates myself. Three weeks, night shifts, sealed orders. I've been telling myself it was mining equipment." He turned and looked at her straight on, and there was something in his face that she recognized from her own mirror on certain mornings—the look of a person auditing their own ledger. "What happens to us now? Kessler holds our contracts. Our certifications. He's got files on every one of us."

"He's got files on me too," Yara said. "Ask him sometime what's in mine. It hasn't stopped me yet."

A few of the men laughed—tired, disbelieving, but real. Voss didn't. He just nodded slowly, the way men nod when they've made a decision they aren't ready to announce.

* * *

Dawn came gray and merciless and showed them the damage entire.

The *Meridian Star* lay heeled over on the gravel bar, flooded to her gunwales, her deck cargo scattered downstream in a brown wake of splintered crates. Plaster fragments floated everywhere—white chips of fake gods riding the flood toward the sea. Here and there a real shape showed among the replicas: a carved panel, a ceremonial drum, objects the river communities had built to sacrifice themselves for the real ones' escape. The river was returning them, piece by broken piece, to no one.

Kessler stood on the apron watching his empire float past in pieces. He had not slept either. When Yara came down off the bank with the last of the rescued men, he turned, and for a moment she thought he was going to say something human—*thank you*, perhaps, or even *you were right*. His mouth opened.

"Eleven of my men," he said instead, "saw which way they'd rather owe their lives."

"That's usually how it works," Yara said. Her voice was wrecked, a rasp barely above the river's noise. "You save people, they remember."

"They also talk." He stepped closer, and though his voice stayed level, she heard the thing underneath it, the thing that had been underneath everything since the portal. "My men, on your line, following your father's rails, trusting your judgment in a flood. Do you understand what you built last night, Vasquez? Not a rope. A claim." His eyes went past her, to Voss standing silent among the defectors, to Anselm, to her own crew gathered wet and exhausted and unmistakably *hers*. "Every man on this river is watching which flag people rally to. Last night you raised one."

"Last night," Yara said, "people were drowning."

"People are always drowning. The question is who profits from the rescue." He almost smiled, and it was the coldest thing she'd seen him do. "Enjoy your morning, Vasquez. Check your equipment stores when you get a chance. I'd hate for anything to go missing before the water drops."

He walked away toward his radio tent, and Yara stood in the gray rain watching the river carry the last of the plaster gods out of the gorge, and felt the ground shift beneath her feet in a way that had nothing to do with water.

Behind her, Voss spoke quietly, for her ears alone. "Madam. If you're planning something—whatever it is—you should know you've got eleven more pairs of hands who owe you, and one of them knows exactly which crates were real."

Yara turned and looked at the big foreman, at the eleven soaked men who had followed her father's rails out of the flood, and understood that the expedition had just changed sides without anyone firing a shot.

"Get them fed," she said. "Then find me. We're going to need every pair of hands before the water drops."

And downstream, faint under the storm's retreat, she could already hear the monsoon's next wave gathering in the highlands, coming down the Fly like a debt called in.
