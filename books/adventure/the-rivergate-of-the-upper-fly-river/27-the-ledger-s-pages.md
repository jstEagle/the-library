## 27. The Ledger's Pages

The rain came in the afternoon like a debt collector—no warning, no negotiation, just the sky opening over the gorge and the whole camp turning to mud. Yara stood under the tarp outside the comms tent and watched the water sheet off the ridge above the portal, and she thought about how everything that mattered now was happening in small movements: a photograph here, a memory card there, a scout moving through the treeline with the patience of a heron.

She had forty-one photographs on a card no bigger than her thumbnail. She had cut them from the ledger pages two nights ago, working by headlamp inside the survey tent with the flap zipped and Nia running the generator loud on purpose, covering the sound of the camera shutter with compressor noise. Forty-one pages of her father's evidence room, photographed flat against the crate lid he'd used as a table twelve years before she was born into this particular grief. Bribes. Manifests. Names she recognized from the funding letters Kessler kept in a locked case in his quarters—the same names, the same handwriting, the same elegant stationery from Port Moresby and Brisbane and one address in Zurich that appeared eleven separate times.

Her father had died holding this. And she had signed the paper that buried it.

"You're doing the thing," Tomas said beside her.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stand in the rain looking at the ridge like it owes you money." He handed her a tin mug of the terrible coffee. "Okonkwo says the river's up another meter overnight. Bea wants to move the dive to Thursday. Kessler wants it tomorrow. In case you were wondering what you're walking into when you walk out of this rain."

"I wasn't wondering."

"You were always wondering. You just pretend the wondering is weather." He drank his own coffee and didn't look at her, which was his way of giving her room. Twelve years of crewing together had built a language made almost entirely of not-looking. "The scout's been in the treeline since dawn. East side, past the pump shed. Bea saw him an hour ago and said nothing, which means Bea knows something, which means you told Bea something, which means I'm the last person in this camp being protected from the truth, and I want you to know I've noticed."

Yara looked at him. Rain ran off the tarp edge between them like a beaded curtain.

"Tonight," she said. "After Kessler's people rotate watch. The exchange happens at the dead rail spur, kilometer marker nine. The scout knows the signal."

"And if Kessler's surveillance is better than we think?"

"Then I'll have a very short conversation and a very long fall."

Tomas grunted. It was, from him, practically a blessing.

* * *

Kilometer marker nine was a stretch of rail the river had eaten decades ago—ties rotted to black lace, rails bent sideways like ribs after bad surgery. The spur ran along a shelf above the flood channel, close enough to the water that in the dark you could hear it breathing, that deep hydraulic swallow the Fly made when the monsoon got its teeth in. Yara went out alone, as agreed, wearing no headlamp, carrying the memory card taped flat under the waistband of her shirt where a pat-down would miss it and a decoy card in her chest pocket that held nothing but photographs of the camp kitchen, taken deliberately, boringly, over three days.

The plan was Nia's, refined by Bea, blessed by Okonkwo's weather math: the exchange would happen during the ninety-minute window when the storm cell overhead killed the drone's downlink. Kessler's surveillance had gone quiet for exactly that window the last two nights. Weather was the one adversary even rich men hadn't figured out how to bribe.

She reached the marker at 21:40. The rain was a solid thing, a weight on her shoulders. She crouched by the bent rail and waited, counting her breaths the way her father had taught her for blast timing—in for four, hold for seven, out for eight—until the dark resolved itself and the scout was simply there, sitting on the rail beside her as if he'd been part of the landscape all along. Younger than she'd expected. A scar through one eyebrow. The carved token's twin hung at his throat on a cord, and seeing it did something to her chest she didn't have time to examine.

"You're the elder's grandson," she said.

"I'm the elder's eyes." His English was clipped, mission-school English. "You are the daughter of the man who sealed the gate."

"That depends who's asking."

"The river is asking." He held out his hand, palm up, patient as stone.

Yara took the decoy card from her chest pocket first—procedure, misdirection, the little theater of it—and pressed it into his palm. Then, hating how much her hands wanted to shake, she peeled the tape from her waistband and set the real card on top of it.

"Forty-one pages," she said. "Bribes. Names. Dates going back to before the railway finished. There's more in the lower gallery but I couldn't photograph all of it without—" She stopped. Without someone noticing. Without becoming the kind of person who got caught. "This is enough to start. Your people will need journalists. Prosecutors. People who can't be bought by the families whose names are on these pages."

The scout turned the cards over once, then tucked them into a waterproof pouch against his skin with the ease of a man who had done this before, or whose people had been doing versions of this since the first surveyors came up the river with their brass tags and their ledgers and their certainty.

"My grandfather says the man who sealed the gate tried to send this out once," the scout said. "Twelve years ago. The courier never reached the coast."

The rain filled the silence between them.

"I know," Yara said quietly. "I've read the journal. I know what happened to the courier."

"Then you know why we do not use couriers anymore." He stood, fluid, already half-vanished into the dark. "We use rivers. We use storms. We use daughters of dead men who finally stop being afraid." He paused, and something almost gentle moved through his voice. "My grandfather also says: tell your cousin the channel she dives on Thursday is not the channel her charts show. The flood has moved it east. Tell her before Thursday."

Then he was gone, and Yara was alone with the rain and the bent rail and the strange lightness of a body that had been carrying forty-one pages for three days and was suddenly, dangerously, unburdened.

She was halfway back to camp when the storm cell broke.

* * *

It broke wrong.

Not gradually, not the way monsoon cells usually frayed apart at the edges—it tore open all at once, like a wound, and the drone's downlink light in Kessler's comms tent went from red to green while Yara was still four hundred meters from the wire, wet to the bone, walking the open ground between the pump shed and the survey tents with nowhere to hide and nothing left to hide.

She heard the drone before she saw it. That insect whine cutting through the rain, climbing, banking. She didn't run. Running was confession. She walked, shoulders squared, and let the machine look at her, and hated every second of it, and understood with cold clarity that the window hadn't failed—the window had been allowed. Two nights of quiet downlink. Two nights of a storm cell sitting exactly where it sat. Either the weather had conspired with her or Dario Kessler had learned patience somewhere in the years since she'd known him, and either way she'd walked into it with her chin up like a woman delivering a telegram.

Tomas met her at the wire. One look at his face told her everything.

"He wants you," Tomas said. "His tent. Alone. He sent his men off the perimeter first, which somehow makes it worse."

"How long ago?"

"Ten minutes. He's waiting." Tomas fell into step beside her, voice dropping. "Yara. If this goes bad—"

"It won't go bad."

"If it goes bad, Nia has charges set on the upper gallery access that could bring half the hillside down on his helipad. She did it without asking anyone. I'm telling you because you should know what your people are willing to do while you're having conversations."

Yara stopped walking. For a moment the rain was the only sound in the world.

"Tell her to stand down," she said. "And tell her thank you. Both. In that order."

* * *

Kessler's tent was less a tent than an annex—a rigid-walled command module flown in piece by piece, climate-controlled, lit white as a clinic. Maps on the walls. The locked case with the funding correspondence. A decanter of whisky and two glasses, one already poured, which was its own kind of message: he'd known the shape of this evening before she'd waded out of the river.

He stood at the map table with his back to her, hands flat on the surface, watching the rain hammer the clear panel ceiling. He looked older than he had at the negotiation in her camp—older, and thinner somehow, as if the expedition had been eating him from the inside the way it ate everyone.

"Sit down, Yara."

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will." He turned. Up close his face had the terrible calm of a man who had stopped performing. "Do you know what I respect about you? Genuinely. It's not your competence—competence is common in this business, it grows on every riverbank like mold. It's that you lie the way your father lied. Completely. With your whole body. He sat across a table from me in 2011 and swore the lower gallery was structurally unsound while he had four hundred pages of evidence in a crate behind his chair. You just walked four hundred meters through open ground in a storm after handing my surveillance footage the most interesting ninety seconds it's ever recorded, and you're standing there right now deciding whether to insult me with a denial."

Yara said nothing. Her heart was a slow, heavy drum. Somewhere beyond the canvas wall, the river rose.

Kessler picked up the poured glass and held it out. When she didn't take it, he set it down and drank from the other himself, unhurried.

"Here's what happens now," he said. "Option one: you tell me where the rest of the copies are, we retrieve them, and we proceed as partners, as originally discussed. Option two: you don't tell me, and I spend tomorrow morning doing something unpleasant to your operation, and then we proceed anyway, except now nobody trusts anybody and the monsoon eats us both. I'd prefer option one. I suspect you'd prefer option two, because option two lets you feel righteous while it kills you, and righteousness is the family business, isn't it? Your father had it too. Right up until the moment it stopped protecting him."

"You knew," Yara said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "About the journal. About the ledger. Before you hired me."

Kessler smiled, and it was the worst smile she'd ever seen on him—not cruel, worse than cruel, *sad*, the smile of a man showing her a door she should have seen months ago.

"Yara. I knew about the ledger before your father found it. Why do you think the families funded my expedition? Why do you think I needed *you*—your name, your father's name, your signature on the closure report? I could have hired any salvage crew on the coast for half your rate. I hired the daughter of the man who sealed the gate because the families believed his daughter might know where he hid the evidence. And because—" he set down the glass, and for one unguarded second something almost human crossed his face— "because I was there, in 2011. I read the report you signed. I've spent twelve years wondering what a seventeen-year-old girl was promised to make her sign it, and whether she'd spend the rest of her life trying to undo it. I had money on both outcomes."

The rain thundered on the roof. Yara became aware, distantly, that her hands had curled into fists at her sides and that she was shaking—not with fear, she realized, but with the specific fury of a person discovering that even her rebellion had been budgeted for.

"The scout," she said. "The exchange tonight. How much did you see?"

"Everything. The drone has thermal imaging, Yara. I watched you hand over two memory cards to a boy from the upper villages while standing in a flooded rail cutting." He tilted his head. "I let it happen. Do you understand that? I could have taken those cards off him at the wire with three men and a flashlight. I let him walk because I wanted to see if you'd actually do it. Because if you did—if you committed—then you'd stop hedging, stop negotiating with yourself, and become predictable. A person with a secret already delivered is a person with nothing left to trade." He picked up the decanter and refilled his glass, and his hand was perfectly steady. "So. Now we're both honest. You stole photographs of my ledger. I let you. The question that decides everything is the one I asked at the beginning: where are the rest of the copies?"

"There are no other copies."

"Yara."

"There are no *other* copies," she repeated, and this time she let him hear what was underneath it—not defiance, not fear, but the flat finality of a woman who had spent two nights with a headlamp and a camera making sure of exactly one thing. "Everything I took is on the cards that just walked into the treeline. Everything else is in the lower gallery, behind a door my father built, in a room that floods the moment anyone opens the sluice. You have the artifacts. You have the barge. You have your drones and your contracts and your families' money. What you don't have anymore is leverage over the truth, because the truth is currently moving downriver in a waterproof pouch against a boy's skin, and no amount of money catches a river in flood."

Kessler looked at her for a long time. The clinical light made his face into a mask with the paint cracking.

"You know what your father said to me, the last time we spoke?" he said finally. "Three weeks before the explosion. He said, 'Dario, when the water comes back, it's going to come back for all of us.' I thought he was being dramatic. Surveyors are dramatic people; it's the isolation." He drained the glass. "Get out of my tent. Go check on your cousin—she's been prepping the Thursday dive with my hydrologist, and I'd hate for anything to happen to her certification. We'll continue this conversation when the water recedes. It always recedes, Yara. That's the one promise this river keeps."

Yara walked to the tent flap. Stopped with her hand on it.

"Dario," she said, without turning around. "In 2011. Did you know what they were going to do to him?"

The silence behind her lasted four seconds. She counted them the way she counted blast timing.

"I knew the families wanted the evidence contained," Kessler said. "I didn't ask how. That's not the same as innocence, and I've never pretended otherwise. But you should know—I didn't kill your father, Yara. I just sold the shovel."

She pushed through the flap into the rain, and the night closed over her, and somewhere downriver a boy was running through the dark with her father's ghosts in his hands, and somewhere upstream the river was rising six inches an hour toward a Thursday that the scout had already told her was a lie.

She had until the water receded, Kessler thought.

He shouldn't have given her a deadline. Deadlines were the only thing her family had ever been good at beating.
