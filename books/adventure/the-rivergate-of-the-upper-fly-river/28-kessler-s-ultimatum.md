## 28. Kessler's Ultimatum

The rain came down in sheets that turned the gorge into a drum, and Yara stood at the portal with her hood up and her hands in her pockets, feeling the brass tag she'd stolen from the upper gallery press its cold edge into her palm. She had been standing there for twenty minutes. Long enough that anyone watching would think she was checking the pump line. Long enough that she almost believed it herself.

The water had come up another foot overnight. Okonkwo's revised models said landfall in four days, maybe three, and the Fly was already behaving like something that had been given permission. It moved brown and muscular past the portal, carrying whole trees with their roots still clutching island-sized plugs of earth. The barge with the first shipment of crates sat mid-channel on its moorings, riding high and light, and Yara tried not to look at it because she knew what was in those crates and she knew what wasn't, and the difference between those two facts was the only thing keeping her upright.

Behind her, boots on wet concrete.

She knew the tread before she turned. Kessler walked the way he negotiated—deliberately, with a slight favoring of the right foot that he'd picked up somewhere he never talked about. He stopped three meters away, close enough to talk under the rain, far enough to make a point about distance. Two of his security men hung back near the tunnel mouth, and she noted them without looking at them. That was new. Kessler didn't usually travel with an audience.

"You've been busy," he said.

"Checking the pumps."

"I know what you've been checking." He pulled back his own hood, letting the rain hit his gray-streaked hair like he didn't care what it did to him. There was something almost ceremonial in it. A man dressing down for a fight. "The eastern ridge. Yesterday, seventeen hundred hours. One of my cameras caught a man from the elder's village taking a package from your hydrologist's assistant. Small package. Waterproof wrapping."

Yara kept her face still. Rain did half the work for her; it streamed down her cheeks like grief she hadn't authorized. "People trade all kinds of things in camp. Tobacco. Medicine. Gossip."

"Ledger pages," Kessler said, "photographed in a lower gallery that no one was supposed to enter. In a room that officially doesn't exist." He smiled, and it was the worst thing she'd seen in weeks, because it wasn't a threat's smile. It was almost fond. "Yara. Sit down with me. There's a room in the survey shack with a table. We shouldn't do this standing in the rain like a pair of foremen arguing over a timesheet."

"I'm fine here."

"You're fine here," he repeated. "All right. Here, then."

He reached inside his coat—not fast, nothing sudden, everything telegraphed the way a man moves when he wants you to watch—and brought out a tablet in a waterproof case. He thumbed it awake and turned it toward her.

"Beatrix Vasquez," he read. "Commercial dive certification, class four, salvage endorsement. Issued Port Moresby, renewed twice. Clean record." He swiped. "Until now."

The screen showed a dive log. Yara's stomach dropped before her mind caught up, because she recognized the format—she'd grown up around these forms, her father had filled out a thousand of them—and she could see, even at a glance, that the entries were wrong. Dates adjusted. Depth readings that didn't match the sump Bea had mapped. And there, in the incident field, a phrase that made her jaw tighten: *diver exceeded certified depth without buddy confirmation. Supervisor notified. Supervisor declined to intervene.*

"Supervisor," Kessler said softly. "That would be you."

"That's fabricated."

"Of course it's fabricated. I fabricated it." He said it the way another man might say *I made coffee*. "I have a man in Port Moresby who processes certification appeals for the maritime board. He's been on my payroll since before you were running spurs. If this log reaches his desk, Bea's certification is suspended pending investigation. Salvage endorsement revoked. And because the dive in question was part of a contracted commercial operation, the investigation extends to the operator." He looked up at her. "To you. To your crew. Criminal negligence, Yara. In this jurisdiction, with the right paperwork, that's not a fine. That's prison. For all of you. Bea, Tomas, Okonkwo, Nia. Even the ones who never got wet."

The rain filled the silence between them, drumming on the concrete apron, running off the portal's broken lip in silver threads. Yara became aware of her own breathing, slow and deliberate, the way it went when she was keeping something caged behind her ribs.

"When," she said.

"Does it reach his desk?" Kessler pocketed the tablet. "That depends on you. I'm not a monster. I've held it back this long because I was hoping it wouldn't be necessary. I hired you because you were the best, and because I thought—foolishly, maybe—that your father's blood in you would mean we wanted the same things." He shook his head. "Instead I get sabotage by photograph. I get my cargo swapped for river clay and plaster—I know about that too, by the way, and I'll come back to it, because the fact that you thought I wouldn't notice tells me you still don't understand your position."

The world narrowed. Yara heard the river, the rain, her own pulse, and under all of it a single clear thought: *he knows. He's known about the swap and he's let the barge sit loaded with fakes for two days, which means the real crates are already moving upstream, which means the diversion worked, which means—*

Which meant nothing, if he took everything else away.

"What do you want," she said. Not a question. She made it flat.

"The journal."

There it was. She'd known it was coming since the night he'd first dangled it in front of her—her father's original survey journal, held like a carrot, now revealed as the stick it had always been. "You don't even know what's in it."

"I know exactly what's in it." Kessler stepped closer, and now the distance between them was the distance of a man delivering a verdict. "I've known since before I hired you. Why do you think I came to you, Yara? Of every salvage crew on this coast, why the woman whose father sealed this tunnel? Because your father found something in the lower gallery twelve years ago, and he didn't just find artifacts. He found the paper. The ledger. The names. And he tried to use it, and he died for it, and the report that came out of that disaster was signed by his own daughter, and I have always, always wondered what she signed and what she was paid to forget."

The words landed like blast charges, each one set and timed. Yara stood in them.

"I'm not asking you to hand me the ledger," Kessler went on. "I don't need the ledger. I have my own arrangements with the people whose names are in it. What I need is the journal, because the journal is the original, and originals have a way of surfacing, and I am a man who is currently trying to retire. I want to close this chapter. I want to pull my cargo out before the monsoon, flood this hole behind me, and spend my last years somewhere the rain is warm and nobody knows my name. Give me the journal, and the dive log dies in a drawer in Port Moresby. Give me the journal, and the swap becomes a misunderstanding that never happened. Give me the journal, and you and your crew walk out of this gorge rich, clean, and free."

"And if I don't."

"Then the water does the deciding." He glanced up at the sky, at the gray ceiling of the monsoon, and when he looked back at her his face had gone almost gentle, which was the most frightening thing yet. "The river's coming up. The pumps are losing. In seventy-two hours this portal floods to the gallery line, and everything below it—your evidence room, your photographs, your father's last words scratched into the door—goes under for good, and no one can prove anything about anything, ever again. Except my log. My log is dry, Yara. My log is in a filing cabinet four hundred kilometers from this rain. That's the difference between us. You keep putting your faith in things that drown."

Yara thought about her father's message. *Flood it. Tell no one. The river keeps what it keeps.* She thought about Bea in the water, arms burning, the dive line humming. She thought about the elder's carved token in her tent, and the villages upstream, and the ledger pages already traveling toward them in a scout's waterproof wrap.

"You're asking me to choose," she said, "between my father's name and my crew's necks."

"I'm asking you to be practical. Your father wasn't. Look where it got him." Kessler turned up his collar. "The water recedes in three days if the storm tracks south, five if it doesn't. Either way, I want the journal on my table before then. You have until the river gives it back to us—the rivergate apron, dry, walkable. That's the deadline. Not a day before, not an hour after." He started to turn, then stopped, and said, without looking at her: "One more thing. Bea dives again tomorrow—final survey of the sluice channel, per my schedule. If she refuses, I'll read that as your answer. If she dives, she dives under my safety officer's supervision, with my dive log in his hand. So think carefully about what you tell her tonight. Because every word you say to her, I will hear, and every word will be evidence."

Then he walked back through the rain toward the survey shack, his security men falling in behind him, and Yara stood alone at the portal with the river rising at her feet and understood, with total clarity, that she had just been given the purest form of power there was: someone else's love, held hostage against her own hands.

* * *

She didn't go straight to Bea's tent. She walked the perimeter first, because her legs needed the motion and her mind needed the pretense of it. Past the pump line, where Okonkwo's crew worked in yellow rain gear, bailing a fight they were losing by inches. Past the magazine tent, where Nia sat cross-legged in the entrance under a tarp, wiring a dummy charge with the concentration of a woman praying. Past the mess fly, where Tomas was arguing with two of Kessler's men over fuel allocations, his voice carrying that hard edge it had carried since the scuffle, since the cut above his eye that was still scabbed black.

Her crew. Her people. Every one of them standing in the rain because she had signed a piece of paper twelve years ago and another one twelve days ago, and both signatures had been lies, and both lies had been meant as protection, and here was Kessler showing her exactly what protection was worth when it ran into a man who owned the paperwork.

She stopped at the edge of camp, under a leaning kanda tree, and let herself feel it for exactly one minute. She timed it by her pulse. Sixty beats of pure, unshared fear—fear for Bea, whose name was already sitting in a fabricated log; fear for the crew, who had followed her into this gorge on a promise of safety margins that Kessler had cut to ribbons; fear for herself, because the journal was in her tent, wrapped in oilcloth, under her cot, and she had read it so many times in the last two nights that her father's handwriting had started to feel like a voice, like he was in there with her, saying *flood it, tell no one* while his daughter stood in the rain calculating whether obedience to a dead man was worth a living woman's certification.

Then the sixty beats were up, and she buttoned the fear away where she kept the rest, and went to find her cousin.

Bea was in the dive tent, checking her regulator by lamplight. She looked up when Yara came in, and whatever she saw in Yara's face made her set the regulator down slowly, the way you set down a tool when someone's about to tell you the rig is coming apart.

"Kessler talked to you," Bea said.

"How did you know?"

"Because he talked to me an hour ago. Asked me how I felt about the sluice channel. Asked me if I trusted my supervisor." Bea's mouth went thin. "I've been in this business long enough to know what that kind of question is. It's not a question. It's a delivery." She stood. "What's in the log, Yara?"

Yara told her. All of it. The fabricated depths, the invented incident, the supervisor who declined to intervene, the suspension, the criminal negligence, the crew. She watched Bea take it the way she took everything—quietly, with her arms folded, her face doing the work of a locked door.

"He's had this the whole time," Bea said at last.

"Probably."

"He hired you knowing he could do this. He hired me knowing he could do this." Bea laughed, one short note with no humor in it. "You know what the worst part is? It would hold up. Not forever—maybe not in court, if we ever got a real court—but long enough. Long enough to hang my cert. Long enough to freeze the crew's accounts. Long enough." She looked at her hands. "I nearly drowned in that sump, Yara. I know exactly how deep it is. And now there's a paper somewhere that says I don't."

"I'm not giving him the journal."

"I didn't ask you to."

"You should ask me to. It's the smart play. One book against five people's futures. Any salvage accountant in the country would make that trade."

Bea looked up, and for a moment Yara saw her at seventeen, at the funeral, standing apart from everyone with her dive bag over her shoulder, already halfway to the coast, already deciding that the family name was a weight she'd rather carry alone.

"You signed the report," Bea said. "Twelve years ago. To protect the crew. And it didn't protect anyone—it just buried everything for twelve years and let the people who did it get rich. So no. I'm not asking you to make the smart trade. I'm asking you to make a different one this time." She picked her regulator back up. "I dive the sluice tomorrow because the sluice has to be mapped and because I'm not letting his safety officer write my dive for me. And you—" she pointed the regulator at Yara like a verdict— "you figure out what you're going to do before the water recedes. Because he's right about one thing. The river is going to decide. It's just not going to decide for him."

* * *

Night came down on the gorge like a lid. The rain slackened to a steady hiss, and the camp lights made yellow islands in the dark, and Yara sat in her tent with the journal open on her knees and the lantern turned low.

She had read the last pages again. Her father's hand, cramped and hurried in the final entries, the letters leaning like men against a wind. The ledger's names. The bribes, itemized, dated, signed. The route the artifacts had taken down the Fly and out through the coast, and the families who had built the railway to carry them, and the families who were, right now, at this moment, funding Dario Kessler's last expedition into the one place that could still convict them.

*Flood it. Tell no one. The river keeps what it keeps.*

Outside, a footstep in the mud. She knew it without looking—Tomas, making his round, the round he'd been making every night since the scuffle without anyone asking him to. He paused outside her tent, a shadow against the lantern glow, and stood there a while, saying nothing, and she said nothing, and after a minute he moved on.

She looked at the journal. At the brass tag beside it, her father's initials catching the lamplight. At the waterproof wrap she'd need if she was going to move the journal anywhere, which she would have to do soon, because Kessler's cameras were on the ridge and Kessler's men walked the camp and the deadline was already falling toward her like the river toward the sea.

Three days. Five at the outside. Until the apron dried—or until it drowned.

Yara closed the journal and, for the first time since she was seventeen, began to draft a plan of her own.
