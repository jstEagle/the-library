## 29. The Long Night

The rain started an hour after Kessler left her tent, and it did not stop.

Yara sat with the journal open on her knees and listened to the water hammer the canvas, and for a long time she could not read another word. Her father's handwriting filled the pages in that cramped, slanting script she knew better than her own—the same hand that had written her birthday cards, the same hand that had drawn the map folded in her chest pocket, the same hand that had scratched four sentences into wet concrete before the water took him.

*Flood it. Tell no one. The river keeps what it keeps.*

She had read those lines a hundred times since the lock came down. She had traced them with her thumb until she could feel the gouges through the paper of her memory. But tonight, with Kessler's ultimatum still hanging in the air like the smell of cordite, the words had changed weight. They were no longer a message from the dead. They were an order. And orders, in her experience, were things you either followed or betrayed.

The camp lantern hissed. Outside, the river had risen again—she could hear it in the changed pitch of the current against the pilings, a low grinding note like something swallowing. Okonkwo's last forecast sat on her cot: landfall moved up again, nine days early, maybe ten. The Fly was already fat and brown and impatient, climbing the gorge walls in slow increments, as if it had been waiting twelve years for permission.

She turned back to the journal's final pages.

Her father had been precise even at the end. Dates. Depths. The sequence of charges he'd set to bring the lower gallery down behind him—except he hadn't brought it down, not all the way. He had flooded it instead, opened the old sluice and let the river do what explosives couldn't: seal the room without crushing what lay inside. He had known, even then, that the evidence had to survive him. That someone would come looking. That the families who built the railway on stolen bones would send people, eventually, to make sure nothing remained but water.

He just hadn't guessed they would send his daughter.

Yara closed her eyes. She could hear the camp settling around her—Nia's generator coughing down for the night, the muffled argument between two of Kessler's loaders that flared and died, Bea's dive gear clinking faintly in the equipment tent whenever the wind gusted. And beneath all of it, steady as a second heartbeat, Tomas's boots on the mud outside her tent. He'd been walking the perimeter for three hours. He hadn't said a word when he took up the watch. He hadn't asked. That was its own kind of conversation, and she wasn't ready to have it.

She opened her survey notebook to a clean page and drew a line down the middle.

Two plans. That was what the night demanded. Not a decision—a decision made alone, at two in the morning, exhausted and grieving, would be a bad one. But plans. Options laid out like rail on ballast, so that whatever she chose in the morning, she chose with her eyes open.

On the left side of the line she wrote: *Comply.*

It looked small and ugly on the page. She made herself look at it anyway.

Comply meant handing over the journal. It meant telling Kessler where—or letting him believe she'd tell him where—the ledger originals were hidden, though she'd photographed them weeks ago and passed copies to the elder's people through the scout, and only the originals' location remained hers. Comply meant watching Kessler's barges load the real cargo under cover of the counterfeit crates Nia and the river people had built, meant standing at the portal while twelve years of stolen heritage floated downstream into private vaults and the evidence room drowned or didn't, depending on Kessler's mood. Comply meant Bea kept her certification, because the falsified dive records stayed buried. Meant the crew walked away whole, unpaid debts forgiven, criminal charges dissolved like sugar in rain.

And then vanish. That was the second half of the plan, and she wrote it out in full because writing it made it real: new contracts under a different company name, a crew scattered across three basins, no forwarding frequency, no return address. She had done it before. Every salvage outfit in the basin had done it once. You ran until the past got tired of chasing you, and you told yourself that survival was a form of victory.

But her father hadn't run. She looked at the journal again—at the page where he'd written, in the last week of his life, *I signed the first report too. I thought I was protecting the crew. I was protecting them straight into the hands of the men who paid me.* He had run for seventeen years, and then he had come back, and coming back had killed him. And now she held the thing he'd died holding, and the plan on the left side of the page required her to hand it to the man who had arranged for her father to be in that tunnel when the charges went off—because Kessler had admitted it, in his careful way, with his careful silences. He had never said *I killed him.* He had said *your father understood leverage,* and *the families don't forgive loose ends,* and he had watched her face while he said it, cataloguing every flinch.

Comply and vanish meant living. It meant Bea diving free, Tomas walking away, Nia keeping her license, Okonkwo publishing nothing. It meant Yara Vasquez alive somewhere south of the basin, drinking cheap rum in a river town, knowing exactly what was in the vaults of six families and saying nothing, forever, while the carved panels hung in private galleries and the ledger rotted in the dark or burned in some lawyer's fireplace.

She had spent twelve years believing she'd signed that first report to protect people. Bea had asked her the question that had cracked her open: *what will you sign this time?*

The left-hand plan was a signature. She could see it already, her own name at the bottom, neat and legible, surrender dressed up as prudence.

She turned the page.

On the right side she wrote: *Flood it.*

Her pen hesitated over the word. Then she wrote the rest, fast, before the part of her that calculated survival could catch up: *Open the sluice. Drown the lower gallery on his terms. Destroy the evidence room, the ledger originals, everything. Give Kessler nothing.*

That was the plan her father had scratched into concrete. Flood it. Tell no one. The river keeps what it keeps. And it would work—Okonkwo had mapped the channel, Bea had found the sluice mechanism and her father's spare brass tag bolted inside it, proof that the flood option had been engineered in from the start, a dead man's contingency waiting twelve years for a diver brave enough to pull it. The monsoon was doing half the work already; the river wanted in. All they had to do was stop arguing with it.

But flooding the gallery meant destroying the originals. The photographs she'd taken were good—Nia had helped her rig the lighting—but photographs were copies, and copies could be dismissed. Forged. Discredited. The families had lawyers who could spend a decade proving a photograph was a fabrication, and journalists who could be bought, and prosecutors who owed their careers to the same old money. The originals, stamped and sealed and preserved in the dry dark of the flood-sealed room, were different. The originals were a verdict.

Flood the gallery and she destroyed the verdict to keep it out of the killer's hands. She saved the artifacts—those were already moving upstream to the communities, plaster fakes riding the barge in their place—and she saved her people, because a drowned gallery gave Kessler nothing to bargain with. But she also handed the families exactly what they'd wanted all along: a tunnel with no evidence in it. She became, permanently and provably, the woman who erased the proof. Her credibility wouldn't just be damaged. It would be gone. Any future testimony, any leaked copy, any photograph—she would stand up in front of it all as the person who flooded the room, and every accusation she ever made afterward would sound like a guilty conscience inventing absolution.

Kessler would know it too. That was the cruelty of it. Even ruined, even cheated of his cargo, he would walk away understanding that she had chosen his crime over her own truth. He would carry that knowledge out of the gorge like a trophy.

Yara set the pen down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw sparks.

*There has to be a third line,* she thought. *There always is. You find it or you die looking.*

But the third line wouldn't come. She picked the pen up and put it down again. She reread her father's message until the letters swam. She drank cold coffee that tasted like the tin it sat in. Outside, the rain thickened, and somewhere upstream there was a long, deep cracking sound—not thunder, she decided after a moment, though she couldn't have said what else it was—and the river's note dropped half a tone, the way a throat drops when it swallows.

Tomas's footsteps stopped outside her tent.

She heard him stand there for a while, not moving, and then heard the soft scrape of him sitting down on the supply crate by her door, settling in. Unasked. Unannounced. As if guarding her tent were simply the next task on a list he'd been keeping for twelve years, one entry longer than she'd ever known.

"Tomas," she said.

"I'm not here," he said, through the canvas.

"You're sitting on my coffee crate."

"I'm guarding your coffee crate. It's valuable."

She almost laughed, and the almost hurt more than crying would have. "You should be sleeping. Everyone should be sleeping. In nine days none of us will."

"Eight," Tomas said. "Okonkwo revised it again at dinner. Eight days." A pause, and then, quieter, in the tone he used maybe twice a year: "You've been writing for four hours, jefa. Paper doesn't argue back. Whatever you're deciding, decide it with us. That's all I'll say. Then I'm not here again."

His boots didn't move. The rain filled the silence between them, drumming on the tent roof, on the tarp over the generators, on the black skin of the river, on the concrete face of the gate two kilometers up the gorge where her father had knelt in the rising water and scratched his last order into the world.

Yara looked down at the notebook. Two columns. Two signatures waiting.

And then she saw it—not a third plan, not yet, but the shape of one, the way you see track bed through fog before you can name the route. The left column assumed she had to choose between her people and the truth. The right column assumed the truth lived or died in that one room, in those original pages, in the gallery her father had sealed. Both columns assumed the ledger was a thing stored in a place.

But she had already sent the photographs upstream. The elder's people had them. And her father—he had never trusted a single copy of anything in his life; she remembered that now, remembered him drilling it into her at seventeen: *a survey without redundancy is a rumor.* Somewhere in the journal's earlier pages, in the margins she'd skimmed past in her grief, there might be more. His own transcription. His own insurance.

She pulled the journal back onto her knees and began turning pages, fast, the lantern light jumping across the paper, while outside her tent Tomas sat his unasked watch and the river rose in the dark, patient as a debt, toward the gate.

Halfway through, in a margin dated eleven days before the explosion, she found it: a column of figures in her father's hand, names abbreviated, sums circled, and beneath them a single line that stopped her breath—

*Copied in triplicate. One stays with the river.*

Yara stared at the words until the ink blurred. One stays with the river. He hadn't just sealed the evidence. He had distributed it. Somewhere along the Fly—in a village, a mission, a cache only the communities knew about—her father had hidden a third copy twelve years ago, and the elder's token in her pack, the carved promise she'd been carrying since the barge landing, suddenly felt less like a gift and more like a key.

She grabbed the notebook and tore both columns out. Crumpled them. Started a fresh page, and this time she didn't draw a dividing line. She wrote one word at the top—*Find it*—and beneath it, the beginning of a list of everyone she would wake before dawn.

Outside, the rain kept falling, and Tomas kept watch, and the river climbed.
