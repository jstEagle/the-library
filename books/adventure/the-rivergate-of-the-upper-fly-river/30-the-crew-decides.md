## 30. The Crew Decides

The rain started an hour before dawn, a soft percussion on the tarp roofs that Yara had learned to read like a language. This was not the monsoon yet. This was only the river clearing its throat.

She stood at the edge of camp with the journal in her hands and watched the water climb the gauge stake Okonkwo had driven into the bank. Six inches overnight. Then eight. The Fly was waking up ahead of schedule, and somewhere upstream Kessler's men were loading crates onto a barge that would never outrun what was coming.

Tomas found her there. He didn't say anything at first—Tomas rarely wasted words—but he stood close enough that she could feel his shoulder against hers, the way he'd done since they were both teenagers rerailing track for men who called them *chicos* and paid them in fuel rations.

"You didn't sleep," he said.

"No."

"The journal?"

Yara looked down at it. Her father's handwriting covered every page, cramped and slanting, the letters of a man who wrote standing up in moving vehicles. She had read it three times through the night, and each time the same sentence rose out of the ink like something surfacing from deep water: *They will come back for it. When they do, flood it. Tell no one. The river keeps what it keeps.*

"He knew," she said. "Twelve years ago, he already knew they'd come back. He built the whole thing—the seal, the sluice, the lower gallery—as a trap he hoped nobody would ever spring." She turned to face Tomas. "And now I've sprung it."

Tomas studied her the way he studied rail: looking for the crack that would spread under load.

"So what do we do?" he asked.

"That's not my decision anymore."

He frowned. "You're the boss."

"I was the boss when I signed the closure report at seventeen because two men with guns told me it would keep everyone alive. I was the boss when I took this contract because refusing meant losing all of you anyway. Every time I've decided alone, someone else has paid for it." She tucked the journal inside her jacket, against her ribs. "Wake the crew. All of them. Even Kessler's people who've crossed over—especially them. Mess tent. One hour."

"Yara." Tomas's voice dropped low. "If you tell them everything, you can't take it back. Some of them will run. Some of them will sell you to Kessler before breakfast."

"Then I'll know who they are," she said. "I'm tired of not knowing."

* * *

By seven, the mess tent held eleven people under its sagging canvas, steam rising off tin mugs of coffee that Nia had made too strong, as always. Yara counted them without meaning to: Tomas, Nia, Okonkwo, Bea. Rios and Mabuka, who had defected from Kessler's crew after the barge rescue and hadn't slept properly since. The Delgado brothers, who'd been with her four seasons. Old Ferro, who'd known her father. And two of Kessler's laborers, Chen and Ayo, who had shown up at the camp perimeter two nights ago soaked to the waist and asking, in careful English, if it was true that Yara Vasquez led crews that came back for their people.

Eleven faces. Eleven chances for this to end before it began.

She laid the journal on the folding table between the coffee urn and the stack of ration crates, and let them look at it.

"This is my father's survey journal," she said. "The real one. Not the report I signed when I was seventeen—that one was dictated to me by the men who killed him. This is what he actually found in the Rivergate, and why he sealed it, and why he died sealing it."

Nobody moved. Rain drummed the canvas. Somewhere down the slope, the river kept climbing.

She told them all of it. The artifacts in the upper gallery, packed by a shipping firm owned by the families whose names were carved into railway station lintels across the basin. The ledger—pages and pages of bribes, dates, amounts, signatures, the paper trail proving the Upper Fly line had been built as a conveyor belt for stolen heritage, and that the same families were funding Kessler's expedition now. Her father's discovery. His argument with the expedition backers. The explosion that wasn't an accident. The report she'd signed with a gun barrel resting against the table where she could see it.

"When I took this contract," she said, "Kessler told me he had my father's journal. He thought that was leverage over me. He was right—it was. But he didn't know what was in it. He doesn't know about the ledger. He thinks the lower gallery holds more artifacts, more inventory, more profit." She paused. "It holds the evidence that ends him. And his plan, once the last crate is out, is to collapse the gallery and bury the evidence forever. Nia can confirm that—she's been ordered to prepare 'stabilization' charges big enough to bring half the mountain down."

Nia nodded once, grim, arms folded.

"Kessler has leverage on me," Yara went on. "Falsified dive records implicating Bea. Criminal charges against anyone who helps me. He's given me until the water recedes to hand over this journal." She looked around the tent, meeting each pair of eyes. "I'm not going to hand it over. But I'm also not going to decide for you what that costs. So here's the truth, all of it, and here are your choices. You can walk to Kessler's camp right now and tell him everything you just heard, and he'll pay you better than I ever have. You can walk downriver and be gone before the storm hits. Or you can stay, and we finish this the way my father drew it—on his terms, not theirs. Whatever you choose, no one follows you. No one remembers it against you. That's a promise."

Silence. The rain thickened.

It was Ferro who spoke first, the old track-layer, his voice like gravel poured slow. "I ran rails behind your father for nine years. Best man I ever worked for, and the only one who ever asked my opinion." He tapped the table with one knuckle. "You're asking. That's enough for me."

Rios glanced at Mabuka. "We worked for Kessler six months," Rios said. "In six months I watched him leave two men on a flooded cutbank because retrieving them would delay the schedule. Last week your crew spent a night in rising water pulling his workers out of a stranded barge—workers he ordered abandoned." He shrugged. "I know which payroll I want to be on when the story gets told."

Chen and Ayo exchanged a few quiet words in a language Yara didn't speak, then Chen said, "We stay."

One by one, the rest of them said it—not with speeches, most of them, just nods, or a lifted mug, or Bea reaching across the table to put her hand flat on the journal cover, anchoring it there like a cleat holding a line in wind.

No one left the tent.

Yara hadn't realized she'd been holding her breath until it came out of her, long and unsteady.

"All right," she said. "Then listen, because we have maybe thirty-six hours before the monsoon makes landfall, and everything has to be in place before it does."

* * *

Nia went first. She unrolled her charge plans on the table, weighting the corners with mugs, and Yara saw immediately that she'd been working all night—the diagrams were dense with recalculation, margins full of figures in her small fierce handwriting.

"Kessler wants stabilization charges in the lower gallery—fourteen kilos of emulsion, placed to drop the roof across the entire evidence room." She tapped the plan. "So I built him fourteen kilos of nothing. Sawdust and detonating cord in the casings, real enough to pass a visual. The actual explosives—" she pulled a second sheet from her jacket, "—are here. Two shaped charges at the old sluice gate housing, placed to shear the retaining pins clean. Your father designed that gate to fail inward under pressure; the pins are the only thing holding it shut. Cut the pins, and the river does the demolition. We don't blow anything. We just open a door and get out of the water's way."

"You're sure the gate will hold until we want it to hold?" Okonkwo asked.

"It's held for twelve years," Nia said. "Concrete doesn't get weaker sitting still. It gets weaker when idiots drill into it, which is why I'm placing these myself, and why nobody else touches them."

"And after?" Tomas asked. "Once the gallery floods, Kessler knows. He'll come for us."

"After," Yara said, "the truth is underwater and the ledger copies are already moving downriver. There's nothing left for him to take except us, and I don't intend to be here when he comes looking."

Okonkwo spread his own work next—a hydrology map of the gorge so detailed it looked almost like art, contour lines flowing into cross-sections, the flood channel rendered in blue ink that faded to pale where the water would spread shallow.

"The numbers changed again overnight," he said. "Landfall's moved up again—I'd now say tomorrow night, possibly earlier. River stage at the portal will rise past the pump intake within six hours of the front hitting. Here's what matters." His finger traced the channel. "Your father's sluice sits at the low point of the old construction adit. When it opens, water won't trickle in—it'll surge, following the grade he cut deliberately toward the lower gallery. The upper gallery stays dry. The access tunnel stays dry for roughly forty minutes, maybe fifty, depending on how fast the river comes up. After that, the whole complex is gone."

"Forty minutes," Bea repeated quietly.

"For anyone inside when the gate opens," Okonkwo said, looking at her steadily, "forty minutes is not a margin. It's a countdown."

"That's why I'll be on the surface," Bea said, "calling the dive."

Every head turned to her.

Bea stood, and Yara saw her cousin plant her feet the way she did before a deep descent—weight forward, jaw set, the stillness of someone committing to cold water. "There's a manual release on the sluice mechanism. Backup to the shaped charges—a wheel-and-screw arrangement your father built so one person could open it without explosives if they had to. It's underwater, it's twelve years silted, and it's going to take hands, not charges, to make sure it opens clean. Charges might shear the pins. Hands guarantee it." She met Yara's eyes. "I'm the diver. It's my job. And before you argue—you broke protocol to pull me out of the sump. You don't get to invoke protocol now."

"Bea—"

"You said no one decides alone anymore." Her voice didn't rise, but it filled the tent. "So don't decide alone. Ask the room."

Yara looked around. Tomas gave a slow nod. Okonkwo: "Her air management is the best I've seen in fifteen years of watching divers." Nia: "I'd rather have her hands on that wheel than trust concrete pins in a flood surge." Even Ferro nodded.

Yara exhaled. "Line and voice comms. Hard tether, no free swimming. Okonkwo calls the current intervals, I call the count. If you're not clear in twenty-five minutes, we come in after you—and this time I mean all of us, together, no lone-hero entries." She swallowed. "That's the deal. Take it or I tie you to a rail spike myself."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Bea's face. "Deal."

* * *

They worked through the morning in a rhythm that felt, strangely, like peace. Nia and the Delgado brothers hauled charge cases up the portal road under tarpaulins, moving with the unhurried precision of people who had stopped pretending the deadline belonged to Kessler. Okonkwo sat in the survey tent updating his models, calling out river stages every hour in a voice that climbed a little each time. Tomas organized the counterfeit shipment's final staging with Rios and Mabuka, checking crate seals twice, three times, while the real artifacts—crated by river hands, marked in the elder's script—waited under guard for the scout's signal to move upstream.

Yara walked the perimeter once with the journal against her ribs and found herself memorizing things without trying to: the way rain beaded on the tarp lines, the smell of diesel and wet clay, Ferro singing something tuneless over the coffee fire. Twelve years of constant motion, of never staying a season, of keeping ahead of debts and memories both—and now, on the eve of the largest thing she'd ever attempted, she felt still. Not trapped-still. Chosen-still.

At midday she found Tomas on the ridge above the portal, watching Kessler's camp through binoculars.

"Activity?" she asked.

"Barge loaded. They're rigging covers—heavy ones, waterproofed. He means to move tonight, ahead of the storm." Tomas lowered the glasses. "Which means he discovers the swap either at the transfer point or not at all. If the storm hits first, he never checks the cargo."

"He'll check it. Men like Kessler check." She squinted down at the portal, the concrete face dark with rain, the pumps chugging their stubborn rhythm. "He'll find plaster and river clay, and he'll know exactly who did it, and he'll come here. That's fine. Everything we need will be finished by then."

"And you? Are you finished?" Tomas asked carefully. "With the journal. With him being right about your father. With all of it?"

Yara thought about the scratched message inside the lock door. *Tell no one.* Her father had died carrying the secret alone, and the carrying had killed him as surely as the blast. She had spent twelve years doing the same thing in slower motion.

"No," she said. "But I'm done carrying it wrong."

Below them, the river rose another inch against the gauge stake, patient as a promise. And far downstream, where the valley bent toward the coast, the sky had begun to organize itself into something with intent.

Tomas handed her the binoculars. "Storm's early," he said. "Okonkwo says we may have lost our night."

Yara raised the glasses to the southern horizon, where the clouds had stacked themselves into a wall the color of wet iron.

"Then we move now," she said.
