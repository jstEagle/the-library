## 31. The Counterfeit Shipment

The plaster came from the pump-house floor.

Nia had scraped it out of the settling basin in the gray hour before dawn, when the slurry was thickest and the generators were too loud for anyone to hear a shovel. She carried it back to the drying tent in a fuel drum cut in half, set it down in front of the elder's people like a woman presenting a bride price, and said, "It's not marble. But at three in the morning, in a flooded tunnel, with a man holding a lantern over your shoulder—neither is anything else."

Old Mekam turned his hands through it, let the wet grit run between his fingers, and smiled for the first time since Yara had known him. "My grandmother made pots from worse," he said. "She made them so well that the government men who came to buy them never knew they were buying her jokes."

That was the word they built the whole plan on: *jokes*. Not lies—lies could be prosecuted, lies had weight and consequence—but jokes, the kind of patient humor that river people had been practicing against collectors and commissioners for a hundred years. The elder's women brought river clay from the oxbow below camp, grey-brown and fine as flour, and mixed it with the plaster and crushed shell until it took an edge like soapstone. Nia drew the profiles of the crates' contents from memory—she had spent one illicit hour inside crate fourteen before Yara ordered it resealed, and she had a surveyor's eye for proportion—and by the second evening the drying racks under the tarp looked like a museum storeroom struck by a polite earthquake: panels, masks, drum faces, the long carved prow-ornament that Okonkwo called "the crocodile that ate the railway."

They worked in the open, deliberately. That was Yara's contribution to the craft of it. Kessler's camp was watched—Tomas had counted four pairs of eyes on the ridge in three nights, and Bea had mapped their positions down to the rock each man sat on—so there was no point pretending the racks didn't exist. Instead they made the copying *boring*. They let Kessler's watchers see the river people squatting around the tarp, shaping clay, arguing about proportions, laughing. Let them see a salvage crew's usual chaos. What you can see every day, you stop looking at.

"They'll report it," Tomas said, watching the ridge through binoculars. "But they'll report it as what? 'Natives making souvenirs.'"

"Report it as exactly that," Yara said. "Souvenirs for the ceremony. The communities want tokens of what came home. It's even true."

It was true, in the way that mattered. Some of the pieces *were* going home—not upstream to the villages yet, but to the ceremony ground, where the elder's people would hold them, sing to them, and then quietly trade them among themselves until no one outside the valley could say which panel had been in which village. Provenance by diffusion. Mekam explained it while he smoothed a mask's brow with a wet thumb: "A thing that belongs to everyone cannot be stolen from anyone. You are trying to protect objects. We are trying to make protection unnecessary."

Yara had thought about that sentence for most of a night, lying in her tent with the journal under her sleeping bag, listening to the river rise. Her father would have understood it. Maybe he *had* understood it, twelve years ago, and that was part of why the families had needed him dead. A man who believed objects should be diffuse was a man who couldn't be bought off with a single museum wing and a plaque.

* * *

The problem was weight.

Plaster and clay lie beautifully, but they lie light. The real crates—the ones already loaded on the barge in the first theft, the ones Kessler's men had carried out under floodlights with the tenderness of men handling their own futures—had been heavy. Museum-grade packing, steel-strapped, dense with hardwood crating. Anyone lifting a replacement would feel the difference in the first meter.

"We pour them solid," Nia said. "Clay around a stone core. I weighed one of the empties." She slapped a chalk figure on the tent pole: 61 kilos. "We match it within five. Nobody hand-checks a strapped crate twice, not at night, not in this mud, not with monsoon coming."

Okonkwo solved the sound. Real crates, tapped, answered with a dull wooden knock; hollow fakes rang like drums. So they packed the replicas tight against the walls, filled the voids with damp sand in stitched rice bags, and Nia practiced tapping them with a knuckle until she could fool herself, which she announced was the only standard that mattered. "I have fooled myself professionally for twenty years," she said. "I'm very good at it."

Bea did the water math. The swap had to happen on the barge, mid-river, because the crates were already aboard and Kessler counted them twice a day against his manifest. Moving forty crates from barge to shore and back without a crane meant rafts, and rafts meant current, and current in the rising Fly meant everything Okonkwo's models kept saying in their flat, terrible voice: nine days early, six feet overnight, don't be on the water after dark.

So they went on the water after dark.

* * *

They chose the night of the dark moon, or rather the dark moon chose itself, because it was the only night left. The monsoon's leading edge was visible now even to eyes that didn't read hydrology—a bruised shelf of cloud stacked up over the western ranges, flickering internally like something thinking. The river had come up two feet in three days and had gone the color of strong tea. The barge lay at its mooring in the channel, riding high and empty except for the forty crates staged for the second shipment, lashed under tarps, guarded by two of Kessler's men who rotated shifts with the enthusiasm of men guarding furniture.

The plan was almost insultingly simple, which Yara knew was the only kind of plan that survived contact with a river.

The river people would fish. They fished every night anyway; their lamps on the water were as unremarkable as stars. Four dugouts would work the eddy above the barge mooring, close enough to seem careless. At the change of guard—Tomas knew the rotation, had bought the schedule with cigarettes and sympathy from a Kessler man named Ruiz who had stopped believing in the job somewhere around the night his own crewmates were left hanging off a rail line in floodwater—the fishing lamps would drift down past the barge, and every eye aboard would go to the lights, because lights on black water do that to you.

And in the shadow of the barge's downstream hull, Bea and two of her cousins would pass the crates across, one at a time, onto a low raft riding ballasted to sit just proud of the surface. Real crates off. Fake crates on. Same straps, same tarps, same stenciled numbers—Nia had restenciled the replica crates by lamplight, matching the faded originals stroke for stroke, even reproducing the coffee ring on number twenty-two.

"Forty crates," Bea said, running it one last time in the tent, her finger moving across the mud map. "Four passes. Six minutes per crate if we're slow, four if we're good. Guard change gives us eleven minutes of lazy backs. Then we're working in front of two awake men and a river that wants us dead."

"Then we're good," Yara said.

Bea looked at her across the map, and for a moment neither of them was talking about crates. Twelve years of silence had burned down to this: a sister-cousin who dove into black water on Yara's word, and Yara learning to give words worth diving on.

"I'll be on the line," Yara said. "Same as the sluice will be. You'll hear my voice the whole time."

"I know," Bea said. "That's the only reason I agreed to any of this." She rolled the map closed. "Don't make me regret having reasons."

* * *

The first pass went like a held breath released slowly.

Yara crouched on the barge's stern deck in the dark, headset pressed to her ear, watching the fishing lamps burn their way down the eddy. The guards sat in the wheelhouse playing cards by hurricane lamp, and she could hear the shuffle and slap of the deal, Ruiz's laugh pitched carefully loud. Below her, muffled by the hull, Bea's hands found the first strap and cut it with a motion Yara felt more than heard.

"Number seven coming off," Bea breathed into the wire.

"Copy. Lamps drifting wide. You have all the time the river gives you."

The crate came across the gap between barge and raft on two bodies and a plank, swinging low, kissing the water once. Its twin went back the same way. Nia had numbered the fakes in chalk on their undersides, matched to the manifest, and Bea's cousin checked each one against a slate by feel alone—no light, no light ever—and gave the double tap that meant *seated and strapped*.

Seven. Eleven. Three. Nineteen. The numbers crawled. Somewhere in the middle of the second pass the rain started, a fat, intermittent spatter that everyone on both sides of the transaction pretended was weather and nothing else, though Yara saw Bea's cousin pause once, head cocked, reading the sky the way his people had read it for longer than there had been barges to hide under.

"Rain inside an hour," he murmured. "Real rain."

"Then we're faster than rain," Bea said.

On the third pass, disaster arrived wearing the face of pure bad luck: one of the fishing lamps guttered out. Just a lamp, soaked wick, ordinary failure—but the darkness it left pulled every eye on the barge toward the eddy, including, fatally, one guard's, who stood up in the wheelhouse doorway with his card hand still fanned and stared directly at the water ten meters from where Bea stood hip-deep beside a crate that did not exist according to any manifest.

Yara did the only thing her body offered. She picked up the mooring winch handle and dropped it.

The clang cracked across the channel like a rifle. Both guards spun toward the stern. "Sorry!" she called, loud, irritated, a woman fighting equipment in the rain. "This thing's older than the war! Give me a hand or keep dealing!"

Ruiz came, because Ruiz wanted to be anywhere but near the man whose nerve was fraying, and together they hauled the fouled mooring line while Yara cursed it with a fluency that surprised even her. Behind them, unheard, unwatched, the river moved thirty-one crates of history off a barge and replaced them with thirty-one jokes made of plaster, clay, and shell.

By the time the fourth pass ended, the rain had arrived in earnest, and the fishing lamps were gone upstream, and the raft was gone, and the river had folded over the whole operation like a page turning.

Forty for forty. Bea climbed aboard at the stern ladder, streaming, grinning, and put her forehead against Yara's shoulder for exactly one second—an entire reconciliation compressed into a second—and then went to check her gear, because sentiment drowns divers.

* * *

The real crates went upriver that same night, which was the whole point of the madness of the timing.

Mekam's people had prepared for it the way they prepared for everything: invisibly. The raft didn't fight the current—it ferried edge to edge, hopping the slack water along the inside bends, moving at the speed of patience. At the first village landing, forty crates walked ashore on the shoulders of men and women who had been told nothing and asked nothing, and were dispersed before dawn into houses, garden sheds, the false floors of fish-smoking racks, the spaces beneath church floors that colonial builders had never suspected would serve the faith better empty. By the time the sun came up, there was no such thing as a cache of artifacts in the valley. There were only villages, being villages.

Yara stood on the bank at first light and watched the last dugout fade into the rain-haze, and felt something in her chest unclench that she hadn't known was clenched. Twelve years ago she had signed a piece of paper that buried the truth in concrete. Last night she had helped carry it out of the dark and give it back to the people it belonged to. Both acts had been done quietly, in the small hours, by someone's daughter. Only one of them had been hers.

"You're smiling," Tomas said, materializing beside her with two cups of terrible coffee.

"I'm wet and I haven't slept."

"You're smiling anyway."

She took the coffee. "Don't tell Kessler. He'll want a percentage."

* * *

Kessler inspected the barge at noon.

He did it the way he did everything now—faster, harder, with less pretense of courtesy, a man who could feel the monsoon breathing down his neck and had started counting his assets like a gambler counting chips near closing time. He walked the rows of crates under the tarps, checked stencils against his manifest, rapped two of them with a knuckle—Yara's heart performed a controlled demolition at each knock—and got back the dull wooden answer Nia had tuned them to give.

"Condition?" he asked, not looking at her.

"Dry. Strapped. Counted twice." All true. Every word true. That was the beauty and the horror of it: the best cover story was assembled entirely from accurate statements arranged in a misleading order.

"The shipment goes the day after the storm front clears. Whatever's left of it." He glanced west at the cloud wall, and for a moment—just a moment—something moved behind his eyes that wasn't greed. It might have been fear. It might have been the look of a man who had spent his life buying things and had just realized the largest thing he'd ever tried to buy was a hole in the weather. "Your people did well," he said. "Tell them there's a bonus in it."

"I'll tell them," Yara said, and meant: *I'll tell them how well they did, later, when it's safe, over food, laughing.*

He paused at the gangway, one boot on the rail, and looked back at her with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. "You know what I can't work out, Vasquez? Twelve years ago your father sealed this place and everyone called it tragedy. Now you're helping me open it and everyone calls it opportunity. Same tunnel. Same family." He tapped the rail. "People don't change sides that cleanly. They just get better at hiding which side they were on all along."

He walked down the gangway into the rain.

Yara stood very still until he was gone, then went to find Nia, because there was one detail left, and it was the detail that would either save them or hang them: the first shipment. The barge with the original stolen crates—the ones taken before the swap, before anyone knew what the job really was—was still sitting mid-channel, stranded by the rising water, waiting for weather. Forty real artifacts, Kessler's property in every legal sense, floating in the path of the biggest storm in a decade.

"He'll move them the moment the wind drops," Nia said, when Yara laid it out. "First calm window, he runs them downriver to the coast. After that, they're gone. Museums, private vaults, whatever. Unrecoverable."

Yara looked at the river. It had risen another foot since dawn and had begun carrying whole trees past the camp, silently, like a procession.

"No," she said. "He won't get a calm window. And neither will we." She turned to Nia, and heard her own voice come out level and cold and certain, her father's voice, the one from the journal, the one that had written *the river keeps what it keeps.* "Which means whatever happens next happens fast, and happens wet, and happens our way or not at all. Get me Okonkwo. And get me Bea."

Behind them, far up the gorge, thunder rolled down the valley like the first car of a train that could not be stopped—and from the ridge, unnoticed in the noise, one of Kessler's watchers slipped away through the rain, carrying news of the night's strange fishing lamps toward the man who paid him.
