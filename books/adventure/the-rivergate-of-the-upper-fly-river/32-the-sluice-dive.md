## 32. The Sluice Dive

The water at the sluice channel had gone from green to the color of wet slate overnight, and Bea Vasquez stood on the rocks above it in her drysuit, testing the current with a weighted line, and thought: *this is the water that killed him.*

Not literally. Her uncle Emilio had died in dry air, in a tunnel, behind a door he'd built himself. But the river had done the killing, and the river was doing it again now, six feet higher than yesterday, brown and muscular, dragging whole trees past the gorge mouth like a man dragging furniture out of a house he'd lost to the bank.

"You don't have to do this," Yara said beside her. She'd said it four times since dawn. She'd say it again before Bea clipped in. It was a ritual now, the way checking her gauges twice was a ritual, the way their mother used to touch the doorframe twice before leaving the house.

"I know." Bea pulled the weighted line up. The current took it sideways at forty degrees. "That's why I'm doing it."

* * *

The sluice gate sat two hundred meters upstream of the portal, in a slot canyon the survey maps called the Throat. Emilio Vasquez had cut it into the rock himself, twelve years ago, when he built the flood channel—a failsafe buried in the blueprints, never filed, never mentioned in the closure report. Okonkwo had found it by triangulating the water table, the pressure readings, and one line in the old journal that Yara had finally, after the long night, read aloud to all of them: *The gate is the mercy. If it ever has to be opened, open it from outside, and open it fast.*

Open it from outside. Which meant a diver, in floodwater, in a slot canyon that funneled the whole rising river through a gap barely wider than a truck.

The plan was simple the way all terrible plans are simple. A guide line from the staging rock to the gate mechanism, forty meters of dive through black water. The gate was a manual rack-and-pinion, because Emilio had trusted hands more than hydraulics. Turn it, and the sluice would open into the lower gallery, and the river would take back what Emilio had sealed inside it—the evidence room, the ledger's original, everything Kessler wanted—and everything Kessler wanted destroyed.

The replicas were already on the barge. The real artifacts were already moving upstream in dugouts, wrapped in oilcloth, paddled by men and women who had waited twelve years for them to come home. All that remained was the water.

All that remained was her.

* * *

Bea had been diving since she was nine years old. Her uncle had taught her, in the same river, in the same basin, before any of it went wrong. He used to say: *The water isn't your enemy, mija. The water doesn't want anything. Fear is the only thing down there that wants something, and fear is yours, so fear is the thing you manage.*

She managed it now, in the grey light, while Nia checked her harness and Okonkwo read the flow rate off his instruments with a face like a man reading a death certificate.

"Forty-one hundred cubic feet per second through the Throat," Okonkwo said. "Visibility will be under a meter. If you lose the line, you do not search for it. You inflate, you go up, you let the river decide. We will find you downstream. We will find you."

"You will find me," Bea agreed, "or the river will keep me, and my cousin will spend the rest of her life feeling guilty, which, honestly, might be the better outcome for her."

"Bea." Yara's voice.

Bea turned. Her cousin stood in the rain in her ruined boots, holding the comms handset like it was something alive, and for a second Bea saw the seventeen-year-old she'd hated at the funeral—the girl who'd signed the report, who'd let the lie stand, who'd let the family fracture over a silence nobody explained. And then she saw past her, the way you see past the surface of dark water, to the woman underneath: the one who had broken her own safety protocol to pull Bea out of the sump; the one who had told them everything, finally, in the lamplit tent, voice shaking, hands open; the one who had chosen, this time, what to sign.

"I'll be listening the whole way," Yara said. "Every meter. I've got your father's notes on the gate. He wrote the clearances in the margin. He wrote them like he knew someone would come."

"He did know," Bea said. "He just hoped it wouldn't be his own blood."

She spat into the river—old habit, an offering, her grandmother's ritual—and went down the rocks to the water.

* * *

The cold hit her like a hand.

Floodwater in the basin didn't get cold gradually; it came down off the highlands with the mountain still in it, and it closed over her hood and pressed against the drysuit and the world became the sound of her own breathing and the roar of the Throat, translated through water into something felt rather than heard—a vibration in the teeth, in the sternum, in the old scar tissue along her ribs where a propeller had caught her in another life.

The guide line ran taut into the black. She clipped her tether, checked it, checked it again, and followed.

One meter of visibility meant the world was a brown blur and a rope. She moved hand over hand, fins kicking against a current that wanted her gone, that pulled at her legs like a crowd pulling at a coat. Her dive computer glowed amber in the murk. Depth: six meters. Eight. The channel bottom dropped away beneath her and she was swimming over the drowned throat of the gorge, over the place where the river poured into the mountain.

"Bea, talk to me." Yara's voice in her ear, tinny through the full-face mask, the most beautiful sound in the world.

"I'm at the drop-off. Current's worse than Okonkwo said."

"Everything's worse than Okonkwo said. That's why we hired him."

Bea laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and she kept moving.

Ten meters. Twelve. The line shivered in her hands—Nia and Tomas topside, paying it out, their hands on her lifeline, literal and otherwise. She thought about the funeral, twelve years ago, the rain, the folded flag they didn't get because there was no flag for a surveyor, just a box of effects and a report with her cousin's signature on it. She'd hated Yara for that signature. She'd spent twelve years hating it, and it had taken a sump dive gone wrong and a near-drowning for her to understand that the signature had been a seventeen-year-old's attempt to keep the rest of them alive, and that Yara had been carrying it the way Bea carried the river—in the chest, always, whether she thought about it or not.

Fifteen meters. The line kinked around something and her heart slammed—snag, rock, deadfall—and then freed, and she was through, into the slot where the gate housing was, and her light found it.

Emilio's work. She knew it before her light finished moving across it. The welds were his—overlapped, deliberate, ugly and perfect, the welds of a man who built things to outlast arguments. The rack-and-pinion mechanism sat behind a debris cage, and the debris cage was clear, and the wheel was greased.

Greased. Twelve years underwater, and the mechanism was greased.

He had maintained it. After the sealing, after the report, after everything—her uncle had come back, or sent someone, and kept the mercy oiled, waiting for the day someone came to open it.

Bea put her hands on the wheel and, for a moment, did nothing at all. The river roared through the slot above her. Her breath hammered in the mask. She was crying, or the water had found a way in; down there it was impossible to tell, and it didn't matter.

"Bea?" Yara's voice, sharp now. "Your breathing's gone ragged. Talk to me."

"He kept it," Bea said. "Yara. He greased the gate. He came back. He always meant for it to be opened."

A silence on the line, two seconds, three—Yara doing the same math she was doing: the maintenance visits, the risk, the secrecy. Their uncle, slipping up the river alone, tending a door he'd built for a flood he'd started, hoping his own daughter or his own niece would one day be the one to turn the wheel.

"Then turn it," Yara said, and her voice cracked down the middle, and Bea loved her completely, finally, without remainder.

* * *

The wheel was stiff, then not. Emilio's clearances were exact. It turned like something waiting.

The sluice opened with a sound she felt more than heard—a deep, tectonic groan through the rock, and then the current changed, and the black water around her began to move, all of it, in one direction, down into the mountain. The river was pouring into the lower gallery. Somewhere behind and below her, Kessler's evidence room was filling with twelve years of held-back water, the ledger's original drowning in the dark, the past sealing itself the way it should have been sealed the first time.

"Gate's open," Bea said. "Flow's going in. Get me out."

"Getting you out," Yara said, and there was something in her voice Bea had never heard in it before—not command, not fear. Wonder. "Bea. He built it in. The whole time, he built it in."

"I know." Bea kicked for the line, for the surface, for the light. "Come up to the staging rock. I found something else. You need to see it."

Because there, bolted to the gate housing, in a waterproof sleeve that had been greased and maintained along with everything else, was a brass survey tag. Stamped with the initials E.V. And beside it, scratched into the metal in her uncle's handwriting, the same hand that had written *flood it, tell no one* on the lower gallery door, four words:

*FOR MY GIRLS. TOGETHER.*

* * *

She broke the surface into the rain and Nia's hands, and they hauled her up the rocks, and she knelt there coughing while Okonkwo pressed a thermos into her grip and Tomas wrapped a blanket around her shoulders, and through all of it she kept her fist closed around the brass tag.

Yara came down the rocks at a run, which Yara never did, and dropped to her knees in the puddles, and Bea opened her hand.

Her cousin looked at the tag for a long time. The rain came down. Somewhere below them, muffled by rock and water, the Rivergate was flooding on their uncle's terms, on their terms, together.

"He was waiting for us," Yara said. "Both of us."

"He was waiting for you," Bea said. "I just did the diving."

And then the radio on Tomas's belt crackled, and the scout's voice came through, fast and ragged: the barge, Kessler's barge, moving upriver ahead of schedule—and Kessler wasn't on it. Kessler's skiff had been seen at the portal. Kessler had found the flooded gallery, and he was coming for them.
