## 33. Kessler's Last Train

The radio crackled to life at 3:14 in the morning, and Yara knew before anyone spoke that the night had turned against them.

She was in the survey tent with the journal open on her knees, reading her father's handwriting for the hundredth time, when Tomas lifted the flap and put two fingers to his lips. Behind him, at the edge of camp, Nia's radio hissed—a cheap handheld they'd confiscated from one of Kessler's defectors, tuned to the expedition's private channel because Kessler's people were creatures of habit and never changed their frequencies.

The voice coming through was thin with distance and rain. *"—movement at the portal, repeat, movement at the portal. Crates going out on the flatcar. Voss out."*

Kessler had a man inside. Of course he did. Yara had counted the defectors twice—seven of Kessler's workers had crossed over after the barge rescue, seven men who'd watched their employer order cargo saved before human beings—and she'd trusted them the way you trust a rope you didn't tie yourself: fully, and with a knife in your teeth.

"Who?" she asked.

Tomas shook his head. "Doesn't matter yet. What matters is the flatcar. It's supposed to go up the spur at dawn with the last shipment."

The last shipment. The counterfeit crates—forty-one of them, plaster and river clay and old packing foam, built in three sleepless days by Nia and six hands from the elder's community, painted with pigment ground from river stone until even Bea had stood in front of them and said, quietly, *I'd sign for these.* They sat in the upper gallery now, stacked where the real objects had been, while the true cargo—the carved panels, the ceremonial masks, the things that had been stolen from living villages a lifetime ago—rode upstream on a barge captained by a woman named Sefu who asked no questions and took no passengers.

And Kessler's man had just told him the crates were moving.

"He'll come tonight," Yara said. "Not dawn. He won't wait for light when he thinks we're loading behind his back."

Tomas looked at her the way he'd looked at her for twelve years, across two continents and a hundred washed-out rails: waiting for the order, ready to argue with it, loyal enough to do either. Rain drummed the tent canvas. Somewhere down the gorge, the Fly River made its low continuous sound, like something enormous breathing in its sleep.

"Get Nia," Yara said. "Wake Okonkwo. And find Bea—she should be back from the sluice by now."

* * *

Bea came back soaked to the collar and grinning, which was how Yara knew the dive had gone clean. Her cousin ducked into the equipment shed where they'd gathered, wrung out her dive hood, and read the room in one sweep—Yara's face, Tomas's clenched jaw, the radio still murmuring static—and the grin died.

"How bad?"

"Kessler knows about the shipment," Yara said. "Maybe not the swap. But he knows the crates are moving, and he's got a man inside telling him everything else."

Bea set her gear down slowly. Water pooled under it. "Then we move the fakes now. Tonight. Put them on the flatcar, send it up the spur before he can—"

"No." Okonkwo spoke from the corner, where he'd been watching the rain gauge with the grim attention of a man whose models had all come true too early. He tapped the gauge's glass. "Look at this. Six inches since midnight. The spur crosses the washout at Kilometer Nine—that cut fills like a bathtub in this rain. A loaded flatcar goes up there tonight, it derails, and Kessler finds forty-one crates of plaster scattered down a hillside. We hand him the truth gift-wrapped."

"So we do nothing?"

"We do what we planned," Yara said. "Dawn run. The water drops an hour before landfall—Okonkwo's models say the river backs up first, holds the tributaries, then releases. There's a window. Narrow, but real."

"And if Kessler doesn't wait for our window?" Bea asked. "If he comes down here with guns and takes the gallery tonight?"

Yara looked at the journal on the table between them—her father's square, patient handwriting, the ledger pages she'd photographed and passed to the scout, the whole buried architecture of a crime twelve years deep. She thought about the message scratched inside the lower gallery door. *Flood it. Tell no one. The river keeps what it keeps.*

"Then we're past planning," she said. "Nia—how fast can you arm the portal charges?"

Nia had been quiet the whole time, sitting on a crate of detonators with her forearms on her knees, and when she looked up there was something in her face Yara hadn't seen since the pump line sabotage: fear, worn openly, like a wound that had stopped pretending to be a bruise.

"Ten minutes," Nia said. "But Yara—if he brings men into that gallery, and those charges are armed, and someone trips a wire—" She stopped. Started again. "I've rigged demolition for twelve years and I've never once armed a charge I hoped wouldn't go off. Don't make me start tonight."

"I'm not arming anything," Yara said. "Not yet. We load at first light, same as planned. If Kessler comes early, we stall him. That's all. We stall."

It sounded thin even as she said it, and everyone in the shed heard how thin. But there was nothing else, so they dispersed into the rain to wait for dawn, and Yara stayed behind with the journal, and the river breathed, and the night got shorter.

* * *

Kessler's train came at 4:40, ninety minutes before first light, running dark with its engine muffled.

Yara heard it before she saw it—a change in the ground, a tremor in the soles of her boots, the particular shudder that heavy steel sends through wet earth. She was standing at the portal with Tomas, having slept perhaps forty minutes in a chair, and the sound went through her like a memory: locomotives on the Rivergate spur, her father's world, the world she'd spent half her life trying not to hear.

Headlights bloomed around the bend below the portal. Not the work train's lights—these were high-mounted, paired, a mainline engine's array. Kessler had brought his own locomotive up from the staging yard, and behind it rode at least two flatcars and a caboose, and riding with them, by the silhouettes visible in the cab glow, were eight or nine men carrying long arms.

"Tomas," Yara said quietly. "Go wake the others. Get them to the upper gallery access, not the portal. Nobody comes out here but me."

"You're not meeting him alone."

"I'm not meeting him alone," she agreed. "You're just not standing next to me when I do. Go."

He went, because twelve years of following her orders is its own kind of argument, and Yara stepped out of the shadow of the portal into the rain and walked down the ramp toward the headlights with her hands visible and her heart going like a trip-hammer.

The train braked fifty meters short. Steam and rain mixed in the light. Doors banged. Men dropped to the ballast and spread out with the loose, practiced economy of hired security, and then Dario Kessler came forward alone, no umbrella, no helmet, silver-haired and immaculate even at four in the morning in a monsoon, as if weather were a formality he'd negotiated away years ago.

In his left hand, held loosely at his side like a book he'd been reading on the journey, was her father's journal.

Yara's stomach dropped through the wet gravel. She'd kept it in her tent. In a lockbox. Under her cot. Which meant either Kessler's man inside was better than she'd feared, or—

"You're wondering," Kessler called over the rain, "about the journal. Don't. Your cousin's tent. She's been sleeping with it since the ultimatum—did you know that? Family habits. You Vasquez women keep your most dangerous possessions close to your hearts." He smiled without warmth. "I had it copied before I ever offered it to you, Yara. The original was always bait. You've been guarding a decoy for a week."

Every plan she'd built in the last hour—the stall, the delay, the dawn window—quietly collapsed. She kept her voice level. "You came yourself. At four in the morning. In this weather. For crates of plaster."

"For my cargo." He walked closer, boots crunching, men fanning wide behind him. "My man on your crew radioed me at three. Crates moving at dawn, he said. Flatcar prepped. So I did the arithmetic I should have done weeks ago." He stopped ten feet from her, close enough that she could see the rain beading on his shoulders and not rolling off—the man even waterproofed expensively. "Forty-one crates went into that gallery. Forty-one crates are scheduled to come out. But I've been reviewing the manifests, Yara. The weights. My crane logs. Do you know what a carved hardwood panel weighs? What a bronze does? I do. I've moved enough of them." His eyes were pale and patient and terrible. "Your crates are light."

The rain filled the silence. Downriver, faint beneath the storm, the Fly kept breathing.

"There's still time," Kessler said, and his voice changed—not softer, exactly, but more intimate, the voice he'd used in her tent the day he'd offered partnership. "That's what I want you to understand. Whatever game you've played, whatever righteous little theater you've staged with the river people, the real objects are on a barge upstream, and barges are slow, and I own every landing on this river above the gorge. You can tell me where, and we finish this transaction like professionals, and your crew walks away rich and uncharged. Or—" he lifted the journal slightly, "—we continue, and I promise you, Yara, I have lawyers who can bury your cousin's certification so deep she'll be diving swimming pools in Dubai for tourist tips, and prosecutors who will take your entire crew apart crate by crate for theft of cultural property. I don't need to win beautifully. I only need to win."

"You're standing in the rain threatening me with paperwork," Yara said. "At my father's grave."

Something flickered across Kessler's face—irritation, maybe, or the ghost of respect. "Your father," he said, "was paid the same as any man on that original expedition. He simply developed scruples late, and inconveniently, and it cost him. Sentiment is expensive, Yara. I'm offering you a discount."

Behind him, his men had begun to move toward the portal. Two of them carried crowbars. One had a cutting torch slung across his back, and Yara understood with cold clarity that Kessler hadn't come to negotiate at all—he'd come to open the gallery himself, verify the swap with his own eyes, and take whatever leverage remained. The conversation was decoration. It had always been decoration.

"The crates aren't in the upper gallery anymore," she said.

Kessler raised one hand and his men stopped. "No?"

"They're on the flatcar. Prepped for dawn, just like my man told you. Go look. Open every one. Enjoy the craftsmanship—the river communities did beautiful work. Plaster and clay, Dario. Every panel, every mask. The real ones have been gone for two days."

For a moment Kessler simply stood in the rain, and Yara watched him do the arithmetic again, faster now, the numbers turning ugly. Then he smiled, and it was the worst thing she'd seen all year, because it was calm.

"Well," he said. "Then we're both out of time, aren't we."

He turned and spoke three words to his men, low and unhurried, and the formation changed. Four of them peeled off toward the flatcar. The rest closed toward Yara in a shallow arc, weapons low but present, and Kessler walked the last ten feet and took her arm—not violently; that was the horrifying part, the courtesy of it, a host guiding a guest—and turned her toward the portal.

"Walk," he said. "We're going to have a look at your father's door. And then, Ms. Vasquez, you and I are going to discuss the ledger—because there is a ledger, there always was, your father's journal makes that abundantly clear, and copies of it are the only thing standing between your crew and a very long legal winter. Where are the photographs, Yara? Who has them?"

The portal loomed ahead, concrete scarred by her father's charges, black water still weeping from the seams twelve years after the flood. Yara let herself be guided, counting steps, counting men, listening past the rain for the sounds she needed—the river rising, the tributaries backing up, the window Okonkwo had promised opening somewhere in the dark above the gorge.

Her hand brushed her coat pocket, where a single brass survey tag rode—stamped *E.V.*, her father's initials, pulled from a crate weeks ago. For luck, she'd told herself. For memory.

Now it felt like instructions.

At the threshold of the Rivergate, with Kessler's grip firm on her arm and his men fanning into the dark behind them, Yara heard it—distant, unmistakable, right on schedule: the deep hydraulic groan of the old sluice gate beginning, inch by inch, to open.

Bea was already in the water.
