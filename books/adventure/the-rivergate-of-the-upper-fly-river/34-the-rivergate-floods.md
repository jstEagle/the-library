## 34. The Rivergate Floods

The rain came down the gorge in ropes.

Yara stood at the portal with her hand pressed flat against the concrete face of the Rivergate, feeling it shudder like a held breath. Behind her, the camp was a chaos of tarps snapping loose and crates being dragged toward the barge landing—Kessler's men shouting over the storm, their headlamps cutting white cones through water that fell so hard it seemed to come from every direction at once. The river had risen another three feet since midnight. Okonkwo's last reading, shouted through the static of a failing radio, put the flood channel at ninety percent capacity and climbing.

The sluice gate was two hundred meters inside, past the upper gallery, past the junction where the track split toward the lower gallery's iron door—the door her father built, the door Kessler's men had breached six days ago. Bea was already in the water.

"Comms check." Yara keyed the radio at her throat. Her voice came out steadier than her hands. "Bea, talk to me."

Static. Then Bea's voice, filtered through a regulator, tinny and calm in the way that only Bea could be calm—her cousin who hadn't spoken to her for twelve years and now trusted her with her life on a line in black water. *"In the channel. Visibility zero. Following your line."*

The guide line ran from the portal through the flooded lower approach to the sluice mechanism—a wheel-and-ratchet assembly set into a maintenance shaft above the gallery ceiling, installed when the tunnel was built and forgotten by everyone except, apparently, Amado Vasquez. He had built the flood option in. He had left his spare survey tag bolted to the gate housing like a signature, like an instruction, like an apology he'd known his daughter would one day have to read.

*"Pressure's wrong,"* Bea said. *"The gate's been cycled recently. Someone's been down here."*

Kessler's divers, probably. Mapping the same channel, looking for the same lever—not to pull it but to make sure no one else could. It didn't matter anymore. The monsoon had made all of Kessler's schedules worthless except one: get the cargo out before the river took the tunnel whole.

Except the cargo wasn't real. Except the real objects were already moving upstream in dugout canoes, wrapped in oilcloth, carried by people who had waited twelve years for them to come home. Except the ledger pages were already beyond anyone's reach, photographed and passed hand to hand through the rain to the elder's scouts.

All that remained was the sealing.

"Bea." Yara closed her eyes. Rain hammered the hood of her jacket. "When you reach the gate—you don't have to do this. I can send Nia's crew in with charges. It'd be messier, but—"

*"It would bring half the gorge down on the villages,"* Bea said. *"You know that. The sluice is clean. The sluice is his way."* A pause, bubbles rising through the transmitter. *"Yara. I found something."*

Her heart stopped. "What?"

*"A tag. Stamped A.V. Bolted to the gate housing. He wanted whoever opened this to know it was meant to be opened."* Another pause, longer. *"He built us a door out, too. There's an escape channel, dry side, marked with reflectors. He thought of everything."*

"He always did," Yara said, and her voice broke on the second word, and she let it.

* * *

Two hundred meters underwater, Bea Vasquez followed the line through the dark with her father-figure's initials in her glove and her cousin's voice in her ear.

She had learned to dive in these rivers, before the funeral, before the silence. She had learned from men who treated the Fly as a thing to be crossed rather than a thing to be answered, and she had spent twelve years unlearning it—learning to read current the way Yara read rail grades, learning patience, learning that the water gave nothing back that you didn't first give up. Now she hung in the flood channel with the whole weight of the monsoon pressing at her back and felt, absurdly, at home.

The gate housing emerged from the murk like a whale's skull. Brass fittings gone green. The ratchet arm thick with silt—but the release lever was clear. Recently cleared. Someone had wanted this option visible, findable, usable.

Amado Vasquez had died sealing this tunnel with explosives. But first, quietly, without telling anyone—not the expedition, not the company, not his daughter—he had built the alternative. Not destruction. Return. Let the river take back what the railway had cut out of it.

Bea set her hands on the lever.

*"I'm at the gate,"* she said. *"On your word, Yara."*

* * *

At the portal, Yara looked at Tomas.

Tomas had been her second-in-command for nine years. He had rerailed spurs in landslides, talked debt collectors off barges, carried her when she'd broken her ankle in the Strickland gorges and never once mentioned it afterward. He stood beside her now in the rain with water streaming off his jaw, and he said, "Say it plain, jefa. What happens when that gate opens."

"The lower gallery floods. All of it. The evidence room, the breach Kessler's men made, the whole floor—it goes under and stays under. The water finds its old channel. That's what my father designed it to do."

"And you?"

"I'm going in after Bea comes out. There's a case in the evidence room. My father's original journal is in it—I need the field notes, the pages Kessler doesn't have. Two minutes. In and out along the catwalk."

Tomas stared at her. Rain drummed between them like applause or gunfire.

"No," he said.

"Tomas—"

"You signed a report at seventeen because someone told you it would protect people. You told me that story once, drunk, on the Karawari run, and then you denied it for a year." His voice was low and absolutely level. "This is the same shape. One small errand. One small risk. You go into a flooding gallery alone, and either you don't come out, or you come out having decided the next compromise is also small." He stepped closer. "I'll go."

"It's my father's tunnel. It's my name on the—"

"Then say it plain, jefa." He didn't blink. "Is it yours to seal, or yours to die in?"

The question went through her like a spike through rail plate. She thought of her father's message scratched inside the lock door: *Flood it. Tell no one. The river keeps what it keeps.* He hadn't written *avenge me.* He hadn't written *retrieve my papers.* He had written *flood it*—the whole verb, complete, needing nothing added.

"Radio check every thirty seconds," she said finally. "If I miss two, you blow the emergency horn and nobody comes in after me. Nobody. Swear it."

Tomas swore it the way he did everything—without ceremony, without taking his eyes off hers.

* * *

Downstream, at the barge landing, Dario Kessler watched the portal through rain-blurred optics and understood, with the cold clarity of a man watching his own arithmetic fail, that he had lost.

The counterfeit crates sat stacked on the barge—plaster and river clay, painted by lamplight by river women who had laughed while they worked, he now realized, the way artisans laugh at forgery they're proud of. His foreman had reported the swap an hour ago, radio crackling with disbelief: the manifests matched, the seals matched, the weights were within tolerance, and every single crate was hollow. Somewhere upstream, in the teeth of the flood, the real collection was moving through channels no barge could follow, into villages that had no addresses and no lawyers and no price.

He still had the journal—or the copy of it he'd taken from Yara's tent, which he'd read twice and understood only now, too late, was bait. She had let him find it. Every step of this expedition, she had been laying rail ahead of him and letting him believe he was driving the train.

His men were loading the fake crates anyway. Pride, or momentum, or the simple animal refusal of a man to stand in the rain admitting nothing. The barge would go upstream. The fakes would be appraised by experts in Port Moresby and Singapore and eventually someone would notice, and the families would cut him loose like a rotten mooring line. He knew how that worked. He had done it to others often enough.

Through the optics, he saw figures at the portal—Vasquez, her engineer, the diver emerging from the water like something born of it. He saw the woman Vasquez turn and look directly at his position on the ridge, across four hundred meters of deluge, as if she could see him. As if she were checking whether he was watching.

He was. That was the point. She wanted him to watch.

Kessler lowered the binoculars. Around him the storm bent the trees flat, and somewhere below, deep in the rock, he heard—or imagined he heard—a sound like a great door beginning to move.

* * *

"On three," Yara said into the radio. "Bea, when that gate opens, you swim for the escape channel—dry side, reflectors, your father—our uncle's route. Don't wait for anything. Nia, kill the pumps and pull everyone back to high ground. Okonkwo, call it."

Okonkwo's voice came through steady as a metronome: *"Channel capacity ninety-four percent. Landfall surge in approximately forty minutes. If you're doing this, do it now."*

"One," Yara said. "Two." She looked at the portal, at the blast-scarred concrete her father had shaped with his own charges twelve years ago, at the dark water standing patient inside it like a congregation. "Three."

* * *

In the channel, Bea threw her whole body against the lever.

For one endless second nothing moved—silt-locked, rust-welded, dead. Then something released with a concussion she felt in her teeth, a sound like a bone setting, and the gate began to rise, and the river came through.

Not water. Force. The flood channel turned into a throat and the river roared down it, and Bea rode the shockwave with her arms locked around a stanchion, ears screaming, world reduced to brown violence and the guide line burning through her gloved hands. She let the current take her downstream toward the escape channel's mouth, kicked twice, found the reflectors—two green dots glowing in her torch beam like eyes—and hauled herself into the dry-side passage as the main gallery behind her filled with the sound of a mountain swallowing.

*"Gate's open,"* she transmitted, coughing. *"I'm in the escape channel. Yara—Yara, the surge—"*

Static.

* * *

Yara was already inside.

She had gone in the moment Bea's transmission confirmed the gate was moving, running the catwalk above the lower gallery with her torch bouncing off wet steel, the journal case's location burned into her memory from a single visit six days ago. Below her, the evidence room was already drowning—black water pouring through the breach in the old flood wall, lifting crates, peeling museum-grade packing foam into floating islands, erasing in minutes what had been preserved for twelve years. Her father's work. Her family's secret. The bribes, the thefts, the names—all of it going under, not destroyed but returned to custody, filed in the one archive no company could buy.

The case was where she'd left it in her mind: a steel document box bolted beneath the catwalk stairs, her father's initials stenciled on the lid. She reached it as the water reached her boots—cold as a fist, fast as a train. She wrenched the box free. Inside: the field notes, the original ledger pages he'd kept back from the report, the photographs. She stuffed them inside her jacket, zipped it, turned—

And the catwalk was gone.

Not gone—moving. The surge had taken the near span, and the section she stood on was tearing free of its anchors, swinging out over the flooded gallery like a drawbridge to nowhere. The water below was a churning millrace, full of debris, full of the tunnel's own entrails. Her torch spun away into the dark. She dropped to her stomach, locked both arms through the railing, and felt the whole structure groan, tilt, begin to go.

*Flood it,* her father had scratched. *Tell no one.* He had never said it would be gentle. He had never promised the river would distinguish between what it was keeping and who came to watch.

The catwalk swung. Water hit her legs, her waist, tore at the box strapped against her chest. She thought, with strange clarity: *this is the shape of it, then—sealed in with the truth, the way he was.* And she was angry, suddenly, furiously angry at him for building a door for the river and none for his daughter—

Hands. Hands on her collar, hauling upward, a voice shouting her name through the roar.

Tomas had come in after her. Of course he had. He had sworn not to and he had, because Tomas's oaths were load-bearing structures and he knew exactly how much weight they could take before he chose to break one. He lay flat on the anchored end of the catwalk with his boots wedged in the stair frame and his arm down through the broken railing, and Yara grabbed his forearm with both hands, and for a moment they hung there—two people on a tearing bridge over a drowning room, the whole past roaring beneath them.

"Don't you dare let go," Tomas bellowed.

"Wouldn't dream of it," Yara gasped, and climbed.

They went up the stairs on hands and knees as the gallery filled below them, water chasing their heels up the switchback, and they came out through the upper gallery into the portal's grey light just as the last of the catwalk let go and vanished. Behind them, the Rivergate drank. The sound was enormous and unhurried, the sound of something resuming a task interrupted twelve years ago, and when it settled it settled completely, and the lower gallery of the Rivergate ceased to exist as a place anyone could enter.

Yara knelt on the wet concrete, shaking, the document box pressed to her ribs. Tomas knelt beside her, breathing hard, saying nothing, which from Tomas was a whole speech.

Bea came out of the escape channel entrance dripping and furious and pulled them both upright by their jackets. "You said nobody goes in," she shouted at Yara over the rain.

"He said it," Yara managed. "I never did."

And Bea—Bea laughed, a wild cracked sound, and hugged her hard enough to hurt, and for a moment the three of them stood in the portal of the sealed tunnel like survivors of a war only they had fought.

* * *

They watched Kessler's barge from the ridge at dawn—if the grey light could be called dawn, the sky so low with rain it seemed to rest on the water.

The barge struggled upstream against the flood, riding low, loaded with plaster and clay and one man's certainty. Through Okonkwo's binoculars Yara counted the crew on deck, heads down, defeated in the specific way of men who have worked hard for nothing. Kessler stood at the bow. Just before the bend took the barge from view, he raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. A concession of the field.

Nia appeared at Yara's shoulder, soaked, soot-streaked from the emergency charges she'd rigged and never needed. "The fakes will fool an appraiser for maybe a month," she said. "Maybe two."

"Long enough," Yara said. "By then the ledger copies will be in print, and nobody will care what's in Kessler's crates, because everyone will know what was supposed to be in them."

Below them, the river ran high and brown and full, carrying the monsoon down from the mountains, filling the channel her father had chosen for it. Somewhere under that water was an evidence room, a locked door with a message scratched inside it, and a man's last good decision, finally executed by his daughters.

Yara took out the carved token the elder had given her—wood worn smooth, shaped like a fish turning—and held it a long moment. Then she handed it to the scout waiting silently at the treeline, the one who had watched their camp for weeks and guided the canoes upstream.

"Tell him," she said. "The gate is closed. The river keeps what it keeps. And tell him—" her voice caught, and she let it, "—tell him my father kept his promise. It just took us all this time to help him finish it."

The scout nodded once and melted into the green.

Behind them, the first real light of morning found the portal of the Rivergate, and the water standing dark and quiet inside it, and the red line on a map folded in Yara's pocket that she would not mark *sealed*—not this time, not ever again.

Tomas touched her shoulder. "Jefa. We should account for everyone. Get off this ridge before the surge peaks."

Yara looked out at the flooded gorge, at the drowned rails gleaming under the water like veins, and nodded. "Everyone," she said. "Count everyone. This time we count everyone."

But even as she turned to go, her hand stayed flat against the map in her pocket, and she was already thinking about ink.
