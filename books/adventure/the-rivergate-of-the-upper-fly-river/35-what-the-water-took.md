## 35. What the Water Took

The water went down the way a crowd leaves a stadium—slowly at first, then all at once, then in stragglers that kept coming for hours.

Yara stood on the rail spur above the gorge and watched it happen. The Fly had swallowed the lower gallery whole, taken Kessler's evidence room, his pumps, his generators, his carefully catalogued crates of plaster saints and river-clay masks, and now it was giving the gorge back piece by piece: silt first, then the tops of the boulders, then the black teeth of the old pilings where her father's crews had once unloaded rail steel. The portal itself was gone. Not damaged—gone. The concrete face she'd known since childhood, half-drowned and blast-scarred, had simply ceased to exist as an opening. What remained was a wall of collapsed rock and churning brown water, and if you didn't know what had been behind it, you would never guess.

She knew. She would always know.

"You're standing in the rain," Bea said beside her.

"I know."

"Okonkwo says another band is coming through tonight. Real one this time, not the leading edge."

"I know."

Bea didn't say anything else. That was new between them—the silence that didn't need filling—and Yara found she could stand inside it without it pressing on her chest. Twelve years of silence had been a wall. This silence was a roof. She wasn't sure when one had become the other, but somewhere between the sluice gate and Tomas hauling her out of the flood channel by the collar of her jacket, something had been rebuilt, and it was still too fresh to name.

Below them, down on the flat stretch of gravel where the camp had been, people were moving with the particular purposefulness of people who have survived something and not yet decided what to feel about it. Nia's demolition crew was walking the perimeter, checking for unexploded charges out of pure habit even though every charge they'd placed had done exactly what it was meant to do. Okonkwo stood at the edge of the high-water mark driving a survey stake into the mud, marking the flood crest for reasons Yara suspected were part science and part memorial. Two of Kessler's defectors—Marek and the quiet one called Idris—were helping the river people load canoes, moving slowly, careful with their hands, men learning to be trusted again one crate at a time.

And everywhere, everywhere, the river people. They had come down from the villages overnight in a flotilla of dugouts and aluminum skiffs, dozens of them, and they had worked through the worst of the storm without complaint or pause, carrying the real artifacts up the goat trails to the ridge road in oilcloth and banana-leaf wrapping while Kessler's counterfeit shipment sat stranded mid-channel for the river to mock. The carved panels from the upper gallery. The ceremonial objects. The drums and masks and ancestor figures that had ridden a stolen railway into a stolen tunnel and waited twelve years in the dark for someone to carry them home.

They were gone now. All of them. Upstream, dispersed across a dozen villages, unwrapped in smoke-filled houses by hands that knew exactly what they were holding.

Beyond reach. Beyond price. Beyond Kessler.

"He'll be back," Bea said, following her gaze to the empty channel where the barge had been. "Kessler. Men like him don't drown. They just float downstream and buy new boats."

"I know." Yara turned away from the gorge. "But he'll come back to nothing. That's the difference."

* * *

The counting took most of the day, and Yara insisted on doing it the old way, the way her father had taught her on her first survey: names out loud, answers out loud, no name closed until it answered.

She stood on a supply crate at the center of camp with the crew manifest in a plastic sleeve, rain dripping off her elbows, and called them one by one.

"Tomas."

"Here." He was sitting with his arm in a sling, ribs strapped, face still purpled along one side from the scuffle at the pump line, and he said it loud enough that the whole camp heard, because Tomas understood ceremony even when he pretended not to.

"Nia."

"Here, boss." A raised hand from the perimeter walk.

"Okonkwo."

"Present, and the gauge station upstream is intact. We have continuous data through the flood peak. Someone will want that someday."

"Bea."

"Here." A beat. "Still here."

There was a small sound from the gathered crew at that—not quite laughter, not quite anything else—and Yara let it pass through her like weather.

"Marek. Idris. Sefa. Anselm." The defectors, named individually, watched closely as she said their names, as if they weren't sure yet whether saying them made them hers or marked them as traitors twice over. She met each man's eyes. "You're on my manifest now. That means you eat at my fire and you get paid when I get paid, which historically means late, so adjust your expectations."

Marek laughed—a short, startled bark—and some knot in the camp's shoulders loosened.

She worked through the rest. River guides. The elder's scouts. The two cooks who had stayed through the storm when three others had walked out with Kessler's pay advance in their pockets. Every name answered. Every name closed.

When it was done, she folded the manifest and stood there a moment longer than she needed to, and the camp waited, because they had learned in the last week that when Yara Vasquez stood silent, what came next was usually the truest thing anyone would say all day.

"Everyone accounted for," she said. "Nobody left behind. Nobody—" Her voice caught, just once, on the edge of a word she hadn't let herself say yet. "Nobody drowned in my tunnel."

"It wasn't your tunnel," Bea said quietly, from the front row.

"It was my father's. Close enough."

* * *

The elder came to her tent at dusk, when the rain had thinned to a drifting mist and the campfires made small orange islands in the gray.

He came alone, which she understood was deliberate. He carried nothing except the walking stick he always carried, and he stood outside her tent flap until she invited him in, because he was a man who understood thresholds better than anyone she had ever met.

"The last canoe reached the third village an hour before dark," he said, settling onto her gear chest with the ease of a man who had been sitting on the ground for eighty years and considered furniture a novelty. "The masks are hung. The panels are in the spirit house at Wabaga, restored where they were cut from. Old Tama wept over the drum. He remembered its voice from when he was a boy."

Yara sat across from him on her cot, hands loose between her knees. "Do they want anything back? For what they did? Carrying loads up the ridge in a storm—"

"They want what they wanted before." He looked at her with eyes gone pale as river fog. "The same thing your father promised."

"I know."

"Do you?" He leaned forward, stick across his knees. "Because I watched you give me back my token on the flats, girl, and you gave it back like a debt returned. But a token isn't a debt. It's a question. *Will you remember us when it costs you?* And you answered it this week. In the flood. With the ledger pages. With the water." He tapped the ground once with the stick. "So now I ask the other question. The one after."

"What's that?"

"What will you write down?"

Yara blinked. "I don't—"

"The report." He said it plainly, without accusation, the way he said everything. "There is always a report. Your father wrote one. You signed one when you were young. The government will want one. Kessler's lawyers will want one. The insurers will want one. And whatever you write in it will outlive all of us, because paper drowns slower than men." He rose, joints cracking like green wood. "Think about whose hands you put your truth into. Some truths protect. Some truths only feed." At the tent flap he paused. "Your father understood that too, at the end. It's why the message on the door said *tell no one.* He wasn't hiding the crime, child. He was starving it."

Then he was gone into the mist, and Yara sat alone with the lantern and thought about paper drowning slower than men.

* * *

She went down to the gorge alone after full dark, against every safety protocol she had ever written, carrying a lantern and a bottle of rum that had survived three expeditions and was meant for a celebration that had never quite arrived.

The water had dropped another meter. The portal was a black wound in the rockface, choked with rubble, seeping. Somewhere behind that wall, forty meters down and two hundred meters in, lay the flooded gallery where her father had died—or rather, where her father had finished dying, because Yara understood now that he had started dying twelve years earlier, in a room in Port Moresby, when men from the families had explained to him what happens to surveyors who find ledgers.

She unscrewed the bottle and poured the first measure onto the rocks. The rum spread dark across the wet stone and the river took it.

"Papa." Her voice sounded strange in the gorge, small against the water noise. "I used to talk to you like you could hear me. Then I stopped, because it felt crazy, and then I talked to you like you'd wronged me, which was worse, because at least crazy is honest."

She drank. The rum burned all the way down, familiar as a scar.

"I signed the report," she said. "Seventeen years old, and they put the pen in my hand and told me it was the only way to keep the crew alive, and I believed them, and maybe it was even true, but I've spent twelve years acting like that signature was something done *to* me instead of something I did." She crouched, set the lantern on a flat stone, and looked at the black seam of the flooded portal. "I'm done with that. You built the flood option into the sluice gate. You wrote *flood it, tell no one.* You knew what you were doing, and you trusted the river to finish it, and you trusted—I think—you trusted that someday somebody who loved you would understand why."

The river said nothing. Rivers never do. But the mist moved through the gorge in long slow banners, and the lantern flame bent, and Yara chose, for once, to read something in it.

"I flooded it," she said. "On your terms. Well—on mine, which I'm choosing to believe are the same terms, because you never explained yourself once in your entire life and I don't expect you to start now." Her throat tightened. "The artifacts are home. The ledger is out of their hands forever. Kessler got plaster and river clay and a barge full of his own lies. And I finally—" The words snagged. She pushed through them the way she'd push through a blocked culvert, methodically, refusing to stop. "I finally cried about you at the right time. Not at the funeral, where I was too angry. Not at seventeen, where I was too scared. Now. Here. Where it counts."

She poured the second measure onto the stones. Then a third, straight into the black water at the portal's lip, where it vanished instantly, claimed.

"For the crew you never met," she said. "Nia, who blows things up more gently than anyone I've ever known. Okonkwo, who reads water like you read rock. Tomas, who took a beating for my stubbornness and never once asked why. And Bea." Her voice broke clean through. "Bea, who dove into your flood channel and found your spare tag on the sluice mechanism and came up holding it like it was a hand reaching out of the water. She has your stubbornness, Papa. God help us both."

She stayed until the lantern burned low. She didn't say goodbye, because she had learned this week that goodbyes were for things that ended, and this—this grief, this inheritance, this river—wasn't ending. It was changing course, the way rivers do, and she intended to follow it.

When she finally stood to go, she took the brass survey tag from her pocket—the one stamped *E.V.*, her father's initials, pulled from a crate in the upper gallery weeks ago when wonder was still possible. She'd carried it like contraband since the day she pocketed it, half ashamed, half desperate.

She pressed it into the mud at the highest point the flood had reached, upright, facing the water.

Not a grave marker. A benchmark. Surveyors' language for: *from this point, all measurements begin.*

* * *

She came back into camp near midnight, soaked past any pretense of dryness, and found Tomas sitting at the cold fire ring with a blanket around his shoulders and two mugs of something hot steaming on the stones beside him.

"One of those is yours," he said.

She sat. Took the mug. It was terrible instant coffee, over-sweetened the way Tomas made everything, and it was the best thing she had ever tasted.

"You were at the gorge," he said. Not a question.

"Yeah."

"Good." He stared into the dead fire. "Twelve years I've watched you carry that map. Red line. One word. I asked you once why you kept a map you never used, and you looked at me like I'd asked about a wound." He glanced sideways at her. "You should redraw it. When you file the report. Whatever you decide to say officially—that map is yours. Say what you want on it."

Yara held the mug in both hands and felt the heat soak into her palms, and thought about red ink, and about the difference between a line that seals and a line that returns.

"There's a copy of the ledger photographs going out with the scout tomorrow," she said. "First run downstream as soon as the river drops to safe levels. Journalists. Prosecutors. People whose whole job is making sure paper doesn't drown." She blew on the coffee. "Which means in a few weeks, everyone with a badge and a camera is going to come up this river asking questions. And the first question they'll ask me—the official question—is what happened at the Rivergate."

Tomas nodded slowly. "And what are you going to tell them?"

Yara looked at the dark gorge, at the place where the portal had been, at the benchmark tag sleeping in the mud under the rising mist.

"I'm going to tell them the truth," she said. "Just not all of it. There's a version of this story where a structurally compromised tunnel failed under monsoon loading and took its contents with it. No recoverable cargo. No survivors inside. No treasure, no ledger, no crime worth prosecuting beyond the ones already documented." She took a sip. "Every word of it technically accurate. My father taught me that trick. Read a survey close enough and the truth is whatever the lines allow."

Tomas was quiet for a long moment. "Is that justice?"

"That's survival," Yara said. "Justice is already on a bus to Port Moresby in a waterproof pouch. This—" she tipped her mug toward the gorge "—this is just the paperwork."

Tomas huffed something that was almost a laugh, then winced and held his ribs. "You're going to hate writing it."

"I know." Yara drained the coffee and stood, looking east where the sky was beginning, very faintly, to consider the possibility of dawn. "But I signed my first lie at seventeen to protect killers. I can sign this one at twenty-nine to protect everyone else."

She picked up the lantern. Behind her, the river ran on through the gorge, steady and indifferent and free, carrying nothing that belonged to anyone else.
