## 36. The Report

The forms came up on the barge's satellite terminal the morning after the water settled, riding in on a government server that hadn't been updated since before Yara was born. *Incident Report — Infrastructure Failure, Upper Fly River Basin, Form 7-C.* The same form. She'd have known it blindfolded, by the weight of it, by the way the fields were ordered—date of incident, nature of failure, estimated loss, signature of reporting party. Twelve years ago it had been paper, three copies in triplicate, and her hands had shaken so badly she'd had to press the pen down twice to make the ink take.

Now it glowed at her from a cracked screen in a cabin that smelled of diesel and river mud, and her hands were steady.

She almost wished they weren't.

Tomas stood in the doorway with two tin mugs, and she took one without looking away from the screen. Outside, the gorge was doing what gorges did after a flood: dripping, settling, rearranging itself in small rockfalls that sounded like slow applause. The Rivergate portal was invisible from here, buried behind a shoulder of collapsed hillside and forty feet of brown, churning water. When Okonkwo's gauges said the channel had stabilized, they'd be able to see it again—a concrete face with no tunnel behind it, drowned to its lintel.

*Structural failure,* the form wanted. *Nature of failure:* she typed it without letting herself think. *Progressive collapse of gallery roof strata under monsoon saturation loading, compounded by failure of original 12-year-old bulkhead seals.*

All true. That was the trick of it, the thing she'd only understood now, at thirty-one, that she couldn't have understood at seventeen: the best lies were built entirely out of true things, laid end to end like rail, and the lie was only in where you stopped laying them.

*Estimated loss of cargo:* She paused.

Nia appeared behind Tomas, arms crossed, a smear of plaster dust still in her hairline from the last of the replicas. "You're doing it now?"

"Before I can talk myself out of it."

"You won't." Nia came in and leaned against the bulkhead. "You've been talking yourself into it for three days. I've watched you draft it four times."

"Five," Yara said. "The first one told the truth."

"And?"

"And I deleted it." She looked up at them both. "Not because it was wrong. Because it wasn't mine to tell yet. The ledger pages are with the elder's people. If I write the truth here, it becomes an official record before the families' lawyers even wake up, and official records get sealed, redacted, bought. This way—" she tapped the screen, "—there's nothing for them to buy. There's just a dead tunnel and a bad monsoon."

"And the real report," Tomas said quietly, "is already traveling downstream in a scout's waterproof bag."

Yara nodded. That was the shape of it. Her father had understood it first, in his scratched message inside the lock door—*flood it, tell no one*—and she'd hated him for it at seventeen because she'd thought he was asking her to bury something. He hadn't been. He'd been asking her to choose who got the truth first. The river communities, not the ministries. The people who'd lost their carvings and their graves and their river, not the men in Kieta who signed shipping manifests with museum letterheads.

*Estimated loss of cargo:* she typed. *Unknown. Manifests incomplete; records destroyed in flooding. No recoverable cargo located as of this date.*

Three sentences. Each one technically defensible. Together they closed the Rivergate like a coffin lid, and somewhere upstream, plaster replicas sat in Kessler's barges rotting in the rain while the real panels traveled in dugout canoes toward villages that would burn sweet-smelling wood under them tonight.

Her chest did something complicated about that, and she let it.

* * *

Bea found her at midday on the portal overlook, where the rails of the old spur ran out of the jungle and simply stopped, sheared off at the edge of the new flood channel like a sentence cut short. Bea sat down beside her without asking, which was new, and handed over half a biscuit, which was newer.

"You signed it," Bea said.

"This morning. Transmitted 0940."

"How'd it feel?"

Yara chewed, watching the water. "Like signing my own name for the first time."

Bea snorted. "That's either very wise or very sad."

"Both. Probably both." Yara brushed crumbs off her knee. "You know what I keep coming back to? At seventeen, I signed that closure report because Dario's people put the pen in my hand and told me it was the only way to keep the crew out of prison. And I believed them. And then I spent twelve years making sure I never stopped moving, because if I ever stood still long enough to read what I'd signed—" she shook her head. "This time nobody handed me the pen. I picked it up myself. Same words, more or less. Completely different document."

"The lawyer's version of you would say the difference is intent."

"I don't have a lawyer's version of me."

"You do now." Bea nudged her. "You wrote a report that survives audit. Okonkwo checked your hydrology numbers twice. Nia seeded the blast debris so the collapse pattern reads exactly like saturation failure. You didn't just sign a lie, Yara. You built one. With your crew. On purpose. For people instead of against them." She paused. "That's not what he taught you. It's better."

Yara looked at her cousin—the diver who hadn't spoken to her at the funeral, who'd taken Kessler's side to force a conversation, who'd opened a sluice gate in rising floodwater with Yara's voice in her ear and their father's spare tag under her thumb. Their father. Not Yara's alone. She kept forgetting that, and Bea kept gently reminding her.

"He built the flood option in," Yara said. "Twelve years ago. Before any of this. He knew someday someone would come back for the lower gallery, and he left the mechanism there like a loaded gun on a high shelf."

"Do you think he knew it would be you?"

"No." Yara smiled despite herself. "I think he hoped it wouldn't be anyone. But he knew rivers, and he knew men like Kessler, and he knew that hope isn't a structural material." She stood, brushing off her trousers. "Come on. Okonkwo wants eyes on the channel gauge before dark, and if we're going to live in one place, we should probably learn what living in one place looks like."

Bea blinked. "One place?"

"I haven't asked anyone yet. Don't ruin it."

* * *

The gauge station was a hundred meters of slick limestone above the new channel, and Okonkwo was already there when they climbed up, hunched over his instruments with the particular contentment of a man whose disaster models had finally been proven right. He didn't turn around.

"Channel's dropped eleven centimeters since dawn. Stabilizing. In six weeks you'll be able to walk to the portal face." He adjusted a dial. "In six months, the jungle will have eaten the approach track. In two years, unless someone maintains it, the whole spur goes the way of the others." Now he turned, and his eyes were shrewd behind the rain-spotted glasses. "Which brings me to the question everyone's too polite to ask. What happens to us?"

"That's actually why I'm here." Yara crouched beside him, checked the gauge reading herself out of old habit. "There's a maintenance contract posted for the upper basin. Rail spur upkeep, channel monitoring, seasonal flood response. Small money. Boring work. One-year term, renewable."

Okonkwo went very still, the way hydrologists did when presented with data that contradicted every model they trusted. "You're proposing we stay."

"I'm proposing we stop running." She said it plainly, because it deserved plainness. "Twelve years I've kept this crew moving because standing still meant reading the report, and reading the report meant feeling it. Well. I've read it now. All of them. Both versions. And I'm tired, Okonkwo. We're all tired. And the river needs someone who knows it, and we're the only ones stupid enough and qualified enough."

He took off his glasses and cleaned them on his shirt, which was what he did instead of expressing emotion. "A season," he said. "You've never taken a contract longer than a season."

"A year. Maybe longer." She met his eyes. "Never take a contract twice. Never stay a season. Those were the rules. Rules are for keeping things out. I'd like to try keeping something in, for once."

Behind her, she heard Tomas laugh once, short and disbelieving, and turned to find him and Nia both standing on the rocks below—they'd followed her up without being asked, the whole crew assembling the way it always did when something mattered, drawn by some gravity none of them named.

"You're staying," Nia said flatly. "You. Yara Vasquez. The woman who pulled us out of the Kerema basin at 3 a.m. because she dreamed the river was going to shift."

"The dream was right."

"The dream is always right, that's not the point." Nia scrambled up the rocks, plaster dust still in her hair, grinning like a woman who'd just been handed explosives and permission. "A permanent shop. A dry store. A roof that doesn't roll."

"A home," Tomas said quietly, and the word landed among them like a dropped tool—loud, unexpected, rolling to a stop where everyone could see it.

Nobody picked it up. Nobody needed to. Yara looked around at them—her impossible, loyal, exhausted crew—and felt the strange vertigo of a future that didn't require motion.

"We'll vote on it properly," she said. "Tonight. Full camp meeting, like always." She hesitated. "But I needed you to hear it from me first. Before the paperwork."

"What paperwork?" Bea asked.

Yara reached into her jacket and drew out a folded sheet, rain-wrinkled already, printed that morning on the barge's dying printer. The maintenance contract application. Her name at the top. A fixed address in the basin. A term of twelve months.

At the bottom, a signature line.

She unfolded it against the limestone, took the pen from her breast pocket—the same pen, absurdly, the same cheap ballpoint she'd used to sign the incident report—and held it over the line.

"Last chance to object," she said.

The river roared softly below them, carrying the last of the flood seaward, carrying nothing else. Somewhere upstream, under forty feet of green water, a tunnel full of true things slept behind a wall her father had built and she had finished. The ledger was downstream. The artifacts were home. The lie was filed, clean and auditable, protecting exactly the people it was supposed to protect.

Yara Vasquez signed her name—slowly, fully, pressing down twice to make the ink take—and for the first time in twelve years, the hand that signed it was hers.

* * *

They voted yes, of course. Unanimous, with Nia demanding it be recorded formally in the camp log, and Tomas writing it in his careful surveyor's hand: *Crew resolves to accept stationary contract, Upper Fly River basin, term one year. Weather permitting everything else.*

It was near midnight when Yara made her last round of the camp, checking tie-downs against the tail winds, and found the satellite terminal blinking with a single incoming message. Encrypted relay, routed through three countries, sender unknown. She almost deleted it unread. Old habit said delete. New habit, twelve hours old, said read.

She read.

*Ms. Vasquez. My clients have reviewed your incident report and find it thorough. They note, however, that certain items listed as destroyed in the flooding appear, per independent appraisal, to be forgeries of recent manufacture. They would welcome clarification regarding the whereabouts of the originals. — D.K.*

She read it twice. Then she laughed—out loud, alone, at midnight, in a metal cabin on a barge in the gorge of the Upper Fly River—and the laugh felt like the first breath after surfacing.

Dario Kessler, holding a crate of river clay and plaster, paying appraisers to authenticate mud.

She typed a reply, brief as a rail spike:

*Dario. Independent appraisal is correct. The originals were never in the tunnel. Ask the river where it keeps what it keeps. — Y.V.*

Send. Delete from outbox. Power down the terminal.

Outside, the rain had stopped. The stars over the gorge were brutal and clear, and below them the river ran steady and deep and patient, holding its secrets the way it always had—not hidden, exactly. Just waiting for the right people to ask.

Tomorrow there would be gauges to check and spurs to maintain and a year of stillness to learn how to stand inside of. Tomorrow, maybe, she'd start drawing the new map.

But tonight, Yara Vasquez stood on the deck of her barge in the place she had finally stopped running from, and let the river answer for her.
