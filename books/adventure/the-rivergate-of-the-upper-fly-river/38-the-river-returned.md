## 38. The River Returned

The river had been waiting a long time, and it did not hurry now.

Yara stood on the shingle bank below the old weir, where the channel narrowed between walls of grey stone, and watched the water come back. It came the way water always comes—finding the path that had been cut for it and then, patiently, over hours, carving the path wider. Okonkwo's sluice calculations had held. The diversion channels Nia's crew had blasted open in the dry season's last week were doing their work. What had been a trickle through a choked culvert for twelve years was becoming, by midmorning, a proper current, brown-green and muscular, folding around the stones like something remembering its own shape.

The communities had arrived before dawn. They came by dugout and by barge and on foot along the ridge trails, from villages whose names Yara was only now learning to say correctly—Wabia, Dudi, Nomad, the smaller settlements up the tributaries—and they brought with them everything a ceremony needed: drums, food wrapped in banana leaf, children too numerous to count, elders in feathers and paint that caught the early light. Tomas counted four hundred people before he gave up counting. Bea didn't count at all. She stood hip-deep in the shallows with her dive mask pushed up on her forehead, testing the new channel with her hands, and when she looked back at Yara she was grinning like a girl.

"It holds," Bea called. "The bed's solid. He built it to hold."

Yara nodded. She could not have said anything else if she'd tried. Her throat had been tight since first light.

* * *

They carried the artifacts home in the same crates Kessler's shipping firm had packed them in—the museum-grade foam, the acid-free tissue, the numbered labels—and there was a bitter rightness in that which Yara did not try to explain to anyone. The packing that had been designed to carry these things away from the river was now carrying them back. Nia supervised the unloading with her arms crossed and her jaw set, daring anyone to handle a single panel carelessly. The carved boards came out into the daylight one at a time, and each time the crowd made a sound—not a cheer, not a gasp, but a low collective hum, like the river itself acknowledging what had been returned.

Yara watched an old woman from Wabia put both palms flat against one of the panels and close her eyes. She kept them closed for a long time. When she opened them she looked straight at Yara, and Yara looked away first.

She had spent twelve years telling herself the artifacts in the lower gallery were cargo. Inventory. Evidence. It had been easier that way—it had been the only way she could sign the report and keep walking. But watching them come out of the crates into the hands of the people they had been taken from, she understood that she had never once thought of them as *stolen*, not really, not all the way down. Stolen implied an owner. These things had never belonged to anyone. They belonged to a place, and the place had missed them.

"You're thinking loudly," Bea said, appearing at her shoulder with two cups of something hot and terrible that passed for tea in camp.

"I'm thinking about how long I've been wrong."

"Twelve years." Bea handed her a cup. "Give yourself another twelve to get it right. That's how family works."

* * *

The elder found her in the late morning, away from the drums, where the new channel ran fast and clean past the foot of the gorge. He moved slowly now—she noticed it more each time she saw him—but his eyes were as steady as ever, and he carried the carved token in his open hand.

He had given it to her on the barge, weeks ago, when the river communities had gone quiet at Kessler's name and Yara had understood, without being told, that they knew things about the Rivergate that no survey map recorded. She had carried it every day since. Through the pumping of the dark, through the opening of the galleries, through the flood. There were nights it had been the only thing in her pocket that made sense.

"My father," she said, because she had to start somewhere, "never told me about this place. About any of it."

"No." The elder turned the token over in his fingers. It was carved from river stone, worn smooth on one side, sharp on the other—a shape that might have been a fish or a knife or a door, depending on how you held it. "He told us. That was different."

"What did he tell you?"

"That the railway took more than it gave. That the men who built it wrote down what they took, because men who steal always write it down—they cannot help themselves. And that one day the writing would be worth more than the taking." The elder looked up at her. "He said a daughter would come. Not soon. But someday. And that she would bring the ledger home or bring the river home, and either would be enough, but she would try for both."

Yara laughed, and it came out broken. "He overestimated me."

"He underestimated you," the elder said. "That is why he sealed the tunnel instead of telling you. He wanted you to have a life where you got to choose. He knew that if he told you everything, you would never get to choose anything—you would only finish what he started." He pressed the token back into her hand and closed her fingers over it. "You chose. Both times. That is the whole of it, Yara Vasquez. The rest is water under the bridge."

"The ledger copies are already with the journalists," she said. "And the prosecutors. And—" she hesitated—"the school teachers, in Wabia and Dudi. Okonkwo printed extra sets. We figured the lawyers might take years."

The elder smiled for the first time. "Lawyers take years. Grandchildren do not." 

* * *

The ceremony itself began when the sun stood straight overhead, and Yara understood quickly that it was not for her, and was glad of it.

There were speeches in languages she didn't speak, and songs whose rhythms she felt in her sternum before she understood them with her ears. The drums went for a long time. Children who had never seen the artifacts except in photographs pressed forward to touch the carved panels, and their grandparents let them, and corrected their grip, and told stories that made the panels not objects but relatives—ancestors, in wood and shell and stone, coming home after a long and involuntary journey. A man from Nomad wept openly over a fishing charm no bigger than Yara's hand, and no one thought it strange.

Bea stood beside Yara through most of it, and at some point their shoulders came together and stayed there.

"When I dove the sluice," Bea said quietly, during a lull, "and I found his spare tag on the gate mechanism—he built the flood option in, Yara. He planned for someone to open it again. Do you think he planned for this? For them?" She nodded at the crowd, the drums, the panels going home.

"He planned for the river," Yara said. "I think the rest was always the river's business."

Tomas found them then, moving through the crowd with the particular walk he used when he was carrying news he wasn't sure he wanted to deliver. He stopped in front of Yara and held out a radio handset.

"Upstream relay," he said. "It's for you. They wouldn't say who."

Yara took it. The voice that came through was thin and distorted by distance and static, but she knew it anyway. She had known it across boardrooms and helicopter pads and the flooded dark of a tunnel. It sounded older than it had three weeks ago. Defeat will do that.

*"Your father would be proud,"* Dario Kessler said.

She looked at the handset for a long moment. Around her the drums went on, and the river ran clean over stones it hadn't touched in twelve years, and the children of four villages were learning the names of things that had nearly been lost forever.

"No," Yara said, and set the handset down on the shingle, face-up in the sun, and walked back toward the music without looking at it again.

Tomas picked it up, listened to the static for a moment, and switched it off. "Was that wise?"

"He'll spend the rest of his life trying to figure out what I meant by it," Yara said. "Let him. I've got a ceremony to get to."

* * *

Near dusk, when the drums had softened and the food had been shared and the artifacts were distributed among the villages according to arrangements the elders had negotiated among themselves with a speed and certainty that made Yara realize how little she still understood about how these communities worked—how much better they worked, maybe, than anything Kessler had ever built—the elder came to find her one last time.

He led her to the new channel, where the water ran full and fast in the gold light, and pointed downstream.

"Your father promised the river would be returned," he said. "Not the artifacts. Anyone can return an artifact; museums do it when they are embarrassed enough. He promised the *river*. The channel. The flow. The thing the railway choked and the tunnel drowned." He looked at her. "Today it is returned. So today the promise is kept. You understand what that means?"

"That the debt is paid?"

"That the debt is *ended*." The elder shook his head. "A debt paid is finished. A promise kept is a beginning. Your father's promise is kept, Yara Vasquez. Whatever your family owes this river, it owed before you were born. From tomorrow, whatever you do here, you do as a free woman."

Yara stood at the edge of the channel and watched the water move, and felt something in her chest unlock that she had not known was locked. Twelve years of motion—rerailing spurs, outrunning debts, never staying a season, never taking a contract twice—all of it built on the fear that if she ever stopped, the past would catch her. And the past had caught her, here, at the end of the world, and it had turned out not to be a debt collector at all. It had turned out to be a promise, waiting patiently for her to keep it.

"Free," she repeated.

"Free," the elder agreed. "Which is its own kind of frightening."

She laughed—a real laugh this time, surprised out of her—and the elder smiled and left her alone with the river.

Downstream, the first evening stars were coming out over the water. Somewhere behind her, Bea was arguing happily with Nia about load ratings, and Tomas was teaching children to read a survey map, and Okonkwo was explaining the monsoon cycle to anyone who would listen. Her crew. Her strange, stubborn, loyal crew, who had followed her into a flooded tunnel and out the other side, and who had, three days ago, done something none of them had ever done before.

They had signed a contract for a permanent maintenance job. One season. Maybe two. Here, in the basin, near the rivergate, on the living lines.

Yara took her father's map from inside her jacket—the original, soft with handling, the red line through the Rivergate still marked *sealed* in his hand—and smoothed it against her knee. Tomorrow, or the day after, she would change it. Not tonight. Tonight the map could rest, and so could she.

The river ran on, returned, in the dark.
