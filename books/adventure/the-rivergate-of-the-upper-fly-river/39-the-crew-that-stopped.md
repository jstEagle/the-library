## 39. The Crew That Stopped

The contract arrived on paper, which was the first strange thing about it.

In twelve years of salvage work along the Upper Fly, Yara Vasquez had signed her name to manifests, damage waivers, hazard acknowledgments, and one closure report she would spend the rest of her life renegotiating with herself—but the work itself had always come by rumor, radio crackle, or a man in a pressed shirt standing at the edge of camp with a folder and a calculator. This time it was a single sheet, hand-delivered by a boy in a dugout canoe who paddled away before she'd finished reading it, and it said, in the careful block letters of someone who had learned to write in a mission school:

*The villages of the upper basin offer the crew of Yara Vasquez work maintaining the rail line from Kiunga junction to the rivergate basin. One year. Wages paid monthly, in kind or in kina, as the crew prefers. The line is yours to keep.*

No signature. No company letterhead. Just the word *yours*, sitting there on the page like something that might bite.

She read it three times at the galley table while the morning fog burned off the water and Bea pretended not to watch her from the doorway of the barge's wheelhouse.

"One year," Yara said finally.

"I heard."

"That's four seasons."

"Math was always your strong suit." Bea came down the steps and sat across from her, forearms on the table, the scar from the sluice dive still pink along her forearm where the gate housing had opened her up. "Say the rest of it."

Yara set the page down. "We've never stayed anywhere longer than it took to fix the rails and get paid. That's not an accident. That's the design."

"I know whose design." Bea's voice was even, but her eyes weren't. "I was at the funeral too, remember. I watched you leave before they finished lowering the box because there was a job waiting in Tabubil and you told me motion was easier than mourning." She turned the contract sheet around so it faced her cousin. "So tell me what's easier about this."

And that was the question, wasn't it. Yara looked out the wheelhouse window at the basin—the gorge walls going green-gold in the early light, the dead spur running along the riverbank toward the concrete face of the Rivergate, half-hidden now under scaffolding the communities had built themselves, a timber cradle around the old portal like a splint on a broken arm. Three months since the flood. Three months since Kessler's barge went upstream heavy with plaster and river clay, since the lower gallery closed for good under forty feet of cold water, since Tomas pulled her coughing onto the pump platform while the tunnel exhaled its last breath of air through the breach.

Three months of not moving. And she was still here. They all were.

Nobody had left. That was the thing she kept turning over at night like a stone in her hand. Nia had a standing offer from a demolition outfit in Lae paying triple. Okonkwo had two universities asking after his flood models. Tomas had a sister in Port Moresby he hadn't seen in six years. Bea could work anywhere with water deep enough to drown in. And every one of them, when the flood settled and the pay ran out and the sensible thing was to scatter—every one of them had started, without discussion, without a vote, doing small repairs on the upper spur. Replacing ties. Clearing brush off the ballast. Straightening rail joints with equipment that technically belonged to no one anymore.

They had stopped moving, and instead of falling apart, they had started building.

"The elder says the line used to carry food up-valley," Yara said. "Before the companies pulled out. Garden produce, fish, fuel. Twelve years of everything going by road, when the road washes out every wet season and the rail never did—not until we let it."

"You're quoting the elder at me now."

"He quoted my father at me first. Fair's fair." She slid the contract back across the table. "It's not a salvage job, Bea. There's nothing to strip, nothing to run from, no debt at the end of it. It's just... keeping something alive."

Bea read the page again, slowly, the way she read current in a sump—looking for the pull underneath. Then she took the pencil stub from behind her ear, wrote *BEA VASQUEZ — DIVER* on the line at the bottom, and pushed it back.

"One condition," she said. "We survey the living lines. All of them. Every stretch of track in this basin that still runs or could run again. Mapped properly, graded, logged. Not your father's way—" she caught herself, and Yara watched her decide not to soften it, "—not hidden in a journal nobody reads until it's leverage. Ours. Public. Where anyone who wants to run a train can find it."

Yara thought about a hand-drawn survey in red ink, marked *sealed*, kept in a waterproof tube for twelve years like a wound she couldn't stop pressing. She thought about the difference between a map made to hide something and a map made to be found.

"Yeah," she said. "That's the whole point."

* * *

The signing, such as it was, happened at the rivergate itself, because the elder insisted, and because some things deserved better scenery than a galley table.

The communities came down from the valley in force—forty people, maybe fifty, poling dugouts and riding the flat barge the villages had bought jointly with the first season's earnings from the mushroom and fish trade that the reopened channel had made possible. The elder walked the length of the dead spur without stopping, his bare feet finding the ties as if the twelve years of rot between them weren't there, and when he reached the portal he put his palm flat against the concrete face and left it there a long moment, the way you'd check a fever, or take a pulse.

Then he turned to the crew, lined up awkwardly on the ballast—Nia with her arms crossed, Okonkwo with his ever-present notebook already open, Tomas standing a half-step behind Yara the way he always did, Bea dripping lake water from her morning swim—and he held out the contract page and a pen.

"Not a signature," he said. "A promise. Say it out loud, each of you, so the river hears it too. The river forgets nothing. That used to frighten me." His eyes found Yara's. "Now I count on it."

Nia went first, because Nia always went first when there was any chance of ceremony turning sentimental. "I promise to keep the line true," she said, flat and fast, like reading a charge sheet. "Gauge within a centimeter. Ballast drained. No charge goes off on this line except to save it." A beat. "And I promise to stay the year. All of it. Even the boring parts." She signed.

Okonkwo promised the drainage, the culverts, the cuttings—"water will be measured here, not feared"—and signed with the careful script of a man whose notebooks were the closest thing he had to children.

Tomas surprised everyone by speaking longest. He talked about his sister, about six years of postcards he'd never answered because answering meant staying still long enough to mean it. "This is the first place I've been where staying doesn't feel like sinking," he said, and then seemed embarrassed, and signed quickly.

Bea promised the survey, the living lines, and—"so the river hears it plainly"—that she would never again let silence do her talking for her. She looked at Yara when she said it. Yara nodded.

Last, the elder held the page out to her.

Yara looked at the portal behind him. Under the timber cradle, the concrete face still carried the blast scars—her father's patterns, his mathematics, his terrible final arithmetic—and below the waterline, forty feet down, the lower gallery held its evidence room and its ledger and its dead men's secrets, sealed now not by a lie but by a choice. Her father had built the flood option into the gate mechanism because some part of him, even then, had understood that the river was the only vault that couldn't be bought. She had finished his sentence. Whatever else the rest of her life was, it would have that in it.

She took the pen.

"I promise to stay," she said, and heard how strange the words were in her own mouth, like a language she'd studied but never spoken. "One year. The line, the crew, the basin. I promise to file honest reports and dishonest ones never again. And I promise—" her voice caught, and she let it, because Bea was watching and Bea had earned the sight of it, "—I promise to finish my father's map. The right way. Marked for the living."

The elder folded the page once, precisely, and did not put it away. "This stays at the portal," he said. "Where the river can read it."

* * *

Work, it turned out, had a different texture when it wasn't escape.

Yara noticed it first in the mornings. For twelve years the crew's dawn routine had been a military exercise—strike camp, load gear, roll—because dawn was when the bailiffs came, or the creditors, or the memory of the last job gone wrong. Now dawn was coffee on the pump platform, and Nia arguing with Okonkwo about whether culvert three needed reaming or replacement, and Tomas teaching two village teenagers how to read a rail gauge, laughing at their mistakes in a way Yara had never once heard from him on a contract job. The same hands. The same competence. But the fear underneath it was gone, and without the fear, the work was just... work. Good work. Work that would still be there tomorrow, which meant it didn't have to be perfect today, which meant—for the first time she could remember—she slept through the night.

The living lines survey took them up-valley through September and October. Forty-one kilometers of track, most of it sound beneath the jungle overgrowth; the railway had been built by people who expected it to last forever, and only the abandonment had broken it. They mapped bridges and classified them. They found a locomotive, a shunting engine, sitting intact in a cutting where its last driver had parked it and walked away, and Bea stood next to it with her hand on the cold steel and said, "He probably meant to come back," and neither of them said anything else for a while.

At Kiunga junction they found the stationmaster's old logbook, and in it, entries from the years when the trains ran heavy and wrong—freight cars listed as empty that the weight records said were anything but. Yara photographed those pages too, and mailed the copies to the journalist in Port Moresby who had turned the ledger photographs into a story that was, as of that week, being read aloud in a courtroom. Kessler's empire was coming apart in installments. She felt about it the way she felt about the monsoon: vast, distant, and no longer hers to manage.

On the last day of the survey, she and Bea sat on the roof of the shunting engine's cab with their boots hanging over the windscreen, eating tinned peaches and watching the sun go down the long way behind the gorge.

"Kessler sent another message," Bea said. "Tomas showed me. Same as the last one."

"I know. I didn't answer that one either."

"You're going to keep not answering?"

"For the rest of my life, probably." Yara tipped the peach syrup into her mouth. "There's nothing he has that I want, and nothing I have that he can take. That's the whole conversation."

Bea was quiet a moment. Down the line, the evening train—the second one ever, carrying taro and fish and a generator for the school at the mid-valley settlement—whistled at the crossing, a long, bright note that rolled up the valley and came back off the gorge walls changed but undiminished.

"When we were kids," Bea said, "you told me you'd never live anywhere the ground didn't shake sometimes. You said stillness was how they got you."

"They got me anyway. Twice." Yara looked at her cousin—at the scar, at the diver's calm that had cost more than anyone knew. "Turns out the trick isn't staying still or staying moving. It's picking which."

"And you've picked?"

Yara looked down the line, at the rails running silver into the darkening valley, at the smoke of the train fading over the trees, at the portal far behind them holding its water and its secrets and its peace.

"I'm looking at it," she said.

* * *

That night, alone in the wheelhouse with the lamp turned low, Yara unrolled her father's map on the chart table for what she had decided would be the last time in its original form.

Twelve years she had kept it sealed in its tube, and twelve years before that it had lived in his survey case, and in all that time the red line through the Rivergate had carried its single word—*sealed*—in his handwriting, in ink that had outlasted him. She had planned, in the quiet weeks after the flood, to redraw it. To mark the line *returned*, the way she'd promised the river and the elder and herself. But every time she'd sat down to do it, her hand had stopped above the paper, because changing his word felt like erasing him, and she wasn't ready to erase him yet. Maybe she never would be.

Tonight she understood that she didn't have to.

She uncapped her own pen—a cheap thing, bought at the Kiunga market, nothing like his drafting pens—and beside his red line, in the margin, in her own hand, she wrote a second word. Not over his. Next to it. Two words on one line, father and daughter, twelve years apart, saying different things about the same ground.

His said *sealed*.

Hers said *returned*.

Below it, she added a date, and below the date, a note in the margin, small enough that only someone who knew both their hands would ever find it: *Survey continues. Living lines only. — Y.V., for E.V.*

She sat back and looked at it for a long time. The map of closed lines was becoming something else, and so, she supposed, was she.

Outside, the basin lay quiet under a high moon. The river moved in the dark, steady as a pulse. Somewhere up-valley, the maintenance shed's new lock clicked as Tomas made his last round, and somewhere downstream, at the portal, the contract page with five signatures waited under its oilcloth for the river to read.

Yara rolled the map carefully, slid it into the tube, and set it upright in the corner of the wheelhouse where she could see it from the helm—not hidden, not carried, just kept.

Tomorrow they would start grading the junction spur for the dry-season schedule. Tomorrow there would be culverts and gauges and Nia complaining about the boring parts, and it would all still be there the day after, and the day after that, all the way through the year and past it, if they chose. Stillness, she thought, wasn't the absence of motion after all. It was motion with nowhere to flee to. It was a train on a line that someone had promised to keep.

She turned down the lamp, and in the dark she could hear the river, and for the first time in her life it sounded less like something chasing her than like something waiting—patient, unhurried, holding what it holds—ready, when she was, to show her what came next.
