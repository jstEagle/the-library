## 1. The Weight of Brass

The compass did not point north.

I had known this for eleven years, and still, standing on the platform at Port Harcourt depot with my one trunk and my father's ledger strapped under my arm like a Bible I didn't believe in, I opened the case and checked. Old habit. Old superstition. The needle swung, wobbled, and settled a full three degrees west of where any honest instrument would have it — and there it stayed, faithful to some private north that existed nowhere on any chart ever printed by the Colonial Survey Office.

My father had called it his lucky deviation. The men who struck him from the corps' rolls had called it evidence.

The morning was already hot enough to make the rails shimmer, and the depot smelled of creosote, diesel, and the particular sweetness of overripe plantains rotting somewhere behind the freight shed. A goods train stood coughing at the far platform, being unloaded by men whose shirts had gone dark with sweat down their spines. Somewhere a whistle shrieked, long and petulant, and nobody moved any faster because of it. That was the first thing you learned about the coast: urgency here was a costume people wore for visitors.

I wore my own costume. Second-hand khaki, pressed that morning with an iron heated over coals until the collar could have stood up on its own. Boots resoled twice. And pinned inside my left breast pocket, where no one could see it but where I could feel its weight against my ribs all the same, the letter that had brought me here.

*Miss Okonkwo — the Department is prepared to consider your application for field reassignment, conditional upon satisfactory conduct, in respect of outstanding obligations associated with the estate of the late E. Okonkwo, formerly Senior Surveyor.*

*Outstanding obligations.* Four words doing the work of forty. My father had been dead six years, and the Department was still collecting on him — not money, though God knew there was precious little of that left to collect, but something less fungible. Labor. Silence. The use of his daughter's hands until such time as the ledger balanced or she broke, whichever came first.

I snapped the compass case shut and put it back in my pocket.

"Passage for Okonkwo." The clerk at the freight office didn't look up when I said it. He was a small man with ink-stained cuffs and the serene expression of someone who had never once been responsible for anything heavier than a stamp. "Third-class carriage is through the yard. You'll want to be aboard before the ten-forty."

"I'm not taking the ten-forty."

That got his eyes up. They traveled from my boots to my face with the unhurried insolence of a man pricing a goat. "The upriver transfer leaves from the goods siding," he said. "Survey party manifest. You're listed as technical staff."

Technical staff. In the corps I had once held a provisional commission and a drafting table by the window. Now I was technical staff, which was a word meaning *portable*, and portable things rode in goods wagons.

"Then I'll wait at the siding," I said, and turned before he could decide whether to be offended. It is a skill, walking away from a man mid-contempt. My father taught me without meaning to. He did it his whole life, politely, to superiors who spoke about him in the third person while he stood in the room.

***

The goods siding was a different country from the passenger platforms — louder, rougher, run on hand-signals and shouting and the ancient diplomacy of men moving heavy things past other men's feet. Crates were stacked in drifts along the ballast, stenciled with the names of places I'd only seen on sealed maps: *KALABA CUTTING. UPPER WORKS. STATION 47 — DO NOT OPEN.*

Do not open. That was the phrase stamped on every crate bound for the highland line, and I confess it worked on me the way a locked door works on a cat. Twenty years ago the line had been driven up toward the highlands — rails, cuttings, a tunnel through the shoulder of the escarpment — and then, abruptly, it hadn't been. No scandal I could find in the back files. No disaster reported in the gazettes. The project had simply closed, the way a mouth closes around a secret, and the maps had been withdrawn into the survey office vaults, and the men who had worked it had scattered or died or stopped speaking about it.

And now, suddenly, the Department wanted it reopened. Wanted it reopened badly enough to reach down into the drawer where they kept me.

I found shade under the water tower and sat on my trunk to wait, and that was when I took out the ledger. Not because I needed to. Because waiting does something to me — it makes my hands want employment, and if my hands can't have honest work they will find dishonest work, which in my case means worrying at old wounds.

The ledger was my father's own, a fat foolscap volume bound in green cloth gone grey at the corners. Half of it was genuine accounts: sums owed to the pension fund, to the corps benevolent society, to two merchants in the capital whose patience had outlived him. Debts of the ordinary, shabby kind. But the last third of the book was different. Figures in his cramped left-slanting hand — elevations, gradients, chainages — running up a route I did not know, dated across months when, according to the official record, he had already left the highlands and taken up his desk posting in the capital.

Figures dated after the closure.

I had read those pages perhaps a hundred times since his death, and each time they gave me the same sensation: of holding a letter addressed to someone else, in a language almost mine. He had never explained them. In his last year, when the illness had thinned him to a voice and a pair of hands too steady for the rest of him, I had asked him outright — *Papa, what did you do up there?* — and he had looked at me with an expression I have spent six years trying to decode, and said only:

"The river keeps what the river keeps, Ada. Mind you don't go asking it to give things up on your account."

Then he had pressed the compass into my palm, closed my fingers over it, and said nothing more about anything, ever again.

A shadow fell across the ledger. I looked up into the face of a European man in a linen suit that had been white within living memory, holding a clipboard as though it were a scepter.

"Okonkwo?" He pronounced it *Oh-KON-kwo*, carefully, the way careful men pronounce things they intend to get wrong only once. "Fenwick. Transport office. You're the — ah — the reinstated one."

Reinstated. As though I were a bridge repaired after flood damage. "I'm the engineer," I said. "For the reopening survey."

His eyebrows performed a small diplomatic incident between them. "Well. The launch is at the end of the week, so you've days yet, but the Department likes its people close at hand. There's a billet at the annex." He consulted the clipboard. "You'll report tomorrow morning to Mr. Vance — Elias Vance, chief overseeing engineer — for briefing. Eight o'clock sharp. He doesn't care for lateness."

"And does Mr. Vance care for engineers with debts?" I asked, before the part of me that survives on the coast could stop it.

Fenwick blinked. Then, to his credit, he almost smiled. "Mr. Vance cares for results, Miss Okonkwo. It's why you're both here." He tucked the clipboard under his arm. "Mind the ten-forty backs through the yard at speed. Keep clear of the four-foot."

He walked off, and I sat in the shade of the water tower with the ledger open on my knees, and I thought about *results*. Results were what the Department wanted from the highlands. Results were what my father had failed — or refused — to give them, twenty years ago, at whatever cost had bought him a desk, a debt, and a grave with a modest stone. And results, if I brought them home clean and complete, were the only currency in which my name could be bought back.

I am not a romantic about my profession. Rails are rails; gradients are gradients; a cut is either stable or it isn't, and sentiment has never kept a cutting from slipping. But sitting there in the coal-smelling heat, with my father's false-northed compass in my pocket and his impossible figures under my thumb, I felt something that was not quite ambition and not quite dread. It was the feeling of a train beginning, very slowly, to move — that first lurch when the couplings take up the slack, car by car, and you understand that the thing is going now, and you are aboard it, and the platform is already sliding away behind you whether you consented or not.

The ten-forty came through the yard at speed, exactly as promised, dragging its heat and noise behind it. I kept clear of the four-foot. I watched it go, and beyond it, far off past the cranes and the godowns, I could see the river — a brown gleam between the palms, broad and slow and apparently harmless, running down out of a country I knew nothing about toward a sea I was tired of.

Somewhere up that water lay Station 47, and the tunnel works, and whatever it was my father had written down in the dark half of his ledger. Somewhere up that water was the answer to a question I hadn't yet managed to ask properly.

I closed the book, tied the strap, and went to find the annex.

Eight o'clock sharp, then. Mr. Vance, who cared for results. And a river that kept what it kept.

I intended, before the dry season ended, to make it give something back.
