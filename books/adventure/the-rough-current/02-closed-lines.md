## 2. Closed Lines

The Survey Office of the Colonial Rail Administration occupied the top floor of a building that had been designed by an architect who clearly hated windows. It sat above the harbor like a man refusing to look at the sea—blind walls facing the water, narrow slits of glass facing inland, as if the whole enterprise of empire were best conducted with one's back to the evidence.

I was shown in at nine o'clock sharp by a clerk named Mr. Pemberton, who had the complexion and general disposition of a mushroom: pale, damp, and inclined to appear suddenly in dim corners. He carried my father's file under his arm the way a gravedigger carries a shovel—professionally, without affection.

"Chief Engineer Okonkwo's papers," he said, setting them on a table that had been cleared for the purpose. "You'll note they are stamped *Closed*. That is not a suggestion."

"I know how to read a stamp, Mr. Pemberton."

"Many people don't," he said, with the mild certainty of a man who had met many people and found them all wanting. "The Director will receive you at eleven. You may study the materials until then. You may take no notes. You may copy nothing. You may—" he paused, savoring it, "—ask no questions I am not authorized to answer."

"That last category being?"

"All of them."

I liked him immediately, in the way one likes a crocodile: with respect, and from a distance.

* * *

They left me alone with the file, which is either trust or a trap, depending on how much you believe in the honesty of locked rooms. The table stood beneath the one generous window—a slit facing east—and morning light came through it in a hard yellow bar, the way light comes into a cell when the jailer is feeling generous.

My father's file was thin. That was the first wrong thing. A man who had spent nineteen years surveying rail lines across three provinces should have generated a small forest of paper: field books, level runs, gradient calculations, correspondence, requisitions, complaints filed against him and by him. What lay before me was perhaps forty pages, and half of those were administrative—the record of his debts, itemized in a hand so neat it looked printed. The company had been meticulous about what he owed them. Of what he had given them, there was almost nothing.

Almost.

The maps came next, unrolled from a canvas tube and weighted at the corners with brass objects whose purpose I chose not to investigate too closely. The highland line. The one they'd sent me upriver to reopen. I had seen the published version of this route a hundred times—it hung in every engineering classroom on the coast, a textbook example of ambitious alignment abandoned: sixty miles of surveyed grade climbing from the navigable head of the river to the plateau, tunnel works begun at the gorge, and then—nothing. A line drawn confidently for forty miles and then simply stopped, like a sentence cut off mid-word.

But this was not the published map. This was the original, and original maps have fingerprints all over them.

I bent close. The ink had gone brown with age, but the drafting was unmistakable—I would have known it anywhere, the way you know a voice through a wall. My father drew contours the way other men wrote prayers: each line laid down with a patience that looked, to the impatient, like obsession. He believed a map should tell the truth even when the truth was inconvenient, which was, I would come to understand, precisely the belief that ruined him.

And there, in the lower right corner of the first sheet, where the draftsman signs his work: **A.O.** Two letters. Small, confident, unhidden.

Adeyemi Okonkwo. My father had drawn this line. Not checked it, not approved it—drawn it, in his own hand, on the original survey from which everything else derived.

I sat back. The brass weight gleamed at the corner of the map like a small smug eye.

Here is what I knew, or thought I knew, growing up: my father built railways. He came home smelling of ink and river mud, and he told me stories about gradients the way other fathers told stories about lions. When I was ten he taught me to read a contour interval before he taught me to swim, and when I asked why, he said, "Because water lies, but numbers only lie if a man makes them." I thought it was a joke. Fathers say things like that, and daughters file them away under *wisdom* without examining the contents.

When the corps expelled me—*indefinitely suspended*, the phrase they used, as though my competence were a fever that might break—they did it quietly, over a bridge inspection I had failed to certify and could not explain why I hadn't. Everyone assumed incompetence. I assumed nerves. Neither of us, I now suspect, was looking at the right document.

Because here was the second wrong thing, and I found it twenty minutes later, when I began comparing the original against the published route sheet that Pemberton had left out, whether by accident or design I still wonder.

The grades didn't match.

Not dramatically. Nothing a casual eye would catch. But I had spent four years doing nothing but checking other men's figures for errors, and my eye had become a kind of instrument itself. Where the published survey showed a ruling gradient of one in fifty along the gorge section—steep but manageable, the sort of number that gets a line approved—my father's original showed one in thirty-eight. And where the published figures claimed a summit elevation of 2,140 feet, his original said 2,310. One hundred and seventy feet of difference. On a mountain railway, one hundred and seventy feet is not an error. It is a different mountain.

Someone had changed the numbers. And the direction of the change was the part that made my neck prickle: the published figures made the line look *easier* than my father had found it. Flatter. Cheaper. More buildable. Whoever altered the data had done so to make the project attractive—to get it approved, funded, begun.

Which meant the line had been sold to the directors on figures my father never gave them.

Or—

I turned the original over. On the reverse, in pencil, faint as breath on glass, someone had written a single word in my father's handwriting: *Ask N.*

I read it four times. Ask N. Ask who? Ask what? There was no name, no date, no context. Just those two words and the ghost of pressure where a pencil had pressed hard enough to nearly tear the paper, as though the man writing them had been angry, or afraid, or both.

"Mr. Pemberton," I said, when the mushroom reappeared to check on me—he did so every eleven minutes, I timed him, "the published figures for the highland line differ from the original survey. Can you tell me why?"

"No," he said.

"Can you tell me who authorized the revision?"

"No."

"Can you tell me anything about why the line closed?"

He considered this. I watched him weigh something behind his eyes—not knowledge, exactly, but the question of how much ignorance it was safe to display.

"The line closed," he said finally, "because the Administration closed it. Twenty-two years ago. There was a review. The review found the continuation uneconomical. The matter is historical."

"A review conducted by whom?"

"The matter," said Mr. Pemberton, with the finality of a man closing a ledger, "is historical."

* * *

At eleven o'clock I was ushered into the Director's office, which was large, cool, and arranged so that visitors sat lower than the desk, a feat of architecture achieved through the simple expedient of raising the floor on the far side. The Director himself was a heavy, sunburned man named Calloway, with the handshake of someone who has crushed many hands and enjoyed it.

"Miss Okonkwo." He pronounced it carefully, having practiced. "Your father's daughter. Remarkable. Sit."

I sat, in the chair engineered for sitting low.

"You've reviewed the file."

"Some of it. The parts I was permitted to see."

Something flickered across his face—amusement, possibly, or its bureaucratic cousin, irritation. "You have your father's tongue, I hear. Good. You'll need it less than you think. The upriver assignment is straightforward. The old highland alignment is to be re-surveyed, condition-assessed, and reported upon. Tunnels, bridges, cuttings. Six weeks' work, weather permitting. You'll have full authority in the field." He smiled. "Within reason."

"And the purpose, sir? If the line is to be reopened, the corps would want a construction estimate. If it's to be abandoned permanently, they'd want nothing at all. Which is it?"

"It is neither your concern nor mine." He said it pleasantly, the way a man says *pass the salt*. "It is the concern of the Board, and the Board communicates through me, and I communicate through your expedition orders, which you received yesterday and signed for. Have you read them?"

"I've read them. They're remarkably vague."

"They're remarkably complete, Miss Okonkwo. Vagueness is a form of completeness. You'll learn that." He leaned back, and the leather creaked like rigging. "Let me be plain with you, because I respect plainness and because your position requires it. You are here because you are competent and because you are expendable, and the second quality is rarer than the first, so do try to preserve it. Your debts to this Administration are substantial. Complete the survey, deliver a clean report, and the Board may look kindly on reinstatement. Complicate the survey—with questions, with delays, with discoveries of a nature not requested—and the debt remains, and grows interest." He folded his hands. "Do we understand each other?"

Perfectly. It was the most honest conversation I'd had since arriving on the coast.

"One question," I said. "One, and then I'll go pack my instruments. The original survey of the highland line carries my father's initials. He drew the route. Yet the file contains none of his field books, none of his correspondence, and the published figures contradict his own elevations. For a historical matter, someone went to a great deal of trouble to make history incomplete."

Calloway regarded me for a long moment. Outside, a gull screamed at the harbor it couldn't see through the windowless wall.

"Your father," he said at last, "was the best surveyor this Administration ever employed. That is not a compliment; it is a fact on file. And the best surveyors are occasionally the most difficult men to close accounts with." He stood, ending the audience the way a stagehand drops a curtain. "Your boat leaves Thursday. First light. Don't be late for your own redemption, Miss Okonkwo. Rivers wait for no one, and neither, unfortunately, does arithmetic."

* * *

I walked back along the harbor road with the wind off the water pushing at me like a hand between the shoulder blades, hurrying me toward something. The town smelled of tar, fish, and diesel, and the dhows knocked together at their moorings with a sound like loose teeth.

Here is what I had, laid out in my head as neatly as instruments on a canvas roll:

A line closed twenty-two years ago for reasons no one would name. Original figures that contradicted the published ones—contradicted them in the direction of difficulty, meaning my father found the mountains harder than the company admitted, meaning either he was wrong or the company lied. Field books missing from the file of the most meticulous surveyor the Administration ever employed. A penciled instruction in his hand: *Ask N.* And a Director who threatened me with my own debts within ninety seconds of offering me salvation.

And underneath all of it, humming like a live wire, the thing I couldn't yet say aloud: my father had walked this route before me. His initials were on the line I'd been sent to reopen. Whatever had happened upriver—whatever had closed the tunnels and emptied the file and silenced the officials—had happened to *him*, or because of him, or around him, and I was walking straight into the middle of a story he had started and never finished telling.

He died owing the company eleven hundred pounds. I had always assumed the debt was money honestly lost—equipment, advances, the slow attrition of a long career. Now I wondered. Debts can be manufactured the same way figures can. A man who knows too much is worth more to an institution indebted than free.

Thursday. First light. Up the river, toward the highlands, toward the tunnel works where the line died and where, I suspected, my father had left something behind—deliberately, the way a man leaves a letter under a loose brick when he cannot say what he knows out loud.

*Ask N.* I turned the two words over all evening, in the boarding house room that smelled of mildew and kerosene, while the harbor lamps swung outside the window. Ask Ngozi? The name meant nothing to me yet. Ask nobody? Ask, and never stop?

I packed the compass last. It had been his—brass cased, glass cracked at the edge, needle still true after all these years, pointing north with the stubbornness of a man who refuses to revise his figures. I set it on top of my kit where I could see it.

"Well," I told it, and the room, and the dark harbor beyond, "we're going to find out what you were hiding."

The compass, being brass, said nothing. But the needle trembled once against its glass, as if in answer, and I chose to take it as agreement.
