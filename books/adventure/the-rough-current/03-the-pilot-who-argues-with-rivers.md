## 3. The Pilot Who Argues with Rivers

The pilot was arguing with the river before I ever met her.

I heard it from the survey office window—actually, I heard it through the wall, through the shutter, and across the yard where the company kept its boats, a woman's voice rising over the morning traffic of the depot like smoke through a thatched roof. She was standing in the bow of a flat-bottomed launch, addressing the water itself with the fluency of long acquaintance and the contempt of a disappointed relative.

"No. You don't get to do that today," she was saying. "You did it Tuesday. You don't get to be clever twice in one week."

I assumed she was mad. This turned out to be only partially true.

Her name was Ngozi, and she had been hired by Mr. Adeyemi in the logistics office against my explicit written objection, which he had read aloud to me twice, enjoying himself enormously both times.

"Miss Okonkwo," he'd said, tapping my memorandum, "you object on the grounds that the pilot is 'unlicensed by any authority this office recognizes,' 'known to dispute official channel soundings,' and—and this is my favorite part—'prone to insubordination toward persons holding company rank.' Miss Okonkwo, who exactly on this river holds company rank? The crocodiles?"

"The river is a company asset," I said. "Its channels are surveyed."

"Surveyed twenty years ago." He folded my objection into a neat triangle and dropped it into his waste basket with the tenderness of a man burying a pet. "The river has had twenty years to disagree with the survey. I'm hiring the person who knows what it thinks now. You're welcome to come down to the boats and meet her, if you'd like to lose the argument in person instead of on paper."

I would like to say I declined. But I have never in my life declined an opportunity to lose an argument in person; it is a family failing. So I went down to the boats, and that is how I came to be standing on the mud-slick planks of the depot jetty watching a small, wiry woman tell a river it was being unreasonable.

She saw me before I decided whether to announce myself. She looked me up and down with the unhurried thoroughness of a customs officer, taking in the corps boots, the surveyor's case, the compass at my breast pocket—my father's compass, brass worn to gold at the hinge—and arrived at some conclusion she did not share.

"You're the engineer," she said. Not a question.

"Ada Okonkwo. I'm leading the survey expedition upriver."

That got the second look. Slower. "Leading."

"That's correct."

"Hm." She turned back to her boat and began coiling a line with two fingers, not looking at me, which I understood to be a technique. "They sent a disgraced engineer to lead an expedition into country no company boat has run in twenty years. With a radio that's already older than most of your porters will be. And they gave you six weeks before the dry season closes the upper water." The coil went over her shoulder and settled in the stern like a sleeping animal. "Either they believe you're very good, or they believe you're very expendable."

"I'm aware of how it looks."

"Do you know what you don't know?" she asked, and finally faced me fully. She had a scar along her jaw, old and white, like a channel marked on a chart. "You know how to read a map. I'm asking if you know how to read water. Because the map says there's a channel past Kobo Bend at four feet. The map is a liar. The river moved its whole self two hundred yards east in the flood of—" she paused, calculating, "—the year your company closed the highland line, as it happens. Nobody surveyed after. Nobody cared. So every sheet of paper in your case is a story somebody told about a river that has since stopped listening."

"I've run surveys on moving water before."

"On *this* water?"

"No."

"Then here's what will happen." She stepped off the gunwale onto the jetty, close enough that I could see the salt dried at her temples, the rope burns calloused into her palms. "You'll give me an order based on your papers. I'll refuse it. You'll pull rank, because you're new to disgrace and still carrying it around like luggage you expect someone to return. Then the river will prove me right in front of twelve porters, and you'll have to decide whether your pride or your expedition matters more." She smiled then, briefly, and it was not a friendly smile but it was an honest one. "Most company men take three days to decide. I'm told you're faster than most. We'll see."

"And if I simply replace you with a pilot who takes orders?"

"You won't." She said it without heat, the way one states the direction of a current. "Because the other pilots who know the upper river are dead, drunk, or working for people whose business upriver isn't surveying. Ask yourself why I was available."

I didn't have an answer for that. I noticed she watched me not have one, and filed it away somewhere behind her eyes, in the drawer where pilots keep the things rivers teach them about people.

"The boats load at first light tomorrow," she said. "Bring less than you think you need. Rivers charge interest on everything you carry."

* * *

Elias Vance arrived that afternoon, and I want to be fair to him, because fairness was not something I managed at the time.

He came off the coastal steamer in a linen jacket that had never known rain, carrying two leather cases and a smile calibrated in a laboratory somewhere north of here. The porters took his cases before he asked. The clerks straightened when he passed. Even the harbor gulls seemed to wheel more respectfully. He crossed the yard to where I stood over the cargo manifest and extended a hand as though we were being introduced at a garden party rather than at the beginning of something that might kill us both.

"Elias Vance. Headquarters engineering division. I'm assigned to observe the reopening survey."

"Observe," I repeated.

"Advise," he amended smoothly. "Observe and advise. Your work will speak for itself, Miss Okonkwo. I'm merely here to make sure headquarters hears it accurately."

There it was—the word *accurately*, doing quiet, terrible work. I had been in the corps long enough to know that when headquarters sends a man to ensure accuracy, what they mean is: to ensure the version of events that reaches the capital is the version headquarters prefers. Every disgraced engineer knows the taxonomy of oversight. There is the inspector, who comes to find fault. There is the auditor, who comes to find money. And there is the observer, who comes to find out whether you are worth saving. Vance was the third kind, and worse, he was the third kind wearing the manners of a friend.

"Your file says Cambridge," I said, because I had read his file the way I read all files of men assigned above me: looking for the knife.

"It does."

"They don't send Cambridge men to observe. They send them to be seen observing. Whose eyes are on us, Mr. Vance?"

He laughed—a real laugh, I'll grant him that, startled out of him—and for half a second the polish cracked and something quicker and younger looked out. "You're direct."

"I'm expendable. It's the same thing, out here."

"Noted," he said, and the polish came back down like a visor. "For what it's worth, Miss Okonkwo, I requested this posting."

"Why would anyone request this posting?"

"Because the last survey of the highland line was done by Okonkwo," he said, "and I've spent two years reading his figures, and I have questions no one in the capital will answer." He picked up his cases. "I thought perhaps his daughter might answer them instead."

I stood with my manifest and felt the ground shift under me the way Ngozi had promised the river would shift under our keels—not violently, just enough to remind you that nothing you were standing on was solid.

"I don't discuss my father's work," I said.

"Of course not," said Elias Vance pleasantly. "First light tomorrow, I understand. I'll try not to be seasick where you can see me."

Ngozi

The company engineer came down to the boats at dawn with too much cargo and a face like a locked door, and I put her in the middle boat where I could watch her.

I had argued with the river all week, and lost, which is normal. The river wins arguments the way old women win arguments—by outlasting you. Tuesday it had moved a sandbar I've steered past since I was nine years old, and Wednesday it sulked, dropping a foot of water overnight out of pure spite, and Thursday it forgave me, the way it does, without apology or explanation, and let me through the lower gorge so smooth I almost trusted it. Almost. That's the trap. The river forgives you right up until it doesn't, and then it keeps you.

So I knew what I was looking at when I looked at Ada Okonkwo. A woman the company had already thrown away once, climbing back into its mouth because the mouth was the only door she had. I've ferried a hundred of them—surveyors, tax collectors, missionaries, soldiers, each one certain the river is a road. The river is not a road. The river is a country that moves, and the company men never learn it because their maps tell them otherwise, and they trust the maps, because the maps were drawn by men like them.

This one, though. This one had checked my lashings herself at dawn without asking permission, and re-tied two of them, and said nothing about it, and when I re-checked her knots I found they were better than mine. That annoyed me. I don't like being annoyed before breakfast.

"You'll sit forward," I told her, pointing. "Weight forward in the rapids. Your Mr. Vance sits amidships, between the crates, where he can't fall on anything important."

"He's not my Mr. Vance."

"He wears your company's brass."

"He wears everybody's brass," she said, and there was something under the words, something with edges. Good. Company men who are comfortable are useless. Company men who are angry can sometimes be reached.

Twelve porters came down the jetty in single file, and I counted them the way I count everything, and stopped at ten. Two short. The tall one, Musa, shrugged and spread his hands: two had taken payment and vanished in the night, which happens, which always happens, which is why I never sleep well the night before a loading. Ada Okonkwo wrote the number down in her book without comment, but I saw her look upriver, toward the gorge, as if the missing men had gone ahead of us somehow. As if she expected the river to give them back.

We cast off at first light, three boats, ten porters, one radio in a crate the color of a coffin, and the whole expedition riding on a woman the company had broken and a pilot the company didn't trust and an observer nobody had hired for the reasons he claimed.

The lower gorge went easy. Too easy. I told the river so, quietly, in the old way, and it pretended not to hear, which is how I knew it was planning something.

It came at the bend below the twin rocks, where the channel kinks left and the water piles up against the far wall like a crowd pushing through a gate. I've run it a hundred times. I ran it wrong on purpose—one hard stroke to starboard, swinging wide, showing the new engineer what the river does to boats that trust the map—and the current took us exactly the way I knew it would, sideways, slewing, the bow swinging at the rocks like a door in a storm.

"Hard aport!" I shouted, and watched her.

Here is the test, and it is not the stroke. Any porter can pull a stroke when shouted at. The test is the half-second before, when the boat goes wrong and the company man reaches for his papers, for his rank, for the thing that has always saved him. Ada Okonkwo reached for nothing. She was already moving—dropped the manifest she'd been reading, got both hands on the yoke beside mine without grabbing it, without fighting me for it, adding her weight to mine the way you add weight to a lever, and together we swung the bow through the pile-up and through the gap and out into the slack water below, where the river, having made its point, went smooth as hammered tin.

Nobody spoke. The porters looked at her. She looked at the water.

"The map says the channel runs close under the twin rocks," I said.

"I know," she said. "I read it last night."

"And?"

"And the river disagrees." She picked her manifest up out of the bilge water and wrung it out with two efficient twists, and something in her face had changed—not softened, exactly. Recalibrated. "How far east did it move?"

"Two hundred yards. In the flood year."

"The year the line closed," she said again, and this time it wasn't a question either, and I wondered—not for the first time, and not for the last—how much this woman already knew, and who had told her, and why the company would send the daughter of Okonkwo up a river it had erased from its own maps unless it wanted something from that country that couldn't be written down.

Behind us, in the second boat, Elias Vance was being quietly, thoroughly sick over the side, and managing even that with dignity, and I decided I disliked him slightly less for it.

Ahead of us the gorge narrowed, and the water darkened, and the river went on saying what it always says, which is: *come and see.*

I put the helm over and followed it in.
