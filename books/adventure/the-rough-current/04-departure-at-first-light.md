## 4. Departure at First Light

The river did not care that I had not slept.

I stood on the mud-slick steps of the landing at half past four in the morning, watching the water move in the dark like something enormous turning over in its sleep, and I thought: *this is the thing they've asked me to master.* A thing with no rails, no gradient, no ballast, no timetable. A thing that had never once, in all its long career, done what it was told.

Behind me, the depot lamps of the city still burned — a smudge of orange against the palm line, the last evidence that the world contained such comforts as bread that wasn't weeviled and letters that arrived when promised. In front of me lay three boats, twelve porters, a radio set crated like a coffin, and six weeks of my life committed to water I could not read.

"You'll want to check your boots for scorpions," said Ngozi, materializing beside me out of the dark the way she seemed to do everything — without effort, without permission, without asking whether I wanted her there. "They like warm leather. They're sentimental about it."

"I'll take my chances."

"Your funeral." She handed me a tin cup of something hot and brown that was either coffee or an apology for coffee. "Drink. The first hour is the worst hour. Everyone's stomach remembers it's afraid."

I drank. It was terrible. It helped.

* * *

By full light the landing had become a small chaos of industry, and I had learned the first lesson of expedition command, which is that command consists mostly of standing somewhere useful-looking while other people work. The porters loaded the boats with a rhythm I came to admire enormously over the following hours: crates passed hand to hand down the line, each man knowing his place in the chain without being told, the whole operation running on trust and muscle and a shared contempt for anyone who tried to help without knowing what they were doing.

I counted twice. Three boats — the two long wooden launches the company called *patience* and *economy*, because the company named things aspirationally rather than accurately, and Ngozi's own craft, a low black river boat she called nothing at all, because as far as I could tell she considered names for boats a form of flattery the vessel hadn't earned.

Twelve porters. Radio equipment in three crates, waterproofed in tarred canvas. Survey instruments — my instruments, the theodolite and levels and chains that were the only part of this enterprise I understood completely, packed in felt and oilcloth like sleeping animals. Six weeks of rice, salt fish, tinned meat whose label promised more than any government ever had. Kerosene. Ammunition, though for what, nobody said. And at the bottom of the second boat, wrapped in oilcloth inside a crate marked SPARES, the sealed orders Elias Vance carried and would not discuss.

I had watched him load them myself. He'd been pleasant about it. That was the worst part — he was pleasant about everything, the way a man is pleasant who has never once had to fight for a seat at a table.

"Miss Okonkwo." He appeared at my elbow with a clipboard, because of course he had a clipboard. "Manifest's confirmed. I've taken the liberty of redistributing some of the weight aft — the way you'd stowed her, she'd have sat bow-heavy in a crosscurrent."

"The way I stowed her," I said, "was according to the loading tables."

"The loading tables," Elias agreed pleasantly, "were written by someone who never met this river."

There was a whole argument in that sentence, and I chose — with the discipline of a woman who had already lost every argument available to her in the last eighteen months — not to have it before breakfast. "Then I look forward to meeting it myself," I said, and went to check the theodolite case, which is where I go instead of saying the other thing.

The truth was the redistribution was correct. I knew it was correct. He knew I knew it. We were going to spend six weeks having conversations entirely in subtext, and I already resented how fluent we both were.

* * *

We cast off at six minutes past seven, which mattered to me and to no one else aboard.

I record the time here because it was the last moment at which the expedition existed as a plan rather than a fact. At six minutes past seven, Ngozi took the lead boat out into the current, the water took hold of her hull the way a hand takes a wrist, and the city began to slide backward behind us — the depot lamps, the coal smoke, the telegraph office where a clerk was even now filing the form that declared me absent from the corps rolls until further notice, if notice ever came.

I stood in the stern of the middle boat and watched my old life get smaller.

It should have been a relief. Exile usually is, once it actually starts — the anticipation is the punishment, and the sentence itself turns out to be mostly weather. But watching the shoreline recede, I felt something less tidy than relief. Every mile of water between me and that shore was a mile I couldn't argue with. There are no appeals filed from a river. Whatever I had been in the city — disgraced, indebted, the engineer who signed off on the Kessel cutting and spent two years learning what the word *liability* sounds like in a hearing room — whatever I had been, the river hadn't heard of it. The river would assess me fresh, daily, and it did not accept references.

"Dread or exhilaration?" Ngozi called across the water, without turning around. She had a pilot's ear; she could hear a person thinking from thirty feet.

"Both."

"Good. If it's only one, you're lying to yourself, and the river hates liars worse than it hates fools." She adjusted the tiller a degree, invisible to me, total to her. "Fools it forgives. It likes an audience."

The gorge walls rose on either side as we left the delta country behind — red stone going gold where the early sun struck it, hung with vines that poured down like green rope thrown from a height. The air changed too. City air is a ledger of everyone else's business; this air smelled of wet stone and crushed leaves and something faintly mineral, like the smell inside a cave, like the world before people got around to naming it.

And underneath everything, the sound. I had expected noise — rapids, we'd been told, lower gorges, white water by the second day. What I hadn't expected was that the river wasn't loud so much as *present*, a continuous low conversation under everything, the way a rail bridge hums when a train is still ten miles out. You don't hear it so much as feel it in the soles of your feet. My father used to say you could tell a man who truly knew railways by whether he stopped feeling bridges hum. I had never stopped. I suspected I never would. And now here was a new hum, older than any bridge, and my feet were taking notes.

* * *

Midmorning, I made my first attempt at reading the water, and it went the way most first attempts go, which is to say it taught me mainly how much I didn't know.

A railway reads top to bottom: grade, curve, cant, the ballast telling you what the drainage is doing. I looked at the river ahead — a broad, apparently placid reach between two wooded points — and applied the same grammar. Smooth surface, deep channel, easy water. I was still congratulating myself on the speed of my education when Ngozi's lead boat swung hard toward the far bank, away from what looked like the obvious line, and I saw why a half-second too late: a submerged shelf, a seam of darker water folding over itself just upstream of the smooth patch, the smooth patch itself a lie — fast water over shallow rock, glassy precisely because it was about to become violent.

Our boatman, a quiet young man named Tombe who had not spoken a word since the landing, corrected our course without drama, and we slid through on the true channel, close enough to the shelf that I could see the water shivering over it like heat haze.

"Gradient," I said aloud, mostly to myself. "It's a hidden grade."

Tombe glanced at me. It was the first time he'd looked at me directly. He said nothing, but the glance had a quality I would come to know well among river people: the look of someone noting, without enthusiasm, that the passenger might be teachable after all.

Ngozi brought her boat alongside ours at the next slack water, close enough to talk without shouting. "You saw it late."

"I saw it."

"But you saw it. Most company men see nothing for a week, then blame the boatman." She studied me with those flat, assessing eyes that made me feel like a specimen pinned to a board. "Your father saw water the same way. Read it like a timetable."

Something went through me at that — not warmth exactly. More like a door opening onto a room I'd assumed was empty and finding furniture in it, someone else's furniture, evidence of a life lived here without me.

"You knew him?"

"Everyone on this river knew Okonkwo." She said it lightly, the way you mention weather, and then pushed off from our gunwale and was gone into the current ahead of us, leaving me holding a fact I had no shelf for yet.

My father had worked the coastal lines. That was the official story, the one in the ledger of debts I carried in my breast pocket like a stone. Coastal lines, and one survey contract inland, twenty years ago, poorly documented, followed by his silence and his decline and the slow mortgage of everything he'd built. I had never thought of him as a man known by rivers.

I wrote the time in my log — 10:40, passing the twin points — and found my hand slightly unsteady, and told myself it was the coffee.

* * *

Noon brought the first real rapids, and with them the first real proof of what I'd signed up for.

The gorge narrowed. The water, which had been conversational all morning, began to raise its voice — a long rolling thunder from ahead, white water stacked in tiers down a chute between walls of stone. Ngozi ran it first, alone in her black boat, threading a line that looked from above like madness and was, I would learn, simply literacy: she was reading sentences in the water that I couldn't yet parse into words.

Then the loaded launches. This was the part I'd dreaded since the survey office — my cargo, my responsibility, my signature effectively on the manifest, going down a chute that ate boats.

Elias took the helm of the first launch. To give the man his due — and I resented every syllable of the giving — he was good. Better than good. He read the chute the way Ngozi did, quick and unhesitating, and brought his boat through the worst of it with barely a shipped bucket of water, and if he looked pleased with himself afterward, well, he'd earned a small portion of pleased.

Our turn. Tombe at the steering oar, me amidships with the cargo lashed, the radio crates wrapped in their tarred canvas like mummies. The roar grew until it wasn't sound anymore but a condition, like weather, like fear itself given a voice. The bow lifted. The gorge walls tilted. And for perhaps forty seconds I experienced the purest, most stripped-down version of myself I had met in years: no rank, no disgrace, no ledger, no name — just a body braced against a lashing, water hitting like thrown gravel, and beneath the terror, unmistakable and appalling, joy.

We came through. The river set us down in the calm below like a cat setting down a kitten it had decided, provisionally, not to eat.

I discovered I was laughing. Actually laughing, breathless, soaked to the ribs, and Tombe was looking at me with an expression that on another face might have been a smile, and Elias's boat pulled alongside and he shouted something congratulatory across the water that I pretended not to hear because hearing it would have required admitting the day had improved me.

That evening we camped on a gravel bar below the gorge, and the expedition became real in the smaller ways: the porters rigging the canvas with the ease of habit, the kerosene stove hissing, rice and salt fish eaten out of tin plates while the river went on talking in the dark, louder now that everything else was quiet. Elias checked the radio set and raised static, and only static, and reported cheerfully that atmospheric conditions were poor and it would clear by morning.

I wrote up the day's positions by lamplight. Twelve souls, three boats, thirty-one miles made good. Six weeks minus one day.

At the bottom of the page, before I could stop myself, I added a line I had no professional reason to add:

*Water reads like track. Ask N. about O.*

* * *

I woke once in the night, as you do on the first night anywhere new, and lay listening to the river and thinking about the sealed orders in the crate marked SPARES, and about my father's initials on a map that had been closed to me, and about the fact that I had just staked the last of my name on a journey whose purpose I understood less with every mile.

The stars over the gorge were dense and indifferent. Somewhere upstream, the water came down out of highlands no map in the survey office had shown me honestly.

Point of no return, I thought. They teach you about those in engineering school — the velocity past which a train can't stop within its block, the commitment point on a signal approach. You're supposed to know it coming. You're supposed to make the decision *before* the point, cleanly, with margin.

Nobody had told me the point could be behind you before you noticed it. Nobody had told me it could sound like this — like thunder, like laughter, like a door closing softly somewhere far downstream.

I slept again. In the morning, the river was waiting, and for the first time in two years, so was I.
