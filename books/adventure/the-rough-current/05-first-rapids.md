## 5. First Rapids

The river announced itself before we saw it. For two days we had traveled a brown, patient thing, wide as a rail yard and about as dramatic, and I had begun to think Ngozi's reputation was built on ferrying bathwater. Then the banks closed in, the water turned from tea to milk to something like polished steel, and the sound came up through the hull of our boat and into my boots—a low, continuous growl, like a train you can't see yet but know is coming.

"That," said Ngozi, without turning around, "is where you stop talking."

I hadn't been talking. I had been narrating, quietly, to myself, in the manner of an engineer assessing grades and clearances, which is apparently indistinguishable from talking. I closed my mouth and did what I do instead of praying: I checked equipment. The boat was a long wooden thing with an outboard engine that Elias had insisted on bringing—"for the flats"—and which Ngozi regarded with the open contempt most people reserve for a fraudulent bill. Behind us came the second boat with six porters and the radio crate lashed under canvas, and behind that the third with the fuel drums, riding low and sullen.

The gorge took us.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because later accounts—Elias's report, if he ever wrote one; my own testimony, such as it was—would make it sound orderly. It was not orderly. It was three hours of the most concentrated terror I have ever sat through while pretending to be useful, and I include the tribunal that ended my career, which at least had the decency to occur on dry land.

Ngozi stood at the stern with one hand on the tiller and one foot braced against a thwart, reading the water ahead of us the way I read a gradient chart. And that is the only way I can describe it, because it *was* reading. The river has a grammar. I didn't know it yet—I was illiterate, a foreigner gawking at script—but I could see her parsing it. A smooth green tongue of water meant a channel; she steered for it. A line of white across the current meant rock just under the surface; she gave it room. Where the water piled up into a standing wave, there was a drop below; where it lay flat and glassy in the lee of a bluff, there was either deep quiet water or a whirlpool, and she seemed to know which by some sense that had nothing to do with anything taught in any engineering school I'd been expelled from.

"V-wood," she said once, pointing left, and the porters paddled hard left, and thirty seconds later we passed a half-submerged tree the size of a church pew, strainers of branches waiting downstream of it like fingers. A boat wrapped on that would have come apart in minutes. She had seen it from a hundred yards, under white water, moving.

"How?" I asked.

She didn't answer. She was busy. But later, moored for the midday meal on a gravel bar the color of old bone, she squatted next to me while I pretended to check the fuel lines and said, as if continuing a conversation we'd been having all morning:

"You look at water like track. Don't. Track is dead. Water is alive. You don't read a living thing the same way."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. You've been doing it since the depot. Gradients, sightlines, ballast." She picked up a pebble and tossed it into the current, watched it vanish. "Track goes where men decided. River goes where it likes. You have to ask it, not survey it."

"And how does one ask?"

"By shutting up," she said, "and listening for about twenty years."

I have been told I am a proud woman. This is slander. I am merely correct at speed, which people mistake for pride. But I will admit that Ngozi's twenty years made my four at the corps feel like a correspondence course, and that stung in a place I hadn't known was tender. I went back to my fuel lines, which did not need checking, and she went back to her boat, and we were both perfectly satisfied with the arrangement.

* * *

Elias Vance, meanwhile, spent the rapids being magnificent.

I mean this without irony, or rather with exactly enough irony to keep me honest. He crouched in the bow of our boat with a paddle he clearly knew how to use, calling depth markers back to Ngozi in a vocabulary I didn't share—"tongue left, hole right, eddy above the bend"—and she used his eyes and he used her hands, and between them they moved forty feet of timber and cargo through water that wanted to kill us with what looked like genuine enthusiasm. When we stopped, he was charming to the porters in their own language, badly accented but sincerely attempted, and he produced a tin of biscuits and distributed them with the easy generosity of a man who has never once counted the cost of a biscuit.

"You held well back there," he said to me, offering the tin.

"I held nothing. I was cargo."

"Cargo doesn't watch the water," he said. "Cargo watches its boots. You watched the whole reach. You'll be reading it inside a week."

I took a biscuit because refusing would have required conversation. "Reading it won't get this expedition upriver. Boats will."

"Boats need readers." He nodded toward Ngozi, who was arguing with the second boat's pilot about tomorrow's line through the narrows, gesturing at the water with both hands as though conducting an orchestra of very stupid violins. "She's the best I've seen. And I've seen the coast pilots, the ones with licenses and brass buttons."

"Why hire her, then? If you had objections." Because he had—I'd heard him argue with the survey office about it, politely, and lose.

He considered the question longer than it deserved. "Because the man who signed off on this route," he said finally, "insisted on her by name. And when a dead man's paperwork insists on something, I've learned to pay attention."

"Her file says she's worked this river ten years."

"Her file," Elias said mildly, "says whatever keeps the office happy. Files usually do." He smiled at me then, that easy, expensive smile, and I understood that I had just been given a piece of information and told, simultaneously, that I hadn't. It was a skill. I recognized it because I'd watched better liars than him use it at tribunals.

I put it away with the other things I was carrying: the compass, the ledger, the growing suspicion that everyone on this river knew more about my father's line than I did—including, possibly, the river itself.

* * *

The near-capsize happened on the third day, at a place Ngozi called, with what I can only assume was local affection, the Grinder.

We had run harder water already—or water that had looked harder—and come through fine, and I confess I had begun to relax into a fool's confidence. The Grinder began innocently, a long chute between bluffs, fast but apparently clean, and Ngozi took it at speed because speed is control in a current, the way momentum carries a locomotive over a bad joint. Then the river bent right, hard, against a wall of stone, and the entire flow of it leaned into that wall like a drunk leaning on a door, and I saw—too late, much too late—the boil line where the main current met the back-eddy behind the point, a seam of broken water as sharp as a rail joint.

"Hold!" Ngozi shouted, and the word was still leaving her mouth when the seam took us.

I have felt trains derail. I have felt a bridge pier shift under load, that sickening millimeter of give that means everything you calculated is now wrong. The seam was worse, because it was instant and total and utterly indifferent to my competence. One moment we were a boat; the next we were a boat-shaped argument between two tons of water going opposite directions, and the starboard gunwale dipped, and the river poured in over my knees like it had been invited.

What happened next happened in perhaps four seconds, and I remember it in fragments, the way one remembers a crash.

Ngozi's voice, flat and absolute: "Weight left! *Now!*"

My body obeying before my mind caught up—throwing myself across the thwarts to port, hauling the nearest porter, a boy called Emeka who weighed less than my tool chest, bodily over the bench beside me.

Elias in the bow, driving his paddle into the water on the high side like a man rowing the air itself, levering us.

The engine screaming as Ngozi opened the throttle full out—not away from the wall, which every instinct screamed for, but *at* it, angling upstream into the very face of the current, using the river's own push to swing our stern around like a pivot on a turntable.

And then we were through. Just like that. Out of the seam, into the eddy behind the point, wallowing in comparatively gentle water, sitting in four inches of river, all of us breathing like landed fish.

Ngozi cut the engine. In the sudden silence I could hear the Grinder working away behind us, that same patient growl, unbothered, unhurried, already forgetting us.

"Who shipped water?" she demanded.

Nobody answered, because everybody had.

"Then bail," she said, "and after bailing, somebody tells me why my weight order was slow."

"It wasn't slow," I said. My voice came out strange. "It was me. I froze. Half a second."

She looked at me then—really looked, the first time since the depot—with those flat, assessing eyes, and whatever she saw made her shrug. "Half a second is a long time in a seam. Next time it's a quarter."

"That's your critique? Not 'well done'?"

"Well done for what? We're wet, the rice is wet, and the engine swallowed a mouthful it'll cough about for a week." She turned to the porters and rattled off orders in their tongue, and they began bailing with the cheerful efficiency of men who had done this many times before, which was somehow more frightening than panic would have been.

But as she turned away, she added, almost too quietly for me to catch it: "You threw yourself left before I finished saying it. That's not freezing twice. That's learning once."

From Ngozi, I suspected, this was a decoration. I found, to my considerable irritation, that I wanted to wear it.

* * *

We camped that night on a shelf of rock above the gorge, boats hauled clear and tied down, fire small because wood was scarce and the cliffs held the heat like an oven anyway. Below us the river ran silver-black in the last light, and the sound of it never stopped—that growl, that grind, the voice of something enormous thinking its slow thoughts about stone.

Elias spread the charts on a flat rock and weighted the corners with stones, and the three of us knelt around them like conspirators, which in a sense we were.

"We make the confluence by day eight if the water holds," he said, tracing the line. "After that the gorges narrow again. Six weeks total to the highlands, if nothing breaks."

"Something broke today," Ngozi said. "The engine."

"The engine coughs. It doesn't break." He glanced at me. "Your assessment, Okonkwo? You've had your head inside it all evening."

I had, in fact, and I gave it honestly: "Carburetor's flooding intermittently. Grit in the float needle, most likely, from the swamping. I can strip and clean it tomorrow, but if it takes river water again at that volume, no promises. There's no spare needle. There's no spare anything." I looked at the chart, at the thin blue line running up into country no map I trusted had ever described accurately. "Every mechanical decision from here is a bet we can't hedge."

"Spoken like a woman who's been burned by optimistic schedules," Elias said.

"Spoken like a woman who's read the maintenance ledgers for this route. Your office's own figures show the last survey crew lost eleven days to equipment failure in the first gorge alone. And they had mules." I tapped the chart. "We have arms."

"Mules also eat," Ngozi observed. "Arms only complain."

Despite myself—the exhaustion, the wet rice, the memory of four seconds in which the river had handled me like a toy—I laughed. So did Elias. Even Ngozi's mouth did something at its corner that in a more generous person might have qualified as a smile.

Later, when the others had turned in, I sat alone at the edge of the shelf with my father's compass open on my knee, not navigating anything, just holding it. The needle swung lazily, settling north, and beyond it the gorge ran black and audible, and somewhere up that blackness was a tunnel my father had helped close, and a debt with my name on it, and answers I was beginning to suspect I didn't want.

The river didn't care what I suspected. That was the thing about it, the thing I was only beginning to learn. It carried us whether we deserved carrying or not. It had drowned better engineers than my father and would drown worse ones than me, and it would do so beautifully, without malice, the way a track wears out rails—simply by being what it is, and being used.

"Can't sleep either?"

Elias settled onto the rock beside me, close enough to be companionable, far enough to be deniable. He handed me a mug of the terrible sweet coffee the porters brewed, and I drank it because the alternative was conversation, and then I drank more of it because the alternative was silence, and silence with Elias Vance had begun to feel like a document I couldn't finish reading.

"The seam today," he said. "At the Grinder. You moved fast."

"Ngozi says I froze."

"Ngozi says a lot of things." He looked down at the water. "She also told me at the depot that you'd either quit by the second rapid or become indispensable by the fifth. She wouldn't say which. She said the river would decide."

"And what has the river decided?"

He smiled at the darkness. "Ask it tomorrow. We run the Narrows at first light—she wants maximum water, and the gauge dropped an inch overnight." He stood, brushing grit from his trousers, and paused. "One inch, Ada. On a river this size, that's not weather. That's the dry season starting early."

He said it lightly, the way he said everything, and went to his bedroll. But I stayed on the rock a while longer, compass in hand, listening to the river grind away at its ancient work, and I thought: an inch. Already an inch.

Somewhere upstream, the clock had started, and none of us had heard it begin.
