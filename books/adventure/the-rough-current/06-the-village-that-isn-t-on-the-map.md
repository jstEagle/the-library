## 6. The Village That Isn't on the Map

I have piloted this river for nineteen years, and I will tell you the first thing a pilot learns: the map lies. Not because the men who draw maps are liars—though some of them are—but because a map is a photograph of a river's mood on one particular afternoon, and the river changes its mind the way a woman changes her mind about a man who talks too much.

The second thing a pilot learns is which villages are not on the map, and why.

We came around the bend below the third gorge in the gold hour of evening, when the water goes the color of strong tea and every heron stands like it's posing for money, and I saw the smoke. Three threads of it, thin and blue, rising off the left bank where the official survey showed nothing but forest and a sandbar shaped like a dead fish.

"Smoke," said the engineer woman, Okonkwo, who had spent four days learning to read water and still read it like a foreign newspaper, sounding out each word twice.

"I see it."

"There's no settlement marked here."

"No," I agreed, and put the helm over toward the bank.

Vance looked up from his charts with that expression he wore when the world declined to match his paperwork. "Ngozi. The route sheet says we make camp at the gravel bar."

"The route sheet was written by a man in an office who has never been bitten by anything larger than a mosquito." I eased the throttle down. "The gravel bar floods at this level. And there is smoked fish up that bank, and roofed shelters, and people who owe me favors. Your porters have blisters like overripe fruit. We sleep under roofs tonight."

Okonkwo watched me steer with those eyes of hers that missed nothing and forgave less. She'd been doing that all week—watching me handle the boat, watching me argue with the current, filing things away behind her face like a clerk who doesn't trust the ledger. I knew why. I was not on her side yet, and she knew I knew it. Good. Trust given too early is worth exactly what you paid for it.

"You know these people?" she asked.

"I know everybody on this river." Which was true, though not in the way she took it to mean.

* * *

The village sat back from the bank among fig trees, a scatter of houses with walls of woven palm and roofs that had been re-thatched recently enough to still smell green. Dogs announced us before the boats touched mud, and by the time we stepped ashore there were perhaps thirty people watching from the shade with the particular patience of people who have seen boats full of strangers before and expect to see them again after everyone concerned has learned some manners.

An old man came forward. He walked slowly, but the slowness was economy, not weakness—the walk of a man who has decided that hurrying is for other people's benefit and he is no longer taking requests. His shoulders were still wide as a doorframe under a shirt gone soft as cloth can go.

"Ngozi," he said, and smiled with maybe six teeth, all of them apparently delighted to see me. "Still alive. The river must be getting old too."

"Baba Ekwueme." I took his hand the way you take a chief's hand, with both of yours, so he knows you're not reaching for anything behind him. "My mother sends greetings."

"Your mother sends greetings and no visit. Tell her the river misses her and so do I, and one of us means it more than the other." He laughed at his own joke, then his eyes went past me to the strangers climbing the bank—twelve porters, two engineers, one radio set wrapped in oilcloth like a corpse prepared for burial—and something in his face closed, quietly, the way a house closes its shutters before weather.

He looked at them a long moment. Then he looked at Ada Okonkwo.

I saw it happen. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is cheap now and will be expensive later. I saw the old man look at her face, and I saw his own face change—not soften, exactly; more like a lock turning somewhere deep in him—and I thought: ah. Here is a thing I did not pack for.

"You," he said to her. "You are Okonkwo's daughter."

The whole clearing went quiet enough that I could hear the river working at the far bank, patient as ever, moving a mountain one grain at a time.

Ada went very still. "You knew my father?"

"Knew him?" Baba Ekwueme made a sound that was half laugh and entirely something else. "Child, come and sit down. All of you sit. This is not a story for standing."

* * *

They brought benches under the big fig, and a woman whose name I never caught brought out a pot of something with pepper in it that made Vance sweat and pretend he wasn't sweating, which I enjoyed more than the food, and the food was good.

Baba Ekwueme sat across from Ada and studied her the way you study a river reach you're about to run for the first time.

"Twenty-two years ago," he said, "men came with chains and tripods and little brass instruments that they looked through sideways. They paid well, they paid late, and they asked questions. Where does the path go. How many households. Where are the graves." He paused on that word. "Always the graves. They wrote everything in books, and whatever they wrote in the books became true, and whatever they did not write—" he spread his hands at the village, at the smoke, at the dogs now sleeping between the porters' feet like they'd known them all their lives, "—did not exist. You understand? We are not on your company's map because a man with a book decided we were not on it. Our fathers' bones are not on it either. On the map, this place is empty. Empty land is land you may do anything with. That is what the book is for."

Vance shifted. I watched him shift. There is a particular way a company man moves when a conversation starts costing money, and Elias Vance had it even when he was trying not to, even when—I'll grant him this—he seemed genuinely sorry.

"That was the original survey," Ada said. Her voice was careful, level, the voice of a woman walking across ice and listening to it. "The highland line. My father worked on it."

"Your father." Baba Ekwueme said the name again, tasting it. "Okonkwo. The quiet one. The others drank and sang and told us our rivers would carry iron horses and our children would wear shoes. Your father drank nothing and said little, and what he said, he said to me, at night, when the others slept." The old man leaned forward. "He came to me and asked where the flood would reach. Not where the rail would run—the flood. The water behind the dam they were already dreaming of. He drew lines on his paper and my answers did not fit his paper, and instead of arguing with me, he erased his paper."

Ada's hands had gone white around her tin cup. "Erased it how?"

"He moved his numbers. I don't know the words for it—he spoke of levels, of heights, of how much water would stand above our roofs. He told me the company's figures would drown this valley and three more above it, and that when he reported what he found, he was told his instruments were miscalibrated. Told twice. The third time, he stopped reporting." Baba Ekwueme shrugged, a slow rolling motion like water deciding. "Then the line died. They said the rock was bad. They said the fever was bad. They said many things. But your father stayed one more season, alone, with two assistants, and he walked every household here and marked our doors on his private paper, and he told me: if the water ever comes, remember where I stood. Remember what I measured. Someone will come asking, someday, and they will need to be reminded that the book lied."

The fire cracked. Somewhere a dog dreamed audibly.

Ada set her cup down with the exaggerated care of someone who does not trust her own hands. "That's not possible. My father was dismissed for incompetence. The closure report—the whole reason I'm here—the line failed because of *him*. That's what the record says. That's why our family—" She stopped. Recalibrated. I could see the arithmetic running behind her eyes, the same cold arithmetic she did with elevations, except now the numbers were twenty years of shame, a dead man's debt, a daughter's whole ruined career built on a foundation that might be a lie poured by somebody else.

"The record," I said, before I could stop myself, "is a book. And we have established what the book is for."

She turned on me with real anger, and I respected it—it was better than the alternative, which was tears. "You knew? You knew these people remembered him, and you said nothing for four days?"

"I knew the village was here. I didn't know what story it held for you." I held her glare without blinking; I've out-stared crocodiles. "But ask yourself, engineer—if I had told you on the first morning, 'your father is a saint to the people upstream,' would you have believed me? Or would you have added it to the list of ways the pilot is manipulating you?"

Her mouth opened. Closed. The anger drained out through some crack in her discipline and left something rawer underneath.

"She has you there," Vance said mildly, and got a look from both of us, which I believe was the first thing Ada and I had agreed on since the coast.

* * *

Later, when the porters were rolled in their cloths and the fire had settled to a red heart, I walked down to the water alone, because a pilot who doesn't sit with her river every evening starts hearing it wrong, and a pilot who hears the river wrong kills people.

Ada found me there. Of course she did. The woman had the instincts of a debt collector.

"How many other villages," she said. "Upstream. In the flood line."

I skipped a flat stone. It went five skips, which pleased me. "Counting this one?"

"Yes."

"Eleven that I know. There may be more above the falls that I don't, because above the falls is not my country, it's my mother's, and even she only claims to know it." I threw another stone. "The dam figures in the published survey put the reservoir at a height that touches none of them. If Baba Ekwueme is telling the truth about your father's numbers being changed—changed *downward*, engineer, so the company's paper showed a smaller lake than the real one—then eleven villages and every grave in them go under the water, and the paper will say nobody lived there. Because the paper will say nobody lived there."

She was quiet a long time. The river talked instead, the way it does at night, low and continuous, a conversation it's been having with itself since before anyone's father was born.

"My father owed eight hundred pounds," she finally said. "That's why I'm here. That's why I'm anywhere. He signed it over before he died and the company has been collecting on me ever since—one disgrace, one exile, one impossible assignment at a time." She laughed, short and bitter as quinine. "And now you're telling me the debt might not even be gambling or folly. It might be hush money."

"It might be the price of silence," I said. "Which is the most expensive thing there is. Ask any of the eleven villages."

She looked at me then—really looked, the clerk's suspicion dropping away for one unguarded second—and what was underneath it frightened her more than the anger had. Hope does that to people like Ada. People who have been paying other people's debts so long they've forgotten they ever owned anything.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "Four days ago you wouldn't share a compass bearing with me."

"Because four days ago you were company." I turned back to the water. "Tonight you're Okonkwo's daughter, and this river remembers him kindly. Those are different cargo, and I charge different rates."

Behind us, up at the fire, I heard Vance's voice rise and fall, smooth as ever, telling the headman some polished nothing. And I heard something else underneath it—a small mechanical sound, a dial being turned, the radio set waking up out of schedule. Calling base camp at midnight, when no schedule called for it.

Ada heard it too. Her head came up like a dog's.

"Elias," she said slowly, "said the radio needed recalibrating."

"Mm," I said. "Men always need to recalibrate something at midnight."

She stood there a moment longer, torn between the fire and the water, between the story she'd just been handed and the man she'd been assigned to distrust and hadn't quite managed to. Then she went up the bank toward the light, and I stayed with my river, skipping stones, listening to the static whisper through the fig trees like rain that hadn't decided to fall yet.

The next morning we would push on upstream, into the gorges, toward the tunnel works where the old line died. And I found myself thinking, as the fire burned down, that we were carrying more cargo than the boats could hold: a dead man's secret, a live man's midnight calls, and a daughter who had just learned that the ledger she'd spent her life balancing was written in somebody else's hand.

The river, as always, said nothing. It just kept going, taking everything with it, keeping what it kept.
