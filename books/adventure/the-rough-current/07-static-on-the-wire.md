## 7. Static on the Wire

The radio began to die on a Tuesday, which I considered typical of my luck. Not dramatically—nothing on this expedition had the decency to be dramatic—but in the small, insulting way of a colleague who stops finishing your sentences.

It was Elias's set, a Marconi portable crated in oiled canvas and treated with more tenderness than he'd shown any human being since we'd left the coast. He'd installed it on the lead boat's flat deck under an awning of green tarpaulin, and every evening at six he wound the crank, tuned through the static, and took down base camp's weather and instructions in his neat engineer's hand. For nine days this ritual had worked. On the tenth, the voice from the coast arrived as if speaking through wet wool.

"Say again," Elias said, leaning into the mouthpiece. "Base, say again."

Static answered him, that great white ocean of it, and somewhere beneath it a syllable or two drowned.

I was mending a porter's sandal strap with waxed thread when it happened, sitting close enough to hear everything and pretending otherwise. I'd gotten good at pretending otherwise where Elias Vance was concerned. It was a professional skill, like reading contours.

"They're fading," he said, to no one in particular.

"It's the gorge walls," I said, not looking up. "You've got iron in these cliffs. It plays hell with propagation. Any textbook will tell you."

"Any textbook," he agreed pleasantly, "will tell you a great many things that aren't true out here."

That was the trouble with Elias. He never argued properly. He just agreed with you in a tone that suggested he was filing your opinion somewhere for later examination.

* * *

I want to record, for the sake of honesty, that my suspicion did not arrive all at once like a train through a station wall. It crept. That's how suspicion works with men like Elias Vance—it has to sneak up on you past all your own defenses, which in my case were considerable, because I had spent three years learning to distrust my own judgment and was only now beginning to take it back.

The first thing was the schedule. Base camp had always transmitted at six, regular as a tide table. After the garbling started, they came through at odd hours—once at eleven at night, once just before dawn, when the whole camp was asleep except the sentry and me, because I slept badly on the river and worse under canvas. The dawn transmission was almost clear. Almost. Enough to catch fragments: *...proceed... source... do not...*

Do not what? Do not proceed? Do not delay? Do not tell anyone? A single ellipsis can hold a great deal of menace if you stare at it long enough.

The second thing was Elias himself. When the signal failed, he didn't curse it the way a man curses weather. He adjusted to it, smoothly, the way a man adjusts to a plan arriving slightly ahead of schedule. He stopped winding the crank at six and started winding it whenever he thought no one was watching—which, given that Ngozi watched everything the way herons watch water, meant he was being watched constantly and simply hadn't done the arithmetic.

"He is lying," Ngozi told me on the eleventh night, crouched by the cook fire while the porters sang low somewhere down the bank. She was sharpening her machete with the unhurried strokes of a woman who had never once needed to hurry it. "Your radio man."

"He's not my radio man."

"He is the company's radio man, which is worse." She tested the edge against her thumbnail. "A river tells you when it's going to rise. The birds go quiet first. Then the ants move their eggs uphill. Then the water goes green and smells wrong. Everything that lies gives warning, Ada Okonkwo. You just have to know its habits." She pointed the machete, gently, toward the awning where Elias sat hunched over the set, cranking. "His habit is that he winds it too often and hears nothing, and is not surprised."

I looked. She was right, damn her. Elias turned the crank with the resigned patience of a man checking a clock he already knew the time of.

"So what do we do?" I asked. "Tie him up and search his kit?"

Ngozi smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting sight. "No. We let him keep his secrets a little longer. A liar on the line is useful. He shows you where the weak rail is before it breaks." She went back to her blade. "But you should count the crates again."

"Why?"

"Because I did," she said, "and there is one more than there was at the coast."

* * *

There are two kinds of lies, and I have told both, so I speak with authority. There is the lie you tell to get something—a job, a second chance, a loan against a name already ruined. And there is the lie you carry, sealed, the way you carry orders in a oilskin packet against your chest, feeling its weight at every inspection, every handshake, every moment someone looks at you a half-second too long.

I had been carrying mine for eleven days now.

The orders were simple enough, as such things always are. Simple, and monstrous, the way a well-drafted contract is monstrous. *Verify the original survey figures of the highland line. Determine whether discrepancies are attributable to error or intent. In the case of intent, document fully. Discretion essential. The Okonkwo name will require handling.*

Handling. As though her father were cargo.

I had read them four times on the boat from the coast and then stopped reading them, because reading them changed nothing and made sleep worse. What I could not stop doing was watching Ada Okonkwo read the river, the maps, the porters' faces, and me. She watched the way her father must have watched, according to the old files—everything noted, nothing assumed. And she was good. That was the complication nobody at headquarters had planned for. They'd sent me expecting either a broken woman grateful for any work or a mediocrity playing at engineering. Instead they'd sent me a woman who cross-checked elevations in her head while mending sandals, and who had already caught, I was nearly certain, the discrepancy between the published figures and the terrain.

The radio was supposed to be my lifeline and my leash. Every evening I reported position, morale, progress—and every evening I omitted the same three facts: that we were ahead of schedule, that the crew trusted her more than me, and that the old survey markers we'd found at the third cutting were not errors. They were deliberate. Precise. Someone had moved those benchmarks by exactly enough to make the valley floor look higher than it was, and only a surveyor of real skill could have done it so cleanly.

Her father. It had to be. And the company wanted it documented as fraud—his fraud—to clear the way for whatever the sealed annex described. I hadn't opened the annex. That was the other weight I carried: a second envelope, marked for opening only at the source, and I was afraid of it in a way I couldn't put into report language.

Then the static came, and I confess I welcomed it at first. A dead wire is a kind of freedom; no one can order you onward if no one can reach you. But freedom, I discovered, curdles fast into exposure. Because if the wire was dead for me, it was dead for everyone—and if it wasn't dead, if it was only dead for certain hours, certain frequencies—

Then someone at base camp was talking to somebody on this river, and it wasn't to me.

* * *

On the twelfth day we made camp below a bend where the river ran wide and brown and slow, and I finally did what Ngozi had told me to do. I counted the crates.

Twelve at the coast. Twelve at the village that wasn't on the map—or so I'd believed. Thirteen now. I stood in the failing light looking at the thirteenth crate, stenciled with the same shipping marks as the others, and felt the hair rise on my arms. Either someone had miscounted at the coast—and I did not miscount—or it had come from somewhere else. From a supply cache. From another party. From the direction we were headed.

Elias appeared beside me with two tin cups of the terrible coffee the cook made, and handed me one, and said, conversationally, "Atmospherics should clear in a few days. The season's turning. We'll have base camp back by the tunnel works."

"The tunnel works," I repeated.

"That's where the old line died, isn't it? Your father's line." He sipped his coffee. His eyes stayed on the river. "I'd think you'd want to see it."

And there it was—the thing I'd been circling for days, laid out between us as casually as a cup of coffee. He knew whose line it was. Of course he knew; everyone in the corps knew about Okonkwo, the surveyor who'd walked away from the highlands and then walked away from everything else, debts and all. But Elias said it the way a man says a password, quietly, watching to see if I recognized it.

"Elias," I said, "when did you last actually hear base camp?"

He didn't flinch. I'll give him that. He looked at me across the steam rising off our cups, and for one unguarded moment something moved behind the polish—something that looked almost like apology, or maybe like a man standing at a door deciding whether to open it.

"Tuesday," he said. "Same as you."

"And the transmissions since?"

"Atmospherics."

"You said the season's turning. Atmospherics don't follow seasons, they follow weather, and the weather's been flat for three days." I set down the cup. "Who else is on this frequency, Elias?"

For a long moment the only sound was the river, moving in the dark with that patient, indifferent strength it never once forgot to remind us of. Then Elias smiled—the polished one, the one I'd learned to distrust—and said:

"Get some sleep, Okonkwo. Tomorrow we run the narrows, and I'd rather have you sharp than right."

"I can be both," I said.

"Yes," he said, turning away. "I'm rather afraid you can."

I didn't sleep. I sat by the embers with my father's compass open on my knee, the needle trembling toward a north none of us could see, and I thought about a thirteenth crate, and a voice saying *do not*, and a man who wound a dead radio every night without surprise.

Somewhere upstream, in the dark, the river kept moving toward the source. And I understood, with the cold certainty of a level finding its bubble, that we were not the only ones moving toward it.

We were just the ones who didn't yet know why.
