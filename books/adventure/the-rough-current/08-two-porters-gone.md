## 8. Two Porters Gone

I have been woken by many things in my life: the whistle of a locomotive through a thin hotel wall, my father's cough through a thinner one, once, memorably, a goat that had opinions about my tent. But I had never been woken by silence, and I learned that morning that silence has a texture all its own. It has weight. It sits on the chest like wet canvas.

The silence in question was Musa's kettle.

For eleven days on the river, the day had begun the same way: the hiss of the kettle on Musa's fire, the low argument between Musa and Daniel about whose turn it was to fetch water, the smell of strong tea drifting into my tent like a small, stubborn promise that the world was still orderly. That morning there was nothing. No kettle. No argument. No smell.

I lay still for a moment, cataloguing. The river spoke as it always did, that low continuous conversation of water over stone. Somewhere a kingfisher made its rattling complaint. But the human sounds—the ones I had stopped noticing because they had become the frame of my days—were gone.

I got up, put on my boots, and stepped out into the grey hour before dawn.

The camp was arranged along the bank in the order Ngozi had decreed on our first night, an order she had defended with the logic of a general and the vocabulary of a dockworker: boats nearest the water, porters beyond them, engineers and their delicate tents furthest up, because, as she put it, "if the river comes for anyone in the night, it should at least have to work for it."

Musa's fire was dead. Not banked—dead, scattered, the stones around it cold when I crouched and touched them. His kettle sat on the cold stones, full of water, untouched.

Nobody sleeps with a full kettle on a dead fire.

"Musa?" I said, and my voice came out smaller than I intended. I said it again, louder. "Musa. Daniel."

Nothing answered but the river.

* * *

By full light, the whole camp was awake and the picture had assembled itself into something worse than I had first imagined. Musa's tent was empty, his bedding still rolled the way he rolled it, tight as a drumhead, the way a careful man rolls bedding when he expects to come back to it. Daniel's was the same. And between them, where the supply crate had sat—the one with the spare radio batteries, the medical kit, and, I noted with a coldness in my stomach that had nothing to do with the morning air, the pay chest—there was nothing but a rectangle of pressed grass and four small stones that someone had placed at its corners.

Stones at the corners. I looked at them for a long time. I could not decide whether they were meant as a marker, an apology, or an insult.

Ngozi stood at the water's edge with her arms folded, watching the current as though it might be persuaded to give up a secret. She had not said a word since I'd woken the camp, which from Ngozi was more alarming than shouting.

"Nobody heard anything," Elias said. He was dressed, of course, immaculately, as though dawn were a meeting he'd prepared for. "No noise, no struggle. They simply—"

"Went," Ngozi said, without turning. "Men don't simply go. Rivers go. Weather goes. Men are taken, or they run, or they are made to run. Pick one."

"Thank you, that's very helpful," Elias said.

"It is not meant to be helpful. It is meant to be true. Helpful comes later."

I let them have their exchange and did the thing I was actually good at, the one skill the corps had never managed to beat out of me: I counted.

Twelve porters had come upriver. Ten stood in a ragged line on the bank, and every one of them was watching me with an expression I recognized, because I had worn it myself in a survey office in the city: the expression of a person waiting to find out how much trouble the day intends to make for them.

Ten porters. Twelve loads that needed carrying. And a supply crate gone that contained, among other things, half our radio batteries—which mattered more with every day that the radio stayed silent—and the pay chest, which mattered in a way I didn't yet want to think about.

"Joseph," I said to the eldest of the porters, a grey-templed man who had been up this river more times than I had been down a staircase. "When did you last see them?"

He considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. "Last night. Musa made the fire. Daniel was laughing at him about the kettle. Daniel was always laughing about the kettle." Something moved in his face, quickly gone. "I slept. I woke in the night—the dog, maybe, or a bird—and I heard nothing. If they had gone past me, I would have heard them. The ground here talks. Everyone knows the ground talks."

"The ground talks," I repeated.

"Everyone knows," Joseph said, with the patience of a man explaining rain to a fish, and Ngozi, behind him, nodded once, as though this were the first sensible thing anyone had said all morning.

* * *

We searched.

I organized it the way I would have organized a track inspection, because it was the only organizational grammar I possessed: divide the ground, assign the sectors, set a time to regroup, and God help the man who came back early with nothing. Elias took the downstream sweep with four porters. Ngozi took the water—she and two of her cousins' sons poling up and down the bank, reading the shallows, checking the reed beds with the flat, professional attention of people who had, I understood with a small internal chill, looked for drowned men before.

I took the old cutting.

I don't know what instinct sent me there. Perhaps it was no instinct at all, only the logic of the map: the abandoned line had been cut into the hillside a mile back, and where there is a cutting there is shelter, and where there is shelter there are answers, or at least there is somewhere to put a body, which is a kind of answer.

The cutting had been reclaimed by the forest with the enthusiasm of a creditor seizing an estate. Twenty years of vine and sapling had gone through the company's earthworks like roots through a coffin. The drainage ditches my father's generation had cut were soft green channels now. The blast-scarred face of the rock had gone black with moss, so that the drill marks looked like something the hill itself had grown. I walked the old formation, boots sinking in leaf-rot, and tried to imagine the noise of it: the steam engines, the blasting, the shouting in three languages. What I heard instead was my own breathing and, underneath it, that sense I had been carrying since the survey office in the city—the sense of walking through a room where a conversation had been stopped mid-sentence.

I found the satchel forty minutes in, off the downstream edge of the formation, tucked under a fallen sapling as though someone had hidden it in a hurry and badly.

It was a company satchel. Canvas, brass buckles, the depot stamp still legible on the flap. And it was torn—not worn, not rotted, *torn*, the strap ripped through at the buckle and the flap split along the seam, as though it had been pulled off someone's shoulder by someone who did not care what it cost.

I crouched over it for a long moment before I touched it, and what I felt in that moment was not fear, exactly. Fear had been a guest in my chest since the radio went quiet, and I had learned to work around it. This was something colder and more precise. It was the feeling from the survey office, the one I had told myself was imagination: the feeling that the ground under this expedition had been prepared. That things were arranged.

Because here is what I knew, standing in the green silence of that cutting with the ruined satchel in my hands: Musa and Daniel had not carried a company satchel. Porters on this expedition carried company loads on company yokes, and their own things in their own cloth. The satchels—the good brass-buckled ones—were issued to the survey staff. To me. To Elias. To the two junior assistants.

I turned it over. Inside, wedged in the torn corner, was a strip of paper, water-stained and soft. I smoothed it out against my knee.

Numbers. Elevations, in a hand I almost recognized, written fast and pressed hard enough to tear the paper in two places. A chain of figures running down the page and then stopping, mid-line, as though the writer had been interrupted. And at the bottom, a single word that might have been *kilo* or might have been *kill*, and beneath it, faint as breath, the initials that I had first seen on a sealed map in a city office, the initials I had been carrying up this river like a stone in my boot:

*J.O.*

My father's initials. In a hand I knew better than my own, because I had learned mine by copying his.

I sat down on the old formation, in the ruins of the line he had surveyed, and held that scrap of paper, and for a moment the forest was very loud and very far away at the same time.

* * *

They came back at the regrouping hour. Elias's party had found nothing but a duiker track and, in Elias's phrase, "a magnificent absence of evidence." Ngozi's boats came back with a wet rope, a broken fish trap that wasn't ours, and no bodies, which she reported with a briskness that told me she had expected worse and was not going to say so.

I showed them the satchel in my tent, because some things should not be discussed in a circle of ten frightened men.

Elias looked at the initials for a long time. Too long. I watched him look, and I watched the half-second where his face did something complicated before it settled into concern, and I thought: *there*. There it is. The same half-second I had seen in the survey office when I mentioned my father's name, in the depot clerk when he stamped my orders, in every official face between the coast and here when the old line came up. A flicker, like a lamp before it steadies.

"You recognize the hand," I said.

"I recognize that it's old," Elias said. "Paper like this doesn't survive twenty years in this climate. Look at it. It's soft as cloth. This has been out here—what, a season? Two?"

"Then someone has been carrying my father's handwriting around this valley recently," I said. "Which I find even more interesting."

"Or someone wrote it recently and wanted it to look old," Ngozi said.

We all looked at her.

"Tea," she said. "Strong tea, rubbed in, dried in the sun. Or river water and ash. My mother could age a letter so well you'd swear your grandmother wrote it. She did it to get a man out of a debt once." She shrugged. "Paper lies easily. It's one of its best qualities."

"Then we have a forged document," Elias said, "found at the site where two men have vanished, and we are speculating about forgers instead of organizing a proper search. With respect, Miss Okonkwo—Ada—we should widen the sweep. The villages downstream—"

"There are no villages downstream on the official map," I said.

"No," Elias said, "but there are villages."

There was a small silence. It was the first time he had admitted, out loud, that the official map and the country were different documents, and I noted it, the way I noted everything about Elias Vance now: carefully, and against a future need.

"Joseph says the ground talks," I said. "He says no one passed the camp in the night without waking him. Ten men heard nothing, saw nothing. Two men and a crate weighing sixty pounds walked out of a camp where the ground talks, and left stones at the corners of the place where the crate sat." I looked at the stones again through the tent flap. "Stones at the corners. That's not how thieves mark a theft. That's how someone marks a *boundary*. A claim. This is ours now. Stay behind this line."

"You've been reading too many survey reports," Elias said, but he said it without much conviction, and I noticed that his eyes had gone, more than once, to the pay chest's empty rectangle of grass.

"Yes," I said. "I've been reading too many survey reports. That is, in fact, my entire problem."

* * *

That evening the camp cooked and ate in a silence with a new flavor. It was not the silence of grief—not yet; grief requires a body, or at least a certainty. It was the silence of ten men doing arithmetic. Ten porters to carry twelve loads. Two men gone, and the pay chest gone with them, and every man in the line wondering the same thing I was wondering, which was: if the chest is gone, what happens to our pay? And underneath that, quieter, the question nobody would say: *who is next?*

I walked the line at dusk and spoke to each man by name. It is a small thing, a name, but I had learned it from watching my father, who never passed a track gang without knowing the foreman's name and his children's names and which one had the bad foot. Names were how he held a crew together. I had thought, for years, that it was a softness in him, an engineer's sentimentality. Walking that line of frightened men, I understood it was engineering of the most fundamental kind. A crew is a structure. Fear is a load. You distribute it or it finds the weak point and everything comes down.

Joseph walked with me.

"The two who went," he said, when we were out of earshot of the others. "You want to know what I think."

"I always want to know what you think, Joseph. I just can't always afford it."

That earned half a smile. "Musa had a brother. Downriver. In the second year after the line closed, men came and took the brother—conscription for the road gangs, the ones they don't put in the reports. Musa never saw him again. Musa took work on this river because the river goes where the road gangs went, and he kept looking." He paused. "Daniel was Musa's sister's son. He came on this trip because Musa asked him. Musa said—" Joseph stopped, choosing the words with care. "Musa said the river was going to give something back this year. He said a man had come upriver asking questions in the dry season, a company man, and had paid Musa to watch, and to carry a message if the right moment came. Musa never said what the message was. But Musa has been sleeping badly since the radio went quiet. Like a man waiting for an appointment."

The firelight was behind us and the river was a black seam of noise below, and I stood there with the cold coming up off the water and felt the last of the day's certainty drain out of me.

"A company man paid him," I said. "Before this expedition. Before me."

"Before the boats were even built," Joseph said. "And Miss—" he hesitated over the name, then used it plainly, which from Joseph was a kind of gift, "—Miss Ada. The satchel you found. The stones at the corners. Musa is a careful man. Careful men leave things where they are meant to be found."

I looked back at the camp. Elias was standing at the edge of the firelight, and I could not read his face at that distance, and I realized that this was the true damage of the day—not the missing men, not the crate, not even the pay chest, but this: that I now looked at every face on this expedition and saw a door I could not open.

"Someone in this party is lying," I said. It came out flat, and it sounded, in my own ears, exactly like my father.

"Yes," Joseph said, without any surprise at all. "Someone has been lying since the coast. The river knew it before you did. That is why Ngozi would not take the contract until she had met you twice." He turned back toward the fire. "Ask her about the second meeting sometime. But not tonight. Tonight, post a watch on the boats, and sleep with your father's papers under your head, not in the survey box. The box has a lock. Locks tell a thief where to look."

He walked back toward the fire, an old man moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who has survived every kind of weather, and I stood a while longer in the dark with the river talking its endless talk below me.

Two men gone. A crate gone. A satchel that belonged to none of them, carrying my father's initials and half a sentence in a hand twenty years dead, aged with tea, or not aged at all. Stones set at the corners like survey markers. And somewhere in the firelight behind me, a man with polished boots and sealed orders, watching the water with the patience of someone who has already read the last page.

I went and woke Ngozi.

"I need to know," I said, "about the second time I met you."

She looked up at me from her blankets, and in the light of the coals her face was unreadable as water, and then, slowly, she smiled—not kindly, but like a river deciding, at last, to show you the rock it had been hiding all along.

"Sit down," she said. "This will take until the fire dies."
