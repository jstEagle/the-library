## 9. Her Father's Numbers

There is a particular kind of insomnia that comes from arithmetic. Not the anxious kind—the kind where your brain, denied the satisfaction of an answer, simply keeps turning the crank long after you've told it to go to bed. I had it that night in the camp above the gorge, lying under canvas with the river muttering its one long syllable below, and my father's survey book open on my chest like a sleeping cat I couldn't bring myself to disturb.

I had told Elias I was checking elevations for the resurvey. That was true, in the way that a confession is technically true if you leave out the interesting parts. The company's standing orders required us to verify the original line's benchmarks wherever the old track bed was passable, and we had passed three of them since the village—brass caps set into stone, green with twenty years of moss, each stamped with a benchmark number and a year. Each stamped, too, with initials.

A.O.

My father's initials. My own, as it happened. When I was nine I thought it was marvelous that we shared them, that somewhere out in the world there were brass discs wearing his name and mine together, patient as coins at the bottom of a wishing well. I had not thought about those discs in fifteen years. Now I'd spent two days crouched over them with a level and a stadia rod while the porters watched me with the polite patience of men who had seen many engineers kneel before small metal gods.

The numbers were wrong.

Not badly wrong. That was the thing that kept me awake. If they had been badly wrong—if the old man's levels were off by meters—I could have shrugged and blamed the instruments of 1901, the heat, the haste, the ordinary sloppiness of a rushed colonial survey. But they weren't sloppy. They were wrong by centimeters, in a consistent direction, along a consistent gradient, which is the signature not of error but of intention. Error scatters. Intention walks in a straight line.

Here is what you must understand about elevation figures on a railway survey: they are destiny. A published grade determines how much water a valley holds behind a proposed dam; it determines whether a tunnel can be bored level; it determines, downstream and decades later, whose rice fields drown and whose churchyard becomes a fishery. An engineer who fudges an elevation is not making a mistake. He is casting a vote about the future, quietly, in a language only other engineers can read.

And my father had voted. Three times that I'd confirmed, and I suspected everywhere the old line ran.

* * *

I did the work by lamplight after the camp slept, because I did not want an audience. There are discoveries you make in public and discoveries you make alone, and this one belonged to the second category, the way grief does.

The company's published figures lived in the route book Elias carried—the official one, printed in the capital on good paper, the version that had been taught to apprentices like me as settled fact. My father's field book held his own reductions: raw staff readings, instrument heights, collimation checks, worked through in his cramped left-slanting hand that I would have known blindfolded, the way you know a voice through a wall.

I laid the two sets side by side on my knee and reduced them again myself, third party, referee, daughter. I checked his arithmetic twice because I wanted so badly to find a mistake. A mistake would have been a mercy. It would have meant my father was merely human, merely tired, merely the same fallible man who had died owing money across half the coast.

No mistake. His sums were beautiful. They always had been—he used to do compound interest in his head at the dinner table to amuse me, and I had thought, being nine, that this was what fathers were for.

The discrepancy ran like this: the published figures placed the valley floor at the upper end of the old alignment roughly four meters higher than his field book said it stood. Four meters. Over the length of the catchment above the tunnel works, compounded across every tributary the maps showed, four meters of invented height translated into a reservoir that would sit—on paper—comfortably below the lowest village gardens. On paper, the flood line kissed nobody. On paper, the dam was a benefaction.

In his field book, the true ground sat lower. In his field book, the water backed up past the gardens, past the graves, past everything Ngozi's mother's people had built in the time their stories took to tell.

He hadn't inflated anything. That was the part I kept turning over, the crank that wouldn't stop. Sabotage, as I understood it from the engineering school case studies, meant exaggerating costs, understating capacity, cooking the books until a project died of its own bad numbers. My father had done the opposite. He had made the land look *higher* than it was—made the valley look shallower, safer, more buildable—and then, apparently, walked away from the project he had helped make look feasible.

It made no sense. Unless.

Unless the falsification wasn't meant to kill the line. It was meant to bait it. To make the scheme look irresistible on paper, get it approved, get crews upriver, get the tunnel started—and then let the truth surface at exactly the moment when abandoning it would cost the company the most. A trap built out of decimals. You could not accuse a man of sabotage who had, on paper, flattered you. You could only discover, too late, that his flattery had teeth.

Or—and this was the colder possibility, the one I kept in my pocket like a stone—my father had falsified the figures for money. Someone had paid him to make the valley look buildable, he had taken the payment, and then his conscience or his cowardice had made him abandon the works and vanish into debt and silence. The debt ledger I carried weighed on me differently depending on which story I told. In one, the debts were the price of principle. In the other, they were just debts, and I had spent my whole life honoring a man who didn't deserve it.

The lamp guttered. Out on the black water something slapped the surface once, twice, and went quiet, the way the river does when it's listening.

* * *

Morning found me short-tempered, which the others received as normal, since morning generally found me short-tempered.

"Your father's benchmarks," Elias said over the porridge pot, casual as a man discussing weather. He had a gift for sounding idle when he was fishing. "You seemed very absorbed yesterday."

"Standing orders," I said. "You wrote them."

"I write many things." He spooned porridge with the unhurried elegance of a man who had never once eaten standing up in the rain. "Curious, though. The old figures. I compared a few against our readings."

My stomach performed a small cold maneuver. "And?"

"And they're within tolerance." He smiled. "Remarkable work, for the instruments of the day. Your father was precise."

"Within tolerance" — I turned the phrase over all day as we moved camp up the overgrown cutting, machetes going ahead of us like a slow mechanical heartbeat. Within tolerance. Of course they were within tolerance. Four meters spread across a forty-kilometer alignment, distributed in centimeter shavings at every benchmark, was precisely engineered to sit inside any tolerance a checking engineer would bother applying. You would only catch it the way I had caught it—not by spot-checking but by reducing the whole run end to end and letting the accumulated bias show itself as a drift, a slow lean in the numbers, like a house settling. Like a lie told in installments.

Elias had spot-checked. Either he genuinely hadn't seen it—which would have required him to be a far worse engineer than his polish suggested—or he had seen it and was watching to see what I would do.

I thought about the sealed orders he wouldn't discuss. I thought about the way headquarters had sent him, a senior man, to shepherd a disgraced junior up a dead line nobody cared about. Companies do not spend senior men on nothing.

So I did what any sensible person does with a secret: I acquired a second one to keep it company. I began copying my father's field book.

Not the whole thing—I wasn't a fool, and paper is heavy—but the reduction sheets, the critical runs, compressed into my own shorthand in a notebook I kept inside my shirt, against my ribs, where the ledger of debts used to ride before I'd transferred it to the strongbox. If the boats sank, if the porters scattered, if Elias turned out to be exactly what his cufflinks suggested, the numbers would survive as long as I did. It occurred to me, pressing the pencil down hard enough to emboss the page beneath, that I was doing exactly what my father had done: carrying dangerous arithmetic close to the skin, telling no one, trusting no one, walking around with a weapon disguised as homework.

I wondered if it had made him feel safe. It did not make me feel safe. It made me feel, for the first time since the depot at the coast, awake.

* * *

Ngozi found me at dusk at the water's edge, where I was washing the lamp soot off my hands and pretending to admire the sunset like a person without a care.

"You sleep like a woman arguing," she said, dropping onto the rock beside me. She said it the way she said most things—as an observation about the natural world, delivered without malice and without much hope of reply. "Talking all night. Numbers, numbers." She tapped her temple. "The river doesn't argue with itself. That's why it wins."

"Do you ever turn it off?" I asked. "The commentary."

"No. Why would I? Commentary is free." She squinted upstream, where the gorge walls had begun to go violet with the last light. "You found something in the old man's book."

It wasn't a question, and I considered lying to her, and then considered what lying to Ngozi would cost me, and decided I couldn't afford it. "Maybe."

"Maybe." She nodded slowly, granting the word its dignity. "The villagers at the last camp—they said your father's name before you told them. You noticed."

"I noticed."

"They also said a word I didn't translate for you." She picked up a flat stone and skimmed it—one, two, three skips, gone. "I don't translate everything. Some things should be asked for properly." She stood, brushed off her trousers, and looked down at me with the frank appraisal she probably used on rapids. "When you're ready to ask, ask. But Ada—" she almost never used my name, and it landed strangely, warmly, "—whatever number you found, remember the water already knows the height of its own valley. Paper only argues. Water decides."

She walked back toward the fires, and I stayed on the rock a while longer, holding my father's field book against my knees, feeling the full absurdity of my position: a disgraced engineer, wanted by no one, trusted by less than no one, sitting on the banks of a river that had allegedly already made up its mind, guarding a dead man's decimals as though they were gold.

They were better than gold. Gold buys silence. These numbers, arranged correctly, could buy justice—or ruin, depending on who read them first and what they chose to see.

Because here was the question I had been circling all day like a bird over a carcass, and now I finally set it down and looked at it straight: if my father had falsified the survey to protect the valley, why had he left the true figures in his own field book, in his own hand, dated and initialed? A man hiding evidence burns it. A man keeping evidence keeps it *findable*. He had written the truth down carefully, beautifully, and then buried it in a dead man's expedition gear and died owing everyone money, leaving the bill—and the book—to me.

That wasn't concealment. That was a letter. A letter addressed to whoever came up this river asking questions, written in the only language he trusted completely.

Me. He had addressed it to me. Or to anyone like me, which amounted to the same thing, because there was no one else like me, God help us both.

I closed the book. Upstream, past the dark shoulder of the gorge, the tunnel works waited—half-built, abandoned mid-breath, holding whatever my father had left there when the official record said he'd already gone home. The maintenance log fragment we'd heard rumors of in the village. The charges never fired. And whatever else a man keeps in a hole in a mountain when he has decided the mountain matters more than his name.

Tomorrow we would reach it. And I was beginning to understand that I hadn't come up this river to reopen a line at all.

I had come to finish reading a letter.
