## 10. Confrontation in the Rain

The storm came down the valley like something with an appointment to keep.

We heard it before we saw it—a long rolling grumble upstream, the sound of the river's older, angrier cousin—and Ngozi stood up in the bow of the lead boat and said, "Canvas. Now." Not shouted. Just said, in the tone of a woman informing you that gravity exists and you are about to become acquainted with it personally.

We made camp on a shelf of gravel above the waterline, and by the time the last guy-line was pegged, the rain arrived—not falling so much as arriving sideways, in sheets, as if the sky had decided to empty itself all at once rather than be bothered with the usual procedure. The river rose visibly while we watched it, brown water climbing the gravel in greedy increments, dragging whole trees past us like matchsticks it had grown bored of.

Twelve of us crowded under two canvas flies that sagged under the weight of water they were never designed to hold. The porters played cards by lamplight, laughing at intervals with the particular cheerfulness of men who had seen worse weather and intended to make sure we knew it. Ngozi sat at the edge of the shelter smoking, watching the river with the expression of a landlady watching a tenant she doesn't trust.

And Elias Vance sat across from me, drying his spectacles on his shirttail, looking—there was no other word for it—composed. Composed the way a locked drawer is composed.

I had been turning it over for three days. The radio static, the garbled replies from base camp, the way he'd gone quiet when I mentioned the frequency drift, the way he checked the wireless set each morning like a man checking a wound he didn't want anyone else to see. And underneath all of it, the thing I kept circling back to: my father's numbers, which didn't lie, and the company's numbers, which did, and the question of who had sent this polished, patient man upriver with me and why.

A man like Elias Vance did not get assigned to disgraced engineers. A man like Elias Vance got assigned *to watch* disgraced engineers.

"Elias," I said, over the drumming of the rain. "Tell me what's in your orders."

The card game behind us didn't stop, but it got quieter, the way a room gets quieter when everyone is listening without appearing to.

He finished with his spectacles and put them back on, unhurried, and gave me the smile he used for cocktail parties I had never been invited to. "My orders are the same as yours, Ada. Survey the line, assess the tunnel works, report on feasibility."

"Base camp stopped answering us two days ago."

"Atmospherics. This time of year—"

"I grew up on radio sets. My father repaired them for pocket money." I leaned forward. "Atmospheric interference doesn't answer in code groups. It doesn't go silent in patterns. And it certainly doesn't explain why you recalibrated the set yourself last Tuesday at four in the morning, when you thought everyone was asleep."

That landed. I watched it land—the small stillness in him, the half-second where the smile stayed on but nothing behind it moved. Around us the rain hammered the canvas, and the lamp guttered, and somewhere out in the dark the river ate another yard of our gravel shelf.

"Ada," he said gently, "you're exhausted. We all are. This weather plays tricks—"

"Don't." My voice came out harder than I'd planned, and I found I didn't mind. "Don't manage me. I've spent two years being managed by men who thought I couldn't handle the truth about my own career. I can survive the truth about anything else. Try me."

Ngozi flicked her cigarette into the rain, where it died with a hiss, and turned around. She didn't say anything. She just looked at Elias, arms folded, and waited, and there was a great deal of speech in that waiting.

Elias looked from her to me, and for a moment—I will always believe this—he considered telling me everything right then. Something moved through his face like weather of its own. Then the moment passed, and he said, "I'm not hiding anything that affects your work or your safety. That's all I can say tonight."

"*Can't*," I corrected him. "You mean can't. There's a difference, and we both know which one applies."

"Ada—"

"Here's what I know," I said, and I stood, because sitting down felt like conceding ground. "I know the company closed this line twenty years ago and sealed the maps. I know my father's initials are on the original survey and nobody in that office would meet my eye when I asked why. I know two of our porters vanished near the old cutting with a company satchel, and I know the satchel was torn open by someone looking for paper, not food. Paper, Elias. Who carries paper worth searching for, up a river nobody wants us on?"

The cards had stopped entirely now. Old Musa, the eldest of the porters, was watching me with an expression I couldn't read—or perhaps could, and didn't like.

"And now I find," I went on, quieter, because the anger was burning down into something colder and more useful, "that the one man on this expedition who reports directly to headquarters, who has a wireless set and sealed envelopes and a talent for being awake at inconvenient hours, tells me there's nothing I need to worry about." I shook my head. "I've heard that sentence before. It's what they said at the inquiry. 'Nothing you need to concern yourself with, Miss Okonkwo.' It's the sentence they use right before they decide things about you without you."

The rain filled the silence. Elias didn't look away, I'll give him that. He held my eyes across the lamplight, and whatever war was going on inside him, he kept it off his face with a discipline I might have admired under other circumstances.

"You think I'm here against you," he said finally.

"I think you're here for something, and it isn't me."

Ngozi snorted. It was not a delicate sound.

"Miss Okonkwo asks a fair question," she said, in her flat river voice, the one that carried over rapids without effort. "I have run this water since I was small enough to fall in it. I know every village, every sandbar, every place the old rail crews bled. And I will tell you what I told the company man who hired me: no one comes up this river twice unless the river wants them to. So either Mr. Vance is here because the river wants him—" she shrugged elaborately— "or he is here because someone with more money than sense sent him. And rivers do not send letters ahead."

"That's very poetic," Elias said.

"It's not poetry. It's hydrology." She turned back to the river. "I side with the engineer. Whatever he's carrying, we should know it before it drowns us. Secrets weigh more than cargo. I have seen boats sink of them."

* * *

I did not sleep. I lay in my tent listening to the rain ease from fury to conversation, and I took out my father's compass and held it in both hands the way I used to when I was nine years old and he let me navigate the walk home from the yards, pretending the streets were track alignments and I was the surveyor of everything.

"Numbers don't lie," he used to say. "People do. That's why we check people against numbers."

Well, Papa. I had his numbers. What I needed was his reasons, and those were buried somewhere upstream, in a tunnel full of charges that had never been fired, in a maintenance log dated after the world was told the line was dead.

Through the canvas I heard Elias's tent flap move. Footsteps on wet gravel, pausing. Then moving on toward the wireless tent, unhurried, deliberate.

I gave him ten minutes. Then I followed.

* * *

From Elias Vance's journal, that same night:

She followed me. Of course she did. I heard her before I saw her—one misstep on the wet stone, quickly corrected—and I could have called out, could have ended the whole miserable theater. Instead I sat down at the wireless table and pretended to adjust a dial, and let her stand in the doorway of the tent with the lamplight behind her, and hated myself with a thoroughness that would have impressed my instructors.

Because here is the truth I could not say under the canvas, with twelve pairs of ears and the rain for a witness: headquarters does not care whether the highland line reopens. Headquarters cares that *no one else* reaches the source first. There is a rival consortium—there is always a rival consortium—smelling the same mineral reports we smell, and the Director's instruction to me, in the envelope I burned the ashes of reading, was plain: reach the headwaters before any survey party flying other colors, establish presence, and document the old Okonkwo survey's discrepancies *in situ*. Document them. Verify them. Make them official.

What happens to a dead man's name once the company makes his errors official, I did not need spelled out. Neither, I suspect, did the Director. He chose me for this job, I realize now, not despite my conscience but because of its precise dimensions—large enough to be reliable, small enough to be managed.

And then there is Ada Okonkwo herself, standing in a doorway in the rain with her chin up and her father's stubbornness written all over her, asking me questions I have answers to and am not permitted to give. I have worked with competent engineers. I have worked with brilliant ones. I have never worked with anyone who reads water the way she read it in the gorges, or numbers the way she read her father's—like a language she was born speaking and someone tried to take away. When she accused me tonight, in front of everyone, part of me wanted to applaud. The rest of me wanted to resign.

These two parts of me have been arguing since the coast. Tonight, for the first time, the argument is not going well for duty.

She spoke from the doorway. "Four in the morning again, Mr. Vance?"

"Insomnia," I said. "It's catching, apparently."

She came in without invitation—that was always going to happen—and looked at the set, at the logbook beside it, at me. "I'm going to ask you one more time, and then I'm going to stop asking and start acting. What did base camp tell you before they went silent?"

"Ada—"

"Because I've done the arithmetic," she said. "Two porters gone, a satchel torn open for paper, a radio dying in patterns, and a route that closes in six weeks. Someone knows where we're going and what we'll find there. And whoever it is, they aren't upstream. They're in this camp." Her voice didn't rise at all, which frightened me considerably more than shouting would have. "If you're not the one lying to me, Elias, then help me. Because if you are the one lying to me, I would genuinely rather know now, while I still think well enough of you to feel it."

There it was. Laid out on the table between the wireless set and the rain: the choice, in plain language, from a woman who had no idea how plainly she had just described the exact fork in my own road.

I almost told her. God help me, I opened my mouth to tell her everything—the consortium, the Director, the order to verify her father's disgrace. And then I thought of what telling her would cost her *now*, mid-river, with the dry season coming and half our supplies already spoken for: a crew split down the middle, a pilot who would turn the boats around, a mission abandoned within sight of the answer she had given up everything to chase. Telling her the whole truth tonight would not free her. It would strand her.

So I gave her the sliver. It was the best I could do and the worst I have ever felt.

"There are interests in the capital," I said, "who want this valley surveyed before anyone else reaches it. That much is true. Whether their interest in the old line is engineering or something else—I don't know yet. But I swear to you, Ada, on whatever you'll accept an oath on: I am not here to harm you, and I am not working against your father's memory. When I can tell you the rest, I will. Not before. You have my word."

She studied me for a long moment, rain dripping off her hair onto the floorboards, and I understood that she was deciding something, and that I was not going to enjoy the decision.

"Your word," she repeated. "That's a currency I can't currently verify. But fine. Here's mine: I'm watching you, Elias. Every dial you touch, every envelope you burn, every hour you can't sleep. And if I find out your secrets put my people in danger—Musa, Ngozi, any of them—then whatever's between us stops mattering, and I will open those orders myself if I have to take them off your body to do it."

She left. The rain swallowed her footsteps.

I sat at the wireless a long while afterward, logging a signal check I did not perform, and wrote in the margin of my private notebook a sentence I have crossed out three times and rewritten each time the same:

*I am no longer certain whose engineer I am.*

* * *

Morning came grey and dripping, the storm spent, the river swollen and fast and glittering with broken light. We struck camp in silence that wasn't hostile exactly, but wasn't comfortable either—an armed truce of canvas and rope.

It was Ngozi who broke it, standing at the water's edge, pointing upstream to where the gorge narrowed into white noise.

"Rain's filled the river early," she said. "That's good for running the next reach—three days of good water, maybe four. After that, the levels drop and the rocks come up to meet you." She looked at me, then at Elias, who stood a careful six feet from us both. "Three days to the tunnel works. Then we see what your fathers and your companies were really doing up here."

She stepped into the boat and took the tiller, and the current took us, and none of us looked back at the camp where the truth had spent the night just out of reach.

But I noticed—as I meant to notice everything now—that Elias checked the wireless tent one last time before boarding, and that when he emerged, his jacket pocket was lighter than it had been.

Whatever he'd burned in the night, he'd burned it close to the fire ring.

I marked the spot.
