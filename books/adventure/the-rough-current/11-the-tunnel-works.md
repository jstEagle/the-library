## 11. The Tunnel Works

The river told us before the map did.

Ngozi had been quiet all morning—not her usual quiet, which was a weapon she kept sharpened for my benefit, but a listening quiet, head tilted like a woman hearing a conversation through a wall. Twice she backed water and held us against the current while she studied the bank, and twice she shook her head and pushed on. On the third try she grunted, that particular grunt I had learned to translate as *there*, and swung the boat toward a shelf of gravel below a wall of green so dense it looked poured rather than grown.

"There's nothing marked here," Elias said, consulting his chart with the confidence of a man who trusted paper over water.

"That's because your mapmakers asked the river politely," Ngozi said, "and the river lied to them."

She was in one of her moods. Since the porters vanished—six days now, six days of searching cuttings and questioning fishermen and finding nothing but a torn satchel and our own growing suspicion of each other—she had spoken to me in short sentences and to Elias in shorter ones. The crew felt it. Fear is a cargo that redistributes itself; you can feel it shifting from man to man as the boat heels.

We tied off under the green wall, and Ngozi parted the vegetation with her machete the way other people part a curtain, revealing not jungle at all but a cutting—man-made, old, the rock faces scarred with drill marks furred over by two decades of moss. A rail bed ran into the dark ahead of us, ties rotted to black lace, rails gone where scrap crews had pried them up, but the grade itself still there, level as a promise.

"The tunnel works," I said. My voice came out strange, and I realized why: I'd been saying it in my father's voice, in the tone he used reading survey reports aloud at the kitchen table, back when I was small enough to think a railroad was a kind of religion.

"You knew?" Elias said.

"I knew there was a tunnel on the original alignment. Eleven hundred feet through the spur." I stepped onto the ballast, and my boots crunched on stone that had been placed by hands, graded by hands, and then left. "I didn't know it looked like this."

Like what? Like a thing interrupted mid-sentence. The cutting was finished. The drainage ditches were cut and functioning—twenty years of rain had not washed them out, which meant someone had maintained them. The portal ahead stood framed in dressed stone, and set into the keystone, weathered nearly smooth, I could just make out letters: an inscription, company-style, the kind they carved into every major work. I wiped the stone with my sleeve.

*OKONKWO CUT — 19—*

My father's name. Not his initials this time, not the J.O. I'd found on the sealed maps in the survey office. His full name, carved into the mountain, and then the date chiseled off. Someone had taken a cold chisel to the year and hammered it into anonymity.

Nobody spoke. Even Ngozi, who had a remark for every occasion including funerals, said nothing. The river talked behind us, indifferent, and somewhere in the green a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"Well," I said finally, because someone had to, and wry is what I do instead of weeping. "Either my father was very important here, or somebody went to a great deal of trouble to make him unimportant. Both options interest me."

* * *

The portal breathed cool air at us, that particular tunnel breath—mineral and wet and older than men. We went in with three lamps between the four of us who were going: myself, Elias, Ngozi, and Musa, the eldest porter, who had appointed himself our shadow since the disappearances and refused, flatly, to let any of us go anywhere without a witness. I suspected Musa thought we were the ones likely to vanish next, and wanted to be able to describe it accurately afterward.

The track bed ran straight for two hundred feet and then the tunnel simply stopped being a tunnel and became a cave—the heading abandoned mid-drive, the face rough where the drills had last bitten. No collapse. No flooding. No geological reason written in the rock for stopping. They had simply stopped, the way a sentence stops when the speaker is dragged out of the room.

And stacked against the wall beside the face, neat as books on a shelf: crates.

Elias got to them first, because Elias always gets to things first; privilege is mostly a matter of walking speed. He pried one open with his knife. Inside, packed in straw gone to brown dust, lay sticks of blasting gelatin, sweating slightly, wrapped in waxed paper printed with a manufacturer's name I recognized from the corps' own stores.

"Gelatin doesn't keep twenty years," Elias said. "Not safely."

"Then these aren't twenty years old," I said.

He looked at me. I looked at him. Behind us, Musa said something rapid and unhappy in his own language, and Ngozi translated without being asked, which was unlike her: "He says the spirits of the tunnel are watching us. He says we should take what we came for and go."

"What did we come for?" Ngozi asked me, and it was the first real question she'd aimed at me in days, and it landed like a drill bit.

"I don't know yet," I said. "But my father did."

Because here is what I could not stop seeing: the crates were stacked, not dumped. Stacked implies intention. Intention implies return visits. And gelatin that sweats is gelatin less than five years old, which meant someone had been coming back to this dead tunnel within living memory, within *recent* memory, keeping charges stocked in a heading the company had officially closed before I finished school.

There was a maintenance log nailed to a post driven into the ballast—a tin box, rust-streaked, its hasp green with age. My hands were steady opening it, which surprised me. I had expected them to shake. Perhaps I'd used up all my shaking on the night the porters vanished, standing in the dark listening to a river that sounded like whispering.

Inside the box: a ledger, canvas-bound, swollen with damp, and a stub of pencil tied to it with wire.

I opened it under the lamp, and the world narrowed to a page.

* * *

You have to understand what my father's handwriting means to me. It is the handwriting of Sunday afternoons and arithmetic drills, of columns of figures he made me check "because an engineer who can't check his own work deserves whatever befalls him," of grocery lists and the ledger of debts I carry now like a second spine. I know his hand the way Ngozi knows her water. I would know it upside down, in the dark, dissolved halfway to illegibility.

This was his hand.

*Maintained drainage, lower heading. Cleared fall at chainage 340. Replaced two ties (spare stock, north cache). Charges inspected, dry. All well.*

Dated fourteen months ago.

Fourteen months ago my father was alive. Fourteen months ago my father was, according to every document the company possessed, a disgraced engineer living two hundred miles downriver, paying off a debt in installments, a broken man who had once surveyed a line and then watched it die. Fourteen months ago my father was walking into this tunnel with fresh explosives and spare ties and a pencil, writing *all well* in a log nobody was supposed to find, in a place the company had erased from its maps.

"All well," I read aloud, because if I didn't say it, it wasn't true yet. "He wrote 'all well.' Fourteen months ago. He was *here*."

"Ada." Elias's voice had gone careful, the way voices go around a frightened animal or a loaded gun. "Read the earlier entries."

I turned pages backward through time. The entries thickened as they aged—weekly, then monthly, then seasonal. The oldest entries were in a different hand, official, clipped: progress reports from the drive, powder consumption, ground conditions. Then, abruptly, the official hand stopped, and after a gap of some months, my father's began, alone, unexplained, continuing a conversation with an empty tunnel.

The last entry was not about maintenance.

*Charges sufficient. If they come to finish it, they will need everything I've stored. If they come to flood it, God forgive me, they will find the valley harder to drown than their figures say. The source gives more than they know. I have measured it. I will measure it again.*

Below that, no signature. Just a string of numbers, a grid reference in the old system, the coordinates running up toward the highlands. Toward the source.

The lamp flame leaned in a draft from the abandoned face, and for one absurd moment I felt my father standing behind me in the dark, reading over my shoulder, waiting to see if I'd check his figures properly.

"Chainage," I managed. "He logged maintenance at chainage markers going all the way—" I stopped. Recounted. "Elias, the entries run past the tunnel. Past the end of the line. There are entries referencing locations forty miles upstream of here. Above the falls."

"Nobody surveys above the falls," Ngozi said flatly. "The falls are the end of the country. After the falls is the beginning of it, and those are different things."

"He surveyed it anyway. In secret. For years." I closed the ledger and held it against my chest like something warm-blooded. "The debt. The disgrace. All of it—it was never about money. He bought his silence with the debt. He let them ruin his name so they'd stop watching him, and then he came up here and kept working."

"Working toward what?" Elias asked.

And there it was, the question I'd been circling for eleven chapters of my life without knowing it. I thought of the sealed maps in the survey office, the officials who wouldn't meet my eye, the way the word *closed* had been used about this line as though it were a wound they preferred not to discuss. I thought of the chiseled-off date on the keystone. You erase a man's name from a tunnel when you want the tunnel forgotten. You forget a tunnel when remembering it costs you something.

"Toward the source," I said. "Whatever's at the source, he measured it, and the measurement frightened someone enough to bury him. Enough to bury the whole line."

Musa touched my elbow—gently, apologetically—and pointed upward. I hadn't noticed the light changing. At the far end of the abandoned heading, high in the roof where the drillers had broken through into softer strata, a shaft of daylight fell through a natural chimney, and in that shaft hung dust, turning slowly, gold-colored, like the tunnel was full of slow golden bees.

Ngozi looked at the light a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge entirely.

"My mother's people say the river begins in a house," she said. "A stone house under the mountain, with a door. They say the door was shut long ago, and the water has been looking for a way out ever since, and that is why the river argues." She turned to me. "Your father was measuring the door."

* * *

We camped that night inside the portal mouth, out of the rain that arrived at dusk with theatrical timing. The porters built fires on the old ballast and the flames lit the dressed stone of the archway, and I sat apart with the ledger under an oilcloth, copying the coordinate string into my notebook, checking my father's arithmetic the way he taught me, because grief needs a task or it eats you.

Elias came and sat beside me without asking. That was new. Elias Vance did not sit anywhere without first establishing that sitting there was advantageous.

"The charges," he said quietly. "Fresh gelatin, primers, det cord. Stored correctly. That's not a memorial, Ada. That's a working magazine. Somebody intended to blast here again."

"Somebody being my father."

"Somebody being your father, recently." He paused. "Or somebody being whoever killed the radio and took our porters."

I had known he'd say it eventually. I'd been carrying the thought myself since the satchel, since the static, since the morning count came up two men short, and I hated him a little for saying it aloud, and respected him more for not dressing it up.

"You think my father's alive," I said. "Fourteen months since the last entry. You think he's up there, blowing boats and stealing crates."

"I think the last entry isn't necessarily the last visit. I think a man who hid a twenty-year secret from an entire colonial administration is capable of hiding himself from four boats' worth of strangers." He fed a stick to the fire. "I also think it's possible the porters weren't taken. I think it's possible they were met."

"Met by whom?"

"By whoever taught them the path through the falls country that isn't on any map. By whoever paid them in advance—we both saw the coin Musa found in the satchel. Company mint, Ada. New strike." He looked at me across the firelight, and his face, usually so polished, had cracks in it I hadn't seen before. "Someone from the company reached this valley before we did. Or someone from the company never left it."

The rain drummed on the oilcloth. Down at the landing, the river argued with the dark, patient, endless, a sound I was beginning to understand the way you understand a person you've argued with for years—by knowing exactly which silences mean danger.

"If my father is alive," I said slowly, "then the debt is a lie. The whole ruin of him was staged. Which means the company knows it was staged, which means—" I stopped, because the arithmetic ran off the page into a country I didn't want to enter.

"Which means they let you believe he was ruined," Elias finished softly. "They let you carry it. They sent you upriver carrying it." He hesitated, and I saw him decide something, weigh something, put something down. "Ada, when we reach the source—"

A shot cracked through the rain.

One shot, from up the cutting, from the direction of the old rail bed where the pickets stood watch. Then shouting—Musa's voice, and others', and the specific chaos of men waking into fear. The porters scrambled for the fires. Ngozi was already moving, machete in hand, moving the way water moves, and I was moving too, ledger stuffed inside my shirt against my heart like the organ it had become.

At the edge of the firelight, at the mouth of the cutting where the overgrown rail bed ran away north into the dark, stood a figure. Tall. Still. Rain streaming off him. Holding a lantern raised high so we could see him clearly, and wanting, apparently, to be seen.

He wore no uniform. But I knew the stance—weight balanced, chin level, lantern held at the exact regulation height for signaling a train—and I knew, before my eyes confirmed anything, before reason caught up with the blood in my ears, whose silhouette that was. I had grown up watching it bend over drawing tables. I had inherited its handwriting, its stubbornness, and its debts.

The figure set the lantern down on an old tie, deliberately, a man laying down a weapon. And then he spoke, and the rain carried his voice down the cutting to me, worn thinner than memory but unmistakable, the voice of every Sunday arithmetic lesson of my childhood:

"Ada. Check my figures. I need you to check my figures."

Behind me, Elias said my name in a voice I'd never heard him use. Ahead of me, at the end of twenty years of silence, my father waited to see whether his daughter would come to him—or run.
