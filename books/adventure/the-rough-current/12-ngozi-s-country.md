## 12. Ngozi's Country

I was born on this river, and I will tell you the first thing it taught me: the water does not care what you believe about it. Not your prayers, not your instruments, not your government's stamps and seals. The river cares about weight, and angle, and patience. Everything else is decoration.

I had run the gorge below a hundred times, but above it — above the great falls, where the water spreads out and forgets its anger for a while — I had not been since I was a girl with my mother's hands on my shoulders, teaching me the names of things. So when the channel opened up before us that morning and the current went gentle as a sleeping animal, I felt something I had not expected to feel on this job.

I felt like a stranger coming home.

The engineer woman stood at the bow of my boat with her father's compass in her fist, the way she always did when the country changed, as if the brass might tell her something the water wouldn't. Behind her, Vance sat with his notebook on his knee, writing down trees. That is not a joke. The man writes down trees. Species, height, estimated yield. I have watched him do it for three weeks. Somewhere in that notebook there is a page where our forest becomes so many board-feet, and he thinks that is a neutral thing to do.

"Slower," I told him, because he was leaning toward the gunwale to peer at something in the shallows. "You lean, the boat listens."

He straightened. He always straightens when I say that. I think he believes I am being mystical. I am not. I am being arithmetic. Boats are honest that way.

The river above the gorge is a different creature entirely. Below, it is all muscle and grudge, white water chewing on black rock. Up here it goes broad and brown-green, lazy on the surface, with the whole weight of the highlands moving underneath in slow, deep channels. A lazy river is more dangerous than an angry one, if you ask me. An angry river tells you what it intends. A lazy one lets you believe your own nonsense until the sandbar arrives.

But it was beautiful. I will give it that, and I do not give beauty away cheap. The banks stepped back into floodplains of tall grass gone gold with the season, and the hills beyond stood blue and layered like cloth folded by someone patient. Egrets worked the margins. Somewhere ahead, hidden by a bend, was my mother's village — the one the company maps call *uninhabited*, because the surveyor who drew them came through in the dry season when half the people were at the fishing camps, and because uninhabited is a convenient word if you intend to drown a place.

"You've gone quiet," the engineer said. Ada. She had a way of watching me sideways, like a person checking whether a dog was going to bite or only growl. Three weeks on the water and she still hadn't decided. Good. Let her keep checking.

"I'm reading," I said.

"Reading what?"

"The country. It's talking."

Vance looked up from his trees. "Talking."

"To people who listen," I said, and let it sit there. I am not above a little theatre. It keeps the company men off balance, and off-balance men don't lean over gunwales.

* * *

We made camp that night on a high bank above an old fish weir — a fence of driven stakes, generations old, that caught the current and the fish together. The porters knew what it was. Two of them, boys from downriver, touched it respectfully as they passed the gear up the slope. The company men walked past it like it was driftwood.

I cooked, because I always cook. There is an understanding on my boats: I take you through the water, I feed you, and in exchange nobody asks me questions before the second pot of tea. Ada had learned this rule by the second day. Vance still hadn't, which told me everything about how he had lived his life — a man who had never been anywhere that made rules he didn't write himself.

But that night it was Ada who sat down beside my fire, cross-legged, uninvited, and broke my own rule.

"The maps say this is empty country," she said.

"The maps say a lot of things." I handed her tea. If you're going to break a rule, do it holding something warm.

"My father's maps too?"

I looked at her. She had earned that question, I suppose, carrying her dead father's debts up a river that eats surveyors. But I wasn't ready to hand her the answer yet, because I was still deciding what the answer was.

"Your father's maps," I said, "say there is a village here." I pointed with the ladle at the darkening floodplain across the water. "A real one. With a name. He marked it. Do you know how many surveyors marked it?"

She didn't answer. She was one of the few company people I'd met who knew when a question wasn't looking for an answer.

"One," I said. "One. Because he asked."

* * *

My mother's people live two days further up, where the river splits around an island and the graves are on the high ground above the flood line, because my people learned long ago where the water reaches when it decides to reach. My mother is buried there. My grandmother. A row of my uncles, and one small stone I do not talk about, for a sister who got four years of the world and no more.

I had not been back in eleven years. I want you to understand that this was not an accident, and it was not laziness, and it was not — as the mission people would have it — because I had turned from my people. It was because the last time I stood at my mother's grave, I had just signed on to pilot a company survey boat up this same stretch of water, and I could not make the two facts sit together in my chest. So I chose the water, and I stayed away, and I told myself the river is my country too, which is true, and also a way of not going where it hurts.

Now here I was, bringing the company back up my own river, and the company was carrying instruments to measure it for drowning.

I had known about the dam scheme for two years. You cannot work these waters and not hear things. The talk comes down from the highlands with the traders: the company wants a reservoir, they want the gorge and the floodplain and everything the river touches between the falls and the highland line, they want a lake where my mother's people have planted millet since before anyone wrote the word *millet* in any language the company reads. The figures had come through my hands once — a pilot is sometimes asked to carry sealed packets, and I look at what I carry, because a pilot who doesn't look at what she carries is a mule, and I am not a mule.

The figures said the reservoir would stand at a certain height. I took that height and I held it in my mind against the country I knew, and I saw it the way you see a thing in a dream that is truer than waking: water over the fish weirs. Water over the millet fields. Water to the door-posts of the village, and then over the door-posts, and then the doors themselves, and the roofs becoming things that float and snag on the drowned fences. Water up the slope to the graves. My mother's grave. The small stone I do not talk about, under moving water, forever, because reservoirs do not have dry seasons. Reservoirs are rivers that have been made into prisoners, and prisoners forget nothing and forgive less.

And the company's figures had said, in the careful language of men who have learned to make drowning sound like arithmetic: *displacement of existing populations: negligible.*

Negligible. I have a good vocabulary in three languages and that word is my favorite of all of them, because it does so much work. It makes a village into a rounding error. It makes graves into a line item. My mother, negligible. My sister's four years, negligible. Somewhere in a coastal office a man wrote that word, drank his tea, and went home to his family, and I am sure he is a good father, and I have thought about him more than is healthy.

* * *

The next morning we reached the island, and I took them to the village, because I had decided — somewhere between the fire and the dawn, in that dishonest hour when you make promises you'd never make in daylight — that the engineer woman should see it. Not the company version. Not the notebook version with its board-feet and its negligible populations. The real one.

Ada stood in the canoe as we came around the bend and the village opened up before us — the houses with their steep thatch, the drying racks, the canoes pulled up along the mud in their patient rows, children already out on the sandbar shouting at the herons. She went very still. I have watched a hundred company faces see a hundred villages, and most of them do a quick sum: how much, how many, how soon. She didn't do the sum. She just looked, like a person looking at a thing that was going to matter later and already knowing it.

"You said the maps were empty," she said quietly.

"I said the maps were wrong. Empty is their word."

My mother's brother came down to the water — old Eze now, bent like a question mark, who used to carry me on his shoulders and tell me the river was a snake that had decided, out of laziness or love, to become a road. He looked at me a long time before he said my name, and when he said it, it was not entirely a welcome. I had earned that too.

"You bring them," he said, in our tongue, nodding at the boats. "You bring them up to measure."

"I bring them up to pass through," I said. "There's a difference."

"There is not," he said, "once they have the numbers." And then, because he was my uncle and because underneath the anger he was glad to see me and hated being glad: "Your mother's grave is well. The grass is cut. Come before you go on."

I went. I left the company men drinking the palm wine the headman offered them — Vance drinking his with the polite grimace of a man cataloguing a local custom, Ada drinking hers like a woman who understood that the wine was the meeting and the talking was decoration — and I walked up the slope past the millet, past the drying floors, to the graves on the high ground.

The grass was cut. He was right. It always is; someone in the village does it without being asked, the way some things get done in a country that no ledger ever records. I stood at my mother's stone and I told her, in the shorthand you use with the dead, what I was doing and whom I was bringing and that I did not yet know if I was a guide or a traitor, and that I suspected the two jobs used the same boat.

Then I looked down at the village from the height of the graves, and I did the sum myself. Not the company's sum. Mine. Water to the door-posts. Water over the roofs. I had done this sum a hundred times in my head and it came out the same every time, and I stood there and let it come out the same again, because some arithmetic you have to keep doing or you start believing the other kind.

* * *

Ada found me on the way down. She had come up the slope alone, leaving Vance arguing amiably about trade routes with the headmen, and she stood beside me at the edge of the graves with her hands in her pockets like a woman who didn't know what to do with them, which was, for her, a kind of honesty.

"Your family," she said.

"My mother. My grandmother. Uncles. A sister." I said the sister flat, and Ada, to her credit, didn't ask. She had a sister's silence in her somewhere; I'd heard it in how she talked about her father — everything important pressed down flat and carried.

"The reservoir," she said. "The height they'd flood to." She had the figures too, then. Of course she did. She'd been cross-checking her father's elevations since the gorge; I'd seen her at night with two sets of numbers and a lamp, wearing the face of a woman doing arithmetic against her own blood.

"You've seen them," I said.

"I've seen them." A pause. The river talked below us, that slow deep talk it uses up here. "The published figures put the waterline lower than his survey does. His numbers show the flood reaching higher — up into this. All of this." She looked down at the roofs, the drying racks, the graves. "I couldn't make it make sense. Why he'd raise the estimate. Unless—"

"Unless he was counting the graves," I said.

She turned and looked at me.

"Your company counts houses," I said. "Houses can be moved. Houses are *negligible*. A man who counted the way your father counted — a man who walked this country and asked its name and wrote it down — he wouldn't stop at the door-posts. He'd count what can't be moved." I nodded down the slope, at the stones. "Maybe he raised the numbers because the true number of a drowned valley isn't the waterline. It's everything standing above it."

Ada didn't say anything for a long moment. Then she said, "He was here. Wasn't he. In this village."

"Twenty years ago. He sat where you're going to eat, and he asked the headman's grandfather the village's name, and he wrote it down, and he asked about the graves, and he wrote that down too. My uncle remembers him. A quiet man with a brass compass who paid for his fish with salt and didn't laugh at anything." I looked at her. "You have his face when you're adding."

"I don't—"

"You do. It's not a compliment. Adding is how your people pray."

She almost smiled at that. Almost. And then the smile went somewhere else, into that flat pressed-down place she keeps, and she said, "If his numbers are right, then the company's are wrong. Deliberately wrong. And if the company's are wrong on purpose, then the whole scheme—"

"Is built on a drowning they've agreed not to see," I finished. "Welcome to my country, engineer. We've known the figures were lies since before you were given your father's debts. What we don't have is paper. Paper is the only thing your people believe. A river can scream and it's just weather. But a number in the right hand—" I shrugged. "A number in the right hand is a god."

* * *

We went down to the village and ate, and it was a good meal, and I watched Ada sit among my mother's people with her awkwardness and her careful manners and her father's compass in her pocket, and something shifted in me that I did not enjoy. I had spent three weeks deciding she was company — company with a woman's face and a debtor's desperation, but company, company, company, the way a fish is a fish no matter how it's cooked. And here she was, asking my uncle's wife about the millet with genuine interest, and here she was, going quiet when the old men sang, and here she was, looking at the graves the way I look at rapids: reading them, respecting them, not summing them.

Belonging, I have learned, is not a place. It is a direction. You can be born on a river and be a stranger on its bank, and a coastal woman can sit down at a foreign fire and be, in one evening, less of a stranger than men who've drunk the wine three times. I did not like learning this. Learning it meant I could no longer sort the world into my people and the company, and I have sorted the world that way since I was old enough to see the survey boats coming, because sorting is how you survive being negligible.

That night, camped again on the bank below the village — the headman would not have us inside the palisade, not with company rifles, and I did not blame him — I sat by my fire and heard Ada and Vance talking low on the other side of the boats. Not arguing. Planning. Something about elevation checks, about the tunnel works two days upstream, about her father's log.

And then, past midnight, when the fire had gone to coals and I was dozing sitting up the way river people do, I heard footsteps. Not Ada's — she walked like a woman counting. Not Vance's — he walked like a man arriving. These footsteps went down to the water, quiet on the mud, and stopped at the boats.

I opened my eyes the width of a knife.

One of the porters — the big quiet one, Musa, who had been with us since the depot and had never once asked for anything — was standing at the stern of the middle boat. He had the fuel drums' lashing in his hands. And he was not checking it.

He was learning it. Fingers moving over the knots, slow, patient, memorizing — the way I memorize a rapid before I run it at night.

I lay still and watched him finish, watched him straighten, watched him look upriver toward the dark line of the hills where the tunnel works waited, and then walk back to his mat as if nothing in the world had weight.

I did not sleep after that. I lay on my back under the indifferent stars and did a new sum, and this one had no negligible terms in it at all: someone on this expedition was counting toward something, and it was not the source.
