## 13. Sabotage

I woke to the smell of petrol and burnt wood, which is a smell I will now associate with catastrophe for the rest of my life.

For a moment—half a moment—I thought it was breakfast. Ngozi had been experimenting with frying plantain over the camp stove, and the results had occasionally resembled arson. Then the half-moment ended and my brain finished its arithmetic: we were camped on a shingle bank with no wood fire burning, and the smell was coming from the water's edge, where our second boat should have been.

It wasn't. There was a black scar of it instead, half-sunk in the shallows, its gunwale charred down to the waterline like something that had tried to become a candle and given up halfway through its ambition. The fuel drums that had been lashed beneath the tarpaulin beside it were gone—two of them entirely, and the third lying on its side in the mud, punctured at the seam, emptying itself into the river with a soft, obscene glugging sound that seemed to go on far longer than three hundred litres reasonably should.

I stood there in my boots, unlaced, watching the river drink our fuel, and I did what any trained engineer does when confronted with an impossible figure. I checked it twice.

* * *

By full light the whole camp was up, and the arithmetic had gotten worse, as arithmetic does when you do it properly.

One boat destroyed. Not damaged—destroyed. The burn pattern told its own story if you knew how to read it, and I did. Petrol poured along the keel line, lit from the upstream side so the current would carry the flames aft and finish the job. Someone had done this standing in our camp, between the hours when Musa said he'd banked the cook fire and the hour before dawn when Joseph took first watch. A window of perhaps five hours, in a camp of eleven people, most of them sleeping close enough to hear a footstep on gravel.

Two drums of fuel gone, the third ruined. That left us perhaps enough petrol for one boat to run the lower gorge section and no more—the stretches above the falls were poling and portage work anyway, but the return journey, running the falling water with the dry season already biting into the river levels, needed reserve. Reserve was the whole discipline of river work. You planned for the river to be against you, because it always, eventually, was.

And then the supplies. The missing crate from two nights ago—the one that had gone with the two porters—had contained rice, salt, most of our medical kit, and, I discovered now, checking the manifest against what remained under the tarpaulin with the cold thoroughness of a woman doing her father's accounts, both spare propeller shafts and the tool roll.

Add it up. One boat. Half the food. No spare parts. No fuel to speak of. No radio—we hadn't heard base camp in nine days, and the set itself had finally died outright the previous evening with a smell of hot bakelite and a final click that sounded almost apologetic.

Eleven people. Six hundred kilometres of river behind us, four hundred ahead, and a window closing like a door in another room.

"Say it plainly," Ngozi said. She'd come up beside me at the tarpaulin without my hearing her, which she could do; she moved the way water moves, without announcing itself. "You've been counting since dawn. Say the number."

"Fourteen days of food at full rations," I said. "Twenty-one if we cut to half. The descent, if the levels hold—and they won't—is eight days hard running. Portage around the falls is two days minimum with one boat and everyone's backs. That leaves us nothing. No margin. Not even bad margin."

"And the radio?"

"The radio is kindling."

She looked at the burned boat for a long time. The river ran past it, indifferent, carrying off the last rainbow film of our fuel in long slow ribbons.

"In my mother's country," she said, "when a canoe is broken on purpose, you don't ask who did it. You ask who benefits." She turned and looked at the camp—not at the porters, who were standing in a silent cluster by the cook fire, but past them, at the canvas shelter where Elias Vance sat alone with a cup of tea he wasn't drinking. "Because someone wanted us stopped. Or someone wanted only some of us to arrive. And those are different crimes, Ada, and they want different answers."

* * *

I spent the morning doing what I am actually good at, which is not courage or leadership or any of the things the corps certificates used to say about me, but counting.

I inventoried everything. Every tin, every cartridge of blasting explosive we still carried from the tunnel works survey, every metre of rope, every tool. I laid it out on a groundsheet in rows like a shop display, and I made Elias and Ngozi witness the count, and I wrote the figures in my notebook in my father's cramped accounting hand, which I seem to have inherited along with his debts and his compass.

The exercise produced two findings.

The first was practical: we could move. Barely. One boat, lightened, carrying six people and the essential instruments, with five porters walking the banks where the going allowed and doubling back at the gorges. The other four would have to be paid off here—which meant sending them home through country where two of their fellows had already vanished, with wages counted out from a fund that would not cover the return journey at anything like fair rates. I wrote that figure too, and hated it.

The second finding was the one I didn't write down, because writing it down would make it real.

Someone in this camp had done this. Or someone outside it had done it while every soul in this camp slept within thirty feet of the boat—and I had stood the last watch myself until midnight, and I had heard nothing, seen nothing, and I do not sleep deeply on expedition, I never have. My father taught me that. You don't sleep deeply in country you haven't surveyed.

So either one of us was a liar of considerable skill, or we had a visitor who moved through a sleeping camp like smoke, and I genuinely did not know which possibility I disliked more.

Elias found me at the groundsheet in the afternoon. He crouched across from me, sleeves rolled, forearms on his knees, and for once the polish was gone off him—he looked like a man who had slept badly in his good shirt.

"You've been at this all day," he said.

"Somebody has to know exactly how little we have. It focuses the mind wonderfully."

"Ada." He lowered his voice. "About the night it happened. I was awake. Part of it."

That got my attention properly. "Define 'part of it.'"

"I went down to the water around two o'clock. Couldn't sleep. I—" He stopped, and I watched him choose, in real time, between two versions of himself. It was oddly moving, watching a man try to become honest at speed. "I was going to check your survey notes. Your father's figures. I copied some pages. I'm not going to pretend otherwise—you already know I was sent to verify them, and after the tunnel log, after all of it, I've been trying to decide what verifying them actually means and who it serves. But I was at the water, and I saw nothing. No one came or went. If someone burned that boat, they did it before I came down or after I left, and they did it quietly enough that a man standing ten metres away on open shingle heard nothing at all."

"Which is impossible."

"Which is impossible," he agreed. "Unless they came by water."

I looked at the river. Wide here, slow, brown-green, deep in the channel. A canoe paddling soft against the current, landing on the downstream side of the shingle where the burned boat's hull would have blocked the sightline from the tents—

"You've thought about this," I said.

"I've thought about nothing else since dawn." He rubbed his face. "There's something else. When I was copying your notes, I noticed the maintenance log from the tunnel—the one in your father's hand. The page after the closure date. There's a column of figures in the margin, smaller than the rest, in a different pencil. I assumed it was yours."

"It isn't mine."

"No," he said. "I didn't think it was. And I think whoever burned that boat may have been looking for that log. Because the supply crate that went with the porters—it wasn't random theft, Ada. I went through what's left of the manifest. They took the rice and the medicine, yes. But they also took the document case. The one with the company correspondence. Everything official we carried. They left your father's field books. Why would thieves leave the leather case worth money and take papers?"

I sat back on my heels. The river went on talking to itself, patient as ever, keeping whatever counsel rivers keep.

"They took the evidence that we were officially here," I said slowly. "And left the evidence of what my father found. Which means either they don't understand what they took—or they understand it perfectly, and what they're afraid of isn't us reporting back. It's what we might carry out of this valley that isn't company paper."

* * *

We held council that evening around the cook fire, and I put the arithmetic in front of everyone, because they'd earned the truth and because a crew that suspects its own officers hoards worse suspicions in the dark.

Musa spoke for the porters, as he usually did, in his careful formal English. He asked the question I'd been dreading, and he asked it looking straight at me, which was worse.

"Engineer. The two men who went. Thomas and Bright. Do you believe they took the crate?"

"I believe," I said, "that Thomas and Bright walked out of this camp toward the old cutting, and something happened to them there, and the crate was found opened near where their satchel was torn. I believe that if they deserted, they deserted carrying things they were told to carry. And I believe that whoever gave them those instructions—if anyone did—is sitting inside the sound of my voice."

Silence. The fire cracked. Downstream, the wreck of the boat knocked gently against its own mooring stone, a small wooden heartbeat.

"You are saying one of us," Musa said.

"I'm saying the numbers say one of us. I wish they said something kinder."

Ngozi, cross-legged at the fire's edge, spoke in the tone she used for arguing with rivers—flat, certain, leaving no room for negotiation. "Then we stop pretending we are one expedition. We are two boats' worth of people who happen to be rowing the same direction, and one of us is rowing for someone else. So here is what happens now. Nothing leaves this camp. No one walks alone, not to relieve themselves, not to fetch water. The instruments, the field books, and the remaining fuel sleep between the three of us"—she nodded at Elias and me—"and we take turns being awake. And when we move tomorrow, we move upriver, all of us together, and nobody is paid off and sent home through country where men vanish. We are not giving this river—or whoever works for it—stragglers."

Elias looked at her, then at me. "Upstream? With these supplies? Ada, the honest calculation says turn back. Half rations, one boat, dead radio—the responsible decision is to run for the coast and report."

"The responsible decision," I said, "is what whoever did this wants. Think about it. They didn't kill us in our beds. They didn't need to. They took our fuel, our spares, our papers, and our margin, and they left us alive with exactly enough to retreat. That's not sabotage, Elias. That's a steering hand on the tiller. Somebody is herding us, and they're herding us downstream."

He was quiet a moment. I watched him do his own arithmetic—the other kind, the kind with careers and orders in it—and then, to his credit, he did the thing I was beginning to learn he could do when it mattered. He chose.

"Then we go up," he said. "To the source. Before they herd us anywhere else."

* * *

I took the middle watch that night, as agreed, sitting between the wrapped instruments and the jerrican of remaining fuel with my father's compass open on my knee, more for the company than the bearing.

The camp slept. The river ran. Somewhere upstream, four hundred kilometres of falling water stood between me and a spring-fed basin where a dead man had hidden the truth, and somewhere behind me, a man in a coastal office was waiting for a radio call that would never come, and would eventually send someone to find out why, and what they would find—or be told they'd found—was a question I couldn't afford to answer yet.

I thought about my father's marginal figures, the small pencilled column Elias had noticed. I thought about a man staying secretly at a closed tunnel works, logging maintenance on a line that would never run, keeping numbers in tiny handwriting because tiny handwriting is what you use when you're afraid of being read.

He had known he was being watched. That was the thing I kept circling back to, the way your tongue returns to a broken tooth. Twenty years ago, my father had stood where I was standing—in this same valley, on this same losing side of an arithmetic—and he had chosen to falsify his survey rather than hand the company the valley. And the debt I'd inherited, the debt that had put me on this river in the first place, was the price of that choice. Someone had been paid to keep quiet, and the bill had come to me.

Now someone was paying again. Fuel and boats and stolen papers and two vanished men—that was money moving, somewhere, in the dark, buying silence a second time.

I closed the compass. In the east, above the black line of the gorge, the sky had begun to go grey at the edges, the way it does before dawn, before decisions.

Tomorrow we went up. Toward the source, toward my father's cache, toward whatever waited there besides the truth.

And behind us, on the shingle, the burned boat knocked softly against its stone, all night, like something trying to tell us what it had seen.
